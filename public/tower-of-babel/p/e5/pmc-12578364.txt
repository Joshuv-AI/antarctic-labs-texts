Parent and Child Influences on Adrenocortical Synchrony During Middle Childhood
Authors: Sarah R Black, Lauren Aaron, Rachel M Kaplan, Kaetlyn Conner Gottschalk, Hannah Duttweiler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParent–child synchrony, or coordination of behavioral, affective, and physiological responding during dyadic interactions, is associated with numerous positive child outcomes. Research considering how parental psychopathology influences synchrony, however, has failed to assess cooccurring parenting behaviors and styles that may influence synchrony. The current study assessed how parental history of depressive disorders, parenting styles and behaviors, and child behaviors during a dyadic interaction influence parent–child adrenocortical synchrony (PCAS). Participants included 40 parent–child dyads with children aged between 8 and 13 years (M age = 10 years), with about half (47%) of parents meeting criteria for lifetime depressive disorders. Parent and child cortisol levels were measured before, during, and after children completed a challenging task with parents observing nearby. Dyads completed questionnaire measures of parenting styles, and video recordings were coded to assess parent and child behaviors during the task. Using multilevel regression analyses, we found that both parent‐ and child‐reported greater parental firmness were associated with negative PCAS. Furthermore, fewer child help‐seeking behaviors during the challenging task were associated with negative PCAS. Parental history of depressive disorders, however, did not influence PCAS. These results suggest that parent and child behaviors may influence the direction and magnitude of PCAS during middle childhood over and above parental psychopathology.

Introduction

Parent–child synchrony is a measure of parent–child relationship functioning that has significant associations with child well‐being beyond the early childhood period during which it is most often investigated (Davis et al. 2017; Feldman 2020; Herbers et al. 2014; Hinnant et al. 2013; Saxbe et al. 2015). While concordant synchrony is often viewed as optimal, of growing interest are conditions under which synchrony is impaired and/or less desirable (e.g., Perlman et al. 2022). Parental depression is a robust risk factor for the development of depression in offspring (Aaron et al. 2024) and may also represent a risk condition under which concordant synchrony may not be adaptive (Amole et al. 2017; Gray et al. 2018; Merwin et al. 2017; Papp et al. 2009). Previous research has highlighted both hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation and parenting behaviors as potential mechanisms through which parental depression may confer psychological risk to children (Koch et al. 2019; Lovejoy et al. 2000; Lupien et al. 2011; Mackrell et al. 2014). Critically, however, not all children with a depressed parent go on to develop depression themselves. Therefore, it is essential to identify factors that may increase the risk of developing psychopathology in these vulnerable youth, including patterns of synchrony.

Previous literature examining how parental depression impacts parent–child synchrony has yielded mixed findings, perhaps due to a focus on parental diagnoses and symptoms, rather than general parenting styles and behaviors that may be associated with these diagnoses, as the primary targets of investigation (Amole et al. 2017; Merwin et al. 2017; Papp et al. 2009; Woody et al. 2016). Furthermore, child behaviors during interactions are infrequently considered as influencing parent–child synchrony, a critical limitation of the existing research on synchrony during middle childhood (Nguyen et al. 2020). The current study, therefore, expanded upon previous investigations to assess how parental depression, parenting styles, and parent and child behaviors during a dyadic interaction influence the direction and magnitude of parent–child adrenocortical synchrony (PCAS) during middle childhood.

Parent–Child Synchrony

Parent–child synchrony, or the coordination between parents and children's affect, behaviors, and physiological activity during dyadic interactions, is a feature of the parent–child relationship that is critical for the development of child self‐regulatory abilities (Feldman et al. 1999; Kochanska et al. 2008). Specifically, before children have developed independent self‐regulation abilities, they are dependent upon their primary caretakers to help them externally regulate their emotions and behaviors (Leclère et al. 2014). Ultimately and ideally, children will internalize the process of self‐regulation across development via repeated exposure to parental expectations for behavior, modeling, and exposure to parental physiological patterns (Kochanska et al. 2008).

Adrenocortical Synchrony

PCAS, or coordination of parent and child's HPA axes during dyadic interactions, has been investigated as a biological analog to (or even a driver of; Byrd‐Craven and Clauss 2019) behavioral synchrony. PCAS likely represents the degree to which one member of the dyad is sensitive to and influenced by the other's physiological arousal (Hibel et al. 2015; Ruttle et al. 2011; Sbarra and Hazan 2008) and facilitates the development of the child's immature stress response systems (Byrd‐Craven and Clauss 2019; Feldman 2015, Feldman 2012, Hofer 1996). Specifically, before children can independently assess environmental threats and opportunities, they may emulate parental physiological responding, which subsequently ensures their safety and survival (Byrd‐Craven and Clauss 2019). In contrast to other forms of physiological synchrony (e.g., synchrony of the autonomic nervous system [ANS] via respiratory sinus arrhythmia [RSA] or electrodermal activity [EDA]), PCAS operates on a relatively slow timescale, as physiological reactions to psychological stressors are not reflected in cortisol activity until approximately 15–20 min after the onset of the stressor (Olivera‐Figueroa et al. 2015). Cortisol activity is altered in conditions of chronic stress among both parents and children (Williams et al. 2013), including in the case of parental psychopathology (Barry et al. 2015; Lupien et al. 2011; Mackrell et al. 2014; Shapero et al. 2017). PCAS, therefore, may represent shared biological adaptation to the familial milieu and shared environmental risk among families experiencing psychopathology. Parents with chronic and persistent depression, for example, may have blunted HPA axis reactivity, which may then be reflected in child HPA axis activity (Burke et al. 2005) and result in concordant, but potentially maladaptive, PCAS. Concordant PCAS is typically found in healthy parent–child dyads during early childhood (Di Lorenzo et al. 2022) as well as middle childhood and adolescence (Birk et al. 2022).

Why Synchrony Matters

The literature on parent–child behavioral synchrony generally demonstrates a positive impact of concordant synchrony on child outcomes. Previous research has demonstrated that concordant behavioral synchrony in infancy and early childhood is associated with increased self‐regulation, attachment security, and fewer behavioral problems (Davis et al. 2017; Feldman 2012; Feldman et al. 1999; Kochanska et al. 2008). Beyond early childhood, concordant synchrony continues to be associated with fewer behavioral problems, greater empathy, and greater emotion regulation abilities during middle childhood and adolescence (Feldman 2007a, 2007b, 2015). Discordant or negative behavioral synchrony, in contrast, has been associated with temperamental difficulties, greater parenting stress, and child psychological problems (Garcia‐Sellers and Church 2000; Im‐Bolter et al. 2015). Physiological synchrony, including PCAS, is most concordant in the context of concordant behavioral synchrony (Davis et al. 2018) and has been independently associated with both positive outcomes such as improved adolescent social cognition (Saxbe et al. 2015) and negative outcomes such as increased risk of intergenerational transmission of negative behaviors (Ahemaitijiang et al. 2021).

Importantly, the adaptive function of concordant parent–child synchrony has been demonstrated to vary by context. Previous research has explored poverty (Kotchick et al. 2021), chronic and traumatic stress (Di Lorenzo et al. 2022; Halevi et al. 2017; Levy et al. 2019; Ouellette et al. 2015), and parental mental illness (Creaven et al. 2014; Suveg et al. 2019) as factors that may impair parent–child synchrony and/or alter the protective role that synchrony typically plays in nonrisk contexts. As such, parent–child synchrony may be adaptive in certain contexts but present risks in others (DePasquale 2020; Perlman et al. 2022). It is possible that strong concordant behavioral and physiological synchrony is adaptive when parents are psychologically and emotionally healthy, as their children may emulate this adaptive behavioral and physiological functioning. Conversely, strong concordant behavioral and physiological synchrony may be less optimal when parents are struggling with their own emotional and physiological dysregulation, as in the case of parental depressive and anxiety disorders. In other words, concordant synchrony may be adaptive when children have healthy behaviors and physiological patterns to emulate.

Impacts on Parent–Child Synchrony

Parental Depression

Parental depression is associated with numerous negative child outcomes, including increased risk for internalizing and externalizing psychopathology (Aaron et al. 2024; Garber 2006; Goodman and Gotlib 1999; Mars et al. 2012; Weissman et al. 2016). Work considering synchrony among children with depressed parents has demonstrated decreased affective and behavioral synchrony within these dyads (Im‐Bolter et al. 2015; Pratt et al. 2019; Priel et al. 2019). Research on PCAS in depressed parent–child dyads, however, suggests that parental depression is associated with stronger concordant synchrony, which opposes the findings with behavioral synchrony (Merwin et al. 2017; Papp et al. 2009).

The impacts of parental depression on parent–child synchrony may be at least partially due to compromised parenting behaviors among parents experiencing depression, as meta‐analytic evidence demonstrated that the association between parent depression and child symptoms is mediated by parenting behaviors (Goodman et al. 2020). Indeed, parental depression has been associated with increased hostility, decreased warmth, and reduced responsivity to children's affect (Lovejoy et al. 2000; Wilson and Durbin 2010). These findings, however, do not suggest that parents with a history of depression will necessarily demonstrate such behaviors or that such behaviors may not be modifiable. Indeed, previous work investigating parents with depressive disorders has indicated that parental behavior, over and above psychopathology, may be a more fruitful target for investigation (Goodman et al. 2020; Hendrix et al. 2018). Parenting behaviors are likely of critical importance to understanding PCAS in depressed parent–child dyads, as recent work in both low‐ and high‐risk dyads suggests that strong physiological synchrony may only be maladaptive in the context of negative parenting behaviors and may actually be protective in the context of positive parenting behaviors (Oshri et al. 2023; Verhagen et al. 2024).

Parenting Behaviors

Previous literature has explored how parenting behaviors, irrespective of clinical diagnoses, impact parent–child synchrony. Parental responsiveness, flexibility, and attention are critical elements in parent–child synchrony, as parents must be attuned to their children's emotional states in order to provide contingent emotion regulation support (Feldman 2015). Accordingly, parenting behaviors and styles that promote flexible engagement, such as sensitivity (Longhi 2009; Sethre‐Hofstad et al. 2002; Thompson and Trevathan 2009) and nurturance (Skuban et al. 2006), have been associated with concordant behavioral and/or physiological synchrony. Interestingly, both high (Borelli et al. 2019) and low (Han et al. 2019) overcontrol have also been associated with concordant synchrony, while parental psychological aggression (Lunkenheimer et al. 2018) and emotional unavailability (Han et al. 2019) were associated with weaker or discordant synchrony. Findings regarding PCAS have been similarly mixed, as concordant PCAS has been positively associated with maternal sensitivity, reciprocity, and firm and overcontrolling parenting styles (Atkinson et al. 2013; Borelli et al. 2019; Hibel et al. 2015; Laurent et al. 2012; Oshri et al. 2021). As noted above, however, the adaptive nature of certain parenting behaviors likely varies by contextual factors, including task demands and emotional content, leading to mixed findings in the existing synchrony literature.

Child Behaviors

Relatedly, and consistent with the principles of developmental psychopathology (Cicchetti and Toth 2009; Rutter 2013), child behavior during dyadic interactions may also significantly influence patterns of PCAS (Brown et al. 2022). This may be especially true during middle childhood, when children are still dependent on their parents for emotional support and guidance (especially as the challenges they face become more complex [Bosmans and Kerns 2015]) but have increasing influence on parent–child dynamics via growing autonomy (Beveridge and Berg 2007; Birk et al. 2022; Feldman et al. 2013; Lougheed et al. 2020). Previous work considering how children's active involvement in dyadic interactions influences synchrony has demonstrated that child effortful control (Brown et al. 2022) and agency (Nguyen et al. 2020) are associated with increased PCS, while child negative emotionality (Pratt et al.2015) is associated with discordant or negative physiological synchrony.

Current Study

To address the limitations noted above, the current study measured PCAS during middle childhood in parent–child dyads with and without parental history of depressive disorders. Previous research has suggested that experiencing psychopathology can negatively impact family functioning even beyond a symptomatic period (Herr et al. 2007). Furthermore, validated self‐report assessments of depression symptoms typically ask respondents to consider symptoms over a short time period (e.g., 2 weeks). This may obscure the impact of depression on children, as parents with even recent depressive episodes may be emerging from a period of illness at the time of assessment, such that symptom scores may not fully represent the magnitude of a child's exposure to parental depression. As such, we considered both categorical, clinician‐rated measures of depressive diagnoses and dimensional, self‐report measures of depressive symptoms in the current analyses.

First, we assessed the degree to which parent and child cortisol levels covaried as parents observed their child complete a challenging task and during a subsequent recovery period. Previous literature supports the notion that parents observing their children complete challenging or difficult tasks often demonstrate both physiological and emotional reactivity, especially when parents are experiencing their own emotion regulation challenges (Borelli et al. 2015, 2017; Borelli, St. John, et al. 2016; Woodruff‐Borden et al. 2002). As such, measuring parent and child cortisol during this task provides an ecologically valid assessment of how parental reactivity to children's struggles may influence PCAS. Based on the existing PCAS literature, we hypothesized that parent and child cortisol levels would covary positively and significantly (concordant synchrony). Second, we assessed how parental factors, including history of depressive disorders, current depressive symptoms, and parenting behaviors and styles influenced patterns of PCAS. We hypothesized that a history of parental depression will be associated with the direction and magnitude of synchrony such that dyads including a parent with a history of depression would demonstrate stronger PCAS than dyads without a parental history of depression. We further hypothesized, however, that parenting styles and observed parenting behaviors would influence patterns of PCAS more than parental depression. Finally, as an exploratory hypothesis, we considered if child behaviors during the challenging task influenced PCAS.

Method

Study Design and Recruitment

The current sample consisted of parents and children from the greater New Orleans area. Participants were recruited through Facebook advertisements and group postings, as well as targeted emails to parenting groups, flyers placed in the community, and word of mouth. Parents of children between the ages of 8 and 13 years were invited to participate in a study investigating how parents and children work together. Parents were directed to an online survey via a QR code or direct link to a Qualtrics survey where they entered their contact information and preferences. Interested participants were then contacted by the study team to complete a brief telephone screening (see below), and eligible participants were scheduled for lab visits following the screening.

Participants

Participants were 40 parent–child dyads. Parent participants were primarily mothers (90% female), were approximately 41 years old (range = 29.05–54.25 years, SD = 5.68 years), and identified their race as White (n = 25, 62.5%), Black (n = 10, 25.0%), or Other/did not report (n = 5, 12.5%). Child participants were split evenly by sex (52% male), were approximately 10 years old (range = 8.09–13.00 years; SD = 1.44 years) and generally in the early stages of pubertal development (mean Tanner equivalent score = 1.87, range = 1.00–3.75; SD = 0.78) at the time of the study visit, and had a similar distribution of racial identities as their parents (White: n = 26, 65.0%; Black: n = 9, 22.5%; Other/did not report: n = 5, 12.5%). Nearly half (47.5%) of parent participants had a history of a depressive disorder (as assessed via the SCID‐5; see below), with 25% of the sample (n = 10) describing a depressive disorder during their child's lifetime and one participant (2.5%) meeting criteria for a current depressive disorder.

Procedure

Recruitment for the current project began in December 2019 and continued through December 2022, with significant breaks in the recruitment period due to the COVID‐19 pandemic. All study materials and procedures were approved by the local institutional review board (IRB) prior to study start. Interested participants first completed a brief telephone screen to establish eligibility for the study protocol. Potential participants were excluded due to parent or child use of cortisol‐interfering medications; maternal pregnancy or lactation; parental bipolar or psychotic disorders and developmental disabilities (assessed via parent report); child diagnosis of depressive or other mood, psychotic, or developmental disorders (assessed via parent report); or inability to participate in study procedures for any reason (e.g., vision or hearing challenges).

Prior to completing any study tasks, informed consent was obtained from parents and assent was obtained from children. During the consenting process, participants were asked to indicate their permission for videotaping during the visit, including during the clinical interview and the challenge task. Following the consenting process, parents and children completed study questionnaires in a quiet environment. Approximately 45 min after arriving at the lab, parents and children participated in a challenging task (PCT; see below) and associated saliva samples were collected (see below for sampling schedule). A trained graduate assistant then interviewed parents regarding their own history of psychopathology, while undergraduate research assistants assisted the child in finishing their questionnaires. Finally, participants were compensated for their time and exited the lab.

Measures

Performance Challenge Task (PCT)

The PCT is a standardized laboratory task that has been used in previous investigations (Arbel et al. 2021; Borelli et al. 2017). Children were instructed to complete a series of computerized puzzles, based on the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC‐V; Wechsler 2014) Block Design subtest. Children were presented with an empty 3 × 3 puzzle frame that could accommodate nine tiles, as well as an array of 10 colored tiles (red, white, or half red and half white). The children were instructed to move the colored tiles into the puzzle frame one by one until their completed puzzle matched the example they were shown. The puzzles were impossible to solve, however, as the children were only presented with eight of the correct tiles to complete the puzzle and two extraneous pieces. Children were presented with a total of six puzzles to complete and received negative feedback on their performance in the form of a frowning face, indicating that they solved the puzzle incorrectly. They then saw bars indicating their performance compared to other children who hypothetically also completed the task. During the PCT, parents were seated nearby and not given explicit guidance how to behave during the task; whether they helped or interacted with their child during the task was ultimately at the parent's discretion. Following the collection of the final recovery saliva sample (T4), parents and children were debriefed about the deception and intention of the task.

Cortisol Collection and Assay

Saliva samples were collected from both parents and children at four points throughout the laboratory visit. Participants were instructed not to eat or drink for 30 min prior to the lab visit, in order to ensure that they had not eaten or drunk 60 min prior to saliva sampling. The first sample was collected immediately before introduction of the challenge task (PCT), approximately 45 min after arriving to the lab (T1). The second sample was collected immediately after the PCT was completed (T2; 20 min after the initial sample), the third was collected 20 min after completion of the PCT (T3; 40 min after the initial sample), and the fourth was collected 40 min after completion of the PCT (T4; 60 min after the initial sample). Such a sampling schedule is comparable to those used during the Trier Social Stress Test and other laboratory stressors, as the effect of stress on salivary cortisol concentrations is delayed by an average of 20 min (Kirschbaum and Hellhammer 1994). Saliva was collected via passive drool into provided tubes, labeled, and refrigerated immediately after collection; after completion of the study visit, samples were aliquoted into multiple smaller samples and moved into deep freezer storage (−80°C).

Cortisol was assayed from each sample in duplicate using commercially available competitive enzyme immunoassays (i.e., ELISAs) specifically designed for use with saliva according to the manufacturer's recommended protocol (Salimetrics, State College, PA). All assaying was completed at the University of New Orleans by graduate student assistants trained on best practices in hormone analyses. Samples with intraassay coefficients of variability (CV%) greater than 10 were excluded from analyses (n = 25 observations; 8% of total samples). Following this, the mean intraassay CV% was <10, while the mean interassay CV% was <15. To minimize time‐of‐day variation in cortisol levels, and consistent with expert recommendations (e.g., Kudielka et al. 2004; Labuschagne et al. 2019), all study visits were completed in the early afternoon on weekend days.

Parental Psychopathology

Graduate assistants trained by the first author (S.R.B.) administered selected sections (overview, mood disorders, and anxiety disorders modules) of the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐5—Research Version (SCID‐5‐RV; First et al. 2015) to all parents at the study visit. The SCID‐5‐RV is a semistructured diagnostic interview that is used to assess lifetime psychopathology and was used in the current study to confirm (clinical sample) or disconfirm (nonclinical sample) a history of depressive disorders in parents. Interviews were video recorded with the parent's permission; a select number of interviews (n = 7) were double coded by graduate assistants not involved in the original interviews to compute interrater reliability statistics for depressive diagnoses (κ = 1.00).

Behavioral Observations

Parent behavior was coded using the Behavior Involvement Parenting Scale (BIPS; Rasmussen et al. 2017), which assesses instances of parental support and overcontrol during and following the PCT. Child behavior was coded using the Children's Help‐Seeking Scale (CHBS; Borelli, Smiley, et al. 2016), including measures of child's potent (direct) and subtle (indirect) bids for assistance from their parent. Potent help‐seeking behaviors included turning to face parent, calling parent's name, or making direct requests for help, while subtle help‐seeking behaviors included disengagement from the computer screen, negative self‐talk pertaining to the task, nonlanguage vocalizations, or exaggerated body movements. Behavioral coding was completed by doctoral trainees and advanced undergraduate research assistants in groups of two or more, all of whom were masked to the diagnostic status of parent participants. Interrater discrepancies were resolved through discussion at weekly meetings, and consensus scores were utilized in all analyses. Global scores of both parent and child behaviors across the PCT were utilized in the current analyses.

Questionnaire Measures

Beck Depression Inventory

Parents completed the Beck Depression Inventory—Second Edition (BDI‐II; Beck et al. 1996), a 21‐item self‐report questionnaire assessing current depressive symptoms. Questions are answered on a 4‐point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating more intense or frequent symptoms and scores under 13 indicating minimal or no depression. Reliability was good (α = 0.86).

Children's Report of Parental Behavior (CRPBI‐30; Schuldermann and Schuldermann 1988)

The CRPBI‐30 is a 30‐item parent‐ and child‐report questionnaire designed to assess parental acceptance versus rejection, psychological control versus autonomy, and firm versus lax control (e.g., “firmness”). Questions are answered on a Likert scale from 1 (Not like) to 3 (A lot like). Reliability for the subscales was adequate to good, ranging from α = 0.64 (child report of firmness) to α = 0.83 (child report of psychological control). Notably, the reliability for parent report of psychological control was unacceptably low (α = 0.41); after investigation of intercorrelations between items and the subscale total as well as consideration of removal of items, this subscale was removed from analyses using parent‐reported CRPBI; psychological control was retained in models using child report of CRPBI, however.

Demographic and Methodological Covariates

Demographic information, including parent and child sex, child age, child pubertal development (measured via the Pubertal Development Scale [PDS; Carskadon and Acebo 1993]), and child race, was collected from parents and included in all analyses (apart from the baseline models). Methodological variables including time of saliva collection and discrepancy between parent and child collection time were collected by the study team during lab visits and also included in all models.

Data Analytic Plan

All data analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team 2024), with primary analyses using the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015). We first conducted bivariate correlations to assess for relations between study variables of interest. We identified right skew in our cortisol measure and subsequently used log‐transformed cortisol outcomes in all analyses to satisfy normality assumptions in residuals.

We then used multilevel regression analyses (MLM) to assess PCAS and variables that may influence this synchrony, as MLM is most appropriate for nested data structures (e.g., repeated measures within an individual). A two‐level model was implemented with repeated measures nested within each individual (e.g., parent and child). Consistent with previous investigations (e.g., Merwin et al. 2017; Suveg et al. 2019), child cortisol across PCT timepoints was the outcome variable, while parent cortisol was the primary predictor variable. “Synchrony” was defined as significant associations between child and parent cortisol over time; “negative” or “discordant” synchrony was indicated when the effect was negative, while “positive” or “concordant” synchrony was indicated when the effect was positive. Additional study variables of interest (including parent and child behavior during the PCT, parent‐ and child‐reported parenting measures, and parental depression diagnosis) served as additional predictors, while demographic factors with theorized relations with parent–child relations and cortisol (including child age and pubertal status, and parent and child sex and race) and timing variables (including time of collection and discrepancy between parent and child cortisol collection times) were included in the models as additional covariates. Parents’ current depression was also included as a covariate to assess the relative contribution of history of depression versus current depressive symptoms on synchrony. Two‐ and three‐way interaction terms with parent cortisol were included to assess factors influencing the direction and magnitude of parent–child synchrony, and nonsignificant three‐way interactions were trimmed from the final models.

We conducted residual diagnostics to ensure optimal fit for all models, including (but not limited to) assessment of outliers. While Cook's (1977) distance did not indicate that outliers significantly influenced our model results, visual inspection of the raw data suggested several cortisol outliers. While these outliers likely represent natural variability in our sample, we conducted supplemental analyses with outliers removed to confirm that these values did not bias our models. We found that our models were robust to inclusion/removal of these outliers and subsequently retained all data points in our final models. Finally, given the low number of fathers (n = 4) versus mothers (n = 36) in our sample, we conducted sensitivity analyses to assess how exclusion of fathers would influence our results. Removing fathers strengthened some associations and attenuated others across models; subsequently, we elected to include fathers in our sample to retain sample size as well as to increase generalizability of our findings to a larger group of parents.

Subtle help‐seeking behaviors were significantly negatively associated with child age (younger children used more subtle help‐seeking behaviors) and positively associated with parent overcontrol during the PCT. Parent overcontrol was also significantly positively associated with child‐reported parental psychological control and negatively associated with child age.

Post Hoc Analyses

To further clarify the findings above, we conducted additional analyses considering the potential interactive effect of parental firmness and child help‐seeking behaviors on PCAS. A three‐way interaction between parental firmness, child potent help‐seeking behaviors, and parent cortisol was not associated with child cortisol levels across the PCT (B = −1.24, SE = 1.20, CI [−3.63 to 1.17]; Table 10). Children's subtle help‐seeking behaviors, however, did significantly moderate the interaction between parental firmness and parent cortisol on child cortisol levels (Table 11). Specifically, when children use more (+1 SD) subtle help‐seeking behaviors, the effect of parent firmness on PCAS was nonsignificant at all levels. At mean levels of subtle help‐seeking behaviors, however, lower firmness is associated with positive PCAS (B = 2.31, SE = 1.15, CI [0.19–6.67]). Discussion

Previous research has demonstrated the utility of concordant parent–child synchrony in healthy dyads during infancy and toddlerhood but much less so during middle childhood. Furthermore, previous work considering parental psychological disorders without attention to parenting styles and behaviors has contributed to mixed findings and limited our understanding of the mechanisms by which parental depression may influence PCAS. Finally, previous literature has typically considered parents as drivers of physiological synchrony without consideration of how children's behaviors influence PCAS. The current study addressed these limitations by considering whether parenting styles and parent and child behaviors during a challenging task, as well as parental history of depressive disorders and current symptoms, were associated with PCAS during middle childhood.

Contrary to our hypotheses, we failed to find evidence of significant concordant or discordant PCAS in the full sample. We also did not find evidence that PCAS was associated with parental lifetime depressive diagnoses or current depression symptoms. While previous investigations of PCAS in the context of parental depression have also utilized lifetime measures (LeMoult et al. 2015; Merwin et al. 2017), as we did in this investigation, it may be that the relatively low levels of recent/current depression in this sample may have obscured potential associations between parental depression and PCAS. Previous research has suggested that experiencing psychopathology can negatively impact family functioning even beyond a symptomatic period (Herr et al. 2007), which theoretically could have downstream influences on offspring; when considering PCAS, however, it may be that current symptomatology is a more potent indicator of parent–child relational problems than a history of depression (Mars et al. 2012).

Considering parent behaviors and styles provided more insight into drivers of synchrony in the current sample. First, both parents’ and children's reports of firmness as a parenting style were associated with negative PCAS. Put another way, at high levels of synchrony, parent firmness was low, while the reverse was true at low levels of synchrony. While firm control includes behavioral control and structure, which are typically associated with positive behavioral outcomes (Kerr et al. 2012), meta‐analytic evidence suggests that firm parental control is significantly, albeit weakly, associated with youth externalizing behaviors (Pinquart 2017). Therefore, while not entirely maladaptive as a parenting strategy, parent firmness may impede synchrony because interpersonal behaviors and decisions may be made not through collaboration and sensitivity to child's signals but rather through rote rules and broad values. Parents who do not regularly attune to their children in order to make child‐rearing decisions, in turn, may demonstrate less physiological reactivity to their children's signals. However, other work has demonstrated that firm control was associated with increased physiological (i.e., RSA) synchrony between parents and children when children were classified as highly reactive (as indicated by greater galvanic skin response reactivity during a stressful task) versus those that demonstrated lower reactivity (Oshri et al. 2021). As such, future work will be needed to clarify the impact of firm control on physiological synchrony and the contextual factors (e.g., parent and child reactivity, relationship quality, etc.) that may influence this relation.

Child behavior also influenced patterns of PCAS in this sample, as fewer direct bids for help (“potent” help‐seeking behavior) during the PCT were associated with negative PCAS. An exploratory analysis of the interactions between parental firmness and child help‐seeking lent more nuance to this finding, however. Potent help‐seeking behaviors did not influence the moderating effect of parental firmness on PCAS, but subtle help‐seeking behaviors did. Specifically, firm control did not significantly influence PCAS when children used higher than average levels of subtle help‐seeking behaviors. At mean and low levels of help‐seeking, however, parental firmness was associated with negative PCAS. In sum, the most negative PCAS was seen among children who used the fewest help‐seeking behaviors and whose parents used more firmness as a parenting style. Importantly, parental firmness and child help‐seeking behavior (both potent and subtle) were negatively associated in the current sample. Taken together, these findings suggest that children whose parents utilize a firmer style may be conditioned to request less help, which may inhibit PCAS. Put another way, child help‐seeking may be buffering the (potentially) negative impact of firm control on PCAS. It is also possible, however, that children whose parents utilize more firm control felt better equipped to manage the challenging task and genuinely needed less help partially due to their parent's firmer style. Future research with larger samples, however, will be needed to clarify and support these conclusions.

Surprisingly, parental behavior during the PCT did not significantly influence PCAS in the current sample. While the task utilized in the current study was designed to be ecologically valid and to mimic experiences children and parents may have regularly (e.g., child completes a difficult task while parent makes decisions about whether or not to assist), it may be that the laboratory setup and presence of both research staff and video cameras may have impeded more naturalistic responding from parents. This may have led to lower variability in our observation of negative or undesirable parenting behaviors (Gardner 2000; Webster‐Stratton 1985). Because parents were not given explicit instructions how to behave (by design), it is also possible that parents were confused about their role in the challenging task and if they were meant to assist their child, which may have similarly influenced rates of supportive parenting behaviors. Again, future research considering these methodological issues will clarify the null findings herein.

The current study had several strengths, including use of both clinician‐rated and self‐report measures of parental depression, use of an ecologically valid challenging task, repeated measurement of cortisol over the PCT task, observational measures of parent and child behavior, and consideration of both parents’ and children's reports of parenting behavior. We also acknowledge notable limitations in the current investigation, including a limited sample size with most parents being mothers. Furthermore, levels of both current depressive disorders (n = 1) and dimensional measures of depression (77% of sample at or below “mild” depression levels) were quite low, which may have impeded our ability to detect impacts of parental depression on PCAS. Additionally, we had limited opportunity to assess outcomes associated with PCAS in this sample. Post hoc analyses considering child depressive symptoms as external validators of children's functioning and the utility of PCAS yielded marginally significant results, but future research with larger samples will be needed to fully explore and elucidate these relations. Future research would also benefit from more fine‐grained assessments of parent and child behavior, as the current observational data were limited to global behavioral codes, rather than multiple ratings across epochs. Finally, the limited racial and ethnic variability in our sample precluded assessment of differences in PCAS across demographic groups.

The findings herein, while based on a small sample, suggest important clinical implications. During middle childhood, children vacillate between desired independence and needed parental support for growing social, academic, and emotional demands. As such, parental loosening of control during this developmental period may facilitate independence and enable children to take control of their own challenging situations. The ability to recruit parental assistance during these situations, however, may be evidence of a strong attachment and facilitate shared physiological responding during moments of stress. Importantly, previous work has indicated that strong concordant physiological synchrony may be less optimal in the context of negative parenting behaviors (Oshri et al. 2023; Verhagen et al. 2024). As such, patterns observed in the current study (lower PCAS in the context of firm parenting) suggest generally adaptive functioning in our parent–child dyads, although this interpretation will require replication with larger samples as well as validation via positive associations with children's well‐being.

In sum, while parents with depression may experience challenges with reflective functioning and emotional socialization with their children (Krink et al. 2018; Waslin et al. 2023), the results herein point to modifiable targets for intervention that can be applied to parents across the diagnostic spectrum. Future research should expand the current work by including larger, more diverse samples with more father participants, as well as by assessing synchrony longitudinally to better understand the stability and utility of PCAS in the context of parental depression.
