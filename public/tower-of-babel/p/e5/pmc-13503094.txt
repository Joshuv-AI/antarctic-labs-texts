Socioeconomic and clinical factors associated with genetic testing completion in Stargardt disease
Authors: Dorothy T Wang, Bani Antonio-Aguirre, Annabelle Pan, Maria Ludovica Ruggeri, Setu P Mehta, Christy H Smith, Kelsey S Guthrie, Carolyn Applegate, Jefferson J Doyle, Mandeep S Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGenetic testing (GT) is crucial for Stargardt disease (STGD) diagnosis and clinical trial (CT) eligibility; however, predictors of GT completion remain understudied. We identified factors associated with GT completion and characterized CT participation among genetically confirmed patients.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 280 patients with clinically diagnosed STGD who were evaluated at a US tertiary referral center (2003-2024). Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify predictors of GT completion.ResultsAmong 280 participants, 246 (88%) completed GT. GT completion was significantly less likely in participants who were Black (odds ratio [OR]: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.16-0.98), under/uninsured (OR: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.19-0.97), without genetic counseling (GC) (OR: 0.09, 95% CI: 0.03-0.22), or without reproductive plans (OR: 0.35, 95% CI: 0.14-0.89). Clinical factors (eg, age of onset, visual acuity, and family history) showed no significant associations. Common reasons for noncompletion included loss to follow-up (41%), patient refusal (26%), and awaiting GC (24%). Among 223 ABCA4-positive participants, 45 (20%) enrolled in CTs, with most (91%) completing GC.ConclusionDespite high overall GT completion rates, significant racial and socioeconomic disparities exist. GC emerged as the strongest modifiable factor, conferring over 10-fold higher odds of GT completion. Socioeconomic barriers drive testing disparities in STGD, rather than disease severity or clinical characteristics.Keywords: ABCA4, Clinical trial recruitment, Genetic counseling, Genetic eye disease, Health disparities

Introduction

Stargardt disease (STGD) is the most common form of hereditary macular dystrophy, with an estimated global prevalence of 1:6562.1 It is primarily caused by autosomal recessive variants in the ABCA4 gene, leading to toxic bisretinoid accumulation in retinal pigment epithelial cells and progressive bilateral central vision loss that typically begins in the first or second decade of life.2, 3, 4

Genetic testing (GT) plays a vital role in diagnosing and managing inherited retinal diseases (IRDs) such as STGD. GT provides diagnostic confirmation, prognosis estimation, identification of inheritance patterns, screening of at-risk family members, and determination of eligibility for current clinical trials (CTs) and future gene-specific therapies.5 Despite these benefits, access to GT has historically been limited by factors such as high costs, insurance restrictions, and variability in provider familiarity with testing protocols.5, 6, 7

In the United States, no-cost GT initiatives, including eyeGENE and the Foundation Fighting Blindness My Retina Tracker GT Study (FFB-MRT-GTS), have expanded access for patients with IRD.8, 9, 10 Studies on IRDs considered as a broad disease class have identified several factors associated with GT completion, including the availability of no-cost testing programs, patient language preferences, referral to specialized centers with integrated genetic counseling (GC) services, an early age of symptom onset, and a positive family history.9,11, 12, 13, 14, 15 However, significant barriers remain, particularly among underrepresented populations, where disparities in referral patterns and access to GC may limit GT completion and subsequent access to investigational therapies.6

Although studies have examined GT patterns in IRDs broadly, few have specifically identified predictors of GT completion in STGD populations.9,16 Furthermore, the impact of socioeconomic factors (ie, education level, employment status, and insurance type) on GT completion in IRDs remains unexplored. Understanding these factors is increasingly important, as numerous therapeutic strategies for STGD—including gene therapy, stem cell therapy, optogenetics, and pharmaceuticals—are under investigation.17,18 With no Food and Drug Administration–approved treatments currently available, maximizing GT completion rates is essential to ensure equitable access to future therapies. Identifying modifiable barriers could inform targeted interventions to improve testing rates.

This study aims to identify demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors associated with GT completion in a large US cohort of patients with STGD. Additionally, we characterize the subset of genetically confirmed patients who enrolled in CTs.

Materials and Methods

Data collection and patient selection

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients seen at our tertiary referral genetic eye disease center between January 2003 and December 2024. Patients were included if they had a clinical diagnosis of STGD confirmed by a fellowship-trained IRD specialist. Individuals with nonspecific diagnoses of retinal, macular, or cone-rod dystrophy without documented suspicion of STGD were excluded.

Demographic variables were extracted from the electronic medical record and included sex, race/ethnicity, insurance type, employment status, education level, English proficiency, and parenting status. Race and ethnicity were categorized using the National Institutes of Health guidelines as American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, White, or Other.19 Insurance status was collected as private, public, international self-pay, or uninsured; for regression analyses, it was dichotomized into private vs under/uninsured (including public, international self-pay, and uninsured), reflecting differing access and coverage in the US health care system. Parenting status was categorized as having or planning to have children vs no children and no plans for children. The latter category required explicit documentation that an adult participant without children did not plan to have children or included postmenopausal women without children. Pediatric participants (age <18 years) and young adults without children who had not explicitly stated that they did not plan to have children were categorized as “having or may plan to have children,” as reproductive plans could not yet be determined.

Clinical variables and GT

Clinical variables included age of symptom onset, age at presentation, duration of follow-up, family history of STGD or other IRDs, history of consanguinity, and baseline and follow-up best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA). BCVA was recorded in Snellen and subsequently converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) for analysis.20 The better-seeing eye was defined as the eye with the lower logMAR, and the worse-seeing eye was defined as the eye with the higher logMAR. If both eyes had identical BCVA, the same value was assigned to both categories. Participants who had undergone GT before the baseline evaluation at our institution were excluded from all analyses that involved baseline BCVA measurements.

GT status was recorded as binary (yes/no). For untested participants, reasons for noncompletion were categorized as follows: awaiting GC, awaiting IRD retinal specialist evaluation, lost to follow-up, participant declined testing, testing not covered by insurance, or other.

ABCA4 GT results and CT participation

Among participants who completed GT, ABCA4-specific GT results (ABCA4-GT) were classified as positive, likely positive, or negative/inconclusive. Positive ABCA4-GT was defined as having 2 pathogenic ABCA4 variants, either confirmed in trans or of unknown phase. Likely positive ABCA4-GT included cases with 1 pathogenic variant plus 1 variant of uncertain significance; 2 variants of uncertain significance/novel variants in trans; or 1 pathogenic variant (or 2 in cis) with a typical STGD phenotype, per the published ProgStar criteria.21 Classification was based on the manual review of clinical laboratory reports by a trained grader.

We additionally assessed CT participation among participants with positive or likely positive ABCA4-GT results. Comprehensive chart review identified CT participation through clinical notes, imaging/procedure reports, and the media section using keyword searches (eg, “clinical trial,” “NCT,” “study/screening visit,” and “study/screening protocol”) and review of informed consent documents. CT participation was recorded as a binary variable, and trial-specific enrollment was extracted. Identified trials included both internal (conducted at our center) and external studies: TEASE (NCT04239625; external), ProgStar/SMART (NCT01977846; internal), SeaSTAR (NCT03772665; internal), POLARIS (NCT06435000; internal), oral metformin (NCT04545736; external), Zimura (NCT03364153; internal), docosahexaenoic acid supplementation (NCT00060749; external), and Stargazer (NCT04489511; external).

Statistical analysis

De-identified data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel (version 16.84.24041420; Microsoft Corp) and Stata (version 18.0; Stata Corporation LLC). Missing data resulted from the absence of relevant documentation in medical records and led to variable denominators. For descriptive and bivariate analyses, participants were excluded only from the analyses of variables with missing data. Multivariable logistic regression used complete case analysis, excluding participants with missing data for any covariate included in the model.

Bivariate analyses compared participants with and without GT completion. The Shapiro-Wilk test assessed the normality of continuous variables; the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Fisher exact test and χ2 test were used for categorical comparisons, with Fisher exact test applied when expected counts were <5. Multivariable logistic regression models assessed predictors of GT completion, with variable selection via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression and reference groups selected based on the highest frequency for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. CT participation among ABCA4-positive participants was analyzed descriptively.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants with STGD

Among 280 STGD participants, 54% were female, with a median age of 47 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 30.5-63 years; range 4-88 years). Most participants were identified as White (62%), followed by Black or African American (26%), Asian (10%), American Indian or Alaska Native (2.5%), and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (<0.5%); 4% of participants reported their race as Other. Hispanic or Latino ethnicity was reported by 4% of participants.

Overall, 86% of participants had or planned to have children; 14% of participants reported neither having children nor planning to have children. Insurance status included 73% of participants with private insurance, 21% with public insurance, 5% with international self-pay, and 1% uninsured. Employment data (n = 247) showed that 52% of participants were employed full-time, 19% were students, 15% were retired, 6% were on disability, 6% were unemployed, and 2% were employed part-time.

Among the participants with education data (n = 213), 57% held a university degree, 15% had postgraduate education, 13% had a high school diploma or general educational development, 11% were children enrolled in age-appropriate schooling, 3% held trade school or associate degrees, and 1% had completed only primary school. For analysis, education was grouped as postsecondary (84%) vs primary or secondary (16%); children enrolled in age-appropriate schooling were excluded to minimize potential confounding from parental educational background and decision-making. English language was used by 96% of participants during the clinic visits.

Bivariate analysis showed significant differences in GT completion by race (23% of tested vs 47% of untested participants identified as Black; P = .002), insurance type (P = .047), education (P = .021), and parenting status (P = 5 × 10−4) (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics and GT history

Median age at symptom onset was 23 years (IQR: 10-40), with presentation to our center at 37 years (IQR: 20-52) and a follow-up of 4.5 years (IQR: 1.6-10.1). A family history of STGD or other IRDs was present in 41% of participants; 3% of participants reported consanguinity.

There were 38 participants who completed GT before baseline evaluation at our institution; these participants were excluded from subsequent analyses with baseline BCVA. Median baseline BCVA was 0.602 logMAR (Snellen equivalent: 20/80) in the better-seeing eye and 0.796 (20/125) in the worse-seeing eye, worsening to 0.903 (20/160) and 1.00 (20/200), respectively, at follow-up. Participants who completed GT had slightly better BCVA at both time points, although this difference was not statistically significant (all P > .06; see Table 2).

Multivariable analysis of factors associated with GT completion

In logistic regression (Figure 1; complete numerical values available in Supplemental Table 1), Black participants had significantly lower odds of GT completion than White participants (odds ratio [OR]: 0.395, 95% CI: 0.160-0.876; P = .044). Under/uninsured participants had lower odds than those participants with private insurance (OR: 0.468, 95% CI: 0.193-0.972; P = .043). Participants who did not have children or plans to have children had 65% lower odds of completing GT (OR: 0.348, 95% CI: 0.137-0.885; P = .027) relative to those who had children or endorsed plans to have children. Lack of prior GC was strongly associated with lower odds of GT completion (OR: 0.085, 95% CI: 0.033-0.221; P = 4.18 × 10−7).

Post hoc analysis: Independence of insurance and GC as barriers to GT completion

To determine whether insurance and GC represented independent or sequential barriers, we conducted post hoc analyses. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression independently selected both insurance status and GC completion as predictors of GT completion. GC completion rates did not differ significantly by insurance status (69.3% private insurance vs 61.3% under/uninsured, P = .303). No significant interaction was observed between insurance and GC completion in predicting GT completion (P = .296). Additionally, the insurance effect was minimally attenuated when GC was added to the model (OR: 0.427 without GC vs 0.468 with GC). These findings indicate that insurance status and GC completion act as independent barriers to GT completion.

Reasons for not completing GT among untested participants

Among 34 untested participants, reasons for noncompletion included loss to follow-up (41%), refusal (26%), awaiting genetic counseling (24%), awaiting retinal specialist evaluation (3%), and lack of insurance coverage for testing (3%) (Figure 2). One participant (3%), categorized as “Other,” cited difficulty performing home-based testing and expressed that GT was not a priority because of personal circumstances and the absence of children who might inherit the condition.

Characterization of ABCA4-positive participants who enrolled in CTs

Among 223 participants with positive or likely positive ABCA4-GT results, 45 (20%) participants enrolled in observational or interventional CTs. This subset demonstrated demographic characteristics similar to those of the overall ABCA4-positive cohort: 51% (n = 23) were female, 62% (n = 28) were White, 20% (n = 9) were Black or African American, 11% (n = 5) were Asian, with the remainder identifying as Other race or Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander. The median age at GT was 37 years (IQR: 21-56), with symptoms being present for a median of 6 years (IQR: 3-15) before testing. Most participants (n = 37, 82%) had private insurance, and 91% (n = 41) had seen a genetic counselor. Most participants (n = 27, 60%) held university or postgraduate degrees, and 89% (n = 40) had children or planned to have children. Baseline BCVA was 0.544 logMAR (IQR: 0.176-0.798) in the better-seeing eye and 0.699 logMAR (IQR: 0.301-1.0) in the worse-seeing eye. The enrolled trials included TEASE,22 ProgStar,23 SMART,24 SeaSTAR,25 Polaris,26 oral metformin,27 Zimura,28 docosahexaenoic acid supplementation,29 and Stargazer.30

Discussion

Our finding that 88% of patients with STGD completed GT at a specialized US academic center exceeds completion rates reported in other IRD populations, including 45% at a Brazilian academic genetic ophthalmology unit and 9.5% at an Australian private tertiary practice.16,31 This difference may reflect differences in health care delivery models, integrated GC services, temporal improvements in testing accessibility, and, notably, the availability of no-cost GT programs in the United States, such as FFB-MRT-GTS and eyeGENE.9,10 However, even with our high overall completion rate, significant disparities emerged: GT completion was lower among Black participants, under/uninsured individuals, individuals with lower educational attainment, individuals without plans for children, and individuals without prior GC. These findings represent a comprehensive analysis of socioeconomic predictors of GT completion specifically in STGD, revealing previously underrecognized barriers to genetic diagnosis in this population.

Despite the availability of no-cost GT programs throughout most of our study period, under/uninsured participants had lower GT completion rates (OR: 0.47). Zhao et al9 found that FFB-MRT-GTS eligibility increased GT odds 14-fold among patients with IRD and also identified persistent disparities among Black patients (OR: 0.10) and non-English speakers (OR: 0.13). Although they reported no association between insurance status and GT uptake, our findings suggest that insurance remains relevant for GT completion. To further support this, oncology studies have consistently demonstrated that insurance status is a key determinant of GT access, with uninsured individuals being significantly less likely to undergo testing.32,33 These contrasting findings may reflect disease-specific differences or the impact of IRD-specific no-cost programs; they also highlight persistent structural barriers that require targeted interventions beyond cost reduction alone. Notably, our post hoc analyses demonstrated that insurance status and GC completion function as independent barriers rather than sequential obstacles, as evidenced by the lack of a significant difference in GC completion rates by insurance status, the absence of interaction effects, and minimal attenuation of the insurance effect when adjusting for GC. This has important clinical implications: interventions targeting only 1 barrier (eg, providing no-cost testing) may be insufficient to achieve equitable GT access, and comprehensive approaches addressing both financial and educational barriers are needed.

Racial disparities also emerged as a significant barrier. The significantly lower GT completion among Black participants (OR: 0.40) aligns with findings from Zhao et al9 (OR: 0.10) and known disparities in GT uptake across specialties.9,34 Contributing factors may include reduced access to GC and GT, insurance coverage, provider referral patterns, and perceptions of GT shaped by historical and cultural experiences.35 Compounding this disparity in GT completion, Black patients who complete testing are less likely to receive definitive molecular diagnoses because of underrepresentation in variant databases and gene discovery efforts.36 This dual burden of reduced GT completion and lower diagnostic yield underscores the need for diversifying genomic databases and implementing culturally sensitive counseling approaches.35,37,38

GC emerged as the strongest modifiable predictor of GT completion, with counseled participants having over 10-fold higher odds of completing testing. Although our retrospective design precludes causal inference, the observed association may reflect multiple pathways: GC may directly facilitate GT completion through education and decisional support, or patients with a preexisting interest in GT may be more likely to attend GC appointments, whereas those who are not interested in testing may decline counseling referrals altogether. Nevertheless, GC remains a valuable intervention point for patient education and support.

Despite this clear benefit, access to GC remains limited in many IRD care settings because of workforce shortages and high physician-to- clinical genetic counselor ratios in ophthalmic genetics.39 Many providers underuse GC referrals because of barriers, such as time constraints, incomplete knowledge of IRD genetics, and perceived limited relevance for clinical management.6 However, GT provides genotype-specific insights that inform eligibility for emerging therapies, family planning, and disease prognosis. Given that diagnostic delays for patients with IRD can exceed a decade, integrating GC early in the diagnostic journey can help patients make timely, informed, and supported decisions about GT and CT participation.40 Telegenetic counseling offers a promising solution to workforce limitations and geographic barriers, potentially improving access for underserved populations.12

Our study period (2003-2024) spans significant evolution in GT accessibility, including the introduction of no-cost testing programs and increased integration of GC services into IRD care. These temporal changes raise the possibility that the observed association between GC and GT completion may reflect more recent practice patterns rather than GC acting entirely as an independent predictor. However, the era of testing could not be incorporated into regression models predicting GT completion, as this variable is inherently undefined for untested participants. To indirectly assess temporal influences, we examined GT and GC completion rates by era of presentation to our center. Both GT completion rates (87.7% pre-2012, 88.3% 2012-2018, and 87.4% post-2018) and GC completion rates (67.7%, 63.3%, and 72.6%, respectively) remained stable across presentation eras, suggesting consistent institutional practices over time. Although the era of presentation does not directly approximate the era of testing because many patients presenting in earlier years underwent testing in later eras, the stability of these rates suggests that temporal changes in GT and GC availability are unlikely to fully explain the strong association between GC and GT completion.

Educational attainment and reproductive considerations also influenced GT completion. Participants without reproductive plans were less likely to pursue GT (OR: 0.35), consistent with literature suggesting that reproductive considerations are a major motivator for testing, with parents often being more concerned about genetic risks to their children than to themselves.41 In contrast, those not planning to have children may perceive less relevance in testing. One untested participant in our cohort exemplified this view, stating that they were uninterested in GT because they had no children to whom they could pass on their eye condition. Similarly, bivariate analysis revealed that participants with postsecondary education (eg, college, trade school, or graduate-level training) were significantly more likely to complete GT than those with only primary or secondary schooling. This aligns with prior research linking higher educational attainment to increased uptake of genetic services, likely because of factors such as enhanced health literacy and greater confidence in navigating health care systems.34,42,43

Unlike previous studies showing associations between clinical severity and testing initiation, we found no relationship between visual acuity, age, or follow-up duration and GT completion.9,16 Our findings suggest that once patients are engaged in specialized IRD care, socioeconomic factors may supersede clinical characteristics in determining testing completion.

Among those with confirmed ABCA4 variants, trial participants shared similar socioeconomic characteristics with GT completers, with higher rates of private insurance and higher educational attainment, and, notably, 91% of participants had received GC. This pattern suggests that GC may play a dual role in facilitating both testing completion and subsequent research engagement. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because enrollment opportunities varied based on trial availability, eligibility criteria, and the timing of patient visits.

This study has several additional limitations. Because this study is a single-center retrospective study at a US academic center, our findings may not generalize to other settings. Selection bias may exist because our cohort included patients referred to a specialized IRD center, potentially overrepresenting those with better health care access. Although GT and GC completion rates remained stable across presentation eras, the study period spanned significant changes in GT technology, availability, and cost that may have influenced GT completion patterns in ways not fully captured by the era of presentation. There was considerable heterogeneity in participant follow-up, ranging from a single visit to longitudinal care spanning 21 years. Pediatric participants (16% of those tested) were categorized as “may plan to have children” because reproductive intentions could not yet be determined; however, for these participants, the decision to pursue GT was made by parents/guardians rather than the participants themselves, which may confound the observed association between parenting status and GT completion. CT participation may be underreported if patients enrolled in trials elsewhere, and our retrospective approach precluded the determination of true eligibility or reasons for non-enrollment. Finally, the retrospective design of this study limited our ability to fully capture participant perspectives, including motivations for or against pursuing GT, and precludes the determination of whether GC preceded and influenced the decision to pursue GT or whether preexisting interest in GT drove both GC attendance and GT completion.

Our findings reveal that socioeconomic factors, rather than clinical variables, are the primary determinants of GT completion in patients with STGD. Although our 88% completion rate is encouraging, persistent disparities among Black patients, the under/uninsured, and those without access to GC highlight critical gaps in equitable care that may extend to CT enrollment. Importantly, GC emerged as the strongest modifiable predictor of GT completion, suggesting that integrating GC more consistently into the IRD care pathway could help mitigate disparities in GT access and support more equitable CT enrollment. Because gene-based therapies for STGD advance toward clinical application, addressing these barriers through expanded GC access, sustained no-cost testing programs, culturally tailored counseling, and diversified genomic databases will be essential to ensuring that all patients, regardless of race or socioeconomic status, benefit from these emerging treatments.
