Solitary Rectal Ulcer Syndrome: Clinical, Endoscopic, and Histopathological Findings in 40 Bangladeshi Children
Authors: Khan Lamia Nahid, Sharmin Akter, Rubaiyat Alam, Fahmida Begum, Md Rukunuzzaman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSolitary rectal ulcer syndrome (SRUS) is a benign disease of unknown etiology. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical, endoscopic, and histopathological findings of SRUS in Bangladeshi children.MethodsThis prospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Pediatric Gastroenterology and Nutrition of Bangabandhu Sheikh Mujib Medical University, Dhaka, Bangladesh, between January 2021 and December 2022. A total of 260 children aged <18 years with rectal bleeding were admitted for a full evaluation, including detailed history taking and colonoscopy. Among them, 40 met the inclusion criteria for SRUS. The collected data contained clinical characteristics and macroscopic and microscopic findings, expressed as numbers and percentages.ResultsThe mean age of the group was 13.07±2.14 years; male patients comprised 57.5% and female patients 42.5%. Among the 40 children who presented with rectal bleeding, the most common symptoms were straining during defecation (85.0%), followed by constipation (82.5%), sensation of incomplete evacuation (72.5%), mucus passage per rectum (70.0%), tenesmus (57.5%), abdominal pain (45.0%), manual digital evacuation (42.5%), perianal discomfort (37.5%), and diarrhea (22.5%). Colonoscopy revealed that most lesions were located in the rectum (97.6%) and ulcerative (65.9%). The mean distance of the lesions from the anal verge; 6.04±0.91 cm. Histologically, most lesions showed fibromuscular obliteration (95.0%) and crypt distortion (85.0%).ConclusionAmong the 40 children with SRUS who presented with rectal bleeding, straining during defecation was the most common symptom. Single ulcerative lesions in the rectum were the most frequent colonoscopic findings, and the majority showed fibromuscular obliteration and crypt distortion.Keywords: Solitary rectal ulcer syndrome, Child, Rectal bleeding, Histopathology, Fibromuscular obliteration

INTRODUCTION

Solitary rectal ulcer syndrome (SRUS) is a mostly benign condition and affects young adults. It is less commonly encountered in children because of frequent misdiagnoses in this age group. Rectal bleeding is the most common clinical manifestation. Other common presentations include mucorrhea, excessive straining during defecation, constipation, feeling of incomplete evacuation, prolonged stay on the toilet, and, in a few cases, prolapse of the rectum.

Diagnosis of SRUS is based upon clinical manifestations, macroscopic features on colonoscopy, and typical histopathological findings [1]. Rectal bleeding may be misinterpreted as originating from an anal fissure caused by constipation or other causes of rectal bleeding, such as a juvenile polyp or a more aggressive disease, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [2]. The endoscopic spectrum of SRUS may vary from simple hyperemic mucosa to small or giant ulcers to broad-based polypoid lesions of different sizes and numbers [3]. SRUS is a misnomer because the lesions are neither always solitary nor ulcerative. Furthermore, they can affect regions other than the rectum. Rectal biopsy is the most accurate diagnostic method for SRUS [4]. The major histological features of SRUS include fibromuscular obliteration of the lamina propria, hypertrophied muscularis mucosa, extension of muscle fibers upward between the crypts, diffuse collagen deposition in the lamina propria, abnormal smooth muscle fibers, and mild glandular crypt abnormalities [5].

This condition was first described by Cruveilhier in 1830, and its detailed clinical and histopathological features were reported by Madigan and Morson in 1969 [6]. It occurs most commonly in the 3rd decade of life in men and in the 4th in women, with only a few reported pediatric case series. The annual incidence rate of SRUS is 0.001% in adults [7]. The etiopathogenesis of SRUS remains poorly understood. However, ischemic injury to the rectum has been proposed as the main mechanism for the development of this condition [8]. Chronic mucosal hypoperfusion causes ischemic injury. Ischemic changes in the rectum are caused by paradoxical contractions of the pelvic floor and external anal sphincter muscles [9]. This dyssynergic defecation leads to mucosal prolapse [10,11]. Local trauma caused by repetitive self-digitation is another cause of SRUS. Anorectal manometry (ARM) can assess the functional status of the anorectum, including resting tone, squeeze pressure of the anal sphincter, rectal sensation, and rectoanal inhibitory reflex, and can be used to diagnose pelvic floor dyssynergia. Patients can be provided with biofeedback training based on ARM [11]. The objective of this study was to determine the clinical presentation, colonoscopic findings, and histological diagnosis of children with SRUS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective study was conducted at the Department of Pediatric Gastroenterology of Bangabandhu Sheikh Mujib Medical University in Dhaka, Bangladesh, between January 2021 and December 2022. Children with rectal bleeding who visited the outpatient department were enrolled. The study population comprised 260 children with rectal bleeding. Of these, 40 were included as a sample. Inclusion criteria for SRUS were clinical features suggestive of SRUS, macroscopic findings suggestive of SRUS, and histological findings. Exclusion criteria were children with rectal bleeding that did not match the histological features of SRUS. A total of 260 children aged <18 years with rectal bleeding were admitted for a full evaluation, including detailed history-taking and colonoscopic procedures. Colonoscopy was performed in all 260 children. A detailed history included a history of straining during defecation, prolonged sitting on the toilet, constipation, diarrhea, any digital evacuation, sexual abuse, tenesmus, passage of mucus, abdominal pain, and psychological conflict or familial disharmony. When macroscopic findings were present on colonoscopy, an endoscopic biopsy was performed for histopathological examination. Hematoxylin and eosin stained slides were reviewed by a histopathologist to confirm the diagnoses. Among them, 40 met the inclusion criteria for SRUS. Patients with other causes of rectal bleeding (IBD, rectal polyps, etc.) were also excluded. After diagnosis, we counseled the patients and guardians about the nature of the disease. Here, we describe the benign but chronic nature of this disease. All children were counseled about behavioral modifications (avoiding prolonged sitting on the toilet, straining during defecation, and abstaining from digital evacuation). If a psychological issue was present, the patient was referred for psychological assessment. Bulk laxatives, namely Psyllium husk, were administered to prevent constipation in all children. Sucralfate enemas were initiated twice daily and continued for one month. Other enemas, such as steroids and sulfasalazine enemas, were not administered because of unavailability in our country. ARM was not performed. Biofeedback training was possible for children aged >6 years. Therefore, we were unable to provide biofeedback training to the children in this study. The patients were checked for clinical improvement during further follow-up. The checklists for clinical improvement included cessation of rectal bleeding and establishment of a normal defecation pattern. No follow-up colonoscopy was performed. On further follow-up, we advised the continuation of behavioral modifications. Informed consent was obtained from guardians before all procedures.

Statistical analyses

Data related to the clinical features and endoscopic findings were documented using a structured questionnaire. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0 (IBM Co.). Discrete variables are presented as numbers and percentages. The research protocol was approved by the Departmental Review Board of Pediatric Gastroenterology and Nutrition of BSMMU (no. BSMMU/paed.gastro./2021/06) and informed consent was obtained.

RESULTS

A total of 260 children with rectal bleeding, aged <18 years, who visited the outpatient department between January 2021 and December 2022 were enrolled. Of the 260 children with bleeding in the rectum, 40 (15.3%) were diagnosed with solitary rectal ulcers. Other causes of rectal bleeding in the current study were colorectal polyps in 126 patients (48.4%), anal fissures in 39 (15%), infectious colitis (dysentery) in 23 (8.8%), IBD in 17 (6.5%), no cause identified in six (2.3%), intestinal tuberculosis in five (1.9%), Meckel’s diverticulum in three (1.2%), and rectal varices in one (0.3%). Among these, IBD has macroscopic features similar to those of SRUS. However, IBD exhibits distinct histological features. IBD is a more aggressive disease that requires long-term treatment. Forty children with colonoscopic features of SRUS confirmed on histopathology were assessed. The mean age of the group was 13.07±2.14 years; male patients comprised 57.1% and female patients 42.9% (Fig. 1).

Clinical characteristics

Among 40 children with SRUS who presented with rectal bleeding, straining during defecation was the most common symptom (85.0% of patients) followed by constipation (82.5%), mucus passage per rectum (70.0%), tenesmus (57.5%), abdominal pain (45.0%), diarrhea (22.5%), and perianal discomfort (37.5%). Manual digital evacuation was reported in 34 (42.5%) patients, a sense of incomplete evacuation in 72.5%, and rectal prolapse in 2.5%. A history of blood transfusions was observed in 7.7% of patients, and a history of weight loss in 5% (Table 1).

Colonoscopic and histopathological features

Colonoscopy revealed ulcers in 40 patients (65.9%). Solitary and multiple lesions were observed in 83.3% and 16.7% of patients, respectively. Simple erythematous lesions were present in 19.5% of the patients. Polypoidal lesions were present in 9.8% of the children. Nodular lesions and polyps were observed in 9.8% and 2.4% of the patients, respectively. The mean distance of the lesions from the anal verge was 6.04±0.91 cm. Most of the lesions were in the rectum (97.6%), and others were in the sigmoid region (2.4%). Fibromuscular obliteration was observed in 95% of the specimens, whereas crypt distortion was observed in 85%. Other findings were ulcerated surfaces in 60% of the specimens, hyperplastic crypts in 32.5%, and distorted glands in 17.5% (Tables 2 and 3).

DISCUSSION

SRUS is a benign disease of unknown etiology. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain this phenomenon. Although benign, overwhelming symptoms may hamper activities of daily living and cause serious morbidity throughout patients’ lifetimes. It is believed to be a disease of adult origin. However, many pediatric patients experience this condition. Pediatricians sometimes misdiagnose this condition as a more common disease, such as IBD, tuberculosis, or proctocolitis of infectious origin. Understanding the pathogenesis of SRUS can provide an accurate diagnosis. Mucosal hypoperfusion was an early concept. Another accepted theory is the abnormal contraction of the puborectalis muscle during attempted defecation [12]. This initiates internal intussusception or mucosal prolapse, which causes venous congestion, hypoperfusion, and rectal mucosal edema. Moreover, manual evacuation of impacted stool or manual reduction of rectal prolapse can lead to mucosal trauma and ulceration [13]. Dyssynergic defecation is another theory of the occurrence of this disease. The normal defecation process depends on anatomical factors and the coordinated interaction between the pelvic floor muscles and the autonomic nervous system. Failure to achieve this condition involves inadequate propulsive force and paradoxical anal contractions. Our study showed that straining during defecation and manual evacuation were important symptoms in our patients.

In our study, the mean age was 13.07±2.14 years; male patients comprised 57.1% and female patients 42.9%. Most studies have shown that females are slightly more affected than males. However, two studies reported a slight male preponderance [14,15]. One study showed that 81% of patients were male, with a male-to-female ratio of 4.3:1 [13]. The reason for the male predominance in our study might be due to more hospital visits by male patients than female patients. This may be due to gender discrimination in our country.

SRUS more frequently affects young adults than younger children and older people [16]. The diagnosis of SRUS is based on clinical, endoscopic, and histological examinations. The most common presenting complaints are rectal bleeding, straining during defecation, and constipation. The clinical and endoscopic findings of SRUS are not specific in most clinical settings and may be misdiagnosed as IBD, infectious colitis, or tuberculosis. Therefore, histological findings of SRUS can confirm the diagnosis. Endoscopic findings vary from simple mucosal erythematous lesions to single or multiple ulcerations. However, nonulcerated polypoid mass lesions that mimic rectal tumors may also be present [17]. The size of the ulcer may range from 0.5 cm to 4 cm. The anterior rectal wall represents the most common site of ulceration, but the sigmoid colon and anal canal may also be affected. According to the literature, ulcers are usually located within 10 cm of the anal verge [8]. The colonoscopy results in the present study showed that ulcerative lesions were present in 66% of patients, and were solitary in nature in 83.3% of the cases (Fig. 2). This indicates that SRUS is a misnomer since not all of the lesions were ulcerated or solitary, which aligns with previous studies [18]. This study revealed polypoidal lesions in 9.8% of children. As shown in Fig. 3, a 15-year-old boy presented with a polypoid lesion that mimicked a rectal tumor. However, this was ruled out by revealing its indolent clinical features and histopathological results. Hence, the variability of endoscopic findings render diagnosis more difficult.

In our study, rectal bleeding was a common finding consistent with observations in adults [19]. Straining during defecation was also a prominent finding in our study (85.0%). Poddar et al. [20] showed that excessive straining during defecation was present in almost all pediatric cases. Dehghani et al. [21] reported similar clinical features in 55 children, showing rectal bleeding in 98% of cases, excessive straining in 91%, and a feeling of incomplete evacuation in 62%. Constipation was present in 81% of cases in our study. We identified abnormal defecation behaviors in the children through proper counseling. We checked for manual evacuation, prolonged sitting on the toilet, and any history of sexual abuse. The children were shy to disclose abnormal defecation behaviors. Although SRUS rarely coexists with ulcerative colitis, three cases were reported in adults [22].

In our study, ulcerated lesions were found in 65.9% of cases, mostly in the rectum. Only 2.4% of the tumors were located in the sigmoid colon. Poddar et al. [20] showed that ulcers were detected in the rectum on colonoscopy in 72% of cases. Kowalsk-Duplaga et al. [23] reported similar results in 31 children with ulcers in 97% of the patients, and 61% of these ulcers were solitary. Ejaz et al. [19] reported that ulcerated lesions were found in 72.6% of their patients. Therefore, most studies have indicated that SRUS is a misnomer, as none of the lesions were ulcerated. Histological examination in the present study revealed fibromuscular obliteration in 95% of patients, surface ulceration in 60%, and crypt distortion in 85%. Behera et al. [24] revealed that fibromuscular obliteration was found in 100% of the cases, which was the most consistent histological finding. ARM was not performed in our patients because of the unavailability of this test in our country. In these 40 children with SRUS, crypt distortion was found on histopathology in 85% of the cases. Crypt distortion is one of the characteristics of IBD. However, the history, physical examination, and laboratory parameters of these children did not match those of IBD. In the case of IBD, fever, abdominal pain, anorexia, malaise, and weight loss would be present. Moreover, the laboratory parameters of these children were not consistent with those of patients with IBD (anemia, thrombocytosis, increased C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, high erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and reduced albumin levels). The laboratory parameters evaluated in the present study were hemoglobin level, total white blood cell count, platelet count, ESR, CRP, and Serum albumin levels. These laboratory findings helped to differentiate IBD from SRUS.

After confirming the diagnosis, the children were advised to consume a high-fiber diet, plenty of water, and bulk laxatives. Counseling regarding toilet habits was performed. Biofeedback is an important mechanism that provides a variety of behavioral changes, to reduce excessive straining during defecation. This maneuver can correct pelvic floor disorders. Several studies have suggested that biofeedback is an important treatment option for patients with SRUS and dyssynergic defecation [11]. Topical therapy has been reported to be effective for short periods in different case series. Many studies have recommended the use of sucralfate, sulfasalazine and corticosteroid enema [25]. Sucralfate enemas were administered to all patients for four weeks and showed improvement for a short time. However, long-term improvement was not observed, as patients were not followed up beyond one month. We quantified the normal pattern of defecation by taking a proper history. The history checklist included reduced time spent on the toilet, absence of straining during defecation and tenesmus, and discontinuation of rectal bleeding. Kowalsk-Duplaga et al. [23] used steroids as topical therapy in 19 of 31 children and reported improvement in 14 (63.2%) patients. Conservative management can dramatically improve symptoms and halt disease progression. This statement is appropriate for mild to moderate disease conditions. In more advanced cases, such as external prolapse and rectal intussusception, conservative treatment is no longer effective. Surgical treatment is recommended for rectal mucosal prolapse and when conservative treatment fails [26]. Surgical treatment options for children include rectopexy, local ulcer excision, and colostomy. Surgery is needed for patients whose rectal bleeding does not improve over a prolonged period. Therefore, treatment of SRUS should be based on the pathophysiology, severity of symptoms, and the presence of rectal prolapse [27]. We diagnosed SRUS for the first time and followed it up for only one month. Therefore, none of the patients in this study underwent surgery.

SRUS is a chronic benign disorder with inconvenient symptoms. This syndrome has a clinicopathological similarity with IBD. Therefore, a proper histological diagnosis of SRUS is important. IBD has a more aggressive clinical course, and morbidity and mortality rates are higher in patients with IBD. Among the 40 children with SRUS who presented with rectal bleeding, straining during defecation was the most common symptom. A single ulcer was the most frequent colonoscopic finding, followed by erythematous lesions, multiple ulcers and polypoid lesions. Notably, patient education, behavioral modification, and dietary manipulation are the first approaches to overcome these symptoms. Surgical treatment is recommended for patients with rectal prolapse who do not respond to conservative management.
