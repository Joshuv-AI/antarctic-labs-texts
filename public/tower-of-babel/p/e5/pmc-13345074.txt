Artificial Intelligence in Chest Computed Tomography Optimization: A Task-based Framework for Balancing Detectability and Radiation Dose
Authors: Suhas Tivaskar, Anurag Luharia, Gaurav V. Mishra, Aditya Shrivastav, Ravi Christian, Sarthak Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Computed tomography (CT) of the chest is essential for thoracic imaging because of its high spatial resolution and broad clinical applications. However, variability in acquisition parameters and reconstruction techniques contributes to inconsistent radiation dose and image quality, while conventional optimization approaches remain limited by complex parameter interactions and operator dependence. Optimization in chest CT is increasingly recognized as a task-based challenge in which diagnostic performance depends on lesion detectability rather than global image quality metrics alone. This review synthesizes artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted approaches for optimizing chest CT protocols within a task-based framework, with emphasis on lesion detectability, diagnostic efficacy, and radiation dose reduction, while examining how AI can address protocol variability and improve patient safety. A structured narrative review of PubMed-indexed literature published between January 2015 and January 2025 was conducted, focusing on studies evaluating AI-assisted acquisition, deep learning reconstruction, and radiation dose optimization in adult chest CT. The review highlights that the most effective outcomes are achieved when AI-based reconstruction is integrated with dose optimization strategies such as automatic exposure control and patient-adapted acquisition, rather than applied in isolation. Although the magnitude of benefit varies according to diagnostic task, study design, and evaluation metrics, AI consistently reduces operator variability and promotes protocol standardization. Overall, AI enables task-based optimization of chest CT by prioritizing lesion detectability and diagnostic performance over traditional image quality metrics alone. By integrating acquisition, reconstruction, and continuous feedback mechanisms, AI has the potential to support consistent, patient-specific optimization while maintaining diagnostic efficacy when appropriately validated.

INTRODUCTION

Computed tomography (CT) imaging of the thorax is a cornerstone of modern thoracic imaging, combining rapid acquisition, high spatial resolution, and robust multiplanar reconstructions to support diagnosis, staging, and follow-up across infections, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary nodules, trauma, and malignancy.[1] As CT utilization and clinical reliance continue to grow, cumulative population exposure to ionizing radiation also increases, making protocol optimization a central quality-and-safety priority for radiology departments.[2] Optimization in chest CT is inherently complex because image quality requirements differ by clinical task (e.g. nodule detection, pulmonary embolism, or diffuse lung disease), while radiation dose is influenced by patient habitus, scanner hardware, acquisition parameters, and reconstruction techniques.[1] From a medical physics perspective, optimization is defined as task-based performance maximization under radiation dose constraints, where diagnostic accuracy depends on lesion detectability rather than global image quality metrics alone.

A persistent challenge is that “one-size-fits-all” protocols generate substantial dose variability between institutions and even within the same department across scanners and operators, highlighting the need for standardized yet flexible approaches aligned with diagnostic reference levels (DRLs) and local performance targets.[2] Although DRLs commonly rely on CTDIvol and dose-length product (DLP), inconsistent reporting practices and heterogeneous dose metrics can limit benchmarking and delay protocol harmonization. However, DRLs remain population-based benchmarks and do not account for task-specific diagnostic performance, thereby limiting their utility for true task-based optimization. Studies establishing local and national DRLs for chest CT consistently demonstrate wide distributions of CTDIvol and DLP, revealing significant optimization opportunities when protocols are tailored to clinical indication and patient size rather than fixed technical settings.[23] Size-specific approaches using patient diameter or water-equivalent diameter further refine dose assessment beyond scanner-reported indices and support more uniform image quality across body habitus.[4]

Historically, dose reduction in chest CT has relied on physics-driven strategies such as automatic exposure control (AEC), tube current modulation, tube voltage selection, optimization of scan length, pitch, collimation, and accurate patient centering.[5] Z-axis tube current modulation, for example, dynamically adapts the output to patient attenuation and reduces unnecessary exposure compared with fixed-mAs techniques. Automatic tube voltage selection, particularly when combined with tube current modulation, can reduce dose while maintaining or improving contrast-to-noise ratios (CNRs), including in chest CT angiography. Collectively, these approaches support patient-adapted acquisition as a practical pathway to optimization.[567] Metrics such as signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and CNR do not fully reflect diagnostic task performance and may not correlate with lesion detectability in low-dose conditions.

Reconstruction technology has been another major driver of dose reduction. Iterative reconstruction (IR) enables lower-dose acquisitions by suppressing noise and artefacts compared with filtered back projection. Systematic reviews and task-based clinical studies demonstrate that IR can achieve meaningful dose reductions while preserving diagnostic confidence, confirming that reconstruction choice is integral to protocol design rather than a purely postprocessing decision.[89] However, optimizing multiple interacting parameters consistently at scale remains difficult using manual, experience-based adjustments alone.[1]

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly positioned to address this operational gap by enabling data-driven, patient-specific, and task-specific optimization across the CT workflow, including prescan planning, acquisition control, reconstruction, and quality assurance. AI-assisted optimization encompasses deep learning based reconstruction (deep learning reconstruction [DLR] and deep learning image reconstruction [DLIR]), automated protocol selection, parameter recommendation (kVp, mAs, and noise index), real-time dose oversight, and continuous learning from local outcomes.[10] Despite these advantages, AI-based approaches remain dependent on training data, scanner-specific implementation, and validation methodology, introducing variability in performance across scanners, populations, and clinical tasks, thereby challenging generalizability. Dose monitoring infrastructure is critical to this framework, as automated tracking allows the detection of protocol drift and provides feedback for iterative improvement.[11]

Among AI-enabled components, DLR has expanded the feasible dose-reduction envelope by learning noise and texture characteristics from high-quality data, producing diagnostically usable images at lower photon counts.[12] Multiple chest CT studies report that DLR substantially reduces noise and improves subjective image quality compared with conventional IR at low dose, sometimes approaching standard-dose appearance for selected tasks.[13] Combined acquisition-reconstruction innovations further demonstrate that AI can synergize with hardware strategies to achieve major exposure reductions while maintaining diagnostic accuracy.[1415]

Importantly, dose reduction must be evaluated alongside diagnostic efficacy, including lesion detectability, confidence, reproducibility, and suitability for the longitudinal follow-up.[16] While AI may improve consistency, concerns remain regarding generalizability, texture alteration, and stability of quantitative biomarkers. METHOD OF THE REVIEW

This review was conducted using a structured and reproducible search methodology in accordance with journal recommendations for review articles, with the objective of comprehensively identifying peer-reviewed evidence related to AI-assisted optimization of CT chest protocols, diagnostic efficacy, and radiation dose reduction.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using the PubMed database exclusively to ensure retrieval of high-quality biomedical literature indexed in MEDLINE and related life-science journals, thereby maintaining consistency and clinical relevance across included studies Although only PubMed was used, this approach was selected to prioritize peer-reviewed, clinically validated studies indexed in MEDLINE; however, this may introduce database selection bias and is acknowledged as a limitation. The publication period was restricted to January 2015 through January 2025, reflecting the era during which advanced IR, deep learning-based reconstruction, and AI driven CT workflow optimization became clinically and scientifically prominent. This time frame was selected to capture both the maturation of conventional dose-reduction strategies and the rapid emergence of AI-based methods in chest CT.

The search strategy combined database-specific subject headings with free-text terms, including medical subject headings related to CT chest imaging, radiation dose optimization, and AI. The key terms included the combinations of “chest CT,” “computed tomography,” “radiation dose reduction,” “CTDIvol,” “deep learning reconstruction,” “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “iterative reconstruction,” “image quality,” and “diagnostic performance.” Boolean operators were applied using the structure: (CT chest OR thoracic CT) AND (AI OR deep learning OR machine learning) AND (dose reduction OR image quality OR diagnostic accuracy) to maximize sensitivity while preserving specificity.

Inclusion criteria comprised English-language studies involving adult human subjects or validated CT phantoms that evaluated AI-assisted reconstruction, acquisition optimization, or protocol automation in chest CT, with explicit reporting of radiation dose metrics and or diagnostic or image quality outcomes. Exclusion criteria included pediatric-only studies, nonthoracic CT examinations, editorials without original data, conference abstracts lacking full methodology, and studies focused exclusively on AI-based image interpretation without implications for protocol design or radiation dose.

Study selection and screening

Study selection followed a structured three-stage screening process to ensure methodological rigor and relevance. In the first stage, titles were screened to remove duplicates and clearly irrelevant records. In the second stage, abstracts were independently reviewed to assess alignment with the review objectives, focusing on AI involvement in CT protocol optimization or reconstruction with reported dose or image quality outcomes. In the final stage, full-text articles were evaluated for methodological completeness, clarity of AI implementation, and adequacy of reported dose and diagnostic metrics. A total of 312 records were identified through database searching. After the removal of duplicates, 268 records remained for title screening, of which 172 were excluded. Ninety-six abstracts were assessed for eligibility, resulting in 58 full-text articles reviewed. Finally, 41 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. The study selection process followed a structured screening approach consistent with PRISMA-style methodology for narrative reviews to enhance transparency and reproducibility.

The review included a broad spectrum of study designs reflecting the translational nature of AI in CT imaging, including phantom-based validation studies, prospective and retrospective clinical trials, observational cohort studies, and technical performance evaluations. This inclusive approach allowed the comparison of controlled experimental findings with real-world clinical performance.

Data extraction and synthesis

From each eligible study, predefined parameters were systematically extracted to support structured comparison and synthesis. Radiation dose metrics included CTDIvol, DLP, effective dose estimates, and size-specific dose estimates (SSDE) where available. Image quality indices included objective measures such as SNR, CNR, noise power spectrum, and spatial resolution, as well as subjective radiologist scoring.

AI-related variables included model type (DLR, acquisition automation, or hybrid systems), training strategy, reconstruction strength, and integration with scanner hardware or workflow. Clinical tasks evaluated, such as lung nodule detection, interstitial lung disease assessment, infection, oncology staging, and screening, were also recorded.

Given heterogeneity in study design and reported endpoints, a qualitative narrative synthesis was adopted as the primary analytical approach, supported by quantitative comparison of reported dose-reduction ranges and image quality outcomes where feasible. Emphasis was placed on task-based interpretation rather than absolute dose minimization, consistent with contemporary diagnostic efficacy driven optimization principles in chest CT. To enhance reproducibility, predefined extraction criteria and consistent outcome measures were applied across all included studies, with emphasis on standardized dose metrics and task-based performance indicators.

CONVENTIONAL COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY CHEST PROTOCOL OPTIMIZATION STRATEGIES

Acquisition parameter optimization

Optimization of acquisition parameters remains the foundation of radiation dose management in chest CT and serves as the baseline against which AI-assisted strategies are evaluated.[19] Tube voltage (kVp) selection directly influences both radiation dose and image contrast. Lower kVP values provide improved iodine contrast and potential dose savings in appropriately selected patients, whereas higher kVp settings may be required in larger patients to avoid excessive noise.[7] Studies demonstrate that individualized kVp selection, particularly when integrated with automated systems, can substantially reduce dose in chest CT angiography and noncontrast thoracic imaging without compromising diagnostic confidence.[6]

Automatic tube current modulation (ATCM) dynamically adjusts tube current based on patient attenuation along the z-axis and angular projections, reducing unnecessary exposure compared with fixed-mAs techniques.[5] In chest CT, ATCM significantly alters dose distribution while maintaining image quality, particularly in the regions of lower attenuation such as the lung apices and bases. AEC systems further integrate attenuation data from scout images to generate patient-adapted tube current profiles, reducing operator-dependent variability and supporting more consistent image quality across patient sizes.[19]

Beyond kVp and mAs, geometric and temporal parameters including pitch, collimation, and scan length play critical roles in dose optimization. Excessive scan ranges and suboptimal pitch values can increase dose without diagnostic benefit, whereas tailored coverage and appropriate collimation reduce exposure.[20] Accurate patient centering enhances the performance of AEC and ATCM, underscoring that protocol optimization is a holistic process rather than a single-parameter adjustment.[17]

Dose metrics and reference standards

Radiation dose assessment in chest CT relies on standardized metrics that enable protocol comparison, quality assurance, and regulatory benchmarking. The most commonly reported indices are CTDIvol and DLP, which provide scanner-reported estimates of radiation output but do not account for patient size.[21] SSDE address this limitation by adjusting CTDIvol according to patient diameter or water-equivalent diameter, improving dose estimation accuracy across a wide range of body habitus.[421]

DRLs represent population-based benchmarks used to identify unusually high or low radiation doses for standard examinations.[2] National and regional DRL surveys consistently demonstrate wide dose distributions for chest CT, reflecting heterogeneity in protocol design, scanner technology, and institutional practice.[3] This variability highlights both optimization potential and the challenge of achieving uniform dose standards across institutions. Importantly, inter-institutional comparisons show that meeting DRLs alone does not guarantee consistent image quality or diagnostic performance, reinforcing the need for task-based optimization rather than dose-centric evaluation.[18]

Iterative reconstruction techniques

Reconstruction methodology is a critical determinant of the dose-image quality trade-off in chest CT. Hybrid IR techniques combine filtered back projection with iterative noise-reduction algorithms, enabling moderate dose reductions while preserving familiar image texture and reconstruction speed.[8] These approaches are widely adopted due to their balance of computational efficiency and improved noise performance.[9]

Model-based IR (MBIR) incorporates detailed system optics, noise statistics, and object models, enabling more aggressive dose reduction through enhanced noise suppression and artifact reduction.[9] Clinical and phantom studies demonstrate that MBIR can substantially reduce radiation dose while maintaining diagnostic accuracy, particularly for high-contrast tasks such as lung nodule detection. However, MBIR is computationally intensive and may alter image texture, potentially affecting reader acceptance and evaluation of subtle parenchymal abnormalities.[89]

Overall, IR techniques have significantly advanced conventional CT chest protocol optimization by shifting the dose-quality balance toward lower exposure. Nonetheless, limitations in handling complex parameter interactions and maintaining consistent texture across dose levels have motivated exploration of AI-based approaches as the next stage of protocol optimization.[10]

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE IN COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY CHEST PROTOCOL OPTIMIZATION

Overview of artificial intelligence techniques in computed tomography

AI in CT imaging encompasses computational techniques that support or automate decision-making across the imaging workflow, extending beyond image interpretation to protocol selection, acquisition control, and reconstruction.[10] Machine learning methods typically rely on handcrafted features and statistical models, whereas deep learning employs multilayer neural networks to learn hierarchical features directly from data.[22] In chest CT, deep learning has gained prominence due to its ability to model complex, nonlinear interactions among acquisition parameters, noise characteristics, and anatomical structures.[12]

AI models in CT optimization can be categorized into supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning paradigms.[17] Supervised learning is most common and relies on paired input output data, such as low-dose images mapped to reference-standard images or acquisition parameters mapped to target image quality metrics.[12] Unsupervised learning explores intrinsic data structure and has limited application in direct protocol optimization. Reinforcement learning, an emerging approach, frames protocol optimization as a sequential decision-making problem and offers potential for adaptive, self-learning CT systems.[1722]

Model performance depends critically on training datasets and ground truth definitions, which influence generalizability and clinical trustworthiness. Ground truth in chest CT optimization may include standard-dose images, expert radiologist scores, task-based detectability indices, or phantom-derived references, each with inherent assumptions.[16] Dataset diversity across patient habitus, scanners, reconstruction kernels, and clinical indications is essential to minimize bias and ensure robust real-world performance.[10]

Artificial intelligence-based acquisition optimization

Beyond reconstruction, AI increasingly influences the acquisition phase by reducing operator dependence and enabling patient and task-specific parameter selection.[17] Automated protocol selection systems use patient metadata, scout images, and clinical indications to recommend or apply appropriate chest CT protocols, minimizing inappropriate parameter choices and protocol drift.[23]

AI-driven kVp and mAs recommendation builds on conventional AEC by incorporating learned relationships between patient size, attenuation, and image quality targets, enabling more precise balancing of dose and noise than rule-based systems.[17] These approaches support more equitable image quality across heterogeneous populations and reduce unnecessary exposure, particularly in radiosensitive regions and patients requiring repeated imaging.[1820]

Several vendors have integrated AI components into CT scanners to optimize positioning, scan range, and acquisition parameters in real time.[17] Early evaluations suggest reductions in positioning errors and over scanning, although independent validation remains essential.[23]

Deep learning-based image reconstruction

Deep learning-based image reconstruction (DLR/DLIR) represents the most mature AI application in CT chest protocol optimization. DLR is trained to map noisy low-dose data to high-quality reference images, learning complex noise and texture characteristics not fully addressed by conventional IR.[12] By separating noise suppression from spatial smoothing, DLR preserves fine anatomical detail while enabling substantial dose reduction, particularly in low-dose and ultra-low-dose chest CT.[13]

Multiple studies demonstrate that DLR provides superior noise reduction and improved subjective image quality compared with IR at equivalent or lower dose levels, while maintaining lesion detectability and diagnostic confidence, especially for lung nodules.[22] However, altered image texture raises the concerns regarding reader adaptation and evaluation of texture-dependent diseases, highlighting the need for task-based validation. DIAGNOSTIC EFFICACY OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE-OPTIMIZED COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY CHEST PROTOCOLS

Image quality assessment

Assessment of diagnostic efficacy begins with rigorous evaluation of image quality using objective and subjective methods, as dose reduction is meaningful only if diagnostic integrity is preserved. Objective metrics such as SNR, CNR, and noise power spectrum quantify noise magnitude, contrast performance, and texture changes.[1318] However, these metrics do not directly reflect task-specific diagnostic performance, and their relationship with lesion detectability is often nonlinear, particularly in low-dose conditions. Task-based image quality assessment frameworks, including model observer analysis and detectability indices, provide a more clinically relevant evaluation by linking image characteristics to specific diagnostic tasks. While studies report that AI-based reconstruction improves SNR and CNR at reduced dose levels compared with IR, these improvements do not necessarily translate into proportional gains in task-specific detectability.[12]

Subjective radiologist scoring remains central to clinical validation, encompassing assessments of overall image quality, noise, sharpness, contrast, and diagnostic confidence. However, subjective assessments remain susceptible to inter-observer variability and may be influenced by altered image texture associated with AI-based reconstruction. AI-optimized chest CT images generally receive higher or comparable subjective scores relative to conventional low-dose protocols, particularly in the lung parenchyma and mediastinal evaluation. Accordingly, task-based image quality assessment aligned with specific diagnostic questions is essential for evaluating AI-optimized CT protocols.[162225]

Clinical performance

Clinical performance endpoints provide the most direct evidence of diagnostic efficacy. Pulmonary nodule detection is extensively studied, with AI-based reconstruction maintaining or improving detectability and measurement accuracy at low and ultra-low doses along the supporting applications such as lung cancer screening.[2325]

In interstitial lung disease, performance is more nuanced due to reliance on subtle texture patterns. While acceptable image quality is reported, concerns persist regarding excessive smoothing affecting pattern recognition.[16] For infection and oncology imaging, AI-optimized protocols preserve diagnostic confidence in identifying consolidation, nodal disease, and tumor extent at reduced dose levels.[22] Performance requirements differ between screening and diagnostic studies, reinforcing the need for indication-specific optimization.[18]

IMPACT ON QUANTITATIVE IMAGING

AI-optimized protocols also affect quantitative imaging, including radiomics and imaging biomarkers. Radiomics feature stability is sensitive to noise, reconstruction algorithms, and dose levels, and AI-based reconstruction can both improve and alter feature reproducibility depending on network design and reconstruction strength.[16] While reduced noise may enhance robustness, texture changes can introduce systematic shifts, particularly relevant for research and predictive modelling.[15]

RADIATION DOSE REDUCTION ACHIEVED WITH ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

Dose reduction magnitude

Across published studies, AI-assisted approaches, particularly deep learning based reconstruction, achieve dose reductions ranging from approximately 30% to more than 70% compared with standard-dose protocols reconstructed using filtered back projection or conventional IR.[12] However, these ranges reflect substantial heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, scanner technology, and evaluation endpoints, limiting direct comparability across studies. These reductions are reported across noncontrast chest CT, lung cancer screening, and follow-up examinations.[25]

AI has enabled low-dose and ultra-low-dose chest CT, with diagnostically acceptable images reported at dose levels near or below 1 mSv for selected tasks and controlled study conditions.[1325] Importantly, achievable dose reduction is strongly task-dependent, with high-contrast tasks permitting greater reductions than low-contrast or texture-dependent applications.

Comparison with conventional techniques

Compared with conventional techniques, AI-assisted approaches generally provide superior dose-reduction capability at comparable image quality.[18] ATCM and AEC achieve moderate reductions but are limited by predefined rules and sensitivity to positioning errors. IR improves dose efficiency but plateaus at very low doses, where texture degradation may occur.[518]

AI-based reconstruction may outperform IR at low doses in selected applications by providing greater noise suppression with improved texture characteristics, enabling more aggressive reductions in tube current or voltage.[22] The greatest benefits are achieved through combined strategies integrating AI reconstruction with optimized acquisition parameters.[17] However, performance gains may plateau at extremely low dose levels, and excessive noise suppression may risk loss of subtle or texture-dependent findings.

Safety and risk-benefit considerations

Despite dose-reduction benefits, clinical adoption requires careful risk benefit assessment. Excessive dose reduction may compromise the detection of low-contrast or texture-dependent abnormalities. Concerns include over-smoothing and missed subtle pathology, underscoring the need for rigorous validation, standardized settings, and radiologist training.[151618] Current evidence supports a favorable risk-benefit profile when AI implementation is guided by diagnostic intent and continuous quality assurance.[10]

VALIDATION APPROACHES AND QUALITY ASSURANCE

Phantom-based validation

Phantom studies provide controlled conditions for evaluating dose reduction, image quality, and algorithm stability independent of patient variability. Anthropomorphic chest and computed axial tomography phantoms allow reproducible comparison of reconstruction algorithms across the dose levels. Phantom-based validation supports protocol calibration, benchmarking, and detection of unintended changes following software or hardware updates.[29]

Clinical validation

Clinical validation confirms real-world diagnostic efficacy. Multi-reader, multi-case studies reduce bias and enable robust comparison of AI-assisted and conventional protocols.[25] Inter-observer agreement reflects interpretability and reliability, with several studies reporting comparable or improved agreement for AI-reconstructed images, although variability persists for texture-dependent diseases.[16]

Dose monitoring and audit systems

Continuous dose monitoring is essential for long-term quality assurance. Automated tracking of CTDIvol, DLP, and SSDE enables the detection of protocol drift and unintended dose escalation.[2] AI-assisted quality control systems further integrate analytics to identify outliers and correlate dose metrics with image quality and diagnostic performance, supporting proactive protocol governance.[10] The key sources of methodological bias and limitations in current AI-based CT chest dose-reduction studies are summarized in Table 3.

CHALLENGES, LIMITATIONS, AND ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Generalizability across scanners and vendors

Generalizability across scanners and vendors remains a major challenge, as AI models are often trained on vendor- and scanner-specific data.[12] Variations in detector design, system optics, and reconstruction pipelines may affect performance when applied across platforms, necessitating site-specific validation.[2430]

Dataset bias and transparency

Dataset bias poses methodological and ethical concerns, as training data may not represent diverse patient populations or disease patterns.[10] Transparency in dataset composition, ground truth definition, and training strategy is essential to assess the limitations and ensure clinical trust.[18]

Regulatory, medico-legal, and explainability issues

AI integration raises regulatory and medico-legal considerations related to safety, accountability, and explainability. Limited transparency of deep learning systems may reduce clinician trust, particularly when image texture differs from conventional reconstruction.[1017] Human oversight, task-based validation, and governance frameworks remain essential.[16] Key technical and clinical considerations influencing safe and effective implementation of AI-assisted CT chest protocol optimization in methodological limitations and potential sources of bias across AI-based chest CT dose-reduction studies are outlined in Table 3.

CLINICAL IMPLEMENTATION, OPERATIONAL IMPLICATIONS, AND QUALITY GOVERNANCE OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE-OPTIMIZED CHEST COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY

Implementation framework for radiology departments

Successful deployment of AI-optimized chest CT requires a structured, stepwise pathway encompassing pilot testing, local validation, controlled deployment, and continuous audit. Initial pilot studies should evaluate performance across representative patient populations and scanners, ensuring compatibility with existing acquisition protocols and diagnostic requirements. Integration with institutional infrastructure including CT scanners, picture archiving and communication system (PACS), and radiology information system is essential to enable seamless workflow and automated data exchange. Dose monitoring systems should be incorporated from the outset to track CTDIvol, DLP, and SSDEs, facilitating the early detection of protocol drift and unintended exposure changes.[211] Resource considerations include hardware capability, software licensing, data storage, and staff training, all of which influence cost-effectiveness and scalability. Implementation strategies must align with established radiation protection principles such as ALARA and national or regional DRLs, ensuring that dose reduction does not compromise diagnostic efficacy.[18] A governance framework with periodic review supports sustainable integration and regulatory compliance. Operational implications for radiographers and technologists

AI integration modifies the technologist’s role from manual parameter selection toward protocol verification and quality oversight. Automated kVp and mAs recommendations reduce operator dependence but increase the importance of accurate patient positioning and scan planning, as algorithm performance remains sensitive to input quality.[17] Technologists must monitor AI-generated protocols, confirm clinical appropriateness, and recognize atypical outputs or system errors. Familiarity with AI-assisted positioning tools and dose alerts is increasingly necessary to maintain patient safety and image consistency. Continuous professional education in AI literacy enables technologists to interpret system feedback, manage exceptions, and ensure patient-specific adaptation, particularly for individuals with atypical anatomy or clinical indications.

Radiologist adaptation and interpretation considerations

Radiologists must adapt to AI-reconstructed image characteristics, which may differ in noise texture and contrast appearance compared with conventional IR. Although studies demonstrate preserved or improved image quality at reduced dose levels, unfamiliar texture may initially affect diagnostic confidence and reader agreement.[1622] Targeted training and standardized viewing protocols can facilitate adaptation and maintain interpretive reliability. Reporting practices may also require harmonization to account for potential effects on quantitative measurements, including lesion size, lung density metrics, and radiomic features. Ongoing validation across clinical tasks ensures that AI-optimized protocols support accurate diagnosis while maintaining reproducibility in longitudinal imaging.[1624]

Quality assurance and governance framework

Robust quality assurance is essential to ensure safe and sustainable implementation of AI-optimized chest CT protocols. Continuous monitoring of radiation dose metrics, including CTDIvol, DLP, and SSDE, enables the early detection of protocol drift and unintended exposure changes over time.[211] Periodic validation is particularly important following software upgrades, hardware modifications, or changes in reconstruction algorithms, as performance characteristics may shift. Structured audit cycles should evaluate image quality, diagnostic outcomes, and adherence to DRLs. Clear accountability structures and multidisciplinary safety oversight help ensure that AI systems operate within established radiation protection principles and maintain consistent clinical reliability.[18]

CLOSED-LOOP ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE-DRIVEN COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY OPTIMIZATION MODEL

AI-enabled chest CT optimization can be operationalized as a structured closed-loop system integrating patient-specific inputs, acquisition parameters, reconstruction outputs, and task-based performance feedback.

The framework consists of four interdependent components:

Input layer (patient and task definition)

Patient-specific variables including body habitus, attenuation profile, and clinical indication define the imaging task (e.g., pulmonary nodule detection vs. interstitial lung disease). These parameters establish task-specific image quality requirements and acceptable dose thresholds.

Optimization engine (artificial intelligence-assisted acquisition and reconstruction)

AI models integrate the input data to optimize acquisition parameters (kVp, mAs, pitch, and scan range) and reconstruction settings (DLR strength and kernel selection). Unlike rule-based systems, this layer adapts parameter selection based on learned relationships between dose, noise, and task-specific detectability.

Output layer (task-based performance metrics)

Output is evaluated using both radiation dose metrics (CTDIvol, DLP, and SSDE) and task-based diagnostic performance indicators, including lesion detectability, diagnostic confidence, and reproducibility. Importantly, optimization targets detectability rather than global image quality metrics such as SNR or CNR.

Feedback and learning layer (continuous optimization)

Performance outcomes are continuously fed back into the system through dose monitoring platforms and clinical validation data. This enables iterative refinement of protocols, detection of protocol drift, and adaptation to local patient populations and scanner characteristics.

Within this closed-loop model, optimization is not a static parameter selection process but a dynamic, outcome-driven cycle in which acquisition and reconstruction strategies are continuously updated based on task-specific performance. This approach enables more consistent, patient-specific, and clinically aligned optimization compared with conventional one-directional workflows.

KEY RECOMMENDATIONS FOR CLINICAL PRACTICE

AI should complement not replace established dose optimization strategies, integrating with techniques such as AEC and patient-adapted acquisition. Protocols must remain task-specific, reflecting differing diagnostic requirements across clinical indications. Site-specific validation is essential before routine deployment, given variability in scanners, populations, and workflows. Continuous dose and image quality monitoring should be mandatory to detect protocol drift and maintain safety. Comprehensive training of radiologists and technologists in AI-assisted workflows is critical to ensure appropriate use, accurate interpretation, and sustained clinical reliability.[1718]

Limitations of current evidence

Despite promising results, the current evidence base for AI-optimized chest CT remains constrained by several methodological and practical limitations. Many studies are conducted at single centers with relatively homogeneous patient populations and scanner platforms, limiting external validity and hindering robust multicenter generalization. Vendor-specific algorithms and proprietary reconstruction pipelines further restrict cross-platform applicability, raising concerns about interoperability and long-term sustainability in the heterogeneous clinical environments.[1224] Dataset bias is another critical issue, as training data may underrepresent diverse body habitus, disease patterns, or imaging conditions, potentially affecting performance in the real-world settings.[10]

Long-term clinical outcomes associated with sustained ultralow-dose imaging such as effects on diagnostic accuracy in longitudinal follow-up remain insufficiently studied. In addition, AI-based reconstruction can alter image texture and noise characteristics, which may influence radiomics feature stability, quantitative biomarkers, and comparability across time or institutions.[1624] Variability in study design, dose metrics, and evaluation endpoints further complicates direct comparison between investigations. Collectively, these factors underscore the need for large-scale, multicenter validation, transparent reporting, and standardized evaluation frameworks before widespread adoption can be considered fully evidence-based.[18]

Future directions and research opportunities

Future developments include adaptive, self-learning CT protocols that refine parameters based on accumulated evidence. International studies demonstrate wide dose variability, highlighting limitations of static protocols.[28] AI-driven adaptive systems could continuously optimize acquisition and reconstruction based on patient and task characteristics.[2627] Integration with radiomics and clinical decision support is critical, particularly for longitudinal imaging. AI reconstruction affects texture and quantitative features, necessitating harmonization to preserve biomarker reliability. Multi-institutional validation across diverse scanners and populations is essential for clinical translation.[2431] These efforts align with personalized radiation medicine, tailoring CT protocols to individual patients and clinical indications to minimize cumulative radiation burden while preserving diagnostic efficacy.

CONCLUSIONS

AI assisted optimization of CT chest protocols offers a transformative approach to balancing diagnostic efficacy with radiation dose safety. By integrating intelligent acquisition strategies, deep learning-based reconstruction, and continuous quality monitoring, AI enables substantial and reproducible dose reduction while maintaining clinically acceptable image quality. In routine radiology practice, these tools support protocol standardization, reduce operator-dependent variability, and facilitate patient-specific imaging tailored to clinical indication and body habitus. When implemented with rigorous validation and oversight, AI is positioned to play an increasingly important role in advancing safer, more consistent, and high-quality CT chest imaging across the diverse clinical environments.
