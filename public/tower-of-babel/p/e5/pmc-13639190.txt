Topology-engineered metallic glass metamaterial-supported NiMo nanoglass catalysts for ultra-efficient water splitting
Authors: Congrui Yang, Chaoqun Pei, Haibo Ke, Shilong Sun, Huaping Zhang, Di Ouyang, Lei Zhang, Huaping Ding, Yongyun Zhang, Yuyang Qian, Baoan Sun, K C Chan, Wei-Hua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTElectrocatalytic water splitting represents a cornerstone for sustainable hydrogen production, yet its industrial implementation remains constrained by inherent trade-offs among activity, stability and mass transport efficiency. Achieving concurrent optimization of these properties in an integrated catalyst is challenging, as they are dictated by structures spanning multiple length scales. Here, we report a multiscale metamaterial catalyst design strategy that couples topology-engineered transport pathways with nanoglass heterointerface-regulated catalytic activity. Specifically, multiscale metamaterial catalysts (i.e. NiMo@Zr MMCs) are constructed by integrating micro-laser powder bed fusion-fabricated Zr-based metallic-glass triply periodic minimal surface scaffolds with pulsed-electrodeposition (PED)-derived NiMo nanoglass heterostructures. The Gyroid architecture provides enhanced permeability, efficient electrolyte/gas transport and mechanical robustness, while the abundant amorphous–amorphous interfaces within the NiMo nanoglass heterostructure optimize the local electronic environment and catalytic activity. Through the synergistic integration of topology engineering, metallic-glass scaffolds and nanoglass interface engineering, the resulting NiMo@G catalyst delivers record-low cell voltages for overall water splitting (1.438 V at 100 mA cm−2 in 1 M KOH) and outstanding long-term durability (>200 h). This work extends the concept of metamaterial catalysts from topology-controlled transport design to the coupling of transport and catalytic-interface engineering across multiple length scales, providing a generalizable framework for next-generation electrocatalysts.

INTRODUCTION

Hydrogen energy has emerged as a promising decarbonization vector, offering a carbon-neutral alternative to fossil fuels [1,2]. Among various hydrogen production routes, electrolytic water splitting stands out for its sustainability, yet its large-scale deployment remains limited by the intrinsic inefficiency of current electrocatalysts [3]. The enhancement of electrocatalytic efficiency relies on a tightly coupled multiscale design framework [4–8], spanning from atomic-level tuning of active sites [9], nano-level facilitation of charge transfer [10], micro-level control over reactant and product fluxes [11,12], to macro-level assurance of mechanical durability in operating devices [13]. However, simultaneously achieving multiscale design within a single catalyst system presents exceptional challenges due to the inherent conflicting structural features among functional requirements across different scales. For example, powder catalysts offer high specific surface areas and abundant active sites at the atomic/nano scale, yet they typically suffer from poor adhesion stability and inadequate charge-transfer pathways [14,15]. Conversely, structurally robust monolithic catalysts provide mechanical integrity at the macro scale but often sacrifice active site density and mass transport efficiency [16,17]. Therefore, developing a holistic design paradigm capable of reconciling these multiscale conflicts through coordinated architectural innovation has become an essential yet challenging pursuit in advanced catalyst engineering.

Nature provides numerous examples of architected materials that achieve multifunctional optimization through hierarchical organization [18,19]. Among them, marine echinoderms possess stereom structures consisting of highly porous and interconnected three-dimensional (3D) networks [20,21] that simultaneously provide mechanical support [22], efficient fluid transport [23] and attachment sites [23] for biological tissues. Previous studies have shown that the morphology of starfish ossicles (Fig. 1a i and ii) resembles diamond-type triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) structures, while the medulla region of their spines [24] (Fig. 1a iii and iv) exhibits cellular features analogous to primitive- and diamond-type TPMS geometries [25,26]. Such naturally occurring systems illustrate how topology can be exploited to integrate structural robustness and transport efficiency within a single framework. Inspired by these characteristics, TPMS geometries were selected as the structural basis for catalyst scaffold design in this work. In such systems, strut geometry, channel connectivity and pore-size distribution govern key characteristics including mechanical robustness, fluid transport, electrolyte accessibility and gas-release behavior [27–29]. Owing to these topology-derived advantages, recent studies have proposed the concept of metamaterial catalysts, in which catalytic performance is enhanced through topology-controlled mass transport and mechanical optimization [28].

Despite these advances, most reported metamaterial catalysts remain primarily focused on architectural design at the macro/mesoscale [28,30]. The active catalytic phase is typically introduced through spray coating [28], dip coating [31] or catalyst-ink deposition [32], while the microstructural characteristics, interfacial structure and intrinsic catalytic activity of the deposited materials are rarely incorporated into the overall design strategy. As a result, the coupling between topology-controlled transport and micro-/nano-scale catalyst structure regulation remains largely unexplored. To fully exploit the potential of metamaterial catalysts, scaffold architecture and active phase must be considered simultaneously. Developing a multiscale metamaterial catalyst framework capable of integrating topology-engineered transport behavior with micro-/electronic-scale catalytic activity therefore represents an important yet largely unexplored direction.

Metallic glasses (MGs) provide a unique platform for constructing architected catalyst scaffolds owing to their exceptional strength, corrosion resistance and structural homogeneity [27]. Beyond their structural role, the intrinsic atomic disorder and nano-scale heterogeneity of amorphous materials have also been shown to influence catalytic behavior [33,34]. The nanoglass concept proposed by Gleiter and co-workers [35] has highlighted the unique role of abundant glass–glass interfaces in tailoring the structural and functional properties of amorphous materials. For example, Feng et al. reported that nano-scale interfacial heterogeneity in Ni–P MGs can effectively modulate local electronic structures and enhance electrocatalytic activity [36]. These findings suggest that amorphous heterointerfaces may provide an additional design dimension for extending metamaterial catalysts beyond topology-controlled transport optimization.

Guided by these insights, we propose a multiscale metamaterial catalyst design strategy that couples topology-controlled mass transport with nanoglass heterointerface-controlled catalytic activity within a single catalyst architecture. Specifically, multiscale metamaterial catalysts (denoted as NiMo@Zr MMCs) were constructed by integrating micro-laser powder bed fusion (μLPBF)-fabricated Zr-based MG TPMS scaffolds with pulsed-electrodeposition (PED)-derived NiMo nanoglass heterostructures. The TPMS microlattices fabricated via μLPBF (Fig. 1b), which closely resemble stereom geometries, are adopted as ideal catalyst frameworks. Following 3D printing, Ni and Mo species were uniformly deposited onto the surface of TPMS MG microlattices (Fig. 1c) through PED, forming the integrated NiMo@Zr MMC architecture illustrated in Fig. 1d. By tuning the PED parameters [37], we engineered a hydrangea-like morphology with complex grooves (Fig. 1e) to optimize wettability and bubble adhesion behavior. Meanwhile, a nano-scale disordered NiMo heterostructure was simultaneously formed (Fig. 1f), where compositional fluctuations at the Ni78Mo22/Ni68Mo32 interfaces modulate the electronic states via d-band center adjustment (Fig. 1g), thereby boosting the catalytic activity. Through the synergistic coupling of topology-controlled mass transport and interface-controlled catalytic activity, the resulting system establishes a unified structure–transport–activity framework spanning from the macro-scale architecture to the nano-scale catalytic interface. At each scale, the structure is precisely tailored to optimize the corresponding performance, and these individually controlled properties are integrated into a single catalyst through multiscale fabrication. Leveraging this multiscale design strategy, the resulting NiMo@Zr MMCs exhibit remarkable superhydrophilic and superaerophobic properties, as well as exceptional bifunctional catalytic performance for both hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER). Impressively, the system achieves an ultra-low cell voltage of 1.438 V at j = 100 mA cm−2 in alkaline media, along with outstanding durability exceeding 200 h for overall water splitting.

RESULTS

Mechanical and transport properties of topology-engineered MG scaffolds

Three types of TPMS structures with high surface area and favorable permeability—Gyroid (G), Fischer–Koch S (S) and Diamond (D)—were selected as architectural models for fabricating Zr-based MG scaffolds (Fig. S1). Zr-based MG lattices with varying geometries were successfully printed via μLPBF. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. S2) revealed a broad halo diffraction peak in both the precursor powders and as-printed substrates, with no detectable crystalline Bragg reflections, confirming their fully amorphous structure. Each unit cell comprises solid shell phases and void channels with characteristic diameter d (Fig. 2a). Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) reconstruction (Fig. 2b) verified the high geometric fidelity and printing quality of the structures. A single-channel scanning strategy was employed to control the shell thickness, which ranged from 90 to 150 µm—near the resolution limit of current LPBF systems. Porosity measurements derived from micro-CT (Table S1) revealed significant variations among the TPMS architectures: the S-Zr MG exhibited the highest relative density (RD) and the lowest porosity (∼52%), while the D and G MGs showed higher porosities (∼72%). Pore sizes of different types of substrates can be directly investigated based on the 3D reconstructed model (Fig. 2c). Although the D and G MGs exhibit similar overall porosity, the G MG clearly shows a larger average pore diameter and a broader pore size distribution, with a bimodal peak between 200 and 600 µm (Fig. S3), indicative of a hierarchical pore network. In contrast, the D structure shows a narrower pore size range of 100–500 µm, while the S structure features predominantly sub-300 µm pores, with a minimum diameter as small as 20 µm.

Based on the 3D reconstruction model of the Zr-based MG scaffolds (Fig. 2d), the transport performance was analyzed to evaluate how different lattice architectures influence fluid flow behavior. Efficient mass transport minimizes concentration polarization and ensures stable electrochemical performance. The fluid flow behavior was simulated using a voxel-based finite volume method that directly incorporates the actual microstructural features obtained from CT scans, including pore shape and connectivity, thereby avoiding geometric simplification inherent to conventional mesh generation techniques. This method offers superior fidelity by inherently accounting for manufacturing-induced variations and imperfections in the additively fabricated substrates. Under standardized simulation conditions with a 1 Pa pressure difference between the inflow surface and outflow surface (Fig. S4), the G and D MG structures exhibited continuous and extended velocity streamlines (Fig. 2d and Fig. S5), indicative of higher permeability compared to the S MG structure, which exhibited shorter, more tortuous streamlines due to its lower porosity. Velocity contour maps of the cross-sections (Fig. 2e and Fig. S6) reveal localized increases in flow velocity within G MG, suggesting the presence of flow instabilities that promote enhanced mixing. In contrast, the D MG shows less velocity fluctuation across the structure (Fig. S7). Interestingly, despite D and G MGs having nearly identical overall porosities (∼28%), they display markedly different flow behaviors. G MG shows fluctuations in effective void fraction along the flow direction (Fig. 2f) with the highest mean flow velocity (Fig. 2g), whereas D MG maintains a relatively uniform distribution with a lower velocity. Although the overall porosity of S MG is ∼50%, the effective void fraction within the internal region drops to only ∼30% (Fig. 2f), resulting in a significantly lower mean velocity compared to the other two structures (Fig. 2g and Fig. S8). Furthermore, G MG demonstrated significantly enhanced vertical transport (z-direction velocity component; Fig. S9), enabling multidimensional reactant flow beyond the primary x–y plane, in contrast to the more uniform but less efficient flow patterns observed in D MG.

Damage-resistant, high-strength catalysts are essential for the long-term durability and operational stability of electrocatalytic systems. To assess the mechanical properties of the 3D-printed Zr-based MG scaffolds, uniaxial compression tests were conducted on 4 mm cubic samples. The resulting stress–strain curves (Fig. 2h) show high compressive strength and an extended stress plateau following the elastic region, indicating excellent damage tolerance of the printed structures. Among them, the S MG demonstrated the highest compressive strength, exceeding 300 MPa, which correlates with its maximum RD. When specific strength is considered (Fig. 2i), the S MG achieved the highest value of over 110 MPa kg−1 cm−3, surpassing many conventional metallic cellular materials. In contrast, the G MG exhibited a lower specific strength of 78 MPa kg−1 cm−3 due to its geometric characteristics. Notably, even the lowest performing G MG surpassed typical catalyst support requirements, such as nickel foam [38], by 10 times while retaining the porosity critical for mass transport.

Taken together, these results reveal that the superior performance of the G MG scaffold originates from its topology-enabled transport characteristics. Compared with the D and S MG architectures, the G structure possesses a continuously curved surface and a highly interconnected 3D channel network, which collectively promote higher permeability, faster electrolyte renewal and enhanced multidirectional mass transport. These characteristics are expected to facilitate reactant delivery and product removal throughout the scaffold while maximizing the accessibility of catalytic sites during electrolysis. More importantly, the advantages of the G architecture extend beyond geometric parameters such as porosity alone. Although the D and G MG structures exhibit comparable overall porosity, the G scaffold displays significantly higher average flow velocity, stronger velocity fluctuations and enhanced transport along the z direction. These features indicate more efficient electrolyte penetration and transport throughout the 3D framework. In addition, the continuous curvature of the G surface is expected to promote favorable wetting behavior and facilitate bubble detachment during gas-evolving reactions, thereby reducing local mass-transfer limitations. Consequently, the G architecture provides a more effective platform for catalyst utilization, leading to superior electrocatalytic performance compared with the D and S structures.

Multiscale modulation of metamaterial catalysts

The tunable MG microlattices guarantee both structural toughness and efficient matter transport. To further elevate catalytic performance, we tailored the surface morphology at the micro scale and engineered the atomic structure at the nano scale. The surface morphology of catalysts serves as a crucial design parameter for regulating their wettability properties, where an optimal balance between superhydrophilicity (enhancing electrolyte wetting) and superaerophobicity (prompt bubble detachment) significantly improves the efficiency and durability of water-splitting systems [39,40]. The optimal catalyst scaffold G (Fig. 3b) and all other substrates (Fig. S10) were observed using scanning electron microscope (SEM), showing no internal defects such as pores or cracks apart from minor surface-adhered unmolten powder particles. These residual particles caused slight surface roughness, which beneficially increased the surface area and enhanced the adhesion of Ni and Mo atoms during the subsequent PED process. Based on the different scaffold architectures, the NiMo@MMCs are categorized into NiMo@G, NiMo@S and NiMo@D. A large number of uniform hydrangea-shaped NiMo particles, ranging in size from ∼60 to 100 µm (Fig. S11), were observed on NiMo@G (Fig. 3c) and across all substrate variants (NiMo@S and NiMo@D) (Fig. S12) after PED [29]. NiMo flowers grown on the scaffold surface create a hierarchically complex morphology that enables precise tuning of the surface wettability in the NiMo@Zr MMCs. The contact angle of the bare scaffold G is 103.65° after 300 s (Fig. 3d) with all the other substrates exhibiting a similar level of hydrophilicity (Fig. S13), whereas all NiMo@Zr MMCs exhibit superhydrophilicity (Fig. S14), with water droplets spreading completely upon contact, particularly for NiMo@G (Fig. 3e) that achieves instantaneous wetting (0° contact angle within just 35 ms). Concurrently, these NiMo@Zr MMCs demonstrate remarkable superaerophobic characteristics in aqueous environments. Specifically, the bubble contact angle (CA) of scaffold G is 155° (Fig. 3f) whereas the NiMo@G shows the ultra-hydrophobicity (CA > 169°) (Fig. 3g). The bubble contact angles significantly improved to varying extents after surface modification of all the different substrates via PED (Figs S15 and S16).

A closer inspection (Fig. 3h–i) reveals the detailed morphology of the NiMo particles, characterized by a highly wrinkled and uneven surface, which increases the specific surface area and exposes more active catalytic sites. The bare scaffolds, without NiMo deposition, exhibited minimal catalytic activity (Fig. S17). To further boost catalytic activity, we modulate the microstructure of the NiMo particles. While elemental mapping demonstrates macro-scale uniformity in Ni and Mo distribution across the entire flower-like structures (Fig. S18), nano-scale analysis through high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) reveals distinct contrast variations (Fig. S19a), indicating phase separation. The overall selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Fig. 3j insert) exhibits a halo ring, indicative of an amorphous structure. High-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) analysis reveals a disordered region (region I) and a more loosely arranged, medium-range ordered atomic cluster (region II) (Fig. 3j and Fig. S19b). The interface region (I) exhibits a highly disordered maze-like structure with inner circular fringes (Fig. 3k) and a featureless fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern, whereas the cluster region (II) displays clear crystalline fringes (Fig. 3l) with distinct diffraction spots in the FFT pattern. HAADF-STEM reveals distinct contrast variations (Fig. 3m), indicating phase separation into Ni-rich (bright) and Mo-rich (dark) amorphous domains. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping confirms this structural heterogeneity, showing significant local Ni concentration variations while Mo maintains a relatively uniform distribution. Integrated compositional line scans (Fig. 3n) quantitatively verify that the brighter STEM regions correspond to Ni78Mo22 domains, while darker areas exhibit Ni68Mo32 stoichiometry—a 10% compositional difference statistically confirmed by EDX mapping (Fig. 3o and Fig. S20). Atomic-scale characterization reveals that the PED-fabricated NiMo hydrangea structures form an amorphous chemical heterostructure, featuring numerous interfaces between compositionally distinct domains (Ni78Mo22 and Ni68Mo32). These interfaces are believed to offer abundant highly active sites for catalytic reactions.

Excellent bi-functional performance with HER and OER and mechanism

We further evaluated the electrocatalytic performance of the NiMo@Zr MMCs for water electrolysis in 1 M KOH, as shown in Fig. 4. NiMo@G (Fig. 4a) exhibits a remarkably low HER overpotential of 26 mV at 10 mA cm−2, significantly outperforming NiMo@S (59 mV), NiMo@D (81 mV) and commercial Pt/C (55 mV). Tafel analysis (Fig. 4c) reveals NiMo@G’s exceptional HER kinetics with a record-low 27.91 mV dec−1 slope, outperforming NiMo@S (76.79 mV dec−1), NiMo@D (91.94 mV dec−1) and commercial Pt/C (33.42 mV dec−1) by 2.7–3.3 times, demonstrating its superior charge-transfer efficiency. NiMo@G exhibits outstanding HER performance at industrially relevant current densities (Fig. 4e), requiring only 79 and 126 mV of overpotential to achieve 100 and 300 mA cm−2, respectively. This superior activity under high-current conditions highlights its strong potential for practical water-splitting applications. We further assessed the OER performance of the NiMo@G, as shown in Fig. 4b. All samples exhibit a distinct oxidation peak during the OER process, corresponding to the oxidation of Ni species that facilitate oxygen evolution. This observation confirms the excellent intrinsic electrocatalytic activity of the NiMo@Zr MMCs. To accurately evaluate the overpotential and minimize the influence of the oxidation peak, cyclic voltammetry (CV) was employed. NiMo@G demonstrates exceptional OER performance with an ultra-low overpotential of 155 mV at 10 mA cm−2, significantly outperforming NiMo@S (181 mV), NiMo@D (204 mV) and commercial IrO2 (Fig. 4b). Tafel analysis (Fig. 4d) reveals NiMo@G’s superior OER kinetics with a record-low Tafel slope of 31.08 mV dec−1, outperforming NiMo@S (42.79 mV dec−1), NiMo@D (54.90 mV dec−1) and IrO2 (128.99 mV dec−1), demonstrating significantly faster reaction rates. Figure 4e presents the overpotentials at various current densities (10, 50, 100 and 300 mA cm−2). Notably, NiMo@G consistently delivers the lowest overpotentials across all current densities, especially in industrial-grade currents. For example, NiMo@G demonstrates superior performance with significantly lower overpotentials (204 mV @ 100 mA cm−2; 261 mV @ 300 mA cm−2), representing a 18%–23% reduction in overpotential across industrial-relevant current densities compared with other samples. Compared with crystallized G scaffolds and Ni foam controls, NiMo@G exhibits superior catalytic performance (Fig. S21). This indicates that the enhanced activity originates not only from the active NiMo phase or surface-area enlargement, but also from the synergistic contributions of the amorphous MG scaffold, topology-engineered mass transport enabled by the TPMS architecture and intrinsically active NiMo nanoglass interfaces. The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) was evaluated by extracting the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) from the CV curves in the non-Faradaic region (Fig. 4f). NiMo@S, with a Cdl of 36.45 mF cm−2, exposes more active sites than NiMo@D (Cdl = 4.65 mF cm−2), leading to higher catalytic activity. Remarkably, NiMo@G exhibits the highest active site density, with a Cdl of 105.31 mF cm−2, which correlates with its outstanding catalytic performance. Furthermore, ECSA-normalized HER and OER polarization curves confirm that NiMo@G remains highly competitive after normalization (Figs S22 and S23). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy results for NiMo@G (Fig. S24) show a smaller Nyquist plot radius compared to both NiMo@S and NiMo@D, indicating lower charge-transfer resistance and superior electron transport, thereby enhancing catalytic performance.

To better understand the origin of the exceptional OER and HER performance of the chemically heterogeneous NiMo MG catalysts, we employed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to evaluate their free energy profiles and electronic structures. Large-scale amorphous Ni78Mo22 and Ni68Mo32 models were first generated through melt–quench molecular dynamics simulations (Fig. S25), followed by the construction of an amorphous Ni78Mo22/Ni68Mo32 interfacial structure (Fig. 4g and ‘Methods’ section). To explore the electronic structures of the three distinct NiMo MGs, the partial density of states (PDOS) for Ni and Mo, as well as the total density of states, were calculated, as shown in Fig. 4g. Catalytic activity is predominantly controlled by the d-band center’s position relative to the Fermi level (EF), where closer proximity enhances adsorbate binding via strengthened d-orbital interactions. The Ni 3d d-band center shifts markedly closer to the EF (1.375 eV) at the interface compared to both Ni68Mo32 (1.500 eV) and Ni78Mo22 (1.461 eV) MGs, indicating enhanced catalytic activity through strengthened adsorbate interactions at interfacial Ni sites. However, the d-band center of Mo remains relatively unchanged, suggesting that the variation in catalytic activity is primarily associated with the Ni 3d orbitals.

To account for the intrinsic structural heterogeneity of amorphous materials, 10 representative local environments (Fig. S26) were randomly sampled from each region (Ni78Mo22, Ni68Mo32 and the interfacial region) for statistical HER calculations. As shown in Fig. 4h and Fig. S27, the interfacial region exhibits consistently more favorable hydrogen adsorption behavior than the two amorphous phases. The statistical distribution of ΔG_H* further indicates that the enhanced HER activity is a general characteristic of the amorphous–amorphous interface rather than a single optimized adsorption site (Figs S26 and S28). In OER, the interface structure reduces the rate-determining step energy barrier (G*O–G*OOH), yielding a substantially lower overpotential (η = 0.349 V) compared to Ni68Mo32 (η = 0.813 V) and Ni78Mo22 (η = 0.741 V), as shown in Fig. 4i and Fig. S29. These results suggest that the abundant amorphous–amorphous interfaces within the NiMo nanoglass heterostructure provide electronically favorable catalytic environments, thereby contributing to the enhanced bifunctional electrocatalytic performance.

Evaluation of the overall water-splitting performance

The NiMo@G demonstrate exceptional HER and OER performance, rendering them highly suitable for overall water-splitting applications. A dual-electrode electrolyzer was assembled using NiMo@G as both the anode and cathode. The polarization curves (Fig. 5a) reveal that this system achieves current densities of 100 and 500 mA cm−2 at ultra-low cell voltages of 1.438 and 1.707 V, respectively—significantly lower than that of the benchmark IrO2//Pt/C system (1.783 V @ 100 mA cm−2). High-speed imaging of bubble dynamics at 100 mA cm−2 clearly demonstrates the operational advantages of this design: while the bubble-release behaviors of conventional porous IrO2 electrodes suffer from persistent bubble adhesion and coalescence into large (>500 μm) gas pockets that block active sites (Fig. 5b and Video S1), the NiMo@G catalyst enables rapid release of small (∼70 μm) bubbles (Fig. 5c and Video S2). Stability testing via chronopotentiometry (Fig. 5d) confirms the excellent durability of NiMo@G, with negligible current density loss over 200 h of continuous operation at 1.438 V. Interestingly, the overall water-splitting performance slightly improved after the 200-h test, as shown by the enhanced polarization curve (pink line in Fig. 5a). Post-OER X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses reveal the disappearance of the peak of Ni0 together with the emergence of Ni3+ peak. Meanwhile, the O 1s spectra show the appearance of OH− peak, suggesting the formation of NiOOH-rich surface phases during prolonged electrolysis (Fig. S30). The formation of NiOOH has been widely recognized as the catalytically active phase for alkaline OER and is therefore likely responsible for the enhanced catalytic activity after long-term operation [41–43]. In addition, the Mo 3d spectra shift toward higher binding energies after the OER test, indicating the formation of higher-valent Mo species, which may act synergistically with the Ni3+ to further promote the OER kinetics. Meanwhile, post-stability SEM and EDX characterizations confirm that both the hierarchical NiMo coating and the underlying MG scaffold remain structurally intact without obvious morphology degradation or elemental segregation after 200 h electrolysis (Fig. S31), demonstrating the excellent durability of the multiscale catalyst architecture.

Furthermore, during the initial operation, the NiMo@G//NiMo@G configuration demonstrated one of the lowest cell voltages among reported catalysts at high current densities (1.438 V @ 100 mA cm−2; Fig. 5e). This work highlights the synergistic combination of 3D printing technology, which enables precise geometric control, flexible design capabilities and tunable mechanical/mass-transfer properties, with PED that addresses the limitations in microstructural control. Compared to conventional porous metal catalysts, precious metal catalysts and metal microlattices, the NiMo@G MG metamaterial catalysts uniquely combine the multiple advantages of structural strength, super hydrophobic and superhydrophilicity, low economic costs and extraordinary catalytic activity (Fig. 5f), establishing a new paradigm for designing high-performance electrocatalytic system. The proposed multiscale metamaterial catalyst framework is not restricted to the NiMo system investigated in this work. Preliminary experiments (Fig. S32) on NiMoFeCo coatings deposited on the same TPMS MG scaffold reveal distinct amorphous/nanocrystalline heterostructures together with promising electrocatalytic performance. These observations suggest that the framework can be readily extended to a broader range of catalyst compositions.

CONCLUSION

In summary, this work proposes a multiscale metamaterial catalyst design strategy that integrates topology-engineered transport pathways, architected MG scaffolds and nanoglass heterointerface-regulated catalytic activity within a single catalyst architecture. Drawing inspiration from the stereom architecture of echinoderms, we translated their unique coupling of mechanical resilience and matter-transport efficiency into MG scaffolds with TPMS geometries. To achieve hierarchical control over structure and composition, PED was employed to integrate NiMo amorphous particles onto TPMS MG scaffolds. This strategy enables concurrent optimization of macro-scale mechanical robustness, micro-scale surface wettability and atomic-scale catalytic activity. Among the architectures, the Gyroid-based NiMo@Zr MMCs exhibited superior compressive strength (>300 MPa), enhanced mass transfer and outstanding bifunctional catalytic performance, requiring only 1.438 V to reach 100 mA cm−2 with stable operation for 200 h. More importantly, this work extends the concept of metamaterial catalysts beyond conventional topology-controlled transport optimization toward the integration of micro-/nano-scale catalyst structure regulation. The proposed multiscale design framework provides a generalizable pathway for designing next-generation metamaterial catalysts.
