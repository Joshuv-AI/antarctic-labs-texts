D’ You Know What I Mean?
Authors: Vincent L Sorrell
Published: 2025

“The questions are the answers you might need”

D’ You Know What I Mean?; Oasis; Be Here Now; Epic Records, 1997

Thanks for reading this CASE editorial. I appreciate your intellectual curiosity. As in previous issues of CASE, this issue includes multiple CASE reports and CASE series that help us to maintain our enthusiasm for horizon expansion. This editorial continues its recently modified goal of providing behind-the-scenes interactions between the authors and the reviewers focusing on the unwritten word as a means to enhance the full understanding of each published CASE report (see my editorial from August).

In this month’s issue of CASE, there are once again multiple reports that inform our diagnostic abilities and provide us with specific CASE-based examples of various pathologic conditions and their image-guided approach to management. Although not all patients have a successful outcome, that is inevitable given the advanced pathologies submitted to CASE. However, we believe that there are opportunities to learn from poor outcomes that are equal (or possibly even superior) to our ability to learn from CASE examples with favorable clinical outcomes.

In Acute Apical Sparing Strain Pattern of the Right Ventricle in Pulmonary Embolism – A Speckle-Tracking McConnell’s Sign Equivalent, the authors presented a relatively straight-forward report on the use of STE bullseye pattern in a patient with confirmed acute pulmonary embolism and the associated common echo finding of RV systolic dysfunction with focal apical-sparing. Their report nicely demonstrates the finding that corresponds to the 2D echo findings, and the authors describe that this ‘semi-quantitative’ method may be useful if studied further for its association with clinical outcomes. During the editorial review process, the authors responded in greater detail regarding the impact of underlying pulmonary hypertension and its impact on the 2DE and STE findings. They stated that “the echocardiographic findings could raise concern for chronic RV remodeling, but in the absence of prior imaging or clinical indicators, we cannot definitively determine whether chronic pressure overload was present preoperatively.” Some unnecessary images were removed; additional comparison figures were added; and greater discussion on the segmental variation in strain was included. Finally, since reviewers pushed for the clinical value of this imaging pattern, the authors reiterated that their “goal [was] to provide evidence for an objective, strain-based measure of McConnell’s sign that may improve diagnostic accuracy, but [may] also be [included in clinical studies to determine if it can be] used for risk stratification [or] for monitoring response to therapy in acute PE.”

In Hypervascular Myxoma in the Left Atrium, the authors described their findings in a teenager with dyspnea and dizziness who was found to have a LA myxoma. During their diagnostic investigation, the authors obtained outstanding contrast perfusion images highlighting the value of the administration of an ultrasound enhancing agent. They provide readers with their technique for perfusion imaging. Through the editorial review process, the authors included additional findings on their CV physical examination and reasons for lack of a tumor plop. They expanded their discussion on contrast perfusion kinetics and importance of identifying rapid perfusion (e.g. hypervascular), and importantly, they included much greater precision in their morphologic and anatomic description(s) of the mass. These insights offer clarity for the readers as they apply this to their own clinical / echo practice.

In Threading the Needle: Left Atrial Ridge Mass Complicating Percutaneous Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion, the authors reported on two patients with left atrial masses. However, unlike the large mass in the above CASE report, these cases had very different appearing masses and were incidentally found during TEE imaging for LAAO device placement. The authors wanted readers to know that their report describes “incidentally found intracardiac masses along the coumadin ridge both during and after percutaneous left atrial appendage occlusion device planning. These incidental masses posed unique clinical conundrums [and we] believe these cases not only demonstrate a unique presentation of pathology but highlight the increasing discovery of incidental masses as percutaneous procedures rapidly expand. Such cases are particularly challenging in a subset of patients likely deemed to be poor surgical candidates and emphasize the importance of a multi-disciplinary approach and careful peri-procedural imaging in managing such scenarios.” During the editorial review process, the authors worked with the expert peer reviewers to avoid overstating a tissue diagnosis based upon imaging alone and instead, offer expanded differential diagnoses for readers to consider. The outstanding graphical abstract with timeline for each representative CASE that was included in their report should be valued by readers.

In Aortic Valve Myxoma in a Young Child, the authors present readers with an unusual CASE of a 5-year-old child with an aortic valve mass that was determined to be a myxoma. They make sure readers are aware of the fact that “aortic valve myxoma may present in young children;” that “a new or worsening murmur could indicate increasing tumor size;” and that “the risk of embolization [warrants] surgical intervention.” During the editorial review process, there was significant discussion related to the differences between adults and children in 2D and Doppler imaging, the use of qualitative versus quantitative reporting, and even the indications for diagnostic surveillance and surgical interventions. In the end, the authors have prepared a CASE report that addresses these in an educationally insightful manner for readers. It should be pointed out that this outstanding report was organized by a sonographer corresponding author.

In Fulminant Streptococcus Dysgalactiae Endocarditis in a Pediatric Patient, the authors reported their unusual findings of multi-valvular fulminant infective endocarditis in a healthy pediatric patient. The authors used this report to highlight the challenges in making this diagnosis given the overlap in symptoms, laboratory, and echocardiographic findings which mimic rheumatic mitral stenosis - a much more common diagnosis in this healthy, young patient population. In fact, these authors helped guide readers through their initial misdiagnosis of acute rheumatic fever which led to delay in diagnosis. Reports that provide these perspectives enhance readers’ diagnostic acumen when they are inescapably confronted with similar clinical scenarios. This report also reiterated the challenges in management of critically ill pediatric patients with severe valvular regurgitation. During the editorial review process, the authors prepared a table “summarizing diagnostic criteria for acute rheumatic fever and infective endocarditis” to help provide readers with some important comparisons. Although this patient did not survive given the rapid progression and complications of the disseminated infection, there is much we can learn from this report.

Lastly, in The Shifting Flow: Aortic Root Pseudoaneurysm with Dynamic Multichamber Fistulization after Aortic Valve Surgery, the authors described their patient who was found by echo Doppler to have a complex fistula involving the sinus of Valsalva. The fistula was noted to have an unusual Doppler pattern for which the authors provided excellent images for readers to fully appreciate. They emphasized that this atypical fistula had a “hemodynamic impact that resembled multivalvular disease and that their case description will benefit readers by enhancing recognition of this rare abnormality and its clinical significance.” During the editorial review process, the authors worked with reviewers to include images that provided the utmost clarity so readers would have the best chance of understanding the imaging aspects of this complex pathology. Their subsequent graphical abstract is an exceptional central illustration that fulfills this editorial request and can be used as a guide for anyone reading this report.

The fellows and residents in training who inevitably must work closely with me frequently hear me say: “D'you know what I mean?” This editorial is dedicated to all of you who take a similar approach to understanding that “the questions” you consider are commonly the source of the intellectual curiosities that form the foundations of “the answers you might need.” I encourage you to listen to Oasis if there is any part of your DNA that appreciates insightful, everyday lyrics similar to what the Beatles are known for.

Remember, every echo you see today has a teaching point and every teaching point is a potential new CASE report!
