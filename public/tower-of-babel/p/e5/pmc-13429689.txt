Selective electrochemical methanol to formate conversion via direct CHO hydroxylation on Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles
Authors: Ke Cheng, Hao Li, Bing Zhou, Haopeng Pei, Lufa Hu, Long Zhao, Xingyue Zou, Jiaxian Wang, Qian Zheng, Rui Zhao, Yan Zhang, Anping Liu, Zewen Wu, Guangming Zhan, Mingming Guo, Lizhi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe electrochemical upgrading of methanol to formate is constrained by CO poisoning from *CHO intermediate dehydrogenation, hindering its industrial application. Herein, we report that engineering atomically polarized Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles on Ti felt achieves a formate Faradaic efficiency of 99% at +0.9 V vs. RHE, which is better than that of commercial Pt/C (62%). Moreover, a formate production rate of 945 mmol gPt–1 h–1 is achieved with stable performance for more than 5 days at 100 mA cm–2. These dipoles comprise spatially adjacent electron-deficient Ptδ+ bonded to lattice O and electron-rich Ptδ– coordinated to unsaturated Ti atoms working synergistically. The Ptδ+ site dehydrogenates CH3OH to *CHO, which adsorbs across the dipole in a side-on Ptδ+–OHC–Ptδ– bridging configuration. Within this configuration, Ptδ– donates electrons via d→π* backdonation to the π* antibonding orbital of *CHO, steering its direct hydroxylation and generating electrostatic repulsion for rapid HCOOH desorption. This strategy reduces environmental impact by 88% and carbon emissions by 11% relative to conventional thermal routes, demonstrating the potential of dipole engineering for C1 electrochemistry.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

The electrochemical methanol oxidation reaction (EMOR) offers a facile and green pathway to upgrade CH3OH—surpassing energy-intensive thermal processes1–3. While nickel-based catalysts have been extensively studied for EMOR, they typically require high onset potentials (>1.35 V vs. the reversible hydrogen electrode, RHE)4–6. In contrast, Pt-based catalysts achieve efficient methanol oxidation at significantly lower potentials, solidifying Pt as the benchmark system for fundamental studies7–9 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Conventional Pt catalysts often feature electron-deficient Ptδ+ sites10,11, where vacant d-orbitals chemisorb CH3OH via O-lone-pair σ donation, while filled d-orbitals cleave C–H bonds through d→σ* backdonation at low overpotentials below +0.6 V vs. RHE12,13. However, this d-orbital synergy becomes a double-edged sword for the critical *CHO intermediate during stepwise dehydrogenation (*CH3OH→*CH3O→*CH2O→*CHO), because d→σ* backdonation of Ptδ+ can destroy the C–H bond of *CHO, generating toxic *CO that poisons active sites to impede HCOOH formation14–17 (Fig. 1a). Even when HCOOH is produced, its carboxyl oxygen lone pairs bind strongly to Lewis acidic Ptδ+ sites, resulting in its trapping and further overoxidation to CO218–20 (Supplementary Fig. 2). These cascading failures of *CO poisoning, selectivity loss, and product overoxidation stem from the coactivation of reactants and intermediates on Pt, severely limiting EMOR efficiency21,22.

An ideal methanol to formate oxidation catalyst must preserve the intrinsic C–H bond activation capacity of Pt while radically improving selectivity at the critical *CHO juncture by directing the reaction towards hydroxylation and rapid HCOOH desorption23,24. Orbital analysis reveals that kinetically favored *CHO hydroxylation requires electrophilic activation of its C=O bond via a π-orbital population25 (Fig. 1b). This requires a secondary electron-rich site of Pt (Ptδ–), whose high-energy d-orbitals possess two characteristics26. One is symmetric resonance with the low-lying C=O π* orbital of *CHO, enabling intense d→π* electron transfer to activate the C=O bond27. The other is asymmetric mismatch with the C–H σ* orbital, inducing weakened d→σ* electron transfer to block dehydrogenation28. Critically, the electron-rich Ptδ– site simultaneously generates electrostatic repulsion towards the carboxyl group of HCOOH, weakening chemisorption and preventing its overoxidation29. The dual mechanism of orbital-engineered activation and charge-driven desorption establishes site polarization as a universal design principle for selective methanol electrooxidation.

Guided by this design principle, we engineered spatially adjacent Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles on Ti felt to decouple CH3OH adsorption at Lewis acidic Ptδ+ sites from selective *CHO hydroxylation via π-backdonation at Ptδ– sites. Ultrafine Pt nanoparticles were anchored on conductive Ti felt (2.4 × 106 S m–1)29, with its native amorphous TiOx layer tailored via oxygen vacancy (OV) generation to create electron-deficient Ptδ+ sites bonded to lattice O and adjacent electron-rich Ptδ– sites coordinated to unsaturated Ti30. These Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles were subsequently used for electrochemical upgrading of methanol to formate, and their dual function and reaction mechanism were comprehensively elucidated through isotopic labeling, in situ spectroscopy, and density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Finally, technical-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) were performed to evaluate this electrochemical formate synthesis mediated by Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles.

Results

Synthesis and characterization

Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles were fabricated on Ti felt (Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti) by reducing Pt precursors under 5 vol% H2/Ar (Supplementary Fig. 3). Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping confirmed the uniform dispersion of ultrafine Pt nanoparticles (~2 nm) and a Pt loading of 4.3 wt% was determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‒MS) (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Figs. 4, 5 and Table 1). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) revealed high crystallinity via lattice fringes corresponding to 0.22 nm for Pt(111), 0.20 nm for Pt(200), and 0.14 nm for Pt(220)—characteristic of face-centered cubic Pt (Fig. 2b)—which is consistent with X-ray diffraction (XRD, Supplementary Fig. 6)31. X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) analysis indicated that the Pt L3-edge of Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti was positioned between those of Pt foil and PtO2 (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 7), confirming its partial oxidation32,33. Moreover, Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy (EXAFS) revealed coordination shells for Pt–O (1.6 Å) and Pt–Pt (2.6 Å), along with an unexpected Pt–Ti path (2.5 Å) (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 8)33–35, directly evidencing multivalent Pt states mediated by interfacial Pt–Ti bonding34,36.

Wavelet-transformed EXAFS further resolved the complex coordination of Pt on Ti felt (Fig. 2e). The intensity maximum at a k value of 6.8 Å–1 confirmed the presence of Pt–O bonds (Ptδ+), whereas a distinct Pt–Ti scattering feature at a k value of approximately 8.5 Å–1, despite Pt–Ti bonding being thermodynamically unfavorable on fully oxidized TiOx, indicated electron-rich Ptδ– formation37,38. This anomaly was rationalized by H2 reduction generating OVs, exposing unsaturated Ti atoms for Pt anchoring39,40, with net electron transfer from Ti to Pt for the generation of Ptδ– sites. EPR spectroscopy confirmed the presence of OVs (g = 2.008 signal in Ti treated in H2, absent in pristine TiOx/Ti; Fig. 2f)41, with signal attenuation observed upon Pt deposition as nanoparticles occupied defect sites (Supplementary Fig. 9). Finally, quantitative EXAFS fitting corroborated the structure of each Pt atom coordinated with approximately two O atoms and two Ti atoms (Supplementary Table 2).

To establish the causal relationship between OVs and Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles, we engineered control catalysts through atmosphere-specific annealing of the parent material (Supplementary Fig. 10), including H2/Ar (5 vol%) annealing (OV-enriched Ptδ–/Ti) and air annealing (OV-depleted Ptδ–/Ti). The XRD patterns and HAADF-STEM images confirmed that Ptδ+/Ti and Ptδ–/Ti retain their original morphology and uniform dispersion (Supplementary Figs. 11–14). XANES spectra demonstrated that Ptδ+/Ti had a higher white-line intensity (more oxidized), whereas Ptδ–/Ti had a lower intensity (more reduced) (Supplementary Figs. 15–19 and Table 3). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of the pristine catalyst (Fig. 2g) confirmed the presence of the following electronically distinct Pt species: Ptδ+ (72.62 eV, 75.95 eV), Ptδ– (70.95 eV, 74.28 eV), and Pt0 (71.20 eV, 74.53 eV)42,43. Following H2 reduction to generate OVs, the Ptδ– content increased from 30 to 55% (Supplementary Fig. 20 and Tables 4–5), providing direct evidence of electron transfer from coordinatively unsaturated Ti sites to Pt atoms anchored at OVs. Conversely, oxidative annealing reduced the content of Ptδ– to 28% while increasing the amount of Ptδ+. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) was used to further probe the surface electronic structure. Compared with Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti, the H2-reduced, Ptδ–-enriched catalyst displayed a significant decrease in work function (Φ) of 0.11 eV, whereas the oxidized, Ptδ+-enriched catalyst revealed an increase of 0.12 eV (Supplementary Figs. 21, 22 and Table 6). This reversible shift originated from the presence of oppositely charged Pt sites at the interface. In the Ptδ–-enriched catalyst, electron transfer from Ti to Pt rendered the surface electron-rich, which decreased Φ by lowering the barrier for electron emission. In contrast, Ptδ+/Ti exhibited the opposite trend, with an elevated Φ. This bidirectional modulation demonstrates that OVs create electron-rich Ptδ– sites via Pt–Ti bonding, whereas adjacent lattice-oxygen coordination stabilizes electron-deficient Ptδ+ sites (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 23). Their intimate spatial proximity enables spontaneous dipole formation, validating our design principle.

EMOR performance

The intrinsic EMOR performance of Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti was evaluated via linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) in 1.0 M KOH with 1.0 M CH3OH (Supplementary Figs. 24, 25 and Table 7). The catalyst exhibited high activity, initiating oxidation at a low onset potential of +0.37 V vs. RHE and achieving a peak current density of 543 mA cm⁻2 (with 95% iR correction), which is 1.7, 2.7 and 5.9 times higher than those of Ptδ–/Ti, Ptδ+/Ti and Pt/C, respectively (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Figs. 26–29 and Tables 8–10)44,45. The electrochemical active surface area (ECSA)-normalized specific activity of Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti reached 97.82 A m–2, significantly outperforming that of the control catalysts (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Figs. 30, 31 and Tables 11–12). Critically, its Tafel slope (178 mV dec⁻¹) was lower than those of Ptδ+/Ti (180 mV dec⁻¹), Ptδ–/Ti (186 mV dec⁻¹), and Pt/C (294 mV dec⁻¹), confirming its accelerated kinetics (Supplementary Figs. 32, 33)46,47.

Product selectivity, assessed by controlled-potential electrolysis at +0.90 V vs. RHE, revealed that formate was the exclusive liquid product (1H NMR; Fig. 3c) with 99% ± 0.67% Faradaic efficiency (FE) for Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti, as quantified by ion chromatography (IC, Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 34)5,48 This performance significantly surpassed those of Ptδ+/Ti (89% ± 0.34%), Ptδ–/Ti (83% ± 0.61%) and commercial Pt/C (62% ± 1.40%), demonstrating dipole-driven electronic synergy for selective methanol oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 35). To further rule out the contribution of Pt nanoparticles or isolated Pt single atoms alone to the high selectivity of EMOR, we prepared ~2 nm Pt nanoparticles supported on inert carbon paper (Pt/CP), and isolated Pt single atoms on Ti felt (Pt1/Ti), both of which lack interfacial Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles (Supplementary Fig. 36 and Table 12). The detailed preparation procedures for these control samples are described in the “Methods” section. As expected, Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti exhibited a specific activity of 97.82 A m–2, which was 2.15 and 2.52 times that of Pt/CP (45.40 A m–2) and Pt1/Ti (38.84 A m–2), respectively. More importantly, the Faradaic efficiencies (FEs) for formate on Pt/CP and Pt1/Ti were 65% and 50% at +0.9 V vs. RHE, respectively, much lower than that of Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti (>99%), demonstrating that neither the bulk Pt nanoparticles nor the isolated Pt single atoms independently accounted for the high selectivity towards formate. Benchmarking shows competitive selectivity at lower potentials in comparison with documented EMOR catalysts. (Supplementary Fig. 37 and Table 13). The Ptδ+-Ptδ– synergy suppressed side reactions, achieving a production rate of 945 mmol gPt⁻1 h⁻1 (Fig. 3e). Under a high current density of 100 mA cm⁻2, the catalyst maintained 5-day stability with a stable formate yield of 12.5 mmol, vastly exceeding that of Pt/C (Fig. 3f). Notably, the structural integrity, crystalline phase, and Pt electronic states of Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti were well-preserved after the long-term test, as evidenced by HAADF-STEM, HRTEM, XRD, and XPS analyses (Supplementary Figs. 38–41).

Mechanism investigation

In situ Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy revealed Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti initiated methanol oxidation at +0.4 V vs. RHE, which is 200 mV lower than that of Pt/C (+0.6 V vs. RHE), with intensified C–O stretch decay (1025/1022 cm–1) confirming CH3OH consumption (Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Fig. 42)4,19,49. Key formate peaks (1316, 1350, 1382, and 1580 cm–1) emerged for both the Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti and Pt/C catalysts6,50, but Pt/C exhibited overoxidation (HCO3–/CO32– at 1458/2348 cm–1) and bridge-bonded CO (*COB at 1870 cm–1), indicating a CO-mediated pathway51,52. Within the potential range of 0.4–1.1 V vs. RHE, no significant CO or CO2 signals were detected for Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti, confirming that the EMOR proceeded almost exclusively via *CHO hydroxylation. This pathway was confirmed by 18O isotopic labeling. In H218O, formate peaks (1351, 1383, and 1582 cm–1) redshifted to 1310, 1373, and 1566 cm–1, respectively, confirming that carboxylate oxygen was originated from electrolyte hydroxide (Fig. 4c). Online differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) further confirmed near-exclusive HCOOH selectivity (m/z = 46) on Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti, with negligible CO2 (m/z = 44) or CO (m/z = 28), in contrast to the dominant CO/CO2 signals on Pt/C (Fig. 4d, e)50,53,54. The favorable CO tolerance of the Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti catalyst was directly quantified by CO-stripping voltammetry. While a distinct CO oxidation peak was observed for Pt/C at ~0.63 V vs. RHE, this peak was dramatically suppressed on Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti (Supplementary Fig. 43). This stark contrast provided direct electrochemical evidence of the catalyst’s robust anti-poisoning capability, which was fully consistent with the absence of a detectable *CO signal in the in situ FTIR spectra (Fig. 4b) and the negligible CO production measured by online DEMS (Fig. 4e). Together, these independent techniques converge to confirm that the Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipole interface effectively suppresses CO formation and adsorption. The satisfactory selectivity of Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti stems from dipole-mediated control at the pivotal *CHO intermediate stage. *CHO adsorption exhibited a 12 cm–1 redshift (2721 vs. 2733 cm–1 on Pt/C; Fig. 4f)55, which is consistent with a side-on Ptδ+–OHC–Ptδ– bridging configuration. Here, the Ptδ– sites enhance d→π* backdonation to *CHO, weakening the C–H bond and reducing the vibrational frequency56. In situ Raman spectroscopy captured direct structural evidence (Supplementary Fig. 44). As the potential increased, the initial Pt–O vibration (545 cm–1) shifted to a Pt–C vibration (490 cm–1; Fig. 4g), confirming the Ptδ+–OHC–Ptδ– configuration57. Concurrently, in situ FTIR revealed a formate adsorption blueshift on Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti (Supplementary Fig. 45), indicating that weakened chemisorption due to electron-rich Ptδ–-induced electrostatic repulsion could accelerate desorption and prevent overoxidation.

DFT and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were employed to elucidate the electron transfer and reaction mechanisms of the EMOR on Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles (Supplementary Data 1). The results revealed that Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles spontaneously formed when the Pt clusters were anchored at OVs on TiOx/Ti surfaces via adjacent Pt–O and Pt–Ti coordination pairs (Supplementary Figs. 46, 47). The Ptδ+ sites exhibited the greatest CH3OH adsorption energy of –1.67 eV and the most robust charge transfer of 0.70 e⁻, surpassing the Ptδ– (–0.99 eV; 0.40 e⁻) and Pt0 (–0.82 eV; 0.60 e⁻) sites (Fig. 4h), thereby confirming that Ptδ+ is the primary Lewis acidic centre for initial CH3OH activation on Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles58.

To understand the initial methanol activation, we compared the Gibbs free energy landscapes for the competitive O–H cleavage (OHC pathway, forming *CH3O) and C–H cleavage (CHC pathway, forming *CH2OH) on both the Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti and Pt/C catalysts59 (Supplementary Figs. 48, 49). The calculations revealed that the OHC pathway was thermodynamically favored for the initial step on both surfaces. However, the subsequent Gibbs free energy diagrams (Fig. 4i) revealed a critical divergence in the fate of the *CHO intermediate, which dictated the ultimate product selectivity. On the conventional Pt(111) surface, the d-orbitals of the Pt atoms aligned well with the C–H σ* orbital of *CHO (Supplementary Figs. 50, 51)60. This effective overlap facilitated d→σ* backdonation, activating the C–H bond and leading to an elongated bond length of 1.12 Å compared with 1.11 Å on Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti (Supplementary Table 14). Consequently, *CHO dehydrogenation to *CO was favored on Pt(111) both thermodynamically (ΔG = –0.21 eV) and kinetically (energy barrier reduction = 0.20 eV) over hydroxylation (Fig. 4j, Supplementary Fig. 52), while *HCOOH desorption encounters a 0.19 eV higher barrier than overoxidation (Fig. 4i).

In contrast, on Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles, *CHO adopted a side-on Ptδ+–OHC–Ptδ– bridging configuration (Fig. 4k, Supplementary Fig. 53). Charge density difference analysis revealed an accumulation of 0.66 e⁻ on the carbon atom, indicating enhanced nucleophilicity61. The high-energy d-orbitals of the Ptδ– sites engage in symmetric resonance with the low-lying C=O π* orbital of *CHO62. The petal-shaped electron cloud of π* matches well with the d-orbital of Ptδ–, enabling intense d→π* backdonation that directly activates the C=O bond, as evidenced by an elongated C=O bond length of 1.26 Å on Ptδ+-Ptδ– versus 1.20 Å on Pt(111) (Supplementary Fig. 53). This site-selective interaction was further confirmed by PDOS and COHP analyses (Supplementary Fig. 54). Consequently, hydroxylation to HCOOH became kinetically preferred, with a barrier 0.19 eV lower than dehydrogenation (Fig. 4l), driven by Ptδ–→π* backdonation. Critically, *HCOOH desorption (–0.38 eV) was thermodynamically favored, with a barrier reduction of 0.99 eV over overoxidation (Fig. 4i) and adsorption weakened by 0.54 eV relative to Pt(111) (Fig. 4m). This dual control of kinetically steering *CHO hydroxylation and thermodynamically promoting rapid HCOOH release explained the observed 99% selectivity and suppression of *CO poisoning (Fig. 4n). However, it should be noted that the DFT and AIMD simulations in this work are based on simplified surface models, which may not fully capture the complex electrochemical environment.

Electrolyzer design and analysis

To assess industrial viability, we constructed a membrane electrode assembly (MEA) flow electrolyzer using Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti as the anode and Pt/C as the cathode (geometric area of 1 cm2), separated by a Fumasep FAB-PK-130 anion-exchange membrane with serpentine flow fields (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 55). The system achieved a current density of 100 mA cm⁻2 at a cell voltage of 0.83 V for EMOR, which was 1.06 V lower than that for oxygen evolution in methanol-free electrolyte (Fig. 5b). To rule out the competitive OER under high current density conditions, both anode potential analysis and online DEMS were employed, and the results conclusively demonstrated that the measured current originated entirely from the EMOR (Supplementary Figs. 56–58). During continuous operation at 150 mA cm–2, the anode displayed negligible activity decay over 120 h while producing H2 at 0.63 m3 h–1 m–2 with >99.9% current efficiency (Fig. 5c). This stability validates the dipoles’ ability to avoid CO-mediated deactivation through nonpoisoning pathways, enabling efficient formate synthesis.

TEA bridging laboratory and industrial scales identified electricity (44.2%) and methanol (33.7%) as the primary cost drivers (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 59)63,64. Critically, the system coproduces high-value streams of formic acid at the anode and high-purity hydrogen at the cathode, with hydrogen revenue enabling a net profit of $514.6 per ton of formic acid (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Notes 1–2). This dual-output strategy establishes a self-sustaining electrochemical value chain. Production cost mapping versus renewable electricity prices and FE confirmed the system’s viability. Even at $0.10/kWh, formic acid costs $914/ton, 26% below the market price ($1200/ton), underscoring its scalability potential (Fig. 5f)65,66.

LCA quantified the environmental advantages of electrochemical formic acid synthesis over conventional thermal processes, revealing transformative decarbonization potential. When powered by renewable electricity, the system achieves a carbon footprint <2.5 kg CO2-eq/kg formic acid, which is 51% lower than that of hard-coal thermal processes and 11% lower than that of fossil-powered alternatives (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Tables 15–16)67,68. Beyond carbon, it results in 33–88% reductions in ecosystem damage across critical metrics including acidification, resource use, and eutrophication (Fig. 5h, Supplementary Table 17)64,69. This establishes Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipole-enabled synthesis as an environmentally dominant and economically viable paradigm for sustainable chemical manufacturing.

Discussion

We demonstrate that the engineered Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles on Ti felt, where Ptδ+ bonds to lattice oxygen and Ptδ– coordinates unsaturated Ti atoms, overcome intrinsic CO poisoning and overoxidation constraints in electrochemical methanol upgrading, achieving 99% formate FE at +0.90 V vs. RHE and over 120 h of stability at 100 mA cm⁻2. This performance arises from cooperative dipole functions: electron-deficient Ptδ+ activates CH3OH and stabilizes *CHO via a side-on Ptδ+–OHC–Ptδ– bridge, while electron-rich Ptδ– directs d→π* backdonation into the π* orbital of *CHO, kinetically favoring hydroxylation over dehydrogenation and accelerating HCOOH desorption. In comparison with thermal processes, this strategy reduces the environmental impact by 88% and carbon emissions by 11%, establishing dipole engineering as a transformative paradigm for sustainable C1 electrochemistry.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

Commercial Ti felt with a thickness of 0.4 mm was supplied by Shaanxi Titanium Plutonium Vanadium Metal Co., Ltd. in China. Pt foil (0.1 mm thick, 99.99%, GaossUnion) was used as received. H2PtCl6·6H2O (≥99.95% metals basis), KOH (99.99%), ethanol (C2H5OH, 99.9%), methanol (CH3OH, 99.9%), sodium formate (NaCOOH, AR), and Pt/C (20 wt%) were all obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. H218O water (97 atom% 18O) was sourced from Meryer (Shanghai) Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., and a 5 wt% Nafion solution was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemical reagents were used as received without additional purification. Lab-scale tests were conducted using ultrapure water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ cm–1.

Electrode synthesis

Synthesis of Ptδ+ -Ptδ– /Ti

The Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti electrodes were synthesized via a droplet-coating and thermal annealing approach. Briefly, a homogeneous ethanolic solution of H2PtCl6 (12 mgPt/mL) was applied to a commercial Ti felt substrate (2 cm × 2 cm) using a precise volume of 1600 μL (with Pt loading amount of 4.8 mg cm–2). To enhance uniformity and adhesion, the coating process was assisted by infrared irradiation, which promoted rapid solvent evaporation. The coated substrate was then annealed under a reducing gas mixture (5 vol% H2/Ar) at 300 °C for 3 h (10 °C/min), yielding the final product of Ptδ+-Ptδ– dipoles anchored on the Ti felt (denoted as Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti).

Synthesis of Ptδ+/Ti and Ptδ–/Ti

To generate control samples, the as-prepared Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti was further annealed under distinct conditions—either a 5 vol% H2/Ar mixture to produce Ptδ–/Ti or ambient air to produce Ptδ+/Ti—without altering the original Pt loading.

Synthesis of Pt/C

The Pt/C catalyst ink was prepared by dispersing 96 mg of commercial 20 wt% Pt/C powder into a mixture of 8 mL ethyl alcohol and 320 µL of 5 wt% Nafion solution (with the same Pt loading as Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti). The mixture was subjected to 30 min of sonication to form a homogeneous ink, which was subsequently deposited onto a carbon paper (2 cm × 2 cm) via a drop-drying method.

Synthesis of Ti (felt)

First, a commercial Ti felt (2 cm × 2 cm) was ultrasonically cleaned in sequence with ethanol and water for 15 min to remove grease from the surface. The collected electrode was dried under an infrared lamp for later incipient wetness impregnation. After preprocessing, the obtained commercial Ti felt was treated at 300 °C in 5 vol% H2/Ar atmosphere for 180 min (10 °C/min) to obtain Ti felt.

Synthesis of Pt/CP

Commercial carbon paper (CP, 2 cm × 2 cm) was pre-treated via sequential sonication in acetone for 30 min, ethanol for 30 min, and thorough rinsing with deionized water, followed by vacuum drying. H2PtCl6 ethanolic solution was drop-coated onto the pre-cleaned carbon paper to achieve the same Pt loading with Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti.

Synthesis of Pt1/Ti

For the synthesis of Pt1/Ti, a homogeneous ethanolic solution of H2PtCl6 (3 mgPt/mL) was applied to a commercial Ti felt substrate (2 cm × 2 cm) using a precise volume of 200 μL (with Pt loading amount of 0.15 mg cm–2). The coated substrate was then annealed under a reducing gas mixture (5 vol% H2/Ar) at 300 °C for 3 h (10 °C/min), yielding the final product of Pt1/Ti.

Characterization

XRD analysis was conducted on a Rigaku Miniflex-600 diffractometer utilizing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm), operated at 40 kV and 15 mA. Morphological characterization was performed via HRTEM using an FEI TECNAI G2 F20. Elemental distribution mapping was acquired via STEM on an FEI Talos F200X microscope. HAADF-STEM utilized a JEOL JEM-ARM200F instrument, featuring spherical aberration correction and an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Elemental composition was determined via inductively ICP-MS performed on a Thermo Scientific iCAP Q. Surface chemical states were probed by XPS using a PHI 5000 Verasa scanning X-ray microprobe (ULVAC-PHI, Inc.) with Al Kα radiation; binding energies were referenced to the adventitious carbon C 1s peak at 284.6 eV. UPS characterization was conducted using a Thermo SCIENTIFIC Nexsa G2 instrument equipped with a monochromatic Al target (1486.6 eV) at 12000 V, 0.006 A and vacuum below 10⁻9 mBar. The detection of OVs was conducted on an EPR spectroscope (Bruker EMX Nano, Germany). The DEMS QAS100 was provided by Linglu Instruments (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. A typical three-electrode cell was used with the anode chamber directly linked to the mass spectrometer. Pt L3-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra were measured at the Singapore Synchrotron Light Source Center. In situ vibrational spectroscopy employed a Renishaw in Via Qontor confocal Raman microscope with a 532 nm excitation laser source. FTIR spectra were recorded at ambient temperature across the range 4000–800 cm–1 using a Nicolet iSOFT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). To capture dynamic surface species, FTIR and Raman spectra were collected during a 600 s chronoamperometry experiment. The HCOOH concentration in electrolyte was detected by a Thermo Scientific Dionex IC system.1H NMR spectra were recorded with a JEOL JNM-ECZL400S 400 MHz NMR instrument.

Electrochemical measurements

All electrochemical tests were carried out using a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation. A conventional H-type electrolytic cell was employed, with the anodic and cathodic chambers isolated by a Nafion-117 proton exchange membrane. A three-electrode system was utilized for the measurements, where the prepared EMOR catalyst constituted the working electrode (WE), while a Pt foil and a Hg/HgO electrode were designated as the counter (CE) and reference (RE) electrodes. The geometric area of the WE in contact with the electrolyte was fixed at 1 cm2 (1 cm × 1 cm). The Hg/HgO reference electrode was calibrated in high-purity hydrogen-saturated 1.0 M KOH electrolyte using a Pt foil as the working electrode via low-scan-rate cyclic voltammetry, and the zero-current potential was averaged to determine its potential versus the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). Unless otherwise stated, all potentials are reported against the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE, ERHE = EHg/HgO + 0.0591 × pH+ 0.098). To ensure an oxygen-free environment, the electrolyte was continuously saturated with high-purity Ar (99.99%) for 30 min before any electrochemical measurement. LSV scans for EMOR activity were recorded with 95% iR compensation at a scan rate of 10 mV s⁻¹, with each test repeated in triplicate; magnetic stirring was maintained at 500 rpm throughout electrolysis to ensure efficient mass transfer. Each experiment was performed using 30 mL of freshly prepared electrolyte consisting of 1.0 M KOH and 1.0 M CH3OH (pH = 13.7 ± 0.1, measured three times with a pH meter and averaged), which was made by adding 1.2 mL of pure methanol to the electrolytic cell and diluting to a total volume of 30 mL with 1.0 M KOH stock solution stored in a sealed 500 mL plastic volumetric flask at room temperature. Chronoamperometry was employed to assess EMOR selectivity by applying constant potentials within the active potential window for 60 min. The stability of the catalyst was assessed by chronopotentiometry at a fixed current density of 100 mA cm–2. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz. The FE of the EMOR was measured by chronoamperometry at 25 °C. The iR compensation and resistance measurements

To account for the potential drop due to solution resistance, iR compensation was applied in real-time via the positive feedback method. Prior to each LSV scan, the uncompensated solution resistance was determined by EIS at the open-circuit potential over a high-frequency range (100 kHz to 1 MHz). The solution resistance value is taken as the high-frequency intercept on the real axis in the Nyquist plot. During the LSV measurement, 95% iR compensation was employed, meaning that the displayed potential (E) is corrected according to: Ecorrected = Emeasured – I × R × 0.95, where I is the instantaneous current. This protocol ensures that the reported potentials closely reflect the true interfacial potential at the working electrode. Only the data in Fig. 3a was processed with 95% iR correction, and all other electrochemical data in this work are presented without iR correction.

Analysis of the products

Product quantification was carried out by ion chromatography (IC). After each electrolysis run, a 1 mL aliquot of the electrolyte was withdrawn and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, then diluted with deionized water, then transferred to a 6 mL IC vial for analysis. An anion-exchange column was used with a 25 mM KOH mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.6 mL min⁻1 and a suppressor current of 50 mA. Under these conditions, the formate peak eluted at approximately 6.1 min, and the carbonate by-product peak appeared at around 7.9 min. The concentration of products was determined from external standard calibration curves. No other liquid phase by-products were detected under the described conditions. Measurement of ECSA

The catalyst was subjected to LSV in 0.05 M H2SO4 electrolyte over a potential range of 0.1–1.6 V (vs. RHE) to record the background current curve for subsequent background subtraction. The electrode potential was then held constant at 0.3 V (vs. RHE) for 120 s in an electrolyte cell continuously purged with Ar gas. During this period, Cu2+ ions in the solution underwent underpotential deposition on the Pt surface, forming a monolayer of copper. After deposition, LSV was performed by scanning positively from 0.3 V to 1.0 V (vs. RHE). During this scan, the deposited monolayer of copper was oxidized and dissolved into Cu2+, yielding a distinct anodic oxidation peak.

CO stripping test

CO stripping measurements were carried out in 1.0 M KOH. The working electrode was polarized at −0.9 V vs. Hg/HgO while CO gas was continuously purged into the electrolyte for 30 min to saturate the catalyst surface with a CO adlayer. After that, N2 was bubbled for 15 min to expel any dissolved CO from the solution. Subsequently, cyclic voltammetry was recorded between −0.85 and 0.35 V vs. Hg/HgO at a scan rate of 10 mV s⁻1 for two full cycles.

MEA measurements

The performance of the MEA flow electrolyzer, featuring a 1 cm2 electrode area, was characterized using a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation. The AEMWE cell, supplied by Mainz Hydrogen Energy, employed a Fumasep FAB-PK-130 anion exchange membrane. The membrane has a typical thickness of 130 μm and was cut into a suitable size for cell assembly. Before use, the membrane was pretreated in NaCl solution following the standard manufacturer protocol. The anode consisted of Ptδ+-Ptδ–/Ti, while the cathode was based on a commercial Pt/C catalyst. Both electrodes were integrated with serpentine flow channels, each covering a geometric area of 1 cm2. The cell was assembled by applying a torque of 5 N•m to each of the four compression bolts. A peristaltic pump ensured continuous electrolyte circulation through both cell compartments. The cathode compartment is circulated with 1.0 M KOH, while the anode compartment uses 1.0 M KOH with 1.0 M CH3OH. Polarization data were acquired by LSV at 10 mV·s–1. For stability assessment, the electrolyzer was operated at a fixed current density of 0.15 A cm–2 at 25 °C, with the temporal evolution of the cell voltage being monitored.
