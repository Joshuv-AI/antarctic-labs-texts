Potential benefits of local radiotherapy in addition to adjuvant chemotherapy in female patients with triple-negative breast cancer in the absence of distant metastases: a SEER database analysis
Authors: Shubing Jia, Ningfeng Zhang, Haohang Luo, Long Yang, Yina Liang, Jiajing Ma, Fuhai Hui, Rongwu Xiang, Mingyi Zhao, Jingyu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is no consensus on the advisability of additional supplementation with local radiotherapy in non-metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients. The present study is the first analysis to construct a risk-scoring model to determine prognosis for TNBC without distant metastases, with a view to identifying groups for whom local radiotherapy would be of benefit.MethodsCases of female patients diagnosed with non-metastatic TNBC patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy were extracted from Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) database. Breast cancer-specific death (BCSD) was used as the primary prognostic indicator; sub-distribution hazard ratio (sHR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to analyze the effect of radiotherapy with risk-stratification. Differences were considered statistically significant if P≤0.05.ResultsA total of 19,371 patients were enrolled. Cumulative incidence function and sub-distribution hazard function showed that year of diagnosis, race, grade, lymph node (N) stage, primary site, lymph node ratio and tumor size were all associated with the probability of cancer-specific death (P<0.05). As regards BCSD, there was no survival benefit in receiving radiotherapy for low-risk (score ≤122) patients (sHR =0.839, 95% CI: 0.69–1.02, P=0.08), but radiotherapy improved survival for medium- and high-risk (score >122) groups (medium-risk: sHR =0.801, 95% CI: 0.682–0.941, P=0.007; high-risk: sHR =0.618, 95% CI: 0.516–0.74, P<0.001).ConclusionsA novel risk scoring system was constructed based on the results of the survival nomogram to screen for medium-high risk patients for whom radiotherapy may bring additional benefit.Keywords: Triple-negative breast cancer, nomogram, risk classification system, radiotherapy

Highlight box

Key findings

• A novel breast cancer-specific death-specific nomogram was established to stratify patients and pinpoint those at medium-high risk who stand to gain the most from radiotherapy.

What is known and what is new?

• Postmastectomy radiation therapy is recommended for patients at high risk of recurrence, including those with ≥4 positive axial LNs or a tumor >5 cm, independently of nodal status and resection margins. However, the benefits of radiotherapy for patients with a low risk of recurrence remain unproven, and no quantitative methods have yet been established to reliably identify those at high risk of recurrence.

• We developed a novel risk-scoring nomogram to stratify non-metastatic triple-negative breast cancer patients and assess radiotherapy efficacy across different risk levels, enabling personalized identification of those most likely to benefit.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The nomogram provides a practical tool for personalized treatment decision-making, helping clinicians move beyond traditional staging to tailor radiotherapy recommendations. Future validation in larger, more diverse cohorts is needed to confirm global applicability. Clinicians should consider integrating this model into clinical practice to optimize radiotherapy utilization and avoid overtreatment.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most frequent cancer affecting females and the second most common cause of female cancer death in the world (1). Every year, almost 276,000 women are newly diagnosed with BC and about 42,000 die from this cause in the United States alone (2). Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), defined by lack of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2) expression, is a subtype of BC with enhanced invasive capacity and accounts for 15–20% of all BC patients (3,4). TNBC carries increased risk of early metastasis, local recurrence and has poorer prognosis than other types of BC (1). A study of 15,204 women from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) centers showed that TNBC was associated with a greater risk of brain or lung metastases and had worse cancer-specific death and overall survival (OS) than other cancers (5). Therefore, global attention has focused on approaches to enhancing survival and prognosis in TNBC by multidisciplinary comprehensive treatment.

Guidelines recommend chemotherapy as an indispensable systemic treatment for TNBC. Radiotherapy, including radiosurgery or hippocampal-sparing, also provides efficacious local TNBC tumor control with minimal side effects (6). Radiotherapy is usually indicated for BC based on the Tumor, Node, Metastasis (TNM) staging and NCCN guidelines currently when more than four positive lymph nodes (LNs) are present (7). However, it is noteworthy that radiotherapy recommendations do not refer to molecular subtypes and other pathophysiological factors, such as tumor size, grade and age. Moreover, some previous studies around the use of radiotherapy in addition to chemotherapy for patients with non-metastatic TNBC have been inconclusive due to small sample sizes, insufficient chemotherapy and insufficient axillary lymph adenectomy. Overgaard et al. found that high-risk premenopausal women with BC who received post-mastectomy radiation in addition to mastectomy and adjuvant chemotherapy experienced reduced locoregional recurrences and prolonged survival (8,9). In addition, it has been noted by Fernando et al. that the advantage of administering radiotherapy synchronously with chemotherapy is the shortening of the overall duration of treatment without a substantial increase in toxicity (10). By contrast, the Danish Breast Cancer Cooperative Group demonstrated that omission of radiotherapy in TNBC patients with positive LNs did not seem to result in poorer outcome (11-14). Thus, radiotherapy for post-surgery TNBC patients undergoing chemotherapy at different pathological nodal stages remains to be evaluated, especially for those with small lesions or negative LNs.

The clinical predictive model is a quantitative tool of risk and benefit assessment which aids personalized decision-making by clinicians and patients and has advantages over the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system. To date, several studies have provided detailed descriptions of the development and validation process of prognostic models for patients with TNBC. For instance, Zhang et al. specifically developed and validated a prognostic model to predict the prognosis of liver metastasis in TNBC patients (15); Yang et al. constructed a predictive model that enabled accurate assessment of mortality risks in TNBC patients diagnosed with invasive ductal carcinoma and distant metastasis (16); Yoen et al. conducted a study to investigate the association of clinical, pathologic, and magnetic resonance imaging variables with progressive disease during neoadjuvant chemotherapy and distant metastasis-free survival in TNBC patients (17); Li et al. explored the potential value of the combined use of ultrasound features and pathological markers in predicting poor prognosis of patients with invasive TNBC, and further developed a preliminary working model for this purpose (18). Additionally, Liu et al. carried out a study to evaluate the prognostic value of clinical characteristics in elderly TNBC patients, with OS as the primary endpoint (19). Notably, most of the aforementioned studies have focused on prognostic analysis of either metastatic or elderly TNBC patients. To date, no research has been specifically conducted to explore the distinct subgroup of “female TNBC patients without distant metastasis who have received adjuvant chemotherapy” in depth, resulting in a lack of systematic analytical data analytical data on the prognostic characteristics of this specific population. Furthermore, all previous studies on TNBC models have centered on prognosis prediction, without integrating prognostic analysis into clinical treatment decision-making processes. Hence, the purpose of the current study was to construct a risk scoring system based on the survival nomogram stratifying the risk of TNBC patients without distant metastases and to compare the effect of radiotherapy at different risk levels. We presented this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1120/rc).

Methods

Data sources and data extraction

The Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) database is the most authoritative statistical cancer database and collates data from 18 registries among 14 states, representing nearly 30% of the US population. The current retrospective analysis extracted data from the publicly available SEER database, thereby avoiding the requirement for informed patient consent or ethical approval. Demographic characteristics (age at diagnosis, race, marital status), pathological features [tumor primary site, tumor laterality, tumor grade, derived AJCC TNM stage (6th), tumor size, lymph node positive rate (lnr)], follow-up information (cause of death and follow-up, SEER cause-specific death classification, months of survival) and treatment information (radiation recode, chemotherapy recode) were collated. Moreover, patient data obtained from Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University were also analyzed. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The external validation used anonymized data from previously published studies at Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University. No new patients were enrolled, and patient care was not affected. The Research Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University confirmed that ethical approval was not required.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Case lists were retrieved from the SEER 18 Regs Custom Data (additional treatment fields) and sub [1975–2016] database using SEER*Stat software (version 8.3.9.1). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) BC subtype was triple negative; (II) female unilateral TNBC diagnosed between January 2010 and December 2016; (III) pathological confirmation of primary BC as the first and only tumor; (IV) patients aged between 20 and 80 years with clinical stage I, II or III tumors without distant metastasis; (V) all patients in this study underwent surgical treatment. In early stage (I and II) TNBC patients, breast conserving surgery and mastectomy are both feasible (20,21). A recent randomized controlled trial indicated similar long-term survival rates for both surgical approaches (22). Therefore, both types of surgery were included in the current analysis; (VI) all patients received standard-of-care systemic therapy according to the treatment era. Hilton et al. [2018] noted: “Despite the importance of chemotherapy in the management of TNBC, the optimal regimen remains unknown” (23). Based on this finding, no restrictions were imposed in this study on the details of chemotherapy regimens, including drug type, dosage, and administration sequence.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) diagnosis obtained from a death certificate or autopsy; (II) patients who died within 30 days of inclusion (24); (III) incomplete follow-up information or missing data.

Definition of variables and outcomes

For cases with missing data on the covariates (grade, lnr, tumor size), we categorized them into an “unknown” group for subsequent analyses. The following classifications were applied: histological grades were re-classified into Grade 1/2, Grade 3/4 or unknown based on the degree of malignancy; tumor size was graded into ≤2, 2< size ≤3, 3< size ≤5, size >5 cm or unknown; the tumor primary sites (newsite) were outer quadrant of the breast, inner quadrant of the breast, overlapping lesions of the breast, center of the breast (including nipple site) or axillary tail of breast, according to the scope of radiotherapy; age was grouped by 20–35, 36–49, 50–64, 65–74, and 75–80 years; marital status was married or unmarried (including separated, widowed, never married, divorced or with unmarried/domestic partner); race was classified as black, white or other; lnr (an acknowledged prognostic factor for gastric, colon, breast, pancreatic, uterine and ovarian cancer survival) was the ratio of the number of positive LNs to the total number of LNs examined (25,26). LNs were divided into five groups according to the optimal cut-offs of the survival curve from Vinh-Hung et al.: no, 0< lnr ≤20%, 20%< lnr ≤65%, >65% or unknown (27); year of diagnosis was divided into 2010≤ year <2012 or 2012≤ year ≤2016 to reflect a follow-up period of more or less than 5 years.

Breast cancer-specific death (BCSD) was defined as the period from the date of diagnosis to the date of death due to BC, and OS was defined as the duration from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from any cause (including BC) or to the date of final follow-up. An identical processing methodology was employed for external validation of the Chinese cohort data.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

All eligible cases were randomly divided into a training group (TG) and a validation group (VG) in a ratio of 8:2. Cumulative incidence function (CIF) and sub-distribution (SD) Hazard Function were used to determine independent risk factors affecting prognosis for BCSD based on the TG. The sub-distribution hazard ratio (sHR), with a 95% confidence interval (CI), was taken as the key outcome indicator. The Gray residual test was applied to quantify the survival differences between each covariate group, and the prognostic results were intuitively presented using CIF curves (28). Meanwhile, the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method and multivariate Cox regression survival analysis were also performed to identify risk factors associated with the prognosis of OS based on the TG. The hazard ratio (HR) with a 95% CI was adopted as the key outcome indicator. The log-rank test was applied to quantify the probability difference of OS between covariate group, and prognostic results were presented as KM curves (29). Akaike information criterion was applied to the key risk factors for BCSD and OS and a predictive nomogram model was established. Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) value [area under the ROC curve (AUC)], decision curve analysis (DCA), and calibration curve were used to determine the predictive accuracy and discriminative ability of the nomogram. A bootstrap with 1,000 reiterations and k-fold cross validation was used for these analyses. All the above methods were implemented equally in internal and external validation cohorts for expanded verification. Risk stratification was performed using X-tile software and cohorts assigned to three different prognostic groups: low-, medium-, and high-risk, by evaluating the optimal cut-off value of the total score for each patient. Then, sHR and HR were to explore the effect of radiotherapy within each risk stratification. A C-index score of around 0.70 indicates a good model whereas a score of around 0.50 indicates random background. Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was used to verify the linear relationship between two variables and r>0.7 assumed to indicate a strong correlation. All statistical analyses were performed using R 3.6.2 software (Institute of Statistics and Mathematics, Vienna, Austria) and X-tile 3.6.1 (Robert L. Camp, Yale University, New Haven, Connecticut, USA). All statistical analyses were double-sided and P<0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Patient characteristics

The 19,371 eligible patients identified from the SEER database were randomly separated into two groups (TG: n=15,499; VG: n=3,872). The process of patient selection and study protocol for the study is shown in Figure 1. Survival outcomes of all TNBC patients without distant metastasis included 1,999 who died of BC and 389 who died of other causes (Table S1). Overall, the survival times of patients with non-metastatic TNBC who received post-surgery chemotherapy were better, with a mortality rate of only 12.33%. Age, race, marital status, tumor primary site, grade, T stage, N stage, lnr, tumor size, stage, degree of lymph node metastasis (SStage), year of diagnosis and radiation therapy all produced significant differences in survival outcomes. Demographic, clinical and pathological characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table S2. The population was predominantly 50–64 years old (43.2%), white (70.7%) and married (60.7%). Early-onset patients were slightly more likely to have cancer of the left breast than of the right (51.2% vs. 48.8%). The majority of patients had stage II BC (51.3%) as opposed to stages I (31.3%) or III (16.7%). A total of 61.8% of patients had radiation therapy and 28.6% were diagnosed before 2012.

Building and validating the prognostic nomogram

C-index value, time-dependent ROC value AUC, DCA curves, and calibration curves were used to assess nomogram discrimination. The discriminating superiority performed well for BCSD prediction (C-index: TG: 0.776; VG, 0.758). 1-, 3- and 5-year mean AUC values for BCSD based on a bootstrap with 1,000 reiterations and 10-fold cross validation were 81.1%, 78.1% and 73.5%, respectively. The C-indices of the nomogram for OS prediction were 0.767, 0.753 and 0.693 in the TG, VG and EVG, respectively. 1-, 3-, and 5-year mean AUC values for OS based on a bootstrap with 1,000 reiterations and 10-fold cross validation were 78.4%, 78.7% and 75.3%. Thus, the predictive model showed excellent sensitivity and specificity; each was superior to the TNM staging system (detailed results are shown in Table 2). In addition, compared with TNM, the nomogram DCA showed good clinical utility (Figure S2).

Performance of the nomogram in stratifying risk scores and exploration of radiotherapy impact in patients with different risk groupings

Risk scores of each independent prognostic factor for BCSD and OS are listed in Table S5 and were used to calculate total risk scores for each patient. Patients were classified as low-, medium- or high-risk according to nomogram risk scores by X-tile software. Cut-off values were 122 and 197 for BCSD and 130 and 217 for OS. Survival differences between patients receiving or not receiving radiotherapy at all risk levels were investigated (BCSD: medium-risk vs. low-risk: 3.56, 95% CI: 3.19–3.98, P<0.001; high-risk vs. low-risk: 10.50, 95% CI: 9.37–11.78, P<0.001; OS: medium-risk vs. low-risk: 3.332, 95% CI: 3.015–3.682, P<0.001; high-risk vs. low-risk: 9.722, 95% CI: 8.767–10.78, P<0.001; Figure S5). The cumulative incidence rate of death curves and the sHR over the three risk levels suggested that radiotherapy was not effective among the low-risk population (radiotherapy vs. non-radiotherapy: sHR: 0.857, 95% CI: 0.72–1.02, P=0.08), but reduced specific mortality in medium- and high-risk patients (radiotherapy vs. non-radiotherapy: sHR: 0.83, 95% CI: 0.72–0.96, P=0.01; 0.66, 95% CI: 0.56–0.78, P<0.001; Table 3). Radiotherapy was shown to improve the survival of all TNBC patients without distant metastasis (radiotherapy vs. non-radiotherapy: HR: 0.795, 95% CI: 0.672–0.941, P=0.008; 0.761, 95% CI: 0.673–0.861, P<0.001; 0.674, 95% CI: 0.58–0.783, P<0.001). Delineation of Sankey diagrams showed the correspondence between our risk stratification and AJCC staging (Figure S6).

Discussion

TNBC accounts for about 10–20% of BC and is characterized by higher invasiveness, heterogeneity and poorer outcomes than other subtypes (30-32). Whereas different treatments, such as endocrine therapy or trastuzumab, are available for other subtypes, the only current systemic treatment option for TNBC is chemotherapy (30). In addition, post-mastectomy radiation therapy (PMRT) is recommended for patients at high risk of recurrence, including those with ≥4 positive axial LNs or a tumor >5 cm, independently of nodal status and resection margins (33). However, benefits or otherwise for radiotherapy in patients with low recurrence risk of have not been established nor have quantitative methods for identification of high recurrence risk patients. As a result, controversies surrounding radiotherapy for TNBC may affect locoregional management decisions.

The current study included 19,371 female TNBC patients without distant metastasis who received surgery and chemotherapy. Patients may be classified as low-, medium- or high-risk groups by AJCC staging and results are shown in the Sankey diagrams. Our model identifies patients likely to benefit from radiotherapy and assists in making individual recommendations. The model represents an extension of the stage grouping in the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual and may be applied in clinical practice with good discrimination (BCSD: 0.776, 95% CI: 0.764–0.788; OS: 0.767, 95% CI: 0.756–0.778) and excellent calibration. The scoring system confirmed that radiotherapy is not beneficial for all TNBC patients without distant metastasis. Radiotherapy improved OS (absolute risk) in low-risk populations but not BCSD (specific risk). The mean survival time of patients suffering non-breast cancer cause-specific death (NCSD) and who did not receive radiotherapy was 25.8 months, significantly shorter than those who had radiotherapy (34.5 months). However, the mean survival time of patients who suffered BCSD and did not receive radiotherapy was 30.4 months, similar to that of those who did receive radiotherapy (32.9 months). These results raise the question of why radiotherapy seems to have an impact on NCSD and why these patients have reduced survival times. The proportion of low-risk patients who died suddenly due to non-disease causes, such as car accidents or suicide, was higher than for other groups. Indeed, NCSD patients in the low-risk group account for a high proportion of all deaths (22.1%). Excluding those who died from non-disease causes abolishes the statistical difference between radiotherapy-treated and -untreated in terms of OS (radiotherapy vs. non-radiotherapy: HR: 0.867, 95% CI: 0.729–1.031, P=0.10). Therefore, any increase in OS among low-risk patients cannot simply be interpreted as a benefit of radiotherapy.

Survival prediction nomograms for OS and BCSD of patients with non-metastatic TNBC who received post-mastectomy chemotherapy were constructed. Independent influencing factors were screened out while the influence of radiotherapy was ignored. Seven independent prognostic factors: race, grade, N stage, lnr, tumor size, primary site and year of diagnosis were associated with both BCSD and OS. Risk scores demonstrated that overall recurrence risk trends of each variable were consistent between OS and BCSD (Table S5). Firstly, higher grades of three variables, tumor size, N stage and grade, predicted higher risk of death, consistent with the clinical significance of TNM stage in the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual. Secondly, the National Surgical Breast and Bowel Surgery Project (NSABP) recommends that at least 10 axillary LNs should be assessed during prognosis and pathological staging (34). lnr is an independent prognostic factor for OS in BC patients with LN metastasis (27). In contrast to AJCC N staging, lnr reflects the quantity and the extent of lymphatic metastasis, especially for TNBC patients with fewer than two-field LN dissection or with underestimated N staging due to insufficient retrieval of positive LNs (35). A higher HR of 4.16 (95% CI: 3.24–5.36) emerged for lnr, giving this factor greater weight than N staging in our nomogram. Thirdly, in agreement with Hazrah’s results, tumor primary site was a prognostic factor for BC patients (36). Patients with overlapping breast lesions, tumors in the inner quadrant or central portion of the breast/nipple had a higher risk of cancer-specific death than patients with tumors in the outer quadrant (P<0.05). It is generally acknowledged that the adverse prognosis of BC patients with tumors in the central and inner quadrants stems from internal mammary LN metastasis. The deep anatomical location and small size of internal mammary LNs (diameter <0.5 cm) lead to inadequate staging and treatment, especially in patients with tumors located in the central region and the inner quadrant (36).

Furthermore, two demographic characteristics, age ≥75 years and unmarried status, were associated with lower probability of OS and these factors were included in the novel nomogram for OS. Previous and current studies have indicated that unmarried patients have higher incidence of cancer and higher mortality rates across various malignancies (37,38). A study of 300,000 BC patients found that OS probability in a married group was higher than that in a single/divorced/separated/widowed group (39). This might partly be attributable to married patients sharing the burden of negative emotions and receiving psychological support from their spouses (40-47). Previous studies of BC have confirmed that social support was a predictor of natural killer cell activation (48). In addition, pathological mechanisms induced by aging may cause more nutritional/metabolic diseases and disorders, such as amyotrophy, metabolism damage and neurological disease, contributing to the impediment of longevity (49). Age ≥75 years was found to be an independent factor for OS prediction. Therefore, the above independent prognostic factors may be incorporated into the evaluation of BCSD and OS.

To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to construct a scoring system to identify high risk TNBC patients without distant metastases who would gain an additional survival benefit from radiotherapy in addition to post-mastectomy chemotherapy. Our model has some unique features. Firstly, only female TNBC patients without distant metastasis receiving surgery and chemotherapy could be included to ensure homogeneity. Secondly, in addition to OS, BCSD was assessed to predict individual survival probability and avoid the influence of additional unmeasured confounders related to the patient’s state of health. Thirdly, the publication “Competing risk bias was common in KM risk estimates published in prominent medical journals” by van Walraven et al. (50). indicated that 46% of KM analyses published in prominent medical journals were susceptible to competing risk bias. Of 16 studies, approximately one third overestimated outcome risks with the KM estimate by 10% or more. We used a competitive risk model to optimize the BCSD model. Finally, many prognostic factors based on clinical significance and statistical methods were included and groupings determined by X-tile. In addition, current Chinese cases were used for external verification on the basis of internal verification with acceptable results (Table 2). However, there are still some limitations to this study. (I) As the prediction model is visualized in the form of an intuitive nomogram, all prediction variables are defined as categorical variables which reduces the accuracy of model prediction. (II) The predictive model was based on data from the SEER database and the study population was more than 80% white. Although an external validation cohort from a single center in China was employed, and the model demonstrated an acceptable predictive C-index (0.693), the validation cohort is small (n=150) and demographically distinct from the predominantly SEER cohort. These limitations may restrict the general applicability of the model. Therefore, more varied cohort studies and external data are needed to verify the global applicability of the model. (III) Specific chemotherapy and radiotherapy regimes were not discussed in detail.

Conclusions

A novel risk scoring system based on a survival nomogram was constructed to identify medium-high risk TNBC patients for whom radiotherapy may bring additional benefit. Furthermore, treatment decisions should consider potential survival benefits predicted by the BCSD and OS models, in conjunction with quality of life, economic conditions and patient specific preferences.
