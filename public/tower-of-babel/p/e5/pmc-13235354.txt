Exercise and nutritional interventions for sarcopenia-related fall prevention in older adults: An umbrella review
Authors: Dhika Dharmansyah, Laili Rahayuwati, Iqbal Pramukti, Kuswandewi Mutyara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Forty-one systematic reviews synthesize evidence on sarcopenia and fall prevention•Three-database search (Scopus, WoS, PubMed) with 2466 records identified•Two-stream synthesis: sarcopenia management and fall prevention convergence•Balance and multicomponent exercise reduces fall rates by 23–34% (high certainty)•Protein ≥1.2 g/kg/day with resistance exercise optimizes sarcopenia outcomesKeywords: Sarcopenia, Falls, Exercise, Protein supplementation, Older adults, Umbrella review

Introduction

Population aging constitutes one of the most profound demographic transformations confronting global health systems in the 21st century. Projections indicate that by mid-century, adults aged 60 years and above will comprise approximately 22% of the world's inhabitants, surpassing two billion individuals [1]. This unprecedented expansion of older populations demands heightened focus on geriatric syndromes—especially sarcopenia and fall-related injuries—that profoundly impair autonomy and wellbeing in later life.

Progressive age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass, strength, and functional capacity—collectively termed sarcopenia—affects between 10% and 27% of community-resident older populations [2]. Contemporary diagnostic frameworks established by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) prioritize muscle strength assessment as the cardinal criterion, supplemented by confirmatory evidence of diminished muscle quality or quantity and compromised physical performance [3]. The clinical ramifications of sarcopenia extend well beyond immediate functional limitations, encompassing elevated risks for hospital admission, residential care placement, and premature mortality [4,34].

Falls represent the second leading cause of unintentional injury-related death globally, with adults aged 65 years and older experiencing the highest burden [5]. Approximately 28–35% of community-dwelling older adults experience at least one fall annually, with consequences ranging from minor injuries to hip fractures, traumatic brain injury, and psychological sequelae such as fear of falling [6]. The annual global economic burden of fall-related injuries exceeds USD 50 billion, encompassing direct medical costs and indirect societal impacts [7].

Emerging evidence establishes sarcopenia as a pivotal modifiable risk factor for falls. The pathophysiological linkage involves multiple mechanisms: reduced lower limb muscle strength impairs postural stability and gait control; decreased muscle mass alters center of gravity positioning; compromised neuromuscular coordination prolongs reaction times; and diminished proprioception undermines balance recovery mechanisms [8,93]. Meta-analytic data demonstrate that sarcopenic individuals exhibit 60–89% elevated fall risk compared to non-sarcopenic counterparts, underscoring the clinical imperative to address sarcopenia within fall prevention frameworks [9,28].

Intervention strategies targeting sarcopenia encompass two primary domains: exercise-based approaches and nutritional supplementation. Exercise interventions, particularly progressive resistance training and multicomponent programs incorporating balance, strength, and aerobic elements, demonstrate efficacy in augmenting muscle mass, enhancing strength, and improving physical performance [10,11,24]. Concurrently, nutritional strategies emphasizing adequate protein intake (≥1.0–1.2 g/kg/day), leucine-enriched supplementation, vitamin D optimization, and emerging nutrients such as omega-3 fatty acids show promise for attenuating sarcopenic decline [12,13,26,65].

While numerous systematic reviews have examined exercise or nutritional interventions for either sarcopenia management or fall prevention independently, a comprehensive synthesis evaluating the convergence of these evidence streams—and whether interventions effective for sarcopenia also reduce fall risk—remains absent. Umbrella reviews, which systematically synthesize evidence from multiple systematic reviews, provide a high level of evidence synthesis and enable identification of converging evidence, knowledge gaps, and research priorities [14]. Given the proliferation of systematic reviews in gerontology and the potential for overlapping primary studies across reviews, umbrella review methodology incorporates quantitative assessment of study overlap via metrics such as the Corrected Covered Area (CCA) [15,94].

Therefore, this umbrella review aimed to synthesize and appraise the effectiveness of exercise and nutritional interventions for sarcopenia management and fall prevention in older adults, examining where these two evidence streams converge. The specific objectives were to: (1) identify and synthesize systematic reviews examining exercise interventions, nutritional supplementation, or combined strategies for sarcopenia outcomes and/or fall outcomes; (2) quantify the overlap of primary studies across included reviews using CCA methodology; (3) assess the quality of evidence using the GRADE framework; and (4) identify convergent evidence supporting integrated clinical approaches.

Material and methods

Study design and registration

This umbrella review was conducted in accordance with the methodological guidance provided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for umbrella reviews [16] and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [17]. The review protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) prior to commencing the systematic search (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/T6YQ9). An addendum to the protocol describing the use of AI-assisted tools in manuscript preparation was added post hoc and is publicly available on the OSF project page.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined using the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, and Study design (PICOS) framework:

Population: Community-dwelling or institutionalized older adults aged ≥60 years (aligned with the World Health Organization [WHO] definition for developing countries) or ≥65 years (developed countries). Although some developing contexts recognize older age onset as early as 50 years, these thresholds align with the WHO standard definition and the predominant thresholds used in the included systematic reviews. Reviews including participants with sarcopenia diagnosed using established criteria (EWGSOP, EWGSOP2, Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia [AWGS], AWGS 2019, Foundation for the National Institutes of Health [FNIH], or International Working Group on Sarcopenia [IWGS]) or those at risk of falls were eligible.

Interventions: (1) Exercise-based interventions, including but not limited to resistance training, balance exercises, multicomponent programs, Tai Chi, Pilates, aquatic exercise, or other structured physical activity; (2) Nutritional interventions, encompassing protein supplementation, leucine or branched-chain amino acids, whey protein, vitamin D, calcium, omega-3 fatty acids, oral nutritional supplements, or multinutrient formulations; (3) Combined exercise and nutritional strategies.

Comparators: Usual care, placebo, attention control, no intervention, or alternative interventions.

Outcomes: Primary outcomes included sarcopenia-related parameters (muscle mass [including appendicular skeletal muscle mass and appendicular lean mass], muscle strength, physical performance) and fall-related outcomes (fall rate, number of fallers, fear of falling, fall-related injuries). Secondary outcomes encompassed quality of life, balance measures, and gait parameters.

Study design: Systematic reviews with or without meta-analysis of interventional studies (primarily randomized controlled trials) published in peer-reviewed journals. One exception was made for Yeung et al. [9], a meta-analysis of observational studies quantifying the sarcopenia–falls association, which was included as anchor evidence to establish the conceptual linkage between the two evidence streams rather than to evaluate intervention effectiveness. Network meta-analyses were eligible. Excluded were narrative reviews, scoping reviews, rapid reviews, umbrella reviews of systematic reviews, conference abstracts, grey literature, and primary studies. Language: English, with no date restrictions applied to maximize evidence capture.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was executed across three databases—Scopus, Web of Science (WoS), and PubMed/MEDLINE—in February 2026. The use of multiple databases addresses a known limitation of single-database reliance, as Scopus, WoS, and PubMed have complementary coverage profiles: Scopus provides extensive biomedical and health sciences indexing; WoS offers superior coverage of high-impact interdisciplinary journals; and PubMed/MEDLINE provides specialized Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) indexing for biomedical literature [18]. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms and free-text keywords related to four concept domains: (1) older adults/aging population, (2) sarcopenia and falls, (3) exercise and nutritional interventions, and (4) systematic review methodology. Database-specific search syntaxes were adapted for each platform's field tags (Scopus: TITLE-ABS-KEY; WoS: TS=; PubMed: [tiab] and MeSH terms). The complete search formulae for all three databases are provided in Supplementary Table A.

No formal forward or backward citation searching was conducted, as the three-database approach substantially mitigated the risk of missing relevant reviews. To verify search comprehensiveness, a random sampling verification was performed: reference lists of the 10 most comprehensive included reviews were manually checked, confirming that no additional eligible systematic reviews were identified beyond those captured by the database search.

Selection process

All retrieved records were imported into Rayyan Intelligent Systematic Review software (https://www.rayyan.ai) [19], with duplicates removed automatically and verified manually (970 duplicates removed from 2466 total records, yielding 1489 unique records). Two independent reviewers (DD, LR) conducted title and abstract screening in blinded mode, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (IP) when consensus could not be achieved. The selection process was documented using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1). Reasons for full-text exclusion were recorded systematically using 10 predefined exclusion codes (E1–E10; see Supplementary Table B).

Data extraction

Data extraction employed a standardized form piloted on three included reviews and refined iteratively. One reviewer (DD) extracted data, with verification by a second reviewer (KM). Extracted elements comprised: (1) review characteristics (first author, publication year, journal, country, objectives); (2) methodology (databases searched, date range, number of included studies, total participants); (3) population characteristics (age, setting, sarcopenia diagnostic criteria, fall risk profile); (4) intervention details (type, duration, frequency, intensity, delivery mode); (5) comparator descriptions; (6) outcome measures and assessment tools; (7) main findings (effect sizes, confidence intervals, statistical significance); (8) review authors' conclusions; (9) funding sources and conflicts of interest.

For reviews reporting multiple interventions or outcomes, data were extracted for all relevant comparisons. When reviews included overlapping primary studies but reported different outcomes or subgroup analyses, both were retained to maximize evidence synthesis.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality of included systematic reviews was appraised independently by two reviewers (DD, LR) using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Syntheses [20]. This 11-item tool evaluates: (1) clarity of review question; (2) appropriateness of inclusion criteria; (3) adequacy of search strategy; (4) comprehensiveness of sources searched; (5) appropriateness of critical appraisal criteria; (6) independent critical appraisal by ≥2 reviewers; (7) methods to minimize data extraction errors; (8) appropriateness of methods for combining studies; (9) assessment of publication bias likelihood; (10) recommendations supported by data; (11) specific directives for new research.

Each item was rated as Yes (criterion met, score = 1), No (not met, score = 0), Unclear (insufficient information, score = 0.5), or Not Applicable (excluded from scoring). Total scores ranged 0–11, categorized as: 10–11 = high quality; 7–9 = moderate quality; 4–6 = low quality; 0–3 = very low quality [21]. Following precedent in umbrella reviews, only reviews achieving ≥7 were included in the main synthesis, ensuring moderate-to-high methodological rigor [22]. A sensitivity analysis including all reviews with JBI ≥ 4 was conducted to assess the impact of this threshold on conclusions (see Supplementary Table F). Item-level JBI appraisal scores are presented in Supplementary Table D, and per-review GRADE certainty ratings with downgrade reasons are provided in Supplementary Table E.

Assessment of overlap

The extent of primary study overlap across included systematic reviews was quantified using the Corrected Covered Area (CCA) metric [15]. Individual primary studies were identified at the DOI level using Crossref and OpenAlex application programming interfaces (APIs), enabling precise matching across reviews. CCA accounts for differences in review scope and size, calculated as: CCA = ((N − r) / (r × c − r)) × 100, where N represents total citations across all reviews (counting each primary study once per review), r denotes unique primary studies, and c indicates number of included reviews. CCA interpretation: 0–5% = slight overlap; 6–10% = moderate; 11–15% = high; >15% = very high [15]. A citation matrix documenting pairwise overlap between reviews was constructed (Supplementary Table C) to identify clustering of primary studies within intervention domains. The complete CCA calculation details are provided in Supplementary Table G.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis employed a two-stream approach aligned with JBI guidance for umbrella reviews [16]:

Stream 1 — Sarcopenia management: Systematic reviews examining exercise, nutrition, or combined interventions for sarcopenia-related outcomes (muscle mass, muscle strength, physical performance, appendicular skeletal muscle mass).

Stream 2 — Fall prevention: Systematic reviews examining exercise, nutrition, or combined interventions for fall-related outcomes (fall rate, number of fallers, balance measures).

Anchor evidence: Yeung et al. [9], as the only included meta-analysis directly quantifying the sarcopenia–falls association, served as the anchor linking these two streams.

Given heterogeneity in review scope, interventions, populations, and outcomes, meta-meta-analysis was not conducted. Within each stream, findings were narratively synthesized according to five intervention categories identified through thematic analysis: (1) Resistance and multicomponent exercise; (2) Exercise and fall prevention; (3) Nutritional supplementation; (4) Vitamin D and fall prevention; (5) Combined exercise and nutrition strategies [77]. For each category, intervention characteristics, population features, outcome measures, pooled effect estimates (when reported), consistency across reviews, and mechanistic insights were narratively synthesized. Convergent and divergent findings were highlighted. Effect sizes were not converted across metrics; where reviews reported the same intervention–outcome pair using different metrics, both are presented with the discrepancy noted.

Assessment of evidence quality

Evidence quality for key outcomes within each intervention category was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [23]. GRADE assessment considered five domains that may downgrade certainty (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias) and three that may upgrade (large magnitude of effect, dose-response gradient, residual confounding favoring null). Evidence was classified as: high (further research very unlikely to change confidence in effect estimate); moderate (further research likely to have important impact); low (further research very likely to have important impact); or very low (estimate very uncertain) [23].

Where included reviews reported GRADE assessments, these were extracted directly. For reviews without GRADE ratings, assessments were conducted by the review team based on information available within the reviews regarding risk of bias, heterogeneity (I2 statistic), sample size, and effect precision. The full GRADE assessment per review is provided in Supplementary Table E.

Results

Study selection

The systematic search identified 2466 records across three databases: Scopus (n = 876), Web of Science (n = 843), and PubMed/MEDLINE (n = 747). Following removal of 970 duplicates, 1489 unique records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 687 were excluded. The remaining 802 articles were sought for full-text retrieval and assessment. After full-text screening, 50 potentially eligible reviews were identified. Following deduplication and verification, 41 unique systematic reviews were assessed, of which 37 achieved JBI scores ≥7 and were included in the main synthesis. Four reviews with JBI scores 6.0–6.5 were included in the sensitivity analysis (JBI ≥ 4, n = 41). The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1. Characteristics of excluded reviews with reasons are provided in Supplementary Table B.

Synthesis of findings by intervention category

Stream 1: resistance and multicomponent exercise for sarcopenia outcomes

Multiple systematic reviews with meta-analysis evaluated resistance and multicomponent exercise for sarcopenia management. The most comprehensive network meta-analysis by Shen et al. [37,92] included 42 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with 3728 sarcopenic older adults (median age 72.9 years). Moderate-to-high certainty evidence demonstrated that resistance combined with aerobic and balance training yielded the largest improvements in quality of life (SMD 0.68–1.11). Moderate-certainty evidence showed that resistance combined with balance exercise yielded superior handgrip strength outcomes, with the most effective combination (resistance plus balance exercise with nutritional supplementation as adjunct) achieving the greatest gains (MD 4.19 kg, 95% CI 2.52–5.86), approaching the minimally important difference (MID) of 5.0 kg [47]—indicating a clinically meaningful trend [45]. Note: the combined exercise-nutrition arm is discussed further in Section 3.5.5.

Chen et al. [44] synthesized nine RCTs (n = 561 participants) and confirmed that resistance training significantly improved lean mass (SMD 0.58, 95% CI 0.28–0.88; moderate effect), muscle strength (SMD 0.84, 95% CI 0.53–1.14; large effect), and physical performance (SMD 0.71, 95% CI 0.41–1.01; moderate-to-large effect) in sarcopenic older adults [46].

Optimal training parameters identified across reviews comprised: frequency 2–3 sessions/week; intensity 60–80% one-repetition maximum; volume 2–3 sets of 8–12 repetitions; and duration ≥12 weeks [48,75].

Stream 2: exercise and fall prevention outcomes

The Cochrane review by Sherrington et al. [35] synthesized 108 RCTs involving community-dwelling adults aged ≥60 years. High-certainty evidence established that exercise reduces fall rate by 23% (RaR 0.77, 95% CI 0.71–0.83; number needed to treat [NNT] ≈ 15 for one fall prevented) and number of fallers by 15% (risk ratio [RR] 0.85, 95% CI 0.81–0.89) [56]. Balance and functional exercises demonstrated particular efficacy, reducing falls by 24% (RaR 0.76, 95% CI 0.70–0.81), while programs incorporating multiple exercise types (balance, strength, aerobic) reduced falls by 34% (RaR 0.66, 95% CI 0.50–0.88) [40,59].

The USPSTF evidence update by Guirguis-Blake et al. [36] reinforced these findings across 83 RCTs with 48,839 participants, reporting that exercise interventions decreased fall rate (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 0.85, 95% CI 0.80–0.91) with high-certainty evidence [55].

Within specific exercise modalities, Tai Chi emerged as an effective standalone intervention. Huang et al. [49] meta-analyzed 18 RCTs (n = 3824) and demonstrated that Tai Chi significantly reduced both the chance of falling (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.72–0.88) and rate of falls (IRR 0.69, 95% CI 0.60–0.80), with moderate-certainty evidence [50].

Nutritional supplementation for sarcopenia outcomes

Protein supplementation constituted the most rigorously studied nutrient [66,83]. Regarding protein supplementation specifically, Nunes et al. [60] synthesized 74 RCTs examining protein intake effects on lean body mass in adults aged ≥65 years. When combined with resistance exercise (see Section 3.5.5 for the combined intervention analysis), protein intake ≥1.2 g/kg/day significantly increased lean body mass (SMD 0.22, 95% CI 0.09–0.35; small effect below MID threshold; moderate certainty). Whey protein supplementation (9.6–40 g/day) significantly increased appendicular skeletal muscle mass index, total muscle mass, and gait speed (gait speed improvement contextualized against MID of 0.1 m/s) across 10 RCTs with 1154 participants (moderate certainty) [29,62,74].

Leucine-enriched protein supplementation (3–6 g/day) yielded significant muscle strength improvements in sarcopenic older adults across six RCTs (n = 699, P < 0.001), though with low-certainty evidence due to heterogeneity and risk of bias [25,61,82,85].

Vitamin D and fall prevention outcomes

Wei et al. [41] conducted the largest synthesis (38 RCTs, n = 61,350) and demonstrated that high-dose vitamin D (≥700 IU/day) significantly prevented falls (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.79–0.96; moderate certainty), while low-dose supplementation (<700 IU/day) showed no significant effect [70]. However, Torres-Lopez et al. [43] reported no significant effect of vitamin D supplementation on fall risk specifically among community-dwelling adults aged ≥65 years (moderate certainty).

Combined exercise and nutrition for sarcopenia outcomes

Five systematic reviews examined combined exercise and nutritional interventions, providing convergent evidence for synergistic effects [38,39,67,[71], [72], [73]]. Two network meta-analyses provided robust comparative evidence. Zhao et al. [38] ranked interventions across 35 RCTs (n = 2331) and identified exercise combined with nutritional supplementation as the highest-ranked strategy for muscle strength, skeletal muscle mass, and physical function (moderate certainty). Yan et al. [39] confirmed through network meta-analysis that combined exercise plus protein supplementation exhibited superior efficacy compared to exercise alone, protein alone, or control conditions (moderate certainty).

Anchor evidence: sarcopenia–falls association

One meta-analysis served as the anchor evidence linking the two evidence streams. Yeung et al. [9] synthesized 36 observational studies (n = 52,838 participants aged ≥65 years) and demonstrated that sarcopenic individuals face significantly elevated fall risk compared to non-sarcopenic counterparts (cross-sectional OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.31–1.97; prospective OR 1.89, 95% CI 1.33–2.68; moderate certainty). This association remained consistent across study designs, sexes, and sarcopenia definition criteria. As this represents a single meta-analysis of observational studies, the findings provide the conceptual rationale for integrated approaches but cannot establish that sarcopenia reversal directly reduces fall incidence—a question requiring intervention trials.

Sources of heterogeneity across included reviews

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across the 41 included systematic reviews in several key dimensions. First, population characteristics varied considerably: mean participant ages ranged from approximately 60–89 years across reviews, with some reviews restricting inclusion to specific subpopulations (e.g., community-dwelling older adults only in Chen N et al. [44]). This population diversity likely contributed to differential effect sizes, as younger-older adults (60–70 years) may respond differently to interventions compared to the oldest-old (>80 years). Second, sarcopenia diagnostic criteria differed substantially: reviews applied EWGSOP (n = 12), EWGSOP2 (n = 8), AWGS (n = 6), FNIH (n = 3), or multiple criteria (n = 8), with four reviews not specifying diagnostic standards. Given that these criteria yield different prevalence estimates and identify partially overlapping populations, cross-review comparisons of sarcopenia outcomes must be interpreted cautiously. Third, intervention parameters showed wide variation: exercise intervention durations ranged from 4 to 52 weeks across reviews, with training frequencies of 1–7 sessions per week and intensities from low (body weight exercises) to high (80% 1-RM). Similarly, protein supplementation doses ranged from 3 g/day (leucine) to 40 g/day (whey protein), with considerable variation in formulation and timing. Fourth, study design heterogeneity was notable: the review pool comprised conventional systematic reviews with meta-analysis (n = 30), network meta-analyses (n = 7), and systematic reviews without quantitative synthesis (n = 4), each employing different analytical approaches that limit direct comparability of effect estimates. Fifth, outcome measurement tools varied across reviews even for ostensibly similar outcomes: muscle mass was assessed via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, bioelectrical impedance analysis, or computed tomography; physical performance was measured using TUG, SPPB, gait speed, or chair stand tests; and fall outcomes were operationalized as fall rate, number of fallers, fear of falling, or balance measures. This measurement heterogeneity precluded meta-meta-analysis and necessitated the narrative synthesis approach adopted in this review.

High-certainty evidence supported balance and functional exercises for fall prevention (RaR 0.76), pooled exercise interventions for fall rate reduction (IRR 0.85), and resistance combined with aerobic and balance training for quality of life improvement in sarcopenia (SMD 0.68–1.11; from Shen et al. Discussion

Two-stream evidence synthesis

This umbrella review synthesized 37 systematic reviews in the main analysis (41 in sensitivity analysis) encompassing approximately 4475 unique primary studies, evaluating exercise and nutritional interventions across two convergent evidence streams: sarcopenia management and fall prevention in older adults. The slight overlap (CCA 0.66%) ensures that synthesized evidence derives from largely independent review contributions. However, substantial heterogeneity across reviews in population characteristics, sarcopenia diagnostic criteria, intervention parameters, and outcome measurement tools necessitated careful interpretation, as detailed in Section 4.8.

Resistance and multicomponent exercise

Resistance and multicomponent exercise constitute cornerstone interventions for sarcopenia management (Stream 1). High-to-moderate certainty evidence from the network meta-analysis by Shen et al. [37], involving 42 RCTs with 3728 participants, established that resistance combined with aerobic and balance training optimally improves quality of life in sarcopenic older adults (SMD 0.68–1.11). The handgrip strength improvement (MD 4.19 kg) approaches clinical significance thresholds (MID 5.0 kg [47]), suggesting that longer or more intensive programs may achieve fully clinically meaningful gains.

These findings align with EWGSOP2 recommendations advocating progressive resistance exercise at moderate-to-high intensity (60–80% one-repetition maximum) performed 2–3 times weekly. The consistency of findings across reviews employing different analytical approaches (conventional meta-analysis vs. network meta-analysis) and varying sarcopenia diagnostic criteria strengthens confidence in these recommendations. However, heterogeneity in training protocols across reviews—particularly regarding exercise progression algorithms, supervision intensity, and adaptation to individual functional capacity—represents a gap that limits the precision of prescriptive guidelines. Future reviews should adopt standardized exercise reporting frameworks (e.g., CERT checklist) to facilitate more granular dose-response analysis.

Exercise and fall prevention

Exercise-based fall prevention interventions (Stream 2) demonstrate the strongest and most extensive evidence base. The Cochrane review by Sherrington et al. [35], synthesizing 108 RCTs, provided high-certainty evidence that exercise reduces fall rate by 23% and fallers by 15%. These robust effect sizes translate to substantial public health impact, with modeling studies estimating that widespread implementation could prevent millions of falls annually [33,54,76,79].

Tai Chi, characterized by slow, controlled movements emphasizing weight shifting and postural transitions, demonstrated moderate-certainty evidence for fall prevention [78]. Accessibility, low cost, and community acceptability position Tai Chi as a scalable intervention, particularly in Asian populations where cultural familiarity facilitates uptake [32]. The differential effectiveness of exercise modalities for fall prevention likely reflects biomechanical specificity: balance-focused exercises directly target the postural control mechanisms implicated in fall risk, while resistance training primarily addresses the muscle strength component [52]. This mechanistic distinction may explain why multicomponent programs incorporating both modalities consistently outperformed single-modality interventions across reviews, despite heterogeneity in program design and delivery settings [53].

Nutritional supplementation

Protein supplementation combined with resistance exercise yielded moderate-certainty evidence for lean body mass gains (SMD 0.22) at doses ≥1.2 g/kg/day for sarcopenia outcomes. This threshold exceeds the recommended dietary allowance of 0.8 g/kg/day, reflecting emerging consensus that older adults require higher protein intakes to offset age-related anabolic resistance [68,80,81,86]. The heterogeneity in protein source (whey, soy, casein, plant-based blends), supplementation timing (pre-exercise, post-exercise, between meals), and participant baseline nutritional status across reviews may explain the variable effect magnitudes reported. Notably, reviews focusing specifically on whey protein reported larger effect sizes for muscle mass outcomes compared to reviews examining mixed protein sources, potentially reflecting the superior leucine content and faster absorption kinetics of whey protein [30,64,91]. Whether these differential effects translate to meaningful differences in functional outcomes and fall risk warrants investigation [27,31,84].

Vitamin D and fall prevention

Vitamin D supplementation at doses ≥700 IU/day demonstrated moderate-certainty evidence for fall prevention (13% risk reduction) for fall outcomes in older adults, consistent with meta-analytic findings from the International Osteoporosis Foundation [87,88]. The absence of significant effect in community-dwelling populations reported by Torres-Lopez et al. [43] suggests that vitamin D benefits may be confined to deficient populations (serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D < 50 nmol/L) or institutionalized settings where baseline deficiency prevalence is higher [89]. Daily administration appears superior to intermittent high-dose strategies for fall prevention outcomes [69].

Combined exercise and nutrition

Convergent evidence from five systematic reviews, including two network meta-analyses, establishes combined exercise plus nutrition as the most effective approach for sarcopenia outcomes (moderate certainty). Network meta-analysis rankings consistently positioned combined strategies at the top of treatment hierarchies for muscle strength, skeletal muscle mass, and physical function [38,39].

Sarcopenia–falls association: conceptual foundation

The meta-analysis by Yeung et al. [9] quantifying the sarcopenia–falls association (OR 1.60–1.89, moderate certainty) provides the conceptual rationale for examining whether interventions effective for sarcopenia may also benefit fall prevention. This association persisted across diverse sarcopenia definitions and study designs, supporting robustness. However, this represents a single meta-analysis of observational studies within this umbrella review, and the finding should be interpreted as foundational rather than definitive. Intervention studies demonstrating that sarcopenia reversal reduces fall risk would provide causal evidence, though such trials remain limited and represent a critical evidence gap.

Sources of heterogeneity and knowledge gaps

The heterogeneity observed across included meta-analyses warrants deeper examination, as it reflects both methodological and substantive sources of variation that have implications for evidence interpretation and future research design. Several factors may explain the inconsistent effect sizes observed across reviews evaluating similar interventions. Population-level differences represent a primary source: reviews including exclusively sarcopenic individuals (diagnosed by EWGSOP2 or AWGS criteria) consistently reported larger exercise-related effect sizes for muscle mass and strength outcomes compared to reviews including mixed populations of at-risk or pre-sarcopenic older adults. This pattern suggests that individuals with confirmed sarcopenia may have greater capacity for intervention-induced improvement, though it may also reflect floor effects in healthier populations. The choice of sarcopenia diagnostic criteria also introduced systematic variation, as EWGSOP2 emphasizes low muscle strength as the primary criterion whereas AWGS retains muscle mass as an equally weighted parameter, potentially selecting different subpopulations for intervention.

Intervention-level heterogeneity further complicates synthesis. For exercise interventions, the optimal dose-response relationship remains incompletely characterized: while most reviews converge on 2–3 sessions per week at 60–80% 1-RM for resistance training, the relative contributions of training volume, intensity, frequency, and duration to sarcopenia outcomes versus fall prevention outcomes may differ. Reviews examining multicomponent exercise programs consistently reported broader benefits across both evidence streams compared to single-modality reviews, yet the specific exercise combinations and sequencing that optimize both sarcopenia and fall prevention outcomes simultaneously remain undefined. For nutritional interventions, the protein source (whey vs. soy vs. casein vs. plant-based), timing relative to exercise, and interaction with baseline nutritional status represent unresolved sources of between-review variation. The vitamin D literature exhibited particularly notable inconsistency, with contrasting findings likely attributable to differences in baseline vitamin D status, supplementation dose and frequency (daily vs. bolus), and outcome operationalization across reviews.

Several critical knowledge gaps emerge from this synthesis. First, no included review specifically examined whether sarcopenia reversal (defined as transition from sarcopenic to non-sarcopenic status) directly reduces fall incidence—this represents the most important evidence gap linking the two evidence streams. Second, the interaction between exercise and nutritional interventions remains incompletely characterized: while combined approaches consistently outperform monotherapy, the optimal sequencing, timing, and dose ratios are unknown. Third, evidence on long-term sustainability beyond 12 months is virtually absent, limiting understanding of whether intervention-induced improvements in muscle mass and function persist after program cessation. Fourth, populations from low- and middle-income countries are substantially underrepresented, with most primary studies originating from East Asia, Europe, and North America—limiting generalizability to diverse cultural and nutritional contexts. Fifth, sex-specific responses to interventions are inadequately characterized, as most reviews either did not stratify by sex or were underpowered for subgroup analysis, despite emerging evidence suggesting differential responses to resistance training and protein supplementation between older men and women.

Strengths and limitations

This umbrella review adhered rigorously to JBI methodology and PRISMA 2020 guidelines, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. Prospective protocol registration on OSF minimized selective reporting bias. The three-database search (Scopus, WoS, PubMed) substantially expanded coverage beyond the original single-database approach. The slight overlap (CCA 0.66%) confirms that synthesized evidence derives from largely independent review contributions. Comprehensive quality appraisal using validated JBI tools and GRADE assessment of evidence certainty with explicit downgrade reasons provide transparent frameworks for interpreting recommendation strength. The sensitivity analysis (JBI ≥ 7 vs. ≥4) demonstrates robustness of findings to quality threshold selection. The inclusion of recent reviews (44% published 2024–2026) ensures contemporary relevance.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, heterogeneity in sarcopenia diagnostic criteria across included reviews (EWGSOP, EWGSOP2, AWGS, FNIH, IWGS) complicates direct comparability, though sensitivity analyses within individual reviews found consistent associations regardless of definition. Second, fall outcome operationalization varied (fall rate, fallers, fear of falling, balance measures), introducing measurement heterogeneity that limits the ability to assess falls as a unified outcome; future umbrella reviews should consider restricting fall outcomes to objectively verified fall incidence (e.g., prospective fall diaries, electronic monitoring), as self-reported falls, fear of falling, and balance measures represent distinct constructs with varying validity. Third, the sarcopenia–falls link is supported by only one included meta-analysis of observational studies (Yeung et al. [9]), precluding umbrella review–level conclusions about this specific relationship. Fourth, most reviews reported short-to-medium follow-up durations (≤12 months), limiting assessment of long-term sustainability. Fifth, language restriction to English may introduce publication bias. Sixth, evidence quality was predominantly moderate or low, indicating that future high-quality RCTs could substantially impact effect estimates. Seventh, generalizability to diverse populations merits consideration, as most included reviews predominantly synthesized studies from high-income countries. Eighth, the age threshold of ≥60 years may miss relevant studies in sub-Saharan African contexts where older age onset is sometimes recognized earlier.

Conclusions

This umbrella review synthesized 37 systematic reviews (41 in sensitivity analysis) encompassing approximately 4475 unique primary studies, providing convergent evidence from two complementary research streams—sarcopenia management and fall prevention—in older adults. High-certainty evidence supports balance-focused exercise for fall rate reduction, while moderate-certainty evidence endorses combined exercise plus nutrition for sarcopenia parameter improvement. The sarcopenia–falls association (OR 1.60–1.89) reported by Yeung et al. [9] provides the conceptual rationale for integrated approaches targeting sarcopenia within fall prevention frameworks, though direct evidence that sarcopenia reversal reduces fall incidence is limited to one meta-analysis of observational studies and requires further investigation through intervention trials.

Clinical implementation should prioritize multicomponent programs combining progressive resistance and balance training with optimized protein intake (≥1.2 g/kg/day) and vitamin D supplementation (700–1000 IU/day), delivered through multidisciplinary care pathways individualized according to sarcopenia severity, functional status, and nutritional needs. Future research must prioritize intervention trials directly targeting falls in sarcopenic populations, alongside long-term sustainability assessment, dose-response optimization, and implementation studies across diverse populations and settings.
