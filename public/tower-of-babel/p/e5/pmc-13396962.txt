Proteomic alterations in patient bone-derived stromal cells and their secretomes in osteogenesis imperfecta
Authors: Kishore Garapati, Dong-Gi Mun, Rex Devasahayam Arokia Balaya, Mayank Saraswat, Santosh Renuse, Gregory Trahan, Richard K. Kandasamy, David R. Deyle, Akhilesh Pandey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Type I collagen is the most abundant form of collagen and forms the organic component of bone. Pathogenic variants in genes encoding its constituent polypeptide chains, COL1A1 and COL1A2, can result in autosomal dominant osteogenesis imperfecta and other connective tissue disorders. Although osteogenesis imperfecta is clinically well-described, the molecular basis of clinical heterogeneity among patients is not well understood. We undertook a global proteomic approach to uncover alterations in osteogenesis imperfecta patient bone-derived stromal cells as well as their secretome. We performed multiplexed tandem mass tag (TMT)-based proteomics analysis of cells from three patients along with three controls to investigate global changes in the proteome of these cells as well as their secretome. This was combined with analysis of proline hydroxylation to catalog the modification of type I collagens in this disorder. We observed significant changes in both the cellular and secreted proteomes including the levels of proteins involved in osteoblast proliferation such as nitric oxide synthase-interacting protein (NOSIP), secreted frizzled-related protein 1 (SRFP1) and transforming growth factor beta-1-induced transcript 1 protein (TGFB1I1) as well as others involved in endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis such as protein disulfide isomerase A6 (PDIA6) and reticulophagy regulator 3 (RETREG3). Notably, a number of alterations were observed in proline hydroxylation in intracellular collagens – COL1A1 and COL1A2. These findings expand our current understanding of the cellular pathophysiology in osteogenesis imperfecta and could lead to the identification of novel therapeutic targets.

Introduction

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a heterogeneous and complex group of bone disorders that have traditionally been defined and classified clinically [1]. Of the ∼20 clinically defined types of osteogenesis imperfecta, Sillence types I-IV are caused by autosomal dominant pathogenic variants in genes encoding collagen chains alpha-1(I) or − 2(I) (COL1A1 and COL1A2, respectively) and account for ∼85% of all OI cases [2]. OI is characterized by short stature, skeletal deformities and bone fragility leading to frequent fractures. In addition to bone, connective tissue in other organ systems is affected leading to dentinogenesis imperfecta, blue sclerae, hearing loss and heart and respiratory defects. There is considerable phenotypic and biochemical variability among OI patients, even those with the same causal genotype. This presents a major hurdle in the identification of potential therapeutic targets. Pharmacologic interventions have focused on increasing bone mass; however, to date there is no therapeutic cure for OI [3].

Over 90% of the organic content of bones is type I collagen, a fibrillar protein that exists as triple helices of COL1A1 and COL1A2. The helical regions are rich in glycine, lysine and proline with extensive hydroxylation of proline residues. The amino acid composition and modifications are critical for the cross-linking of collagen and its structural properties. Most frequently, causal variants of autosomal dominant OI result in substitution of glycines that occur at every third residue along the helical regions by an amino acid with a bulky or charged side chain [4]. These substitutions disrupt collagen folding and structure, leading to excessive modification of collagen prior to secretion. This results in collagen that is weak and inadequately cross-linked, which in turn affects bone mineral quality. Alterations in collagen synthesis in OI affect many cellular processes including protein synthesis and quality control, cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation. Further, cell responses to growth factors and other signaling molecules are altered owing to defective extracellular matrix composition, affecting their function and viability [3]. For example, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-beta) signaling is upregulated in OI and is associated with high osteoclast activity and reduced osteoblast differentiation [5].

To unravel the molecular changes associated with altered intracellular processes in OI, we analyzed the cellular proteome of OI patient bone-derived stromal cells. We used a tandem mass tag (TMT)-based approach for a quantitative analysis of proteomic alterations in OI cells as compared to controls. Further, we examined the secretome of these cells to study changes in cytokines, growth factors and extracellular matrix proteins. Finally, we also analyzed post-translational modifications of the collagen type I chains that are affected in these cells.

Materials and methods

Study approval

This study was approved by Mayo Clinic IRB 21–012890.

Cell culture

Patient and control primary bone-derived stromal cells were obtained from discarded bone chips as previously described [6], [7]. Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco 11965092) with 15% fetal bovine serum (Gibco A5670701) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich P4458). All cells were maintained at passage 5 to minimize variability. Cells were cultured up to ∼80% confluence and incubated for 24 h with 200 mM ascorbic acid to upregulate collagen expression (Sigma-Aldrich A4544) [8]. They were washed thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco 10010023) and treated with serum-free medium for 12 h prior to harvesting. When ready, the serum-free medium was aspirated and collected. Cells were collected by scraping in PBS.

Sample preparation

Cell pellets and supernatant media were processed for protein extraction and digestion. Cell pellets were lysed in 8 M urea, 50 mM TEAB buffer, pH 7.4 and sonicated with a tip sonicator at 40% amplitude for 3 cycles of 10 s each. The samples were spun at 21,000 ×g for 10 min and the supernatants were collected as protein extracts. Protein estimation was done by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, and equal amounts of protein were taken from each condition. Proteins were reduced with dithiothreitol (DTT, Sigma, D9163-5G, final concentration of 10 mM) and alkylated with iodoacetamide (IAA, Sigma, I1149-100G, final concentration of 40 mM). Digestion was carried out overnight with sequencing grade trypsin (Worthington, LS003744) added at a ratio of 20:1 (w/w, total protein:trypsin) after diluting the sample 10× to bring the concentration of urea to <1 M. The resulting peptides were cleaned up using C18 cartridge (Glygen, TT2C18) and labeled with tandem mass tag 6-plex (TMT 6-plex) reagents as per the manufacturer's protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A44520). A small aliquot was taken from each sample and pooled, and a label check was done by mass spectrometry. Peptide samples were normalized based on the label check and all labeled samples were pooled. The pooled TMT-labeled peptides were cleaned up by C18 cartridge (Glygen TT2C18) and fractionated by basic pH reversed-phase liquid chromatography (bRPLC) operated by Dionex Ultimate 3000 UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Separation was performed on a C18 column (Waters, Xbridge 4.6 mm × 50 cm mm) over a 120 min gradient using solvent A (20 mM ammonium formate in water) and solvent B (20 mM ammonium formate in 80% acetonitrile). A total of 96 fractions were collected using a gradient from 2 to 40% solvent B and concatenated into 24 fractions. The fractions were dried and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid for liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis for total proteomics. In a parallel experiment, supernatant media were first filtered through a 0.22 mm filter to remove residual floating cells or cell debris, followed by concentration of proteins using a 10 kDa centrifugal filter (Millipore UFC8010) before being processed in the same manner as cell lysates as described above.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Twenty-four fractions from each experiment were analyzed on an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Ultimate 3000 nano HPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were loaded onto a trap column (PepMap, C18, 20 mm × 75 μm) and separated over 150 min on an analytical column (PepMap, C18, 50 cm × 75 μm) using a mobile phase consisting of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. The gradient used for separation was as follows: 3% to 28% solvent B over 125 min, 28% to 40% solvent B over 10 min, 40% to 80% solvent B over 5 min followed by a hold at 80% solvent B for 10 min. Mass spectrometry data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode with cycle time of 3 s. Full MS scans were monitored from 370 to 1700 m/z at a resolution of 60,000. Precursor ions with charge states from +2 to +7 were selected for MS/MS with an isolation window of 0.7 m/z, and fragmented by higher-energy collisional dissociation at normalized collision energy of 34%. Fragment ions were analyzed at a resolution of 30,000.

Database searching and data analysis

Mass spectrometry data were searched using SEQUEST in Proteome Discoverer (version 2.5) against the UniProt Human protein database 20,432 entries, release 2020_05). Cleavage specificity was set to fully tryptic with up to 2 missed cleavages and precursor and fragment tolerance were set to 10 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modifications and protein N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation were set as variable modifications. In addition, oxidation of proline and lysine, modification of lysine to allysine, deamidation of glutamine and asparagine and hexose and dihexose modifications of oxidized lysine were set as variable modifications. TMT 6-plex at lysine and peptide N-terminus were also set as variable modifications. Data were analyzed for individual proteins and peptides by comparing the average fold-changes for each (average abundance values in OI samples)/(average abundance values in control samples) and statistical analysis was performed by Student's t-test.

Results and discussion

Bone-derived stromal cells from three individuals with osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) (Sillence types III and IV) were included. Three controls from anonymous donors were included for comparison. Cellular proteomic alterations

Analysis of the proteins harvested from the cells led to the identification of 5419 proteins (Table S1). In order to identify if there were systemic proteomic alterations between OI patient and control bone-derived stromal cells, principal component analysis (PCA) was carried out using TMT-based quantitation of proteins across samples. As shown in Fig. 2A, the PCA plot showed a clear separation between the two sample groups. Statistical analysis revealed that 893 proteins were significantly altered between the patient and control samples (p < 0.05). Of these, 67 proteins were significantly increased in OI cases whereas 826 proteins were significantly decreased. The distribution of selected proteins between samples is shown as box plots in Fig. 2D. Transforming growth factor beta-1-induced transcript 1 protein (TGFB1I1), was among the most significantly increased proteins (average fold-change (OI/controls) = 1.67, p-value = 0.006). This increase likely reflects enhanced transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-beta) signaling. Importantly, excessive TGF-beta matrix-cell signaling is recognized as a primary mechanism in OI pathogenesis via activation of SMAD-dependent signaling with abnormal bone remodeling. Further, anti-TGF-beta treatment has been shown to improve bone phenotype in OI with fresolimumab, a TGF-beta neutralizing antibody, showing promising results as a disease-specific therapy for OI in a phase I study [5], [9]. Nitric oxide synthase-interacting protein was also increased significantly (NOSIP, fold-change = 1.4, p-value = 0.001). This protein is known to inhibit endothelial nitric oxide synthase activity thereby regulating nitric oxide production [10]. Nitric oxide has biphasic effects on osteoblast survival by stimulating osteoblast replication and improving survival while inducing apoptosis at high levels [11]. Thus, high levels of NOSIP may indicate protective negative regulation of nitric oxide production in OI. Another protein with significantly increased levels was multivesicular body protein 2b (CHMP2B, fold-change = 1.48, p-value = 0.0017). CHMP2B is an essential component of the ESCRT-III (endosomal sorting complex required for transport-III) complex and is involved in the formation of multivesicular bodies and quality control of procollagen, promoting the degradation of misfolded collagen [12], [13]. Its increased levels likely indicate altered turnover of collagen in OI. Another significantly elevated protein was spartin (SPART, fold-change = 1.33, p-value = 0.001). Spartin is involved in lipophagy and plays a role in the transfer of different classes of lipids including triglycerides and the delivery of lipid droplets to autophagosomes [14], [15]. Lipid droplets and their triglyceride content have previously been shown to be increased in an OI mouse model [16]. The increased levels of spartin in our data indicate likely alterations in lipid metabolism and lipophagy in OI (Fig. 2D).

Among the significantly decreasing proteins in OI cells was 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (PGD, fold-change = 0.7, p-value = 0.003). Part of the pentose phosphate pathway, PGD has an important role in cellular energy and carbohydrate metabolism. Importantly, decreased levels of PGD are associated with altered glutathione levels and ER dilation, which are themselves associated with decreased protein secretion [17]. The retention of improperly folded collagen in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) in OI is known to trigger pathways involved in managing the resulting cellular stress [3]. Consistent with this, several proteins involved in the ER stress response were altered. For example, protein disulfide isomerase A6 was also decreased significantly in OI samples (PDIA6, fold-change = 0.7, p-value = 0.006, Fig. 2D). In addition to being implicated in collagen biosynthesis and folding [18], PDIA6 is known to negatively regulate the ER stress response, and reduced levels indicate a likely active or exaggerated response to ER stress [3], [19]. Reticulophagy regulator 3, which is involved in quality control of collagen and aids in the lysosomal degradation of misfolded proteins, was decreased in OI cells (RETREG3, fold-change = 0.7, p-value = 0.003) [20]. Cullin-7, a component of RING ligase complexes, was found to be significantly decreased in OI (CUL7, fold-change = 0.6, p-value = 0.0006) [21]. CUL7 is involved in the degradation of misfolded proteins and ER quality control [22]. Other significantly altered proteins include cytoskeletal proteins such as tubulin chains beta-3 (TUBB3, fold-change = 0.7, p-value = 0.002), alpha-1 A (TUBA1A, fold-change = 0.7, p-value = 0.03) and dystrophin (DMD, fold-change = 0.6, p-value = 0.04) (see Fig. 2D and Table S1).

A total of 1813 proteins were identified from the secretome (Table S2). We first carried out PCA to identify if there were global changes in the secreted proteome of OI patient-derived cells in comparison with controls. As shown in Fig. 3A, the PCA plot showed clear separation of two controls from OI samples whereas one of the control lines clustered along with patient-derived samples (Fig. 3A). Statistical analysis revealed that 58 proteins were significantly altered between the patient and control samples (p < 0.05). Among these, 30 proteins were significantly increased in OI samples while 28 proteins were significantly decreased. A volcano plot depicting these changes is shown in Fig. 3B. The levels of the extracellular enzyme, sulfatase-1 (SULF1) were increased in patient samples compared to controls (average fold-change (OI/controls) = 2.7, p-value = 0.02). This membrane-bound enzyme catalyzes the removal of sulfate from proteoglycans and its expression correlates with collagen aggregation [23]. Interestingly, its deficiency is itself associated with a bone developmental disorder, mesomelic chondrodysplasia [24], [25]. Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 was significantly increased in the secreted proteome of OI patient-derived cells (PCSK9, fold-change = 2.0, p-value = 0.009). PCSK9 prevents the recycling of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptors, thereby decreasing LDL-cholesterol clearance in plasma [26]. PCSK9 inhibitory agents have bone-protective effects through improving hyperlipidemia and anti-inflammatory effects [27]. Inhibin beta A chain was also significantly higher in OI samples compared to controls (INHBA, fold-change = 2.4, p-value = 0.002). A member of the TGF-beta superfamily, INHBA is involved in several processes including cell proliferation and migration along with extracellular matrix [28]. Chemokine-like protein TAFA-5, which is considered a neurokine associated with improved outcomes in cerebrovascular diseases, was found to be significantly increased (TAFA5, fold-change = 1.9, p-value = 0.02) [29]. Interestingly, this chemokine also has a role in inhibiting osteoclast formation, and its high levels may indicate that this protein may also be involved in altered dynamics of osteoclastic bone resorption in OI [30].

Among the significantly decreased proteins was laminin subunit alpha 1 (LAMA1, fold-change = 0.6, p-value = 0.002). LAMA1 is an extracellular matrix protein that is essential for the assembly of basement membranes [31]. Laminins are also involved in osteogenic differentiation via multiple signaling receptors [32]. Ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase family member 1 was found to be of significantly lower levels in the secreted proteome of OI patient-derived cells compared to controls (ENPP1, fold-change = 0.3, p-value = 0.008). ENPP1 generates pyrophosphate through its catalytic activity and is a regulator of bone mineralization [33]. ENPP1 deficiency is itself associated with hypophosphatemic rickets and early onset osteoporosis [34]. It is likely that decreased levels of secreted ENPP1 that are observed here are related to altered bone mineralization status in OI [35]. Carboxypeptidase X, M14 family member 2, a secreted protein thought to be involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, was also found to be decreased significantly (CPXM2, fold-change = 0.65, p-value = 0.006). This protein has associations with cardiac disorders [36] and osteosarcoma [37], as well as with a rare bone disorder of uncertain etiology, fibrogenesis imperfecta ossium [38]. Secreted frizzled-related protein 1, which an antagonist of Wnt signaling, was found to be decreased in the secretory proteome of OI bone-derived stromal cells (SRFP1, fold-change = 0.3, p-value = 0.02). Overexpression of SRFP1 inhibits osteoblast maturation and function [39]. Decreased levels of secretion is likely related to alterations in bone homeostasis in OI. Signal peptide, CUB and EGF-like domain-containing protein 3 was also significantly decreased in the secretome of OI cells (SCUBE3, fold-change = 0.33, p-value = 0.009). It is a co-receptor for bone morphogenetic protein BMP2 involved in osteogenic differentiation and is required for proper bone development [40], [41]. Overall, these changes in the secreted proteome clearly indicate that the cell-cell communication and signaling milieu related to osteoblast and osteoclast activity are altered in OI. Notably, many of these proteomics alterations, although related to bone function, have not been described previously in the context of OI.

Cellular collagen is elevated

COL1A1 and COL1A2 were identified in both the cellular and secretory proteome experiments. COL1A1 was identified by 77 unique peptides in the cellular proteome and by 112 unique peptides in the secretory proteome. Similarly, COL1A2 was identified by 63 unique peptides in the cellular proteome and 105 unique peptides in the secretory proteome. These numbers likely reflect increased collagen secretion and abundance in the secretory proteome related to ascorbic acid supplementation. In bone-derived stromal cells from patients with pathogenic variants in COL1A1, we observed a small but significant increase in COL1A1 levels in the cellular proteome suggesting that collagen molecules accumulate intracellularly in OI (fold-change = 1.26, p-value = 0.01). Next, we examined post-translational modifications of collagens. In the cell lysate, a total of 58 peptides were identified from COL1A1 with proline oxidation. Similarly in the cell lysate, 36 peptides were identified from COL1A2 with proline oxidation. On the other hand, in the cell culture supernatant medium, 111 identified from COL1A1 with proline oxidation and three peptides were identified with lysine modified with hexose. Likewise in the supernatant medium, 93 peptides were identified from COL1A2 with proline oxidation. To identify quantitative alterations in proline hydroxylation relative to total protein level, we first normalized the abundance of COL1A1 and COL1A2-derived peptides to the abundance of their parent proteins. Volcano plots of these normalized levels of COL1A1- and COL1A2-derived peptides in the cell lysate samples are shown in Fig. 4A and B, respectively. Unmodified peptides and peptides with proline hydroxylation are represented with color-coded dots as shown, with the horizontal dashed line representing a p-value of 0.05. Volcano plots showing levels of different modified COL1A1- and COL1A2-derived peptides in the cell culture supernatant samples are shown in Fig. 4C and D, respectively. As indicated, subtle changes were observed at the peptide-level in both collagens. Importantly, a higher number of statistically significant alterations in peptides modified by proline oxidation, both from COL1A1 and COL1A2, were identified in the cell lysate compared to the cell culture supernatant. Conclusions

OI due to autosomal dominant pathogenic variants in COL1A1 and COL1A2 is well-described clinically. Although molecular alterations in OI have been well-studied at the level of protein-level changes in the encoded collagen, global proteomic alterations in bone-derived stromal cells and their culture supernatants have not been described in detail so far. Further, although advanced mass spectrometry technologies are available, they have not been previously applied to study how post-translational modification of collagen is altered in OI patient-derived cells and in their secreted proteome. With the goal of characterizing alterations in the proteome and post-translational modifications of collagen chains in collagen-expressing cells, we analyzed bone-derived stromal cells from OI patients in comparison with controls. Through our global proteomic analysis, we identified a number of significant changes in known proteins associated with OI as well as changes that could help fuel further discovery into the pathology and possible novel therapeutic approaches in OI. Our analysis also identified alterations in post-translational modifications in collagen. Although limited by the number of samples included, our results uncover proteomic changes that indicate alterations in nitric oxide signaling, extracellular matrix remodeling and cell-cell communication in OI. Future studies can validate these changes in larger patient-derived samples and also explore the value of some of the markers we describe as potential therapeutic targets.
