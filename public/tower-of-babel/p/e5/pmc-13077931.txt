Metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis impairs cochlear integrity and delays auditory recovery after noise trauma
Authors: Sheng Jin, Soo Jeong Kim, Ah-Ra Lyu, Sun-Ae Shin, Tae Hwan Kim, Long Jin, Jun Young Heo, Min Jung Park, Yong-Ho Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic hearing impairment represents a major global health burden, profoundly diminishing quality of life due to its association with depression and cognitive decline. Recent studies have identified metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) as an independent systemic risk factor for accelerated hearing loss, implicating a functional liver–inner ear axis. However, the molecular and vascular mechanisms through which MASH-induced systemic inflammation compromises cochlear integrity remain uncertain.MethodsWe investigated the impact of MASH on cochlear pathology and auditory function. MASH was induced in 8-week-old male BALB/c mice by feeding a methionine–choline-deficient (MCD) diet for 4 weeks. After this induction period, the mice were exposed to acoustic trauma and maintained on the same diet for an additional 2 weeks. Systemic and cochlear evaluations were performed to assess the impact of MASH on auditory function and related physiological alterations.ResultsThe MCD diet induced severe hepatic steatosis and lobular inflammation, accompanied by cochlear stria vascularis (SV) hyperpermeability and inflammation characterized by elevated interleukin (IL)-1β and lipocalin-2 (LCN2), without overt hearing loss or hair cell damage. Following noise exposure, MCD-fed mice demonstrated significantly delayed auditory recovery compared to chow-fed controls. This delay was associated with cochlear synaptopathy (CtBP2 degeneration), exacerbated BLB dysfunction, and ultrastructural alterations in endothelial cells, pericytes, and perivascular macrophages within the stria vascularis. PDGFRβ expression was significantly reduced, while proinflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-1β, and LCN2) were upregulated, suggesting concurrent vascular injury and inflammatory activation.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that MASH sensitizes the cochlea to noise-induced injury through disruption of the BLB and sustained inflammation, providing a preclinical model and mechanistic insight into the link between MASH and hearing loss.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02760-z.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), Methionine-choline deficient (MCD) diet, Blood-labyrinth barrier (BLB), Pericyte, Lipocalin-2 (LCN2), Noise-induced hearing loss

Introduction

Metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), the progressive form of metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), has emerged as a global health concern that extends beyond hepatic pathology [1]. Once regarded primarily as a liver disorder, MASH is now recognized as a systemic inflammatory and metabolic condition that disrupts multiple organ systems through circulating cytokines, lipotoxic metabolites, and endothelial stress mediators. Recent epidemiological studies have revealed a compelling association between metabolic liver disease and auditory dysfunction, implying that hepatic inflammation may alter the homeostasis of the inner ear [2, 3]. Patients with MASLD or MASH demonstrate an increased incidence of sudden sensorineural hearing loss and subclinical elevations in auditory thresholds, implicating a previously underrecognized liver–inner ear axis [2, 3]. However, the molecular and vascular mechanisms underpinning this interorgan communication remain unclear, particularly the manner in which metabolic liver injury compromises cochlear vascular integrity and auditory resilience.

The cochlea depends on a precisely regulated vascular microenvironment to sustain auditory transduction and maintain ionic and metabolic homeostasis. Central to this regulation is the blood-labyrinth barrier (BLB), a highly specialized vascular interface analogous to the blood-brain barrier [4, 5]. The BLB functions as a selective permeability barrier that shields sensory hair cells from systemic fluctuations, toxins, and inflammatory mediators. Structurally, it comprises endothelial cells interconnected by tight junctions, pericytes that provide mechanical and paracrine support, and perivascular macrophages that modulate immune and oxidative balance [5]. This tri-cellular organization ensures vascular stability and tightly regulated exchange between the systemic circulation and the cochlear milieu. Disruption of any BLB component can trigger vascular leakage, immune cell infiltration, and oxidative stress, leading to cochlear dysfunction and diminished auditory sensitivity [6, 7]. Indeed, BLB breakdown has been implicated in multiple auditory pathologies, including sudden and noise-induced hearing loss, emphasizing its pivotal role in cochlear integrity and auditory resilience [5, 8–10]. Within this framework, the stria vascularis represents a highly specialized anatomical component of the BLB, and vascular leakage observed in this region serves as a sensitive indicator of BLB functional impairment.

Recent studies have underscored the critical role of platelet-derived growth factor receptor beta (PDGFRβ) signaling in maintaining vascular homeostasis across multiple organ systems, including the liver and cochlea. In the liver, PDGFRβ⁺ pericytes and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) coordinate sinusoidal stability and extracellular matrix remodeling during injury and repair [11–14]. Chronic activation of PDGFRβ signaling in HSCs is a defining feature of fibrogenesis in MASH, driving the pericyte-to-myofibroblast transition and promoting the deposition of collagen-rich scar tissue [11, 12]. This maladaptive response disrupts sinusoidal permeability and contributes to systemic vascular inflammation. Similarly, in the cochlea, PDGFRβ⁺ pericytes play a vital role in preserving endothelial barrier integrity within the BLB. Their degeneration or detachment leads to vascular leakage, edema, and impaired cochlear homeostasis [5, 15–17]. Disruption of PDGFRβ signaling has been associated with reduced pericyte coverage, microvascular rarefaction, and consequent hearing impairment.

Beyond vascular dysregulation, chronic metabolic stress in MASH fosters a proinflammatory vascular milieu that further disrupts endothelial–pericyte interactions. Among the circulating mediators implicated in this process, lipocalin-2 (LCN2) has emerged as a pivotal inflammatory effector linking hepatic injury to systemic vascular dysfunction [18–22]. LCN2, a stress-inducible glycoprotein abundantly produced by hepatocytes and activated hepatic stellate cells, is significantly upregulated during steatohepatitis and is released into the circulation at elevated levels. Once secreted, LCN2 interacts with endothelial and pericyte receptors to induce oxidative stress, upregulate adhesion molecules, and promote vascular permeability [23–25]. Elevated LCN2 has also been reported to modulate vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and PDGFRβ signaling, amplifying vascular instability in extrahepatic tissues [26, 27]. Within the cochlea, increased LCN2 expression correlates with microvascular leakage, macrophage activation, and BLB disruption—pathological features that mirror those observed in metabolic and noise-induced cochlear injury.

Recent studies indicate that systemic inflammatory mediators may constitute key molecular links between hepatic pathology and cochlear dysfunction. Of these, LCN2 has garnered particular attention. LCN2 is a stress-inducible glycoprotein that is significantly upregulated in the liver during MASH and is readily detectable in the systemic circulation [18–20]. Elevated circulating LCN2 has been implicated in endothelial activation and vascular instability across multiple extrahepatic tissues [23–25]. Similarly, proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-1β, which are elevated in MASH, disrupt vascular integrity and immune homeostasis [28]. Within the cochlea, these mediators compromise BLB stability by perturbing endothelial–pericyte interactions and altering perivascular macrophage function [26]. Collectively, these processes may form a mechanistic bridge between hepatic inflammation and auditory dysfunction; however, direct experimental validation remains uncertain.

Despite increasing recognition of the liver–inner ear axis, the mechanisms through which hepatic inflammation compromises cochlear vascular integrity remain uncertain. Considering the shared dependence of hepatic sinusoids and the cochlear microvasculature on pericyte–endothelial signaling, we hypothesized that metabolic and inflammatory stress in MASH disrupts cochlear vascular stability through PDGFRβ-dependent pericyte dysfunction and LCN2-mediated endothelial activation. Therefore, we established a murine MASH model using methionine- and choline-deficient (MCD) diet feeding and examined systemic inflammatory profiles in parallel with cochlear vascular pathology. Through integrated histological, molecular, and vascular analyses, we delineated how hepatic injury propagated extrahepatic vascular stress, identified key mediators linking the liver and cochlea, and defined the role of the PDGFRβ–LCN2 inflammatory axis in BLB disruption. Our findings provide mechanistic insight into how chronic liver inflammation destabilizes cochlear vascular homeostasis, establishing a potential pathogenic connection between MASH and auditory dysfunction.

Method

Ethical statement

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 86 − 23, revised 2011) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Chungnam National University Hospital (Approval numbers. CNUH-2023-IA0058-00 and CNUH-2025-IA0013-00).

ARRIVE guidelines statement

This study was conducted and reported in line with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines.

Mice and induction of MASH

Male BALB/c mice (n = 3–10 mice per group, total 120 mice) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). Animals were maintained under controlled environmental conditions (22 °C, 45–55% humidity) with a 12 h light/dark cycle (0700 to 1900 h) and had ad libitum access to pelleted chow (Envigo, Teklad Global 18% Protein Rodent Diet, #2018 C) and water.

To induce MASH, mice were fed a methionine–choline-deficient (MCD) diet (A02082002BR, Research Diets, Inc., NJ, USA) for 4 weeks. Age-matched control mice received a methionine–choline-sufficient diet (A02082003BY, Research Diets, Inc.). Body weight was recorded weekly during the feeding period. At sacrifice, livers were excised, rinsed with cold PBS, blotted dry, and weighed prior to subsequent analyses. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and each mouse was considered an independent experimental unit, except for molecular assays, in which a single cochlea represented one unit. To minimize potential confounding effects, treatment order, measurement timing, and cage placement were randomized and counterbalanced across groups. All animals were monitored daily throughout the experimental period for general health and welfare. Routine assessments included evaluation of activity level, grooming behavior, posture, and overall appearance. Animals were also observed for signs of distress, reduced mobility, or abnormal behavior. No animals exhibited morbidity or distress requiring early termination or exclusion from the study.

Biochemical and histological analyses

Serum concentrations of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total cholesterol, and triglycerides were quantified using an automated biochemical analyzer (DooYeol Biotech, Seoul, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Blood samples were collected at sacrifice, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, and centrifuged at 1,500 x g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain serum.

Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness using a rotary microtome. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) following standard histological procedures to evaluate hepatic morphology. Stained slides were scanned using a digital slide scanner Panoramic MIDI version 1.23 (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary), and representative high-resolution images were acquired for morphometric evaluation. Histological features were semi-quantitatively scored in a blinded manner according to established NAFLD Activity Score (NAS) criteria [29]. Steatosis (0–3), hepatocellular ballooning (0–2), and lobular inflammation (0–3) were each scored based on severity and extent, and the total NAS (0–8) was calculated as the sum of individual components. Scores were derived from five randomly selected non-overlapping fields per section, and the average score per mouse was used for statistical analysis.

Noise exposure

After 4 weeks of dietary intervention, mice were exposed to octave-band noise (1–8 kHz, 115 dB SPL) for 7 min in a soundproof reverberation chamber. The noise signal was generated by a computer and delivered through a power amplifier (INTER-M R300 Plus; Canford Audio PLC, Washington, UK) to speakers (Electro-Voice, MN, USA). Noise levels were calibrated and continuously monitored using a sound level meter (Type 2250; Brüel & Kjær, Denmark), and a condenser microphone (Type 4189; Brüel & Kjær, Denmark). Following exposure, animals were returned to their home cages for recovery and monitored daily.

Auditory functional assessments

Auditory function was evaluated by auditory brainstem response (ABR), distortion product otoacoustic emission (DPOAE), and wave I amplitude analyses [6, 30]. ABR thresholds at 4, 8, 16, and 32 kHz and click stimuli were recorded before and after noise exposure (0, 1, 3, 7, and 14 days) under anesthesia with zolazepam (40 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) in a soundproof chamber (Sontek, Republic of Korea) [30, 31]. The thresholds were assessed longitudinally in the same cohort of mice, with repeated measurements obtained from each individual animal at all post-noise time points (0, 1, 3, 7, and 14 days). Signals were collected using the TDT System-3 platform and BIoSig RP software (version 4.4.1; Tucker-Davis Technologies, FL, USA) with subdermal electrodes placed at the vertex and mastoids.

DPOAEs were obtained using an ER-10B+ probe (Etymotic Research, Il, USA) coupled with two TDT MF-1 transducers (L1 = 65 dB SPL, L2 = 55 dB SPL, f2/f1 = 1.2) across 0.5–35 kHz. Responses amplitudes at 2f1-f2 were analyzed. Following DPOAE recordings, ABR waveforms were averaged (1,024 sweeps), filtered (0.3–3 kHz), digitized (25 kHz), and used to determine wave I amplitudes. Thresholds were defined as the lowest stimulus intensity eliciting reproducible responses (> 0.2 µV) and expressed as shifts relative to baseline.

Cochlear blood flow measurement

Relative cochlear blood flow was measured using a laser Doppler flowmeter (PeriFlux System 5000; Perimed AB, Sweden). Under anesthesia, both tympanic bullae were exposed, and mice were placed on a stereotaxic frame. A 0.1-mm-diameter laser Doppler probe was consistently positioned on the bony surface overlying the basal turn of the cochlea. Blood flow was recorded as standard Doppler signal oscillations, reflecting relative changes in cochlear perfusion. Measurements were normalized to individual baseline values and expressed as percentage changes relative to pre-exposure levels.

Assessment of cochlear vascular permeability

To assess cochlear vascular permeability associated with blood–labyrinth barrier (BLB) function, mice were intravenously injected with FITC–dextran (75 kDa, 40 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) via the tail vein under anesthesia, 30 min before sacrifice. The cochlear stria vascularis was dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, then mounted on glass slides.

Fluorescence images were obtained using a confocal microscope (LSM 900; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually defined to encompass the stria vascularis based on consistent anatomical landmarks across samples. Mean fluorescence intensity within each ROI was quantified using ImageJ under identical imaging parameters (laser power, gain, and exposure time) for all experimental groups. Fluorescence quantification was restricted to intravascular FITC–dextran signal within strial capillaries, excluding extravascular fluorescence, to assess vascular tracer retention. All image acquisition and quantitative analyses were performed in a blinded manner with respect to experimental group.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

Temporal bones were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h at room temperature, decalcified in 0.12 M EDTA (pH 7.4) for 48 h, and dissected into individual cochlear turns. For whole-mount (WM) staining, samples were permeabilized and blocked with 5% normal goat serum (Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) and 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), followed by incubation with MyoVIIa (Proteus Biosciences, CA, USA) and Phalloidin (Invitrogen, CA, USA) to visualize hair cells and actin filaments.

For immunofluorescence labeling, cochlear tissues were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Iba-1 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Desmin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA), PDGFRβ (Abcam), Lipocalin-2/NGAL (Abcam) 24p3R (Invitrogen), IL-6 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and βIII-tubulin (Abcam), and CtBP2 (BD Biosciences, CA, USA). After washing, Alexa Fluor 488– or 594–conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) were applied for 1 h at room temperature, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen). Confocal images were acquired using a laser scanning microscope (LSM 900; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). For synaptic analysis, CtBP2 punctae were quantified from the middle turn of the cochlea (12–24 kHz frequency region), a region known to be particularly vulnerable to noise-induced synaptopathy and corresponding to the frequency range showing ABR threshold shifts in this study. CtBP2 puncta were counted per inner hair cell (IHC) within 150 μm cochlear segments, and fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, MD, USA). Image contrast, overlay, and fluorescence colocalization were adjusted using Adobe Photoshop CS6 (Adobe Systems, CA, USA).

Western blot analysis

Cochlear and liver tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Halt™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg per sample) were separated by 8–12% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against VEGF (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA), PDGFRβ (Abcam), IL-6 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), IL-1β (Abcam), LCN2/NGAL (Abcam), and β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA).

After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Amersham, MA, USA) and imaged with the Azure 300 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Azure Biosystems, CA, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, MD, USA) and normalized to β-actin expression levels.

Measurement of circulating IL-1β and LCN2

Blood was collected via cardiac puncture at euthanasia, and serum was obtained by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 10 min. Serum IL-1β and LCN2 concentrations were quantified using ELISA kits (R&D Systems, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples, standards, and controls were added to 96-well plates and incubated at room temperature for 3 h. After washing, 50 µL of conjugate solution was added and incubated for 1 h, followed by a 30 min incubation with substrate solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, NC, USA), and concentrations were calculated from standard curves.

Image processing and statistical analysis

Image contrast adjustment, overlay, and fluorescence color merging were performed using Adobe Photoshop CS6 (Adobe Systems, CA, USA). All experimental procedures were performed under double-blinded conditions to minimize bias, with both treatment administrators and outcome assessors unaware of group assignments. The normality of data distribution was evaluated using the D’Agostino–Pearson omnibus test when sample size permitted. All data were derived from independent biological samples, and sample sizes were selected empirically without prespecified effect size assumptions.

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used. For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was applied, depending on the experimental design. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

An MCD diet induces hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and systemic alterations consistent with MASH-like phenotypes

To establish a murine model of MASH, 8-week-old male BALB/c mice were fed either a methionine–choline-sufficient control diet or a MCD diet for 4 weeks (Fig. 1A). The MCD diet induces rapid hepatic steatosis by impairing the synthesis of phosphatidylcholine, which is essential for the assembly and secretion of very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL), thereby causing an accumulation of intrahepatic triglycerides. This nutritional deficiency further compromises mitochondrial β-oxidation and promotes oxidative stress, leading to the characteristic lobular inflammation and hepatocellular injury observed in this MASH model. As anticipated, MCD-fed mice exhibited a progressive decline in body weight compared to controls throughout the feeding period (Fig. 1B), consistent with previous findings that MCD feeding disrupts hepatic lipid export and energy metabolism [32–34]. Despite this overall weight loss, the MCD-fed mice showed a significant reduction in absolute liver mass (Fig. 1C) and developed macroscopic and histological features characteristic of steatohepatitis. Gross examination revealed pale, friable hepatic parenchyma, whereas hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrated extensive macrovesicular steatosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and hepatocellular ballooning. Histological analysis revealed that MCD-fed mice exhibited markedly increased hepatic steatosis, hepatocellular ballooning, and lobular inflammation compared with control mice, resulting in a significantly elevated total NAFLD Activity Score (NAS) (Fig. 1D).

Serum biochemical analyses confirmed pronounced hepatic injury and altered lipid metabolism in MCD-fed mice. Circulating alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase levels were significantly elevated, indicating hepatocellular damage (Fig. 1E and F). In contrast, total cholesterol and triglyceride concentrations were significantly reduced, consistent with impaired hepatic lipoprotein synthesis and secretion (Fig. 1G and H). Systemic inflammation was further evidenced by increased serum IL-1β levels in the MCD group (Fig. 1I). Histological examination of the kidney and spleen revealed no overt structural abnormalities, implying that MCD-induced injury was predominantly hepatic at this stage (Fig. 1J). These findings confirm that 4 weeks of MCD diet feeding effectively induced hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and metabolic alterations consistent with MASH-like pathology in mice.

MCD-diet-induced MASH does not compromise auditory function or cochlear hair cell integrity

To assess whether MASH-like systemic alterations affect auditory function, we evaluated auditory brainstem responses (ABRs) and cochlear outer hair cell function in MCD-fed mice. Despite pronounced hepatic and systemic inflammatory changes, ABR thresholds across frequencies of 4–32 kHz were comparable between control and MCD-fed animals, indicating preserved hearing sensitivity (Figs. 1 and 2A). Similarly, ABR wave I amplitudes—reflecting auditory nerve activity—did not differ significantly between groups at 8, 16, or 32 kHz across stimulus intensities (Fig. 2B–D).

Consistent with intact auditory responses, distortion product otoacoustic emission amplitudes, which serve as an indicator of outer hair cell function, remained unchanged in MCD-fed mice compared with controls (Fig. 2E). Morphological analysis of the organ of Corti using MyoVIIa and phalloidin staining further confirmed normal hair cell architecture throughout the cochlear turns, with no evidence of loss or disorganization in the apical, middle, or basal regions (Fig. 2F). Quantitative analysis of cochlear whole-mount preparations also revealed no significant differences in outer or inner hair cell counts between control and MCD-fed mice across the apical, middle, and basal turns of the cochlea (Fig. 2F–H). These findings indicate that MCD-induced metabolic dysfunction does not cause overt hair cell loss under baseline conditions.

These findings indicate that 4 weeks of MCD diet, while sufficient to induce MASH-like hepatic pathology and systemic inflammation, did not directly impair peripheral auditory function or cochlear hair cell integrity. These findings imply that metabolic liver injury alone is insufficient to elicit overt hearing deficits under basal, non-stressed conditions.

MASH induces cochlear vascular barrier dysfunction and inflammation via LCN2 signaling

Considering that the MCD diet did not directly affect hearing thresholds (Fig. 2), we investigated whether MASH alters the integrity of the cochlear microvasculature, particularly the BLB. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran permeability assays revealed increased vascular leakage in the stria vascularis and spiral ligament of MCD-fed mice compared to controls, indicating compromised BLB integrity (Fig. 3A). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant decrease in intravascular FITC–dextran fluorescence intensity within strial capillaries of MCD-fed mice compared with controls, suggesting altered vascular retention of FITC–dextran in BLB-associated regions (Fig. 3A). Consistent with this vascular instability, perivascular architecture was disrupted, as evidenced by reduced coverage of perivascular macrophages (Iba-1⁺) and pericytes (Desmin⁺) (Fig. 3B). Immunostaining demonstrated a pronounced reduction in the pericyte marker PDGFRβ, accompanied by elevated expression of the stress-associated protein LCN2 and its receptor 24p3R within the stria vascularis (Fig. 3C). Despite these microvascular alterations, overall cochlear blood flow remained unchanged (Fig. 3D).

At the molecular level, western blot analyses revealed downregulation of PDGFRβ and upregulation of VEGF, IL-1β, and LCN2 in cochlear tissues from MCD-fed mice (Fig. 3E–I). In parallel, both VEGF and LCN2 expression were markedly elevated in the liver, implying enhanced hepatic release and increased circulating LCN2 levels (Fig. 3J–M). These findings indicate that hepatic LCN2 induction during MASH may contribute to secondary cochlear inflammation and vascular instability. Our findings uncover a previously unrecognized link between metabolic liver injury and cochlear vascular pathology, and identify LCN2 as a potential systemic mediator that bridges hepatic inflammation with cochlear barrier dysfunction.

MCD-diet-induced MASH delays auditory recovery and exacerbates cochlear damage following acoustic trauma

To assess the effect of MASH on auditory resilience to acoustic injury, mice fed a methionine–choline-sufficient diet and a MCD diet were subjected to acute noise exposure, and auditory recovery was evaluated by ABR measurements. Both groups exhibited delayed functional recovery following acoustic stress (Fig. 4B–F). However, MCD-fed mice demonstrated significantly delayed recovery across multiple frequencies, indicating that MASH compromises auditory repair capacity after acoustic trauma.

MASH delays auditory functional recovery following acoustic trauma. (A) Experimental timeline showing MCD diet feeding, noise exposure, and hearing assessments. (B–F) ABR data represent longitudinal measurements obtained from the same mice across all post-noise time points. Auditory brainstem response (ABR) threshold shifts at 4 (B), 8 (C), 16 (D), and 32 (E) kHz and click stimuli (F). MCD-fed mice exhibit prolonged threshold elevation relative to chow-fed controls. (G–I) ABR wave I amplitudes (90 dB SPL) at 8 (G), 16 (H), and 32 (I) kHz showing attenuated neural response amplitudes in MCD-fed mice. Data are presented as mean ± SEM (n = 8–10 mice per group). Statistical significance was assessed using two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test (a, main effect of time, p < 0.05; b, main effect of diet, p < 0.05). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001

At 4 kHz and 8 kHz, MCD-fed mice exhibited greater threshold shifts at days 1 and 3 compared to controls, with only partial recovery by day 14 (Fig. 4B and C). In contrast, control animals exhibited near-complete recovery by day 7. At 16 kHz, threshold shifts persisted in MCD-fed mice throughout the observation period, with significant differences at days 1, 3, and 7 (Fig. 4D). Similarly, at 32 kHz, recovery was delayed in MCD-fed mice (Fig. 4E); however, group differences diminished at later time points. Consistent with these findings, ABR click responses confirmed delayed auditory recovery in the MCD group, supporting that MASH exacerbates susceptibility to and recovery from noise-induced cochlear injury (Fig. 4F).

Analysis of ABR wave 1 amplitudes, an indicator of auditory nerve integrity, revealed reduced amplitudes in MCD-fed mice across multiple frequencies. At 8 kHz and 16 kHz, wave I amplitudes (90 dB SPL) were significantly decreased in MCD-fed mice on days 1 and 3 after noise exposure, with both groups recovering by day 14 (Fig. 4G and H). At 32 kHz, MCD-fed mice exhibited significantly reduced wave 1 amplitudes at days 7 and 14, indicating sustained cochlear dysfunction (Fig. 4I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that although MCD-diet-induced metabolic liver injury does not impair baseline hearing, it heightens cochlear vulnerability to noise-induced damage, leading to delayed auditory recovery and prolonged neural dysfunction, as reflected by persistent threshold shifts and attenuated wave 1 amplitudes following acoustic trauma.

MASH delays auditory recovery following noise trauma by inducing cochlear synaptopathy without hair cell loss

To assess whether MASH alters cochlear vulnerability to acoustic injury, we exposed control and MCD-diet-fed mice to transient noise and evaluated cochlear pathology. Notably, neither group exhibited significant inner or outer hair cell loss up to 2 weeks post-exposure, indicating that MASH does not increase hair cell susceptibility to acoustic trauma (Fig. 5A–C). In contrast, synaptic ribbon analysis revealed a significant group difference. In control mice, ribbon synapses (CtBP2⁺ puncta) partially recovered within 14 days after noise exposure, whereas MCD-fed mice exhibited persistent synaptic loss across all cochlear turns (Fig. 5D and E). These findings imply that MASH compromises synaptic repair capacity rather than promoting sensory cell degeneration.

To evaluate the vascular contribution to delayed auditory recovery, cochlear blood flow was assessed using laser Doppler flowmetry. In MCD-fed mice, relative cochlear blood flow transiently declined at 7 days after noise exposure and remained lower than in controls, indicating persistent vascular instability (Fig. 5F). These findings demonstrate that MASH delays auditory recovery following acoustic trauma through sustained ribbon synapse degeneration and vascular dysfunction, without causing overt hair cell loss.

MCD-diet-induced MASH exacerbates BLB breakdown and cochlear hyperpermeability after acoustic trauma

Because the delayed auditory recovery in MCD-fed mice implied cochlear vascular dysfunction, we investigated whether MASH affected BLB integrity following noise exposure. Vascular hyperpermeability was defined as extravascular leakage of intravenously administered FITC-dextran, reflecting disruption of the blood–labyrinth barrier. In Noise + MCD mice, FITC-dextran signal was frequently detected outside vessel lumens within the stria vascularis, indicating focal tracer extravasation (Fig. 6A, arrows). To quantify permeability, mean fluorescence intensity was measured in predefined intravascular regions of interest, allowing objective assessment of tracer leakage. This analysis revealed a significant increase in vascular permeability in MCD-fed mice at both 1 and 7 days after noise exposure compared to controls (Fig. 6A, right). Notably, increased vascular permeability was detected at time points when cochlear blood flow alterations were transient, indicating a temporal dissociation between blood flow dynamics and barrier integrity (Fig. 5F).

Consistent with the FITC-dextran leakage findings, Western blot analyses revealed significant upregulation of vascular-permeability-associated proteins in the cochlea of MCD-fed mice. VEGF and PDGFRβ levels were elevated at day 7 after noise exposure, with significant main effects of diet and time confirmed by two-way analysis of variance (Fig. 6B–D). The temporal increase in VEGF expression was particularly prominent in the MCD group, implying sustained endothelial activation and pericyte signaling imbalance during recovery. These findings indicate that MASH amplifies noise-induced cochlear vascular leakage and BLB disruption through dysregulated angiogenic signaling, establishing a mechanistic link between systemic metabolic injury and inner ear microvascular instability.

Delayed recovery from noise trauma in MASH is associated with increased cochlear inflammatory responses

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying BLB dysfunction and delayed auditory recovery in MASH, we investigated cochlear inflammatory responses following acoustic trauma. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed significant induction of IL-6 in spiral ganglion neurons and the organ of Corti of MCD-fed mice compared to chow-fed controls (Fig. 7A). Notably, elevated IL-6 expression persisted up to 14 days post-noise exposure, indicating sustained proinflammatory activation within the cochlear neural compartment.

Western blot analyses demonstrated that MCD feeding enhanced the expression of key inflammatory mediators, including IL-6, IL-1β, and LCN2 (Fig. 7B). Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly higher IL-6 and IL-1β levels across all post-noise time points in MASH mice, with peak expression at day 7 (Fig. 7C and D). Cochlear LCN2 expression was elevated in MCD-fed animals (Fig. 7E), consistent with its role as a vascular stress-responsive factor promoting endothelial activation and permeability. These findings indicate that MASH amplifies and prolongs cochlear inflammatory responses to acoustic injury, characterized by persistent cytokine upregulation and increased LCN2 expression. This sustained inflammatory milieu exacerbates BLB disruption and hinders restoration of cochlear homeostasis, contributing to the delayed auditory recovery observed in MCD-fed mice.

Discussion

Our study establishes a previously unrecognized link between MASH and delayed functional recovery to noise-induced hearing loss. We systematically investigated the effects of MASH on cochlear vascular integrity and auditory resilience following acoustic stress. Mice fed an MCD diet developed hallmark features of MASH, including hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and early fibrotic remodeling, without exhibiting overt baseline hearing deficits. Despite preserved auditory thresholds, MCD-fed mice displayed subtle cochlear abnormalities characterized by increased BLB permeability and structural alterations in the stria vascularis. These pre-existing microvascular changes were associated with delayed auditory recovery after noise exposure, accompanied by cochlear synaptopathy and aggravated BLB dysfunction. These findings indicate that MASH induces a subclinical state of cochlear vulnerability, undetectable under basal conditions but sufficient to impair auditory resilience under environmental stress.

The clinical association between metabolic syndrome, MASLD, and auditory impairment has been consistently observed in epidemiological studies, yet the underlying mechanisms remained largely speculative. Our findings provide the first direct mechanistic in vivo evidence supporting the existence of a liver–inner ear axis. In this model, the MASH liver functions as a source of proinflammatory and lipotoxic mediators that circulate systemically and disrupt the highly specialized microvasculature of the inner ear. The preservation of baseline hearing thresholds implies that MASH does not induce direct cochlear hair cell injury under basal conditions but instead establishes a state of pathological priming—characterized by vascular leakage and low-grade inflammation—that diminishes the cochlea’s ability to recover from acoustic stress. These findings shift the understanding of the liver–auditory connection from epidemiological correlation to defined pathophysiological causation.

Vascular dysfunction is a defining feature of MASLD and MASH [35, 36]. In our study, delayed auditory recovery following noise trauma emerged as a principal consequence of MASH, most likely driven by BLB disruption and vascular injury. A central objective was to identify the factors responsible for BLB hyperpermeability and subsequent damage to the cochlear microvascular niche. Our findings implicate the hepatokine LCN2 as a key systemic mediator of hepatic pathology in the liver–inner ear axis. LCN2, a prototypical stress-inducible glycoprotein, is significantly upregulated in hepatocytes and hepatic stellate cells under MASH conditions, leading to substantial elevation of its circulating levels [18, 19, 37]. This rise in systemic LCN2 establishes a direct pathogenic connection between hepatic inflammation and cochlear vascular vulnerability. The relevance of this finding is emphasized by prior evidence that LCN2 compromises the integrity of the blood-brain barrier [24, 38–40], implying that similar mechanisms may extend to the BLB.

Extrapolating this established mechanism to the inner ear, we propose that MASH-derived LCN2 similarly targets the highly sensitive BLB microvasculature. Mechanistically, LCN2 exerts its deleterious effects through interaction with the endothelial receptor 24p3R (SLC22A17), triggering a cascade characterized by increased generation of reactive oxygen species and upregulation of adhesion molecules, such as intercellular adhesion molecule-1 and intercellular adhesion molecule-1, inducing endothelial activation within the stria vascularis [41–44]. In parallel, LCN2 compromises vascular stability by reducing pericyte viability and disrupting endothelial-pericyte junctions [24, 37–39, 45–47]. Collectively, these processes promote microvascular leakage and facilitate macrophage infiltration, directly contributing to BLB disruption in our MASH model. Therefore, LCN2 emerges as a critical pathogenic mediator—a molecular conduit translating chronic hepatic inflammatory injury into dysfunction of the auditory vascular barrier—predisposing the cochlea to heightened vulnerability and impaired recovery following acoustic stress.

Furthermore, in the cochlea of MCD-fed mice, elevated VEGF and PDGFRβ expression indicated baseline vascular injury and pericyte-mediated remodeling, even in the absence of noise exposure. The observed increase in BLB permeability arises from direct endothelial dysfunction, pericyte detachment, or extracellular matrix remodeling driven by hepatic or circulating LCN2 influx from the periphery into the inner ear. LCN2 may further potentiate these alterations by promoting endothelial activation and pericyte dysfunction [19, 24, 26, 37–39, 47, 48]. Previous studies have demonstrated that LCN2 disrupts endothelial tight junctions and enhances vascular leakage, while acting synergistically with cytokines, such as IL-1β and IL-6, to amplify local inflammatory responses [27]. The concurrent elevation of these mediators in our model supports a cooperative mechanism in which metabolic stress induces vascular destabilization and inflammatory amplification within the cochlea.

The delayed auditory recovery is closely linked to a persistent and pronounced state of cochlear inflammation. Sustained elevation of key inflammatory mediators—particularly IL-6, IL-1β, and LCN2—was observed on days 7 and 14 post-noise exposure in MASH-fed animals (Fig. 7). This unresolved inflammatory state is inherently detrimental to neurosensory tissue repair. Mechanistically, it appears to be driven by the LCN2–IL-1β–IL-6 axis [49–53], wherein elevated IL-1β and IL-6 perpetuate a proinflammatory milieu that reciprocally induces robust upregulation of LCN2, further amplifying inflammation within the inner ear. The persistent activation of this self-reinforcing cytokine loop likely impairs critical reparative processes, compromising auditory recovery following noise-induced injury.

In the present study, cochlear synaptopathy and sustained inflammation emerged as closely associated pathological features in MASH-fed mice following noise exposure, suggesting a mechanistic interplay between these processes. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, IL-6, and LCN2, were detected in the cochlea during the post-traumatic period, coinciding with persistent synaptic loss and delayed auditory recovery. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 have been shown in neural systems to impair synaptic repair, disrupt glutamate homeostasis, and reduce neuronal resilience after excitotoxic or mechanical injury, while LCN2 has been implicated in neuroinflammatory signaling and synaptic vulnerability in both central and peripheral sensory pathways [54, 55]. Thus, increased inflammatory signaling within BLB-associated cochlear regions may directly compromise synaptic maintenance and regeneration following acoustic trauma.

In parallel, our data support an indirect, vascular-mediated mechanism linking inflammation to synaptopathy. Disruption of BLB integrity and altered cochlear perfusion may exacerbate metabolic and oxidative stress at the inner hair cell–afferent synapse, a structure with exceptionally high energy demands [56]. Such vascular dysfunction is likely to increase susceptibility to ribbon loss and to limit the capacity for post-injury synaptic repair [57, 58]. Importantly, these findings allow a distinction between injury-phase vulnerability and post-injury recovery failure: while an elevated inflammatory state at the time of noise exposure may exacerbate acute synaptic damage, the persistent elevation of inflammatory mediators observed in MCD-fed mice is more consistent with impaired synaptic repair during the recovery phase. This interpretation aligns with the prolonged auditory deficits observed despite comparable noise exposure and hair cell survival, indicating that unresolved inflammation contributes to delayed synaptic restoration rather than solely to initial injury severity.

While noise trauma induced alterations in cochlear blood flow (Fig. 5F) that became detectable at later time points, vascular hyperpermeability (Fig. 6A) was evident as early as 1 day post-noise exposure and remained present at 7 days. This temporal dissociation indicates that blood–labyrinth barrier disruption is not strictly coupled to changes in cochlear perfusion. Rather, endothelial barrier dysfunction may precede measurable alterations in blood flow, raising the possibility that a threshold of endothelial leakage exists before vascular permeability changes are reflected as hemodynamic disturbances. Together, these findings suggest that noise-induced endothelial injury contributes to blood–labyrinth barrier breakdown through mechanisms that are not solely secondary to altered cochlear blood flow.

A key question remains regarding which specific cochlear cell types are responsible for the elevated expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and LCN2 observed in our MASH model. Given the systemic nature of MASH, the inflammatory landscape within the cochlea is likely a result of a complex interplay between resident and infiltrating cells [59–61]. The stria vascularis, a key anatomical component of the BLB, is the most probable site of initial activation [62, 63]. Endothelial cells expressing the LCN2 receptor (24p3R) may respond to circulating hepatokines by initiating local pro-inflammatory signaling [64]. Similarly, perivascular macrophages, which are essential for maintaining BLB integrity, could be vulnerable to metabolic stress, contributing to the cytokine pool [65, 66]. While our current findings highlight a generalized inflammatory state, this milieu likely involves the recruitment of infiltrating immune cells, such as CCR2+ monocytes, which amplify the local response [67, 68]. Furthermore, although hair cells and neuronal structures are the primary targets of noise-induced injury, their potential role in secondary inflammatory signaling—triggered by chronic exposure to systemic lipotoxicity—cannot be excluded. Future studies utilizing single-cell RNA sequencing or co-localization analyses with cell-type-specific markers will be required to definitively map the cellular origins of these signals and their precise contribution to cochlear vulnerability.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were conducted using male BALB/c mice, which limits the generalizability of our findings, particularly with respect to potential sex-specific differences in MASH progression, inflammatory signaling, and auditory vulnerability. Second, although the MCD diet reliably induced hepatic steatohepatitis, it was accompanied by significant body weight loss; food intake was not directly measured, precluding a more detailed assessment of the contribution of altered caloric intake or nutritional status to the observed cochlear phenotypes. Third, auditory functional assessment focused on auditory brainstem responses, and distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs) were not evaluated; thus, subtle alterations in outer hair cell function may have gone undetected. Finally, while our data implicate LCN2 as a key mediator of the liver–cochlea axis, we did not directly block LCN2 or its receptor (SLC22A17/24p3R) within the cochlea. Receptor-specific or cochlea-targeted interruption of LCN2 signaling would provide more definitive causal evidence. Addressing these limitations in future work will be important to further refine the mechanistic understanding and translational relevance of the liver–inner ear axis.

Taken together, these findings provide mechanistic insight into how metabolic dysfunction and liver pathology prime the cochlea for injury and diminish auditory resilience (Fig. 8). Our results indicate that subclinical cochlear pathology in MASH arises from a multifactorial process involving vascular destabilization, pericyte and endothelial dysfunction, synaptopathy, and sustained proinflammatory signaling. This study emphasizes the importance of systemic metabolic and inflammatory health in determining susceptibility to noise-induced hearing loss and identifies LCN2, VEGF, PDGFRβ, and proinflammatory cytokines as key mediators of liver–ear crosstalk. Future studies should delineate the precise cellular targets of LCN2 within the cochlea, characterize the temporal dynamics of vascular and synaptic repair, and explore therapeutic strategies to mitigate cochlear vulnerability in the context of systemic metabolic disease. In summary, our study validates the clinical relevance of the liver–inner ear axis and identifies LCN2 and BLB integrity as promising therapeutic targets for preventing or attenuating noise-induced and metabolically accelerated hearing loss.
