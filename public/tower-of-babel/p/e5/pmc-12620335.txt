Suicide Deaths in People With Bipolar Disorder: Characteristics and Treatments at Time of Death
Authors: Ayal Schaffer, Prudence Po Ming Chan, Vera Yu Men, Rosalie Steinberg, Rachel HB. Mitchell, Gin Malhi, Lakshmi N. Yatham, Mark Sinyor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aimed to compare suicide deaths in those with or without bipolar disorder (BD), taking into particular consideration the degree to which treatment at the time of death, according to toxicology data, reflects evidence‐based pharmacotherapy.MethodsCoroner data were coded for all verified suicide deaths in the City of Toronto, Canada (1998–2020). Suicide decedents with BD (n = 340) were compared to the non‐BD group (n = 4948) on demographic, clinical, and suicide‐related variables. In a subsample of decedents, the presence and/or lethality of medications or substances at the time of death was identified from toxicology data and compared between groups and across time (two 11.5 year epochs). Data were analyzed using univariate analyses.ResultsSeveral noteworthy demographic, clinical, and suicide‐specific differences between BD and non‐BD suicide groups emerged. Antidepressants (48.9%) and benzodiazepines (46.7%) were the most commonly present medications among people with BD, at a proportion similar to the non‐BD group. There was a significant decrease in the presence of mood stabilizers across time (33.3% vs. 13.5%, p = 0.006), and lithium was present in only 5.8% of decedents with BD. Opioids (22.1%) and antipsychotics (22.1%) were the most common substances detected in lethal amounts in the BD group.ConclusionsThe findings of this study suggest that a substantial proportion of people with BD who die by suicide are not receiving first‐line treatments at the time of death. While causality cannot be established, further exploration of pharmacotherapy at the time of death would ideally link to living controls to address issues of risk and lethality.

Introduction

The suicide rate in people with bipolar disorder (BD) is estimated to be up to 24 times the rate in the general population [1, 2, 3], and therefore individuals suffering from BD account for a significant proportion (4%–14%) of all suicide deaths [1]. One model for suicide in BD [4] that shares features with others [5, 6] incorporates neurobiological elements alongside clinical and environmental factors [4]. Collectively, these factors predispose people with BD to suicidal thinking that may culminate in a lethal attempt. While suicide in BD is thought to be preventable, current predictive models, both general [7] and those specifically developed for BD populations [8, 9], have failed to achieve sufficient accuracy to provide confidence in the prediction of suicide in individuals in clinical settings. This has compelled a shift towards broad‐based suicide prevention efforts that ensure all people at risk for suicide receive care to diminish their risk of suicide [10, 11]. A central aspect of any suicide prevention strategy is ensuring patients receive evidence‐based care for managing their condition, and this is particularly important in BD, which, as highlighted, carries a disproportionately greater risk [12].

Prospective data have demonstrated the anti‐suicide benefits of receiving appropriate evidence‐based treatment for BD [13, 14] and this includes algorithm‐guided pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy for BD [15]. Specifically, first‐line BD treatments such as mood stabilizers and atypical antipsychotics reduce suicidal behaviors and deaths [16]. Of these, lithium in particular has been extensively studied in this regard, with substantive evidence supporting a likely direct anti‐suicide effect in addition to benefits achieved through more general mood stabilization [17, 18]. There are now several well‐established and easily accessible clinical practice guidelines that recommend specific pharmacotherapeutic strategies for acute and maintenance phases of BD [19, 20, 21], and large clinical datasets confirm frequent use of efficacious medications [22] which serve as a key component of effective suicide prevention efforts [23, 24].

The nature of pharmacotherapy received by people with BD around the time of their suicide death has been examined in a small number of previous studies [25, 26], including our own [27]. In a small Australian sample, the majority of people with BD who died by suicide did not receive benchmark standards of care [25]. In a much larger study of suicides in people with BD in England from 1996 to 2009, among those receiving mental health care, 60% had been prescribed an antidepressant, 58% were prescribed lithium or another mood stabilizer, and 38.9% were prescribed an atypical antipsychotic [26]. The findings did not differentiate between monotherapy and combinations, and also did not report data from the subpopulation not receiving any mental health care. A past analysis of our group's Toronto dataset of suicides in people with BD up to 2012 included all suicide deaths irrespective of recent mental health contact, and found benzodiazepines and antidepressants to be the most common medication classes present in toxicology samples among those with BD who died via self‐poisoning [27].

The available studies to date reported on suicide data up to 2012, with concerning findings about the extent of evidence‐based care at the time of death. Over the past decade there has been substantial progress, with several new treatment options becoming available and evidence‐based clinical practice guidelines increasingly shaping clinical care for patients with BD [19], with resultant shifts in prescribing patterns [28]. We therefore sought to conduct a contemporary examination of potential changes over the years in the medications and/or substances found at the time of death among BD suicide decedents using a large dataset. In this study, we have also further expanded our approach to include toxicology results from a subsample of those who died by suicide methods other than self‐poisoning—data that was not considered in our previous research. Characterizing which compounds were detected has the potential to shed light on aspects of the suicide method as well as the presence of medications at any level that could serve as a proxy for understanding the medications patients had been prescribed and were taking at the time of death.

Therefore, our objective for this study was to characterize all BD suicide decedents in Toronto over a 23‐year period (1998–2020) and gain insights into key factors surrounding suicide in this highly vulnerable group by examining which medications and substances were present and/or lethal at the time of death from their toxicology reports and how this changed over time.

Methods

Data Source

All verified suicide deaths in the City of Toronto, Canada between January 1, 1998 and December 31, 2020 were identified through systematic chart reviews and abstraction at the Office of the Chief Coroner (OCC) of Ontario. These were identified by the internal data capture system at the coroner's office and provided to our research team for review. Verified deaths had to have been ruled a suicide by the investigating coroner, and the deceased had to reside and die in the City of Toronto. It can take up to 2 years for coroner investigations to be completed. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board at Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre, Toronto, Canada. Strict privacy procedures utilized by the OCC were fully adhered to, with all extracted data maintained in an encrypted and de‐identified format.

Data Retrieval

Trained coders reviewed all documentation within the coroner investigation records, including the coroner's report, rough notes, post‐mortem report, toxicology report, and police reports. Psychiatric and medical history included data from either the deceased's medical records or interviews with relatives, friends, or acquaintances. Coders used a standardized template to extract the following information: (i) demographics (age, sex, relationship/marital status and living situation); (ii) relevant stressors (bereavement, employment/financial/academic, relationship problems, interpersonal conflict, recent medical/health, police/legal); (iii) mental health history (psychiatric diagnosis, alcohol/substance use problems, past suicide attempts, past week acute care mental health contact); (iv) history of physical problems (e.g., pain, cancer, diabetes, cardiac issues) and (v) details of the suicide (method of death, presence of a suicide note, place of death). All coders reached high inter‐rater reliability (κ ≥ 0.8) on a training set of charts and then independently extracted information from charts. Coders would engage in frequent discussion throughout to resolve uncertainties, with the research team leads (M.S. and A.S.) resolving any disagreements.

Sample

Consistent with prior reports from our research group using this data source [29], the presence of a diagnosis of BD was established based on information in the coroner's investigation records. Diagnostic information was obtained by the coroner from a variety of sources, including medical records, collateral information from family (e.g., a child reporting their parent having a longstanding history of BD), police reports of personal records or other evidence that was present at the scene of death or home of the deceased, and content of the suicide note that may have stated a diagnosis of BD. Specific symptom reviews or psychological autopsies were not part of the study protocol, and since diagnostic evaluation was not the primary focus of the coroner investigations, complete accuracy of the diagnosis could not be established. It is likely that a small number of decedents with BD were not recorded as having the diagnosis, and it is also possible that a small number of decedents identified as having BD were misclassified. All suicide deaths in people with a BD diagnosis documented in the coroner's investigation records were included in the sample of BD suicide deaths, and all other suicides were included in the non‐BD suicide sample.

The OCC primarily conducts toxicology tests at the time of death in cases where self‐poisoning is suspected, but at times at their discretion will also obtain these for other modes of death. Toxicology reports include information on the level and distribution of all substances detected in the blood or urine, which is interpreted based on the approximate time since death. The pathologist uses this information to determine whether the substance was a cause of death (which we describe as “lethal”) or present at any level (lethal or not) at the time of the death (which we describe as “present”). Such determinations are complex, as they can depend on the deceased's medical history as well as substance levels that are influenced by the timing of bodily fluid retrieval. We relied exclusively on the coroner's determination of the presence and/or lethality of specific substances in each case rather than arriving at our own conclusions from the review of raw toxicology data, and prior studies using OCC toxicology data in other datasets have firmly established these procedures for collection and determination [30, 31]. The broad categories of substances we utilized included specific classes of prescription psychotropic medications, prescription non‐psychotropic medications, over‐the‐counter medications, alcohol, opioids, recreational drugs, and poisons.

Data Analysis

Descriptives of each of the variables of interest were calculated. Comparisons of BD and non‐BD groups were conducted by univariate analyses (t‐test or χ 2) of all demographic, clinical, stressor, and suicide‐specific variables. Differences in the proportion of different medication/substance classes or specific agents detected as being present or lethal at the time of death were compared between the BD and non‐BD groups using a series of χ 2 tests. In a subgroup analysis, χ 2 tests were also used to examine for differences in the proportion of medications/substances present between the first and second 11.5‐year time epochs. While this is an arbitrary dividing point between the two epochs, and changes are likely to occur more continuously, equally separating the time periods assisted with maintaining sufficient power to explore differences over time.

Results

During the period from 1998 to 2020, suicide deaths among people in Toronto identified as having BD accounted for 340 out of 5288 recorded suicides (6.4%).

Table 1 compares the sociodemographic, mental health history, and suicide‐related variables between those identified as having BD and those without BD. The BD suicide group was found to be significantly younger, less likely to be married, and to have a significantly lower male‐to‐female (M:F) ratio (M:F ratio = 1.30 in BD vs. 2.46 in non‐BD). Decedents with BD were significantly less likely to have experienced a recent employment, financial, and/or academic stressor, or a stressor related to their medical health. They were significantly more likely to have a history of prior suicide attempts, comorbid alcohol/substance use problems, or recent acute mental health care presentation. With regard to method of death, the decedents with BD were significantly more likely to die via self‐poisoning with a medication, and significantly less likely to die by hanging.

Table 2 lists the classes of medications or substances present at the time of suicide death, irrespective of level found (i.e., present but not necessarily at lethal levels). An average of 2.1 prescription psychotropic medications was present in the BD group as compared to 1.4 in the non‐BD group. There was an average of 3.5 different medications or substances present in the BD group compared to 2.7 in the non‐BD group. Antidepressants were the single most common specific medication group present among decedents with BD (48.9%) followed by benzodiazepines (46.7%). Antipsychotics and mood stabilizers were present in 37.2% and 22.6% of decedents with BD, respectively. When combining antipsychotics and/or mood stabilizers, that number increased to 51.1%. An unopposed antidepressant (i.e., without a mood stabilizer or antipsychotic) was present in 18.2% of BD decedents. There were no significant differences in the presence of opioids (27.7% vs. 28%), alcohol (42.3% vs. 43.9%), or recreational drugs (15.3% vs. 14.1%) between the BD and non‐BD groups.

Discussion

In this large cohort of all suicide deaths in Toronto over a 23‐year span, those with BD accounted for 6.4% of all suicide deaths, which is within the expected range for the proportion of suicide deaths identified in the literature, thus indirectly supporting the diagnostic precision of this group. Demographic and clinical differences between the BD and non‐BD groups fell along expected patterns, including the BD group being younger, experiencing fewer precipitating psychosocial stressors, and having higher rates of prior suicide attempts and contact with acute mental health services. The significantly lower male‐to‐female ratio is also in keeping with prior studies and demonstrates the larger relative impact of the illness on suicides among females with BD [32].

The more notable findings stem from the available toxicology results for the BD group. Data on the presence of medications at the time of death (irrespective of lethality of the agent) can serve as a proxy measure for pharmacotherapy received at the time of death. Similar to prior studies conducted before 2012, antidepressants remain the most common medication class present (48.9%), followed closely by benzodiazepines (46.7%). Only when combining an antipsychotic and/or mood stabilizer do usual first‐line treatments for BD reach similar values (51%; see Table 2). Mood stabilizers, well established as the core pharmacotherapy class in BD, were less commonly present (22.6%) than opioids (27.7%). Among mood stabilizers, carbamazepine was the most common single agent, with lithium being present in only 5.8% of those with BD, followed by lamotrigine in 5.1%. The comparison of earlier and later epochs found a significant decline in the presence of mood stabilizers at the time of death. Since it is unlikely that the anti‐suicide effectiveness of mood stabilizers has changed, these findings suggest an increasing lack of concordance between the treatments used in people with BD who die by suicide and clinical guidelines that promote first‐line mood stabilizer use or even first‐use of lithium [19, 20, 33]. However, the prescription patterns of antidepressant use, especially without concomitant mood stabilizer and/or antipsychotic use, show a numerical reduction between the earlier and later epochs, which is consistent with guideline recommendations.

The interpretation of these findings cannot veer into causality for several reasons. We did not have access to data that would have allowed a linked representative or matching living BD or non‐BD sample. Therefore, the denominator of how many people were prescribed or took these medications cannot be determined. Furthermore, at the time of death, a large proportion of BD suicide decedents were seemingly not receiving the pharmacotherapy with the strongest evidence, but there are several possible reasons for this. Firstly, it could be that this relates to prescribers failing to adhere to evidence‐based guidelines; secondly, a greater severity or refractoriness of illness in decedents with BD could have prompted the use of antidepressants or other medications, even if they are lower in the treatment algorithm (e.g., in the context of prior failure of treatments with more evidence); thirdly, there are specific clinical scenarios when medications such as antidepressants have a strong evidence base (e.g., BD‐II, or for acute or maintenance depression treatment when other agents have not been sufficient) [34]; or fourthly, a survivor bias wherein those who received evidence‐based care were less likely to die by suicide. This conundrum is most apparent when considering the very low percentage of BD suicide decedents who had lithium present at the time of death (< 6%). Lithium is known to protect against suicide over and above its mood stabilizing properties [33], and while one interpretation of our results could be to lament the extremely low numbers who were receiving lithium (which may have prevented their suicide), another interpretation is that, once a patient is taking lithium, they are at a much reduced likelihood of suicide and, therefore, this could be indirect evidence of the anti‐suicide effects of lithium. It is noteworthy that the two interpretations are not mutually exclusive and that both are likely. Indeed, a prior epidemiological survey conducted in 2002 of 852 people living with BD in Canada found that only 8.2% had been prescribed lithium at any point during the prior 12 months [35], which is in keeping with other recent data showing low frequency of lithium use during bipolar depressive and other phases [36], suggesting that, in fact, the large majority of people with BD are not provided lithium treatment, and the low numbers in our suicide cohort more likely highlight the under‐utilization of lithium and other first‐line treatments, with reduced use over time, and the tragic lethal consequences of this.

While toxicology data alone cannot serve as a measure for the quality of pharmacotherapy received around the time of death, it does have some advantages over pharmacoepidemiological data which cannot confirm actual ingestion of the medications or substances. These data should therefore be interpreted within the broader literature on population‐wide prescribing patterns in BD and their impacts on illness stabilization and suicide‐related outcomes [37, 38, 39]. As a whole, this literature continues to suggest major gaps in the delivery of the first‐line treatments for BD, including low rates of lithium use.

The most common lethal self‐poisoning ingestions within the BD group were quetiapine, oxycodone, diphenhydramine, and codeine. All other specific compounds fell below the privacy cut‐off of < 5, so could not be individually reported. Other than quetiapine, the most common substances ingested in lethal self‐poisoning are medications that do not fall within the category of prescription psychotropic medications, which highlights the need to broaden consideration of medication safety and means restriction beyond agents that are being prescribed for the purpose of BD management. Means restriction and close monitoring of potentially lethal agents such as opioids and diphenhydramine that are readily accessible to people with BD should be considered as part of a safety planning approach [40, 41].

Interpreting the pattern of compounds present and/or lethal at the time of death also needs to incorporate the element of relative toxicity. We did not have data on compounds used in non‐lethal self‐poisonings, so we could not directly examine this. However, differences in toxicity are clearly present, with, for instance, evidence that the case fatality of carbamazepine is more than twice that of lithium [42], which likely impacts the ratio of medications in the lethal vs. present categories.

There are a number of further limitations to consider when interpreting these results. First, there is no validated approach to ensuring BD diagnostic accuracy from coroner records, and even if the BD diagnosis is fairly accurate, which is supported by the proportion of suicide deaths among the full cohort, the specifics of the bipolar illness such as subtype, phase of illness, comorbidities, and past treatments are not systematically recorded and therefore could not be included in our analysis. This is especially relevant when trying to establish whether first‐line or even generally appropriate pharmacotherapy was being received, since the nature of evidence‐based care varies across these different illness characteristics. Clinical guideline recommendations have also changed from the 1990s to current times, and newer treatments were only available for a shorter duration of the study period. However, it is difficult to imagine a scenario where antidepressants and benzodiazepines would be consistently much more commonly recommended than atypical antipsychotics or mood stabilizers. Also, lithium only being present in 5.8% of suicide decedents with BD, alongside pharmacoepidemiological data of only 8.2% in Canada being prescribed lithium, is highly suggestive of under‐utilization in a group that ultimately died by suicide. Second, while toxicology results provide highly accurate information about what was present in the body at the time, they do not directly identify prescription or dispensing patterns, with non‐adherence certainly a common issue in BD. Similarly, we cannot determine the timing of any potential non‐adherence. For example, some decedents may have been taking other prescribed medications that were discontinued proximal to death but far enough in time from death/toxicology, based on their rate of clearance, to not have been detected. Common presence of alcohol (42.3%) and opioids (27.7%) also points to an interplay of acute states of intoxication and comorbidities that further complicate the picture. The BD group had a significantly higher proportion of comorbid alcohol/substance use problems, which underscores the importance of addressing substance use as a chronic and acute risk factor for suicidal behaviors in this patient population. Other differences between the BD and non‐BD groups such as likelihood of an identified psychosocial stressor should not be over‐interpreted given the possible differences in the degree to which coroners seek out additional contributing/motivating factors once diagnoses of major mental illness have been established. Finally, toxicology results were only available for a subsample of both groups, mostly among those who died by self‐poisoning. Coroner investigation methodology and toxicology panels have also evolved over time, which could complicate the interpretation of some of the findings across time and for newer medications, although it is unlikely to be a systematic bias. Therefore, while the results are informative, they may not fully generalize to all BD suicide deaths.

In conclusion, the results from this large sample of BD suicide deaths reinforce known patterns of sociodemographic and clinical differences between BD and non‐BD suicide groups. The patterns of medications and substances present at the time of death also suggest some change over time in the pharmacotherapy and utilization pattern of compounds among those with BD who die by suicide, such as a reduced presence of mood stabilizers. While causality cannot be claimed based on this study alone, there is certainly reason for concern and need for further exploration of pharmacotherapy at the time of suicide death in people with BD, ideally also linked to treatment patterns in living controls with BD.
