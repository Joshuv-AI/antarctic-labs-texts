Latent profile analysis and influence factors of self-compassion among colorectal cancer patients with enterostomy from China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Han Hu, Wei Dou, Min Qian, Yingdi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study was designed to explore the potential categories and their characteristics of self-compassion in Chinese enterostomy patients and then to investigate related factors.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingThe research focused on enterostomy patients who were hospitalised in two tertiary hospitals in Yangzhou City, China, between Nov 2022 and Aug 2023.Participants222 adult enterostomy patients in China completed the questionnaires.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThis study investigated scores from the Self-Compassion Scale, Perceived Stress Scale and the Social Support Rating Scale. Information on the patients included: age, gender, marital status, monthly household income, types of medical insurance, education level, place of residence, enterostomy complications, postoperative time and whether adjuvant chemotherapy was given.ResultsThree profiles of self-compassion in enterostomy patients were identified: ‘low self-compassion group’ (class 1), ‘moderate self-compassion group’ (class 2) and ‘high self-compassion group’ (class 3), accounting for 40.5%, 28.0% and 31.5%, respectively. The multivariate logistic analysis showed adjuvant chemotherapy, social support (P＜0.05), types of enterostomy, education level and gender (P<0.05) were the factors influencing the potential profile characteristics of enterostomy patients.ConclusionsThere is significant heterogeneity in self-compassion among enterostomy patients, and nearly half of them belong to the ‘low self-compassion group’. Focused interventions are required for females, patients with permanent enterostomy, low educational level and undergoing adjuvant radiotherapy. The self-compassion ability of patients can be effectively improved by reducing perceived stress and enhancing social support. These findings provide a basis for constructing targeted intervention strategies.Keywords: Observational Study; Adult psychiatry; Stress, Psychological; Anus Neoplasms; Cross-Sectional Studies

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent malignancy within the digestive system. According to the World Cancer Statistics 2022, the global incidence and mortality rates of CRC rank third and second among malignant tumours. There are about 1.92 million new cases of CRC globally each year, accounting for 9.3% of all cancer deaths.1 Enterostomy is a commonly effective surgical intervention for CRC patients,2 which effectively relieves intestinal obstruction symptoms, smooths intestinal contents’ output and promotes distal intestinal anastomosis healing.3 According to the United Ostomy Associations of America, about 725 000 and 1 million patients are living with an enterostomy in America;4 the number of enterostomy patients has reached 1 million in China, with approximately 100 000 new life-saving enterostomy operations added annually.5 Hence, enterostomy patients constitute a large group.

Enterostomy, despite life-saving for patients, unfortunately, individuals often encounter a range of physical, psychological and social challenges postoperative recovery. Physically, they may experience stoma-related complications, such as skin erosion, necrosis, prolapse, parastomal hernia and other related issues.6 Psychosocially, patients may experience changes in defecation dysfunction, body image disturbance and sexual dysfunctions, among other concerns.7 These challenges may severely impair patients’ mental well-being, often encompassing anxiety, depression, social isolation, stigma and diminished self-worth.7,9 Anxiety and depression are particularly common among enterostomy patients.10 A systematic review and meta-analysis of global CRC patients with enterostomy revealed that 47.6% of patients experienced anxiety, while 38.86% suffered from depression. In comparison to the global prevalence rate of depression at 3.8%, the prevalence among enterostomy patients is approximately 10 times higher.4 11 Previous literature had documented that anxiety and depression are associated with increased risk of unfavourable outcomes, including reduced treatment adherence, poorer prognosis, diminished quality of life, escalated medical expenses and increased higher rehospitalisation rates.12 Moreover, the psychological distress of enterostomy patients may persist for a long time after the operation and is often inadequately valued and effectively addressed in clinical practice.13

At present, it is particularly important to identify positive psychological traits that may alleviate the psychological burden of this vulnerable group. In recent years, the rapidly developing field of positive psychology has increasingly emphasised those intrinsic protective factors that can enhance psychological resilience and emotional regulation ability.14 Among these, self-compassion, as a highly psychological resource, has demonstrated significant clinical value. American psychologist Kristin Neff first proposed self-compassion in 2003 and applied it to psychology.15 Self-compassion includes three elements: self-kindness and self-judgement, common humanity and isolation, and mindfulness and over-identification. Self-kindness refers to the ability of an individual to exhibit kindness, understanding and tolerance towards themselves in times of difficulty, setbacks or when life is not progressing as desired. Common humanity refers to viewing suffering as a shared human experience that connects the world. Mindfulness involves embracing suffering in the present moment rather than engaging in rumination or avoidance. Self-criticism, isolation and over-identification represent the opposing poles of these dimensions. The three components of self-compassion work together to mitigate an individual’s negative thought patterns and tendency to catastrophise. This, in turn, promotes a kind and tolerant attitude towards life’s challenges.

A meta-analysis of 21 cross-sectional studies reported a modest negative association between self-compassion and psychopathology in non-clinical samples (r=−0.052),16 suggesting that higher self-compassion may be associated with better mental health conditions. Similar results were found in clinical populations. A meta-analysis of 51 studies involving individuals with chronic illnesses revealed a robust negative correlation between self-compassion and psychological distress (r =−0.54). This relationship was particularly significant when psychological distress was specifically measured as the level of depression. Based on these cross-sectional study findings, longitudinal studies have provided preliminary evidence for the causal relationship. Longitudinal research on cancer patients have suggested that self-compassion is associated with reduced psychological distress and improved psychological adjustment over time.17 Intervention studies further support these findings. Numerous systematic reviews have shown that interventions based on self-compassion training are effective in reducing depressive and anxiety symptoms in patients with chronic health conditions.18,20 Beyond its psychological benefits, self-compassion also significantly contributes to the physical and behavioural aspects of the patient. It is a protective factor against stress-induced inflammation and may reduce interleukin-6 levels.21 Among cancer patients, self-compassion can promote engagement in positive health behaviours, including improved treatment adherence and symptom management.22 Taken together, these research findings highlight the multidimensional role of self-compassion in promoting psychological adjustment, physiological regulation and adaptive health behaviours. Self-compassion constitutes an essential internal resource for the cancer patients’ mental well-being. Therefore, as a valuable psychological resource for cancer patients, self-compassion merits further exploration and uncovering its intrinsic value.

Most of the existing studies on self-compassion have focused on patients with lung and breast cancer, whereas limited attention has been paid to those with enterostomy patients.23 24 Due to the variations in cognitive levels among different categories of cancer patients, the extent of self-compassion is not consistent. However, a standardised criterion for classifying varying levels of self-compassion in this group has yet to be established, and the influencing factors may also be impacted by the heterogeneity among different groups. Moreover, most previous studies have adopted a variable-centred analysis approach that neglects the heterogeneity among this population with differing characteristics. This study fills a gap by focusing on self-compassion in enterostomy patients through a person-centred approach. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a person-centred approach that enables researchers to identify unobserved subgroups based on response patterns. The differences between these groups represent critical targets for precise intervention. The potential characteristics of individuals can be assessed based on their response patterns to explicit variables. Individuals exhibiting similar traits may be grouped into the same category, facilitating accurate calculation of class classification probabilities.25 This provides a solid foundation for targeted interventions.

Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) investigate the current status of self-compassion among enterostomy patients; (2) identify potential latent profiles using LPA; and (3) explore whether general demographic features, perceived stress and social support are relevant in potential classes of self-compassion in enterostomy patients. We hypothesised that enterostomy patients’ self-compassion could be divided into three distinct latent subgroups by LPA and that different subgroups would demonstrate systematic differences in demographic characteristics, social support and perceived stress.

Objects and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from Nov 2022 to Aug 2023 using a convenience sampling method to recruit enterostomy patients who were hospitalised in two tertiary grade A hospitals in Yangzhou, China. Data were collected through validated self-report questionnaires assessing self-compassion, perceived stress and social support. The inclusion criteria for prospective participants were as follows: (1) a diagnosis of CRC; (2) underwent enterostomy more than 1 month before participation in the study; (3) age ≥18 years old; (4) conscious without communication difficulties, and able to complete the questionnaire independently or with assistance; and (5) voluntarily engaged in this project and provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) combined with serious heart, brain, kidney and other serious diseases or cancer metastasis and (2) currently receiving psychiatric or psychological treatment.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using Kendall’s clinical research estimation formula, which recommends the sample size should be 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables. A total of 26 independent variables are included in this research, including 14 demographic and disease-related variables and 12 scale-related variables. Among these, the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS) comprised six dimensions, the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) had two dimensions, the General Self-Efficacy Scale included one dimension and the Social Support Scale had three dimensions. This study determined the sample size based on five times the independent variable, requiring a minimum of 130 cases. After considering a 15% loss rate and invalid questionnaires, the final estimated sample size ranged from 153 to 306 cases. A total of 256 enterostomy patients were recruited for this study, of whom 222 completed valid questionnaires, with an effective response rate of approximately 96.5%.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Data collection

The study was performed over ten months from 1 Nov 2022 to 31 Aug 2023. The investigators were trained, and 20 to 25 questionnaires were collected as a presurvey to test the readability of the questionnaire items, forming a formal questionnaire. During the formal survey, the investigators selected the respondents with strict adherence criteria for the inclusion and exclusion. The research purpose, requirements, filling methods and precautions were explained clearly to the respondents. The survey was conducted using paper questionnaires after the respondents had been fully informed and provided their consent. The investigators participated in the whole process. If there were any questions, the investigators would provide objective guidance and answer them without subjective suggestions or judgement.

Measures

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients were designed by the researchers to include the age, gender, education level, marital status, place of residence, monthly household income, types of medical insurance, enterostomy complications, self-care ability, enterostomy location and adjuvant chemotherapy that had been administered.

The self-compassion scale

The SCS was developed by Neff26 to measure the level of self-compassion in individuals. Composed of six dimensions—self‐kindness, self‐judgement, common humanity, isolation, mindfulness and over‐identification—there are 26 items in total. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 (‘rarely’) to 5 (‘almost always’). The three dimensions of self-criticism, isolation and over-identification are reversed scored; the lowest total score was 26; and the highest was 130. The SCS was revised by Chinese researchers in 2011, and the Chinese version has demonstrated adequate internal consistency.27 Cronbach’s alpha for this scale in this study was 0.752.

The perceived stress scale

The PSS was developed by Cohen28 in 1983 and revised by Chinese researchers in 2003.29 It includes two dimensions, tension and loss of control, with 14 items in all. Each item on the questionnaire was scored using a 5-point Likert scale; with the range of points from 0 to 4, the items within the loss of control dimension being reversed and scored. The total measurement range is from 0 to 56, with 0 to 28 indicating no stress or normal stress, 29 to 42 representing a moderate stress level, and 43 to 56 indicating a high stress level. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for PSS in the present study was 0.776.

Social support rating scale

The Chinese version of the SSRS was developed in China by Xiao in 1986.30 It comprised 10 items across three dimensions, including objective support (three items), subjective support (four items) and social support utilisation (three items). The total score represents the level of social support received. The total score is classified into three levels, ranging from 12 to 66, with <33 indicating that the individual has low social support; 33 to 45 is considered average; and >45 is considered high social support. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale in question was 0.835.

Statistical analysis

Mplus 8.3 software was used for the LPA. Using the scores of the dimensions of the SCS for enterostomy patients as explicit variables for the model, we began with a single-category model, and the number of categories in the model was progressively increased until the best model fit index was obtained.31 Indicators of the fit of the model included: (1) the Log-likelihood ratio, the Akaike information criterion (AIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and the adjusted BIC (aBIC) were used to estimate the predictive value of BIC. The lower the AIC, BIC, and aBIC, the more accurate the fit of the model. (2) A value of information entropy (ranging from 0 to 1) closer to 1 was indicative of a more accurate classification. (3) In the Lo-Mendell-Ruben test index (LMRT) and bootstrapped likelihood ratio test index (BLRT), when the value of P was less than 0.05, the K profiles were considered to be better than the k-1 profiles. As well as assessing the suitability of the model, the number of classes should be decided in conjunction with the practical significance of the classification.

After determining the best potential profiles, statistical data analysis was carried out using SPSS 25.0. For measured data that follow a normal distribution, the mean±SD was employed to describe the data, and the groups were compared using an analysis of variance to determine the differences between the groups. The data that did not satisfy the criteria for normality using the median (IQR) and the rank sum test was applied to compare between groups. The data were expressed in terms of counts with frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were analysed using χ2. A disordered logistic regression model with multiple classifications was used, with the potential category of self-compassion among enterostomy patients as the dependent variable. The factors exhibiting notable discrepancies in the univariate analysis outcomes were employed as independent variables, and the influencing factors of several possible potential profiles of self-compassion of enterostomy patients were discussed. Statistically significant at p<0.05. The total score of the SCS is converted to a Z-score for standardisation.

Ethical considerations

The Declaration of Helsinki was strictly implemented in this research, and all patients signed the informed consent form and participated voluntarily. This study had the approval of the Ethics Committee of Yangzhou University School of Nursing·School of Public Health (YZUHL20220086).

Demographic characteristics of participants

Among the 222 enterostomy patients, the average age was 62.18±8.56 years old; of these, 19.4% were 18–54 years old, 37.4% were 55–65 years old and 43.2% were 66–75 years old. A significant gender difference was observed, with male patients comprising 60.4% and female patients making up 39.6%. Nearly half of the patients (54.9%) had attained an education level below high school. Most patients were married (93.7%), and half resided in rural areas (44.6%). The most common payment method was the new rural cooperative medical system, accounting for 50.5%. Most patients had an annual household income below ¥5000 (70.3%), and the majority did not receive chemotherapy (58.6%). Regarding self-care ability, 33.8% of patients were completely dependent on others. Naming latent profile categories of self-compassion in enterostomy patients

The distribution of conditional mean values of the three potential profiles on the six dimensions of self-compassion is presented in figure 2. The categories were named according to the explicit characteristics of each dimension of the scale. The class 1 group consisted of 90 individuals; they represented 40.5% of the total sample. It demonstrated that the positive dimension scores were all at a low level, and the negative dimension scores were all at a high level, especially the over-identification. As a result, the name of this group was aptly named ‘low self-compassion group’ (C1). The class 2 group was made up of a summary of 222 participants (28.0%). In which the scores of positive and negative dimensions were at a relatively moderate level, indicating a peaceful mood in individuals in this subgroup, ‘moderate self-compassion group’ (C2) was used. The third group comprised 70 individuals, representing 31.5% of the sample. This group was distinguished by high scores in self-kindness, mindfulness and common humanity, but low scores in isolation, self-judgement and over-identification. It suggested individuals have a high level of acceptance over their enterostomy, with few relatively negative impacts in this group. This group was therefore named the ‘high self-compassion group’ (C3).

Multivariate analysis of latent potential profiles of self-compassion in enterostomy patients

Taking the latent profile category of self-compassion in enterostomy patients was considered the outcome variable, and factors that were statistically significant in the univariate analyses were taken as independent variables. An unordered multinomial logistic regression model was constructed for multivariate analysis. As unordered multinomial logistic regression is designed to make automatic comparisons of categorical data, dummy variables are not required. Class 1 and class 3 were used as target groups to compare class 2 vs class 1, class 3 vs class 1 and class 3 vs class 2. In class 1, in comparison to class 2, participants with higher social support scores were more frequently classified as class 2, particularly females. However, patients with higher perceived stress scores, those with low education and those without adjuvant chemotherapy were frequently classified as class 1. In the comparison between class 3 and class 1, individuals with lower levels of perceived stress scores, higher social support scores, male and tertiary education were more likely to be in class 3. In addition, patients with a temporary enterostomy were more frequently classified to class 3 compared with those with a permanent enterostomy. In class 2, compared with class 3, males, individuals with lower education and those with higher social support scores were more frequently classified as class 2. Moreover, patients with permanent enterostomy were more frequently classified as class 2 than those with temporary enterostomy. Detailed outcomes are presented in table 3.

Discussion

This study used LPA to identify the potential subgroups of self-compassion among enterostomy patients, and to explore their associations with demographic characteristics, perceived stress, and social support. The findings revealed that self-compassion can be divided into three distinct latent profiles. As far as we know, this is the first study to explore the heterogeneity of self-compassion in enterostomy patients using a person-centred approach. This approach provides a foundation for developing tailored psychological and supportive interventions to enhance self-compassion in patients undergoing enterostomy.

Latent profile analysis (LPA) of self-compassion among enterostomy patients

In this study, the population was classified into three distinct subgroups based on the varying dimensions of self-compassion: ‘low self-compassion group’, ‘moderate self-compassion group’ and ‘high self-compassion group’, accounting for 40.5%, 28.0% and 31.5%, respectively. This classification result supports our initial hypothesis that the self-compassion levels of enterostomy patients are not homogeneous but show significant individual differences. This finding indicates that nearly half of the patients exhibited low levels of self-compassion. Notably, these results differ from previous findings observed in broader cancer patient populations.32 Wei reported that patients suffering from cancer exhibited five profiles that can be identified and exhibited ‘average self-compassion’ (54%). A possible explanation could be differences in disease severity among different cancer patients.33 Enterostomy patients not only contend with the psychological and physical challenges of cancer but also have to grapple with the loss of normal physiological functions and an impaired body image.33 This situation may cause considerable distress to patients, leading to the development of negative emotions such as stigma and avoidance. Furthermore, this study only focused on CRC enterostomy patients, whose psychological adaptation process may be different from that of patients with Crohn’s disease. Cancer patients often face cancer diagnoses, urgent surgeries, fears of recurrence, and the burden of treatment, all of which may lead to reduced levels of self-compassion.34 35 In contrast, patients with other non-cancer chronic diseases often endure symptoms for a long time and tend to view ostomy as a way to improve their quality of life,36 which may increase self-compassion.

Enterostomy patients from the ‘moderate self-compassion group’ (C2) exhibited relatively balanced scores on positive and negative dimensions. The findings may indicate that the negative and positive dimensions of self-compassion may be scored at the same level in individuals. This result had been confirmed in previous studies.37 Several reasons may have contributed to this viewpoint. First, rather than losing their lives, patients prefer enterostomy to save their lives. Second, Chinese traditional culture advocates a doctrine of the mean and does not encourage extremism, which might boost perceptions of self-compassion in that group of patients. However, this group of individuals accounts for a relatively small number (28.0%), suggesting that emotional regulation remains a challenge for most patients.

Enterostomy patients from the ‘high self-compassion group’ (C3) held an optimistic and positive attitude towards their enterostomy. The group was characterised by patients with a temporary stoma, which had less impact on patients.

Factors affecting classification

Gender differences in self-compassion profiles. Our study showed that males were more likely to be classified into the high self-compassion group, whereas females tended to fall into the low self-compassion group. This result was also found in a meta-analysis of differences in self-compassion by gender.38 There are several potential psychosocial mechanisms that may explain this gender difference. For instance, women are often socialised to prioritise empathy and looking after others, which may limit their ability to extend the same empathy to themselves. Although these traits are beneficial in society and interpersonal relationships, when facing physical and mental challenges, they may unintentionally prevent women from dealing with personal predicaments with kindness and self-understanding. In addition, women are more likely to exhibit characteristics such as emotional sensitivity, delicacy, self-criticism and rumination, which may negatively affect their ability to self-compassion. In contrast, men may be more inclined to adopt problem-focused coping strategies and exhibit a more open-minded attitude when encountering difficulties.39 These traits might facilitate a more adaptive response to the bodily changes and psychosocial stress associated with enterostomy. However, other studies have reported no significant gender differences in self-compassion.33 Despite these inconsistencies, the role of gender in self-compassion remains a topic of ongoing debate and deserves further exploration. Regardless of these differences, self-compassion is generally recognised as a beneficial psychological resource in all populations. Therefore, in clinical practice, healthcare professionals should be aware of these gender differences and pay more attention to the mental health needs of female patients. Early psychological support may help alleviate their emotional distress and enhance their self-compassion.

Differences in self-compassion across educational levels. The findings of this investigation showed that individuals with lower educational levels were more likely to be classified into the low self-compassion group. Better-educated patients may be more proactive in obtaining disease-related information, thereby having a broader understanding of their disease. This greater health literacy may help to assess disease progression and prognosis more accurately, increase acceptance of enterostomy and demonstrate greater proficiency in acquiring knowledge related to enterostomy and self-care skills.40 41 However, patients with low levels of education may have limited cognition of the disease and be immersed in the traumatic emotions brought by the stoma. Healthcare professionals should place more emphasis on the psychological status of patients with lower education levels. It is imperative that accessible methodologies be developed to facilitate patient comprehension and proficiency in the knowledge and skills associated with enterostomal nursing.

Impact of adjuvant chemotherapy on self-compassion. Limited research has directly examined the relationship between radiotherapy and self-compassion. The results of the study demonstrated that enterostomy patients receiving chemotherapy were more likely to belong to the low self-compassion group. Cancer patients receiving adjuvant treatments often experience elevated psychological distress due to disease severity, physical symptoms and treatment-related toxicity.42 For instance, common side effects of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, such as fatigue, nausea and immunosuppression, may intensify emotional tension, weaken patients’ sense of self-friendliness and coping ability.43 In the context of enterostomy, these treatment-related stressors may be further compounded by stoma-related stigma and altered body image, exacerbating emotional vulnerability. Although the current findings are consistent with existing evidence regarding psychological distress in cancer care, it is difficult to establish causal relationships due to the cross-sectional study design.

Association between enterostomy type and self-compassion. This study found that patients with temporary enterostomy were more inclined to the high self-compassion group, which contributes new evidence to the existing research, demonstrating that the type of enterostomy significantly influences self-compassion. Patients with temporary enterostomy have the opportunity to wait for the restoration of their stoma to a normal body image, while patients with permanent enterostomy must rely on a stoma bag for life and are unable to control their bowel movements independently. This situation significantly impacts their daily lives and work, leading to considerable psychological distress for these patients.44 45 In addition, permanent enterostomy is prone to enterostomy complications, such as peristomal dermatitis and parastomal hernia, which increase their hospitalisation rate and financial burden.46 These findings have implications for clinical practice, and personalised and continuous guidance strategies—during the orientation, sensitive, control and evaluation stages—can be used to support patients. In addition, internet-based platforms can assist in monitoring and follow-up, which may help patients manage enterostomy complications and psychological adaptation more effectively. These findings indicate that the type of enterostomy significantly affects self-compassion levels. Temporary enterostomy patients may exhibit higher self-compassion due to expectations of stoma reversal and body image recovery.

The role of perceived stress in self-compassion. Patients with higher perceived stress had lower scores of self-compassion. This finding is consistent with the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, which emphasises that stress responses are shaped not only by external events but also by an individual’s cognitive evaluations and available coping resources.47 Among enterostomy patients, impaired physical function, uncertain prognosis and concerns about body image may all lead to heightened stress levels.48 These psychological burdens may prevent practising self-kindness or emotional regulation, which is a core element of self-compassion.15 Previous research suggests that positive thought-based approaches may help mitigate perceived stress and indirectly support self-compassion.49 Therefore, caregivers need to recognise patients with high perceived stress and provide psychological help such as positive thought-based interventions to help patients manage their stress correctly, such as positive thought-based interventions, to help patients manage their stress effectively,50 improve their psychological coping skills for perceived stress and further enhance the subjective feeling of self-compassion.

Social support is a key factor influencing self-compassion. This study found that SSRS scores significantly differed across self-compassion subgroups (p<0.05), and logistic regression analysis suggested that social support may serve as a protective factor for self-compassion. Patients with higher perceived social support tended to be in the higher self-compassion group, consistent with the previous findings.51 According to the Stress and Coping Model, the occurrence of stressful events does not directly impact individuals’ mental health; rather, it is the individual’s available internal and external coping resources.52 Social support, particularly emotional and instrumental support, may buffer the effects of stress by fostering a sense of belonging, self-worth and reassurance. For enterostomy patients, social support is an important resource for patients and allows them to cope with the disease.53 It may also promote acceptance of the enterostomy, increase present-moment awareness, and foster hope and resilience. Spousal or family involvement in patient education and care may help mobilise these support resources more effectively. Additionally, the accessibility of healthcare professionals as part of a patient’s support network can provide psychological safety.50 54 Telemedicine platforms are expected to further enhance communication efficiency and the accessibility of medical services.55 Therefore, it is essential to systematically assess the social support status and encourage family members such as spouses to participate in daily care. In addition, medical staff can provide support through telemedicine to help patients enhance self-compassion.

Limitations

This study provides initial evidence regarding self-compassion among enterostomy patients. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the samples were obtained from tertiary hospitals within the same city, which may limit the generalisability of our results to other regions or community healthcare settings. Expanding the sampling to include patients from different levels of healthcare institutions in future studies is encouraged. Second, data collection relied primarily on self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce reporting bias. Future studies may consider alternative methods to mitigate this limitation. Third, the exclusion of patients with severe psychiatric disorders may limit the applicability of the findings to this population. Future research should focus on this group to explore potential differences. Finally, due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships between variables cannot be established. Longitudinal research is recommended to further validate the observed associations.

Conclusions

This study used a latent profile model to identify three distinct categories of self-compassion in enterostomy patients: low, moderate and high self-compassion groups. The main factors associated with the low self-compassion group included female, a lower education level, adjuvant radiotherapy and having a permanent enterostomy. These findings highlight the necessity for psychological interventions targeting high-risk populations. Social support was also identified as a protective factor for self-compassion. For clinical management, early screening and personalised interventions should be prioritised, focusing on patients in the low and moderate self-compassion groups. Future research should expand sample sizes and longitudinal designs to validate these findings.
