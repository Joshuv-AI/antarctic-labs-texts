A Quality Improvement Project to Improve the Utilization of an Intraoperative Rapid Response System
Authors: Asheen Rama, Daniel Qian, Ty Forbes, Ellen Wang, Lynda Knight, Marc Berg, Thomas J. Caruso
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Rapid response teams (RRTs) improve morbidity by reducing the incidence of cardiac arrests. Although providers commonly activate RRTs on acute care wards, they are infrequently used perioperatively. At our institution, two intraoperative calls for help exist: staff assists (SAs) and code blues (CBs). The SA functions analogously to an RRT, and the CB indicates cardiopulmonary arrest. Given the success of RRTs, this project aimed to increase the use of the SA system. Our primary goal was to increase the ratio of SA to CB alerts by 50% within 6 months.Methods:A quality improvement team led this project at an academic pediatric hospital in Northern California. The team analyzed the current state and identified an achievable goal. After developing key drivers, they implemented monthly simulations to teach providers the signs of clinical deterioration and to practice activating the SA system. In addition to measuring the ratio of SA to CB alerts, the team surveyed the etiologies of SA and measured process satisfaction.Results:Before the introduction of this initiative, the ratio of SA to CB alerts were 1:13.3. These improvements efforts led to an increase of SA to CB alerts to 1.5:1 (P = 0.0003). Twenty-three anesthesiologists provided etiologies for SA, reporting laryngospasm as the most common reason (30.4%). Nineteen nurses completed the SA survey and reported high satisfaction.Conclusion:This project successfully increased the utilization of a rapid response protocol in a pediatric perioperative setting using improvement methodologies and a simulation-based educational program.

INTRODUCTION

Rapid response teams (RRTs) provide early interventions in acutely deteriorating patients, typically in the acute care setting. In 2004, an initiative by the Institute of Healthcare Improvement promoted the development of RRTs in US hospitals as part of a greater effort to improve patient safety.1 An RRT is often comprised of intensive care physicians, nurses, and respiratory therapists who quickly mobilize to manage adverse respiratory or cardiac events to reduce the risk of further morbidity. In the perioperative setting, anesthesiologists are responsible for managing highly dynamic medical care while surgeons operate. The differentiation between typical perioperative resuscitation and intraoperative crisis is more ambiguous than in acute care patients.

Intraoperative cardiac arrests (CAs) are low-frequency, high-risk events. The incidence of pediatric CA is 5.3 per 10,000, and the overall mortality for pediatric perianesthetic CA is 17.7%.2 Pediatric rapid response systems (RRSs) are associated with reduced incidence of CA and mortality in hospitalized patients.3,4 Providers have reported a 38% reduction in cardiopulmonary arrests and a 21% reduction in pediatric mortality after the implementation of RRS.4 Pediatric CA survival may improve from 27% to 80% with early identification of patient deterioration.5 Furthermore, in a large, multicenter cohort of pediatric medical emergency events with rapid response team activations, 53% of patients were transferred to the intensive care unit but few required cardiopulmonary resuscitation (0.5%).6 Although this evidence supports reductions in mortality from RRS, investigations are needed to optimize RRS to proactively identify high-risk patients.7,8 Barriers to effective RRS activations include inadequate activation mechanisms based on physiologic criteria and lack of intangible factors such as institutional safety culture.9

In our operating rooms, similar to RRS, the call-for-help system consists of a binary choice of staff assist (SA) or code blue (CB). SA alerts activate the support of perioperative providers to reduce further patient decompensation, while providers initiate CB alerts in response to cardiopulmonary arrests. Perioperative staff must distinguish between a SA and CB to mobilize appropriate resources. During an SA, only an anesthesiologist responds for urgent assistance. However, a CB alert activates an interdisciplinary group of expert providers from the perioperative environment and the intensive care unit. Intensivists, pharmacists, and critical care nurses supplement perioperative resources to improve compressions, prepare critical medications, and administer massive volume blood transfusions. The perioperative leaders encourage all providers, including nurses, trainees, anesthesiologists, and surgeons, to initiate SA and CB. Despite the presence of the SA system, providers underutilized it due to ambiguities around which conditions warrant SA alerts and how to activate it. Although providers were initiating CB alerts and directly calling anesthesiologists to help, the underutilization of the SA alert and using nonstandard processes to call for help were suboptimal.

Utilizing quality improvement (QI) methodology, a multidisciplinary team conducted a current state analysis, identified key drivers, and initiated a series of interventions to improve the use of SA alerts to improve patient morbidity. The primary aim of this QI initiative was to increase the SA-to-CB ratio from 1:13.3 by 50% within 6 months and demonstrate sustainment for 10 months. Our secondary aims explored the incidence of SA, typical etiologies for SA activations, and nurse satisfaction with the SA process.

METHODS

Context

We conducted this QI project between September 2019 and December 2021 at Lucile Packard Children’s Hospital Stanford, an academic, quaternary care pediatric hospital in Northern California. The hospital contains 16 operating rooms, 10 preoperative bays, and 15 recovery rooms. It is a teaching hospital for physician and nurse trainees. The hospital performs approximately 1,000 operations per month, including Level 1 trauma patients. General pediatric anesthesiology cases requiring an anesthesiology care team include all main operating rooms and radiology sites (magnetic resonance imaging, computed tomography, and interventional radiology). The project team excluded pediatric cardiac anesthesiology cases because they utilize a unique intraoperative help system that includes cardiopulmonary bypass and cardiovascular intensive care unit providers. The internal review board granted a waiver of consent due to the QI nature of this project.

Intervention

As part of continuous quality and safety efforts, perioperative providers discussed the current state of the intraoperative call-for-help system. The team’s participants included faculty physicians, quality managers, simulation educators, nurse educators, and QI leadership. During a perioperative SA alert, the expected process is for providers to push the appropriate wall button and request help using a application on their cellular phones called Voalte (Voalte, Inc., Sarasota, Fla.). The wall button activates local alarms and alerts specific perioperative personnel on their Voalte applications. Providers trigger the CB alert similarly. After a problem analysis related to underutilizing the SA alert system, the team recognized that providers used nonstandard processes to call for help, such as contacting an anesthesiologist directly. When coupled with the low incidence of perioperative urgencies and hesitancy to activate the call system, the overall SA utilization was low. Furthermore, there is the added challenge of knowledge retention involving the appropriate actions to perform during low-frequency, high-risk events such as SAs and CBs. Based on this analysis, the team formulated a primary goal and several key drivers to improve the SA alert system (Fig. 1). The team established several interventions based on these key drivers, including cognitive aids, recurring educational programs, and training in-services.

Key driver diagram. A multidisciplinary team used QI methodology to perform a current state analysis and identify key drivers. Key drivers were used to create a series of interventions to increase the ratio of SAs to CB alerts by 50% within 6 months.

They implemented this multifaceted educational initiative in July 2020. The QI team provided monthly in situ operating room simulations that taught how and when to activate SA and CB alerts. A simulation instructor provided interdisciplinary teams of anesthesiologists, surgeons, nurses, and technicians with training scenarios and debriefs that focused on the appropriate use of the call system. Scenario debriefs focused on highlighting the various kinds of perioperative medical crises. Monthly simulations included various medical emergencies, such as management of malignant hyperthermia and cardiac arrhythmias. All scenario debriefs focused on the educational goals related to the call-for-help system. We conducted the simulations on Monday morning due to a standing delayed start, allowing maximum staff participation. During the first simulation debrief in July 2020, participants discussed the lack of cognitive aids that explained the call-for-help system. In August 2020, the team placed cognitive aids next to the call for help wall buttons throughout the perioperative setting. These aids consisted of laminated, color-coded wall posters describing the steps to take when nurses called for an SA or a CB. Monthly OR nursing meetings were reminders to reinforce concepts and help with knowledge retention. Additionally, starting in July 2020, at monthly perioperative nursing meetings, including the entire nursing staff, the project team reinforced the educational goals on properly utilizing the call-for-help system. Finally, the team added the appropriate use of the SA and CB alert systems to new employee orientation.

Outcomes

The project team sought to improve the ratio of SA to CB activations by 50% within 6 months of the educational initiative. The secondary outcomes explored the incidence of SA activations, etiologies for SA activations, and nurse satisfaction with the SA process.

Measures

The investigators measured the primary outcome by examining the monthly wall button activations for SA and CB. The Director of Perioperative Clinical Informatics retrieved the data through a database that logged the number of alerts. The team normalized the data based on the average number of monthly pediatric general anesthesia cases, excluding cardiovascular cases.

The team analyzed the incidence of SA activations by reviewing the same dataset. Investigators determined the typical etiologies of SA activations through surveys of anesthesiologists who initiated them. They measured SA process satisfaction through surveys directed to perioperative nurses, who primarily activated the SA alerts.

Analysis

To analyze the primary outcome, investigators used a statistical process control X bar chart (Fig. 2). The y-axis represents a ratio of the number of SA to the number of CB in a specific month, divided by the average number of monthly surgical cases completed during the preintervention or postintervention period. Process mean lines, representing average pre and postintervention ratios, reflected the response to the implementation of the interventions. The baseline process mean line was approximately zero (0.000076), as many of the preintervention period months had little to no staff assist utilization. The statistical process control chart included three SD control limits (CLs), and a CL break indicated special cause variation attributed to nonrandom conditions, per Western Electric Rules.10 We collected and analyzed the data using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, Wash.). Unpaired t test calculations were performed using GraphPad Software (graphpad.com, San Diego, Calif.). The team considered a P <0.05 as significant.

After the team completed the interventions, the SA-to-CB ratio averaged 1.5:1.

Regarding secondary outcomes, a run chart displayed the number of monthly SAs with process means representing average monthly SA incidence (Fig. 3). The team used unpaired t test calculations (GraphPad Software) to determine the difference in SAs. We surveyed anesthesiologists to determine the most common etiologies of SA. The team collected the data using a web-based survey tool called Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Inc., Provo, Utah), and displayed the results as a Pareto chart (Fig. 4). Last, we administered a process satisfaction survey to nurses with responses ranging from “satisfied,” “neutral,” or “dissatisfied (Fig. 5).” We analyzed the data using Microsoft Excel and displayed the descriptive results as a bar graph.

RESULTS

Primary Outcome

The initial query identified 10,954 noncardiac general pediatric anesthesia cases before the launch of this initiative from September 2019 through July 2020 with an average monthly case volume of 996 cases. From August 2020 through December 2021, there were 17,589 cases after the interventions, with an average monthly case volume of 1034 cases. Before the project, the SA-to-CB ratio was 1:13.3, indicating that for every 1 SA, there were 13.3 CBs. After the team completed the interventions, the SA-to-CB ratio averaged 1.5:1, indicating that for every 1.5 SA, there was 1 CB. This represents a 20-fold increase in the SA-to-CB ratio (P = 0.0003; 95% confidence interval [CI] −0.0027, −0.00095; Fig. 2).

Secondary Outcomes

The SA system utilization had a baseline monthly average incidence of 0.18 (SD [SD] 0.40) before the interventions and 3.18 (SD 2.10) after the intervention, representing a 17-fold increase in SA alerts (P < 0.0001; 95% CI −4.32, −1.67; Fig. 3).

Twenty-three anesthesiologists provided etiologies for SA alerts. The most commonly reported reason for SA activation was laryngospasm (30.4%), followed by vascular access help (19.8%), difficulty with intubation (16.3%), bronchospasm (15.7%), and hypoxemia (11.7%) (Fig. 4). The remaining 6% of reasons included refractory hypotension, regional anesthesia support, and logistical support for administering blood products.

Nineteen nurses completed the SA process survey. For all metrics, most participants expressed overall satisfaction with their SA experience. The nurses reported the highest rates of satisfaction for “The speed at which personnel responded after a call for help was initiated” (89% satisfied; 0% dissatisfied), “The knowledge regarding the difference between a SA and a CB” (74% satisfied, 5% dissatisfied), and “The knowledge regarding which wall button to activate to elicit additional intraoperative help” (63% satisfied, 0% dissatisfied). The remaining two metrics included “The knowledge on which to call for intraoperative help” (53% satisfied, 16% dissatisfied) and “The simplicity/ease of the call for help system” (58% satisfied, 21% dissatisfied) (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

At our institution, perioperative providers initiated CB alerts more frequently than SA alerts. Given the reduction in morbidity associated with rapid responses, we sought to increase the use of the SA alert system.4 This project resulted in an increase in the SA activation system. After 17 months, the ratio of SA to CB had increased to 20 times the baseline. The project team attributes the success of this initiative to the strengths of the project design and implementation, including engagement of multidisciplinary stakeholders early in the process, effective governance through resuscitation meetings, development of a comprehensive improvement model, and consistent education and messaging throughout monthly simulation training.

Before the initiation of this QI project, providers rarely used the standard initiation process for SAs, and by the conclusion of the data collection period, standard SA activations increased by 17-fold. A survey of the most common reasons that anesthesiologists initiated an SA included assistance with adverse airway events such as laryngospasm, which is consistent with call for help etiologies at other institutions.11 Nursing staff surveys reported the highest satisfaction for staff response time during calls for help. Nurses indicated that the ease of use and simplicity of the process were the areas with the greatest opportunity for further improvement. This finding may be attributed to past confusion related to multiple, nonstandard pathways for calling for help. Before implementing this project, providers utilized multiple pathways to initiate the help system, leading to poor coordination of response teams and misallocation of timely resources.12

Through our efforts, we improved the patient safety culture in the perioperative setting by standardizing the process for requesting early assistance. We deliberately designed the optimization of the SA and CB activation system to mirror the RRS that providers use in acute care settings. Similar to RRS, which reduce morbidity, we implemented our SA system to reduce intraoperative morbidity. Although it was beyond this project’s scope to measure objective patient outcomes, the authors plan to conduct further studies to determine if the increased incidence of SA alerts may have been associated with reduced CA morbidity. Given that results from previously published studies demonstrate a correlation between early assistance and reduced pediatric CA mortality in acute care settings, the authors seek to determine if such findings apply to the operating room setting.5 Given the low incidence of major morbidity and mortality, the project team plans to review this outcome after they have collected many years of data to adequately power the analysis. Since the incidence of mortality will always be low, the authors may consider using unplanned intensive care unit admission as a surrogate for improved patient outcomes. Future studies will investigate the efficacy of mandatory RRS activations based on physiologic criteria, given that this strategy may eliminate human error.8 Also, RRS systems may be designed to proactively identify high-risk patients before surgery commences to alert the relevant team members of potential intraoperative complications and their treatments.7–9 Additional studies will address balancing measures to determine if the increase in SA resulted in unintended consequences. Because nurses reported the highest dissatisfaction with the challenges of using the call-for-help system, the educational interventions aligned with improving these challenges. Other surveys of staff perception may yield valuable information to improve future iterations of the call-for-help system.

One of the key interventions were the in situ simulations. The project leaders designed the simulations to provide perioperative teams with opportunities to manage medical crises. Perioperative simulations improve technical and nontechnical skills and are an effective tool for teaching new techniques.13 The simulations were efficient and widely accepted by participants. Our perioperative simulations incorporated crisis resource management education, which increases preparedness, improves closed-loop communication and decreases team anxiety.14,15 With multiple educational interventions being implemented simultaneously and due to the lack of a control group, it was impossible to determine which interventions contributed to the project’s success. Despite the project’s success, the nursing satisfaction survey provided our team with areas for iterative improvement, including improving and further simplifying the process. In addition, the survey results inform the development of future simulations and other educational programs.

There were several limitations to this initiative. First, due to the low incidence of intraoperative CA in this pediatric population, we could not measure morbidity. Although we could not demonstrate direct clinical benefit, the association between calling for early help and reducing morbidity is well documented.3–9 Second, given the heterogeneity of the patient population, we were unable to control for patient factors that may have influenced SAs over the project period, but we did control for case volume. There is no reason to believe that the acuity of the patient population changed over the project period. Despite part of this project taking place during the initial phases of the COVID-19 pandemic, no significant process changes occurred in our perioperative environment that would affect this work. In addition, as with projects that are conducted at a single academic institution, our hospital’s centralized paging system may not be generalizable to other institutions that rely on alternative call-for-help systems. However, the general concept of a standardized intraoperative SA response team that follows rapid response principles may still be applied.

This improvement project describes implementing a rapid response protocol in a pediatric intraoperative setting. By training providers to mobilize help early using a standardized process, we dramatically increased the SA-to-CB alerts ratio. Ongoing educational simulations provide opportunities for continued program expansion and optimization. Future efforts will investigate differences in patient outcomes, including morbidity and other intraoperative and postoperative outcomes.
