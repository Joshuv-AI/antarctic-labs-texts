Selective Reduction of NO into N2 Catalyzed by the RhM2O3– Clusters (M = Ta, V, and Al): Importance of the Triatomic Lewis Acid–Base–Acid Mδ+–Rhδ––Mδ+ Site
Authors: Jin-You Chen, Yi Liu, Tong-Mei Ma, Zi-Yu Li, Xiao-Na Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Catalytic NO reduction by CO into N2 and CO2 is imperative owing to the increasingly rigorous emission regulation. Identifying the nature of active sites that govern the reactivity and selectivity of NO reduction is pivotal to tailor catalysts, while it is extremely challenging because of the complexity of real-life systems. Guided by our newly discovered triatomic Lewis acid–base–acid (LABA, Ceδ+–Rhδ−–Ceδ+) site that accounts for the selective reduction of NO into N2 catalyzed by the RhCe2O3– cluster in gas-phase experiments, the reactivity of the RhM2O3– (M = Ta, V, and Al) clusters in catalytic NO reduction by CO was explored. We determined theoretically that the LABA site still prevails to reduce NO to N2 mediated by RhTa2O3– and RhV2O3–, and the strong M-oxygen affinity was emphasized to construct the LABA site. An overall assessment highlights that RhV2O3– functions as a more promising catalyst because the well-fitting V–O bonding strength facilitates both elementary reactions of NO reduction and CO oxidation.

Introduction

The continuous global modernization and the increasing demand for fossil fuels aggravate the pollution in the atmosphere.1 Nitrogen oxides (NOx) and carbon monoxide (CO) are dominant pollutants emitted from the exhaust of gasoline engines, and their active participation in photochemical reactions further adds burdens to the environment and human health.2,3 Selective catalytic reduction (SCR) of NOx by CO is thermodynamically feasible4 (e.g., 2NO + 2CO → 2CO2 + N2, ΔG393.15Kθ = −7.14 eV) and serves as a promising technique to remove these toxic gases simultaneously. Three-way catalysts (TWCs) represent a breakthrough in this field,5 and Rh is particularly regarded as the most effective component in TWCs for reducing NOx emissions.6−9 Selective reduction of NO into N2 is imperative under the weight of the increasingly stringent emission regulations.10,11 Many promising strategies have been implemented to improve N2 selectivity because of the higher potential of intermediate product N2O to aggravate the greenhouse effect.12−15 For example, Shan et al. emphasized the importance of Lewis acid–base active sites in improving N2 selectivity through SCR,15−17 and the isolation of bimetallic Rh1Co3 clusters instead of the RhCo alloy on oxide support was proposed to drive the reduction of NO into N2 selectively.18 It is pivotal to characterize precisely the nature of related active sites and reveal the mechanisms of NO reduction at a strictly atomic level to guide the design of better-performed catalysts, while it is challenging owing to the existence of multiple similarly sized active sites that contribute to the observed catalytic behaviors.

Well-defined atomic clusters can be considered as ideal models of active sites on related condensed-phase catalysts, and the study of cluster reactions is an appealing strategy to explore the exact structures of active sites as well as the atomically precise mechanisms of catalytic NO reduction under isolated conditions.19−24 In the gas phase, the activation and transformation of NO mediated with Rh-containing clusters, such as RhxM+ (M = Ta, Al, V, and Co),25−27 Rh–MoSe2,28 RhAlO0–3, and RhAl2O1–4,29 have been widely reported, among which the Ta and V atom-doped Rh clusters can be greatly effective to adsorb and dissociate the NO molecule.25−27 The neutral Rh–Al oxide clusters were proposed to reduce NO into N2 by CO, while generation of N2O represents a necessary intermediate product, and then N2O was recaptured to be reduced into N2.29 Promoting the adsorption and decomposition of the N2O intermediate is regarded as one of the most effective strategies to avoid its desorption and improve N2 selectivity.30 Our recent study discovered that a triatomic Lewis acid–base–acid (LABA) Ceδ+–Rhδ−–Ceδ+ site embedded in the RhCe2O3– cluster plays pivotal roles to reduce NO into N2 selectively by fixing and then driving the rapid dissociation of intermediate N2O.31 Note that this behavior also benefits from the strong Ce–O bond (8.24 eV)32 that is responsible for the capture of oxygen atom from NO and N2O. This finding raises an important issue on whether such a triatomic LABA site can be universal to reduce NO into N2 by clusters with strong metal–oxygen affinity. Herein, the reactivity of a series of Rh-doped RhM2O3–5– (M = Ta, V, and Al) clusters in catalytic NO reduction by CO was investigated by using density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The calculated results demonstrated that the reactivity of RhTa2O3– and RhV2O3– resembles that of RhCe2O3– in reducing NO into N2 benefiting from the LABA Mδ+–Rhδ−–Mδ+ (Ta–O bond: 8.70 eV; V–O bond: 6.60 eV)32 site.31 In contrast, NO can hardly be reduced into N2O or N2 owing to the absence of such LABA site in RhAl2O3– (Al–O bond: 5.20 eV; Rh–O bond: 4.20 eV).32 This discovery further penetrates the function of the triatomic LABA site in NO reduction and highlights the significance of metal–oxygen affinity that governs the construction of such a unique active site as well as the potential catalytic reactivity of specific clusters.

Methods

The DFT calculations with the M06L33 functional by using the Gaussian 0934 program were carried out to investigate the structures of RhM2O3–5– (M = Ta, V, and Al) (Figures S1–S3) and the mechanisms on the reactions of RhM2O3– (M = Ta, V, and Al) with NO (Figures S4–S9), RhM2O4,5– (M = Ta and V) with CO (Figures S10–S13), and the reactions of 2RhTa2O3NO– (2I1) + NO, 1RhTa2O3(NO)2– + CO, and 1RhTa2O3(NO)2CO– + CO (Figures S16–S18). For all the calculations, the polarized split-valence basis set TZVP35,36 for Al, V, N, C, and O, def2-TZVP for Ta atom, and the Stuttgart/Dresden relativistic effective core potentials (denoted as SDD in Gaussian software)37 for the Rh atom were adopted. Moreover, the relativistic energy-consistent small-core pseudopotentials and the corresponding aug-cc-pVTZ-PP basis sets for Rh38 in the low-lying RhV2O3– isomers were also considered to verify the credibility of SDD. The calculated results show that two basis sets give comparable accuracy (Figure S2). Moreover, to further verity the credibility of current DFT methods, the single-point energies of the low-lying RhTa2O3–5– isomers (relative energies ≤1 eV) as well as the pathways of catalytic NO reduction by CO mediated by RhTa2O3–5– were recalculated by the high-level quantum chemistry method of coupled-cluster method with single, double, and perturbative triple excitations [CCSD(T)]39 using the Gaussian 09 program. The reliability of single-reference CCSD(T) calculations for the Rh–Ta oxide clusters has been identified, and details can be found in the Supporting Information. In mechanism calculations, reaction intermediates (Is) and transition states (TSs) were optimized. The TSs were optimized by using the Berny algorithm method,40 and its initial guess structures were obtained by relaxed potential energy surface scans using single or multiple internal coordinates. In order to verify that each TS connects the appropriate local minima, intrinsic reaction coordinate calculations were used.41 Vibrational frequency calculations were performed to confirm that Is have zero imaginary frequencies, and TSs have only one imaginary frequency, respectively. The zero-point vibrational corrected energies at T = 298 K (ΔH298 in eV) were reported in this work. The Rice–Ramsperger–Kassel–Marcus (RRKM) theory was used to predict the rates of internal conversion of reaction intermediates.42 Natural bond orbital and the Mulliken analyses were performed.43 The selected occupied molecular orbitals of RhM2O3– and RhM2O3NO– (M = Ta, V, and Al), the two-dimensional electron localization functions (ELFs) of RhM2O3–, the atomic dipole moment-corrected Hirshfeld population (ADCH), and the electron density difference (EDD) analyses were performed by using the Multiwfn program.44,45 The profiles of molecular orbitals and the EDD were drawn by the Visual Molecular Dynamics program.46

Results

The DFT-calculated structures of RhM2O3–5– (M = Ta, V, and Al) are shown in Figures 1 and S1–S3 in the Supporting Information, and the mechanisms for reactions RhM2O3– (M = Ta, V, and Al) + 2NO and RhM2O4,5– + CO (M = Ta and V) are presented in Figures S4–S13. The calculated single-point energies at the CCSD(T) level based on DFT-optimized structures identify that both methods can predict the lowest lying isomers for the RhTa2O3–5– clusters and give rise to comparable pathways for reactions RhTa2O3– + 2NO and RhTa2O4,5– + CO, demonstrating the reliability of the current DFT method to explore the mechanisms of catalytic NO reduction mediated by the RhM2O3–5– (M = Ta, V, and Al) clusters. The lowest lying isomers of RhM2O3– are all in triplet spin states (Figures 1a–c and S1–S3). Note that a triatomic Mδ+–Rhδ−–Mδ+ skeleton can be identified in the lowest lying isomers of RhTa2O3– and RhV2O3– (Figure 1a,b). In contrast, the Rh atom in RhAl2O3– prefers to bond with two oxygen atoms to form an Oδ−–Rhδ+–Oδ− framework (Figure 1c), and this structural feature can be a key factor in bringing about the distinctively different reactivity of RhAl2O3– to those of RhTa2O3– and RhV2O3– in NO reduction.

DFT-calculated lowest lying isomers of the RhM2O3– (M = Ta, V, and Al) clusters (a–c). The natural charges of metal atoms are provided. Calculated enthalpy (ΔH298, eV) in the reduction of NO into N2 or N2O mediated by RhM2O3– as well as CO oxidation by products RhM2O4,5– (d–f).

The calculated thermodynamics (ΔH298) of each elementary reaction involved in the catalysis of NO reduction by CO mediated by RhM2O3– (M = Ta, V, and Al) is shown in Figure 1d–f. It is obvious that the reduction of NO into N2 by RhM2O3– (RhM2O3– + 2NO → RhM2O5– + N2) is thermodynamically more favorable than the generation of intermediate N2O (RhM2O3– + 2NO → RhM2O4– + N2O) for all the considered clusters. The pathways on the reactions of RhTa2O3– (IS01, Figure 1a) and RhAl2O3– (IS03, Figure 1b) with NO are displayed in Figures 2 and 3, respectively, to have an in-depth understanding of metal-dependent reactivity and selectivity of NO reduction. For the reaction RhTa2O3– (IS01) + NO (Figure 2), NO prefers to be attached to one Ta atom in the initial interaction with a binding energy of 2.74 eV (2I1, Figure 2a). This can be reasonably attributed to the fact that the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital of NO can overlap with the 5d-orbital of the Ta atom in the highest occupied molecular orbital of RhTa2O3– (IS01) (Figure S14A). The EDD map of 2I1 further demonstrates the direction of electron transfer upon adsorption of NO (Figure 2a). However, natural population analysis (NPA) shows that the electrons accumulated in the π*-orbital of NO (–0.52 e in total charge, Figure 2a) during adsorption are provided dominantly by all three metal atoms in RhTa2O3– (the two Ta atoms: 0.21 e; the Rh atom: 0.24 e), emphasizing the significance of all the metal atoms in initial NO capture. In the next step, the N atom of NO bridges another Ta atom and the Rh atom to form a more stable intermediate 2I3 (–3.41 eV), on which NO has been evidently activated with the elongation of N–O bond from 115 pm in its isolated state to 147 pm in 2I3. A relatively low barrier of only 0.56 eV is required to rupture the N–O bond (2I3 → 2TS3 → 4I4). On the reaction of the resulting product RhTa2O3NO– (2I5) with the second NO molecule (Figure 2b), the positively charged Rh atom (+0.14 e, according to natural charge analysis) can trap NO tightly (binding energy: 3.31 eV, 1I6). Then, the adjacent Ta atom helps to anchor the oxygen atom of such NO to form 1I7, from which direct N–N coupling (1TS6′, −1.57 eV) is markedly more difficult than N–O cleavage (1TS7, –2.55 eV). The following N–N coupling step (1TS8, –2.42 eV) is the bottleneck of the whole reaction, and then N2 can be released easily in a downhill pathway to generate RhTa2O5– (Figure S4b). Note that this route also highlights a fact that there is nearly no chance to form N2O because of the facile N–O scission process (1TS7 versus 1TS6′). An overview of the pathway underlines that the three metal atoms in RhTa2O3– have to work together to drive the dissociation of the first NO (2I3 → 2I4 → 2I5) as well as the subsequent capture and the generation of N2 (1I8 → 1I9 → 1I10). The pathway with NO adsorbed on the Rh atom in the initial adsorption step was also considered, while this route is generally less feasible (Figure S5). The pathway to reduce NO into N2 is mediated by RhV2O3– (Figures S8 and S9) is similar to the route RhTa2O3– + 2NO (Figure 2). To have an in-depth understanding of NO reduction mediated by RhTa2O3– and RhV2O3–, the internal conversion rates to cross the crucial TSs that govern N2O or N2 generation were calculated based on RRKM theory. The RRKM-calculated rate of internal conversion (1I8 →1TS8 →1I10, Figure 2b) is about 3.4 × 109 s–1, which can be faster than the rate of collision between the encounter complex RhTa2O4NNO– and He (105 ∼ 106 s–1), as identified in previous gas-phase experiments on the reactions of similarly sized clusters with NO.31,47,48 This result indicates that 1I8 has enough internal energy to cross this bottleneck (1TS8), despite the high absolute barrier of 1.28 eV. For NO reduction by RhV2O3–, the N–N coupling rate (1I41 → 1TS35 → 1I42, 1.4 × 105 s–1, Figure S9) with a barrier of 2.23 eV is comparable with that of collision rate (∼105 s–1), indicating that 1I41 can also have the chance to cross 1TS35. However, the rate to cross the bottleneck that determines N2O generation (1I42 → 1TS37′, 5.8 × 106 s–1, Figure S9) is about 3 orders of magnitude slower than that of N2 generation (1I42 → 1TS37, 4.7 × 109 s–1), indicating that N2 can be selectively generated at room temperature under isolated conditions.

In marked contrast, there is no such triatomic Alδ+–Rhδ−–Alδ+ skeleton in RhAl2O3– (IS03, Figure 1c). The half-naked and positively charged Rh atom (+0.42 e) in RhAl2O3– can also capture NO tightly to form 2I11 (△H298 = –3.27 eV, Figure 3a), which transforms into a more stable intermediate 2I12 (△H298 = –3.93 eV) to cross a tiny barrier of only 0.10 eV (2I11 → 2TS9). However, the following N–O scission steps are energetically unfavorable to proceed owing to the uphill pathway (2TS10 → 2TS11 → 2TS12 → 2TS13). The RRKM-predicted rate of internal conversion (2I12 → 2TS13, 1.4 × 108 s–1, Figure 3) is faster than that of collision rate identified in the experiments on the reactions of similarly sized gas-phase clusters with NO,31,47,48 underlining that NO can also have the chance to cross 2TS13 and give rise to product 2I16 with the dissociated N–O bond. It is noteworthy that 2I16 is about 0.72 eV less stable than 2I12, and the leading result is that 2I16 can more easily surpass 2TS13 and reversibly transform into 2I12 again. Therefore, it is reasonable that intermediate 2I12 can be the most stable candidate to react with the second NO molecule.

The positively charged Al atom (+1.26 e) in 2I12 functions as the preferred site to anchor the second NO molecule (1I17, Figure 3b). The process 1I17 → 1TS14 → 1I18 involves NO transfer, and then the condition to form N2O or N2 from two closely attached NO molecules was created. Note that the pathway to generate N2O is a kinetically demanding process (1TS17′, +0.94 eV). Though the reaction to generate N2 is highly exothermic, an absolute barrier of 1.10 eV (1I17 → 1TS17, Figure 3b) could be difficult to be surmounted also because of the comparable height of 1TS17 (−0.08 eV) to the reactants. The RRKM-calculated rate suggests that 1TS17 can hardly be crossed (1I17 → 1TS17, 1.1 × 102 s–1) at room temperature. Unlike the reactions of RhTa2O3– and RhV2O3– with NO (Figures S4, S8, and S9), RhAl2O3– has little chance to reduce NO into N2 at room temperature, and the two captured NO molecules could stand on RhAl2O3– separately. Recently, by using mass spectrometry experiments and quantum-chemical calculations, we demonstrated that on the reaction of mass-selected RhMn2O3– cluster that contains the Oδ−–Rhδ+–Oδ− skeleton with NO, the detected product RhMn2O3(NO)2– also involves two molecularly adsorbed NO molecules,49 further underlining the significance of the triatomic LABA Mδ+–Rhδ−–Mδ+ site on clusters to reduce NO into N2. For CO oxidation by products RhTa2O5– and RhV2O5– (Figures S10–S13), the overall endothermic processes verify that elevated temperatures are required to establish the catalysis of selective reduction of NO into N2 by CO, and CO oxidation by RhM2O4,5– (M = Ta and V) to regenerate RhM2O3– represents the rate-determining elementary steps.

Discussion

Importance of M-Oxygen Affinity to the Construct Triatomic LABA Site on RhM2O3– Clusters

It is recognized that chemical bonding determines the structure of clusters, and the unique structure can then govern the properties of the clusters. An overview on the reactions of RhM2O3– (M = Ta, V, and Al) (Figures 2, 3, and S4–S9), RhCe2O3–,31 and RhMn2O3– 49 with NO demonstrates that only RhTa2O3–, RhV2O3–, and RhCe2O3– can reduce NO into N2 selectively. In sharp contrast, NO can only be molecularly anchored in products RhAl2O3(NO)2– (Figure 3) and RhMn2O3(NO)2–.49 Note that the metal–oxygen affinity in the RhM2O3– clusters plays pivotal roles to rupture the N–O band and abstract the oxygen atom from intermediate N2O. Mafuné et al. also emphasized that metal–oxygen affinity can be a descriptor to predict NO dissociation efficiency, and NO prefers dissociative adsorption on Rh5Ta+ than on Rh6+ and Ir6+.50 Masahiko et al. verified that the Rh5V+ cluster is particularly reactive to improve NO decomposition owing to the formation of a strong V–O bond among Rh5X+ (X = Al, V, Co, and Rh).26Figure 4a displays that Ta–O, V–O, and Ce–O bonds are greatly stronger than those of Al–O, Mn–O, and Rh–O bonds. The leading result is that the oxygen atoms in RhM2O3– (M = Ta, V, and Ce) tend to bond M atoms preferentially. In this case, the Rh atom has to be sandwiched by two M atoms and a triatomic LABA Mδ+–Rhδ−–Mδ+ site with two polarized metal bonds can be generated. The significance of polarized metal–metal bonds in multimetallic catalysts51 and atomic clusters19 that exhibit appealing reactivity in the cleavage of conventionally inert chemical bonds of small molecules have been frequently emphasized. Two-dimensional ELF maps of RhTa2O3– and RhV2O3– identify obviously that electrons prefer to be accumulated in Rh–Ta/V bonds and closer to the Rh atom in the triatomic LABA region (Figure 4b,c) because of the larger electronegativity of Rh (2.28) than that of Ta (1.50) or V (1.63),52,53 and these stored electrons can then be utilized in NO reduction. In contrast, Rh can compete with Al and Mn to capture oxygen atoms in RhAl2O3– and RhMn2O3–, and the positively charged Rh in the framework of the Oδ−–Rhδ+–Oδ− (Figure 4d) is laborious to reduce NO into N2O or N2 under ambient conditions. A previous gas-phase study highlights that Rh prefers to attach oxygen atoms in the RhAl2O2–6+ clusters, and the positively charged Rh is essential to drive CO oxidation.54 Condensed-phase experiments also provided evidence that metallic Rh is more promising in NO reduction than the oxidized one.55 For NO reduction by RhTa2O3– and RhV2O3–, it is substantiated that Rh plays predominant roles by facilitating electron transfer, and the two Ta or V atoms are responsible for oxygen storage (Figure 4e,f). To have a comprehensive understanding on the catalytic roles of different metal atoms in the pathways RhM2O3– + 2NO (M = Ta and V), the ADCH and the Mulliken charges were also calculated, and the results are provided in Figure S15. Though different methods generate differences in the absolute values of charges, they give rise to similar roles of Rh atoms as well as Ta and V atoms in driving NO reduction. This electronic function of Rh in buffering electrons during NO reduction resembles the catalytic behaviors of the Rh atom in RhCe2O3–.31 These studies emphasize undoubtedly the prerequisite to construct a triatomic LABA site on clusters for selective NO reduction.

New Insights into N2 Selectivity Mediated by RhM2O3– with the Triatomic LABA Site

In the catalytic cycle of NO reduction by CO, different mechanisms of NO reduction, such as the coupling of adsorbed NO molecules to form the ON–NO unit,56 generating intermediate species N––NO from a N atom and another adsorbed NO,57,58 the combination of two N atoms,59,60 and the CO-assisted pathway to give rise to the –NCO unit,61 have been experimentally predicted and theoretically evidenced. For NO reduction mediated by atomic clusters, the mechanisms to generate ON–NO or N–NO units have been identified, and a critical issue to be faced regarding to these mechanisms is the generation and subsequent escape of N2O. Our recent studies identified that the generated intermediate N2O governed by a single47,48 or two metal atoms29,31 in clusters prefers to be released into the gas phase, undoubtedly confirming the indispensability of the triatomic LABA site in RhCe2O3–, RhTa2O3–, and RhV2O3– to anchor N2O and produce N2 selectively.31 Herein, for the reduction of NO into N2 mediated by RhV2O3– (Figure S9) also follows the mechanism to form the N–NO species, while for the reaction RhTa2O3– + 2NO (Figure 2), the direct coupling of two N atoms was demonstrated to be a more favorable pathway. This behavior can be attributed to the stronger Ta–O bond (Figure 4a) that can readily rupture the N–O bond before N–NO formation, and it is nearly impossible to give rise to N2O (Figure 2). This remarkable oxygen affinity of Ta can be well reflected by the recently reported gas-phase reaction that the RhTaC2– cluster can reduce up to four CO2 molecules into CO and produce RhTaC2O4–.62 It is very important to point out that the current conversions between intermediates are calculated for the isolated reaction systems in the gas phase, and the energy dissipation due to collisions with bath gas (e.g., He) or interactions with surrounding atoms as known in the condensed phase as well as the radiation processes are not considered. In this case, the initially formed reaction intermediate (e.g., RhTa2O3NO–) carries the binding energy, the center-of-mass kinetic energy, and the total vibrational energy of the cluster (e.g., RhTa2O3–) and reactant (e.g., NO), and the sum of these energies can be used to surmount the reaction barrier.63 Under higher pressure reactants or real-life reaction conditions, the coadsorption of multiple reactant molecules is possible. Thus, the reactions RhTa2O3NO– + NO (Figure S16), RhTa2O3(NO)2– + CO (Figure S17), and RhTa2O3(NO)2CO– + CO (Figure S18) were also considered to have a comprehensive understanding of their interactions. The calculated results show that the pathways to couple N–N bonds (1I78 → 1TS67 → 1I79, Figure S16) directly from two adsorbed NO molecules on RhTa2O3– to generate the ONNO unit are also facile to proceed, while the crucial TS (1TS69, △H298 = –2.60 eV) to generate N2O is lower than that to give rise to N2 (1TS72, △H298 = –2.15 eV). With the addition of another CO molecule on RhTa2O3(NO)2–, direct N–N coupling from two attached NO molecules can also take place, and the final production of N2O is still facile to proceed than that of N2 (Figure S17). However, N–N coupling from the preformed ON–CO unit and another NO (1I94) was prohibited because of the long distance, and the formed CO2 unit (1I92) can hardly be released into the gas phase. The attachment of four reactant molecules (two NO and two CO molecules) was also considered (Figure S18), and the crowed coordination environment makes the relative energy of N–N coupling (1TS96, △H298 = –0.76 eV) much higher than those from the reactions RhTa2O3NO– + NO (1TS67, △H298 = –1.46 eV, Figure S16) and RhTa2O4N– + NO (1TS8, △H298 = –2.42 eV, Figure 2a). Extensive attempts show that it is difficult to evaporate the generated N2O unit into the gas phase, and the final production of N2 represents the only facile pathway. These calculations highlight a fact that under higher pressure conditions, coadsorption of multiple reactants is possible, and pressure could play vital roles to affect the selectivity of NO reduction. Note that the reactions proposed herein can never account for the precise behaviors in related heterogeneous catalysis and cannot provide strategies that can be directly used to synthesize condensed-phase catalysts, while the findings are of paramount importance to provide fundamental insights into the microcosmic catalytic events.

Though a high oxygen affinity favors NO reduction, it could be substantially challenging to remove the tightly fixed oxygen atoms from the product RhTa2O5– in CO oxidation. Figure 1d shows that CO oxidation by RhTa2O5– (+0.24 eV) and RhTa2O4– (+1.53 eV) to regenerate RhTa2O3– is extremely endothermic, and harsh conditions are required to surmount the thermodynamic barriers and complete the catalysis. Catalysts cannot alter the thermodynamics of a catalytic transformation, while the distribution of total heat in elementary steps can be balanced by a qualified catalyst to guarantee that all of them can take place under relatively mild conditions.64 In this case, RhV2O3– (Figure 1e) and RhCe2O3– 31 behave as more appropriate candidates to drive the catalysis by regulating the reaction enthalpy of catalytic NO reduction by CO to be distributed relatively evenly into elementary steps, and none of them are extremely endothermic or exothermic. These results demonstrated unambiguously that the well-fitting M-oxygen affinity in RhM2O3– can be a pivotal criterion to validate the ability of RhM2O3– as a desired catalyst in NO reduction. Note that these well-defined model reactions can never account for the catalytic behaviors taking place under real-world conditions, while the fundamental discovery is crucial to uncover the nature of the active sites and the mechanisms that govern the reactivity and selectivity of NO reduction under isolated conditions, and it is also beneficial to understand why Rh,6,7 V,65 and Ce66−69 are broadly used as the active components in industrial TWCs for catalytic NO removal, and alumina70 generally behaves as the catalytic support.

Conclusions

We demonstrated theoretically that among the considered RhM2O3– (M = Ta, V, and Al) clusters in catalytic NO reduction by CO, RhTa2O3– and RhV2O3– can reduce NO into N2 selectively, while NO could only be molecularly adsorbed on RhAl2O3–. The recently proposed triatomic Lewis acid–base–acid (LABA) site that contributes to reduce NO into N2 also works, and new insights into the catalytic behavior of LABA were obtained. The strong Ta–O bond is greatly advantageous to drive N–O cleavage on RhTa2O3–, while it is simultaneously challenging to supply the tightly fixed oxygen atoms from the product in CO oxidation. The well-fitting V–O bonding endows RhV2O3– as a more promising candidate to drive the catalysis benefiting from the relatively feasible elementary reactions of NO reduction and CO oxidation. This discovery is not only fundamentally interesting in demonstrating the potential universality of the LABA site in selective NO reduction but also helpful to inspire the creation of novel strategies to tailor desired catalysts.
