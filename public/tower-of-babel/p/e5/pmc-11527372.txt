Interaction between trouble sleeping and diabetes on metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease and liver fibrosis in adults results from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2017–2018
Authors: Cui Zhang, Lili Cao, Bo Xu, Wei Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), trouble sleeping, and diabetes, as major public health problems, were closely related. The study examined the interaction between trouble sleeping and diabetes on MAFLD and liver fibrosis in adults with MAFLD.MethodsThe data were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2017–2018. Multivariate logistic regression model and subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the relationship between either trouble sleeping or diabetes on MAFLD and liver fibrosis. Relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion of interaction (AP), and synergy index (S) were utilized to assess the additive interaction.ResultsUltimately, 3747 participants were included, with 2229 known MAFLD subjects. Compared with participants without diabetes, those with diabetes had a higher risk of MAFLD [odds ratio (OR) = 5.55; 95% confidence interval (CI) = 4.07–7.56] and liver fibrosis risk (OR = 3.61; 95% CI = 2.67–4.89). We also found a significant association of trouble sleeping with an increased risk of MAFLD (OR = 1.54; 95% CI = 1.17–2.02) and liver fibrosis risk (OR = 1.51; 95% CI = 1.06–2.16), compared with those without trouble sleeping. Moreover, there was a significant interaction between diabetes and trouble sleeping on MAFLD [RERI = 1.76 (95% CI: −0.22 to 3.73), AP = 0.35 (95% CI: 0.08–0.63), S = 1.80 (95% CI: 1.02–3.16)] and liver fibrosis risk [RERI = 1.79 (95% CI: 0.37–3.21), AP = 0.44 (95% CI: 0.20–0.69), S = 2.44 (95% CI: 1.18–5.08)].ConclusionThe findings highlight that trouble sleeping and diabetes had a synergistic effect on MAFLD and liver cirrhosis. The study highlights the importance of addressing both trouble sleeping and diabetes management in adults to mitigate the risk of MAFLD and liver fibrosis.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a significant global health issue affecting individuals of all ages [1]. It is characterized by the accumulation of fat in the liver among those who do not drink alcohol, with potential consequences ranging from simple steatosis to more severe conditions such as nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), which can progress to advanced stages such as fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer [2]. Research indicates that approximately 25% of the global population may have NAFLD [3], with approximately 59% of biopsied NAFLD patients showing signs of NASH [4]. Fibrosis, characterized by the excessive deposition of proteins in the liver tissue, is a crucial stage in the advancement of NAFLD and is closely linked to poor health outcomes, including an increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma [5]. Recent advancements in hepatology have brought about a significant shift in the understanding of NAFLD. The term ‘metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD)’ has been introduced as a more inclusive label for this condition [6]. MAFLD encompasses a range of fatty liver diseases linked to metabolic irregularities, extending beyond the mere absence of alcohol intake [7]. This shift in terminology underscores the crucial role of metabolic factors such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and insulin resistance in the onset and advancement of fatty liver disease [8]. As NAFLD is becoming the leading cause of chronic liver disease in Western nations, affecting over 30% of the world’s adult population [9], the urgency to address this public health concern is evident. Efforts to prevent the progression of fibrosis have become central to reducing liver-related mortality. By understanding and addressing the underlying factors contributing to MAFLD development, interventions can be targeted to prevent the progression of the disease to more severe stages, ultimately improving outcomes in individuals with MAFLD.

MAFLD is a multifaceted disorder affected by genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. Adequate sleep is essential for the regulation of various physiological processes including the management of glucose levels and hormones. Sleep quality is a growing concern in the realm of public health. Numerous individuals experience difficulties with sleeping, leading to adverse impacts on their well-being [10]. Sleep-related issues such as trouble sleeping, snoring, or sleep apnea symptoms are prevalent among individuals with MAFLD [11].

Diabetes has long been recognized as a contributing factor to MAFLD and its progression into fibrosis. The characteristic insulin resistance observed in diabetes alters liver function, causing an accumulation of fat and possible fibrotic alterations. Adipose tissue insulin resistance was observed to be elevated in individuals with NAFLD compared to those with a normal liver. Additionally, NAFLD patients with higher adipose tissue insulin resistance tended to exhibit more severe liver fibrosis [12]. The accumulation of hepatic fat, regardless of body weight and fat mass, was likely a significant factor in the pathogenesis of MAFLD [13]. It was also noted that insulin resistance in adipose tissue worsened as NAFLD/NASH progressed [14]. Moreover, the persistent inflammation linked to diabetes may promote the progression of liver fibrosis.

Trouble sleeping and diabetes are commonly observed in patients with MAFLD, and their impact on disease progression is increasingly recognized. The coexistence of MAFLD and diabetes or trouble sleeping can create different risk combinations, emphasizing the importance of identifying these comorbidities in individuals with MAFLD. Despite the significant implications, few studies have directly examined the interaction of trouble sleeping and diabetes on MAFLD. The aim of this study was to analyze data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to investigate the interaction between trouble sleeping and diabetes in relation to MAFLD and liver fibrosis in adults with MAFLD. This study sought to fill the gap in understanding the combined effects of trouble sleeping and diabetes on MAFLD progression. By examining these relationships, this study aimed to provide insights into the potential interventions and management strategies for individuals with MAFLD and its associated liver fibrosis.

Methods

Study population

The NHANES is a comprehensive collection of studies conducted in the USA to assess health indicators and nutritional aspects in the general population. Known for its adherence to ethical standards, the survey protocol for NHANES was approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. In the most recent NHANES conducted between 2017 and 2018, transient elastography (TE) was used for the first time to estimate liver fibrosis by measuring liver stiffness, while the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) was utilized to quantify liver fat. TE is a noninvasive tool recognized for its effectiveness in assessing liver fibrosis, whereas CAP stands out as a promising technique for rapid and standardized steatosis quantification [15]. The results of this study offer valuable insights into the potential impact of these factors on liver health, contributing to the body of knowledge surrounding MAFLD. A total of 9254 individuals completed interviews in this survey cycle, providing robust data for the analysis of the relationship between trouble sleeping, diabetes, and liver fibrosis. We implemented exclusion criteria for participants: (1) missing data on trouble sleeping (n = 3093), (2) missing data on diabetes (n = 333), (3) missing data on CAP or missing data on median liver stiffness or incomplete liver ultrasound TE examination (n = 825), (4) liver cancer, autoimmune hepatitis, hepatitis B and C (n = 783), (5) missing data on interesting covariates such as age, sex, race, education attainment, smoking status, and recreational physical activity (n = 473). Finally, 3747 participants were included in the analysis. The flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart for the inclusion of study participants.

Assessment of trouble sleeping

In the NHANES 2017–2018 survey, trouble sleeping was evaluated through the personal interview question, ‘Have you ever mentioned to a healthcare provider that you have difficulty sleeping?’. If the response was yes, it was concluded that the individual had experienced trouble sleeping. Conversely, participants who answered no were categorized into the group without trouble sleeping.

Assessment of diabetes

Participants in the study were determined to have diabetes if their glycated hemoglobin levels were equal to or greater than 6.5% or if their fasting plasma glucose levels were equal to or greater than 126 mg/dl. Additionally, individuals who self-reported a medical history of diabetes or using insulin were also considered to have diabetes [16].

Assessment of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease and liver stiffness

TE has been widely utilized as a noninvasive and reliable method for assessing liver stiffness [17], with Food and Drug Administration approval for evaluating liver fibrosis. Individuals with a CAP score exceeding 248 dB/m were categorized as liver steatosis [18]. The establishment of MAFLD diagnostic criteria is based on the presence of liver steatosis along with meeting one of the following criteria: (1) being overweight or obese with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, (2) having type 2 diabetes, and (3) exhibiting at least two signs of metabolic dysregulation, such as waist circumference ≥ 102 cm in men and ≥88 cm in women, blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg, triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dl, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) < 40 mg/dl in men and <50 mg/dl in women, prediabetes, insulin resistance homeostasis model score ≥ 2.5, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein > 2 mg/l [19]. In this study, significant fibrosis was identified using a median liver stiffness measurement exceeding 8.2 kPa [20].

Assessment of covariates

The following categorical variables were included in our analysis as covariates: age group, sex, race, education attainment, smoking status, BMI group, and recreational physical activity. Continuous covariates included in our analysis were age, poverty income ratio (PIR), BMI, C-reactive protein, albumin, total bilirubin, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels. BMI was categorized as underweight, normal weight, overweight, or obese. Recreational physical activity was evaluated through personal interviews regarding engaging in vigorous or moderate-intensity exercise, fitness, or recreational activities. Participants’ smoking status was classified based on their history of smoking: never smoker (those who had never smoked), current smoker (those who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and were currently smoking), and former smoker (those who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes but were no longer smoking).

Statistical analysis

The continuous data were presented as mean and standard error (SE), with a comparison made between the MAFLD and non-MAFLD groups using a weighted t-test. Categorical variables were described in terms of case numbers and weighted prevalence [n (weighted%)], and the variance between the MAFLD and non-MAFLD groups was evaluated using a chi-square test. Multivariable logistic regression models were applied to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between trouble sleeping, diabetes, and their interactions with MAFLD. Model 1 did not include any adjustments, while Model 2 incorporated age, sex, PIR, and education attainment as covariates. Model 3 included additional adjustments for smoking status and recreational physical activity. An additive model was constructed to examine these interactions. The joint effect of trouble sleeping and diabetes was compared to the independent effects using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (S). A lack of additive interaction was indicated if the CIs of RERI and AP included 0 and the CI of S enclosed 1. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, sex, and smoking status. All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical analysis software (version 4.3.2), with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study enrolled 3747 participants between 2017 and 2018, as detailed in Table 1. The average age of the participants was 48.5 years, with a sex distribution of 49% males and 51% females. Among the participants, 54% of males and 46% of females were found to have MAFLD. Those with MAFLD tended to be older, more likely to be male, engage in less recreational physical activity, have higher BMI levels, and exhibit elevated levels of C-reactive protein. Additionally, individuals with MAFLD had a higher prevalence of diabetes and reported more trouble sleeping. Furthermore, patients with MAFLD generally display lower levels of albumin, total bilirubin, and HDL-C than non-MAFLD patients. Table 2 presents the characteristics of subjects with or without significant fibrosis in MAFLD. Of the 2229 participants, 306 individuals reported liver fibrosis, resulting in a prevalence of significant fibrosis in MAFLD of 13%. Education attainment, recreational physical activity, BMI, C-reactive protein, albumin, HDL-C, diabetes, and trouble sleeping were all found to be significantly different between the two groups. Participants with liver fibrosis were more likely to be physically inactive, obese, have a history of diabetes and trouble sleeping, exhibit higher levels of C-reactive protein, and have lower levels of albumin and HDL-C.

Associations with metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease

Three multivariable logistic regression models were developed to investigate the independent relationship between diabetes, trouble sleeping, and MAFLD (Table 3). Model 1 did not include any covariates; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, PIR, and education attainment; Model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, PIR, education attainment, smoking status, and recreational sports activities. In Model 1, patients with diabetes were significantly associated with a higher risk of MAFLD compared to patients without diabetes (OR = 5.55; 95% CI = 4.07, 7.56). This association remained statistically significant in Model 2 and Model 3 after accounting for covariates (all P < 0.001). Individuals who reported trouble sleeping had an increased risk of developing MAFLD compared to those without sleep issues (OR = 1.54; 95% CI = 1.17, 2.02). Furthermore, the partially adjusted model in Model 2 also showed a significant positive association (OR = 1.43; 95% CI = 1.03, 2.00; P < 0.001).

Associations with liver fibrosis

Three multivariable logistic regression models were developed to investigate the independent relationships between diabetes, trouble sleeping, and liver fibrosis incidence (Table 4). Model 1 did not include any covariates, while Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, PIR, and education attainment, and Model 3 was further adjusted for smoking status and recreational sports activities. In Model 1, patients with diabetes showed a positive association with liver fibrosis risk (OR = 3.61; 95% CI = 4.07, 7.56) compared to nondiabetic patients. The finding remained significant in Model 2 and Model 3 after adjusting for confounders (all P < 0.001). Individuals reporting trouble sleeping had an increased risk of liver fibrosis compared to those without sleep issues (OR = 1.51; 95% CI = 1.17, 2.02, P = 0.013). This association, however, was not significant in either the partially or fully adjusted models (P > 0.05).

Subgroup analysis of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, sex, and smoking status to explore the relationship between trouble sleeping, diabetes, and MAFLD across the various demographic groups (Fig. 2). Within different age brackets, a notable positive correlation between trouble sleeping and MAFLD was found in the 40–49 years age group. Moreover, the association between diabetes and MAFLD was particularly strong in the 30–39 years, 40–49 years, 50–59 years, and 60–69 years age groups. When considering sex as a stratifying factor, diabetes exhibited a significant positive correlation with MAFLD in both male and female participants, whereas trouble sleeping did not show a significant association with MAFLD in either sex. Subgroup analysis by smoking status revealed a significant positive association between trouble sleeping and MAFLD in the former smoker group (P = 0.005); however, this association was not statistically significant in other smoking categories. Conversely, the positive correlation between diabetes and MAFLD was statistically significant across all the smoking groups.

Subgroup analysis of liver fibrosis

A subgroup analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between trouble sleeping, diabetes, and liver fibrosis across different demographic groups (Fig. 3). The results revealed no significant correlation between trouble sleeping and liver fibrosis across all the age groups. Interestingly, the association between diabetes and liver fibrosis was significant in the 20–29 years, 40–49 years, 50–59 years, and 60–69 years age groups. When stratified by sex, diabetes showed a significant positive correlation with liver fibrosis in both male and female participants. Trouble sleeping, however, was not significantly associated with liver fibrosis in either sex group. Further subgroup analyses based on smoking groups indicated a significant positive association between trouble sleeping and liver fibrosis in individuals who had never smoked (P = 0.004), but this association was not statistically significant in the other smoking groups. In contrast, the positive association between diabetes and liver fibrosis was statistically significant across all smoking groups.

Effect of interaction between diabetes and trouble sleeping on metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease

The results presented in Table 5 demonstrate a significant synergistic effect of trouble sleeping and diabetes on MAFLD in Model 3. The adjusted RERI was 1.76 with a 95% CI of −0.22 to 3.73. Additionally, the adjusted AP was 0.35 with a 95% CI of 0.08–0.63, and the adjusted S was 1.80 with a 95% CI of 1.02–3.16. Specifically, the AP value of 0.35 in Model 3 suggests that 35% of MAFLD cases in the study sample were attributed to the interaction between trouble sleeping and diabetes. Furthermore, Fig. 4 visually supports the presence of an additive interaction between trouble sleeping and depression on MAFLD.

Discussion

The global prevalence of MAFLD is approximately 30% [21], making it a prominent public health concern. Lifestyle factors, such as obesity, diabetes, high cholesterol, and metabolic syndrome, are strongly linked to the development of MAFLD [8]. Despite this, the exact mechanisms underlying the progression of MAFLD remain complex and have not been entirely elucidated. A previous study suggested that trouble sleeping is common in individuals diagnosed with MAFLD [11]. The interplay between different factors, namely trouble sleeping and diabetes, could hold significance in the advancement of MAFLD and liver fibrosis. Understanding how trouble sleeping and diabetes affect MAFLD and liver fibrosis progression in adults with the MAFLD is crucial for a comprehensive insight into the condition. By exploring the relationship between trouble sleeping and diabetes in individuals with MAFLD, this study aimed to shed light on their combined effects on liver health and fibrotic processes.

The results of our study indicate a strong association between trouble sleeping and MAFLD. Sleep disturbances can throw off the body’s internal clock, impacting metabolism [22] and potentially contribute to the development of metabolic syndrome [23]. Inadequate sleep can disrupt the body’s biological rhythms, affecting processes such as insulin secretion and sensitivity, and leading to liver fat accumulation [24]. Long-term sleep deprivation or poor sleep quality may trigger a chronic stress response, resulting in increased inflammation levels that can worsen liver damage and fat buildup [25]. Moreover, trouble sleeping can affect hormonal equilibrium by raising cortisol levels and fostering insulin resistance [26].

Moreover, the results of our study show a durable association between trouble sleeping and liver fibrosis. The potential reasons for the association between trouble sleeping and liver fibrosis are diverse. Lack of sleep may cause an increase in inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress levels, triggering a stress reaction in liver cells. This can lead to the emergence of NASH and the advancement of fibrosis [27]. Moreover, low-quality sleep can disrupt the balance of hormone secretion, including growth hormone and cortisol, impacting liver function [14]. Additionally, individuals experiencing sleep disruptions may engage in unhealthy behaviors such as poor diet and lack of exercise, which could contribute to liver disease progression [15].

Diabetes is a significant risk factor for MAFLD and appears to promote the progression of liver disease in individuals with MAFLD [8]. Insulin resistance accompanying diabetes can lead to fat buildup in the liver and subsequent development of steatohepatitis [17]. Research indicates that the prevalence of liver fibrosis is more common in patients with diabetes compared to those without diabetes [28], and diabetes remains an autonomous predictor of fibrosis advancement [29]. Our study further indicated an effective association between diabetes and liver fibrosis. The main reason may be that elevated blood sugar levels could trigger the buildup of free fatty acids and the release of inflammatory cytokines, resulting in the activation of liver fibrosis [30].

The interaction between trouble sleeping and diabetes in the context of MAFLD is particularly intriguing. Both trouble sleeping and diabetes can independently contribute to MAFLD, but their combined effect may be even more pronounced. Research has shown that approximately half to over two-thirds of individuals with diabetes suffer from sleep disorders [31]. People with diabetes are more susceptible to disruptions in their sleep patterns, which can exacerbate the advancement of liver illnesses [19].

Furthermore, the study’s findings on liver fibrosis are of particular concern. The identification of trouble sleeping and diabetes as potential risk factors for liver fibrosis underscores the importance of managing these conditions in patients with MAFLD. It is crucial for clinicians to recognize that patients diagnosed with MAFLD who experience difficulties with sleep or have diabetes may face an elevated risk of liver fibrosis. To mitigate this risk, routine screening for these concurrent conditions and proactive measures to address both sleep and blood glucose metabolic irregularities are advised.

The limitations of this investigation involve its cross-sectional design, which hinders the establishment of causal relationships. Longitudinal research is necessary to evaluate the chronological connection between sleep disturbance, diabetes, MAFLD, and liver fibrosis. Moreover, the utilization of self-reported information may introduce inaccuracies in measurements and recollection biases.

Despite these limitations, our outcomes are deemed plausible and trustworthy by utilizing inclusive survey data and adjusting for potentially diverse variables. Our results underscore the necessity for additional exploration of the interplay between trouble sleeping and diabetes among individuals with MAFLD and liver fibrosis. Subsequent studies should delve into the fundamental mechanisms that contribute to this synergistic effect and assess the efficacy of interventions aimed at addressing both sleep issues and metabolic-related complications.

Conclusions

The current research offers important insights into the synergistic effect between trouble sleeping and diabetes on MAFLD and liver cirrhosis in individuals with MAFLD, suggesting that patients with MAFLD could receive appropriate instruction on establishing healthy sleep habits and blood glucose management. These findings may provide epidemiological evidence for the independent association of trouble sleeping and diabetes with MAFLD and the interaction association between trouble sleeping and diabetes on MAFLD and liver cirrhosis in individuals with MAFLD.
