Running on empty: Fatigue, supplements, and the trials we need
Authors: Jessica B. Rubin
Published: 2026

Most hepatologists know that their cirrhosis patients suffer from profound fatigue. Yet we rarely ask about it, and quietly hope our patients won’t either, because we have very few effective therapies to offer. Fatigue has received attention in specific chronic liver diseases, such as primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), but even in these conditions, very few studies have tested agents specifically aimed at treating fatigue itself. In patients with decompensated cirrhosis, the paucity of data is even more profound, despite fatigue affecting most (50%–86%) of this population and significantly eroding quality of life. 1 As hepatology has begun to take symptom management in cirrhosis seriously, fatigue has largely been left out, as its etiology in cirrhosis patients is poorly understood and thus, its treatment targets are limited. Left without guidance, our patients, desperately trying to manage disabling fatigue, turn to social media and artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots, where they find hundreds of supplements marketed for energy. Few of these have been studied rigorously, and none in patients with cirrhosis. Were they to ask us, we would warn of the harms while overlooking any potential benefits. This is the gap Sasidharan and colleagues set out to address.

Their trial, reported in this issue of Hepatology Communications, is a single-center, open-label, randomized comparison of L-taurine (1 g daily) added to standard care in adults with decompensated cirrhosis and clinically significant fatigue. Taurine is a common supplement in the energy drinks marketed for stamina and alertness that our patients so often try. It is an amino acid concentrated in muscle, with roles in antioxidant defense and mitochondrial function. Trials in healthy athletes have linked it to improved endurance and lower markers of exertional fatigue. 2 But whether the benefit of exercising healthy muscle extends to the fatigue of decompensated cirrhosis is unknown. Over 12 weeks, taurine did not outperform standard care on the Fatigue Assessment Scale, the trial’s primary outcome, across the full study population. Yet in a prespecified subgroup analysis, taurine showed a large effect among patients without anemia, whereas it had no effect in anemic patients. The authors offer an explanation for this difference. They suggest that taurine may act on the oxidative and metabolic pathways that drive fatigue when anemia is not the cause. It is a reasonable hypothesis, though not one the trial was designed to test.

Despite the null primary result, the trial should not be dismissed. It addresses 2 areas where the evidence is thin: fatigue as a treatment target, and a dietary supplement as a candidate therapy rather than a toxin. Fatigue lags even within its own field. For other burdensome symptoms of cirrhosis, such as pain, muscle cramps, and pruritus, we have begun to characterize the burden and identify candidate therapies, however imperfect.1,3 Fatigue has almost none. Its sole pharmacologic candidate studied in liver disease, modafinil, failed in a randomized trial, and that trial was in PBC rather than cirrhosis. 1 Exercise has been shown to reduce fatigue in cirrhosis, but the evidence comes largely from small trials mostly in patients with compensated disease, and exercise is notoriously hard to sustain in the cirrhosis population. 4

The gap is even wider for supplements. In hepatology, supplements enter the literature almost exclusively as causes of harm, cataloged in the drug-induced liver injury registries; far less often does anyone ask whether one might help. Yet up to 40% of those attending liver clinics report taking dietary supplements, and this is likely an underestimate, given under-reporting. 5 Use has also risen across all adult age groups over the past decade. In the absence of evidence, supplement use is guided by marketing rather than data. 6 A randomized trial of a supplement is how the field reclaims the question: it answers with evidence, in either direction. Taurine is a reasonable place to start. It is inexpensive and widely available, and could be deployed at scale if it works.

The value of a symptom trial depends on its design, and the trial by Sasidharan and colleagues has limitations. First and most important, it was open-label, and its primary outcome was entirely self-reported. Fatigue is among the most subjective endpoints in medicine, and taurine carries a popular reputation as an energy aid. Patients who know they are taking it may report feeling better solely because of the placebo effect. The null across the cohort offers some reassurance. The benefit was confined to roughly 40 patients without anemia, yet this was in an analysis the authors call underpowered. A small, unblinded subgroup is exactly where observed effects most often overstate real ones. It was also one of several subgroups examined; although the authors suggest a plausible hypothesis, this mechanism was not directly measured, and the difference noted in this subgroup may have been due to chance alone. Measurement compounds the problem. The Fatigue Assessment Scale was validated in sarcoidosis, not in liver disease, and meaningful change is judged here against a threshold carried over from other conditions. 7 These limitations do not diminish the contribution of this trial. Rather, they inform the design of future trials. Studying subjective symptoms and unregulated supplements is inherently difficult, and we all benefit from the authors’ willingness to tackle this question.

Our responsibility now is to build on their work. Several questions remain open about fatigue in patients with cirrhosis. Whether a treatment effect can be cleanly attributed to a supplement, rather than to expectation, will require blinding and a placebo. The choice of instrument matters as well. While the reflex may be to call for a fatigue measure validated specifically in cirrhosis, that demand may delay the work more than improve it. Generic instruments retain their performance in this population, and the stronger case is to adopt one and move forward rather than build a bespoke tool. 8 PROMIS-29 is a sensible choice, as it has a fatigue domain, and meaningful change thresholds have now been derived in liver disease, including in patients with decompensated cirrhosis. 9 The anemia signal highlighted in this trial is a reasonable hypothesis. The natural next step is a study designed to test it directly, in patients selected for it. Regarding candidate agents, the field need not stop at taurine. Conditions where fatigue is better studied, such as cancer and chronic kidney disease, offer a ready source of alternative therapeutic options. And because fatigue travels with depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, and minimal hepatic encephalopathy, future studies will need to measure and account for these confounders. 10

Trials of symptoms and cheap supplements are the hardest to fund. Symptom endpoints have long competed poorly against survival and transplant-free outcomes, and an unpatentable supplement has no commercial sponsor with a stake in the result. This is part of the reason supplement use has run so far ahead of the evidence, and why a trial like this is worth highlighting. Sasidharan and colleagues took on a question few would pay to answer, about a symptom the field has long overlooked, and addressed it with a randomized trial. Taurine did not relieve fatigue overall, though a benefit in non-anemic patients remains a possibility worth testing further. Regardless of the outcome, the trial gives the field a place to start. Our patients are already asking what will help their fatigue, and the many other symptoms that diminish their quality of life. We still cannot tell them what works. But thanks to this work, we can now say that taurine and the energy supplements built around it are probably not the answer. Our patients deserve more complete answers, grounded in evidence, and the well-designed, well-funded trials that can provide them.
