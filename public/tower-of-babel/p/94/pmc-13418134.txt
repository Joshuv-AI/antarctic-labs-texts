Massive retropharyngeal lipoma, a benign yet potentially lethal condition: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Pawan Kumar Mishra, Kartikeya Mishra, Prabhat srivastava, Mangal Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRetropharyngeal masses are uncommon head and neck masses. Early identification is necessary because of the location, which renders even benign lesions potentially fatal. Early clinical diagnosis remains challenging. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is the investigation of choice, although final histological confirmation is essential. It is crucial to include them in differentials for early diagnosis because of their uncommon incidence and diverse clinical presentation.Case presentationWe present the case of a 22-year-old, male of Indo-aryanethnicity presenting with compressive symptoms due to a massive retropharyngeal lipoma extending bilaterally into carotid space. Flexible nasal endoscopy revealed a mass arising from the oropharynx, obscuring the view of laryngeal structures. Magnetic Resonance Imaging suggested the diagnosis of retropharyngeal mass. The tumor was surgically removed by transcervical approach and the patient had an uneventful recovery. The histological examination confirmed the diagnosis. A literature search was conducted in all major medical databases where male predominance was found. Dysphagia was the most common symptom, followed by snoring. The most used imaging modality was Computed Tomography. Surgery was the treatment in most of the cases with transcervical approach in majority. Complete recovery was reported, following treatment in the majority of cases.ConclusionsRetropharyngeal lipomas are uncommon head and neck tumors that, with early diagnosis and treatment, can have favorable outcomes. When assessing retropharyngeal mass, it is crucial to maintain them in differential.

Introduction

Lipomas are benign tumors of mesenchymal origin that develop slowly and exhibit non-infiltrating behavior. About 13% of these occur in the head and neck regions, most commonly subcutaneously at the posterior neck [1]. However, retropharyngeal space lipomas are very rare. The retropharyngeal space runs from the bottom of the skull to the fourth thoracic vertebra, where two layers of tissue called the deep cervical fascia and the alar fascia come together. Retropharyngeal lipomas are rare, slow-growing tumors. Their clinical presentation is atypical due to their relatively secluded anatomical site, which makes the symptomatology even more subtle. Typically, significant growth is required before bothersome symptoms occur [1]. Symptoms are caused due to increasing pressure on surrounding structures [2], such as dysphagia and dyspnea. These tumors grow to a large size because of the distensibility of the retropharyngeal space before causing symptoms of upper aerodigestive tract obstruction [3]. Despite their benign nature, the ease of approach, and the possibility of total resection and cure, these may represent a potential cause of death if not recognized early and attended to promptly and adequately.

In this report, we describe a case of giant retropharyngeal lipoma extending up to the level of the epiglottis and pushing it, leading to stridor and severe dysphagia, which was surgically treated. We present this case, along with a review of the worldwide literature, due to its numerous interesting findings.

Since this diagnosis is uncommon, most of the information we have about its demographics, symptoms, treatment, and results comes from individual case reports and small groups of cases, which gives us a disjointed understanding. In this regard, we sought to systematically evaluate the literature for reported cases of retropharyngeal lipomas (RSLs) to provide innovative cumulative descriptive data.

Materials and methods

A literature search was conducted using the Google Scholar search engine, Scopus, and PubMed databases, with keywords such as retropharyngeal space lipoma, retropharyngeal lipogenic tumors, lipomatosis, and head and neck. We also linked the search keywords using the operators "AND" and "OR" in appropriate combinations. We did not apply any time limitations. We conducted the last search in February 2025.

Inclusion and Exclusion criteria—tumors were considered retropharyngeal if a benign lipomatous tumor appeared to develop originally in the retropharyngeal space. Those that extended into the retropharyngeal space but were derived from a nearby area were excluded. We included cases with syndromic lipomatosis that had at least one such tumor in the retropharyngeal space. We also excluded retropharyngeal lipomas with special histological features, such as spindle cell origin or infiltrating pattern.

Case report

A 22-year-old man of Indo-aryan ethnicity presented to our department with a 4-year history of progressive dysphagia, obstructive sleep apnea, dyspnea, dysphonia, and hyponasality. There were no neurological symptoms (nasal regurgitation, fatigability, or diurnal variation of dysphagia). A physical examination of his throat revealed a mass covered by normal mucosa and filling the oropharynx visible through the open mouth, bulging into the soft palate and pushing the tongue forward. No swelling was visible in the neck. A flexible nasal endoscopy showed a growth coming from the oropharynx that blocked the view of the larynx and caused saliva to collect in both valleculae. Routine biochemical and hamatological examinations were unremarkable. The MRI revealed a low-density, heterogeneous hyperintense on T1–T2 w images lesion measuring 3.5 cm × 5.7 cm × 1.8 cm in the posterior pharyngeal wall extending laterally to carotid space Right > Left. The mass was displacing the airway and carotids laterally (Fig. 1). The lesion extended anteriorly to compress the oropharynx, trachea, and esophagus, and posteriorly the paravertebral muscles, which were seen flattened (characteristic for massive lipoma). There was no invasion of the adjacent muscular structures. These findings suggested the diagnosis of a benign retropharyngeal lipoma. The intraoral approach was impossible due to the huge size of the lipoma. The patient underwent excision of the lipoma under general anesthesia via the transcervical approach. A temporary tracheotomy was performed under local anesthesia. General anesthesia was then administered through the tracheotomy. Via a right curvilinear extended submandibular skin incision, the submandibular gland was identified and retracted anteriorly. The right carotid sheath was separated from the mass and retracted laterally. The trachea, esophagus, and pharynx were retracted medially. The thin fascia investing the mass was split longitudinally, thus exposing it. It was fatty in appearance and consistency. With blunt dissection, it shielded easily from the surrounding structures. In its deepest extension, it lay over the right side of the vertebral bodies of C4 and C5. It adhered to the tissue beneath the longus colli muscle (Fig. 2). Fibers of the latter were excised, and the lipoma was completely freed and delivered in a single piece (Fig. 3). The size of the tumor was found to be 8.5*9.5*3.5 cm. The wound was closed in layers. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of a benign lipoma without evidence of malignant change (Fig. 4). Tracheostomy was closed after 14 days. The postoperative course was unremarkable. Discussion

Lipomas are the most common mesenchymal neoplasms, which are encapsulated and consist of adipose tissue cells separated by fibrous trabeculae. They are mostly in subcutaneous tissue but rarely in the retropharyngeal space [4, 5]. Approximately 13% of them occur in the head and neck, most of them in the subcutaneous plane [6–8]. Less than 80 occurrences of retropharyngeal lipomas have been documented in the literature, making them uncommon tumors that usually occur sporadically. Taylor's RSL from 1877 is the oldest known RSL case [9].

Most of these lesions have a slow, insidious growth and are clinically asymptomatic until they achieve considerable size [10]. Pain is unusual and depends on the amount of pressure the mass exerts on surrounding tissues [1]. They can extend from the base of the skull to the superior mediastinum [11–13]. To include this entity in the differential diagnosis, a high degree of suspicion is necessary. It is necessary to rule out malignancy, abscess, hemorrhage, and other benign tumors, such as retropharyngeal space tumors of neuroectodermal origin. Imaging is a vital tool for distinguishing between possible diagnoses. Moreover, it provides valuable information regarding preoperative planning. Almost always, surgical excision is chosen as the treatment, and full recovery is anticipated.

In 1940, Putney and Fry [14] added two of their instances to the 15 that they had discovered in the literature. Haddad et al. evaluated the literature and reported an RSL case in 2005 [15]. We obtained information for about 24 cases from the literature review conducted in these two publications. We obtained full-length articles once we made the final decision about which periodicals to include. Data about patients' age and sex, tumor characteristics, clinical presentation, duration of symptoms, imaging modalities and imaging findings, management and surgical approaches, treatment outcomes, and follow-up strategy were extracted from 53 publications covering 79 eligible cases of RSLs from 1877 to 2024, when available. In addition to a narrative review, the entire set of data was examined and merged to produce fresh summary data that were displayed as descriptive statistics for the entire group. Another 2 cases were multiple symmetric lipomatosis cases (MSL, or Madelung disease, or Launois–Bensaude syndrome) fulfilling the aforementioned criteria [16, 17]. These publications are collectively listed in Appendix A. Only two examples of concurrent Multiple Symmetrical Lipomatosis (MSL) and Retropharyngeal Lipomas (RSLs) that satisfied our inclusion criteria were found in our literature search. Ojo et al. [18] evaluated 19 cases of MSL with direct laryngeal involvement [18]. However, Zhang et al. found that only six of those individuals had retropharyngeal lipomatosis [19]. Moreover, these six cases concern lipomas with either infiltrating characteristics or symptoms caused by generalized neck lipomatosis rather than RSLs [2]. Our review exclusively identified RSLs as a component of MSL. Patients with MSL, a condition characterized by subcutaneous fatty deposits mostly affecting the head, anterior and posterior necks, upper torsos, and upper extremities, are often men between the ages of 3 and 7 [20]. Additionally, the Mediterranean countries seem to have a higher prevalence [21]. The research has proven a robust correlation between moderate to high alcohol use and a few pathogenetic ideas [20–23].

In 78 out of 81 cases, age data were retrievable. Tusini's example does not specify the patient's age or sex, while Pfalz, Faulder, and Jefferson all use the terms "aged" and "middle-aged" to describe their patients, respectively. According to an analysis of data from the remaining 78 instances, patients with RSLs seem to range widely in age, from 3 months to 76 years, with a mean of 46.1 ± 20.4 years and a median of 50.5 years (Fig. 5). In our case, it was 22 years old. A male predominance was discovered, disregarding Tusini's case. (61.72%, n = 50) (Fig. 6). The review did not contain background history because it was hardly mentioned in the literature. No symptoms were documented in 13 cases. These cases, which Putney and Frey collected, relate to instances published between 1879 and 1934, but we could not get the full texts. Consequently, information from 67 symptomatic instances was examined (Fig. 7). With a mean of 38.75 months, the duration of symptoms varied greatly. In three cases, the symptoms were described as lasting "several weeks", "a few months", and "a few years", while in 21 cases, no pertinent information was given. Ten patients (12.34%) presented with a single symptom, and 48 patients (59.25%) reported more than one. Dysphagia (55.55%, n = 45) was by far the most common symptom, followed by snoring (32.09%, n = 26), dyspnea (29.62%, n = 24) and dysphonia (27.16%, n = 22). Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was reported in 17 cases (20.98%) and daytime somnolence in 12 (14.81%). In 12 cases (14.81%), neck swelling was also reported as an initial complaint. Stertor or stridor (7.4%, n = 6) and a pharyngeal mass (4.93%, n = 4) were less commonly encountered. Nine instances (11.2%, n = 9) had "phlegm in the throat" or "speech disturbances" as one of the several symptoms. In our case, the patient also had multiple symptoms, all four of which were prominent. The symptoms that patients report are due to the pressure from the lipomas, mainly depending on which part of the airway and digestive system is affected, and these symptoms often overlap. Retropharyngeal space lesions frequently manifest as progressive respiratory distress, voice changes, and swallowing difficulties. The most common symptom reported in the literature is dysphagia, while dyspnea, dysphonia, snoring, and stridor have also been reported. Our review also found that the most prevalent symptom was dysphagia. It could be explained by the mechanical obstruction brought on by the projecting lipoma, which narrows the pharynx. Dyspnea, which is brought on by a partial blockage of the airway because of the tumor and the corresponding bulging of the posterior pharyngeal wall, usually become worse with time and is commonly described as being worse when resting prone or supine. Since the RSL grows slowly and individuals progressively experience symptoms that eventually compel them to seek medical attention, emergency obstruction of the upper aerodigestive tract is extremely uncommon. The turbulent airflow in the constricted airway is the cause of snoring. The fact that it usually goes away once the tumor is removed confirms that the lipoma, not other anomalies of the upper aerodigestive tract, is the cause. Dysphonia, which ranges from hoarseness of voice to thick and occasionally incoherent speech, is likely the most ambiguous of the symptoms linked to retropharyngeal lipomas. As previously mentioned in most of the pertinent studied cases, patients typically report this symptom during the questioning stage rather than as their first complaint because of the malignancies' gradual progression. Similar to snoring, stertor is caused by turbulent airflow surrounding the retropharyngeal lipoma. Our review found that OSA may be present in about 25% of patients with a retropharyngeal lipoma, so it must be considered when treating them. However, not all those cases have undergone a full OSA workup. Only seven of the 17 cases with reported OSA have used polysomnography to establish a diagnosis. Similarly, two out of those 12 cases evaluated somnolence using scoring tests like the Epworth Sleepiness Scale. Because retropharyngeal lipomas can show various symptoms and may appear in unusual ways, it is a good idea to use polysomnography and keep detailed records, especially for patients who snore or feel sleepy during the day.Fig. 7Symptoms and their frequency

Concerning the size of these tumors, comparative and general terms were used in five cases, like "enormous", "larger than a fist", "a fist", "large", and "giant". Three cases used the lipoma's extent ("C2–T2", "C2–T4", and "palate–hypopharynx") to describe its size. In nine cases, no information regarding tumor size was provided. In all other cases, the largest dimension provided in histology reports was used to calculate tumor size. The mean size of RSLs was found to be 9.24 ± 3.7 cm (range 3–19 cm). In our case, the tumor size in the largest diameter was 9 cm. Regarding the objective physical examination results, nearly all writers report a variably restricted pharynx and a projecting posterior pharyngeal wall that were discovered during endoscopy and physical examination. In a few cases, lateral asymmetry was evident. The pharyngeal mucosa is always intact, and the bulge is said to be soft-structured and movable if it can be palpated. The tumor may be palpable on one or both outer lateral neck surfaces, depending on the lipoma's location in the retropharyngeal region and, more importantly, its size. To include this item in the differential diagnosis, a high degree of suspicion is necessary. The differential diagnosis must also include malignancy, abscess, hematoma, and other benign tumors of the retropharyngeal space.

Computed tomography (CT) was the most commonly implied imaging modality, accounting for 54.32% of the cases (n = 44). CT was used alone in 18 cases and in combination with another imaging modality in 25. A simple neck radiograph (X-ray) was performed in 19 cases (23.45%). X-rays were used in half of the cases before CT became popular, and they have never been used as a single imaging modality again. The first documented use of MRI in assessing an RSL occurred in 1991. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) was used in 21 cases, representing 25.92% of the total. MRI was then employed alone in six cases and in conjunction with CT in 15. Two cases (2.46%) involved the use of neck ultrasonography (U/S), and in both, CT and MRI were added. Dysphagia was identified in four (4.93%) of the individuals that underwent a barium swallowing study. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) was used in conjunction with MRI in one of the most recent occurrences (1.23%). No data regarding imaging investigations were provided in 15 cases, while in four cases, no imaging was performed. CT and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging are advantageous in the preoperative assessment of the patient [24, 25]. The retropharyngeal lipoma appears as a well-defined, low-attenuation mass of fat density on a CT scan, devoid of ossifications or infiltrating features [1]. MRI can assist in a more accurate evaluation of the surrounding structures, such as great neck vessels, and the tumor’s relation to them when needed [26]. The lipoma on the MRI shows homogeneous post-contrast enhancement on T1-weighted images, heterogeneously hyperintense on T1- and T2-weighted images, and hypointense on T1 fat-sat images. Tumors of neuroectodermal origin have a low signal intensity on T1-weighted sequences, a high signal intensity on T2-weighted sequences, and a strong enhancement after gadolinium administration. No flow voids are visible in these tumors [27]. However, none of these characteristics are specific for a clear diagnosis. Given their advantages over traditional CTs, such as improved picture quality and processing with a reduced radiation dosage, more recent techniques, including CBCTs, can be employed with positive outcomes [28]. Their application in evaluating retropharyngeal space tumors is still restricted; nonetheless, more research is required. Fat-suppression pulses are also used to suppress fat [8].

Fine Needle Aspiration (FNA) was performed in two cases, excluding malignancy in the first and confirming the diagnosis of a lipoma in the second. A CT-guided needle biopsy was performed in one case. When it comes to needle biopsy, it can be useful for ruling out other issues in the retropharyngeal space, like hematoma, infections, or cancerous growths. Nonetheless, research generally shows that mature, benign adipocytes resemble healthy fat tissue [29]. However, its application has been limited, and further information is required. Notably, routine imaging of RSLs is typically highly indicative of a fatty tumor, which appears to be a common reason why routine FNA use is avoided.

Surgery was the treatment of choice in 77 cases (95.06%). One case did not provide any treatment-related data. Three of the cases that were treated lacked a specified surgical approach. In one instance—the first time documented—the RSL was discovered after death. The most popular method was the transcervical (45.67%, n = 37), which was followed by the transoral (34.56%, n = 28). The submandibular approach was selected in two cases (2.46%), and a combined transoral and submandibular approach was the option in one case (1.23%). Finally, one case of RSL was treated with excision via Transoral Robotic Surgery (TORS) (1.23%). In two early cases, the patients had undergone radiotherapy at some point before surgical excision (Fig. 8). The location of the lipoma affects the surgical strategy [14, 15, 29]. An external or transoral strategy might be used to treat retropharyngeal lipoma. Recently, certain cases of retropharyngeal lipoma have been excised using transoral robotic surgery [30]. Due to the reduced postoperative morbidity, shorter hospital stay, and lack of scar tissue, transoral excision is the most popular surgical technique [11, 12, 24, 25, 29–31]. However, because it provides faster access to the mass, the transcervical route is a viable alternative for big lipomas. However, the risk of harming the carotid artery and cranial nerves is higher than with the transoral method, and post-surgery morbidity is higher [13, 31, 32]. Tracheostomy was performed in 22 cases (27.16%) either as an elective (68.18%, n = 15) or as an emergency procedure (31.81%, n = 7). A feeding tube was placed in 10 cases, in seven of which transoral excision was performed, while the transcervical approach was selected in the other 3. The mean duration of nutrition via a feeding tube was 8.8 days; however, the duration is stated as "a few days" in two instances. The 'wait and see' strategy was used in five instances (6.17%). Histopathological confirmation is necessary to differentiate between lipoma and liposarcoma following surgical removal. Lipomas consist of lobulated, mature, slow-growing adipose tissue that contains a small amount of connective tissue stroma. They are commonly enclosed in a thin, fibrous capsule.

Complete recovery was reported, following treatment, in 63 cases (77.77%), while in 4 cases where RSL was followed up, no change was documented (4.93%). In two cases (2.46%), death was the outcome (Fig. 9). According to reports, septic complications after surgical excision of the RSL in the second instance and the fatal outcome in the first case were caused by airway obstruction. Thirteen cases (16.04%) have no outcome data reported. There are two cases where an RSL recurrence is reported and 35 cases where no recurrence is reported. 43 cases had no recurrence data available, except for follow-up cases where the RSL remained stable. The mean duration of follow-up was 38.5 months. The vast majority of cases demonstrate complete recovery regardless of the selected approach. In most cases, the intensity of the symptoms diminishes on the first postoperative day. The available follow-up data demonstrated that the symptoms can either further improve or completely disappear within a few months. When an individual discovers RSL accidentally or presents with minimal symptoms, a cautious wait could be considered a suitable initial strategy. The present study is subject to several limitations. Evidence of a high level was lacking because the particular entity was uncommon and there were no quasi-experimental, case–control, or cohort studies. Data drawn from descriptive studies, case series, and individual instances were attempting to be synthesized. Both the data and the level of reported detail showed notable heterogeneity. Additionally, in several instances, some of the data were either unclear or absent, which diminished the comprehensiveness and strength of our findings. However, we are confident that we covered the vast majority of RSL institutional case series and individual case reports. Even if we might have overlooked any case reports or series that were published in less popular and frequently used databases, we do not believe that such an oversight would significantly affect our conclusions.

Conclusion

Retropharyngeal lipomas are rare benign tumors with a higher prevalence in male patients. This case is reported because of the rarity of the site, presentation and age group. Since a tumor is often encapsulated, it can be easily dissected and excised. Clinical presentation is usually diverse, with the chief or initial complaint varying among patients. Symptoms are attributed to pressure phenomena because of tumor growth, with dysphagia being the most common. Retropharyngeal lipomas can grow to a large size rather silently. Imaging is necessary for diagnosis, with CT scanning being usually sufficient as a single-modality test. In the vast majority of cases, these lipomas are treated surgically; a 'wait and see' approach is selected as a management plan only in those cases that either are unfit for surgery or the patients deny or withdraw consent. The literature reports that more than half of the patients received treatment through an external approach. Most preferred is excision through a curvilinear submandibular or longitudinal transcervical approach. Transoral excision is preferred when the lipoma is mainly in the midline, at a higher level in the pharynx, and of relatively manageable size. In one case, the use of TORS demonstrated particularly good postoperative outcomes. If required, a tracheostomy can be done to administer anaesthesia where the airway is obstructed. Diminishing symptoms and full postoperative recovery are the most common outcomes.
