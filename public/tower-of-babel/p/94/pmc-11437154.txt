A global dataset of forest regrowth following wildfires
Authors: Jinlong Zang, Feng Qiu, Yongguang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Wildfires result in forest loss or degradation and release substantial emissions into the atmosphere. Forest regrowth following these fires allows for ecosystem repair and carbon replenishment. However, there is a lack of datasets explicitly characterizing the forest regrowth after fires. Here we employed multiple remotely sensed datasets to generate the first global maps of forest structure regrowth including forest height, aboveground biomass (AGB), leaf area index (LAI), and fraction of photosynthetically active radiation (FPAR) following wildfires at a 30 m spatial resolution. The regrowth index for each structural parameter includes regrowth ratio and rate at 5-year intervals, primarily from 2000 to 2020. The dataset developed in this study provides detailed insights into the characteristics of global forest regrowth following forest fires in both spatial and temporal dimensions, contributing to the assessment of forest ecology equilibrium and the quantification of forest carbon dynamics.

Background & Summary

Forest wildfires have been increasing since 2000, resulting in significant forest loss or degradation and the release of substantial amounts of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere1–3. Following these fires, the forest recovery such as naturally regenerated forests and tree plantations plays a crucial role in ecosystem restoration and carbon sequestration4,5. For example, naturally regenerated forests have stored significant amounts of carbon6 and hosted diverse tree species7. However, the benefits and effects of this restoration are contingent not only on the extent of forest recovery but also on its duration and the degree of new forest growth8. Therefore, a comprehensive quantitation of the spatial, temporal, and structural aspects of the secondary forest growth following wildfires is needed to inform the condition of forest ecosystems and facilitate the design of effective policies for forest management from local to global scales.

Remotely sensed products derived from satellite data are extensively utilized for monitoring large-extent forest change dynamics9–12. However, existing remotely sensed datasets often provide information on the spatial extent of secondary forests without explicit structural characterization, such as forest height. Recent advancements in satellite sensor technology have enabled a more comprehensive acquisition of forest structure information. For example, Potapov., et al.13 mapped global forest canopy height at 30 m spatial resolution through integration of Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation (GEDI) footprint-level height measurements14 and global Landsat analysis-ready data. Nevertheless, there is a lack of datasets that integrate both the spatial extent and structural information of the forest regrowth following wildfires. Additionally, the design of indices for explicit characterization and calculation of the regrowth conditions in forest structure presents technical challenges.

In this study, we aim to generate global maps of the forest regrowth in multiple structural parameters following wildfires during 2000 to 2020. Specifically, we: (1) determined regrowth types following wildfires using existing remotely sensed datasets; (2) developed multiple regional age-height models by linking forest height and regrowth time to generate global time-series forest height maps within forest regrowth areas after the wildfires; (3) generated global time-series aboveground biomass (AGB) maps based on the global time-series forest height maps using regional height-AGB allometric equations; (4) mapped global time-series forest regrowth ratios and rates for forest height and AGB; (5) mapped global time-series forest regrowth ratios and rates for Leaf Area Index (LAI) and Fraction of Photosynthetically Active Radiation (FPAR) within forest regrowth areas after the wildfires using available time-series datasets.

Methods

Data preprocessing

Forest fire dataset

We define the forest wildfire as stand-replacement forest fires, encompassing both natural and human-ignited fires that lead to direct loss of tree canopy. This definition aligns with the one provided by Tyukavina, et al.3. Incidents involving the burning of previously felled trees are excluded from this definition. Tyukavina, et al.3 created the first global 30-m resolution satellite-based map of annual forest loss due to fire based on the extent of the global 30-m resolution 2000–2019 forest loss dataset10. This map offers a depiction of the global extent of forest wildfires and records the year of occurrence in each pixel. User’s accuracy and producer’s accuracy of the forest fire classification are 0.84 ( ± 0.02) and 0.82 ( ± 0.02), respectively. We selected the fire pixels with medium and high certainty level in this map as reliable forest wildfire distributions. Based on the recorded year of the forest wildfire, we calculated regrowth time following the wildfire at the pixel scale for year 2020.

In the United States, the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity (MTBS), an interagency program, offers a longer time-span burned area dataset including the burn severity and extent of large fires across all lands of the United States from 1984 to present15. They primarily used Landsat (TM/ETM +/OLI) imagery (30 m resolution) to identify and delineate the burned area boundaries and counted the number of pixels for each type of burn severity within each vector boundary. The burn severity has been classified into low, moderate, and high classes. This dataset can substitute the corresponding portion for the United States in the global forest fire dataset due to its longer time series. We selected the burned area boundaries where the combined count of moderate and high burn severity pixels exceeds 70% because fires at the location of these pixels pose a threat to species composition15. Similarly, we calculated the regrowth time following the burn in the United States for year 2020 based on the provided burn year information.

In Canada, the Canada Centre for Mapping and Earth Observation and the Canadian Forest Service developed a National Burned Area Composite (NBAC) that provides the area of forest burned on a national scale for each year since 198616–18. They also primarily used the Landsat imagery (30 m resolution) to detect the burned area, and delineated forest fire boundaries along with the corresponding year of occurrence. This dataset can substitute the corresponding portion for Canada of the global forest fire dataset due to its longer time series. We have employed all the forest fire boundaries from this dataset because the majority of fires in Canada’s forests tend to burn at high severity19,20. Similarly, we calculated the regrowth time following the forest fire in Canada for year 2020 based on the recorded years of wildfires.

Forest cover dataset

Shimada, et al.21 generated global forest/non-forest map (FNF) through classifying the Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) image (backscattering coefficient) in the global 25-m resolution Phased-Array L-band Synthetic Aperture Radar (PALSAR) and Phased-Array L-band Synthetic Aperture Radar - 2 (PALSAR-2) SAR mosaic so that strong and low backscatter pixels are assigned as forest and non-forest, respectively. The accuracy assessments indicated the overall agreement of 85%, 91% and 95% against the Degree Confluence Project, the Forest Resource Assessment, and high-resolution Google Earth images, respectively. This dataset has been updated to cover the time range from 2007 to 2020. We used high-resolution 2020 Google Earth imagery to verify the forest areas mapped in 2020 across various global locations, and the results indicate a high mapping accuracy (Fig. S1).

Prior to employing the FNF map, we applied the averaging method to aggregate the pixels of the map from 25 m to 30 m resolution for matching the resolution of the forest wildfire map. We considered the forest area in the FNF map for the year 2007 as the early forest area and used the wildfire map as a mask to extract it to derive early forest areas disturbed by wildfires. This step is necessary for the United States because we have utilized the burned extents15 there rather than the forest wildfire extents. Then, we used the forest area in FNF map for the year 2020 as a mask to identify forest recovery areas (secondary forests) in 2020 from the early forest area disturbed by wildfires. Next, we used the secondary forest area as a mask to extract the regrowth time map in 2020, resulting in a secondary forest regrowth time map for year 2020. Finally, we derived multiple regrowth time maps of secondary forests for different target years according to the secondary forest regrowth time map for 2020. For example, the secondary forest regrowth time map for year 2015 was derived by subtracting the value of 5 from the secondary forest regrowth time map for year 2020 at the pixel scale. Resulted pixels with negative or zero values were excluded. This process resulted in regrowth time maps of secondary forests for the years 1990, 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, and 2015 in North America and the years 2005, 2010, and 2015 in other regions.

Forest height dataset

Potapov., et al.13 mapped global forest canopy height at 30 m spatial resolution for years 2000 and 2020 through integration of GEDI footprint-level forest canopy height measurements and global Landsat analysis-ready data. We used the secondary forest area in 2020 as a mask to extract the forest canopy height for the years 2000 and 2020, resulting in a virgin forest height map for the year 2000 and a secondary forest height map for the year 2020. However, the forest wildfire dataset includes fires occurred before 2000 in North America (the United States and Canada), thus the virgin forest height in North America around the year 1980 before the forest wildfire needs to be determined. Given the absence of a forest height dataset before 2000, we assumed: (1) the virgin forest height before disturbances is similar to that in nearby undisturbed forest areas, and (2) the virgin forest height no longer increases over time. Consequently, we calculated the mean value of 2020 forest heights in forest areas without any disturbances before as the virgin forest height in North America. In this step, to determine the forest areas without any disturbances before, we utilized global 30-m resolution forest loss data during 2000 to 2020 from the Global Forest Change (GFC) dataset10 in addition to the forest wildfire dataset to exclude the disturbed forest areas.

LAI and FPAR dataset

Ma and Liang22 and Ma, et al.23 used deep learning models to generate new version of global LAI and FPAR products (V6) at 250 m resolution from Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) surface reflectance data from 2000 to present, as part of the Global Land Surface Satellite (GLASS) product suite. The LAI product (V6) was directly validated using 79 high-resolution LAI reference maps from three in situ observation networks, demonstrating high accuracy with a root mean square (RMSE) of 0.92 at 250 m resolution. The FPAR product (V6) was validated using high-resolution FPAR maps from the Validation of Land European Remote sensing Instrument (VALERI) and ImagineS networks, revealing higher accuracy than comparable products, with an RMSE of approximately 0.10 at the 250 m resolution.

We calculated the LAI and FPAR medians at the pixel scale from all maps within a year to represent the LAI and FPAR values for that year, and applied the bilinear interpolation method to resample them to a 30 m resolution for matching the resolution of other datasets. The bilinear interpolation method produced relatively smooth results and achieved a good resampling accuracy from 250 m to 30 m resolution, with the coefficient of determination (R2) exceeding 0.8 (Fig. S2). Then, we used the secondary forest area in 2020 as a mask to extract the LAI and FPAR maps for the year 2000, resulting in virgin forest LAI and FPAR maps, respectively, corresponding to the secondary forest area. For North America, we employed the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) LAI and PFAR dataset (1981-2013) at a resolution of 0.05° from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Climate Data Record to supplement the data before 2000. Similarly, we calculated the AVHRR LAI and FPAR medians at the pixel scale from all maps within a year of this dataset, and resampled them to the 30 m resolution. Then, we used the secondary forest area in 2020 as a mask to extract the AVHRR LAI and FPAR maps for year 1982 to derive virgin forest LAI and FPAR in North America, respectively.

The external data utilized in this study includes: (1) the forest fire dataset3,15–18 accessed from https://glad.umd.edu/dataset/Fire_GFL/, https://mtbs.gov/direct-download, and https://cwfis.cfs.nrcan.gc.ca/datamart/metadata/nbac, respectively; (2) the forest cover dataset10,21 sourced from https://storage.googleapis.com/earthenginepartners-hansen/GFC-2022-v1.10/download.html and https://www.eorc.jaxa.jp/ALOS/en/dataset/fnf_e.htm, respectively; (3) the forest height dataset13 downloaded from https://glad.umd.edu/dataset/GLCLUC2020; (4) the LAI and FPAR dataset22,23 acquired from https://glass-product.bnu.edu.cn/ and the NOAA Climate Data Record 10.7289/V5M043BX, respectively.

Mapping regrowth type following wildfires

According to the Version 2 FNF map from Shimada, et al.21, the “forest” is defined as the tree covered land with an area larger than 0.5 ha and a canopy cover of over 10%, in accordance with the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) definition of forest. Based on this, they further divided the forest areas into two types: forests with a canopy cover of 90% or more (dense forest) and forests with a canopy cover of 10% to 90% (non-dense forest). We remained the two types within secondary forest areas and used them as a mask to identify non-forest recovery areas from early forest areas disturbed by wildfires. The non-forest recovery areas were classified as “other”, signifying other various regrowth types following wildfires. Developing age-height model to generate historical forest height and AGB maps

According to the geographical regions specified by the height-biomass allometric equations in Xu, et al.24 and the secondary forest extent in the regrowth land type map, we divided the study area into following regions: North America, Europe, North Eurasia (North of 40°N), South Eurasia (South of 40°N), South America, Africa, Southeast Asia, and Australia (Fig. 2). We utilized the approach outlined in Zang, et al.25 to estimate the secondary forest height for historical years in these regions. This approach involves calibrating the regional age-height model for tree growth to derive the historical forest height. Following this approach, we used the secondary forest regrowth time map in 2020 and secondary forest height map in 2020 to calibrate the secondary forest age-height model for each region.Fig. 2Spatial distribution of calibration and validation data in secondary forest areas.

Spatial distribution of calibration and validation data in secondary forest areas.

We first randomly generated 300,000 points for each region to extract samples from both the secondary forest regrowth time and height maps in 2020. Valid samples within the secondary forest area were employed and divided into two parts, with 70% used for calibrating the age-height model and 30% used for validating the estimated result (Fig. 2). The valid samples in different regions vary in number due to the different areas of secondary forests.

The exponential model, known for its effectiveness in simulating the growth of forest height with stand age26, was employed in this study to fit the curve between the regrowth time and secondary forest height. Figure 3 shows the best fitting results for each region, and Table 1 provides detailed information on the fitted models. Although the R2 ranges from 0.38 to 0.48 (Table 1), which is lower than the R2 of 0.62 reported in Potapov, et al.27, the RMSE falls between 3.05 m and 4.22 m (Table 1), outperforming the RMSE of 6.6 m reported in Potapov, et al.27. Therefore, the calibration performance of the exponential models for tree growth in these regions is deemed acceptable. These models reveal that the growth patterns of forest height vary across different regions, but after approximately 20 years of growth, most forests in these regions tend to reach a height of around Using the regional age-height models (Table 1, equations of the regrowth time to height conversion), we estimated the secondary forest height at the pixel scale for historical years 2005, 2010, and 2015 (additional years 1990, 1995, and 2000 for North America) based on the secondary forest regrowth time map for the respective year. Then, we calculated the secondary forest AGB at the pixel scale for years 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020 (additional years 1990, 1995, and 2000 for North America) based on the secondary forest height maps for the respective years using the regional height-AGB allometric equations in Xu, et al.24 and Huang, et al.28 (Table 1, equations of the height to AGB conversion). Each calculation at the pixel scale for the height or AGB for a specific year generates a corresponding map. The virgin forest AGB in North America was calculated using the corresponding allometric equation from Table 1 based on the virgin forest height in North America.

Mapping forest regrowth ratio following wildfires

We utilized four structural parameters of the forest, including forest height, AGB, LAI, and FPAR, to characterize forest regrowth following wildfires. The forest regrowth ratio following wildfires is defined in this study as the ratio of a structural parameter value in secondary forests after wildfires to the structural parameter value in virgin forests before wildfires. Data Records

The dataset developed in this study is stored in GeoTIFF format at a spatial resolution of 30 m, which can be downloaded on the figshare (10.6084/m9.figshare.27000520)29. The visualization results of the dataset can be accessed using the Earth Engine APP (https://woshigou123456789.users.earthengine.app/view/fire-regrowth). The organization of the dataset is outlined in Fig. S3. We segmented the regrowth dataset globally into multiple regions due to the varied time series of data in different regions. For each region, we categorized the regrowth land type into the secondary forest (dense and non-dense forests) and other regrowth types. In areas of the secondary forest, we mapped the regrowth ratio and rate for each parameter (Height, AGB, LAI, and FPAR) of the secondary forest for multiple years at 5-year intervals. Taking Africa as an example, the filename and naming convention for the bands are:

Filename:

Africa_Height_regrowth.tif: Height regrowth of secondary forests following wildfires in Africa.

Africa_AGB_regrowth.tif: AGB regrowth of secondary forests following wildfires in Africa.

Africa_LAI_regrowth.tif: LAI regrowth of secondary forests following wildfires in Africa.

Africa_FPAR_regrowth.tif: FPAR regrowth of secondary forests following wildfires in Africa.

Bands of a file such as the Africa_height_regrowth.tif are:

Regrowth_type_2020: Regrowth land type in 2020 following wildfires between 2000 and 2019. The dense forest, non-dense forest and others are coded as 10, 15, and 20, respectively.

Height_regrowth_ratio_year: Height regrowth ratio of secondary forests following wildfires for a target year.

Height_regrowth_rate_year: Height regrowth rate of secondary forests following wildfires for a target year.

The unit of the regrowth ratio for all structural parameters of secondary forests is “%”, and the unit of the regrowth rate for height, AGB, LAI, and FPAR of secondary forests is “m/year” with a scale of 0.1, “Mg/ (900 m2 · year)” with a scale of 0.1, “/year” with a scale of 0.01, and “/year” with a scale of 0.0004, respectively.

Technical Validation

We exclusively validated the estimated secondary forest height and AGB values since the forest LAI and FPAR values are directly employed from existing datasets. For the estimated secondary forest height, we first extracted height samples from the estimated secondary forest height map in 2020 using the 30% validation points (Fig. 2), and then compared these estimates with reference height data from Potapov., et al.13 across multiple regions (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Comparisons between our estimated secondary forest heights in 2020 and reference heights from Potapov., et al.13 across multiple regions.

Comparisons between our estimated secondary forest heights in 2020 and reference heights from Potapov., et al.13 across multiple regions.

The validation results demonstrate a reasonable accuracy of the estimated secondary forest height, with R2 ranging from 0.40 to 0.53 and RMSE within the range of 2.64 m to 4.91 m (Fig. 6). Notably, Africa exhibits exceptional performance, achieving an R2 of 0.53 and an RMSE of 2.94 m, indicating superior accuracy in forest height estimation. While some taller trees in regions such as North America, Europe, and North Eurasia exhibit moderate errors, this limitation is common not only in our estimates but also in the methods employed by Zang, et al.25 and Potapov, et al.27 for mapping tree height. Nevertheless, these validation outcomes collectively suggest that our estimated secondary forest heights demonstrate good accuracy and reliability across diverse regions, laying a foundation for the calculation of the forest height regrowth ratio and rate.

For the estimated secondary forest AGB, we utilized the newly released 2020 global forest AGB map of the European Space Agency’s (ESA) Climate Change Initiative (CCI) Biomass products30 as the reference map to validate corresponding map of our results (Fig. 7). The 2020 global forest AGB map was created using revised allometries which are based on the record of spaceborne LiDAR data, with a superior validation performance (RMSE from 29 Mg/ha to 107 Mg/ha within the AGB range of below 400 Mg/ha) against the plot-level AGB data than other existing products reported in Araza, et al.31. First, we aggregated the pixels of our estimated secondary forest AGB map for the year 2020 from 30 m to 100 m resolution using the averaging method, and converted the unit from Mg/900 m2 to Mg/ha to match the data with a resolution of 100 m in Santoro and Cartus30. Then, we randomly generated 300,000 points for each region and selected valid samples within the secondary forest area for the year 2020 to extract AGB samples from both the estimated and reference AGB maps in 2020.Fig. 7Comparisons between our estimated and reference AGBs of secondary forests in 2020 across multiple regions.

Comparisons between our estimated and reference AGBs of secondary forests in 2020 across multiple regions.

The comparison results indicate a reasonable accuracy of the estimated secondary forest AGB, with R2 values ranging from 0.33 to 0.52 and RMSE values within the range of 44.24 Mg/ha to 73.17 Mg/ha (Fig. 7). The secondary forest AGB estimation in tropical regions consisting of South America, Africa, Southeast Asia, and Australia (RMSE from 61.98 Mg/ha to 73.17 Mg/ha) demonstrates suboptimal performance than that in other regions (RMSE from 44.24 Mg/ha to 56.47 Mg/ha). Nevertheless, these comparison results exhibit acceptable accuracy of our estimated secondary forest AGBs in different regions, thus supporting the reliability of the forest AGB regrowth ratio and rate calculations.

Usage Notes

The forest regrowth dataset developed in this study are the first dataset for global forest regrowth following wildfires at 30 m resolution. We specifically focused on the structural regrowth of forests rather than the traditional emphasis on area recovery in previous studies, providing more explicit information on global forest regrowth following wildfires over the past two decades. This dataset serves as a valuable reference for the evaluation of the forest ecology equilibrium and carbon dynamics before and after forest wildfires. In addition, the regional age-height models for tree growth and the regrowth rate equations for both secondary forest height and AGB derived in this study (Table 2) contribute significantly to the regional ecological analyses for forests.

Potential bias in this dataset may arise from the following two aspects: (1) For the 250 m-resolution LAI and FPAR datasets produced by other teams, we employed bilinear interpolation method to resample them to 30 m resolution. Despite achieving a good resampling accuracy (R2 > 0.8, Fig. S2), there may still be discrepancies compared to the actual LAI and FPAR parameters at 30 m resolution. We plan to replace them in the future when finer resolution LAI and FPAR datasets become available. Nevertheless, this limitation does not impede forest regrowth analysis at the regional level; (2) In estimating secondary forest AGB, we utilized regional age-height models and height-AGB models for tree growth. The error transfer of multiple models might result in a higher estimation error for the secondary forest AGB values. However, our comparisons with existing products indicate that the estimates are generally satisfactory, with the exception of slightly higher errors in tropical regions (Fig. 7).

Additionally, while the majority of fires in Canada’s forests tend to burn at high severity19,20, there might be low-severity wildfires that have not yet resulted in a direct loss of tree canopy. The NBAC in Canada will incorporate burn severity information in upcoming tasks18, and we will refine our results in Canada accordingly. However, the low-severity wildfires are expected to be in small quantities, not significantly affecting user’s analytical results or conclusions regarding forest regrowth following wildfires.
