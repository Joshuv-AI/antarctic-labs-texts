The Relationship and Predictive Power of Systemic Thinking With the Attitude, Knowledge and Skills of Nursing Students in the Field of Patient Safety
Authors: Mahya Torkaman, Marzieh Momennasab, Mohammad Hosein Fadaei, Fariba Asadi Noghabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThe aim of this study was to determine the relationship and predictive power of systemic thinking with the attitude, knowledge and skills of nursing students in the field of patient safety.DesignDescriptive correlational design.MethodsThis study was conducted among 228 final‐year nursing students selected via census from three universities in southern Iran from September 2023 to January 2024. Information was collected followed by administering demographic, systemic thinking and attitude, knowledge, skills questionnaires regarding patient safety.ResultsThe participants' systemic thinking mean score was at a high level. Their total mean score of attitude, knowledge, and skills towards safety was at a moderate level. Furthermore, a relatively weak significant relationship was observed between the participants' systemic thinking and their total mean score of attitude, knowledge and skills.ConclusionsThe professors and managers of nursing schools in the field of policy making can employ the present study findings to provide patient‐centered care by planning and improving the nursing students' knowledge in the field of patient safety.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareBy promoting systemic thinking and increasing knowledge, attitude and skills in the field of patient safety, it helps nurses to provide safe and quality care.Public ContributionAll the participants of this study were final year nursing students in three nursing schools of Shiraz, Kerman and Bandar Abbas in Iran who were included in the study based on the entry criteria.Keywords: attitude, knowledge, nursing students, patient safety, skills, systems approach

Introduction

Patient safety is one of the pivotal concepts in the quality of health care services (Choudhury and Asan 2020). Patient safety is defined as preventing any mental, psychological and physical harms in the provision of health care (Chauhan et al. 2020). The World Health Organisation believes that millions of patients around the world suffer from disability, injury or death caused by unsafe care, including healthcare‐associated infections, misdiagnoses, delays in treatment, injuries from inappropriate use of medical devices, as well as medical accidents and errors (Organisation 2021). Based on a national study conducted in Tehran, each nurse committed an average of 19.5 medical errors during a 3‐month period, whereas only about six errors were reported. In other words, the statistical reports on the frequency of hospital errors are not reliable in Iran. However, the high frequency of medical error lawsuits referred to the medical council indicates that the medical teams at hospitals have a greater number of errors than what is reported officially. Furthermore, the money spent annually by the Iranian Ministry of Health and Medical Education in Iran on the medical care of patients hospitalised due to medical errors is another piece of evidence for this claim (Ghobadian et al. 2021). Various studies in recent years have focused on the nurses' attitude, knowledge and skills towards patient safety as one of the main predictors of safe services (Tajari et al. 2023; Fekonja et al. 2023; Han and Roh 2020). In this regard, weakness in delivering safe services is a precursor to medical accidents and errors since these factors play a significant role in behavior formation. Therefore, an enhancement in the attitude, knowledge and skills of the health staff can create an effective impact on delivering safe care services to patients (Choudhury and Asan 2020). In the Iranian health system, which faces challenges such as nursing shortages, high workloads and limited hospital facilities, patient safety is of paramount importance. Given the incidence of medical errors, especially in teaching hospitals, changing the attitude, increasing the knowledge and improving the skills of nurses in the field of patient safety are fundamental concerns of the government's health care system. Among these, systemic thinking is one of the effective factors in reducing these errors. In this context, one of the key factors in reducing these errors is systemic thinking (Kakemam, Albelbeisi, et al. 2022). Systemic thinking, as one of the components of strategic thinking, solves organisational problems by focusing on the problems that arise during the implementation and formation of a conceptual framework through a systemic approach (Chang and Manojlovich 2023). Given that many problems of organisations and societies (especially in developing countries) are rooted in the lack of a systematic view on various phenomena, a nurse with systemic thinking deals with problems systematically, seeking to identify the problem‐causing factors and investigate the association among them (Stalter et al. 2020). The literature has shown that increased levels of systemic thinking improved the employees' performance (Mahsoon and Dolansky 2021; Ahmed and Ibrahim 2023). According to Noseir et al. (2023), the nursing staff's systemic thinking plays an instrumental role in the drug safety of patients. An investigation into the concept of leadership on the quality and safety of health care through systemic thinking clarified that systemic thinking increased the quality and safety of nursing care (Merriam et al. 2022). In Iran, Kakemam, Ghafari, et al. (2022) stated that systemic thinking, root cause analysis and error correction, as well as changing individual and organisational attitudes, can play an important role in providing safe care.

Existing studies often did not examine the relationship of systemic thinking with attitude, knowledge, and skills simultaneously and mainly focused on the participants' behaviour. Although most investigations have studied nurses and limited studies examined students, findings of the research conducted among students notified a lack of thinking skills' development among university pupils. Given that nursing students are attending the patients' bedsides, such skills are necessarily needed to reduce possibility of errors. Furthermore, systemic thinking along with attitude, knowledge, and skills towards patient safety are critical factors in quality improvement and accreditation programs. So, considering the limited number of related studies in Iran, and bearing in mind that nursing students are the future builders of nursing community in safe care, the present study was conducted.

Method

Study Design and Settings

This descriptive correlational (Cathala and Moorley 2018) study was conducted in three nursing and midwifery schools affiliated with Iran University of Medical Sciences in the cities of Shiraz, Kerman, and Bandar Abbas in the south and southeast of Iran.

Participants and Sampling

The target population consisted of 228 final year nursing students. These students were selected due to their clinical experience and concurrent theoretical and clinical nursing coursework.

The sample size was calculated using Medcalc software, considering r = 0.192 at a confidence level of 0.95 and a power of 0.8; 210 people, and with a probability of attrition, 228 were included in the study. The number of nursing students from Hazrat Fatemeh University of Shiraz (n = 79), Razi Nursing School in Kerman (n = 74) and Bandar Abbas University of Nursing (n = 75) was selected for the study using a census method. The inclusion criteria were being a final‐year student at the time of data collection, spending the internship in an inpatient department, and having suitable mental and physical conditions when completing the questionnaire according to the respondents' self‐report. The exclusion criterion was submission of an incomplete questionnaire; when at least 10% of the questions were not responded to or the answers were distorted.

Instruments

To collect data, three questionnaires were administered. The demographic information questionnaire included the respondents' age, gender, marital status, school of study, clinical work experience, history of participating in patient safety workshops, experience of making medical‐care errors during the education, and/or witnessing medical‐care errors made by a member of the therapeutic team.

The systemic thinking questionnaire, designed and validated by Moore et al. (2010) in France, has 20 items that should be answered on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from ‘never’ (no score) to ‘most of the time’ (4 scores). The highest and lowest attainable scores from this questionnaire are 80 and 0, respectively, where higher scores indicate higher levels of systemic thinking. In this vein, scores within the ranges of 0–27, 28–54, and 55–80 reveal undesirable, moderate, and desirable levels of systemic thinking (Moore et al. 2018).

The questionnaire's face and content validity were confirmed based on the opinions of an expert panel in medical sciences, and its reliability was corroborated using the test–retest (r = 0.74) and internal consistency (α = 0.89) (Moore et al. 2010). The Persian version of this tool was localised following the re‐translation process by Moazez et al., and its content validity was confirmed by 10 experts in this field. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient was also calculated as 0.86 for the questionnaire reliability (Moazez et al. 2018). In the present study, the reliability of the questionnaire was examined, and Cronbach's alpha was reported as 0.85%.

The third questionnaire, adapted from the studies by Madigosky et al. (2006) and Leung and Patil (2010), investigated the respondents' attitude, knowledge and skills regarding patient safety. This questionnaire was first translated into Persian by Nabilou et al., re‐translated and revised by two faculty members fluent in English, and then its validity was corroborated by 10 experts. Later, the questionnaire was piloted among 15 students and its Cronbach's alpha coefficient was reported as 0.723. The obtained scores within the ranges of 26–61, 62–96, and 97–130 manifest the respondents' low, moderate, and high levels of attitude, knowledge, and skills towards patient safety. The questionnaire includes 26 items covering three dimensions of attitude towards patient safety (12 items) with two subscales of error causes (items 1–4) and error management (items 5–12), respondents' knowledge of patient safety (six items), and skills regarding patient safety (eight items). The items were answered on a five‐point Likert scale including ‘totally disagree’ (one score), ‘disagree’ (two scores), ‘no idea’ (three scores), ‘agree’ (four scores), and ‘totally agree’ (five scores). The lowest and highest attainable scores are 26 and 130, respectively (Nabilou et al. 2015).

The reliability of this tool was calculated and Cronbach's alpha for the total and the domains of attitude, knowledge, and skills were 0.92, 0.89, 0.79 and 0.91, respectively.

Data Collection

Data were collected using an anonymous, self‐report, and structured questionnaire online from September 2023 to January 2024. To collect data, the first, second, and third researchers collected data from nursing students at the Shiraz, Kerman, and Bandar Abbas Universities. To this end, three separate WhatsApp groups were formed for the students from each of the three universities. The students were provided with comprehensive explanations on the study purpose and data collection process. Later, the questionnaire link was posted in the group, and a specific time frame was set for participants to fill out the questionnaires. Furthermore, reminder messages were sent to students via WhatsApp messages to fill out the questionnaires. Since 20 students filled out the questionnaires incompletely and were excluded from the study, the response rate was 91.22%.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of REDACTED (IR.REC. REDACTED). To conduct the study, all three authors approached the university authorities and made the necessary arrangements by providing a letter of introduction. Also, before data collection, the purpose and method of the study were explained to the participants. The participants were then explained about the confidentiality and anonymity of the information collected, as well as voluntary participation in the study. Finally, eligible students were asked to sign informed consent forms for participation in the study and then email them to the researchers.

Data Analysis

The collected data were entered into SPSS software version 25 and analysed using descriptive (mean, frequency, percentage) and analytical (Pearson's correlation coefficient and independent t‐test and ANOVA) statistics at a significance level of 0.05. Given that the data had a normal distribution according to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, parametric tests were also run.

Discussion

The present study was conducted to determine the relationship and predictive power of systemic thinking with the attitude, knowledge, and skills of nursing students in the field of patient safety. The mean score of systemic thinking was at a high level in the participants, which is in agreement with the findings of studies conducted by Mahsoon and Dolansky (2021) in Saudi Arabia, Ahmed and Ibrahim (2023), and Noseir et al. (2023) in Egypt. The high level of students' systemic thinking can be attributed to the fact that during their studies, students find out that nurses consult with their colleagues when facing clinical risks because the creation and promotion of group synergy and teamwork are among the most basic requirements of increasing systemic functioning and safety in patients (Kakemam, Ghafari, et al. 2022). Furthermore, studies conducted worldwide and in Iran indicated that increased levels of systemic thinking not only enhanced the culture of patient safety but also prevented unwanted errors and decreased their frequency (Merriam et al. 2022; Moazez et al. 2018). According to McNab et al. (2020), understanding systemic thinking plays a decisive role in the medication safety of patients by nurses (McNab et al. 2020). In the same vein, Belaro et al. (2023) investigated the enhancement of quality and safety of health care through systemic thinking, reporting that systemic thinking increased the quality and safety of nursing care (Belaro et al. 2023).

In a heterogeneous study, Stewart et al. (2023) promoted systemic thinking and professionalism by simulating hospital experiences in nursing and laboratory students in the United States. They mentioned that analysing the root cause of events is an important tool in systemic thinking for improving patient safety. Despite the effort to institutionalise systemic thinking in the health care organisations, conducting root analysis of events and finding systemic causes of events, as components of systemic thinking, are still faced with many weaknesses and challenges (Stewart et al. 2023). Based on a review study, individualistic and partial thinking prevails in most health organisations. Partial thinking considers each organisation individually and separately from other units, causing irreparable human and financial damages (Emami et al. 2023). This inconsistency of the results can be due to the fact that although systematic thinking is emphasised in academic training in Iran, it has been mainly neglected practically at the bedside. Contrary to the present findings, Kharazmi and Kharazmi (2022) indicated that a culture of resistance existed among the health staff in Iran regarding the change towards the prevailing systemic approach in Iranian hospitals. This discrepancy in the results can be attributed to the research method because Kharazmi et al. conducted a qualitative study to investigate and explain the nurses' deep experiences of the safety culture and systemic thinking, but in the present study, a quantitative study was carried out through a self‐report questionnaire.

In the present study, the mean score of students' attitude, knowledge, and skills towards safety was at a moderate level supported by some studies carried out in Iran, China, and Saudi Arabia (Hafezi et al. 2022; Yan et al. 2021; Alquwez et al. 2019). However, Akkajit et al. (2020) considered health care workers in Thailand and stated a high level of attitude, knowledge, and skills towards patient safety among them (Akkajit et al. 2020). The variety in the results can be due to various research populations and the fact that there is no patient safety course in the educational curriculum of the undergraduate level in Iran. In fact, only a 4‐h safety workshop is held for the second‐year students, which is ineffective to change the attitude, knowledge, and skills of students towards safety.

The results of a study in South Korea in 2019 notified that the attitude, knowledge, and skills towards safety were low in newly graduated nurses; they concluded that recruiting enough numbers of experienced nurses in the wards would facilitate providing comprehensive and high‐quality care (Jin and Yi 2019). In the current study, the research community consisted of the final year nursing students who had little professional experience, and their attitude, knowledge, and skills were not at the desired level.

Moreover, the present study results showed that the students' attitudes were higher than their knowledge and skills. However, two studies reported that nurses' knowledge of patient safety was higher than their attitude (Sarfraz 2019; Awan et al. 2017). This discrepancy in the results can be justified by more frequent presence of nurses at the bedside and in‐service training courses on patient safety in the studied communities. Another reason for the nurses' high level of knowledge is that they are faced with the reality of patients' threatening conditions in the hospital environment, which can endanger their positive attitude towards patient safety.

According to the findings of an intervention, more training should be provided to increase the students' knowledge and skills in the field of patient safety by holding educational courses and updating the curricula related to patient safety (De Miguel et al. 2023).

Our results indicated a significant relationship between systemic thinking and the total score of students' attitude, knowledge, and skills towards patient safety. Chang and Manojlovich (2023) in Korea and Mahsoon and Dolansky (2021) in Saudi Arabia reported results consistent with the present study findings. In the same vein, several studies in Spain (Tetuan et al. 2017) and Iran (Hafezi et al. 2022) concluded that systemic thinking had a significant and direct relationship with safe care. This can be justified by mentioning that systemic thinking increases awareness and sense of responsibility among nursing students, which subsequently increases safety (Stewart et al. 2023). Moreover, students are more willing to work as a team during internships, which can be a key strategy to reduce medical errors, increase safe care, and create a safety culture in the health care environment.

The results showed no significant relationship between knowledge and systemic thinking. Given that systemic thinking is a new concept, it does not exist in the educational curriculum of Iran and has been less discussed in the formal and informal trainings of professors. Consequently, the knowledge of nursing students regarding systemic thinking is formed based on their experience and observation in the clinical environment.

The present findings indicated that students' systemic thinking is a strong predictor for their attitude, knowledge, and skills. In line with the present study, Chang and Manojlovich (2023) noted that systemic thinking is a predictor of nurses' safety competence in providing safe and qualified care. Considering that systemic thinking is a strategic approach obtained through investigation, experience, and training, more awareness is required towards the components and pillars of systemic thinking (Kakemam, Albelbeisi, et al. 2022). A qualitative study (2022) in the United States stated that innovative educational programs had a significant impact on promoting systemic thinking and patient safety competence (Merriam et al. 2022).

Our findings also indicated that the attitude, knowledge, and skills of students towards patient safety were higher in men than in women. Al Ma'mari et al. (2020) in Oman described that high work pressure and work fatigue had an effect on the quality of care, safety culture, and safety competence (Al Ma'mari et al. 2020). Considering that men are less affected by such psychological pressures than women, their safety competence and safety culture are higher.

According to the present study findings, students who had experienced medical‐care errors during their studies had higher levels of attitude, knowledge, and skills. In line with these results, Alsulami et al. (2019) in Saudi Arabia also stated that nurses who reported previous experience of medical errors had higher knowledge scores (Alsulami et al. 2019). They explained that since these students have directly faced the complications caused by errors, they achieved higher levels of attitude, knowledge, and skills to prevent error repetition and their consequences.

A significant difference was also observed in the mean scores of students' attitude, knowledge, and skills regarding patient safety according to their school of study so that students' attitude, knowledge, and skills towards patient safety were higher in Bandar Abbas University of Medical Sciences. This can be associated with the fewer number of graduate students in Bandar Abbas School, justifying that most clinical work is entrusted to undergraduate students. However, in Shiraz and Kerman nursing schools, a wide range of post‐graduate courses are presented and less attention is paid to undergraduate students.

The present study results also indicated a significant difference between systemic thinking and age group, representing higher levels of systemic thinking in the age group of 25–45 years. In the same line with this finding, Ahmed and Ibrahim (2023) noted that nurses' systemic thinking improved with age, which can be explained by the fact that systemic thinking is a strategic approach obtained through investigation, experience, and training through the path of becoming a professional.

Finally, the mean scores of systemic thinking were higher among the students of Kerman University of Medical Sciences, which can be attributed to the difference in organisational culture of Kerman University. As a result, nursing students have learned sharing errors with each other without being blamed and reprimanded in the field of clinical education.

Conclusion

Based on the findings, the nursing students were at a high level of systemic thinking but at a moderate level of attitude, knowledge, and skills towards patient safety. Moreover, the results confirmed the positive and significant role of systemic thinking in students' attitude and skills towards patient safety. Considering the importance of systemic thinking as well as attitude, knowledge, and skills of students regarding patient safety in providing quality care, managers and professors of nursing schools are required to pay special attention to these two concepts using different educational strategies to enhance the students' systemic thinking.

Finally, policymakers in the educational system of Iran are recommended to integrate the topics of systems thinking and patient safety into the educational curriculum, thereby providing a basis for improving the analytical, decision‐making, and safe care skills of nursing students. Furthermore, some mentoring and interprofessional education programs can be implemented between nursing students and other medical disciplines, providing them with a better understanding of systemic thinking and its role in improving patient safety through interaction with treatment teams. In this vein, future scholars are recommended to carry out studies on systemic thinking and patient safety in other cultures, societies, and ethnicities among nursing students and nurses.

Limitation

The administration of self‐report questionnaires is considered a limitation of this study. So, future scholars are suggested to employ other data collection methods, such as interviews and observations, to increase the reliability of the collected data regarding patient safety.

Implications for Nursing Knowledge and/or Language Development

Nursing instructors can use the results of this research in the theoretical and clinical training of nursing students in order to adapt to clinical conditions, increase the use of systems thinking and the knowledge, attitude and skills of students towards patient safety.

Also, nursing managers should increase the safe and holistic care by emphasising the improvement of systemic thinking and the knowledge, attitude, and skills of nurses.
