Diagnostic Performance of Sōna GM LFA in Pulmonary Infections Caused by Different Aspergillus Species
Authors: Manuela Loaiza‐Oliva, Paula Funes, María Mercedes Romero, María Sol Barbagelata, Soledad Gamarra, Guillermo Garcia‐Effron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Aspergillus spp. are opportunistic fungal pathogens that can cause severe pulmonary infections, particularly in immunocompromised patients. The worldwide estimated burden is substantial, with chronic pulmonary aspergillosis affecting approximately 3 million individuals annually, and invasive aspergillosis (IA) accounting for an estimated of more than 2 million new cases each year across diverse geographic regions [1] Historically, IA represented a diagnostic challenge. During the 1970s, researchers discovered that the Aspergillus galactomannan (GM) polymer could be detected in the bodily fluids of immunocompromised individuals suffering from Aspergillus infections [2, 3]. Later, Latge's group described a double‐direct sandwich ELISA able to detect this antigen (galactofuran) in IA patient's serum [4]. Since the commercial release of the Platelia Aspergillus ELISA kit (BioRad), GM antigen detection in serum and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid has become a cornerstone in the diagnosis of IA. In contemporary diagnostic frameworks—such as the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer/Mycoses Study Group (EORTC/MSG) and the 2020 guidelines of the European Confederation of Medical Mycology/International Society of Human and Animal Mycoses (ECMM/ISHAM) consensus definitions—a positive GM result, when interpreted alongside relevant epidemiological and clinical data, is considered sufficient to establish a diagnosis of probable IA [5], probable influenza‐associated pulmonary aspergillosis (IAPA) in Intensive Care Units (ICU) [6], and probable Covid‐Associated‐Pulmonary‐aspergillosis (CAPA) [7]. This classification provides adequate justification to initiate antifungal therapy [8].

This central role of GM detection in aspergillosis diagnostics has driven the development of monoclonal antibodies targeting this biomarker, which were subsequently incorporated into lateral flow assay (LFAs) to enable rapid, point‐of‐care testing [9]. This format was recognised as an eligible mycological criterion for probable CAPA in the 2020 ECMM/ISHAM guidelines [7].

Among available assays, the Sōna Aspergillus GM LFA (IMMY) is widely adopted as a rapid, cost‐effective, and accessible tool, showing high diagnostic performance (92% sensitivity, 91% specificity) in serum and respiratory samples [10] Its performance is comparable to established GM ELISAs such as Platelia Aspergillus ELISA kit (BioRad) [11], and multiple studies have confirmed its utility for diagnosing IA and CAPA [12, 13]. Large‐scale multicenter investigations using BAL specimens further validated its specificity and demonstrated that, particularly with the cube reader, sensitivity and specificity approach those of GM Platelia Aspergillus ELISA kit (BioRad) and PCR [14, 15]. Additional evaluations reported improved predictive values when combined with other methodologies [16, 17, 18]. Notably, assay performance varies by patient group, with sensitivities ranging from < 50% in chronic pulmonary aspergillosis [17], ~70% in oncohematological patients [19], and > 90% in ICU cohorts [20]. Collectively, these studies provide robust evidence supporting the integration of Aspergillus LFA into diagnostic algorithms and highlight its value in routine clinical practice.

Although Aspergillus fumigatus remains the predominant etiological agent of IA, an increasing number of cases involving other sections and cryptic species of the genus are being reported, both as clinical pathogens and as environmental isolates. These emerging pathogens often exhibit different antifungal susceptibility profiles, highlighting the importance of precise species‐level identification for both patient care and epidemiological surveillance [21, 22, 23, 24]. GM, initially characterised as specific to A. fumigatus , is now recognised as a common component of the cell wall across Aspergillus species [2, 3, 4] and now, GM‐based assays are routinely employed for the diagnosis of aspergillosis, irrespective of the infecting species [5, 6, 7, 8]. Nevertheless, while these assays have demonstrated strong diagnostic performance in A. fumigatus infections, their accuracy in detecting disease caused by non‐fumigatus species and cryptic species remains insufficiently defined. To address this gap, we evaluated the sensitivity and diagnostic performance of the Sōna GM LFA (IMMY) in BAL samples from patients with pulmonary infections attributed to different Aspergillus spp., including cryptic species. By examining its reliability and limitations in these underexplored etiological agents, our study aims to support species‐conscious diagnostic strategies and enhance early detection across diverse epidemiological landscapes.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a retrospective study to assess the diagnostic performance of the Sōna LFA‐IMMY assay for detecting GM antigens in BAL samples. The analysis included 49 consecutive BAL specimens, each obtained from a distinct patient between December 2019 and December 2023 at two participating laboratories: CEMAR's Microbiology Laboratory (Rosario Argentina) and the Fungal Disease Response Center (Mycology and Molecular Diagnostics Laboratory) at the Universidad Nacional del Litoral (UNL). Consecutive patients were enrolled based on the following inclusion criteria: clinical and radiological findings consistent with IA or CAPA; BAL obtained via bronchoscopy; and microscopic evidence of septate hyphae in Giemsa‐stained smears accompanied by a positive culture for Aspergillus spp.

Cases were subsequently classified as probable CAPA according to the ECMM/ISHAM consensus criteria or as probable IA based on the EORTC/MSG definitions [5, 7]. All participants provided informed consent for the use of their samples in research.

Aspergillus GM Dosage

GM quantifications were conducted using the Sōna LFA + cube reader (IMMY Diagnostics, OK, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 300 μL of BAL supernatant was pretreated with 100 μL of treatment buffer, heated at 120°C for 6–8 min, and centrifuged for 5 min at 12,000 RPM. Then, 80 μL of the supernatant was combined with 40 μL of running buffer in a new assay tube, and a lateral flow strip was added. The test was incubated for 30 min. Numeric results were obtained using the Sōna cube reader.

Microbiological Analysis

Mycological studies were conducted in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M54 guidelines [25]. Briefly, BAL pellets were inoculated onto Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) supplemented with chloramphenicol and incubated at 28°C and 37°C for up to 28 days. Residual pellets were subjected to microscopic evaluation, including Giemsa staining and direct wet mount preparations with and without calcofluor white, as well as with and without 20% potassium hydroxide (KOH). Fungal growth was monitored daily, and colony macro‐ and micromorphological features were analysed to enable identification at the Aspergillus section level, following established taxonomic criteria [26].

ITS, Calmodulin and β‐Tubulin Gene Sequencing

Species‐level identification of Aspergillus isolates was achieved using sequencing of the ITS rDNA region, calmodulin (CaM) and β‐tubulin (BenA) gene. DNA was extracted as previously described following a pheno‐based procedure [27]. The CaM and BenA genes were amplified using the primer sets CMD5/CMD6 and Bt2a/Bt2b, respectively. PCR products were sequenced, and resulting sequences were analysed using BioEdit 7.1.3.0. ITS sequences were compared using the NCBI rRNA/ITS database, while CaM and BenA sequences were aligned and compared to reference databases for standard Aspergillus species identification [26].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated using percentage tables and geometric medians for LFA index values. Galactomannan positivity was defined according to the cutoff values established by the ECMM/ISHAM consensus criteria for CAPA patients and by the EORTC/MSG definitions for IPA. The diagnostic performance of the LFA was descriptively evaluated across Aspergillus species by calculating the proportion of samples with GM index ≥ 1.0 (LFA‐positive) per species. Microsoft Copilot assisted with English language editing in portions of the manuscript.

Results

A total of 49 isolates of Aspergillus spp. were recovered from 49 BAL samples that fulfilled the described inclusion criteria. The samples were obtained from 31 adult patients with acute myeloid Leukaemia (AML), nine paediatric patients with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), and nine adult patients with documented COVID infection.

The majority of the isolates were identified as A. fumigatus sensu stricto (ss) (n = 23; 46.9%), followed by A. terreus ss and A. flavus ss (n = 8 each; 16.3%), A. niger ss (n = 3; 6.1%), and A. nidulans (n = 2; 4.1%). Five of the isolates (10.2%) were identified as cryptic species within the Aspergillus genus, including A. lentulus (n = 2; 4.1%), A. udagawe, A. pseudoterreus, and A. calidoustus (n = 1 each; 2.0%). GM values varied according to species and clinical group. In A. fumigatus s.s., GM indices ranged from 1.56 to 18.03 in adult AML patients, 1.19–2.65 in paediatric patients and 0.2–4.5 in CAPA cases. For A. terreus , values were from 1.76 to 12.10, while for A. flavus, they ranged from 2.21 to 6.03 in adults and 1.82 in paediatrics. Overall, six out of 49 BAL samples (12.25%) yielded low GM values (0.09 to 0.31 by Cube Reader), considered negative according to the manufacturer, Donnelly's and Koeller's guidelines, corresponding to an overall assay sensitivity of 87.8% for IA detection. Five of these six low‐GM cases involved cryptic species: A. lentulus (Cube Reader lecture: 0.15–0.20), A. calidoustus (0.28), A. pseudoterreus (0.31) and A. udagawe (0.22). Only one low‐GM sample (0.2) corresponded to A. fumigatus s.s. (Table 1). Thus, assay sensitivity was 95.6% for A. fumigatus s.s., 100% for other non‐fumigatus sensu stricto species, and 0% for cryptic species.

Discussion

Most published studies evaluating the Sōna Aspergillus GM LFA (IMMY) as a diagnostic tool for IA and its clinical variants are primarily descriptive, focusing on the diversity of presentations according to patient type, underlying disease, or treatment received [28, 29, 30]. In contrast, studies that specifically address the causative agents of IA and viral‐associated forms—such as IAPA and CAPA—consistently identify A. fumigatus as the predominant species and the sole information reported [28, 29, 30]. The objective of this work was not to replicate these analyses, but to provide complementary data by documenting the spectrum of Aspergillus species isolated from BAL and evaluating the performance of the Sōna LFA in diagnosing IA, with particular attention to infections caused by non‐fumigatus species. This dimension has been notably absent from prior studies, many of which were retrospective and did not incorporate culture results. By integrating species‐level identification with diagnostic performance, our study enhances clinical relevance and expands upon existing literature. Specifically, we highlight the diversity of Aspergillus isolates recovered from BAL samples in immunocompromised patients, including those with hematologic malignancies and COVID‐19. The prevalence of A. fumigatus as the causative agent of IA (46.9% in immunosuppressed patients and 88.9% in CAPA cases) mirrors global epidemiological trends, reaffirming its central role in the pathogenesis of these mycoses [31, 32, 33].

The recovery of non‐fumigatus isolates—such as A. terreus , A. flavus , and several cryptic species—in more than half of the samples suggests that these organisms may be underrecognized contributors to disease in certain clinical contexts. Their distinct antifungal susceptibility profiles and variable therapeutic responses underscore the importance of precise mycological characterisation for guiding therapy and informing epidemiological surveillance [10, 34, 35].

Current aspergillosis diagnosis relies heavily on GM quantification, largely due to the low sensitivity of conventional fungal cultures. However, if GM levels fluctuate according to the infecting species, such variability could comprise the utility of GM‐based assays. Species related differences in GM levels in BAL were reported earlier for A. flavus and A. fumigatus ss [36]. Yet, to the best of our knowledge, no data exist regarding GM performance in infections caused by cryptic or uncommon species; yet data on cryptic or uncommon species remain lacking. One of the few reports addressing this issue showed that A. flavus infections yielded lower GM values than A. fumigatus when tested by ELISA, thereby reducing assay sensitivity [37]. In our cohort, six BAL samples yielded GM index values ≤ 0.31, five of which were associated with cryptic Aspergillus species. The low GM indices observed in A. lentulus , A. calidoustus, and A. udagawe may reflect reduced antigen production, altered cell wall composition, or suboptimal assay affinity for these galactomannan epitopes. Importantly, these values were considered negative across all published cutoff (manufacturer's, EORCT/MSG [5]) including those lowered to enhance sensitivity [38, 39, 40].

We also found differences in GM values between studied populations. BAL samples obtained from paediatric patients showed lower GM values. This fact was observed earlier, especially for immunosuppressed children suffering from IA [41].

The detection of cryptic species such as A. lentulus , A. calidoustus, A. pseudoterreus and A. udagawe in 10.2% of our samples is significant. These species are increasingly recognised in immunocompromised hosts and often exhibit reduced susceptibility to triazoles and amphotericin B, complicating treatment decisions [42, 43, 44, 45]. Importantly, all cryptic isolates in this cohort were recovered from patients with leukaemia, suggesting a potential association between these rare species with profound immunosuppression and prolonged neutropenia.

Taken together, our findings underscore the need for species‐conscious diagnostic strategies in suspected IA, particularly in hematologic and intensive care settings. Integrating molecular identification with complementary biomarkers may improve detection rates and guide more precise therapeutic decisions. To date, no studies have systematically assessed GM index values in BAL samples from infections caused by cryptic Aspergillus species. Our cohort provides the first clinical evidence of consistently low GM levels in cases involving A. lentulus , A. pseudoterreus, A. calidoustus, and A. udagawae, highlighting potential limitations in assay sensitivity for these emerging pathogens. Multicenter investigations are needed to confirm these findings and optimise diagnostic algorithms accordingly.
