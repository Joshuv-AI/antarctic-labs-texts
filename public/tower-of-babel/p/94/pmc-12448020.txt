Technical and clinical validation of a novel deep learning-based white matter hyperintensity segmentation tool
Authors: Benno Gesierich, Lukas Pirpamer, Dominik S Meier, Michael Amann, Minne N Cerfontaine, Frank-Erik de Leeuw, Pauline Maillard, Sue Moy, Karl G Helmer, Michael Kühne, Leo H Bonati, Julie W Rutten, Saskia AJ Lesnik Oberstein, Marco Duering
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•New state-of-the-art deep learning-based WMH segmentation methods.•Systematic technical and clinical validation in relevant patient populations.•Excellent generalizability and thus no need for re-training in unseen datasets.•Ready-to-use, available as prebuilt software container images.Keywords: Small vessel disease, White matter hyperintensities, Segmentation, Deep learning, Biomarker validation

Introduction

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) of presumed vascular origin are a marker of cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) on MRI [1]. WMH are frequent in aging populations and their lesion load is associated with cognitive decline, functional impairment, disability, and increased stroke risk [2]. Traditionally, the assessment of WMH has relied on visual rating scales; however, this approach is inherently crude and susceptible to rater bias and error. Lesion segmentation techniques that quantify lesion volume facilitate more detailed quantitative analyses in clinical research [3], such as the assessment of lesion volume change over time [4]. Manual segmentation is labor-intensive and continues to be affected by intra- and inter-rater variability. Consequently, there is high interest in the development and implementation of fully automated WMH segmentation methods.

An optimal, automated WMH segmentation method for clinical research studies has multiple requirements: It should be 1) publicly available, 2) easily deployable, preferably as software container to facilitate reproducible scientific computing, 3) suitable for both modern (3D with ≤ 1 mm isotropic resolution) and traditional (2D with thicker slices) image acquisitions. 4) out-of-the-box applicable to a wide range of image acquisition parameters, without retraining, 5) benchmarked against state-of-the-art methods, 6) properly validated in meaningful target populations, both technically and clinically. A review of publicly available WMH segmentation methods against the specified requirements revealed relevant gaps in all of them (Supplementary Table 1). Thus, despite numerous available methods, there is a clear need for a novel WMH segmentation method to fill these gaps.

The goal of this study was to develop a WMH segmentation method that satisfies all of the above requirements. To enable broad application without the need for retraining, our strategy was to train and validate our deep learning models on a large variety of 3D fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and 3D T1-weighted (T1w) images acquired across multiple studies with varying patient populations. We performed benchmarking against state-of-the-art algorithms and thorough technical and clinical validation, in accordance with consensus recommendations [5,6]. For clinical validation, we focussed on longitudinal lesion volume change as a monitoring biomarker and surrogate endpoint candidate for clinical trials. To meet the requirement for public accessibility, an additional goal was to develop ready-to-use container images with user-friendly processing pipelines.

Methods

Training of WMH segmentation models

We selected two deep learning-based image segmentation methods. The first is based on multi-dimensional gated recurrent units (MD-GRU) and has achieved excellent results segmenting white matter lesions in multiple sclerosis patients [7,8]. The second method is based on the U-Net architecture [9], which is widely recognized for its effectiveness in medical image segmentation [10,11]. In particular, we used nnU-Net, a method recently introduced [12] to automatically self-configure preprocessing, U-Net architecture, training and post-processing.

Ground truth dataset

Details on the ground truth dataset are provided in Supplementary Table 2. It comprised FLAIR and T1w image data from three different studies: ADNI [13], VASCAMY [14] and RUNDMC [15]. Patient selection was performed to cover a large range of WMH volumes and to keep the dataset balanced in terms of MRI hardware. For ADNI, a roughly equal number of patients was included for each available scanner model. Thus, the final ground truth dataset covered a wide range of MR acquisitions (different scanner models, different 3D FLAIR and T1w sequences, e.g. with and without fat saturation, and also including MP2RAGE). Ground truth WMH masks were created semi-automatically, by first segmenting images with an MD-GRU model previously trained for white matter lesions in multiple sclerosis patients [8], and then correcting the resulting masks manually by an expert rater (MD) according to STRIVE consensus criteria [1]. Cavities (i.e., lacunes) were excluded. Great care was taken to exclude regions with ependymitis granularis and insufficient CSF suppression, in particular at the posterior horns of the ventricles, which often collapse and result in false-positive hyperintense signal.

The ground truth dataset (total n = 304; ADNI n = 244; RUNDMC n = 38; VASCAMY n = 22) was split into two subsamples for training (n = 277) and validation (n = 27). Usually a third subsample (test subsample) is held out for benchmarking the algorithms against each other. However, due to involvement of MD-GRU in the preparation of the ground truth masks, we performed benchmarking in a new ground truth dataset, as described below, to avoid annotation bias.

Preprocessing

The T1w image was affine registered to the FLAIR using the “BRAINSFit” function [16]. Brain masks created with “HD-BET” [17] were provided as additional input to “BRAINSFit”, to limit the region considered during registration. For training with MD-GRU, registered images were further brain extracted, using the FLAIR brain mask. The resolution of the images was not changed. Given that both MD-GRU and nnU-Net ignore image orientation matrices, it was important to assert that world-space orientations of the image voxel axes were the same in all images. We allowed however RAS+ and LAS+, meaning that the first voxel axes was allowed to increase towards either the right (R) or left (L) side of the brain, while the second and third voxel axes had to increase always towards respectively the anterior (A) and superior (S) part of the brain.

Training of the MD-GRU model

MD-GRU (https://github.com/zubata88/mdgru; commit 791ee4f) was trained with default parameters (cross-entropy loss, no data augmentation, no filtering), a patch size of 100 × 100 × 100 voxels, padding of 20 × 20 × 20 voxels, and the preprocessed FLAIR and T1w images as input. During training, the current checkpoint was validated every 5,000 iterations by running inference in the validation subsample and calculating the Dice similarity coefficients (referred to as Dice coefficients hereafter) against ground truth labels. The training experiment was run until the Dice coefficients reached a stable plateau in the validation subsample to avoid overfitting (Supplementary Figure 1A). The final model has been published at Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15173317).

Training of the nnU-Net model

nnU-Net (https://github.com/MIC-DKFZ/nnUNet.git; commit 96d44c2) was trained with nnU-Net’s fixed and rule-based parameters as defined during auto-configuration (Dice and cross-entropy loss function; poly learning rate schedule (initial, 0.01); data augmentation, including rotations, scaling, Gaussian noise, Gaussian blur, brightness, contrast, simulation of low resolution, gamma correction and mirroring; 1000 epochs × 250 minibatches, with foreground oversampling; batch size 2; patch size 160 × 128 × 112), using the “3d_fullres” configuration, and the preprocessed FLAIR and T1w images as input. The image resolution used by nnU-Net internally during training was fixed to 1 mm isotropic voxel spacing. By default, the nnU-Net training terminates automatically after 1,000 epochs and uses 5-fold cross-validation to assess performance of the final checkpoint. The Dice coefficients in the validation sample of the 5 folds are shown in Supplementary Figure 1B Consequently, nnU-Net was also used during subsequent inference with all 5 models combined in an ensemble prediction. The final model has been published at Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15173302).

Pipeline design for inference

For inference, the input images should be prepared in the same way as for training.

Therefore, individual pipelines were designed for MD-GRU and nnU-Net, including the respectively required preprocessing steps, the inference with the trained model and, if necessary, post-processing to match the original axes orientation and resolution of the input FLAIR image.

In more detail, registration of T1w to FLAIR and the creation of brain masks required for this task was implemented in both pipelines as described above in the section on preprocessing of training data. Also image axes orientation was checked and adjusted in both pipelines if deviating from LAS+ or RAS+. Only for MD-GRU, resolution was adjusted, when voxel scaling deviated from 1 mm by >0.05 mm. This was done only for the FLAIR and before the registration of T1w to FLAIR, to avoid having to interpolate the T1w twice. For nnU-Net image resolution was not adjusted, given that nnU-Net handles this by itself. Also, only for MD-GRU, brain extraction was performed before inference.

The code for the resulting pipelines (for both algorithms, i.e. MD-GRU and nnU-Net) and the ready-to-use, pre-build container images are publicly available at https://github.com/miac-research/MARS-WMH.

Technical validation

All validation steps, including statistical analyses and plotting, were conducted with R (version 4.4.0, packages: tidyverse 2.0.0, blandr 0.5.1, car 3.1–2, pwrss 0.3.1).

For technical validation of WMH volume as an imaging biomarker, we first estimated bias and precision against semi-automatically created and unbiased reference WMH volumes. These reference volumes were created in a subsample of the MarkVCID study [[18], [19], [20]]. Furthermore, precision was estimated under repeatability and reproducibility conditions in two subsamples of the MarkVCID study, acquired specifically for these purposes. Details are reported in the corresponding subsections below, and the scanning protocols used in MarkVCID were previously described [18]. For WMH segmentation, only the 3D FLAIR and 3D T1w sequences were used. Finally, although the method was primarily trained for the use in modern 3D imaging sequences, we also validated bias and precision in 2D imaging data from the Swiss-AF cohort [21] using a manual ground truth as reference.

In each step of the technical validation, the newly trained MD-GRU and nnU-Net models were benchmarked against each other and against state-of-the-art WMH segmentation algorithms.

The use of transformed values is common in the literature due to the typically skewed distribution of WMH volumes. Thus, for technical validation both non-transformed and square-root transformed WMH volumes were analysed.

Algorithm selection for benchmarking

For the selection of state-of-the-art benchmarking algorithms, we considered algorithms with proven performance in segmentation challenges or benchmarking studies. A further condition for inclusion was the availability of a software pipeline that included all necessary preprocessing steps. An exception was made only if a pipeline required registered images as input, and in this case, the registration was added as an additional preprocessing step. The first of the algorithms selected in this way was PGS [22], a deep learning-based method and the winner of the MICCAI WMH segmentation challenge [23], when the challenge closed in December 2022. The PGS algorithm did not include registration. Therefore, we provided T1w registered to FLAIR as input.

The second algorithm was the SHIVA-WMH tool [24], another deep learning-based method which was reported to yield higher Dice coefficients than LPA from the LST toolbox [25], PGS [22] and the HyperMapp3r tool [26] in SVD patients with a wide range of WMH severity. The required preprocessing steps and the segmentation with the SHIVA-WMH tool can be conducted using the SHiVAi preprocessing and deep learning segmentation workflow, available as a container image and separately downloadable configuration and model files. We used SHIVA with the default parameters specified in that configuration file and the most recent available model, released on April 27th 2023, which is based on the published model [24] but trained with additional images from other datasets. In contrast to all other algorithms, SHIVA outputs WMH masks in the T1w space. Since all our validations are based on the WMH volume, we directly used the volume extracted from these masks. Only for the creation of the unbiased WMH reference masks using STAPLE (see next section) we had to transform the SHIVA WMH masks into the FLAIR space.

As a third algorithm we selected the UCD WMH Segmentation tool (hereafter referred to as “UCD” algorithm), because it is to date the best validated method [19] and it allowed us to compare our deep learning-based methods to a classical method based on template registration and Bayesian segmentation.

All three benchmarking algorithms were used in their containerized version available at: https://hub.docker.com/r/wmhchallenge/pgs, https://github.com/pboutinaud/SHiVAi, and https://github.com/davisidealab/UCD_WMH_Segmentation.

Creation of unbiased reference WMH masks using STAPLE

Reference masks were created in the dataset of the MarkVCID study [[18], [19], [20]] intended for scan-rescan repeatability analysis and comprising 46 SVD patients, each with two scans of the same protocol acquired within 14 days on the same scanner. Reference WMH masks were only created for the first scan of each patient. In order to receive unbiased ground truth masks, the patient datasets were segmented by all 5 algorithms (the two in-house trained algorithms and the three benchmarking algorithms). The five resulting masks per patient were then combined using the Simultaneous Truth and Performance Level Estimation (STAPLE) algorithm [27], and the combined mask was further corrected manually by one expert rater (MD). Two patients were excluded due to strong image artifacts and two patients due to the presence of large infarcts, resulting in 42 patients for analysis.

Bias and precision against reference WMH volumes

The WMH volumes produced by each of the five algorithms were compared against the WMH volumes of the above described reference masks, using Bland-Altman plots and statistics [28], in particular constant bias, proportional bias and – as a measure of precision – the limit of agreement (LOA). Bland-Altman analysis is commonly used to compare methods in an unbiased way, and therefore the difference between the methods is plotted against their mean value. However, given that here our goal was to compare each algorithm against a reference WMH mask, we plotted against the WMH volume of this reference method instead of plotting against the mean volume.

For qualitative benchmarking, face validity was assessed by two expert raters (BG, MD) for each method, by visually inspecting the WMH masks inferred by the five algorithms for misclassifications.

Scan-rescan repeatability and inter-scanner reproducibility

Scan-rescan repeatability and inter-scanner reproducibility were assessed in two subsamples of the MarkVCID study [[18], [19], [20]]. The first MarkVCID subsample of 42 patients, used for scan-rescan repeatability, has already been described above in the context of reference mask creation. Here we used both scan and rescan for each patient, and excluded the same 4 patients as before. The second MarkVCID subsample, used for inter-scanner reproducibility, comprised 20 patients with scans acquired on 4 different scanners: Philips Achieva, Siemens Trio, Siemens Prisma, GE 750 W. Philips Achieva scans were missing for one patient. Bland-Altman statistics were calculated for the comparison of WMH volumes between either scan and rescan session or between scans from different scanners.

Bias and precision in 2D data using a manual reference

The MD-GRU and nnU-Net models were trained with high resolution 3D FLAIR. To assess the generalizability of the new models to 2D FLAIR data with thicker slices, we assessed bias and precision versus a manually created ground truth in a representative subsample of the Swiss-AF cohort, comprising the baseline visit of 55 patients. Patients were selected to represent the WMH volume distribution observed in the entire Swiss-AF cohort. All FLAIR images were acquired with an in-plane axial resolution of 1 × 1 and a slice distance of 3 mm [21]. The manual ground truth in the Swiss-AF cohort was created by the in-house analysis team of MIAC AG, independently of the current project. Infarcts were also segmented but excluded from the ground truth as well as the WMH inference mask output. Bland-Altman statistics were calculated to assess constant and proportional bias and precision (LOA) against the ground truth WMH label.

Clinical validation and power analysis

Clinical validation was performed in data from the DiViNAS study, a prospective cohort study of 126 CADASIL patients with uniformly collected clinical and neuroimaging data (including FLAIR and T1w images) available at baseline and at 2-year follow-up [29]. We validated the ability of the algorithms to detect a longitudinal increase of WMH volume as determined by a positive percentage WMH volume change (PWMHVC). To further assess the feasibility of using PWMHVC as a biomarker for treatment effects in clinical trials, we conducted a power analysis to estimate the sample size required to detect a treatment effect (i.e., a slowing of WMH volume increase over time) of 30 % with a power of 80 % and an alpha level of 5 % [30]. Given that PWMHVC values appeared to be non-normally distributed , two approaches were pursued. The first approach calculated the power for a non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the second approach calculated power for a t-test, after applying a power transformation towards normality to the PWMHVC values. In detail, for the first approach we calculated the power for a non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test, using simulations based on the actually measured PWMHVC values in DiViNAS. Specifically, we created a sequence of sample sizes ranging from 10 to 700, with a step-size of 20. For each sample size N in this sequence, we conducted 10,000 simulations. For each simulation, two samples of length N were simulated, by sampling from the actually measured PWMHVC values with replacement. For one of these two samples, a treatment effect was simulated, by subtracting 30 % of the median from each PWMHVC value. The power was then calculated as the percentage of simulations yielding a p-value < 0.05 in a Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with the alternative hypothesis that PWMHVC in the simulated sample with treatment is smaller than in the simulated sample without treatment. The sample size required for 80 % power was then determined by interpolation. For the second approach, we transformed PWMHVC using a power transformation based on the Yeo-Johnson family [31], which allows for a few negative observations. The transformation was estimated separately for each of the compared segmentation algorithms. Statistical power was then calculated for a t-test in the same range of sample sizes, i.e. from 10 to 700, with an alpha of 0.05 and a treatment effect of 30 %.

Results

Technical validation

Bias and precision against reference WMH volumes

In the unseen MarkVCID dataset, the newly trained algorithms MD-GRU and nnU-Net demonstrated comparable segmentation performance against reference masks and outperformed the three selected benchmarking algorithms (Table 1, Fig. 1). Both constant and proportional bias estimates were lowest for the newly trained algorithms. MD-GRU and nnU-Net also demonstrated superior precision as indicated by smaller one-sided limits of agreement (LOA) and confidence intervals (Table 1) not overlapping with those of the benchmarking algorithms. Results were similar for untransformed and square-root transformed data.

We combined Bland-Altman analysis with visual quality control, as shown in Fig. 1B, to provide a more comprehensive characterization of segmentation problems (for more examples see Supplementary Figure 2). MD-GRU and nnU-Net performed well, without visually identifiable problems. PGS showed major issues with large inconsistencies across image slices, mainly explained by large false negative areas in some slices (Supplementary Figure 3). Due to these large errors, we excluded PGS from further analyses. SHIVA showed generally good segmentation performance, with only few false-positive clusters in non-WMH areas with high FLAIR intensity values (like the septum pellucidum). It however tended to overestimate the extent of WMH clusters. While UCD tended to underestimate the extent of WMH clusters, it also detected false positive clusters, resulting in systematic over-segmentation in patients with low WMH volumes, which explains the observed proportional bias.

Scan-rescan repeatability

In Bland-Altman analysis between scan and rescan, a constant bias was not observed for any of the four algorithms (Table 2, Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figure 4A). A proportional bias was present for MD-GRU and nnU-Net, but was driven by a single patient with the highest WMH volume. The bias disappeared after removing this measurement (data not shown).

Inter-scanner reproducibility

Reproducibility of WMH volumes was compared pairwise between the four scanners (Philips Achieva, Siemens Trio, Siemens Prisma, GE 750 W), using Bland-Altman analysis (Table 3, Fig. 2B, Supplementary Figure 6A). WMH volumes obtained from the GE scanner data were generally smaller than those for the other three scanners (Fig. 2B, rows 1 to 3; please note the larger y-scale for the first three rows). This volume difference increased with lesion burden, resulting in a proportional bias that complicates comparisons across algorithms. In contrast, comparisons of WMH volumes between the other three scanners showed no or only a small constant and proportional bias in most cases. Notably, when comparing across algorithms, the proportional bias increased in the following order: MD-GRU, nnU-Net, SHIVA, and UCD. For the comparison between Siemens Prisma and Philips, there was no significant bias for any algorithm, but precision tended to be higher (i.e., LOA smaller) for nnU-Net and MD-GRU. For the comparison between Siemens Trio and Philips or Siemens Prisma, there was a significant proportional bias only for SHIVA and a mild constant bias for MD-GRU (only in the comparison Siemens Trio versus Philips) and nnU-Net (only in the comparison Siemens Trio versus Siemens Prisma). Again, the precision tended to be higher for nnU-Net and MD-GRU.

Bias and precision in 2D data against a manual reference

In the independent and unseen Swiss-AF dataset with 2D FLAIR data, both MD-GRU and nnU-Net demonstrated consistent performance, exhibiting no failures. The SHIVA algorithm failed for 11 patients and the UCD algorithm for 10 patients (3 failed completely, while 7 had obvious, large segmentation errors). Three of the patients excluded for SHIVA and UCD were the same, resulting in overall 18 patients that had to be removed and leaving 37 patients for the calculation of bias and precision. Bland-Altman analysis (Table 4, Fig. 3) showed that constant bias was absent for MD-GRU, while nnU-Net and SHIVA showed a mild bias. For UCD, bias was dominated by a strong proportional bias, while proportional bias was mild in MD-GRU and nnU-Net and absent in SHIVA. Precision was similar across MD-GRU, nnU-Net and SHIVA, but UCD showed a larger LOA, although this was probably mainly driven by the proportional bias.

Clinical validation through power analysis

The percentage WMH volume change (PWMHVC) over the 2 year follow-up period is plotted for the 126 DiViNAS patients in Fig. 4A. The four segmentation algorithms yielded a positive volume change in 111 (88 %; MD-GRU), 116 (92 %; nnU-Net), 103 (82 %; SHIVA) and 105 (83 %; UCD) patients.

Percentage WMH volume change and power analysis. (A) Percentage WMH volume change (PWMHVC) for the four segmentation algorithms and statistical power for the detection of a 30 % treatment effect with (B) a non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test or (C) a parametric t-test using Yeo-Johnson-transformed PWMHVC values, both calculated over a range of sample sizes.

We calculated the power of a Wilcoxon rank-sum test to detect a 30 % treatment effect for a range of sample sizes using simulations (Fig. 4B). The highest power was found for nnU-Net, followed by MD-GRU, UCD and SHIVA. The interpolated sample size required per treatment arm to reach 80 % power was 198 for MD-GRU, 161 for nnU-Net, 274 for UCD and 311 for SHIVA. Hence, compared to the best performing algorithm nnU-Net, the required sample size was 1.2-fold higher for MD-GRU, 1.7-fold higher for UCD, and almost double for SHIVA. Additionally, we calculated the power for a t-test based on the PWMHVC data transformed towards normality using Yeo-Johnson transformation, resulting in the same ranking of the segmentation algorithms (Fig. 4C). The sample size required per treatment arm to reach 80 % power was 200 for MD-GRU, 150 for nnU-Net, 281 for UCD and 360 for SHIVA.

Discussion

By following a comprehensive strategy in algorithm development and validation, our new segmentation algorithms are, to our knowledge, the first to fulfil all major requirements for an optimal, automated WMH segmentation method. The key elements of our strategy included: 1) We trained on a dataset acquired with the most common scanner models and with a wide range of acquisition parameters. This ensures that the model generalizes well to new datasets, as demonstrated by our validation using the MarkVCID, SWISS-AF and DiViNAS datasets. 2) We used two network types for training, which are well established in the field of medical image segmentation, namely MD-GRU and U-Net, the latter as implemented in the nnU-Net framework. 3) We developed a complete processing pipeline that includes pre- and post-processing steps, addressing image resolution, alignment and registration. 4) Systematic validation and benchmarking against state-of-the-art algorithms were performed in relevant patient populations, including different SVD types and multiple studies. 5) Validation was focused on the proposed biomarker, i.e., the volume calculated from the segmentation masks. 6) The containerization of pipelines for portability across computing platforms facilitates the application of the novel methods and promotes reproducible science.

Our newly trained models outperformed carefully selected, state-of-the-art benchmarking algorithms in technical and clinical validation. Of note, all validation experiments were conducted in datasets that were not included in the training dataset. The validation results thus indicate excellent generalizability of our models to new, unseen datasets. In the scan-rescan repeatability experiment, MD-GRU and nnU-Net demonstrated comparable precision, with both new methods clearly outperforming the benchmarking algorithms. In the inter-scanner reproducibility experiment, the agreement in terms of bias and precision tended to be higher for MD-GRU and nnU-Net than for the other algorithms, making them the best candidates for multi-center studies. In clinical validation, performance was best for nnU-Net, followed by MD-GRU, UCD and SHIVA. The relatively poor performance of the modern SHIVA algorithm in the sample size estimation was unexpected. The underlying causes of differences in segmentation performance between algorithms remain speculative. However, considering that SHIVA also utilizes a state-of-the-art algorithm, observed differences may be attributable to the ground truth dataset. While SHIVA was trained on a wide range of WMH severity, its performance was optimized for early stage WMH, i.e., small, punctate lesions [24]. This may lead to lower specificity, resulting in a higher number of false positives and a tendency toward over-segmentation. Therefore, SHIVA may not be suitable for more extensive WMH, such as those observed in CADASIL patients in our clinical validation sample.

The PGS algorithm was the winner of the MICCAI WMH segmentation challenge [23] and was thus selected as one of the benchmarking algorithms. However, in our first validation step, it exhibited large inconsistencies across image slices, primarily attributed to extensive areas of false negatives. This can be explained both by the ground truth data used to train PGS, consisting of 2D FLAIR images with slice distance of 3 mm, and by the fact that a 2D U-Net was used, predicting each 2D slice separately [22]. We concluded that the pre-trained PGS model is unsuitable for high-resolution 3D data and thus excluded it from the subsequent validation steps. In contrast to PGS, our segmentation methods use 3D network architectures and were trained on 3D data with high resolution. Still, we also validated their performance in 2D data with thicker slices, observing robust performance for MD-GRU and nnU-Net, while SHIVA and UCD failed in a significant number of patients. In the remaining patients, MD-GRU, nnU-Net and SHIVA showed similar and good agreement with the manually created ground truth. In summary, because of the failed processing in many cases when using SHIVA, this validation experiment also favours MD-GRU and nnU-Net for the use in 2D FLAIR data.

The UCD algorithm was included as a benchmark due to its status as the most rigorously validated algorithm available to date [19]. Furthermore, its inclusion enabled a comparative analysis with a state-of-the-art algorithm that is not based on deep learning. Notably, with the exception of the PGS algorithm, all deep learning-based models demonstrated enhanced performance relative to the conventional UCD segmentation tool.

While we have made both of our new methods publicly available, we recommend using the nnU-Net-based algorithm in most cases, as it performed slightly better than the MD-GRU-based algorithm in most validation experiments. The similar segmentation performance of MD-GRU and nnU-Net is not unexpected, given that both methods use a state-of-the-art deep learning algorithm and were trained on the same, high-quality ground truth data. However, as seen in our validation experiment on the SWISS-AF dataset, MD-GRU might have a slight advantage for segmenting 2D FLAIR images.

To enhance algorithm setup and promote reproducibility in scientific research, we offer both algorithms as readily accessible, prebuilt, and versioned container images through a public container registry (https://github.com/miac-research/MARS-WMH). The implementation of deep learning algorithms can pose significant challenges, often stemming from the requirements for specific software dependencies that may be conflicting with the local software environment. Containers effectively mitigate these challenges by standardizing and isolating the environment. This approach ensures consistent and reproducible results across diverse computing platforms and is largely independent from the local software environment [32]. While running the algorithms on a graphics processing unit is preferable (1 to 3 min calculation time depending on available hardware and image properties), running on a central processing unit is also supported as a fallback (30 to 90 min calculation time). Due to the excellent generalizability of the new algorithms observed in the validation datasets, we are confident that they can be applied out-of-the-box in a wide range of datasets, without the need for re-training or other modifications.

A particular strength of our study is the independence of most validation steps from a manual ground truth. Although manual segmentation of WMH is often considered the gold standard, it is highly reliant on the image reader. Many validation studies suffer from annotation bias, by testing algorithms using ground truth data created by the same readers that created the ground truth data for training. In our study, repeatability, reproducibility and clinical validation were performed entirely without a ground truth. Although we used ground truth masks for the validation of bias and precision in the 3D dataset, this ground truth was created in an unbiased manner by combining the output of all five algorithms. Only for validation of bias and precision in 2D data, we used completely manually created ground truth masks. Importantly, however, there was no overlap between the readers who generated the ground truth masks used for validation and those who generated the ground truth masks used for training.

Another strength of our study is that we focused our validation on the WMH volume, which is the proposed imaging biomarker. We also included square-root transformed volumes, as transformations are commonly employed to address the skewed distribution of WMH volumes in patient populations. Many studies on segmentation algorithms focus on the segmentation mask itself, which is compared to a ground truth mask in terms of overlap and distance metrics. While this is a valid approach, it remains insufficient for validating the actual biomarker, i.e., the WMH volume.

A potential limitation of our study pertains to the MarkVCID dataset, in which image data acquired from the GE scanner exhibited a resolution of 0.5 mm along one axis. This contrasts with the isotropic resolution of 1 mm of image data acquired on other scanners. We are unable to ascertain whether the observed bias for GE data can be attributed to this discrepancy in imaging resolution. The reliance on a single data set for clinical validation is another potential limitation, particularly given our focus on patients with the rare, genetically defined small vessel disease CADASIL. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to patients with sporadic forms of small vessel disease. However, by using CADASIL patients, who typically show accelerated WMH progression over time [33], we were able to establish a testable requirement for clinical validation, i.e., the requirement that WMH progression should be detectable in as many patients as possible over a 2-year follow-up period.

Conclusion

Our publicly available, containerized tools provide robust and reliable WMH quantification, facilitating the use of WMH volume as a SVD biomarker in clinical research, irrespective of dataset or imaging protocol.
