Third-generation lentiviral gene therapy rescues function in a mouse model of Usher 1B
Authors: Juliane W Schott, Peixin Huang, Michael Morgan, Jennifer Nelson-Brantley, Ally Koehler, Bryan Renslo, Hildegard Büning, Athanasia Warnecke, Axel Schambach, Hinrich Staecker
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsher syndrome 1B (USH1B) is a devastating genetic disorder with congenital deafness, loss of balance, and blindness caused by mutations in the myosin-VIIa (MYO7A) gene, for which there is currently no cure. We developed a gene therapy approach addressing the vestibulo-cochlear deficits of USH1B using a third-generation, high-capacity lentiviral vector system capable of delivering the large 6,645-bp MYO7A cDNA. Lentivirally delivered MYO7A and co-encoded dTomato were successfully expressed in the cochlear cell line HEI-OC1. In normal-hearing mice, both cochlea and the vestibular organ were efficiently transduced, and ectopic MYO7A overexpression did not show any adverse effects. In Shaker-1 mice, an USH1B disease model based on Myo7a mutation, cochlear and vestibular hair cells, the main inner ear cell types affected in USH1B, were successfully transduced. In homozygous mutant mice, delivery of MYO7A at postnatal day 16 resulted in a trend for partial recovery of auditory function and in strongly reduced balance deficits. Heterozygous mutant mice were found to develop severe hearing loss at 6 months of age without balance deficits, and lentiviral MYO7A gene therapy completely rescued hearing to wild-type hearing thresholds. In summary, this study demonstrates improved hearing and balance function through lentiviral gene therapy in the inner ear.Keywords: Usher disease, Usher1B, MYO7A, unconventional myosin, hearing loss, balance loss, lentiviral vector, gene therapy

Introduction

Hearing loss constitutes the most common human sensory disorder and one of the most common birth defects, with severe consequences for affected patients and society. More than 50% of hearing loss cases are caused by genetic reasons. Pathogenic variants causing non-syndromic hearing loss have been identified to cover more than 100 genes. Additionally, more than 400 syndromes have been genetically characterized that involve hearing loss. Among these, Usher disease represents one of the most devastating syndromic forms of hearing loss. Usher syndrome is classified into three major types, which are characterized by the causative genes, time points of symptom onset, as well as the presence or absence of balance dysfunction.

Usher type 1 is generally the most severe form of the disease, characterized by congenital deafness, loss of vestibular function, and loss of vision due to adolescent-onset retinitis pigmentosa, with an incidence of 1 in 25,000. Among Usher type I patients, 53%–63% of individuals are classified as subtype B (Usher syndrome 1B [USH1B]), which is caused by a variety of mutations in the myosin-VIIA (MYO7A) gene. The 6,645-bp coding sequence of MYO7A produces a 2,215-amino acid unconventional myosin motor protein that plays a key role in mechanosensory transduction in the inner ear, keeping the tip links connecting the stereocilia on top of the hair cells under tension at rest.1,2 Mouse mutants demonstrated that Myo7a plays a role both in the development and in the function of mature hearing. Depending on their specific location, MYO7A mutations can, therefore, cause a spectrum of developmental and progressive disorders of the stereocilia on cochlear and/or vestibular hair cells, ranging from USH1B (recessive inheritance)3,4,5,6 and atypical Usher syndrome to recessive and dominant forms of hearing loss only (DFNB2 and DFNA11).3,7 The Shaker-1 Sh1/LeJ mouse, which carries a point mutation in the Myo7a gene causing a R502P amino acid substitution and, with that, a highly diminished motor activity of the protein, is an established model to study the otologic manifestations of USH1B.8,9 While homozygous mice initially develop hearing, thresholds are lost closely after hearing onset, i.e., between approximately 18 and 21 days of age.10,11,12 In contrast with USH1B patients, vision is not affected in the mice.

Treatment of USH1B is currently limited to cochlear implantation, which only achieves partial hearing restoration. Although this allows the development of speech, hearing resolution does not reach the native level of a normal-hearing individual, and requires the presence of intact spiral ganglion neurons. Moreover, the combination of vestibular hypofunction and visual impairment leads to severe imbalance, which drastically affects patients’ daily life. The balance deficits in patients suffering from USH1B or from other vestibulocochlear syndromes are currently not treatable.

Because of the genetic nature of many inner ear disorders, including Usher disease, gene therapy is a promising novel treatment approach. Currently, adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors are the most widely explored delivery system for gene therapy in the inner ear.13,14 However, MYO7A is too large to be delivered using standard AAV technology. Although dual AAV vector systems, in which the transferred gene is split into two separate vectors that recombine in the target cell,15 can allow the use of AAV vectors to target disorders with larger genes, this strategy may be associated with a loss in efficiency.16,17 In contrast, lentiviral (LV) vectors have a high coding capacity beyond 10 kb, allowing the delivery of the entire coding sequence of large genes, plus a range of regulatory sequences, in a single vector.

Earlier approaches using LV vectors were unable to show efficient transduction of otic cells.18,19,20,21 However, here we show that a state-of-the-art third-generation LV vector system is capable of efficient in vivo transduction of the murine inner ear. In contrast with previous studies, reporter gene expression was achieved in both inner and outer hair cells, the main inner ear cell types affected in USH1B. Therefore, we used LV gene therapy to treat an inner ear disease, demonstrating the efficacy of this vector system to deliver the cDNA of native MYO7A and to rescue vestibulocochlear function in the Shaker-1 mouse model of USH1B.

Results

LV vectors are able to package, transfer, and express native MYO7A

To establish a gene therapy option for USH1B, a state-of-the art and high-capacity third-generation LV vector was equipped with the large 6,645-bp cDNA sequence of the native human MYO7A canonical isoform. The vector harbored a self-inactivating (SIN) architecture devoid of the enhancer and promoter elements naturally present in the long-terminal repeats of the vector (Figure S1A). This design confers an improved safety profile by decreasing the risk of insertional mutagenesis (Figure S1B).22,23,24,25 The SIN design has emerged as the gold standard in retroviral vector-based gene therapy and has been demonstrated safe in diverse clinical applications to treat hematological diseases in humans.26 It furthermore allows the usage of an internal promoter of choice to fine-tune transgene expression. Here, the spleen focus-forming virus (SFFV) promoter as ubiquitously active and reportedly strong promoter was chosen to mediate high-level and sustained expression of the transgene cassette. To facilitate titration of viral vector particle preparations and identification of successfully transduced cells upon in vitro and in vivo administration, the MYO7A cDNA was linked to a dTomato reporter gene via an internal ribosomal entry site (IRES) to create the LV vector LV-MYO7A (Figure 1A). A smaller counterpart expressing dTomato only served as a reference and control (LV-ctrl).

Transient production using a split-packaging system successfully generated LV particles despite the challenging size of the MYO7A cDNA (Figure 1B). Although titers were 1–2 log lower for LV-MYO7A as compared with the much smaller LV-ctrl vector, with an average of 107 TU/mL, they were in a range that is sufficient for in vitro and in vivo applications.

To evaluate vector functionality and the capacity to transduce inner ear cells, LV-MYO7A was applied in vitro to the established hair-cell-like cell line HEI-OC1.27 Upon transduction at different multiplicities of infection (MOIs), i.e., applying defined numbers of viral vector particles per seeded cell, no significant difference in the percentage of successfully transduced, dTomato-positive cells was observed by flow cytometry analysis between LV-MYO7A and LV-ctrl across all MOIs tested (Figure 1C). This confirmed that the transduction efficiency of the LV vector encoding the large MYO7A cDNA was comparable with smaller vectors.

Staining using an anti-MYO7A antibody followed by immunofluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry revealed no endogenous MYO7A expression in the non-transduced HEI-OC1 cells and no signal for dTomato (Figures 1D and 1E). The shift of the cell populations in the MYO7A channel of flow cytometry plots seen upon antibody addition could not be confirmed as true MYO7A protein expression, but rather represented a staining artifact. In cells transduced with LV-ctrl, the MYO7A signal was unaltered as compared with the non-transduced cells, but a high signal for dTomato was present. In contrast, in HEI-OC1 cells transduced with LV-MYO7A, a clear MYO7A-positive cell population was detected. Furthermore, the same cells that showed a MYO7A signal were positive for dTomato, indicating successful co-expression of MYO7A and the dTomato reporter from the vector. Of note, dTomato expression levels from LV-ctrl were higher than from LV-MYO7A. This is in line with reports on decreased expression of the second gene in bicistronic vectors that use IRES elements,28 as it is the case for LV-MYO7A, but not LV-ctrl, and thus does not indicate any disadvantage or inferior performance of LV-MYO7A. Altogether, despite the challenging size of the MYO7A transgene, fully functional LV vector particles could be produced that successfully transferred MYO7A and expressed the protein in inner-ear-derived, hair-cell-like HEI-OC1 cells.

The Shaker-1 mouse model of USH1B: Homozygotes show early-onset loss of balance and hearing, while heterozygotes show normal balance but late-onset hearing loss

The functional effects of LV-MYO7A gene therapy were assessed using the established Shaker-1 mouse model for USH1B, which carries a Myo7a missense mutation resulting in an R502P amino acid substitution and highly diminished motor activity.9 To define the interval given for therapeutic intervention, to be able to assess the magnitude of therapeutic effects, and to discriminate between the effects possible in homozygous versus heterozygous Shaker-1 mutant mice, the model was deeply characterized with regard to different genotypes and ages. For this, Shaker-1 wild-type (Shaker-1WT, +/+), heterozygous mutant (Shaker-1het, +/−), and homozygous mutant (Shaker-1mut, −/−) mice were subjected to hearing and balance testing at postnatal day 16 (P16), P21, P30, P60, and/or P90, followed by histological analysis (n = 5–10 per condition). A subset of Shaker-1mut animals (n = 5) was followed by balance analysis for 270 days and then allowed to survive to 1 year of age, at which point they were evaluated with histology.

Hearing was determined through auditory brainstem recording (ABR) in response to 4-, 8-, 16- and 32-kHz tone bursts. For Shaker-1WT mice, ABR measurements revealed normal thresholds of less than 45 dB across all frequencies and time points tested (Figure 2A). Interestingly, Shaker-1het mice presented with a hearing loss phenotype. At P30, the Shaker-1het animals still showed normal ABR thresholds (Figure 2B). However, hearing was observed to decline at later time points, starting between P90 and P180, resulting in hearing thresholds averaging 60–70 dB by 6 months of age. In Shaker-1mut mice, in line with previous studies, hearing was already diminished, but still detectable at P16, with the hearing thresholds ranging from 65 dB at the low frequencies to 75 dB at the high frequencies (Figure 2C). Hearing loss progressed to the 80-dB range across all frequencies by P21 and declined to profound hearing loss at all frequencies by P30. Thus, homozygous Shaker-1mut animals showed an early-onset hearing loss, and their Shaker-1het littermates manifested with a late-onset hearing loss.

Balance function was characterized by rotarod analysis and force-plate actimetry. Rotarod analysis determines the time that the mice remain balanced on a rotating rod without falling off, with a maximum test duration of 200 s. Actimetry allows for tracking of the total distance traveled, the area covered during movement and the total degrees of turns as a measure of circling behavior. Shaker-1het mice showed normal rotarod times throughout the 3-month test period, with the mice managing to stay balanced on the rod for the test duration of 200 s (Figure 2D). For Shaker-1mut mice, circling behavior is reported to manifest between P16 and P21. However, the mice demonstrated essentially normal time balancing on the rod at P30 (Figure 2D). Then, rotarod times decreased rapidly and progressively over the next 2 months, showing a significant difference to Shaker-1het mice at P60 and reaching a nadir at P90. A small number of animals (n = 5) were allowed to survive to nine months of age (P270) and did not show any improvement of function through repeated testing, which ruled out improvement through learning.

Force-plate actimetry tests at P30, P60, and P90 confirmed the rotarod results. Shaker-1het mice never showed any changes in the total distance traveled and the area covered during movement, or any circling behavior (Figure 2E). In contrast, Shaker-1mut mice demonstrated impaired balance already at P30. In line with the rotarod results, balance function in Shaker-1mut mice progressively worsened over the next 2 months in terms of the area covered and the total degrees turned, with a maximum at the final time point of analysis at P90 (Figure 2E). Thus, profoundly impaired balance was seen in homozygous Shaker-1mut animals, while heterozygous Shaker-1het mice showed normal balance function.

Gene therapeutic intervention requires the presence of a cellular target (i.e., the target tissue needs to be preserved at administration of the therapy), so that the time point of any potential degeneration of the cell type(s) affected by the disease defines the therapeutic window. Physiologically, MYO7A is very specifically expressed in cochlear and vestibular hair cells only, so that hair cells constitute the primary target for treatment with LV-MYO7A. Histology on cochlear sections from Shaker-1mut mice demonstrated the presence of a normal-appearing organ of Corti, including the presence of hair cells, at P30 (Figure 2F). However, at P90, both the inner and outer hair cells as well as the supporting cells underneath the outer hair cells, while present, stained positive for the apoptosis marker annexin V, indicating the onset of degeneration in the organ of Corti (Figure 2G). At 1 year of age, Shaker-1mut animals showed a complete loss of the cellular architecture of the organ of Corti, including the loss of all hair and supporting cells (Figure 2H). Interestingly, as a secondary effect, the spiral ganglion had also degenerated at this time point. Altogether, histology confirmed the target tissue to be present after birth, making a gene therapy-based treatment concept possible up to at least P30 in the Shaker-1mut mouse, a time point at which the inner ear is already physiologically non-functional.

LV-MYO7A efficiently transduces the in vivo cochlea and does not disturb hearing function in normal-hearing mice

Before assessment of the therapeutic effects of LV-MYO7A in the Shaker-1 model, the potential of the employed third-generation LV vector platform to transduce the inner ear was investigated as, until now, LV vector application to the inner ear has generally been ineffective and limited to few cell populations, which importantly did not include hair cells.19,20,21 For all in vivo applications, LV-ctrl and LV-MYO7A were equipped with the short version (CAGs) of the CAG promoter (a hybrid of the cytomegalovirus enhancer fused to the chicken beta-actin promoter), which is less prone to silencing than the SFFV promoter. To determine transduction of the mouse inner ear, 1 μL of VSV-G pseudotyped LV particles was injected into 1-month-old WT, normal-hearing C57BL/6 mice through canalostomy via the posterior semicircular canal (PSCC).

Three-dimensional reconstruction of the dTomato reporter signal in whole cleared cochleae demonstrated efficient transduction of multiple cell types, including hair cells and spiral ganglion cells, throughout all cochlear turns, as well as transduction of the vestibular ganglion (Figures 3A and S2A–S2C). Cochlear cross-sections confirmed dTomato expression in the spiral ganglion, as well as in inner and outer hair cells in multiple turns of the cochlea upon application of the small LV-ctrl vector (Figures 3B, S2D, and S2E). This showed that the employed vector platform can efficiently transduce the USH1B-relevant target cells, i.e., inner and outer hair cells, in the cochlea. Importantly, injection of 1 μL LV-MYO7A, despite the challenging vector size and the associated lower titer (7.78 × 106 TU/mL), also achieved dTomato expression in both inner and outer hair cells, as well as spiral ganglion cells in multiple cochlear turns (Figures 3C and S3A–S3D). Whole mount organ of Corti preparation furthermore demonstrated a high efficiency of hair cell transduction using LV-MYO7A, with up to 100% of the inner and outer hair cells covered by the whole mount showing dTomato signal (Figures 3D and S4A–S4P). Notably, the structure of the tissue in the cochlea was preserved upon LV vector administration, without any signs of degeneration (Figures 3A–3D and S2–S4).

To determine if any potential negative functional effects resulted from the canalostomy approach used for in vivo vector administration and/or from ectopic MYO7A overexpression, hearing function was assessed in the injected C57BL/6 mice at 1 week after administration of LV-MYO7A. ABR recordings revealed no difference in the hearing thresholds of the same C57BL/6 mice before and after treatment with LV-MYO7A (Figure 3E). Altogether, the dTomato signal indicated activity of the CAGs promoter and expression of the transgene cassette from LV-MYO7A in the cochlea. The characteristics of successful packaging of LV-MYO7A and the high efficiency of LV transduction of cochlear hair cells in the absence of adverse effects indicate LV-MYO7A to be a suitable candidate for in vivo gene therapy of USH1B.

Vestibular hair cells are a target for LV-MYO7A gene therapy

Besides hearing loss, inner ear-related deficits in USH1B patients and homozygous Shaker-1 mice include severe imbalance. Therefore, the employed third-generation LV vector platform was also characterized for its potential to transduce the vestibular organ of WT C57BL/6 mice. Injection of 1 μL LV-MYO7A into 1-month-old mice resulted in successful transduction of the vestibular neuroepithelium, including the vestibular ganglion (Figures 3A, S2A, and S2B), the utricle, and the saccule (Figures 4A and 4B), as indicated by dTomato expression. Quantification of dTomato-positive cells in the utricle revealed a high transduction efficiency, ranging from 89% to 95% among the hair cells, which were identified through phalloidin-assisted labeling (Figure 4C). As before for the cochlea, the structure of the vestibular tissue was normal and without any signs of damage or degeneration (Figure 4A).

Any potential negative effects of the canalostomy and/or of MYO7A overexpression in the vestibular organ were investigated through functional testing of the balance behavior by rotarod analysis. Rotarod times recorded for the mice did not change due to LV-MYO7A treatment, with similar balance function of the mice before and after LV-MYO7A treatment (Figure 4D).

In the Shaker-1 mouse model, injection of 1 μL LV-MYO7A at P16 also did not cause any degeneration of vestibular hair cells, with similar or even higher type I and type II hair cell counts in the treated versus non-treated mice as determined at 3 and 4 months of age (Figure 4E). Importantly, the numbers of vestibular hair cells in untreated homozygous Shaker-1mut animals as counted in serially sectioned utricles at 3, 4, and 12 months of age remained stable, and type II hair cell numbers were not different from the utricles of heterozygous Shaker-1het littermates. This implicates the presence of a cellular target for LV-MYO7A gene therapy, even at time points far beyond the onset of functional impairment, and, together with efficient LV vector transduction, indicates vestibular hair cells as a promising target for LV-MYO7A gene therapy.

LV-MYO7A gene therapy at P16 partially improves hearing and rescues balance function in homozygous Shaker-1mut mice

Having confirmed the functionality of LV-MYO7A in vitro as well as the effectiveness of the employed LV vector platform in transduction of the in vivo inner ear, we performed an in vivo gene therapy approach to explore the therapeutic potential of LV-MYO7A for treatment of the vestibulo-cochlear deficits in homozygous mutant Shaker-1mut mice. VSV-G pseudotyped LV-MYO7A particles were administered by injection into the PSCC (canalostomy). Injections were performed at P16, i.e., after physiological hearing onset, but early enough to ensure that the targeted hair cell population was still present at intervention.

Fluorescence microscopy on cochlear sections from Shaker-1mut mice at P90 showed successful transduction of inner and outer cochlear as well as of vestibular hair cells with LV-MYO7A, as indicated by the signal from the vector-encoded dTomato reporter protein (Figures 5A–5D). The dTomato signal further confirmed that the transgene cassette was still active and expressed in vivo at 10 weeks after vector-delivery. While the vestibular tissue was without any signs of degeneration, the outer hair cells in the section from the basal turn of the cochlea already seemed to be damaged at this time point, despite LV-MYO7A treatment (Figure 5A), and annexin V staining indicated ongoing apoptosis throughout the supporting cell layer at 6 months of age (Figure 5D). Counts of inner and outer cochlear hair cells showed LV-MYO7A treatment to decelerate hair cell degeneration, with close to 100% of expected hair cell numbers at 3 months of age and higher hair cell counts in the treated as compared with the non-treated contralateral ear at 6 months (Table S1). Comparing hair cell numbers at 3 and 6 months of age, however, treated cochleae also showed a progressive decline in hair cell survival, even though at slower kinetics as compared with the untreated ear, suggesting that supplementation of native MYO7A does not fully rescue auditory hair cells in the long term.

To investigate the functional effects of LV-MYO7A treatment, hearing was determined at P90 through ABR measurement and compared with age-matched untreated Shaker-1mut mice (Figure 5E). None of the latter had measurable ABR thresholds. In contrast, hearing thresholds showed a trend for recovery of auditory function in the LV-MYO7A-treated cohort at all four tested frequencies. With a 91.6% success rate, 11 of the 12 treated animals showed some improvement in ABR thresholds and, thus, a functional effect of the LV-MYO7A gene therapy. The maximum effect was observed at a frequency of 8 kHz, with hearing thresholds as low as 40 dB in the best-responding mouse and a statistically significant improvement across the cohort. The mean improvement in hearing thresholds in the treated group was 15 dB at the high frequencies (16 and 32 kHz), 20 dB at the lowest frequency of 4 kHz, and 25 dB at 8 kHz. Despite this improvement, LV-MYO7A-treated mice did not reach the normal hearing thresholds seen in control Shaker-1WT mice.

A profound therapeutic effect was achieved in terms of balance function measured at P60 (rotarod) and P90 (rotarod, force-plate actimetry). Rotarod testing demonstrated a halt in balance loss in LV-MYO7A-treated Shaker-1mut animals upon treatment, as dysfunction did not progress to the degree seen in untreated Shaker-1mut mice (Figure 5F). Rather, rotarod times in LV-MYO7A-treated animals were preserved at an intermediate level between P90 Shaker-1het mice, which have normal balance, and untreated P90 Shaker-1mut mice. Differences between treated and untreated Shaker-1mut animals were statistically significant at P60 and at P90. Also, there was no statistical difference between the P60 and P90 time points within the group of LV-MYO7A-treated Shaker-1mut animals, indicative of a long-term effect of the treatment without any worsening of the phenotype since therapy.

Actimeter tracking demonstrated more directed movement despite occasional circling for LV-MYO7A-treated Shaker-1mut mice, while untreated Shaker-1mut mice showed uncontrolled circling (Figures 5G and 6). Quantification of actimeter performance at P90 revealed no significant differences in the total distance traveled between control Shaker-1het, untreated Shaker-1mut, and LV-MYO7A-treated Shaker-1mut littermates, indicating that the mice in all groups were active (Figure 5G). Importantly, however, there was a statistically significant decrease in the total area covered in LV-MYO7A-treated as compared with untreated Shaker-1mut mice, and the performance of the treated mice was statistically similar to control Shaker-1het littermates. Moreover, evaluation of the total degrees circled showed a decrease in circling behavior in LV-MYO7A-treated as compared with age-matched untreated Shaker-1mut mice (Figures 5G and 6). Interestingly, the degree of both left and right turns was decreased, although only the left ear had been treated with LV-MYO7A. Importantly, all mice (8 of 8 animals) in the LV-MYO7A-treated cohort showed this strong improvement in balance function, entailing a 100% success rate. Altogether, LV-MYO7A gene therapy at P16 only temporarily improved hair cell survival and partially improved hearing function, but halted the progression of balance loss in homozygous Shaker-1mut mice.

LV-MYO7A gene therapy at P4 improves balance, but not hearing, in homozygous Shaker-1mut mice

LV-MYO7A gene therapy at P16 showed a therapeutic effect in homozygous mutant Shaker-1mut mice. However, the rescue was incomplete with hearing and balance not reaching WT levels. Therefore, we tested whether the delivery of MYO7A prior to maturation of hearing would improve rescue of function. For this, Shaker-1mut mice were treated at P4 with VSV-G pseudotyped LV-MYO7A through canalostomy using the same vector batch and dose (1 μL) as before. Functional effects were analyzed at P30 (hearing) and P90 (balance). ABR analysis to compare treated with untreated Shaker-1mut littermates showed improvements of up to 20 dB at individual sound frequencies in 37% (3/8) of the treated mice (Figure 7A). However, when mean hearing thresholds were averaged across the entire cohort of treated mice, no statistically significant improvement of hearing was found. Rotarod testing showed better performance of LV-MYO7A-treated as compared with untreated Shaker-1mut animals, but the difference was not statistically significant (Figure 7B). However, similar to animals treated at P16, Shaker-1mut mice treated at P4 showed a statistically significant decrease in circling behavior, as indicated by a lower number of total degrees turned right or left on the actimeter plate (Figure 7C). Of note, the treatment achieved a 100% success rate (4/4 treated animals) in terms of balance function in Shaker-1mut mice. Altogether, treatment of homozygous Shaker-1mut mice with LV-MYO7A at P4 did not increase the magnitude of therapeutic effects as compared with the gene therapy performed at P16.

LV-MYO7A gene therapy at P4 prevents hearing loss in heterozygous Shaker-1het mice

As characterized above, heterozygous mutant Shaker-1het mice, which harbor a single defective Myo7a allele, develop significant hearing loss by 6 months of age. To determine whether LV-MYO7A gene therapy can halt or even rescue this late-onset hearing loss phenotype, Shaker-1het mice were injected with 1 μL of VSV-G pseudotyped LV-MYO7A through canalostomy at P4. As Shaker-1het mice have normal balance function, the analysis of therapeutic effects of the gene transfer was restricted to the effects on hearing.

LV-MYO7A treatment at P4 prevented onset and progression of hearing loss at all frequencies. At 6 months of age (P180), all (8/8) LV-MYO7A-treated Shaker-1het mice had maintained normal ABR thresholds and waveforms with no difference to Shaker-1WT mice (Figures 8A and S2), showing that the gene therapeutic approach could completely rescue the phenotype in these animals at a 100% success rate.

In addition to ABR analysis, distortion product otoacoustic emission (DPOAE) thresholds were determined to characterize outer hair cell function. Shaker-1WT mice and Shaker-1het mice demonstrated normal outer hair cell function at P30 (Figure 8B). In contrast, DPOAE thresholds in untreated Shaker-1het mice were decreased at 6 months of age, in line with the late-onset hearing loss phenotype. LV-MYO7A-treated mice showed a decrease in DPOAE that was similar to untreated Shaker-1het mice at 6 months of age. However, Shaker-1WT control mice also presented with decreased DPOAE thresholds at this age. Importantly, these were not significantly different from the LV-MYO7A-treated Shaker-1het mice. Also, despite the decline, DPOAE were still present in all groups tested, and there was mainly a decrease in DPOAE amplitude in the middle frequencies when comparing P30 with P180. This general and probably age-related decrease in DPOAE performance that seems to be associated with the Shaker-1 mouse strain precludes evaluation of long-term functional outer hair cell rescue through LV-MYO7A. Altogether, LV-MYO7A completely prevented hearing loss in Shaker-1het mice as indicated by normal ABR thresholds.

Discussion

Hearing loss is developing as a major target for gene therapy, as thousands of pathogenic variants that cause congenital or progressive hearing loss have been identified across more than 100 different genes.29,30,31,32 Initial preclinical programs have focused on recessive disorders that present postnatally or where there is certainty that a cellular target for gene therapy is present, such as DFNB9.16,29,33,34 Viral vector technology offers the delivery of an intact copy of the defective gene to diseased cells, with AAV vectors as the predominant system currently used in the context of the inner ear. However, many of the potential recessive targets in the inner ear would require a split AAV vector system, including the MYO7A gene mutated in USH1B. USH1B represents one of the most devastating syndromic deafness conditions, causing loss of hearing, loss of vision, and loss of balance function. Therefore, in the current study, we used state-of-the-art LV vector technology, which has the natural coding capacity to package the entire MYO7A sequence on a single vector, for delivery to the inner ear.

LV vector technology has already been successfully applied clinically for the treatment of genetic disorders in diverse organ systems, and maturation of vector architecture through several generations has drastically improved safety.26 Thus, despite reports of insertional mutagenesis using early vector generations, no serious adverse events have so far been observed in clinical trials employing the third-generation SIN LV vector system used in our study. Until now, LV vectors have, however, not been effective in the transduction of inner ear hair cells or spiral ganglion neurons.19,20,21 In contrast with previous reports, we demonstrated successful transduction of both the cochlea and the vestibular organ. Importantly, transduced cell types included hair cells and spiral ganglion cells, which represent the target cells in many genetically based inner ear disorders. Hair cells in multiple cochlear turns were successfully modified with a single injection of 1 μL of vector preparation, and in the vestibular organ transduction achieved an average efficiency of 90% of the hair cell population even with the large, low-titer LV-MYO7A vector. Differences from previous reports that may have contributed to the good performance of our vector platform in the inner ear include, but are not limited to, the use of a third-generation vector system, the application via canalostomy instead of cochleostomy or round window membrane injection, and the formulation of viral vector particles in HEPES-supplemented PBS upon concentration. However, side-by-side comparison of different protocols and vector generations would be necessary to assess the key difference(s) that determine successful inner ear transduction by LV vectors.

Significant challenges in developing gene therapy specifically for USH1B include the congenital hearing loss onset and the size of the transgene. We demonstrated the suitability of SIN-LV vectors for efficient packaging, delivery, and expression of the native MYO7A cDNA, despite its size. Although vector titers were lower as compared with the much smaller control vector counterpart, the transduction efficiency of the large vector was similar to that of the smaller control vector, and, in contrast with most other organ systems, the number of cells resident to the inner ear is very low. Therefore, therapeutic intervention in the inner ear will require several logs fewer absolute vector particle numbers than needed for modification of hematopoietic stem cells. Nevertheless, as the volume that can be injected into the inner ear is limited, improvement of vector titers may be helpful as a future step.

The functional effects of the LV-MYO7A gene therapy strategy were validated in the Shaker-1 mouse model, which was deeply characterized in terms of hearing and balance loss progression for the different genotypes. The Shaker-1 model is based on a functionally inactive Myo7a point mutant. This reflects the situation found in many patients undergoing gene therapy, who mostly do not show a complete knock-out of the affected gene product, but who rather express an incomplete or non-functional protein that may interact with or even interfere with the activity of the WT protein expressed from the therapeutic vector. The Shaker-1 mouse represents an attractive model for testing gene therapy, because cochlear hair cells—and thus a cellular target for the therapy—and some degree of functional hearing are still present at maturation of hearing. Hearing in the homozygous mutant mice is then rapidly lost over the next 1–2 weeks, resulting in profound hearing loss, but the hair cells persist until at least P30. It remains to be determined if the functional effects of the LV-MYO7A gene therapy will also apply to human patients suffering from congenital hearing loss. Temporal bones from young patients are unavailable to determine if cellular loss lags behind hearing loss as it does in Shaker-1 mice. However, although hearing loss has a congenital onset, studies of USH1B patients demonstrate severe to profound loss of residual hearing even into adulthood.35 This suggests that there may be an adequate neuro-epithelial reservoir to effect a gene therapy-based rescue in humans, at least in the apical third of the cochlea.

In terms of therapeutic effects of LV-MYO7A gene therapy in the homozygous Shaker-1mut mice, hearing in the treated animals was partially improved at P90 as compared with non-treated littermates, and there did appear to be an at least temporary arrest in the progression of hearing loss even when the vector was delivered at postnatal day 16, i.e., after hearing maturation (P14). However, hearing was not rescued to WT levels, and hair cell degeneration was not arrested in the long term, as indicated by the presence of annexin V-positive cells in the organ of Corti of the P90 cochlea and by a general decrease in cochlear hair cell numbers over time until P180. Thus, in homozygous Shaker-1mut mice, LV-MYO7A gene therapy appears to slow, but not completely rescue, the degeneration of cochlear hair cells and the associated hearing loss. Several studies on mouse models of other types of Usher syndrome have suggested that rescue of the genetic defect is most effective prior to loss of function, or that a functional copy of the defective gene has to be delivered before maturation of hearing.36,37 While in other settings one strategy to improve hearing outcomes was to deliver the correct cDNA prior to the onset of hearing,36 in our case, delivery of LV-MYO7A at an early time point (P4) failed to correct the hearing loss in some of the homozygous mutant animals, although hearing improvement was observed in the majority of mice that were treated at P16. One possible reason for this is differences in the efficiency of inner ear transduction between P4 and P16 canalostomy, which will have to be experimentally addressed in the future. As MYO7A is known to play different roles within development and maturation of hearing, it may also be that much earlier delivery of the native gene is needed.12 An alternate explanation is that, in the current approach, we lack the correct regulatory regions for the MYO7A transgene. Prior studies using a mouse model of Usher type III required the regulatory elements of clarin 1 for effective rescue.37 Also, different MYO7A isoforms are expressed in the inner ear. While the canonical isoform was shown to be primarily expressed in the inner hair cells, a shorter version generated through alternative splicing appears to be predominantly expressed in the outer hair cell layer.2 Therefore, it could be of interest to test the effects of delivering alternative MYO7A isoforms alone or in combination with the canonical variant in the context of our in vivo gene therapy setting. Furthermore, it could be envisioned to establish LV vector modifications that achieve hair cell-specific transduction or transgene expression, so that MYO7A is only expressed in the physiologically relevant cell types of the inner ear, even though its ectopic overexpression did not show any adverse effects in normal-hearing mice in our study.

Beyond cochlear effects, injection of LV vector particles into the inner ear may yield improvement of vestibular deficits that co-present with hearing loss in several genetic disorders of the inner ear. While hearing loss is currently partially treatable through cochlear implant technology, balance loss remains unaddressed and, thus, constitutes an urgent clinical target. In the Shaker-1 mouse model, vestibular hair cell counts were found to remain stable in homozygous mutant mice in between 3 and 12 months of age, despite the functional impairment (balance deficit, circling behavior) observed as early as at P16. Thus, vestibular hair cells seem to be a promising target for LV-MYO7A gene therapy to achieve functional correction in USH1B. In line with this, high transduction levels in vestibular hair cells were achieved upon LV-MYO7A application, and delivery of LV-MYO7A in both the early postnatal period (P4) and after maturation of hearing (P16) led to correction of the circling behavior in homozygous Shaker-1mut mice. These results are remarkable, considering that only one ear per animal was treated with LV-MYO7A. Since vestibular dysfunction in human USH1B does not show a congenital onset, but rather occurs at a later time point, the balance deficit may be a potential target for translational therapy. Here, LV-MYO7A would be an urgently needed treatment option for the devastating and so-far unaddressed impaired balance in USH1B patients.

Interestingly, the presence of only one defective allele of the Myo7a gene in heterozygous Shaker-1het mice resulted in a delayed loss of hearing without manifestation of vestibular deficits. Importantly, delivery of LV-MYO7A at P4 completely prevented hearing loss in these heterozygous mice, which had normal ABR thresholds, similar to WT control mice, at 6 months of age, demonstrating the therapeutic efficacy of our approach. The approach might, thus, be of high interest for clinical translation. As the incidence of heterozygous MYO7A mutations in the human population remains to be clearly defined, it will be interesting to determine this number. In line with this, a recent study demonstrated the presence of mutant MYO7A alleles in presbycusis patients.38

Altogether, our results indicate the potential for developing a translational LV-MYO7A therapeutic to help correct or prevent the balance manifestations of USH1B, and to potentially treat late-onset hearing loss in patients heterozygous for a mutant MYO7A allele. In perspective, LV-MYO7A could further be tested for the treatment of vision loss in USH1B patients, with the prospect to treat all sensory deficits in these patients using a single product. Beyond treatment of USH1B, LV-MYO7A could also be explored to treat other forms of hearing loss caused by MYO7A deficiency, such as DFNB2 and certain forms of presbycusis. Having provided proof of principle for the suitability of third-generation LV vectors for gene therapy of an inner ear disorder in this study, LV vector technology can be easily further adapted for gene therapy strategies in other genetic contexts of hearing and/or balance loss.

Materials and methods

Cloning

The human MYO7A cDNA sequence as deposited in NCBI (GenBank: NM_000260.4) was flanked by a 5′ Kozak consensus sequence and SgrAI/AgeI restriction sites as well as a 3′ SalI restriction site by PCR. The expression plasmid pCMV-Sport6-Myo7a (a kind gift from Manuel Taft, Institute for Biophysical Chemistry, Hannover Medical School, Hannover, Germany) served as the template for the PCR reaction. The MYO7A sequence was cloned into a state-of-the-art, third-generation SIN LV vector harboring an internal SFFV promoter by exchanging the transgene for MYO7A using the SgrAI (for MYO7A)/AgeI (in the vector backbone) restriction sites, which create compatible ends, at the 5′ end and the SalI restriction site at the 3′ end.39 As sequence verification by Sanger sequencing identified point mutations and a small deletion as compared with the reference sequence, this part of the MYO7A coding sequence was exchanged for the correct sequence ordered as a gene synthesis product (Twist Biosciences, South San Francisco, CA) and inserted through restriction enzyme based cloning. A reporter cassette consisting of a dTomato transgene behind an IRES and flanked by a SalI restriction site at either end was inserted behind the MYO7A transgene using the SalI restriction site, generating the final construct pRRL.PPT.SF.hMYO7A.i2.dTomato.pre (LV-MYO7A). A control vector only expressing the dTomato reporter driven by an SFFV promoter was generated by inserting the dTomato sequence flanked by AgeI and SalI into the vector backbone using the unique AgeI and SalI restriction sites, generating pRRL.PPT.SF.dTomato.pre (LV-ctrl). For in vivo application, vector counterparts harboring a short version of the hybrid promoter of cytomegalovirus enhancer fused to the chicken beta-actin promoter and the rabbit beta-globin intron (CAGs) to drive expression of the transgene cassette were cloned by exchanging the SFFV promoter for CAGs using NheI and SgrAI (LV-MYO7A) or XhoI and AgeI (LV-ctrl) restriction sites. Cloning details are available upon request.

Cell culture

Human embryonic kidney HEK293T cells and human fibrosarcoma HT1080 cells were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 in DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Langenselbold, Germany) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (h.i. FBS), 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (all PAN Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany). Murine House Ear Institute-Organ of Corti 1 (HEI-OC1) cells were kindly provided by Dr. Federico Kalinec (UCLA Head and Neck Surgery, Los Angeles, CA) and cultured under permissive conditions at 33°C and 10% CO2 in DMEM supplemented with 10% h.i. FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 100 U/mL penicillin (Sigma-Aldrich Biochemie GmbH, Hamburg, Germany). All cell lines were passaged every 2–3 days using trypsin (PAN Biotech)-assisted detachment.

Viral vector particle production and titration

Details can be found in the supplemental information.

Transduction of HEI-OC1 cells

HEI-OC1 cells were seeded at 3 × 104 per well of a 24-well plate on the day prior to transduction. Three wells were harvested for counting to determine the cell number at the time point of transduction, and the volume of viral vector supernatant was calculated based on the vector’s titer to apply defined MOIs, i.e., defined particle numbers per seeded cell. The transduction procedure followed the same protocol as described under titration. The percentage of cells expressing the vector-encoded dTomato reporter protein was assessed by flow cytometry as described under titration.

Intracellular staining for flow cytometry

Cells were harvested using trypsin-assisted detachment and pelletized by centrifugation for 5 min at 400×g. The pellets were resuspended in 500 μL Fixation Buffer (Cat # 420801, BioLegend, San Diego, CA) and cells incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Samples were pelletized again and washed with 1 mL FACS buffer, followed by three cycles of resuspension in 1× Intracellular Staining Perm Wash Buffer (Cat # 421002, BioLegend) and centrifugation for 5 min at 400×g. Incubation with the primary antibody polyclonal rabbit-anti-MYO7A (Catalog # 25–6790, Proteus BioSciences Inc., Ramona, CA) was performed at 1:300 dilution in 1× Intracellular Staining Perm Wash Buffer for 20 min at room temperature, followed by two washes with 1× Intracellular Staining Perm Wash Buffer. Incubation with the secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 488 AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (Catalog # 711-545-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch Europe Ltd, Ely, UK) was performed at 1:800 dilution in 1× Intracellular Staining Perm Wash Buffer for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. After two washes with 1× Intracellular Staining Perm Wash Buffer, cell pellets were resuspended in FACS buffer, processed on a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer and analyzed using CytExpert software.

Immunofluorescence of cultured cells

To investigate MYO7A expression in cultured cells, vector-transduced or non-transduced HEI-OC1 cells were seeded onto 24-well plastic plates in standard culture medium 1–2 days prior to analysis. At a confluence above 50%, the cells were rinsed in PBS (PAN Biotech) followed by fixation for 15 min in 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Science Services GmbH, Munich, Germany). Cells were permeabilized for 10 min in 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS and then incubated for 30 min in 5% FBS in PBS. Incubation with the primary antibody polyclonal rabbit-anti-MYO7A (Catalog # 25–6790, Proteus BioSciences Inc.) was performed overnight at 4°C and 1:300 dilution in reaction buffer (RB) containing 0.5% BSA (PAN Biotech) and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. The following day, the cells were rinsed once in washing buffer (WB, 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS), followed by two further washes of 10 min each in WB. Incubation with the secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 488 AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (Catalog # 711-545-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch Europe Ltd) was performed at 1:800 dilution in RB for 1h at room temperature in the dark. After one short and one 10-min wash in WB, the cells were kept in PBS until analysis using an Axio Observer microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany).

Animals and ethics

C57BL/6 mice and Shaker-1 Sh1/LeJ mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and maintained in a breeding colony. All animal care and procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Kansas, University Medical Center. Studies on effects of LV-MYO7A in normal hearing mice were carried out in C57BL/6 mice. Evaluation of MYO7A gene therapy was tested in Shaker-1 mice aged P4-P270. Outcomes measures included hearing testing using ABR, balance testing using rotarod and actimeter testing, and histology/immunofluorescence staining.

Genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from mouse pinna by incubating ear punches with 40 μL tissue preparation solution (Sigma, Cat No. T3073) and 40 μL extraction solution (Sigma, E7526) at 55°C for 30 min. This was followed by incubating the mixture for 5 min at 90°C, then adding 10 μL neutralization solution (Sigma, N3910). The genotype was then determined by PCR assay adapted from Self et al.12 The primers CATGTCCAAGGTCCTCTTCC forward and CCTAGAATCAGTGCAGAGCA reverse were designed to amplify a DNA fragment across the mutation, annealing at 58°C with Dream Taq DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, EP0702) and PCR using a dNTP mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, FERR0192), followed by an MspI digest to give a 328 bp product in homozygous mutants, 189 bp and 139 bp products in WT, and 328 bp, 189 bp, and 139 bp products in heterozygous littermates. Homozygous mutant mice are designated as Shaker-1mut (−/−) and heterozygous mutant mice as Shaker-1het (+/−) mice to clarify which allele group is being evaluated.

Delivery of vector to the PSCC through canalostomy

Canalostomy was performed as previously established.40 Adult C57BL/6 mice aged 1 month (average weight, 11g) or Shaker-1 +/+, +/−, or −/− mice aged 16 days were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine (150 mg/kg), xylocaine (6 mg/kg) and acepromazine (2 mg/kg) in sodium chloride 0.9%. A dorsal postauricular incision was made and the PSCC exposed. Using a microdrill, a canalostomy was created, exposing the perilymphatic space. Subsequently, 1 μL of vector was injected using a Hamilton microsyringe with 0.1 μL graduations and a 36G needle. The canalostomy was sealed with bone wax, and the animals were allowed to recover. A single batch of LV-MYO7A vector preparation with a titer of 7.78 × 106 TU/mL was used for all in vivo experiments.

Anesthesia and posterior canal delivery in P4 Shaker-1 mice

P4 Shaker-1 mice were anesthetized with cold as described by Isgrig et al.41 The PSCC was exposed in the postauricular space and a sharp pick was used to open the canal. Vector particles at a dose of 1 μL (7.78 × 106 TU/mL) were injected using a microsyringe as described above. Genotyping was carried out at P21 as described.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal applications of phenobarbital (585 mg/kg) and phenytoin sodium (75 mg/kg) (Beuthanasia-D Special, Schering-Plough Animal Health Corp., Union, NJ, Canada) and sacrificed via intracardiac perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. The temporal bones were removed and trimmed. The stapes was removed and the round window was opened with a needle. The temporal bones were postfixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at 4°C. After rinsing in PBS three times for 30 min, the temporal bones were decalcified in 10% EDTA for 48 h. For histology, the temporal bones were rinsed in PBS and embedded in paraffin. Sections (7 μm) were cut in parallel to the modiolus, mounted on Fisherbrand Superfrost/Plus Microscope Slides (Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA) and dried overnight. Samples were deparaffinized and rehydrated in PBS two times for 5 min, then three times in 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min and finally in blocking solution 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS with 10% FBS for 30 min at room temperature. After blocking, specimens were treated with anti-annexin V rabbit polyclonal antibody (Catalog # ab182646, Abcam) diluted 1:100 in blocking solution. The tissue was incubated for 48 h at 4°C in a humid chamber. After three rinses in 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS, immunofluorescent detection was carried out with anti-rabbit IgG (1:50; Alexa Fluor 488 nm; Invitrogen Inc.). The secondary antibody was incubated for 6h at room temperature in a humid chamber. The slides were rinsed in 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS three times for 5 min and finally coverslipped with ProLong Gold antifade reagent (Invitrogen Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR). For whole mount analysis, cochleae were harvested at one week post-vector-injection and decalcified as described above. Clearing and whole mount imaging of the dTomato signal was then carried out as described in Risoud et al.42

For organ of Corti preparation, the decalcified otic capsule was carefully removed and the organ of Corti dissected away from the modiolus. For utricle preparation, the decalcified bone between the oval and round windows was removed. The macular organs were identified and the utricle removed. Residual otolith debris was brushed off the neuroepithelium. The tissue was then incubated in PBS +0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min followed by a 10 min incubation in phalloidin-FITC (Catalog # ab235137, Abcam) diluted 1:80 in PBS +0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature. The tissue was then washed three times for 10 min in PBS and mounted in Vectashield Plus mounting medium (Vector Labs, Newark, CA) between two coverslips. Quantification of vestibular hair cells was carried out by placing a counting grid over five areas of each utricle and counting total stereocilia-bearing hair cells and total stereocilia-bearing cells that expressed dTomato.

Confocal imaging was carried out on a Nikon TI2-E inverted microscope with perfect focus attached to a Yokagawa CSU-W1 spinning disk confocal system with SoRa super-resolution or a Nikon Eclipse TiE inverted motorized microscope with perfect focus attached to an A1R-SHR confocal system. Images were analyzed using Nikon Elements software and scale bar determination for individual images was completed in Adobe Photoshop 21.0.1 based on exemplary scale bar measurements in the source data.

Evaluation of cochlear hair cell survival

Midmodiolar sections were evaluated for the presence of auditory sensory hair cells within the apical and basal turns of the cochlea. Within each chamber, the presence or absence of an inner hair cell and three outer hair cells were noted. Positive hair cell counts were made if the inner and outer hair cells were appropriately located within the organ of Corti and possessed normal nuclear staining. The total number of inner and outer hair cells was determined for all treatment groups (n = 5 mice per group) across all sections (n = 5 sections per mouse); the percentage of hair cells present was then calculated for the entire group (number of hair cells found divided by the expected number x 100). This method is not intended to determine the absolute number of sensory hair cells, but rather a qualitative evaluation of sensory hair cells throughout multiple regions of the cochlea.

ABR measurement

ABR thresholds were recorded using the Intelligent Hearing Systems Smart EP program (IHS, Miami, FL). Animals were anesthetized as described above and kept warm on a heating pad (37°C). Needle electrodes were placed on the vertex (+), behind the left ear (−) and behind the opposite ear (ground). Tone bursts were presented at 4, 8, 16, and 32 kHz, with a duration of 500 μs using a high-frequency transducer. Recording was carried out using a total gain equal to 100K and using 100-Hz and 15-kHz settings for the high- and low-pass filters, respectively. A minimum of 128 sweeps was presented at 90 dB SPL. The SPL was decreased in 10-dB steps. Near the threshold level, 5-dB SPL steps using up to 1,024 presentations were carried out at each frequency. The hearing threshold was defined as the SPL at which at least one of the waves could be identified in two or more repetitions of the recording. The preoperative threshold was measured in P16 animals prior to the first operation and the final postoperative threshold was measured before sacrificing the animals. P4 treated animals did not receive pretreatment hearing screens. We tested mice prior to vector delivery and at P30 or P180.

DPOAE measurement

To evaluate the functional damage on the OHC, DPOAEs were recorded on both sides using the IHS Program described above. The distortion products were measured for pure tones from 2 kHz to 32 kHz using the IHS high frequency transducer. The Etymotic 10B+ Probe was inserted to the external ear canal. L1 Level was set to 65 dB, L2 Level was set to 55 dB. Frequencies were acquired with an F2-F1 ratio of 1.22 using 16 sweeps. Nine stimulus levels ranging from 65 dB SPL to 31 dB SPL were used in 5-dB steps.

Rotarod

To evaluate balance function, mice were tested on a rotarod treadmill (ENV-575M, Med Associates Inc., Fairfax, VA). The rod started at an initial velocity of 4 rpm and accelerated to 40 rpm at 100 s. The time from the start of acceleration, until the mouse fell completely off the rod, was recorded. The test was stopped after a maximum of 200 s. All mice were trained on the rod for 2 days prior to the baseline recording.

Force-plate actimetry

BASi Force-Plate Actimeter (FPA) (Bioanalytical Systems, Inc., Mount Vernon, IN) was used to test locomotor activity and exploratory behaviors of the mice. Mice were placed into the testing arena and allowed to move freely for 10 min (60 frames). Total distance traveled, area traveled, left and right turns, and total degrees turned were recorded and analyzed by FPA Analysis.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed with Prism v9.0 using ANOVA for repeated measures, if not stated otherwise in the figure legends. A p value of ≤0.05 was considered significant. ∗∗∗∗p ≤ 0.001; ∗∗∗p ≤ 0.005; ∗∗p ≤ 0.01; and ∗p ≤ 0.05.
