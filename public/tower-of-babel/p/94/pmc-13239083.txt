Safety evaluation of the food enzyme containing metalloendopeptidase and leucyl aminopeptidase activities from the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus oryzae strain HBI‐POP01
Authors: EFSA Panel on Food Enzymes (FEZ), Holger Zorn, José Manuel Barat Baviera, Claudia Bolognesi, Francesco Catania, Gabriele Gadermaier, Ralf Greiner, Baltasar Mayo, Alicja Mortensen, Yrjö Henrik Roos, Marize L M Solano, Henk Van Loveren, Laurence Vernis, Ana Criado, Jaime Aguilera, Magdalena Andryszkiewicz, Daniele Cavanna, Cristina Fernández‐Fraguas, Giulio di Piazza, Yi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe food enzyme containing metalloendopeptidase (EC 3.4.24) and leucyl aminopeptidase (EC 3.4.11.1) activities is produced with the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus oryzae strain HBI‐POP01 by HBI Enzymes Inc. The food enzyme was considered free from viable cells of the production organism. The food enzyme is intended to be used in four food manufacturing processes. Since residual amounts of total organic solids (TOS) are removed in one process, dietary exposure was calculated for the remaining three food manufacturing processes. It was estimated to be up to 1.821 mg TOS/kg body weight (bw) per day in European populations. Genotoxicity tests did not indicate a safety concern. The systemic toxicity was assessed by means of a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats. The Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level of 500 mg TOS/kg bw per day which, when compared with the estimated dietary exposure, resulted in a margin of exposure of at least 275. A search for the homology of the two amino acid sequences to known allergens was made and a match with one respiratory allergen was found. The Panel considered that a risk of allergic reactions upon dietary exposure to the food enzyme cannot be excluded, but that the likelihood is low. Based on the data provided, the Panel concluded that this food enzyme does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.Keywords: Aspergillus oryzae, EFSA‐Q‐2022‐00861, leucyl amino peptidase, metalloendopeptidase

INTRODUCTION

Article 3 of the Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 1 provides a definition for ‘food enzyme’ and ‘food enzyme preparation’.

‘Food enzyme’ means a product obtained from plants, animals or microorganisms or products thereof including a product obtained by a fermentation process using microorganisms: (i) containing one or more enzymes capable of catalysing a specific biochemical reaction; and (ii) added to food for a technological purpose at any stage of the manufacturing, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of foods.

‘Food enzyme preparation’ means a formulation consisting of one or more food enzymes in which substances such as food additives and/or other food ingredients are incorporated to facilitate their storage, sale, standardisation, dilution or dissolution.

Before January 2009, food enzymes other than those used as food additives were not regulated or were regulated as processing aids under the legislation of the Member States. On 20 January 2009, Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes came into force. This Regulation applies to enzymes that are added to food to perform a technological function in the manufacture, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of such food, including enzymes used as processing aids. Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 2 established the European Union (EU) procedures for the safety assessment and the authorisation procedure of food additives, food enzymes and food flavourings. The use of a food enzyme shall be authorised only if it is demonstrated that:

it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;

it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;

there is a reasonable technological need;

there is a reasonable technological need;

its use does not mislead the consumer.

its use does not mislead the consumer.

All food enzymes currently on the EU market and intended to remain on that market, as well as all new food enzymes, shall be subjected to a safety evaluation by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and approval via an EU Community list.

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Background as provided by the European Commission

Only food enzymes included in the Union list may be placed on the market as such and used in foods, in accordance with the specifications and conditions of use provided for in Article 7(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1332/20081 on food enzymes.

Five applications have been introduced by the Association of Manufacturers and Formulators of Enzyme Products (AMFEP) for the authorisation of the food enzyme consisting of Protease, Leucyl amino‐peptidase, Oryzin and Aspergillopepsin I from Aspergillus oryzae and the companies “BENEO‐Palatinit GmbH” for the authorisation of the food enzyme Isomaltulose synthase from Protaminobacter rubrum (strain Z12A), “Naganase (Europa) GmbH” for the authorisation of the food enzyme Chitinase from a genetically modified strain of Streptomyces violaceoruber (strain pChi), “Clasado Ingredients Ltd” for the authorisation of the food enzyme Beta‐galactosidase from a genetically modified strain of Escherichia coli (strain BgIA MCB3) and “Meiji Seika Pharma Co., Ltd” for the authorisation of the food enzyme consisting of Aspergillopepsin I and II from Aspergillus niger var. macrosporus (strain DBD‐0406).

Following the requirements of Article 12.1 of Regulation (EC) No 234/2011 3 implementing Regulation (EC) No 1331/20082, the Commission has verified that the five applications fall within the scope of the food enzyme Regulation and contain all the elements required under Chapter II of that Regulation.

Interpretation of the Terms of Reference

The present scientific opinion addresses the European Commission's request to carry out the safety assessment of a food enzyme containing Protease, Leucyl amino‐peptidase, Oryzin and Aspergillopepsin I activities from Aspergillus oryzae submitted by AMFEP.

The application was submitted initially as a joint dossier 5 and identified as EFSA‐Q‐2015‐00619, EFSA‐Q‐2018‐01029, EFSA‐Q‐2018‐01030 and EFSA‐Q‐2018‐01031. During an ad hoc meeting between EFSA, the European Commission and the Association of Manufacturers and Formulators of Enzyme Products (AMFEP), 6 it was agreed that joint dossiers will be split into individual data packages.

The current opinion addresses one data package originating from the former joint dossier. This data package, identified as EFSA‐Q‐2022‐00861, initially concerned the food enzyme containing four activities (protease, leucyl amino peptidase, oryzin and aspergillopepsin I) from the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus oryzae (strain HBI‐POP01) and submitted by HBI Enzymes Inc. When submitting additional data to EFSA in October 2023, 7 the applicant clarified that ‘there are two main enzymes in the food enzyme product, neutral protease and leucyl aminopeptidase. The other two remaining enzymes aspergillopepsin I and oryzin, listed in the original application, are present in the product in negligible amounts and should therefore not considered further in the assessment’. As a result of this clarification, the present opinion evaluated a food enzyme only with two endopeptidase activities (neutral protease and leucyl aminopeptidase).

Purity

The lead content in all batches was below 0.1 mg/kg, 26 , 27 which complies with the specification for lead, as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006).

The food enzyme preparation complies with the microbiological criteria for total coliforms, Escherichia coli and Salmonella, as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006). 28 No antimicrobial activity was detected in any of the tested batches. 29

Strains of Aspergillus species, in common with most filamentous fungi, have the capacity to produce a range of secondary metabolites (Frisvad et al., 2018). The presence of aflatoxins (B1, B2, G1, G2), ochratoxin A, sterigmatocystin, T2‐toxin and zearalenone was examined in the three food enzyme preparation batches intended for commercialization, and in addition the presence of ochratoxin A was examined in three batches, one of which was used for the toxicological studies; all results were below the limits of quantification (LoQ) of the applied analytical methods. 30 , 31 , 32 Adverse effects caused by the possible presence of other secondary metabolites are addressed by the toxicological examination of the food enzyme.

The Panel considered that the information provided on the purity of the food enzyme was sufficient.

Toxicological data

A battery of toxicological tests was provided, including a bacterial reverse mutation test (Ames test), two in vitro mammalian micronucleus tests, a combined in vivo mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus and alkaline Comet test in rats and a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats.

The batch 4 (Table 1) used in these studies has a lower enzyme activity/TOS ratio; hence, it is less pure compared to the batches intended for commercialisation and is therefore considered suitable as a test item.

Genotoxicity

Bacterial reverse mutation test

A bacterial reverse mutation test (Ames test) was performed according to the Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) Test Guideline 471 (OECD, 2020) and following good laboratory practice (GLP). 34 Four strains of Salmonella Typhimurium (TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537) and Escherichia coli WP2uvrA (pKM101) were used with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix), applying the pre‐incubation method.

The experiment was carried out in triplicate, using six concentrations of the food enzyme, ranging from 156 to 5000 μg TOS/plate.

No cytotoxicity was observed at any concentration of the test substance. Upon treatment with the food enzyme, there was no biologically relevant increase in the number of revertant colonies above the control values in any strain tested, with or without S9‐mix.

The Panel concluded that this food enzyme did not induce gene mutations under the test conditions applied in this study.

In vitro mammalian cell micronucleus tests

First study

The first in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test was carried out according to the OECD Test Guideline 487 (OECD, 2016a) and following GLP. 35 The experiments were performed with duplicate cultures of human lymphoblast cell line (TK cells) treated with the food enzyme with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix).

In the range‐finding test, cytotoxicity (established by the cell growth inhibition test) above 50% was seen at ≥ 1300 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment (3‐h exposure and 24‐h recovery period) without S9‐mix, at ≥ 1780 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment with S9‐mix and at ≥ 203 μg TOS/mL in a long‐term treatment (24‐h exposure without recovery period) without S9‐mix.

Based on these results, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for the frequency of binucleated cells with micronuclei (MNBN) at three concentrations of 597, 947 and 1300 μg TOS/mL in the short‐term treatment without S9‐mix, of 852, 1440 and 1780 μg TOS/mL in the short‐term treatment with S9‐mix and of 51, 102 and 203 μg TOS/mL in the long‐term treatment without S9‐mix.

The frequency of MNBN was statistically significantly increased compared to the negative controls at 1300 μg TOS/mL tested without S9‐mix in the short‐term treatment, with concentration response and outside the 95% percentile of the historical control range.

The frequency of MNBN was statistically significantly increased compared to the negative controls at 1780 μg TOS/mL tested with S9‐mix in the short‐term treatment, without concentration response and outside the 95% percentile of the historical control range. These results were considered equivocal.

The frequency of MNBN was not statistically significantly different to the negative controls at any concentration tested in the long‐term treatment without S9‐mix.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme induced an increase in the frequency of MNBN under the test conditions applied in this study.

Second study

The second in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test was carried out according to the OECD Test Guideline 487 (OECD, 2023) and following GLP. 36 Three main experiments were performed with duplicate cultures of a human lymphoblastoid cell line (TK6). The cell cultures were treated with the food enzyme with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix).

In the first experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for the frequency of mononucleated cells with micronuclei (MN) at concentrations of 988, 1481 and 2222 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment (3‐h exposure and 24‐h recovery period) with S9‐mix, or at concentrations of 658, 988, 1481 and 2222 μg TOS/mL without S9‐mix and at concentrations of 87, 130 and 195 μg TOS/mL in a long‐term treatment (24‐h exposure and 24‐h recovery period) without S9‐mix.

In the second experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for mononucleated cells with MN at concentrations of 116, 140, 154, 169, 195 and 205 μg TOS/mL in a long‐term treatment (24‐h exposure and 24‐h recovery period) without S9‐mix.

In the third experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for mononucleated cells with MN at the concentrations of 52, 116, 140, 169 and 186 μg TOS/mL in a long‐term treatment without S9‐mix.

In the short‐term treatment, cytotoxicity of 54% and 58% was observed at the highest concentration tested, with or without S9‐mix, respectively. The frequency of MN was not statistically significantly different to the negative controls at any concentration tested.

In the long‐term treatment (based on relative population doubling), cytotoxicity of 50%, 54% and 52% was observed at the highest concentration tested in experiments 1, 2 and 3, respectively.

In experiment 1, a concentration‐related increase in the frequency of micronucleated cells was observed with a statistically significant difference to the negative control at the highest concentration tested in the long term treatment without S9‐mix, where the values were outside the 95% of the historical control range.

In experiment 2, the frequency of micronucleated cells was statistically significantly different to the negative controls at concentrations of 140 and 169 μg TOS/mL, without concentration response and outside the 95% of the historical control range.

In experiment 3, a statistically significant and concentration‐related increase in the frequency of micronucleated cells was observed with all values outside the historical control range.

To investigate the genotoxic mode of action, additional slides at the highest concentration tested in the long‐term treatment (186 μg TOS/mL) were processed for mechanistic analysis by fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) with pancentromeric DNA probes and scoring for the absence or presence of a centromeric signal. The percentage of micronuclei centromere negative cells (C‐MN) was 73%, indicating a potential clastogenic mode of action.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme induced an increase in the MN frequency in the absence of metabolic activation under the test conditions applied in this study. The mode of action for the induction of micronuclei was consistent with that of an in vitro clastogenicity.

Combined in vivo mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus‐in vivo mammalian alkaline Comet test

A combined in vivo mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus test in bone marrow and in vivo mammalian alkaline Comet assay in the liver and glandular stomach of rats was conducted according to the OECD Test Guidelines 474 (OECD, 2016b) and 489 (OECD, 2016c), respectively, and following GLP. 37

Groups of six male Sprague–Dawley (Crl:CD(SD)) rats were dosed once daily by gavage with vehicle (water for injection) or 500, 1000 and 2000 mg TOS/kg bw per day for three consecutive days. The femoral bone marrow, liver and glandular stomach were collected 3 h after the last treatment from all rats. The samples from five animals in each group were used for genotoxicity evaluation.

No mortality or treatment‐related clinical signs were observed in any group.

No statistically significant differences were observed in the frequency of immature erythrocytes or micronucleated immature erythrocytes in any of the treated groups compared to negative controls.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme did not induce micronuclei in bone marrow when tested up to 2000 mg TOS/kg bw under the experimental conditions employed, but considered this result of limited validity because no evidence of bone marrow exposure was provided.

In the Comet assay, no statistically significant increase in mean % DNA in tail was observed in the liver and glandular stomach of any treated group compared to the concurrent vehicle control group.

The Panel concluded that this food enzyme did not induce DNA damage in the liver and glandular stomach of rats administered via oral gavage with up to 2000 mg TOS/kg bw, the maximum feasible concentration.

Conclusions on genotoxicity

The food enzyme was tested in a battery of in vitro and in vivo genotoxicity studies. The test item, in the presence or absence of S9‐mix, did not induce gene mutations in bacteria. Two in vitro micronucleus studies, carried out in human TK6 lymphoblastoid cell cultures, showed an increase of micronucleated cells in the absence of metabolic activation. The results of the FISH analysis indicated a potential clastogenic mode of action. The negative results obtained at the site of first contact (glandular stomach), in the in vivo follow‐up with comet assay allowed concerns for clastogenicity observed in vitro in the absence of S9‐mix to be excluded.

Repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats

The repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study was performed under GLP and according to the OECD Test Guideline 408 (OECD, 2018), 38 with the following deviation: urea was not measured. The Panel considered that this deviation was minor and did not impact the evaluation of the study.

Groups of 10 male and 10 female Sprague–Dawley (Crl:CD(SD)) rats received the food enzyme by gavage at doses of 500, 1000 or 1500 mg TOS/kg body weight (bw) per day. Controls received the vehicle (water for injection).

No mortality was observed.

The feed consumption was statistically significantly increased in weeks 9, 10, 12 and 13 of administration in mid‐dose males (−12% at all four occasions). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were recorded inconsistently; they were only observed in one sex, there was no dose–response relationship and there was no statistically significant change in the final (measured over the entire study) feed consumption, the body weight or the body weight gain.

Haematological investigations revealed a statistically significant decrease in white blood cells in mid‐dose females (−29%). The Panel considered the change as not toxicologically relevant, as it was only observed in one sex and there was no dose–response relationship.

Statistically significant changes in hormone levels included an increase in triiodothyronine (T3) in high‐dose females (+60%). The Panel considered this difference as not test item related because there was no evidence of disruption of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis (i.e. thyrosine, thyroid stimulating hormone and thyroid histopathology).

The urinalysis revealed a statistically significant decrease in sodium concentration (−40%) and in chloride concentration and excretion (−44% and −26%, respectively) in mid‐dose females. The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only observed in one sex and there was no dose–response relationship.

Changes detected in organ weights were a decrease in relative uterus to body weight ratio in females at all doses (−19%, −18% and −28%), which reached statistical significance at the high‐dose group; absolute uterus weight was also lower in all treated groups (−18%, −15% and −27%) compared to control, although the change was not statistically significant. The Panel considered that the differences in the uterus weights are not test‐item related because: (i) the high inter‐ and intra‐individual variability normally seen in uterus weight associated with different phases of the oestrus cycle, (ii) the absence of histopathological changes in the uterus and other reproductive organs examined (i.e. ovaries, cervix, vagina and mammary gland) and (iii) the lack of an effect on the oestrus cycle, as shown by cytological examination of vaginal smears on day 91.

The microscopic examination revealed test item‐related changes in the kidneys and the limiting ridge of the stomach.

In the kidneys, slight karyomegaly was observed in the tubular epithelium, limited to the S3 segment of the proximal tubule, in mid‐ and high‐dose groups; the incidence of the change appeared dose‐related and higher in males than in females (males: 7/10 and 10/10; females: 3/10 and 7/10 in the mid‐ and high‐dose, vs. 0/10 in all concurrent controls). The Panel noted that karyomegaly represents an increase in nuclear size, resulting from nucleic acid replication without nuclear division; it is a hallmark of early nephrotoxicity induced by some renal carcinogens, such as ochratoxin A or citrinin, and it is considered by some authors to be a preneoplastic lesion that can lead to tubule neoplasia in chronic studies (Boorman et al., 1992; Hard, 2018a, 2018b; Seely & Brix, 2023). The Panel considered the change as test item‐related and adverse.

In the stomach, a slight and dose‐related increase in the incidence of globule leucocytes and eosinophil infiltrates in the mucosa/submucosa of the limiting ridge was observed in mid‐ and high‐dose groups, with the non‐glandular (forestomach) side of the ridge being more affected than the glandular slope. Inflammatory infiltrates were observed in 2/10 mid‐dose and 4/10 high‐dose males, affecting both the non‐glandular and glandular sides, in 2/10 mid‐dose and 4/10 high‐dose females affecting the non‐glandular side, and in 1/10 high‐dose females affecting the glandular side. Inflammatory changes in the limiting ridge were variably associated with squamous cell hyperplasia of the non‐glandular side mucosa in mid‐ and high‐dose males (2/10 and 4/10 vs. 0/10 in controls) and females (1/10 and 2/10 vs. 0/10 in controls) and with mucous cell hypertrophy of the glandular side mucosa in high‐dose males (3/10 vs. 0/10 in controls) and females (1/10 vs. 0/10 in controls). The severity of all changes described above was slight. The Panel considered the changes test item‐related, a consequence of the bolus gavage procedure and local irritation of the stomach mucosa, with the resulting chronic inflammation leading to squamous cell hyperplasia (reactive hyperplasia). The Panel regarded histological lesions of the gastric limiting ridge as not adverse, considering their limited extension and severity.

No toxicologically relevant or other statistically significant differences from controls were reported.

The Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of 500 mg TOS/kg bw per day, the lowest dose tested, based on the histopathological effect observed in the kidney.

Allergenicity

The allergenicity assessment considered only the food enzyme and not additives, carriers or other excipients that may be used in the final formulation.

The potential allergenicity of the food enzyme containing metalloendopeptidase and leucyl aminopeptidase produced with the Aspergillus oryzae strain HBI‐POP01 was assessed by comparing each amino acid sequence with those of known allergens as described in the EFSA GMO Scientific Opinion (EFSA GMO Panel, 2010). Using higher than 35% identity in a sliding window of 80 amino acids as the criterion, a match with one respiratory allergen was found for metalloendopeptidase 39 and no match was found for leucyl aminopeptidase 40 in the AllergenOnline database.

The matching respiratory allergen was Asp f 5 (96.3% sequence identity), a metalloprotease from common mould (Aspergillus fumigatus).

No reports on oral or respiratory sensitisation or elicitation reactions of the food enzyme under assessment have been published. 41

Whereas metalloproteases have been described as respiratory allergens, several studies have shown that individuals respiratorily sensitised to a food enzyme are usually able to ingest the corresponding enzyme without acquiring clinical symptoms of food allergy (Armentia et al., 2009; Cullinan et al., 1997; Poulsen, 2004). No allergic reactions upon dietary exposure to any metalloendopeptidase and leucyl aminopeptidase have been reported in the literature. 42

The production strain belongs to the Aspergillus genus, which is known to cause respiratory allergy (Kurup et al., 2000; Shen & Han, 1998; Vermani et al., 2015). Allergic reactions upon dietary exposure have been observed, but are rare (Xing et al., 2022). The biomass is removed during the production process; however, allergenic proteins of the production strain can be released into the culture medium from which the food enzyme is obtained.

■■■■■ and ■■■■■, products from ■■■■■ and ■■■■■ that may cause allergies or intolerances (listed in the Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 43 ), are used as raw materials. In addition, ■■■■■ and ■■■■■, known sources of allergens, are also present in the culture medium. During the fermentation process, these products will mostly be degraded and utilised by the production strain.

Taken together, concerning the potential allergic reactions due to the production strain and the raw material in the culture medium, the Panel considered that residual amounts of allergenic proteins could be present in the food enzyme. Taking into account the level of dietary exposure (see Section 3.5), this would result in minute amounts in the final foods, from which allergic reactions are usually not expected.

In conclusion, when used for the production of distilled alcohol, the Panel considered that a risk of allergic reactions upon dietary exposure can be excluded. For the remaining intended uses, the risk of allergic reactions upon dietary exposure to this food enzyme cannot be excluded, but the likelihood is low.

In the production of protein hydrolysates, the food enzyme is added to a variety of protein‐rich materials from animal (e.g. meat, fish) or plant sources to enhance functional properties and to improve yield. 48 The food enzyme–TOS remains in these protein hydrolysates.

In the production of brewed products, the food enzyme may be added to cereals during the liquefaction or directly to the wort after fermentation 49 to catalyse the release of free amino nitrogen for the growth of yeast and to prevent haze formation. The food enzyme–TOS remains in the brewed products.

In the production of distilled alcohol, the food enzyme may be added to cereals during the slurry mixing, the liquefaction, the saccharification or the fermentation 50 to improve the yield and to enhance the access of amylolytic enzymes to the starch granules, facilitating the degradation of starch and non‐starch polysaccharides into fermentable sugars. The food enzyme–TOS are not carried over with the distilled alcohols (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023).

Based on data provided on thermostability (see Section 3.3.1) and the downstream processing within the respective food manufacturing processes, the Panel considered that this food enzyme may remain in its active form in the food manufacturing processes listed in Table 2 in which the food enzyme–TOS remain, depending on the heat treatment conditions.

Dietary exposure estimation

In accordance with the guidance document (EFSA CEP Panel, 2021), dietary exposure was calculated for the three food manufacturing processes where the food enzyme–TOS remain in the final foods.

Chronic exposure to the food enzyme–TOS was calculated by combining the maximum recommended use level with individual consumption data (EFSA CEP Panel, 2021). The estimation involved selection of relevant food categories and application of technical conversion factors (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023).

Table 3 provides an overview of the derived exposure estimates across all surveys. Detailed mean and 95th percentile exposure to the food enzyme–TOS per age class, country and survey, as well as contribution from each FoodEx category to the total dietary exposure are reported in Appendix A – Tables 1 and 2. For the present assessment, food consumption data were available from 48 dietary surveys (covering infants, toddlers, children, adolescents, adults and the elderly), carried out in 26 European countries (Appendix B). The highest dietary exposure was estimated to be 1.821 mg TOS/kg bw per day in adults at the 95th percentile.

The conservative approach applied to estimate the dietary exposure to the food enzyme–TOS, in particular assumptions made on the occurrence and use levels of this specific food enzyme, is likely to have led to an overestimation of the exposure.

The exclusion of one food manufacturing process from the exposure assessment was based on > 99% of TOS removal. This is not expected to impact the overall estimate derived.

Margin of exposure

A comparison of the NOAEL (500 mg TOS/kg bw per day) from the 90‐day rat study with the derived exposure estimates of 0.005–0.418 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the mean and from 0.008–1.821 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the 95th percentile resulted in a margin of exposure of at least 275.

CONCLUSION

Based on the data provided and the derived margin of exposure, the Panel concluded that the food enzyme containing metalloendopeptidase and leucyl aminopeptidase, produced with the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus oryzae strain HBI‐POP01, does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.
