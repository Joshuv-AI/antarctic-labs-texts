“Et tu, Brute?” - A Case of Ixazomib - Induced Cancer Therapy-related Cardiac Dysfunction
Authors: Yusuke Hirao, Kensuke Matsumoto, Yu Mizutani, Shinnosuke Tsukamoto, Akari Odagi, Yuto Shinkura, Ryo Nishio, Masahiko Hoshijima, Satoru Kawasaki, Hogara Nishisaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn 81-year-old man with a history of multiple myeloma presented with decompensated heart failure (HF). Although his baseline left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was preserved, it significantly declined to 35% at 13 months after the initiation of ixazomib. Considering the chronological relationship between the timing of ixazomib treatment and symptom onset, prior normal echocardiography, and multimodal imaging findings, ixazomib-induced cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) was strongly suspected. Despite the discontinuation of ixazomib and continued administration of guideline-directed medical therapy, his LVEF has not recovered to date. We herein report a rare case of ixazomib-induced irreversible CTRCD. Keywords: cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction, heart failure, ixazomib, proteasome inhibitor, cardiotoxicity

Introduction

Multiple myeloma is the most common plasma cell dyscrasia and the second most common hematologic malignancy (1). The emergence of proteasome inhibitors has dramatically improved the outcomes in patients with multiple myeloma (2), indeed, they have been established as highly effective therapeutic options in this field (3). On the other hand, however, as these drugs have become widely used in clinical practice, cardiotoxicity has also become increasingly recognized as a critical problem (4,5). In fact, a nonnegligible number of cases of left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, new-onset heart failure, and even cardiovascular death have been reported (6), leading to warnings issued by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

Recently, a new-generation proteasome inhibitor ixazomib, has been developed (7), and based on the results of a phase III trial (8), this novel proteasome inhibitor has received FDA approval. It has demonstrated adequate antitumor efficacy while maintaining a cardiovascular safety profile (7,8), and the “all-oral regimen” significantly improves patient convenience. Thus, ixazomib has brought about a paradigm shift in the treatment of multiple myeloma owing to its favorable cardiovascular safety profile (9) and convenience, leading to its widespread use in clinical practice, particularly in the elderly and in treatment-refractory cases.

We here report a rare case of ixazomib-induced cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) that ultimately progressed to acute decompensated heart failure.

Case Report

An 81-year-old man presented to the emergency department in April 2025 with worsening dyspnea and wheezing from the previous evening. His medical history included immunoglobulin G (IgG)-type multiple myeloma, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Although lenalidomide and dexamethasone were initiated for the treatment of multiple myeloma in July 2011, chemotherapy was subsequently discontinued because of a severe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection. Subsequently, a significant elevation in the IgG levels was observed, indicating an increased disease activity, and the treatment regimen was switched to combination therapy with bortezomib and dexamethasone in July 2023. Echocardiography showed an LV ejection fraction (LVEF) of 53% and global longitudinal strain (GLS) of 15.2%, indicating a preserved systolic function (Fig. 1A, Supplementary material 1). Thereafter, ixazomib was initiated as a maintenance therapy in March 2024. It was administered at a dose of 3 mg once weekly, with each treatment cycle comprising three consecutive weeks of dosing followed by a 1-week drug-free interval (i.e., a 4-week cycle). This regimen was continued for 13 months, with corresponding to a cumulative dose of 117 mg.

A bull’s eye display of a speckle-tracking strain analysis from an 81-year-old man. Prior to the administration of ixazomib (June 2023), the global longitudinal strain (GLS) was relatively well preserved at 15.2% (A), while the value significantly decreased to 7.1% (B) at the onset of decompensated heart failure (July 2025).

In the emergency department, his blood pressure was significantly elevated to 192/119 mmHg, heart rate was 86 beats/min, and critical hypoxia was observed with an saturation of percutaneous oxygen (SpO2) of 75% in ambient air. Physical examination revealed a distended internal jugular vein extending up to the auricular level. Prominent wheezing was auscultated in both the lower lung fields. Peripheral findings included cold extremities and slow pitting edema over the anterior aspect of the tibia. Chest radiography revealed pulmonary edema and cardiomegaly (Fig. 2A). Twelve-lead electrocardiography revealed sinus rhythm with conduction disturbances, including right bundle branch block, left anterior fascicular block, and Wenckebach atrioventricular block (Fig. 2B). Laboratory findings revealed renal dysfunction with a serum creatinine level of 1.35 mg/dL (estimated glomerular filtration rate, 39.6 mL/min/1.73 m2) and mild anemia with a hemoglobin of 11.1 g/dL. As shown in Fig. 3, the brain natriuretic peptide level was markedly elevated at 2,086 pg/mL (reference range ＜18.4), with leakage of cardiac troponin I at 64.4 pg/mL (reference range ＜26.2). Transthoracic echocardiography showed significantly reduced LV contractile function (LVEF of 35%, Supplementary material 2) compared with that evaluated before ixazomib administration. Doppler echocardiography revealed an elevated pressure gradient of 55 mmHg between the right ventricle and the right atrium, indicating significant pulmonary hypertension. Moreover, a speckle-tracking strain analysis revealed a significantly reduced GLS value of 7.1% (Fig. 1B).

The patient was admitted for further evaluation and treatment of acute decompensated heart failure and was initially treated with intravenous nitroglycerin and furosemide. Thereafter, heart failure treatment was gradually switched to oral combination therapy including sacubitril/valsartan (100 mg/day), dapagliflozin (10 mg/day), finerenone (10 mg/day), and bisoprolol (0.625 mg/day) which successfully alleviated the symptoms of heart failure (Fig. 3). After achieving hemodynamic compensation, the patient underwent coronary angiography, which revealed no significant obstructive lesions that could account for severely impaired LV systolic function (Fig. 4). Due to concerns of advanced cardiac conduction disturbance, an endomyocardial biopsy was not performed, thus prompting a gadolinium-enhanced cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) study. T1 mapping images demonstrated diffusely elevated native T1 values of 1,091 ms (Fig. 5A, the upper limit of the T1 value was set at 1,050 ms) and contrast-enhanced CMR revealed late gadolinium enhancement predominantly involving the mid-wall of the interventricular septum and lateral wall, indicating fibrosis as a result of myocardial damage (Fig. 5B, arrows). However, no contrast-enhanced findings suggestive of cardiac amyloidosis were observed. Based on the clinical course of ixazomib introduction, chronological relationships of symptom onset, previously normal echocardiography findings, and findings on multimodality imaging, ixazomib-induced CTRCD was highly suspected. Six months after the discontinuation of ixazomib and continued treatment with maximally tolerated guideline directed medical therapy, the New York Heart Association functional class significantly improved to class II, and the serum brain natriuretic peptide and troponin levels markedly decreased to 65.7 pg/mL and undetectable levels, respectively (Fig. 3); however, the LV systolic function did not improve at all (Supplementary material 3), suggesting the irreversible nature of cardiac dysfunction induced by ixazomib-induced CTRCD. Given the sustained disease stability after the discontinuation of ixazomib, the patient is currently under watchful observation without any active treatment for multiple myeloma, with a regular follow-up based on blood tests, including the serum immunoglobulin levels.

Discussion

The proteasome is a large intracellular enzyme-complex responsible for degrading unnecessary or deteriorated proteins in the intracellular environment, and recognized as a “key player” in maintaining cellular homeostasis and its viability (10). Proteins targeted for degradation are tagged with a small protein ubiquitin, a “molecular tag”, which allows their recognition and subsequent degradation into peptides by the proteasome. Through the proteolytic function of “ubiquitin-proteasome pathway”, essential processes for cellular integrity - including cell cycle regulation, immune responses, and intracellular signal transduction - are exquisitely controlled (10). Conversely, impaired proteasomal regulation leads to the accumulation of misfolded proteins in the cells, resulting in impaired proteostasis, cellular stress, premature cell senescence, cell cycle arrest, and ultimately apoptosis (11).

In recent years, pharmacological agents that inhibit proteasomes have been increasingly used as clinical therapeutics. In particular, myeloma cells, which continuously produce large amounts of abnormal immunoglobulins, are chronically exposed to proteotoxic stress; therefore, effective inhibition of the proteasome induces profound, myeloma-specific proteotoxic and oxidative stress, ultimately leading to selective cellular death (12). However, as proteasome inhibitors have become widely used in clinical practice, cardiotoxic adverse effects have emerged as a significant concern (4,5). In fact, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis of 24 clinical trials (13), the incidence of cardiovascular adverse effects associated with carfilzomib, a representative former generation proteasome inhibitor, is reported to be 18.1%, with heart failure occurring in as many as 4.1% of cases (13).

Because cardiomyocytes have an especially high metabolic demand and a low turnover rate, one can speculate that the disruption of proteostasis caused by proteasome inhibition may lead to fatal cellular injury (14,15). In particular, risks for adverse cardiac events may be further accelerated in the presence of underlying heart diseases or aggregation of multiple risk factors for myocardial damage (16). Furthermore, it is well established that activity of the “ubiquitin-proteasome pathway” is already diminished in elderly; therefore, adverse effects associated with proteasome inhibitors are expected to occur even more readily in these patients, as illustrated in the present case.

Compared to the previously reported frequency of cardiovascular adverse effects caused by proteasome inhibitors, such events are considered rare with ixazomib. In the double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 trial TOURMALINE-MM4 Study (17), 706 patients with refractory multiple myeloma who did not undergo autologous stem cell transplantation were randomly assigned to receive ixazomib or a placebo. At a median follow-up of 21.1 months, progression-free survival was significantly longer in the ixazomib group than in the placebo group. Notably, no between-group differences in heart failure, arrhythmias, or myocardial infarction were observed (17). Although both bortezomib and ixazomib are selective inhibitors of the proteasome β5 subunit and induce time- and dose-dependent effects on ubiquitinated proteins (18), ixazomib differs from bortezomib in its binding kinetics. Specifically, ixazomib exhibits a significantly shorter proteasome dissociation half-life - approximately sixfold shorter (18 minutes) than that of bortezomib (110 minutes) (18). These pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic differences may partly explain the lower likelihood of cardiotoxic adverse events associated with ixazomib.

To date, only one case of heart failure due to ixazomib-associated LV dysfunction has been reported (19). Notably, however, in that case, the LV dysfunction did not improve even after the discontinuation of ixazomib. These observations imply that ixazomib-induced CTRCD may be irreversible in nature, as observed in our case. If that is the case, then it can not be overemphasized that heightened vigilance regarding ixazomib-related cardiotoxicity would be warranted.

Multiple myeloma is inherently an incurable malignancy, mainly affecting elderly patients; therefore, therapeutic priorities should be set such that maintaining, or at least not compromising, the quality of life takes precedence over the mere prolongation of survival. If treatment leads to a deterioration in a patient's well-being, then it would be counterproductive and contrary to the fundamental goals of care. Therefore, when initiating therapy, it is especially important to conduct pretreatment risk stratification for cardiovascular adverse events associated with proteasome inhibitors. In studies involving the former generation proteasome inhibitor carfilzomib, risk factors such as advanced age, obesity, a twice-a-week regimen, concomitant use of immunomodulators, total dosage, and comorbid COPD have been reported to be associated with the development of CTRCD (16). Assuming that such epidemiological data also apply to ixazomib, the present very elderly patient with underlying COPD and a baseline LVEF near the lower limit of normal at the initiation of ixazomib would be considered at particularly high risk for developing cardiotoxicity. As emphasized in the European Society of Cardiology cardio-oncology guidelines (20) and the Japanese Circulation Society/Japanese Heart Failure Society guidelines (21), comprehensive cardiovascular risk stratification prior to the initiation of potentially cardiotoxic therapies, together with structured cardiovascular monitoring during and after treatment, is very important. In particular, a baseline assessment, including clinical risk factors, cardiac imaging, and cardiac biomarkers, should be performed to identify high-risk individuals. Furthermore, multidisciplinary discussions to carefully balance the risks and benefits of cardiotoxic anticancer therapies in high-risk patients are strongly recommended (20,21). We therefore would like to underscore the importance of establishing a well-coordinated multidisciplinary team with effective communication.

Conclusion

Ixazomib has been increasingly used in recent years because of its cardiovascular safety profile and patient convenience. However, it cannot be regarded as entirely safe, as previously assumed, but it can exhibit significant cardiotoxicity similar to that of other conventional proteasome inhibitors. As demonstrated in the present case and in a previous case report (19), ixazomib can cause severe LV systolic dysfunction, which may not improve even after discontinuation of the drug and the introduction of guideline-directed medical therapy. Therefore, appropriate risk stratification, close monitoring, and prompt intervention in the event of CTRCD are essential, especially in elderly patients and in those with cardiovascular risk factors.
