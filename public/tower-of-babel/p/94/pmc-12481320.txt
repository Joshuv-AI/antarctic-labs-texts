Beyond the field: injury and illness patterns among Team USA athletes at the Paris 2024 Olympic and Paralympic Games
Authors: Eric G Post, Ashley N Triplett, Travis Anderson, Olivia Samson, Alexis D Gidley, Heather K Vincent, Amber Donaldson, Jonathan Finnoff, William M Adams
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo (1) describe the incidence and characteristics of injuries and illnesses reported by Team USA athletes competing in the 2024 Paris Summer Olympic Games (PSOG) and Paralympic Games (PSPG); (2) compare injury and illness incidence between Olympic and Paralympic cohorts and (3) quantify the burden of respiratory and thermoregulatory illnesses under standard (post-COVID) public health conditions.MethodsThe United States Olympic & Paralympic Committee (USOPC) Injury and Illness Surveillance system was used to document the details of all injuries and illnesses reported by any of the 862 athletes, alternates and guides competing for Team USA in the PSOG and PSPG. Illness and injury incidence per 1000 athlete-days (AD) and incidence ratios (IR) were calculated, both with 95% CI.ResultsTeam USA Paralympic athletes sustained 20.5 injuries per 1000 AD versus 14.6 among Olympic athletes (IR (95% CI): 1.4 (1.1 to 1.8)). Illness incidence was 15.7 per 1000 AD among Paralympic athletes versus 8.3 in Olympic athletes (IR (95% CI): 1.9 (1.4 to 2.6)). Among Team USA Paralympic athletes, injuries sustained outside of sport settings accounted for 38.5% (30/78) of all Paralympic injuries, the largest single setting category. The most common system affected by illness at both PSOG and PSPG was the respiratory system, with 7.4% of all athletes reporting a respiratory illness. Despite concerns ahead of the Games related to extreme heat, only two (0.3%) Olympic athletes and zero (0.0%) Paralympic athletes from Team USA reported a heat-related illness.ConclusionInjury and illness rates were higher among Team USA Paralympic athletes compared with Olympic athletes during the Paris 2024 Games. Respiratory illnesses were the most frequent medical problem, despite a pre-Games prevention campaign, highlighting the need for stronger infection-control strategies at future Games. The absence of heat illness suggests current heat-mitigation measures were effective. Comprehensive injury-prevention and illness-prevention strategies are needed for both Olympic and Paralympic athletes, with additional attention to the environmental and accessibility risks that disproportionately affect Paralympic competitors.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study provides the first post-COVID Summer Games surveillance of a national delegation under routine public health conditions. Our data also demonstrates a rebound in respiratory illness incidence while documenting very low heat-illness rates, informing medical staffing, prevention priorities and resource allocation for future games. We also found that approximately 38% of all Paralympic injuries occurred in non-sport environments, highlighting accessibility hazards that clinicians and organising committees can address.

Introduction

Injury and illness surveillance at large multisport events underpins evidence-based athlete care. Prospective documentation of every medical encounter improves clinical risk determination and decision-making for resource allocation and refines prevention strategies that benefit both elite and grassroots sport. The Olympic Games1 (~10 500 athletes)2 and Paralympic Games3 (~4400 athletes)4 concentrate this work into a 3-week window, creating an unparalleled setting to conduct high-quality injury and illness surveillance and learn about risks within sports (especially those unique to the Olympic or Paralympic programme) and relative risks across elite athlete populations (such as male vs female athletes, or Olympic vs Paralympic athletes). The United States Olympic and Paralympic Committee (USOPC) also has a unique epidemiological opportunity compared with other National Olympic (NOC) and National Paralympic Committees (NPC) because it is one of the only combined NOC/NPC organisations in the world. Therefore, because the USOPC manages both delegations within the same electronic health-record system, direct comparisons within the same dataset of Olympic and Paralympic athletes are possible.

Every Olympic and Paralympic Games is characterised by unique challenges, including the local (ie, environmental conditions, geographical challenges and sociocultural considerations) and global (ie, political, economic, climate and public health considerations) contexts of those Games; this was certainly true for the Paris 2024 Summer Olympic (PSOG) and Paralympic Games (PSPG). Specifically, the Paris Games were the first Olympic and Paralympic Games held without the COVID-19 infection prevention measures used during the Tokyo 2020 Summer Games and Beijing 2022 Winter Games.57 Further, significant concern was raised ahead of the Paris Games related to the potential for extreme heat, humidity and possible water pollution during the Games.8 Consequently, respiratory and thermoregulatory illnesses were designated a priori as key endpoints in the USOPC Injury and Illness Surveillance system to evaluate the effectiveness of newly implemented prevention campaigns. Finally, Paralympic athletes face unique environmental challenges, including potential accessibility limitations in athlete housing and competition venues. Considering all these various contextual factors, rigorous injury and illness surveillance during the Games is vital to understanding the impact of each Games’ unique circumstances on athlete health and performance.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to describe the incidence and characteristics of all injuries and illnesses reported by Team USA athletes competing at the 2024 PSOG and PSPG. Specifically, we aimed to compare the incidence of injury and illness between Team USA Olympic and Paralympic athletes and to describe the incidence of respiratory and thermoregulatory illnesses reported during the Paris Games.

Methods

This descriptive epidemiological study assessed data from the USOPC Injury and Illness Surveillance (IIS) system9 during the 2024 PSOG and PSPG. The manuscript follows the STROBE-SIIS10 checklist for reporting injury and illness surveillance studies and the CHAMP11 statement for statistical analysis and data presentation.

Patient and Public Involvement

There was no direct involvement of the athletes competing in the 2024 PSOG or PSPG in the design or conduct of this study. However, the USOPC Research Review Group, which includes athlete representatives, reviewed and approved the development and implementation of the USOPC IIS system for use to gather and analyse injury and illness data during Olympic and Paralympic Games. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of North Carolina at Greensboro (IRB-FY22-218).

Equity, Diversity and Inclusion Statement

The data for this study were reported from the diverse population of athletes who competed for Team USA at the 2024 PSOG and PSPG (biological sex: male=47.8% and female=52.2%; Games: Olympic=73.7% and Paralympic=26.3%; self-identified race/ethnicity: white=60.3%, black or African American=15.9%, more than one race or ethnicity=11.7%, Asian=3.8%, Hispanic or Latino=2.2%, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander=0.7%, American Indian or Alaska Native=0.2%, Middle Eastern or North African=0.2%, prefer not to say=3.1%, Unknown: 1.7%). The research team consists of a diverse group of expert clinicians and researchers (56% female, 44% physicians/physical therapists/athletic trainers, 44% early career researchers).

Data Collection

Data were captured during the full period of time that Team USA athletes were in Paris for training/competition and therefore at risk for an injury or illness. Data collection for this study occurred across an 11-day preopening ceremony period prior to the Opening Ceremony of each Games (PSOG: July 26, 2024; PSPG: August 28, 2024), the competition period (17 days for PSOG, 12 days for PSPG) and a 3-day post-closing ceremony period following the Closing Ceremony of each Games (PSOG: August 11, 2024; PSPG: September 8, 2024). Team USA–credentialed healthcare providers documented all injury and illness evaluations of Team USA athletes during the 2024 PSOG and PSPG into the USOPC IIS system.9 The full methodology and data management processes for the USOPC IIS system have been described previously.9 Briefly, the system records details of injuries and illnesses according to the 2020 IOC Consensus Statement for recording and reporting sport epidemiology.10 Mandatory fields captured athlete identifier, sport/discipline, date of onset, setting, mechanism of injury, mode of onset, body region or system, diagnosis (OSIICS v15) and time-loss status.9 A fully deidentified version of the USOPC IIS form is available freely online.9 Mental-health encounters were captured in a separate form based on the IOC Mental Health Reporting Form, but these data were collected on a first-time pilot basis for feasibility only and were not integrated into the validated IIS data export and are therefore outside the scope of the present analysis.

Definition of Injury and Illness

Within the USOPC IIS system, injuries are defined as ‘tissue damage or other derangement of normal physical function due to participation in sports, that requires evaluation by a healthcare provider and results in a diagnosis’ and illnesses are defined as ‘a physical health-related complaint or disorder experienced by an athlete, that requires evaluation by a healthcare provider and results in a diagnosis’.9 These definitions are derived from the 2020 IOC Consensus Statement.10 Within the USOPC IIS, each illness and injury is assigned a diagnosis using the Orchard Sports Injury and Illness Classification System (OSIICS v15.0).12

Calculation of Athlete Exposure

Athlete exposure was expressed as athlete-days (AD), defined as the number of accredited athletes multiplied by the days they were under medical surveillance in Paris. This denominator is endorsed for Olympic and Paralympic surveillance when uniform session-level data are not obtainable across all sports5 10 13 14 and improves on the traditional ‘per-100-athletes’ metric by incorporating time at risk. Athlete arrival and departure dates, extracted from official travel and accommodation records, defined the surveillance window for each athlete. ADs were calculated separately for Olympic and Paralympic delegations (11920 and 3811 AD, respectively) and for the combined sample. We acknowledge that AD does not account for within-day variations in playing time; future improvements in Games’ surveillance should consider incorporating specific practice and competition schedules for the calculation of athlete exposure.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were injury incidence and illness incidence, each expressed per 1000 AD with 95% CI. Incidence was calculated for the overall samples, as well as by sex, sport, timing (pre-opening ceremony, competition period, post-closing ceremony), injury or illness presentation, injury mechanism, injury onset, injury setting, injury anatomic location and illness medical system.

Secondary outcomes included incidence ratios (IRs) with 95% CI to compare illness and injury incidence based on Games (PSOG and PSPG), sex and timing within Games (preopening ceremony, competition period and postclosing ceremony).

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted using R statistical software (v4.3.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).15 Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the number of athletes competing in the PSOG and PSPG, total number of AD, frequency of injuries and illnesses, sports represented, athlete ages and time loss from sport. For descriptive summaries, and per regulations from the approving institution’s Institutional Review Board, sports with fewer than 10 athletes competing were combined based on relative similarities to maintain athlete confidentiality. Continuous variables were summarised as mean±SD when normally distributed and as median (IQR) when skewed. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. The epiR package was used to calculate all incidence, IR and 95% CIs as described above, using the arguments: ctype = “inc.rate” and method = “exact”.16 Significance was established at p<0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 635 athletes (53.2% women), including alternates, represented Team USA at the 2024 PSOG across 45 sports. A total of 227 athletes (49.3% women), including alternates and guides, competed for Team USA at the 2024 PSPG across 21 sports. Overall, both injury (PSPG: 20.5 (16.2 to 25.5) per 1000 AD; PSOG: 14.6 (12.5 to 16.9) per 1000 AD) and illness (PSPG: 15.7 (12.0 to 20.3) per 1000 AD; PSOG: 8.3 (6.8 to 10.1) per 1000 AD) rates were greater during the PSPG compared with the PSOG (injury IR (95% CI), 1.4 (1.1 to 1.8); illness IR (95% CI), 1.9 (1.4 to 2.6)) (table 1).

Paris Summer Paralympic Games (PSPG)

During the PSPG, a total of 78 injuries (23.4% of PSPG athletes) and 60 illnesses (20.7% of PSPG athletes) were reported across a total of 3811 AD.

Injury Rates Overall, by Sex and Sport

The overall injury incidence among athletes during the PSPG was 20.5 injuries per 1000 AD (table 1). There was no difference in injury incidence during the PSPG between male and female athletes (IR (95% CI), 1.1 (0.7, 1.7)). The sports with the highest injury rates were para table tennis (148.2 (64.0 to 291.9) per 1000 AD), para taekwondo (88.2 (18.2 to 257.9) per 1000 AD) and sitting volleyball (52.6 (28.8 to 88.3) per 1000 AD) (figure 1). Only six (7.7%) injuries resulted in time loss, with a median of 2 (1.3–2.0) days lost from sport.

Injury Rates by Timing, Setting and Anatomic Location

During PSPG, injury incidence was nearly twice as high during the pre-opening ceremony period compared with the competition period (IR (95% CI), 1.8 (1.1 to 2.9)) (table 2). The most common setting where injuries occurred was outside of sport (7.9 (5.3 to 11.2) per 1000 AD), followed by during training (5.8 (3.6 to 8.7) per 1000 AD). Injury incidence was highest at the neck and hand (both 2.1 (0. to, 4.1) per 1000 AD), followed by the shoulder (1.8 (0.7 to 3.8)) (figure 2).

Illness Rates Overall, by Sex and Sport

The overall illness incidence during the PSPG was 15.7 per 1000 AD, with just over 1 in 5 (20.7%) PSPG Team USA athletes reporting an illness (table 3). Illness incidence was over two times higher among female athletes compared with male athletes (IR (95% CI), 2.4 (1.4 to 4.6)). The sports with the highest illness rates were para judo (50.0 (1.3, 278.6) per 1000 AD), followed by paratriathlon (34.8 (15.9, 66.0) per 1000 AD) and then sitting volleyball (33.8 (15.5, 64.2) per 1000 AD) (figure 3). Only four (6.7%) illnesses resulted in time loss, with a median of 6 (3.5–6.0) days lost from sport.

Illness Rates by Medical System, Timing and Presentation

The most common illness type during the PSPG was respiratory illnesses (36.7% of all PSPG illnesses), with 7.5% of all PSPG athletes reporting a respiratory illness (table 4). The next most common illness types were dermatological illnesses (20.0% of all PSPG illnesses, 5.3% of all PSPG Team USA athletes) and gastrointestinal illnesses (15.0% of all PSPG illnesses, 4.0% of all PSPG Team USA athletes). No thermoregulatory illnesses were reported during the PSPG. Illness incidence was nearly twice as high during the preopening ceremony compared with the competition period (IR (95% CI), 1.8 (1.0 to 3.0)) (table 5).

Discussion

This study provides the first injury and illness surveillance data for elite athletes competing at the Paris 2024 Olympic and Paralympic Games. Key findings were a higher injury and illness incidence in Paralympic versus Olympic athletes, a predominance of respiratory illness and an unexpectedly low incidence of heat-related illness.

When directly comparing incidence between Team USA Olympic and Paralympic athletes, both injury and illness rates were higher among Paralympic athletes. Further, female Paralympic athletes were more than two times (2.4 (1.4, 4.6)) as likely to report an illness during the Games compared with male Paralympic athletes. Previous Team USA data from 2022 Beijing Winter Olympic and Paralympic Games17 and from the 2023 Santiago Pan American and Parapan American Games18 revealed higher rates of both illness and injury reported by Paralympic athletes. In contrast, Team USA data from the 2020 Tokyo Summer Olympic and Paralympic Games found no differences in injury or illness incidence between Olympic and Paralympic athletes.19 There are several plausible reasons for increased risk among Paralympic athletes, including factors related to the individual athlete’s specific impairment, equipment failure or equipment-related injuries, and even the interaction of athletes with a sport and community environment that may not be adequately designed to accommodate the needs of Paralympic athletes. For example, the most common setting for injuries among Team USA Paralympic athletes was outside of sport (38.5% of all Paralympic injuries, 9.7% of all Paralympic athletes), with incidents such as falls occurring in athlete housing or during transportation. Event infrastructures may still fall short of fully accommodating athletes with diverse mobility and accessibility needs, which could help explain the higher proportion of non-sport injuries documented among Paralympic competitors. These findings highlight the importance of optimising accessibility in the Games’ environment to further reduce injury risk.

It should also be noted that the apparent differences in injury risk between Team USA Olympic and Paralympic athletes may be a result of limitations in our measurement of athlete exposure and therefore do not represent a true inherent difference in risk between Olympic and Paralympic athletes. In other words, while the rates reported here are accurate for an athlete-day denominator, some portion of the risk difference between Olympic and Paralympic athletes may stem from the way exposure was measured rather than from an intrinsic disparity in injury susceptibility. As Danielsen et al20 highlight in their critique of epidemiological comparisons by gender/sex, a term used by the authors to encompass both gender and sex, methodological choices for measuring athlete exposure can create artificial differences in injury risk that are not related to true differences between populations, but instead based on factors such as differences in training and sporting environments. As an example, it is possible that Paralympic athletes participate in a greater number of competitions (where injury is more likely) relative to practice/training sessions as compared with Olympic athletes and do so within a more constrained time period (12-day competition period for Paralympics, compared with 17 days for Olympics). Therefore, increased injury risk observed among Paralympic athletes may be a result of the differences in the compressed intensity of training/competition schedules as compared with athletes competing in the Olympic Games. Future surveillance during Games periods should aim to capture athlete exposure more precisely. For example, athlete-exposure data could be recorded using specific practice or competition schedules rather than AD. Recording athlete exposure at that level would improve the ability to identify the true causes of risk disparities and develop effective preventative interventions. Indeed, the current analysis considers a day of training, a rest day and a competition day as equivalently exposing athletes to injury, which may not accurately reflect the true state of injury risk. In addition, participation in team versus individual sport exposures may introduce different illness and injury-related risk factors that could be shaping the results presented here. This limitation is potentially magnified when comparing sports in which exposure is defined by elapsed time (e.g., a 2 hour marathon) with sports organised around bouts or points (e.g., tennis matches) because an “athlete-day” does not capture the very different minutes-at-risk inherent in each format.

Respiratory illness was the most common type of illness reported by Team USA athletes during both the PSOG and PSPG, with 7.4% of all Team USA athletes sustaining a respiratory illness during the Paris Games. The incidence of respiratory illness for Olympic (4.3 per 1000 AD) and Paralympic (5.8 per 1000 AD) Team USA athletes was similar to the rates reported for Team USA athletes during the 2023 Santiago Pan American (3.6 per 1000 AD) and Parapan American Games (8.5 per 1000 AD).18 In both samples, the incidence of respiratory illness is significantly greater than what was observed among Team USA athletes during the 2020 Tokyo Summer Olympic (0.5 per 1000 AD) and Paralympic (1.0 per 1000 AD) Games,19 suggesting that the illness prevention measures implemented in Tokyo were highly effective, and the removal of these measures has resulted in elevated rates of respiratory illness for Team USA athletes. Following the high illness rates observed in Santiago,18 the USOPC Department of Sports Medicine implemented a respiratory illness prevention programme for Paris focused on educating athletes and staff on simple illness prevention measures (e.g., masking, hand hygiene and avoiding crowds) with emphasis on reducing risk during travel to Paris. Anecdotally, athlete and staff adherence with this guidance was low and there was a relatively high volume of staff respiratory illnesses, which may have contributed to illness spread among Team USA athletes. In the absence of Games-wide mandates or more Team-wide support for implementing illness prevention measures, future research is necessary to understand the most effective pathways to encourage risk-mitigating attitudes and ultimately, behaviours, by Team USA athletes and staff during Games to prevent respiratory illness. In addition to stated illness prevention techniques, respiratory illness rates are likely influenced by other behavioural and environmental factors, such as vaccination status, nutrition, sleep and training/recovery practices. Regrettably, we do not have the data to analyse how these covariates may have contributed to the respiratory illness rates observed among Team USA athletes in Paris.

Significant concern was raised prior to the Paris Games related to the potential for high temperatures and a resulting increased risk of heat illness.8 21 Although the Olympic Games Opening Ceremony will be remembered in part for the heavy rain that occurred during the event, a significant heat wave hit Paris just a few days later on July 29, resulting in extreme temperatures at outdoor venues.22 23 A multi-level approach to heat illness prevention occurred during the Paris Games, with interventions at both the Games (ie, Paris Organising Committee for the 2024 Olympic and Paralympic Games, International Olympic Committee, International Paralympic Committee) and national (ie, USOPC) levels. These interventions at the Games, some of which have also been successfully implemented at previous Games and international events, included shifting the schedule of specific outdoor events to cooler times of the day to avoid the hottest time of the day and implementing heat and hydration breaks to several outdoor sports, such as football and tennis. At the USOPC level, interventions included pre-Games educational campaigns focused on proper acclimatisation, physical interventions such as providing cooling vests for athletes participating in high-risk sports, and preparation by the USOPC Sports Medicine, Performance and Dietitian staff to monitor, identify and rapidly treat athletes at risk of heat illness. This multi-level, multifactorial approach to prevention appears to have been successful for Team USA, with only two athletes in total across both PSOG and PSPG reporting a thermoregulatory illness. While the direct causal effect of each of these strategies in preventing heat illness remains unknown, our data suggest these strategies should continue to be deployed and evaluated.

Different injury and illness rates by sport existed among Team USA athletes in the PSOG and PSPG, despite the relatively small sport subgroups (figures1 2). Injury incidence was greatest during the PSOG in surfing, BMX freestyle riding and triathlon, and highest in the PSPG in table tennis, taekwondo and sitting volleyball. Previous Olympic and Paralympic Games have consistently shown variation in injury and illness incidence across different sports, with rates influenced by factors such as sport type, athlete characteristics, competition structure and environmental conditions.56 13 14 2432 These variations can be attributed to combinations of athlete-specific factors, such as age, sex, individual and team sport type, and (for Paralympic athletes) impairment type, which can affect joint stability, visual perception and overall athletic performance.56 13 14 2432 However, caution is warranted when interpreting differences in injury and illness rates across sports within our data, as the small sample sizes within individual sports likely result in variability in incidence that does not necessarily reflect true differences in risk.

It is important to recognise that our data captures only the illnesses and injuries reported by Team USA athletes who sought medical care and, therefore, may not fully reflect the overall burden of illness and injury experienced by athletes during the Paris Games. Further, as highlighted in our comparison of Olympic and Paralympic athlete injury risk, we measured exposure using AD rather than training or event-level data, and therefore, our observed differences in incidence may reflect disparities in the number of practices versus competitions rather than true variations in risk. This study’s data pertains to a single country’s delegation during a specific event—the Paris 2024 Olympic and Paralympic Games. Further confirmatory research is necessary to determine whether the trends identified here are consistent across other delegations in Paris or during future Games. Finally, we did not categorise injury or illness incidence based on athlete impairment type during the Paralympic Games. Since certain sports are exclusive to athletes with specific impairments, the reported sport-specific incidence is likely a result of both sport-related and impairment-specific risk factors.

Clinical implications

Our findings highlight critical opportunities to enhance athlete care and optimise health outcomes during multi-sport events like the Olympic and Paralympic Games. The higher injury and illness rates we observed in Paralympic athletes, especially among female Paralympic athletes, suggest a need for tailored preventative strategies, such as improved accessibility in athlete housing, transportation, and other non-sport settings. Respiratory illnesses were the most frequently reported illness type in both Olympic and Paralympic athletes, underscoring the importance of proactive respiratory health management, such as pre-Games health screenings and targeted interventions like environmental monitoring and education about respiratory hygiene. Additionally, the rare occurrence of thermoregulatory illnesses despite pre-Games concerns about heat and the presence of a heat wave during some outdoor events emphasises the effectiveness of mitigation measures, such as acclimatisation protocols and hydration strategies, which should continue to be prioritised in future hot-weather Games. These findings can inform policy changes, pre-Games planning and resource allocation to better address the specific health needs of both Olympic and Paralympic athletes.

Conclusions

Our study provides the first comprehensive documentation of injury and illness incidence at the Paris 2024 Summer Olympic and Paralympic Games. Paralympic Team USA athletes experienced a higher rate of injury and illness than their Olympic peers, driven in part by injuries sustained in non-sport environments and a rebound in respiratory illness after COVID-era restrictions were lifted. These findings underscore the need for targeted efforts for improving accessibility, stronger infection-prevention measures during travel and the Games and continued heat-mitigation protocols that appeared effective in Paris. Capturing session-level exposure data in future Games will help disentangle environmental from denominator effects and refine future prevention strategies.
