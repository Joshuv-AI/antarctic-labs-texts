Diagnostic Accuracy of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale for Youth (DASS-Y) Indonesian Version for Screening Internalizing Disorders Among Adolescents
Authors: Andra Kurnianto, Meita Dhamayanti, Rodman Tarigan Girsang, Eddy Fadlyana, Kusnandi Rusmil, Mei Neni Sitaresmi, Bintang Arroyantri Prananjaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs adolescent mental health issues are increasingly recognized as a public health concern, evaluating the accuracy of the DASS-Y is important for early detection and intervention. This study evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of the Indonesian version of the DASS-Y in screening internalizing disorders among adolescents aged 10–18 years.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional observational study across eight schools in Palembang, South Sumatra, Indonesia, from July to December 2025. A total of 274 adolescents were included. Diagnostic accuracy of the DASS-Y for screening internalizing disorders, such as major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, and Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), was evaluated against psychiatric clinical assessment based on the DSM-5 as the gold standard. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cutoff score. Diagnostic performance was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, likelihood ratios, and area under the curve (AUC).ResultsAmong 274 adolescents aged 10–18 years, a DASS-Y total score cutoff of ≥24 showed good sensitivity (0.83) and specificity (0.85), with excellent overall diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.93), indicating strong ability to discriminate between adolescents with and without internalizing disorders.ConclusionThe Indonesian version of the DASS-Y demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy for screening internalizing disorders among adolescents. The DASS-Y may be considered as a potential screening tool for adolescents aged 10–18 years in Indonesia. Further studies are needed to evaluate its implementation in routine practice.Keywords: adolescents, DASS-Y, diagnostic accuracy, internalizing disorders, screening

Background

Around the world, about 14.3% of adolescents aged 10–19 years suffer from mental health disorders, which account for 13% of the total global burden of disease.1 Many of these disorders begin in adolescence or early adulthood. Over one-third of adolescents meet criteria for a past-year mental health disorder, with about half experiencing severe impairment.2 Mental health disorders not only arise during adolescence but also persist and affect individuals throughout their later lives. Even more alarming is the rising trend in these issues; evaluating adolescents’ mental health is a crucial prerequisite for creating and applying prevention and intervention strategies.3 Internalizing problems affect up to 20% of American adolescents’ behavioral, social, and emotional functioning. According to the Indonesian National Adolescent Mental Health Survey (I-NAMHS), over a third of Indonesian adolescents (34.9%) have mental health problems, with anxiety being the most prevalent (26.7%) among 10–17-year-olds.4,5

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scales for Youth (DASS-Y) is one tool employed to screen for internalizing disorders in adolescents.6 This 21-item self-report scale includes three subscales. The depression subscale evaluates hopelessness, dysphoria, self-deprecation, low self-esteem, anhedonia, reduced positive affect, and lack of interest. The anxiety subscale gauges situational anxiety, autonomic arousal, musculoskeletal symptoms, and subjective anxious arousal. The stress subscale, meanwhile, assesses agitation, tension, irritability, difficulty relaxing, and negative affect. The DASS-Y was specifically designed for adolescents, employing simple, concise language to enhance comprehension and reduce translation challenges, grammatical/syntactic errors, and idiomatic equivalence issues. It demonstrates robust validity and reliability, with subscale and total scores exceeding 0.8 in countries including China, Australia, Serbia, Lebanon, Poland, and Indonesia.6–10 However, there is limited evidence regarding the diagnostic accuracy of the DASS-Y against psychiatric diagnosis in adolescents.

With adolescent mental health issues on the rise and limited evidence on the diagnostic accuracy of the DASS-Y, evaluating the Indonesian version of the DASS-Y’s diagnostic accuracy is vital for early detection and intervention. This study aimed to assess the diagnostic accuracy of the DASS-Y Indonesian version in internalizing disorders—such as major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, and PTSD—among Indonesian adolescents aged 10–18 years.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Setting

This multicenter, cross-sectional observational study was conducted across eight schools in Palembang, South Sumatra, Indonesia, from July to December 2025. Using a multistage random sampling design, schools (elementary, junior high, and senior high) were first selected via cluster random sampling, followed by stratified random sampling of students within each school. Initially, 355 adolescents were recruited for this validation and reliability study of the Indonesian version of the DASS-Y. The inclusion criteria were aged 10–18 years, fluency in Bahasa Indonesia, and the ability to follow all study procedures. Individuals were excluded if they declined to participate, failed to attend the clinical assessment, or could not be contacted. Consequently, 81 participants (22.8%) were excluded, leaving a final analytical sample of 274 adolescents (77.2%; Figure 1).

Study Flow.

The study protocol aligned with the Republic of Indonesia Law Number 23 of 2002 on child protection and the Regulation of the Minister of Health of the Republic of Indonesia Number 25 of 2014, which defines adolescence as ages 10 to 18 years. The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and has received ethical approval (No.139/UN6.KEP/EC/2025) from the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Padjadjaran. Written informed consent from parents and verbal assent from participants were obtained before recruitment.

Instruments

Developed by Szabo and Lovibond, the DASS-Y is a self-report measure that evaluates the severity of core symptoms in depression, anxiety, and stress. Each of its three seven-item subscales prompts respondents to rate statements from the past week on a 4-point scale (0 = not true; 3 = very true). Higher scores reflect more frequent symptoms. Subscale scores are calculated by summing relevant items (ranging from 0 to 21), while the total score sums all items (0 to 63).6,10

Previous research on the Indonesian DASS-Y version showed good-to-excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.94, item reliability = 0.98, person reliability = 0.84) and strong construct validity (explained variance = 50.1%, unexplained variance = 6.4%). No misfitting items emerged (infit = 0.78–1.41, outfit = 0.61–1.96, point-measure correlation = 0.47–0.72). Differential item functioning (DIF) analysis indicated negligible bias across demographic variables.10 The full Indonesian version of the DASS-Y is accessible on the official DASS website https://www2.psy.unsw.edu.au/dass/DASS-Y%20Indonesian%20Bahasa/Kurnianto/Kurnianto%20Indonesian_Bahasa_DASS-Y.htm.

To evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the DASS-Y as a screening tool for internalizing disorders in adolescents, psychiatrist-administered clinical assessments based on the DSM-5 were utilized as the gold standard. In addition to examining individual disorders—specifically panic disorder, social phobia, major depressive disorder, agoraphobia, PTSD, and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD)—we combined these conditions into a single composite “internalizing disorder” category. To ensure unbiased responses, the DASS-Y was completed by participants immediately before the clinical assessment.

Data Analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted using frequency distributions, means, and standard deviations. The prevalence of internalizing disorders was calculated across different sociodemographic variables for secondary exploratory analyses, and the chi-square test was used to compare the distributions of these characteristics (age group, sex, height-for-age, nutritional status, ethnicity, level of education, and type of school) between adolescents with internalizing disorders and those without. Concerning the regression analysis, univariable logistic regression was initially performed to examine the isolated association of each sociodemographic characteristic with internalizing disorders. Variables with p-values < 0.25 in the univariable analysis were eligible for inclusion in the subsequent multivariable model. Finally, multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate adjusted Odds Ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to identify the sociodemographic variables most strongly associated with internalizing disorders. For all final analyses, statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05.

Sensitivity was defined as the proportion of adolescents with disorders who tested positive (true-positive rate), and specificity as the proportion of those without disorders who tested negative (true-negative rate). Positive predictive value (PPV) represented the probability that a positive test indicates a true disorder, whereas negative predictive value (NPV) indicated the probability that a negative test confirms the absence of a disorder. Additionally, the positive likelihood ratio [LR(+)] was calculated as the probability of a positive test in individuals with the disorder, divided by the probability of a positive test in those without the disorder (ie, the false-positive rate). Conversely, the negative likelihood ratio [LR(−)] equals the probability of a negative test in individuals with the disorder (false-negative rate) divided by the probability of a negative test in those without the disorder (true-negative rate).11,12 DSM-5 defined internalizing disorders.13

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed, and overall diagnostic accuracy was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC). AUC values were interpreted as follows: 0.5–0.69 = poor; 0.70–0.79 = fair; 0.80–0.89 = good; ≥0.90 = excellent discrimination.14

To prioritize high sensitivity and avoid missing adolescents with true disorders, we selected the lowest DASS-Y cutoff score that yielded a sensitivity exceeding 0.80. The diagnostic performance was evaluated using the scale’s original clinical severity thresholds: mild (≥24), moderate (>30), severe (>40), and very severe (>47). All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 26.0.

Results

The majority of participants were female (65%), had normal height (92.3%) and nutritional status (81.8%), and identified their ethnicity as other (55.5%), including Javanese, Sundanese, Bataknese, Minangnese, and Lampungnese. The average age was 14.2 ± 2.7 years. Most attended public school (75.5%), with senior secondary being the most common level of education (40.1%). Clinical assessment classified 246 participants (89.8%) as normal and 28 (10.2%) as having internalizing disorders: 12 (4.4%) with major depressive disorder, 10 (3.6%) with anxiety disorder, and 6 (2.2%) with PTSD (Table 1).

Figure 2 illustrates the ROC curve for the DASS-Y total score, plotting sensitivity (true-positive rate) against 1-specificity (false-positive rate) across all cutoffs, with internalizing disorder as the criterion. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.90–0.96; p < 0.001). As shown in Table 4, the evaluated cutoffs followed the original clinical severity thresholds: ≥24 (mild), ≥30 (moderate), ≥40 (severe), and ≥47 (very severe). Among 274 adolescents aged 10–18 years, a cutoff of 24 yielded sensitivity of 0.83, specificity of 0.85, PPV of 0.41, and NPV of 0.98. At this threshold, the DASS-Y correctly identified 23 true positive cases with only 5 adolescents with internalizing disorders misclassified as false negatives. Raising the cutoff to 30 increased specificity to 0.93 and PPV to 0.56, correctly identifying 21 true-positive cases; however, sensitivity decreased to 0.77, NPV slightly decreased to 0.97, and 2 additional adolescents with internalizing disorders were missed as false negatives. A cutoff of 40 further increased specificity to 0.98 and PPV to 0.68, but sharply decreased sensitivity to 0.43 and NPV to 0.93, identifying only 19 true positive cases while leaving 17 adolescents as false negatives. Finally, a cutoff of 47 maximized specificity at 0.99 and PPV at 0.80, but identified only 8 true positive cases, as sensitivity dropped to 0.29, NPV decreased to 0.92, and 20 adolescents were misclassified as false negatives. Across these original clinical cutoffs, a clear trade-off was observed, with higher sensitivity achieved at the expense of lower specificity. Notably, the cutoff of 24 optimized screening utility by minimizing false negatives, with the highest NPV (0.98) despite a lower PPV (0.41) compared to higher thresholds.

Discussion

In this study, internalizing disorders affected 10.2% of adolescents in Palembang, Indonesia, comprising 4.4% major depressive disorder, 3.6% anxiety disorders, and 2.2% PTSD. This prevalence exceeded the 2021 I-NAMHS findings (5.2%) but was lower than in a US study (20%).4,5 Research consistently highlights the substantial burden of adolescent internalizing disorders like anxiety and depression. Barker et al reviewed 37 studies (19 on anxiety, 36 on depression), reporting pooled prevalences of 19.1% for anxiety and 14.3% for depression.5

In terms of sociodemographic factors, the risk of internalizing disorders was higher among middle/late adolescents, females, and secondary school adolescents. Multivariate analysis was used to assess the strongest sociodemographic factor associated with internalizing disorders among adolescents. This study found that level of education emerged as the strongest sociodemographic factor for internalizing disorder, with an odds ratio of 8.04 (95% CI 2.04–32.39). Academic achievement and internalizing disorders are closely linked in adolescents—a well-established connection. These disorders often drain energy and motivation, erode self-confidence, prompt school withdrawal, and hinder learning by impairing concentration.15,16 Consequently, adolescents with internalizing disorders typically show poorer school performance—such as lower grades and test scores—along with elevated dropout risk.17–19 The relationship may be bidirectional: poor academic performance can erode self-esteem, heighten stress from struggling to keep up, foster worries and hopelessness about the future, and elevate risk of internalizing disorders. School failure—such as early dropout, failing grades, unmet standards, or lack of qualifications for advancement—is particularly distressing due to the associated stigma and fears of long-term repercussions.18,20 Evidence suggests that schooling in many countries has grown more performance-driven, featuring heightened competition and expanded testing or grading. Higher levels of education are associated with greater distress due to competition.21,22

We assessed the DASS-Y’s utility as a screener for internalizing disorders in Indonesian adolescents aged 10–18 years. The optimal total score cutoff of ≥24 was selected based on ROC analysis prioritizing high sensitivity that indicates a positive screening result for internalizing disorders, yielding 0.83 sensitivity, 0.85 specificity, 0.41 PPV, 0.98 NPV, LR(+) 5.65, LR(−) 0.20, and AUC of 0.93 (excellent discrimination). This outperformed the DASS-21 cutoff of 44 for youth internalizing disorders (0.81 sensitivity, 0.62 specificity, 0.80 PPV, 0.64 NPV, 0.80 AUC).2 Although specificity and sensitivity were high, the relatively low PPV suggests a moderate rate of false positives in this population. This choice favors fewer false negatives over higher specificity (accepting more false positives), which we viewed as acceptable to avoid missing adolescents with true disorders. Hence, the DASS-Y appears ideally suited as a screener to determine if further diagnostic evaluation for adolescent internalizing disorders is needed. This aligns with growing evidence supporting its total score as a reliable indicator of overall psychological distress in youth. Total-score screening also outperforms subscale approaches, given the substantial overlap among internalizing disorders.2,6,23,24

In our study, other cutoffs (30, 40, 47) involved a sensitivity-specificity trade-off: higher specificity typically reduced sensitivity, and vice versa, all else equal. No single value is inherently superior; outcomes hinge on researcher or clinician preferences.

We deliberately selected a low DASS-Y total score cutoff to ensure high sensitivity, prioritizing the minimization of false negatives—adolescents with disorders that the screener missed. Raising the cutoff for greater specificity would reduce false positives but increase false negatives, the outcome we aimed to avoid (see Table 4, illustrating this sensitivity-specificity trade-off).

In clinical settings, the true disease status is typically unknown, making sensitivity and specificity less directly useful for assessing test validity. Clinicians instead focus on the probability of disease presence or absence based on screening results.25 We observed a high NPV (nine in ten true negatives) but modest PPV (two in five true positives) for screening internalizing disorders. Notably, predictive values depend heavily on disorder prevalence (among other factors)—higher prevalence boosts PPV, while lower prevalence elevates NPV.11

Strengths of the study are that (1) this is the first study to evaluate the DASS-Y’s accuracy as a screener for internalizing disorders in adolescents, and (2) we employed psychiatrist-administered clinical assessments based on DSM-5 as the gold standard. Study limitations include that (1) we did not compare DASS-Y performance against other broad-spectrum distress screeners for adolescents, such as the Pediatric Symptom Checklist 17 (PSC-17) or Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ),26,27 and (2) as this study was conducted only in Palembang, we recommend replicating it in other cities or provinces to ensure nationwide applicability, such as multi-site validation, rural-urban settings, and diverse socioeconomic populations.

Conclusions

The Indonesian version of the DASS-Y demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy for screening internalizing disorders among adolescents. A cutoff ≥24 showed high sensitivity and a strong negative predictive value, supporting its use as a first-stage screening tool. Positive results should be followed by clinical assessment, and further validation across other Indonesian regions is needed before wider implementation. The DASS-Y may be considered as a screening tool for adolescents aged 10–18 years in Indonesia, and periodic screening may be considered. The screening should facilitate earlier detection and intervention for internalizing disorders. Personnel administering the DASS-Y, including healthcare professionals and educators, should be well-trained to ensure accurate administration, with brief training in standardized procedures, score interpretation, and awareness of referral pathways. Further studies are needed to evaluate its implementation in routine practice.
