Adenoid cystic carcinoma of the thymus: a case report and literature review
Authors: Shota Awane, Kanji Otsubo, Moeka Tamura, Norifumi Kakizaki, Takayuki Hatakeyama, Hiroki Sakai, Takahiro Homma, Hideki Marushima, Koji Kojima, Hirotaka Koizumi, Junki Koike, Hisashi Saji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) of the thymus is extremely rare. Although thymic ACC is generally slow growing, complete surgical resection is essential due to its tendency for local invasion. Here, we report a case of primary thymic ACC successfully treated by complete resection using a single-port subxiphoid thoracoscopic approach. A 63-year-old asymptomatic male patient was referred after an anterior mediastinal mass was incidentally detected during a routine health screening. Imaging revealed a small, well-circumscribed tumor without evidence of invasion. At 2.2 cm, the lesion was among the smallest thymic ACCs reported to date. Thymoma was suspected and single-port subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy was performed. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful. The histopathological and immunohistochemical findings were consistent with ACC, and no other primary lesion was identified. Complete resection was achieved with minimally invasive thymectomy. This case illustrates that thymic ACC may rarely present as a small, early-stage tumor indistinguishable from thymoma, and it highlights the importance of complete resection with negative margins and long-term surveillance given the potential for delayed recurrence.

Introduction

Primary adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) arising in the thymus is an exceptionally uncommon neoplasm with only sporadic cases described to date [1–4]. Although thymic ACC is typically slow-growing, it is characterized by a tendency for local invasion and the potential for delayed recurrence or distant metastasis. Accordingly, complete surgical resection with negative margins is the cornerstone of treatment. However, due to the limited number of reported cases, the optimal operative strategy has not been clearly described.

In recent years, surgical techniques for minimally invasive thymectomy have advanced substantially. The subxiphoid approach has gained particular attention because it provides a direct midline view of the anterior mediastinum and facilitates bilateral visualization of the phrenic nerves [5–7]. Several reports suggest that this approach offers favorable perioperative outcomes and adequate oncological clearance in selected thymic epithelial tumors [5]. Nevertheless, evidence supporting its oncological validity for rare primary thymic malignancies like ACC remains limited.

Here, we report a case of primary thymic ACC successfully managed with complete resection via a single-port subxiphoid thoracoscopic approach. Unlike most reported cases, which present as large, locally advanced masses, the present tumor was small and detected at an early stage. We describe the diagnostic work-up, operative course, and pathological findings, and review the literature to discuss the clinical characteristics of this rare entity and the oncological considerations of minimally invasive resection.

Case report

A 63-year-old asymptomatic male patient was referred to our department after an anterior mediastinal mass was incidentally detected on a noncontrast computed tomography (CT) scan performed as part of a routine health screening at another hospital. As subsequent contrast-enhanced CT suggested a thymoma, he was referred to our institution for further management. The patient had no significant medical history. Serum tumor marker levels were within normal limits and pulmonary function testing revealed no abnormalities. The patient had no symptoms suggestive of myasthenia gravis, and serum anti-acetylcholine receptor antibodies were negative. Chest radiography at the initial visit was unremarkable.

Contrast-enhanced CT and three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction revealed a well-circumscribed solid nodule measuring 2.2 × 2.0 cm located anterior to the ascending aorta in the anterior mediastinum (Fig. 1a and b). No lymphadenopathy or other abnormal findings were observed. Positron emission tomography (PET) indicated fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in the lesion, with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 3.8 (Fig. 1c). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a well-defined mass with contrast enhancement and a slightly high signal intensity on T2-weighted images (Fig. 2a and b). Given these findings, and in the absence of any radiologic evidence of invasion of adjacent structures, thymoma was considered the most likely diagnosis.

Based on this preoperative diagnosis, subxiphoid single-port thoracoscopic thymectomy was performed. Because the lesion was a well-circumscribed, resectable anterior mediastinal mass strongly suspected to be a thymic epithelial tumor, a preoperative biopsy was not performed, in accordance with current guidance for resectable suspected thymoma; total thymectomy with resection of the tumor and the entire thymus was carried out, rather than the extended thymectomy reserved for cases associated with myasthenia gravis. The operative time was 106 min. Intraoperative blood loss was minimal. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful. The chest drain was removed on postoperative day (POD) 1, and the patient was discharged on POD 7 without complications. At the most recent follow-up, approximately 7 months postoperatively, the patient was alive and recurrence-free, with no clinical or radiologic evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis. Given the recognized risk of late recurrence in ACC, long-term surveillance with periodic contrast-enhanced CT is planned.

Histopathological examination supported ACC. Gross examination showed a well-demarcated tumor measuring 22 × 20 × 18 mm with focal intratumoral hemorrhage (Fig. 3a). Low-power microscopic examination demonstrated a solid tumor surrounded by a thick fibrous capsule within normal thymic tissue (Fig. 3b). At higher magnification, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed a characteristic cribriform architecture comprising cells with a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio (Fig. 3c). Immunohistochemically, the luminal epithelial cells were predominantly positive for cytokeratin 7 (CK7) (Fig. 4a), whereas the abluminal cells surrounding the tubular structures were positive for p63 (Fig. 4b). Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining demonstrated PAS-positive material within the glandular lumina (Fig. 4c), whereas Alcian blue staining was negative (Fig. 4d). Overall, the findings were consistent with ACC.

Given the extreme rarity of primary thymic ACC, the diagnosis was confirmed by comprehensive pathological evaluation and exclusion of other primary sites. Postoperative contrast-enhanced CT and otolaryngologic assessment revealed no evidence of primary tumors in the lungs or salivary glands, leading to a definitive diagnosis of primary thymic ACC.

Discussion

Primary thymic ACC is exceedingly rare and its clinicopathological characteristics have been largely extrapolated from salivary gland ACC. Based on previous reports (Table 1), thymic ACC predominantly affects middle-aged to elderly individuals and shows a marked male predominance. Tumor size at diagnosis varies widely. Many reported tumors are relatively large, suggesting that thymic ACC is often detected at an advanced stage [1, 4, 7]. In contrast, the present tumor measured only 2.2 cm and was detected incidentally in an asymptomatic patient at an early stage. To our knowledge it is among the smallest thymic ACCs reported, representing a presentation rarely captured in the existing literature and offering an opportunity to discuss the management of early, resectable thymic ACC rather than the advanced disease that dominates prior reports.

Despite its generally slow-growing nature, thymic ACC is characterized by aggressive biological behavior including local invasion, perineural spread, and the potential for delayed distant metastasis [1–3]. Although distant metastasis was only reported in one prior case, the median follow-up period in the literature is relatively short. The low incidence of metastasis should thus be interpreted with caution and long-term surveillance remains essential even after complete resection. By analogy with salivary gland ACC, perineural invasion is a hallmark of the tumor and may allow microscopic extension well beyond the macroscopic margin, so histologically negative margins are the principal surgical objective and intraoperative assessment of resection adequacy is important; postoperative radiotherapy has been employed in selected cases with close or positive margins. The natural history is also characterized by very late recurrence and distant metastasis, with survival continuing to decline beyond ten years, which justifies surveillance extending well past the conventional five-year period [12].

Preoperative diagnosis of thymic ACC is generally not feasible, because its clinical and imaging features overlap with those of thymoma and other thymic epithelial tumors. For a well-circumscribed, resectable anterior mediastinal mass strongly suspected to be a thymic epithelial tumor, current guidance recommends upfront complete resection without preoperative biopsy, both to avoid capsular disruption with potential pleural seeding and because biopsy rarely alters management [8]. In the present case, the low FDG uptake (SUVmax 3.8) further argued against lymphoma, for which biopsy would have been required, and supported a direct surgical approach [9]. MRI was used selectively to characterize the lesion and to exclude invasion, rather than as a routine investigation for every mediastinal mass. Consequently, the ACC histology was an unexpected postoperative finding. The value of this report therefore lies not in any ACC-specific preoperative strategy, but in documenting that this rare and potentially aggressive tumor can present at an early, resectable stage and be managed with complete resection.

Surgical resection is the mainstay treatment for thymic ACC. In most reported cases, surgery alone was performed. The role of adjuvant therapy remains unclear due to the rarity of this disease. Nevertheless, these findings support that complete resection with negative margins is the most important prognostic factor.

In recent years, minimally invasive thymectomy has been increasingly adopted for thymic epithelial tumors [5, 6]. However, most previously reported cases of thymic ACC were treated by open surgery. In the present case, complete resection was achieved using a single-port subxiphoid thoracoscopic approach, which provided a midline view of the anterior mediastinum and allowed identification of both phrenic nerves with minimal invasiveness. For early-stage thymic epithelial tumors, minimally invasive thymectomy—whether transthoracic VATS, robotic, or subxiphoid—has been reported to achieve oncological outcomes, including R0 resection and short-term recurrence-free survival, comparable to those of open surgery while reducing perioperative morbidity [10]. Comparative studies of the subxiphoid approach have reported complete-resection rates similar to those of intercostal VATS and median sternotomy in selected patients, with the subxiphoid route providing direct midline exposure and lower early postoperative pain [11].

Given the oncological characteristics of thymic ACC, indications for minimally invasive surgery should be carefully considered. Single-port thoracoscopic thymectomy may offer adequate oncological clearance in selected patients with small, well-circumscribed tumors without evidence of invasion. To our knowledge, reports of thymic ACC resected using a single-port approach are extremely limited. In the present case, the subxiphoid route was chosen over a lateral VATS or open approach because the tumor was small, midline, and showed no radiologic evidence of invasion: the anterior subxiphoid view provides direct, symmetrical access to the prevascular space and simultaneous visualization of both phrenic nerves, facilitating complete thymic clearance through a single incision while avoiding rib spreading. Its limitations, however, are relevant to malignant tumors: the working space is confined, control of bleeding from mediastinal vessels can be difficult, and exposure of laterally extending or invasive disease is restricted. For tumors with suspected capsular breach, vascular or pericardial invasion, or larger size, a lateral VATS, robotic, or open approach with the capacity for en-bloc resection and reconstruction may be more appropriate, and the operative strategy should therefore be individualized to tumor size, location, and the likelihood of invasion rather than applied uniformly.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This is a single case with limited follow-up, and no firm conclusion can be drawn regarding the oncological adequacy of a minimally invasive or single-port approach specifically for thymic ACC. The subxiphoid route was selected for a small, non-invasive mass on the basis of established practice for thymic epithelial tumors and was not chosen for the ACC histology, which was unknown preoperatively. Because ACC of the salivary glands is characterized by perineural invasion and late recurrence, with survival continuing to decline beyond 10 years, prolonged surveillance is warranted, and the favorable early course observed here should not be interpreted as evidence of cure [12].

In conclusion, this case suggests that favorable outcomes can be achieved with early detection and complete surgical resection of thymic ACC, a rare tumor with potentially aggressive long-term behavior. In selected patients with small, non-invasive tumors, minimally invasive thymectomy is a reasonable option for achieving complete resection, although the approach was not specific to the ACC histology; further accumulation of cases with long-term follow-up is required to define the oncological role of minimally invasive resection and the optimal management of this rare malignancy.
