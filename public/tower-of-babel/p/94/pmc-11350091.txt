Radiation sensor based on a 1D-periodic structure infiltrated by (B-co-MP) a conjugated copolymer
Authors: Shimaa El-Shemy, R. Semeda, M. Mobarak, M. F. Eissa, Fatma A. Sayed, Ali S. Alshomrany, Arafa H. Aly
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this study, a novel gamma-ray radiation sensor has been developed depending on a 1D photonic crystal (1D-PhC). Based on porous silicon (PSi) layer that has been penetrated by a conjugated copolymer (B-co-MP) which consists of BEHP-PPV and MEH-PPV, with a fractional ratio of 60:40. The suggested method for the development of the dosimeter is based on the shift of photonic band-gap to shorter wavelengths, where exposure to gamma-ray radiation at doses ranging from 0 to 20 kGy alters the refractive index of the (B-co-MP) copolymer. The fitted experimental data, the equation of Bruggeman effective medium, and the transfer matrix method (TMM) are the main axes in the framework of the current theoretical approach. The collected data shows that, within the visible range, the suggested sensor's sensitivity (224 nm/RIU) is high and stable over a 0-20 kGy applied-dose range. Also, we compared these results with previous research.

Introduction

Many physico-chemical changes are caused in materials that are exposed to environmental radiation, depending on the intensity of the radiation. Based on the kind of radiation, radiation dosage, exposure duration, material properties, ambient factors, etc., different materials are affected by radiation to differing degrees1,2. Because of the widespread use of radiation in numerous applications, many studies are focused on radiation dosimetry3,4.

Any device capable of producing an output value representing the mean dosage absorbed of ionizing radiation deposited in its sensitive volume is called a dosimeter3,5. What is required is an easy-to-use and set-up system that can quickly and accurately detect radiation at long distances. It needs to be economical and long-lasting as well.

In the last few decades, polymers with exceptional surface and bulk properties have gained prominence because of their low weight, low cost, easy processing ability, and ease of producing thick and thin samples. The optical and electrical properties of conjugated polymers, including the poly(phenylene vinylene) (PPV) group, have drawn interest because of their potential uses in a variety of fields6,7.

B-co-MP is a conjugated copolymer composed of two components: BEHP-PPV and MEH-PPV. These components in B-co-MP have a fractional ratio of 60:40. The abbreviation for 2-[2,5-bis(2-ethylhexyloxy)phenyl]-1,4-phenylenevinylene is BEHP-PPVV. MEH-PPV refers to 1,4-phenylenevinylene-2-methoxy-5-(2-ethylhexyloxy)-1. The optical and structural properties of B-co-MP are influenced by these components, making it an excellent material for studying its behavior under diverse conditions, such as gamma irradiation8. For this reason, the B-co-MP shows potential utility in various applications, including optoelectronic devices and biomedical applications9–11. In contrast, even at larger concentrations, the (B-co-MP) conjugated polymers emit laser light and show robust absorption and emission, a notable Stokes shift, high luminescence, and a fluorescence quantum yield. Conversely, the ionizing radiation method is frequently employed to modify the optical and structural characteristics of polymers exposed to radiation12. Ref.9 has used gamma irradiation to study the impact of radiation on the optical and chemical properties of B-co-MP. The irradiation led to blue-shifted absorption, fluorescence, and ASE spectra, indicating changes in the energy gap and carbon atom content. These modifications were ascribed to chain scission brought on by gamma irradiation and/or conformational changes in the chain.

Interactions with high-energy radiation, such as X-rays, gamma rays, etc., cause chain scission in polymeric materials, which lowers molecular weight and induces intermolecular cross-linking. In addition to generating free radicals and reactive oxygen species (ROS), this also results in a rise in molecular weight through the emission of atoms, molecules, and molecular fragments. All of this ultimately results in brittleness, hardness, colour change, and deterioration and other undesirable characteristics. Because of these structural changes, It may be possible to improve the optical, thermal, mechanical, dielectric, and conductivity characteristics of composite materials subjected to radiation1,13. To comprehend the special qualities of doped polymer composite materials, numerous scientists have been studying the effects of radiation on these materials14–16.

Based on radiation effects, four categories can be used to categorise optical techniques for radiation dose monitoring. The radiation-induced luminescence and thermoluminescence effect are at the heart of the first type15,17,18. The radiation-induced attenuation (RIA) effect is used in the second kind19. Changing the frequency of Brillouin scattering in dispersed optical fibers is the third technique20. Bragg gratings and PhC fibers serve as the foundation for the last type3.

Recently, PhCs have gained popularity in a wide range of applications, including optical fibers, sensors, and biomedical devices21–23. Photonic band gaps (PBGs) are a critical and distinguishing property of PhCs that prevent light from propagating at specific frequency intervals caused by destructive interference of the PhC's constituent materials. Examine how well a (1D-PhC) based sensor detects blood plasma and cancer cells24.

In this paper, a 1D-PhC radiation sensor based on PSi layers penetrated by (B-co-MP) conjugated copolymer has been developed. Additionally, we investigated the behavior of this structure under different gamma-ray dosages produced by a 60Co- radioactive source. The outcomes also show how the PSi-layer enhanced the (B-co-MP) conjugated copolymer's optical characteristics and how our structure is impacted by PSi-layer porosity. The effect of gamma-ray dosages ranging from 0 to 20 kGy on our structure is then investigated. Finally, we talk about how the two layers' thicknesses impact our structure and how much they change this radiation dosimeter sensitivity. The obtained results of the used B-co-MP copolymer show a higher radiation sensitivity (225 nm/RIU) compared with other researchers (Fatma et al., 195 nm/RIU)25.

This section's first point addresses how radiation impacts our dosimeter size and the rationale behind our material selection. Equipment operating in radiation-prone areas must be protected against radiation harm. During Gamma-radiation, External sources of pressure, temperature, or tension can affect radiation dosimeters. Si material is used because, at (Dγ = 1000 kGy), its refractive index changes below (5 × 10–5). Furthermore, at (Dγ = 66.5 kGy, and 32 °C), the thermo-optic coefficient of Si is equal (2.3 × 10–4)3,32,33. In addition, the highest rise in temperature of Si owing to the radiation impact is equivalent to (3.2 °C). Furthermore, the thermal expansion coefficient of Si is relatively minimal, equaling (2.6 × 10–6)34,35. As a result, the change the refractive index of Si produced by radiation processes can be ignored across a -dose range of 0 to 20 kGy. Furthermore, radiation's effect on the geometrical dimensions (thermal expansion) of the suggested sensor (radiation dosimeter) is overlooked. Radiation-induced thermal strain can thus be neglected36. To summarize, we believe the proposed structure is suitable for monitoring gamma rays without destroying them.

Conclusion

Our very sensitive 1D-PhC radiation dosimeter is built on PSi-layers with (B-co-MP) conjugated copolymers infiltrated into them. The theoretical analysis is investigated by the PSi-layer Bruggeman's effective medium equation and the transfer matrix method for estimating the optical characteristics of the dosimeter structure. The numerical outcomes showed that the PSi-layers improved the (B-co-MP) conjugated copolymer's refractive index. Additionally, they demonstrated how the PBG shifts when subjected to gamma radiation, which is the basis for the radiation dosimeter's theory. Physically, this change results from the (B-co-MP) copolymer's refractive index varying with the γ-doses (measured in kGy units). As a result, when exposed to γ-doses, PSi-layers' refractive index changes. When gamma-ray dosages ranging from 0 to 20 kGy are applied to the design, the radiation dosimeter achieves a high sensitivity of 224 nm/RIU at Dγ= 5 kGy, and an incidence angle of 75°, with excellent stability across the range of visible wavelengths. To attain the best sensitivity, the effects of the PSi layer thickness and incident angle are also researched. Our ultimate goal is to employ this radiation dosimeter as a radiation-resistant FBG for applications involving temperature and strain monitoring in nuclear settings.
