The consultant’s guide: evidence-based digital transformation framework for healthcare institutions in developing and resource-constrained economies
Authors: Uchechukwu Solomon Onyeabor, Okechukwu Onwuasoigwe, Wilfred Okwudili Okenwa, Omolade Ayoola Lasebikan, Thorsten Schaaf, Niels Pinkwart, Felix Balzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital transformation is increasingly reshaping healthcare delivery worldwide; however, the adoption of digital health systems in developing and resource-constrained economies remains constrained by infrastructural limitations, poor alignment with clinical workflows, inadequate user involvement, and resistance to organizational change. Many digital healthcare initiatives in these settings continue to experience implementation and sustainability challenges, partly due to the absence of context-specific and evidence-based transformation models. Consequently, this study aimed to develop a customized evidence-based digital transformation framework to support the sustainable adoption of digital healthcare systems within tertiary and university teaching hospitals in developing economies.MethodsThis study emerged from a prolonged mixed-methods doctoral research project conducted across three tertiary healthcare institutions in southeastern Nigeria: the University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital (UNTH), ESUT Teaching Hospital (ESUTTH), and the National Orthopaedic Hospital Enugu (NOHE). The research involved close to 600 stakeholders, including doctors, nurses, medical records officers, patients, and clinical consultants. Data were collected through structured questionnaires, qualitative interviews, participatory co-design interactions, observational methods, usability testing, and iterative system evaluations. The study adopted an agile human-centered software engineering and participatory development approach to guide the design, implementation, and evaluation of the proposed TUMERIC digital healthcare support system and the resulting transformation framework.ResultsThe findings demonstrated generally positive institutional readiness toward digital transformation, with over 70% of participating healthcare professionals indicating support for future digital initiatives within their institutions. Usability evaluations of the implemented TUMERIC system also demonstrated high levels of user acceptance and perceived usability among clinicians and patients. Qualitative findings further highlighted the importance of aligning digital systems with existing clinical workflows, involving end users throughout the development lifecycle, and addressing infrastructural and organizational constraints during implementation. These findings informed the development of the proposed evidence-based digital transformation framework tailored to resource-constrained healthcare environments.ConclusionThe study demonstrates that agile human-centered and participatory design approaches can support the development and adoption of context-appropriate digital healthcare systems in developing economies. The proposed framework provides an empirically informed model that may assist healthcare institutions and policymakers in planning and implementing more sustainable and user-aligned digital transformation initiatives within tertiary healthcare settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03713-9.Keywords: Framework, Digital transformation, Developing countries, Healthcare, Digital adoption, Human-centered software engineering, Digital adoption, Design thinking, Agile

Background

We are currently in a critical era, and several institutions are deploying strategies, procedures, and protocols, including a digital transformation campaign to sustain relevance, competitiveness, and efficiency [1]. The current wave of the COVID-19 global pandemic has further brought to the fore the urgency of the moment [2]. And as a result, the pressure on our healthcare system and institutions has suddenly become so massive that many of these healthcare providers barely cope with the sudden upsurge of demand on their facilities; some are already overwhelmed. So there is a need to transform! There is an urgent need to change the approach to healthcare delivery. There is a need for improved processes. There is a need to incorporate new measures, tools, and technologies that support service optimization and engender efficiency. By way of definition, digital transformation can be defined as a comprehensive process that requires organizations to rethink how they operate and interact with their customers. It involves the integration of digital technology into all areas of the business, from operations and marketing to customer service and product development [3]. Digital transformation goes beyond just the implementation of new technology; it also requires a shift in culture and mindset. Organizations must be willing to challenge the status quo, experiment, and accept failure as part of the learning process [3]. Companies need to be open to new ideas and methods and embrace change as an opportunity to innovate and create value for their customers. Digital transformation in healthcare

Healthcare information technology is daily transforming the way and manner in which healthcare is administered to patients [5]. Digital transformation in the healthcare context can be considered the incorporation of digital and often emerging technologies in all aspects of healthcare service delivery. Digital technology has shown strong efficacy in improving the overall quality of health service delivery, ranging from the clinical workflow to patient health record management and the overall clinician and patient digital experience [6]. Therefore, digital transformation is an incredibly powerful tool for healthcare organizations, allowing them to revolutionize their approach to patient care, business strategy, and organizational processes [7]. By leveraging the latest advancements in information technology, healthcare organizations can harness the power of telehealth, predictive analytics, and artificial intelligence (AI) to gain a competitive edge [8]. Telehealth, for example, allows healthcare organizations to provide remote patient care and reduce the need for physical visits to the doctor’s office [9]. Predictive analytics, meanwhile, can be used to analyze historical patient data and predict future trends in healthcare, enabling organizations to proactively respond to the needs of patients before they even arise [10]. AI-powered technologies can be used to automate tedious and repetitive tasks, freeing up healthcare professionals to focus on delivering better care to their patients [11, 12]. With the right combination of technologies, healthcare organizations can unlock their full potential and revolutionize the way they deliver care [13]. As a result, countries around the world, like the United States of America, are investing huge resources in the form of billions of dollars and efforts towards the implementation of electronic health records systems [14]. In fact, healthcare policymakers look up to digital technology as a tool that could guarantee healthcare systems’ safety and ensure more accessible and affordable healthcare opportunities for the teeming population [15]. Digital transformation frameworks and benefits

The Digital Transformation Framework is an essential tool deployed by modern businesses to achieve a successful and sustainable digital transformation campaign. The Digital Transformation Framework can be defined as an approach or methodology that enables organisations to leverage digital technologies and processes to enhance their products, services, and customer experiences [28]. A framework can be thought of as a roadmap for digital transformation, providing a clear path to success and enabling the implementation of technology to help achieve the desired goals [29]. It can be used to create an actionable strategy that outlines the steps needed to realise the desired outcome, such as which technologies should be used, how they should be used, and when they should be implemented [29]. Again, digital transformation frameworks provide businesses with a comprehensive roadmap of transformation strategies that are specifically tailored to their unique needs and goals [30]. By taking the guesswork out of the process, these frameworks offer businesses a clear path to success, outlining the steps they need to take in order to achieve their desired outcomes [30]. It helps to identify the areas for improvement and develop an action plan for implementing the necessary changes [30]. It also helps to identify the areas where the organisation needs to focus to become more efficient and competitive [30]. Furthermore, the digital transformation framework provides a clear roadmap for how to best utilise technology to increase productivity and efficiency [31]. Additionally, the digital transformation framework is a comprehensive guide to help organisations effectively develop their capabilities during the digital transformation process. It provides a holistic approach to digital transformation by outlining the different stages of the process and the essential elements required for successful implementation [32]. The framework helps organisations identify their digital objectives, assess their current capabilities, and identify areas for improvement. It also provides guidance on how to develop and maintain the necessary infrastructure, resources, and processes needed to drive digital transformation [32]. Additionally, the framework offers guidance on how to develop strategies and initiatives to ensure the successful integration of digital solutions into the organisation’s overall business operations [32]. Ultimately, the framework is designed to help organisations maximise the potential of digital transformation and ensure it is a successful endeavour.

Now the proposed evidence-based digital transformation framework was designed to support context-sensitive and sustainable digital adoption in healthcare settings, particularly within the context of Nigerian teaching hospitals. This framework aims to address the unique challenges faced by these institutions, such as resource constraints and the need for alignment with existing clinical workflows, by incorporating agile and human-centered design thinking principles. By providing a structured approach that involves all relevant stakeholders and emphasizes practical experience, the study contributes significantly to filling the knowledge gaps in the literature regarding digital health adoption, offering a tailored guide that reflects the realities of healthcare delivery in developing economies and with the potential to support improvements in healthcare delivery processes.

This study is positioned as an evidence-based framework development study grounded in mixed-methods empirical research and agile human-centered digital health system design. The proposed framework emerged from multiple interconnected research activities conducted across tertiary healthcare institutions in Nigeria, including organizational readiness assessments, clinician usability evaluations, qualitative investigations of implementation barriers, participatory requirements gathering, iterative system prototyping, and usability testing of the developed TUMERIC platform. The study therefore integrates empirical healthcare systems research with human-centered digital health intervention design to inform the development of a context-sensitive healthcare digital transformation framework for resource-constrained environments.

Methods

Research setting

This research was conducted across three tertiary healthcare institutions in southeastern Nigeria: the University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital (UNTH), Ituku-Ozalla; the ESUT Teaching Hospital (ESUTTH), Enugu; and the National Orthopaedic Hospital Enugu (NOHE). These institutions were selected because they represent major tertiary and referral healthcare centres within the region and provide diverse clinical environments for investigating healthcare digital transformation within resource-constrained settings.

The University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital (UNTH) serves as the primary teaching hospital for the University of Nigeria College of Medicine and has existed for over 60 years. Between 1991 and 2000, it was designated a World Health Organization (WHO) Centre for Innovation in Medical Education. Additionally, in 1986, the Federal Government of Nigeria recognized UNTH as a Centre of Excellence in Cardiothoracic Surgery. The hospital currently operates nine training programs and schools, encompassing 41 departments and three outposts, all within a 500-bed capacity. UNTH provides specialized and referral healthcare services across multiple disciplines, including family medicine, radiomedicine, orthopaedic and trauma surgery, gynaecology, cardiology, pharmacy, and ophthalmology, among others [33].

The ESUT Teaching Hospital (ESUTTH) originated in the 1930s and was formally upgraded to a state university teaching hospital in 2006. The hospital has an estimated capacity of 350–400 beds and serves as both a tertiary healthcare provider and a teaching institution for the ESUT College of Medicine. ESUTTH provides healthcare services across general medicine, obstetrics, paediatrics, surgery, physiotherapy, radiography, pharmacy, and laboratory diagnostics [34].

The National Orthopaedic Hospital Enugu (NOHE) was established by an Act of the National Assembly of Nigeria in 1979 and specializes in orthopaedic surgery, trauma surgery, burns and plastic surgery, physiotherapy, radiology, anaesthesia, and rehabilitation services. The institution also serves as a residency training centre for healthcare professionals and is one of Nigeria’s major specialized orthopaedic hospitals [35].

The broader research activities that informed the development of the proposed framework were conducted over several years as part of a doctoral research project investigating healthcare digital transformation and agile human-centered software engineering approaches within tertiary healthcare systems in developing economies.

The broader research investigated the role of agile human-centered software engineering approaches in supporting healthcare digital transformation initiatives within tertiary healthcare institutions in Nigeria. Throughout the study, agile software development principles and human-centered design thinking paradigms guided the iterative design, implementation, and evaluation of the proposed cloud-based and voice-enabled healthcare support system. The primary contribution of the study was the development of an empirically informed digital transformation framework grounded in iterative stakeholder engagement, organizational assessment, system design activities, and usability evaluation within real-world tertiary healthcare settings.

Study population and sampling procedure

The study population comprised clinicians, healthcare administrators, patients, and medical records personnel working within the selected tertiary healthcare institutions. Participants included doctors, nurses, medical record officers, consultants, and patients who had direct interaction with existing or newly implemented digital healthcare systems within the participating hospitals.

Participants were selected using purposive and convenience sampling approaches depending on the specific phase of the research. Inclusion criteria included healthcare professionals actively involved in clinical workflows within the selected hospitals, clinicians with direct experience using existing electronic health record (EHR) or digital systems, stakeholders involved in healthcare documentation or patient management processes, and patients who interacted with the developed TUMERIC system during the usability evaluation phase. Individuals who had no interaction with digital healthcare systems or who declined participation were excluded from the study.

Quantitative data collection

The first quantitative phase involved a survey assessing organizational readiness for digital transformation across the three tertiary healthcare institutions. The survey instrument was adapted from Paré’s organizational readiness model [36] and employed a 5-point Likert scale. The instrument consisted of 39 indicator variables designed to evaluate nine latent organizational dimensions, including vision clarity, change appropriateness, change efficacy, top management support, organizational flexibility, collective self-efficacy, organizational conflicts and politics, organizational history of change, and the presence of effective champions. A total of 580 healthcare professionals participated in this phase of the study.

The second quantitative phase examined clinicians’ experiences with existing digital healthcare systems. A structured 20-item questionnaire based on a 5-point Likert scale was administered to clinicians, including doctors, nurses, and medical record officers. The questionnaire focused on system usability, workflow compatibility, user satisfaction, documentation burden, interface design, and perceived technical limitations associated with the existing systems. A total of 131 clinicians participated in this evaluation.

Questionnaire development and validation

The adapted usability evaluation questionnaire underwent expert review involving clinicians, healthcare researchers, and system development stakeholders to ensure contextual relevance and content validity. In addition, pilot testing was conducted prior to deployment to refine ambiguous items and improve clarity. The iterative adaptation process was undertaken to ensure that the evaluation instrument adequately reflected the operational realities and workflow requirements of the participating tertiary healthcare institutions.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the ethical review committees of the participating hospitals, ensuring compliance with institutional and research ethics requirements. Copies of the questionnaires and interview guides were uploaded as supplementary materials alongside the manuscript submission.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data were collected through structured interviews, direct observations, and focus group discussions. The focus group discussions were conducted with purposively selected stakeholders drawn from the participating tertiary healthcare institutions. Participants included doctors, nurses, medical records officers, and ICT personnel who possessed direct experience with existing healthcare information systems and digital transformation activities. The focus group discussions followed a semi-structured format guided by predetermined questions relating to digital transformation readiness, implementation challenges, workflow processes, system usability, stakeholder expectations, and perceived barriers to adoption. The discussions also formed part of the requirements engineering process used to support the design, modelling, and subsequent development of the TUMERIC system. The discussions were documented through detailed field notes and subsequently incorporated into the qualitative thematic analysis alongside interview responses, workflow observations, and open-ended survey responses. Meanwhile, the structured interviews employed open-ended questions designed to explore clinicians’ perceptions regarding barriers affecting healthcare digital transformation implementation within tertiary healthcare institutions. Approximately 400 qualitative responses were obtained from participating clinicians across the three hospitals. The interviews explored issues relating to infrastructural challenges, organizational resistance, workflow disruptions, user experience concerns, training limitations, and implementation barriers associated with digital healthcare initiatives.

Direct observational methods were also employed to examine clinicians’ interactions with existing systems and workflows within routine clinical environments. The observations focused on system usability challenges, workflow inefficiencies, clinician frustrations, documentation practices, and user-system interactions. These observations provided contextual insights that informed the subsequent system design process.

Additionally, a focus group consisting of three senior consultant orthopaedic surgeons and departmental heads was established to support participatory requirements gathering for the proposed digital healthcare intervention system. The focus group sessions facilitated discussions relating to clinical workflow requirements, documentation challenges, communication needs, collaborative care processes, and essential system functionalities required within tertiary healthcare environments. The findings from these sessions informed the development of the TUMERIC cloud- and voice-enabled clinical support system.

System development and iterative evaluation

The proposed digital healthcare support system was modelled using Unified Modeling Language (UML) techniques and developed iteratively following agile and human-centered software engineering principles. Repeated prototype evaluations and stakeholder feedback sessions were conducted throughout the development lifecycle to improve usability, workflow alignment, and overall user experience. The resulting system incorporated cloud-based and voice-enabled functionalities intended to support collaborative clinical workflows and healthcare documentation within resource-constrained healthcare settings.

System usability evaluation

Finally, the completed system underwent another round of usability evaluation using an adapted usability evaluation questionnaire inspired by usability assessment concepts derived from the System Usability Scale (SUS) [37–39]. The instrument was modified to reflect context-specific clinical workflow requirements, perceived usability dimensions, and user interaction characteristics relevant to the developed cloud- and voice-enabled healthcare system. The questionnaire employed a 5-point Likert scale to assess perceived ease of use, workflow compatibility, learnability, interface usability, and overall user satisfaction among clinicians and patients who interacted with the developed system. Because the instrument was adapted for contextual applicability, the findings were interpreted descriptively rather than as standard SUS scores. A total of 20 patients participated in the TUMERIC usability assessment reported in this study. The clinician usability evaluation involved 75 healthcare professionals (30 doctors, 30 nurses, and 15 medical record officers) and has been reported elsewhere.

Data analysis

Quantitative data obtained from the structured questionnaires and System Usability Scale evaluations were analyzed using descriptive statistical techniques, including frequency distributions, percentages, means, and comparative analyses across participant categories and hospital sites. Likert-scale responses were summarized using mean scores to evaluate organizational readiness, user experience, and perceived system usability.

Qualitative data obtained from interviews, observational activities, focus group discussions, and open-ended responses were analyzed using thematic analysis techniques. The qualitative responses were reviewed iteratively to identify recurring patterns, implementation concerns, workflow challenges, infrastructural barriers, usability issues, stakeholder expectations, and organizational factors influencing healthcare digital transformation initiatives.

The coding process involved the identification of preliminary categories and recurring concepts across the qualitative datasets, which were subsequently refined into broader thematic groupings aligned with the objectives of the study. The qualitative analysis focused primarily on identifying themes capable of informing the development of the proposed evidence-based digital transformation framework. To improve analytical consistency and contextual reliability, findings from interviews, observations, participatory engagements, and usability evaluations were triangulated during the synthesis process. Discrepancies in interpretation were resolved through repeated reviews of the qualitative responses and iterative discussions among the research team and participating clinical stakeholders.

Framework development procedure

The proposed evidence-based healthcare digital transformation framework was developed through the synthesis of findings obtained from multiple interconnected empirical research activities conducted across the selected tertiary healthcare institutions. The framework development process followed an iterative and human-centered approach grounded in agile software engineering, participatory systems design, mixed-methods inquiry, and healthcare workflow analysis.

The organizational readiness assessment phase contributed to the framework components relating to institutional preparedness, leadership support, organizational change management, workforce readiness, and strategic digital transformation planning. Findings from the readiness surveys revealed variations in organizational readiness, vision clarity, change efficacy, top-management support, and collective self-efficacy across the participating institutions. These findings informed the inclusion of organizational readiness evaluation, leadership engagement, staff preparedness assessment, and digital transformation governance considerations within the proposed framework.

The clinician user experience and existing system evaluation phase informed framework dimensions relating to workflow alignment, system usability, interface design, interoperability considerations, and end-user involvement in digital transformation initiatives. The findings demonstrated recurring concerns regarding poor usability, workflow disruption, documentation burden, and inadequate alignment between existing digital systems and routine clinical processes. These observations reinforced the importance of incorporating workflow-centered design principles, usability evaluation, and continuous stakeholder participation into the framework structure.

The qualitative interview phase contributed significantly to the framework’s stakeholder engagement, implementation planning, and risk identification components. Clinicians and healthcare stakeholders identified infrastructural limitations, resistance to change, limited digital literacy, inadequate training, managerial challenges, and weak policy implementation as major barriers to digital transformation. These findings informed the inclusion of stakeholder engagement strategies, training and capacity development, infrastructural assessment, change management planning, and policy alignment mechanisms within the framework.

The focus group discussions and participatory requirements gathering activities informed the framework’s requirements elicitation and collaborative system development dimensions. Through iterative engagement with senior clinicians and departmental stakeholders, the research identified critical workflow requirements, documentation needs, communication processes, and collaborative care expectations necessary for designing context-appropriate digital healthcare systems. These findings reinforced the framework’s emphasis on participatory co-design and clinician involvement throughout digital transformation initiatives.

Furthermore, the iterative prototyping and usability evaluation phases informed the framework’s continuous evaluation and system refinement dimensions. The usability evaluation of the developed TUMERIC system demonstrated the importance of iterative testing, user feedback integration, and adaptive system improvement in enhancing user acceptance and perceived usability. Consequently, the framework incorporates continuous usability assessment, iterative refinement processes, and user-centered evaluation mechanisms as core implementation principles.

In addition to the empirical findings, the framework was further informed by existing literature, global digital health implementation principles, agile development methodologies, and human-centered software engineering theories. The integration of these empirical and theoretical inputs collectively informed the final structure of the proposed evidence-based healthcare digital transformation framework for tertiary healthcare institutions in developing and resource-constrained environments.

Results

Overview of the empirical research phases and datasets

The empirical findings presented in this study emerged from multiple interconnected phases of a broader doctoral research project investigating healthcare digital transformation within tertiary healthcare institutions in southeastern Nigeria. The study adopted a multi-phase mixed-methods and participatory human-centered systems engineering approach involving organizational readiness assessments, clinician evaluations of existing digital systems and workflows, qualitative investigations of implementation barriers and stakeholder experiences, participatory requirements elicitation activities, iterative prototype development, and usability evaluation of the developed TUMERIC platform. Different participant groups contributed to the various phases of the study based on the objectives and methodological requirements of each empirical component.

The organizational readiness assessment phase involved 580 healthcare professionals across the participating tertiary healthcare institutions. The clinician evaluation of existing digital systems and workflow processes involved 131 clinicians, including doctors, nurses, and medical record officers. The qualitative investigation phase generated approximately 400 open-ended responses relating to barriers affecting healthcare digital transformation implementation. Participatory requirements elicitation and co-design activities further involved senior clinical consultants, departmental stakeholders, and end-user representatives across multiple hospital units and departments. The final usability evaluation phase involved clinicians and patients who directly interacted with the developed TUMERIC cloud- and voice-enabled healthcare support system within routine clinical settings.

The findings presented in this manuscript primarily focus on the empirical results most directly relevant to the development of the proposed evidence-based healthcare digital transformation framework for tertiary healthcare institutions in developing and resource-constrained environments.

Organizational readiness findings

The first empirical phase of the study assessed organizational readiness for healthcare digital transformation across the participating tertiary healthcare institutions. The assessment examined multiple organizational dimensions influencing institutional preparedness for digital transformation initiatives, including organizational vision clarity, leadership commitment, organizational flexibility, collective self-efficacy, change efficacy, infrastructural preparedness, workforce digital readiness, and organizational support for technological change.

The findings demonstrated moderate but variable levels of organizational readiness across the participating institutions. While many healthcare professionals expressed positive perceptions regarding the future adoption of digital healthcare initiatives, several institutional and infrastructural concerns were identified as potential barriers affecting implementation sustainability. Recurring concerns included inconsistent management support, inadequate digital infrastructure, funding limitations, insufficient digital literacy, unstable power supply, poor internet connectivity, excessive workload pressures, and resistance to organizational change.

Participants further emphasized the importance of strong leadership commitment, stakeholder engagement, institutional preparedness, staff motivation, and long-term organizational support structures in facilitating sustainable healthcare digital transformation initiatives. Collectively, these findings highlighted the importance of organizational readiness assessment, governance structures, infrastructural preparedness, workforce readiness, and strategic institutional alignment as foundational requirements for healthcare digital transformation within resource-constrained healthcare environments.

Findings from existing digital systems and workflow evaluations

The second empirical phase of the study examined clinicians’ experiences with existing digital healthcare systems and workflow processes across the participating tertiary healthcare institutions. The findings revealed several technical, usability, infrastructural, and workflow-related limitations associated with previously implemented electronic healthcare systems.

Recurring concerns identified by clinicians included workflow disruption, excessive documentation burden, poor interface usability, inefficient system navigation, recurrent network failures, poor interoperability, inadequate technical support, and limited alignment between the implemented systems and existing clinical workflows. Several participants reported that previously implemented systems were “poorly designed” and “not consistent with our operational protocols,” while others described the systems as time-consuming within already high-volume clinical environments. Additional concerns included interrupted documentation processes caused by system failures, slow information retrieval processes, inadequate hardware infrastructure, and poor system responsiveness during routine clinical operations.

Direct observational activities similarly revealed practical challenges associated with healthcare documentation processes, delays in retrieving patient information, fragmented communication processes, and user frustrations during routine patient care activities. The findings further demonstrated that infrastructural instability, workflow incompatibility, and usability limitations significantly influenced clinicians’ perceptions regarding the effectiveness and sustainability of existing digital healthcare systems.

Collectively, these findings underscored the importance of workflow-sensitive system design, interoperability considerations, usability optimization, infrastructural reliability, low-bandwidth operational considerations, and human-centered implementation approaches during healthcare digital transformation initiatives within developing and resource-constrained healthcare environments.

Qualitative findings from interviews and observational activities

The qualitative phase of the study explored stakeholders’ perceptions regarding barriers affecting healthcare digital transformation implementation within tertiary healthcare institutions. Approximately 400 open-ended responses, interview transcripts, observational notes, and focus group discussions were analyzed using a thematic qualitative analysis approach. Initial open coding was conducted independently by members of the research team to identify recurring concepts and implementation-related concerns emerging from the datasets. The preliminary codes were subsequently reviewed, refined, grouped into broader thematic categories, and reconciled through iterative discussions among the researchers to improve coding consistency and thematic alignment. The final themes were derived through consensus and mapped to the major components of the proposed evidence-based healthcare digital transformation framework.

The analysis revealed several recurring themes influencing the successful adoption and sustainability of digital healthcare initiatives within resource-constrained environments. Major themes included infrastructural limitations, workflow incompatibility, poor usability of existing systems, insufficient stakeholder involvement, inadequate training, digital literacy limitations, resistance to organizational change, and concerns regarding sustainability and technical support.

Participants frequently identified infrastructural limitations as major barriers affecting digital transformation initiatives. Recurring concerns included unstable electricity supply, poor internet connectivity, inadequate funding, and insufficient technical support capacity. These infrastructural and organizational concerns informed framework components relating to organizational readiness assessment, infrastructural preparedness, governance structures, and sustainability planning.

Another prominent theme concerned workflow disruption and poor alignment between existing digital systems and routine clinical practices. Several participants expressed dissatisfaction with the usability and operational inefficiencies of previously implemented electronic systems. One participant noted that “The current EHR was poorly designed, not consistent with our operational protocols was our major issue” [40]. Another respondent stated that “The existing system is time-consuming, and its use frustrates doctors’ daily routines” [40]. Similarly, another participant observed that “The EHR causes delays in our work. We end up focusing on the computer screen rather than the patient” [40]. Additional concerns were raised regarding documentation burden and inefficient system interaction, with one clinician explaining that “The current electronic system makes things very slow; typing the history is very cumbersome and slows things down” [40].

The findings also revealed concerns regarding implementation practices and insufficient stakeholder participation during previous digital initiatives. One participant stated that “EHR here is failing not because our staff is not ready, but because the EHR system introduced to us was inefficient.” [40]. These findings reinforced the importance of participatory stakeholder engagement, workflow-centered design, iterative usability evaluation, and human-centered implementation approaches during healthcare digital transformation initiatives.

Collectively, the qualitative findings highlighted the importance of context-sensitive implementation planning, participatory co-design, infrastructural preparedness, organizational support, and continuous user engagement during healthcare digital transformation initiatives within developing and resource-constrained healthcare environments. These empirical findings directly informed the development of the framework domains relating to organizational readiness and governance, participatory needs assessment and workflow analysis, human-centered system design and technical architecture, and deployment, sustainability, and capacity building.

Emergent qualitative themes informing the framework

The qualitative findings directly informed the conceptual structure and implementation dimensions of the proposed evidence-based healthcare digital transformation framework. Four major thematic areas emerged from the analysis: organizational and infrastructural readiness, workflow and usability challenges, stakeholder participation and organizational support, and iterative human-centered implementation practices.

Themes relating to infrastructural limitations, inadequate technical support, unstable internet connectivity, and funding concerns informed the framework dimensions associated with organizational readiness assessment, governance, infrastructural preparedness, and sustainability planning.

Similarly, recurring concerns regarding workflow disruption, inefficient documentation processes, usability limitations, and poor alignment between existing systems and routine clinical practices informed the inclusion of workflow-centered and human-centered design principles within the framework. For example, one participant noted that “The current EHR was poorly designed, not consistent with our operational protocols was our major issue” [40]. Another participant stated that “The EHR causes delays in our work. We end up focusing on the computer screen rather than the patient” [40].

Themes associated with inadequate stakeholder engagement, insufficient training opportunities, and weak organizational support structures informed framework components relating to participatory implementation, training and capacity development, and organizational change management. Additionally, findings relating to iterative usability evaluation and participatory co-design informed the framework’s emphasis on continuous system refinement and agile human-centered implementation processes.

Collectively, these qualitative themes provided empirical support for the framework domains relating to organizational readiness and governance, participatory needs assessment and workflow analysis, human-centered system design and technical architecture, and deployment, sustainability, and capacity building.

TUMERIC system development and usability evaluation findings

The findings obtained from the organizational readiness assessments, workflow evaluations, qualitative investigations, and participatory requirements elicitation activities informed the iterative development of the proposed TUMERIC cloud- and voice-enabled collaborative healthcare support system. The system was developed using agile human-centered software engineering principles involving iterative co-design, UML-based system modelling, repeated stakeholder feedback cycles, and continuous prototype refinement throughout the system development lifecycle.

The completed system subsequently underwent usability evaluation using an adapted usability evaluation questionnaire informed by System Usability Scale (SUS) principles [37–39]. The usability evaluation involved clinicians and patients who directly interacted with the developed system within routine clinical environments.

The usability findings demonstrated generally positive perceptions regarding system learnability, ease of use, workflow compatibility, interface usability, accessibility, and overall user experience associated with the implemented healthcare support platform. Participants further reported positive perceptions regarding the collaborative functionalities, usability simplicity, and practical applicability of the proposed cloud- and voice-enabled healthcare support system within routine clinical workflows.

The iterative usability evaluation activities additionally demonstrated the importance of participatory co-design, continuous stakeholder engagement, iterative refinement processes, and workflow-sensitive system development approaches during healthcare digital transformation initiatives. However, the findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the present study did not employ a comparative experimental design capable of establishing causal relationships between the adopted agile human-centered development methodology and the observed usability outcomes.

Framework synthesis and development

The proposed evidence-based healthcare digital transformation framework emerged from the cross-phase synthesis and integration of findings obtained throughout the multiple empirical phases of the broader doctoral research project. The framework components were informed by organizational readiness assessments, clinician workflow evaluations, qualitative stakeholder experiences, participatory requirements elicitation activities, iterative prototype development processes, usability evaluations, and practical implementation experiences generated during the development and evaluation of the TUMERIC platform.

The organizational readiness findings informed framework dimensions relating to institutional preparedness assessment, leadership engagement, governance structures, organizational change management, workforce readiness, infrastructural preparedness, and strategic digital transformation planning. Findings from the clinician workflow evaluations informed the inclusion of workflow alignment mechanisms, human-centered design principles, interoperability considerations, usability optimization processes, and continuous system evaluation strategies within the framework structure.

Similarly, the qualitative findings relating to infrastructural instability, stakeholder concerns, digital literacy limitations, resistance to organizational change, inadequate technical support structures, and implementation sustainability challenges informed framework components associated with participatory stakeholder engagement, implementation planning, training strategies, infrastructural assessment, organizational adaptation, and long-term sustainability planning.

Furthermore, the iterative prototyping and usability evaluation activities reinforced the importance of participatory co-design, iterative system refinement, continuous usability evaluation, agile human-centered software engineering approaches, and workflow-sensitive implementation methodologies during healthcare digital transformation initiatives within resource-constrained healthcare environments.

Discussion

This study investigated the challenges, readiness factors, user experiences, and implementation barriers influencing healthcare digital transformation across tertiary healthcare institutions in southeastern Nigeria and subsequently developed an evidence-based digital transformation framework grounded in agile human-centered software engineering principles. The findings demonstrated moderate but variable organizational readiness for digital transformation across the participating hospitals, with many healthcare professionals expressing support for future digital initiatives. The study also identified substantial usability and workflow-related limitations within existing digital systems, including poor interface design, documentation burden, workflow disruption, and inadequate alignment with clinical practices. Qualitative findings further revealed recurring concerns relating to infrastructural limitations, insufficient stakeholder involvement, resistance to change, digital literacy gaps, and weak organizational support structures. Through participatory design, iterative prototyping, and usability evaluations, the developed TUMERIC cloud- and voice-enabled healthcare support system demonstrated high levels of perceived usability and user acceptance among clinicians and patients. Collectively, these findings informed the development of the proposed evidence-based healthcare digital transformation framework tailored to the realities of tertiary healthcare institutions within developing and resource-constrained environments.

Indeed, drawing from our field experience and in consideration of the hectic healthcare milieu characteristic of developing economies where clinics are frequented by large patient populations, it is advisable that system development iterations, which involve substantial user participation, should be limited to no more than two.

These user evaluations are conducted at the unit or departmental levels, contingent upon the various dimensions of the system currently under development. Each department or unit is exclusively exposed to the specific dimension or segment of the system that directly pertains to their operational activities. It warrants acknowledgment that this procedure is time-intensive, and the overall success or failure of the entire system implementation is contingent upon the integration of the ultimate end-users throughout the system development value chain, adhering to the user participatory development or co-creation paradigm. Thus, the first user prototype evaluation constitutes a critical component of the user-centric system development process.

During this initial phase of the prototype evaluation, users are encouraged to articulate their perceptions regarding the execution of certain system functionalities. They are prompted to express their preferences for modifications or the incorporation of additional features and functionalities, as well as the elimination of existing ones. Users may also raise issues concerning any discrepancies within the system interface design; they could notify the development team of challenges related to dialogue boxes and notifications pertaining to errors and successes. It is imperative that users are afforded the opportunity to freely convey their impressions and viewpoints regarding the system prototype. Employing ethnographic protocols, direct observations, and interviews are effective methodologies at this stage of prototype evaluation. This approach enables the development team to promptly address users’ feedback.

Subsequently, the development team revisits the feedback gathered from users, including their opinions, impressions, grievances, and commendations, and begins to reconcile these modifications with the project sign-off document, which delineates the expectations of all stakeholders concerning the proposed project.

Second prototype user testing and evaluation

In this subsequent phase of prototype evaluation, users seek to ascertain whether all requested modifications have been comprehensively integrated. During this stage, they commence familiarizing themselves with the new system and evaluating its consistency with the clinical workflow of the care facility. It remains possible for users to identify additional adjustments to the system; realizing all user-suggested changes would facilitate their full ownership of the system as co-creators. At this juncture, users would also juxtapose the enhanced system or prototype against the project sign-off document to verify that all stakeholders’ expectations have been satisfactorily fulfilled. It is important to note that this second prototype user evaluation will continue to occur at the unit or departmental level, with each unit or department being exposed solely to the dimensions of the system that influence their clinical operational workflow.

Moreover, the developers will once again gather the final user input and reflect accordingly; in instances where user complaints are minimal at this stage, subsequent improvements may not necessitate another extensive user evaluation. A limited number of user representatives would likely find this acceptable.

9.Top Management Takeover: Subsequent to the completion of user co-creation through evaluations of user prototypes, which were also overseen by a representative from Top Management, the subsequent phase involves the transition to the conclusive review and evaluation conducted by management. During this phase, it is anticipated that the management representative will collaborate with the Information and Communication Technology (ICT) Department of the healthcare facility to meticulously assess the full range of functionalities of the system; the safety and security protocols will be scrutinized in accordance with all pertinent governmental regulations and standards. Upon confirming that all protocols and standards have been satisfied, the rollout and deployment of the system, in partnership with the ICT Department, will commence.

Potential applicability of the evidence-based digital transformation framework to other developing economies

Now, the proposed evidence-based digital transformation framework offers a structured approach to enhancing healthcare delivery through digital solutions, particularly in resource-constrained environments. As many developing economies faced similar challenges in their healthcare systems, understanding the commonalities and differences in their contexts can broaden the framework’s relevance and applicability. This discussion explores these aspects, highlighting how the framework can be adapted to meet the unique needs of various developing economies while maintaining its core principles.

Commonalities between other developing economies and Nigeria vis-à-vis the framework

Developing economies often operate under tight budgets, limiting their ability to invest in comprehensive digital solutions, which necessitates a focus on prioritizing essential digital tools that deliver maximum impact with minimal investment. Many healthcare systems in these regions struggle with inadequate technological infrastructure, including unreliable internet access and outdated hardware, hindering effective implementation. Additionally, engaging a diverse range of stakeholders is critical for success, as healthcare providers, patients, and administrative staff may have differing perspectives and needs. The framework’s emphasis on participatory design ensures that all voices are heard, fostering a sense of ownership and increasing the likelihood of successful adoption. Furthermore, the integration of digital tools into existing clinical workflows is essential for minimizing disruption and enhancing efficiency, ensuring that new technologies complement established practices.

Differences between other developing economies and Nigeria vis-à-vis the framework

Cultural attitudes towards technology and healthcare can significantly influence the acceptance of digital solutions, with some regions exhibiting a strong preference for traditional methods that may lead to resistance against new technologies. Each country also has its own healthcare regulations and policies that can impact the implementation of digital transformation initiatives, necessitating flexibility in the framework to accommodate legal requirements. Economic stability and growth rates vary widely among developing economies, affecting the availability of funding for digital initiatives, which may require prioritizing low-cost, high-impact interventions. Additionally, the level of technological advancement and digital literacy can differ significantly, with some countries needing foundational training and support before adopting advanced digital solutions, highlighting the importance of assessing local technological readiness.

Limitations of the proposed framework

Despite the important contributions of this research, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The successful implementation of the proposed framework remains dependent on several external institutional and infrastructural factors, including stable electricity supply, broadband connectivity, technical support capacity, digital literacy, organizational leadership commitment, and governmental policy support. These structural limitations remain significant challenges within many developing and resource-constrained healthcare environments and may therefore influence implementation outcomes differently across institutions.

In addition, although the study integrated both quantitative and qualitative methodologies, some qualitative findings were based on self-reported experiences and perceptions from clinicians and stakeholders, which may reflect variations in individual experiences and institutional contexts. Nevertheless, the use of mixed-methods triangulation, participatory stakeholder engagement, direct observational methods, and iterative usability evaluations helped strengthen the credibility and contextual reliability of the findings.

Despite these limitations, the proposed framework provides an important empirically grounded contribution toward understanding and supporting healthcare digital transformation within tertiary healthcare institutions in developing economies.

Conclusion

This framework has been meticulously designed to reflect global best practices, incorporating the principles of agile methodologies and design thinking. It is inherently human-centric and end-user-focused, particularly within a participatory context. Moreover, it empathetically addresses the prevailing challenges faced by healthcare institutions in developing and resource-constrained environments. This report outlines a step-by-step approach—many aspects of which the researchers have practically implemented over the years with rewarding outcomes - while investigating factors that may support more effective and sustainable healthcare digital transformation programs and projects. The focus is particularly on the complex tertiary healthcare landscape of university teaching hospitals in Nigeria. The report presents these dimensions in a simplified manner, ensuring clarity while capturing the overall essence of any applicable healthcare institution. It also emphasizes the involvement of all relevant stakeholders within a participatory systems engineering framework. The framework is informed by the researchers’ direct experience working within the same healthcare environment in a developing country. Additionally, it incorporates crucial policy and regulatory considerations that must be factored in when deploying healthcare technologies. Empirical findings from this study showed that patients rated the usability of the digital healthcare support system implemented in this research journey with high mean scores, demonstrating high usability. These findings suggest that agile and human-centered systems engineering practices may have contributed positively to usability perceptions within the study context. More results showed that over 70% of healthcare workers thought their facility was ready to embrace new digital initiatives. These outcomes, as expected, would guide further leadership decisions on strategy for future digital transformation initiatives. It is important to note that these outcomes underscore the relevance of the proposed digital transformation framework, which was built upon this evidence and design philosophy. It is, therefore, anticipated that this evidence-based framework will serve as a practical guide for healthcare decision-makers, researchers, and digital transformation consultants exploring opportunities in the sub-Saharan African market. Additionally, it highlights key considerations essential for developing digital strategies in resource-constrained contexts.
