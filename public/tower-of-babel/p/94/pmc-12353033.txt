Initial opioid prescribing practices among providers treating an opioid-naïve US military sample
Authors: Jenneth Carpenter, R S Adams, A Linton, K B Highland, M Bauer, G Zolotusky, N Moresco, M J Larson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGeneral opioid prescribing has decreased in response to practice guidelines and policy changes, but information on recent practices in initial opioid prescribing is needed to support interventions to reduce the risk of long-term opioid prescription use.MethodsThis cross-sectional study examined Military Health System data between 1 October 2015 and 30 September 2021 to explore initial opioid prescriptions and prescribing practices among providers serving 1.66 million US service members. We compared the characteristics of a subgroup (n=372 960) receiving an initial opioid within 7–24 months after study entry with those of the sample as a whole. We described characteristics of initial opioid prescriptions, including healthcare encounters associated with those prescriptions, prescriber characteristics and opioid prescribing practices.ResultsThe analytic sample was composed of 1 666 019 service members, 372 960 of whom filled an initial opioid prescription 7–24 months after study entry. The analytic sample was 83% assigned male; 58% white, non-Hispanic, 16% black, non-Hispanic and 15% Hispanic; and had a mean age of 27 (8.02). Initial opioid fills had a median days’ supply of four (IQR 3.0–5.0) and milligram morphine equivalents (MME) of 30 (IQR 20–50). 10% of prescribers wrote the majority (64%) of initial opioid fills, representing 67% of total initial opioid MME. Dental encounters were associated with 38% of initial opioid fills, and 19% of fills were associated with musculoskeletal conditions. Dentists were associated with 32% of total initial opioid fills and 25% of total initial opioid MME.ConclusionsDuring recent years in the Military Health System, dental care was an important driver of initial opioid receipt among a relatively young population with full dental coverage. Similar populations may benefit from interventions that support dental providers in maintaining within-guideline prescribing practices.Keywords: Health policy, Military Personnel, PAIN MANAGEMENT

Introduction

High levels of opioid prescribing provide a supply for potential diversion1 and are associated with opioid-related mortality.2 3 In a US representative sample, patients’ initial opioid prescription (IOP) characteristics (eg, IOP day supply, receipt of cumulative 700 mg morphine equivalents (MME, a standardised measure of opioid prescription strength)) were each associated with long-term opioid prescription receipt.4 An array of federal, state and clinical practice guidelines and policies focused on opioid prescribing—such as the first Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Chronic Pain5—has led to a declining number of prescribers who initiated opioid therapy among commercially insured and general US population patients who are opioid naïve.6 7 However, national studies have identified continued patterns of long duration, high dose (>50 daily MME) prescriptions to patients who are opioid naïve.7 In a study of commercial insurance claims, primary care physicians, among all types of prescribers, most commonly prescribed IOPs with more than a 7-day supply (40%), while high-dose IOPs were most common among specialists.7 Within an employer-insured population, surgical, emergency department and dental encounters were found to account for a growing share of IOPs.8 Among providers of a large US national, private insurer, 1% of providers accounted for 27% of all opioid prescriptions and 49% of all opioid doses.9 These findings point to the need to describe initial opioid prescribing patterns outside of the realm of private insurance and to further investigate the patient conditions associated with initial opioid receipt.

To address gaps in knowledge, our study aims were to examine IOP receipt among a large sample of opioid-naïve US military service members to identify intervention targets for mitigating long-term opioid prescription use and other adverse consequences. To do so, we analysed Military Health System (MHS) data between 1 October 2015 and 30 September 2021 to examine initial opioid prescribing among providers serving 1.66 million service members. Service members represent a relatively young population with full medical and dental coverage, who may frequently experience acute injuries and chronic pain.10 11 The study compared the characteristics of a subgroup of members receiving an IOP within 7–24 months after study entry with those of the sample as a whole and described the IOPs and the healthcare encounters associated with those prescriptions. We also examined prescriber characteristics and opioid prescribing practices. Study results are expected to add to the knowledge base on current initial opioid prescribing patterns, thereby providing information that can be used to inform pain management policies and practice guidelines.

Methods

Study data represent healthcare received in medical and dental facilities operated by the MHS and care received in civilian settings through the TRICARE insurance programme (ie, purchased care). Data originated from the MHS Data Repository, which contains demographic and eligibility data, records of all healthcare services rendered to eligible beneficiaries paid for by the MHS and records of prescriptions filled by beneficiaries in military and retail pharmacies as well as other locations including TRICARE mail-order, in-theatre fills and Department of Veterans Affairs pharmacies. See online supplemental table 1 for MHS Data Repository files used. We obtained prescriber specialty data from the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System (NPPES), published monthly by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS).

Study sample

Study participants were from the Substance Use and Psychological Injury Combat Study Next-Generation (SUPIC) cohort, including over 1.68 million US service members with 24 months of continuous TRICARE eligibility between 1 October 2015 and 30 September 2020.12 After we excluded members with missing data and those in subgroups with very small numbers (eg, age over 70), the analytic sample included 1 660 019 members, representing 84% of all eligible members over the 5-year period (online supplemental figure 1).

We selected the earliest date of opioid receipt after study entry as the IOP date. We defined the initial opioid sample as members who filled an IOP at least six but no more than 24 months after entry to the SUPIC cohort. We excluded members who received their first opioid more than 24 months after entry because some members were censored at 24 months and because of an increase in prescriptions that could not be matched to prescribers. There were 920 737 (55%) members who received an opioid prescription after entry to the study; 293 736 (18%) had an IOP written >24 months after study entry and were excluded; 254 041 (15%) received their initial opioid <6 months after study entry and were excluded. The remaining 372 960 members (23%; 41% of members with an opioid prescription) met criteria for opioid naivety (figure 1).

Initial opioid study flow chart. a Reflects a total of 383 148 opioid scripts; 2.6% of initial opioid patients received two or more opioid scripts on their initial opioid date.

We categorised prescriptions as originating from one of three programme sources: direct care in military treatment facilities (MTFs), stand-alone military dental treatment facilities (DTFs) or the purchased care system. We based this categorisation on information from the script including prescriber National Provider Identifier (NPI), encounter date and the date the prescription was written and the Appointment Identifier which links the script to its originating encounter when the encounter occurred in the same MTF as the prescription fill. We used MTF employment records to assign prescriptions to an MTF when they could not be matched directly to an encounter. We assumed that dental providers with no MTF employment record and no purchased care claim records worked exclusively at DTFs. We assigned prescriptions originating from an indeterminable or unknown location to the Other/Unknown category (online supplemental figure 2).

We linked IOP fills to clinical encounter records using a multi-step logic described in the previous paragraph and further detailed in online supplemental figure 3, allowing multiple prescriptions to be linked to the same encounter.

Measures

Patient characteristics included assigned sex in the medical record, race and ethnicity based on administrative records, age group at time of IOP, Rank group, Service branch and Component at study entry (Active, National Guard, Reserve).

IOPs were filled between 1 October 2015 and 30 September 2021 and included therapeutic agents prescribed in outpatient settings classified as an opioid, following CDC conventions, inclusive of tramadol and exclusive of liquid and injectable formulations, and medications for opioid use disorder.13 Over 97% of members in the initial opioid sample filled only one prescription on the date of their initial opioid fill; for the remaining 3% who filled multiple opioid prescriptions on that date, we aggregated the prescriptions for analysis. We used CDC documentation13 to identify each prescription’s therapeutic agent, identify it as long acting or short acting and calculate its MME.

We assigned each IOP to a category of outpatient care using the product line code of the matched direct care encounter or purchased care claim. DTF-based encounter data exist but were not available for this study. We assumed that all IOPs written by an NPI classified as a dentist and not linked to an outpatient encounter originated from a DTF.

We defined encounter reason by the primary diagnosis (ICD-10) and classified it by CMS Major Diagnostic Category (MDC) taxonomy using information from the matched encounter record. We coded all prescriptions written by a dentist as dental care, regardless of the programme source.

We obtained prescriber specialty by matching the NPI on the opioid prescription to the primary Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act taxonomy code in the NPPES file.

For care occurring within MTFs, the Numeric Rating Scale Pain Score (range 0–10)14 is documented as a routine part of some encounters on records in the Vital Records File. By selecting vital records recorded within 7 days prior to or following matched encounter dates, we associated 84 592 IOPs (54% of MTF encounters matched to IOPs prescribed by non-dental providers) with pain intensity scores. We coded pain intensity scores as no pain (0), mild (1–3), moderate (4–7) and severe (8–10).

We summarised total daily MME of IOPs for each prescriber (NPI) across all prescription sources for the study period. We rank-ordered unique prescriber NPIs by total MME, and we classified prescribers in the 90th percentile of daily MME. We excluded prescriptions containing invalid or missing NPIs from this ranking process.

Statistical analysis

We generated descriptive statistics on member characteristics for the opioid-naïve sample and total sample, and on the properties of the IOPs among the opioid-naïve sample. We calculated means with SD, medians and IQRs, and 90th percentiles for opioid days’ supply and daily MME of the IOPs. We generated frequency distributions on the category of outpatient care and reason (MDC) for matched encounters. For the 59 990 unique prescribers, we generated descriptive statistics on IOPs. We conducted analyses restricted to the 90th percentile of prescribers based on total MME, characterising these prescribers by programme source at time of prescription and primary specialty. Sample selection, file construction and analyses were conducted using SAS V.9.4 statistical software.15

Results

The sample included mostly younger service members (24% were 18–20 years old and 23% were 21–24 years old); the majority were assigned male (83%); over half (58%) were white, non-Hispanic; most were Active Component (87%); and most were Junior Enlisted (51%) or Senior Enlisted (31%; table 1). Army members were more prevalent in the initial opioid sample than in the full analytic sample (45% vs 40%), and Navy members were less so (18% vs 23%).

Prescription characteristics

Most IOPs originated from an MTF (54%) and were filled in MTF pharmacies (81%). The most common therapeutic agents were oxycodone (44%) and hydrocodone (39%), with hydrocodone prescriptions representing a greater portion of prescriptions originating in purchased care (50%) and DTFs (49%) than those originating within MTFs (30%; table 2). The median days’ supply was 4 days (table 2); 17% of IOP fills were for >7 day supply. The median daily dose was 30 MME; however, 25% of IOPs were written for >50 MME and the top 10% of prescriptions had an MME of 75 or greater. Almost all of the IOPs (99%) were for opioids categorised as short acting, rather than long acting. Most prescriptions (93%) were filled on the day they were written (data not shown).

Clinical characteristics of prescription reason

The largest proportion of IOPs was from dental care (38%), followed by care at orthopaedic clinics (14%), primary care clinics (14%) and emergency departments (12%; table 3). When grouped by the encounter’s MDC, dental conditions (38%) were the most common reason, with musculoskeletal system and connective tissue conditions second most common (19%). The proportion of IOPs written for patients reporting no pain or low pain was nearly equivalent to the proportion written for those reporting moderate or severe pain; however, these data were only available on 54% of opioids originating from MTF care and prescribed by non-dental providers (online supplemental table 2).

Prescriber characteristics

A total of 12 943 (22%) MTF prescribers, 6542 (11%) stand-alone DTF prescribers and 34 150 (57%) purchased care prescribers wrote one or more IOPs; the remaining 6812 (11%) prescribers could not be associated with any source (online supplemental table 3). Surgeons constituted 16% of all prescribers, followed by emergency medicine providers (14%), dentists (12%), physician assistants (12%), generalist physicians (10%), advanced practice nurses (7%) and other skill type 2 prescribers (2%; online supplemental table 4). The top 10% of prescribers wrote 64% of the IOPs, representing 67% of the total initial opioid MME (figure 2 and online supplemental table 5a–5c). Dentists constituted 18% of the 90th percentile prescribers and wrote 31% of the total prescriptions filled. Surgeons represented 29% of 90th percentile prescribers but wrote only 9% of the prescriptions.

Discussion

In the present study, among service members who had 2 years of continuous eligibility in the MHS between 1 October 2015 and 30 September 2021, 23% received an opioid within 24 months of study entry and were opioid-naïve in the first 6 months of study eligibility, representing 41% of all members with an opioid prescription during the period. We used recent, comprehensive data that permits linkage of opioid prescription data with medical encounter data for better understanding of prescription reasons and care settings, including integrated data on dental care, in a large population of relatively young adults with full dental coverage. As the study includes analysis of over 75 000 IOPs written by over 34 000 civilian prescribers in purchased care, it also provides insights to the prescribing of non-military prescribers.

Among the opioid-naïve sample, 38% of IOPs were associated with dental care. This percentage is higher than a national study of opioid-naïve patients with commercial insurance, in which 4% of IOPs were written by dentists.8 This prior study of individuals aged 18–64 years likely undercounted dental prescribing: US health plans are required to make dental insurance available to children, but this requirement does not apply to adults, who often choose not to purchase dental insurance or purchase insurance through an insurer for which data are frequently not captured in studies. One strength of the present study is its capture of all dental prescription fills for a sample whose members uniformly have comprehensive dental coverage. While there is some evidence that military service members and civilians have fairly similar rates of routine dental care utilisation,16 the availability of coverage and the role of dental health in military readiness could promote higher rates of more extensive dental work (eg, third molar extractions) among service members. The present results are similar to a study of national paediatric opioid prescribing (majority of patients with opioids were adolescents or young adults), in which 38% of all opioid prescriptions were from dentists.17 As with the general population, opioid prescribing to a young population is not without risks. A national survey of 12th graders followed to age 23 found that prescription opioid receipt before high school graduation was independently associated with a 33% increase in the risk of future opioid misuse.18

The American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons clinical practice guidelines,19 the American Dental Association20 and the 2022 CDC guidelines21 recommend non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs as first-line treatment for acute dental pain management. A multidisciplinary panel of dentists developed consensus recommendations on opioid use specific to the dental procedure: for routine tooth extractions the recommendation was zero opioids; for extraction of impacted molars the recommendation was 0–15 tablets of 5 mg oxycodone (0–112.5 MME).22 Adherence to guidelines is important to minimise risk among patients with opioid prescriptions and to minimise ‘leftover’ opioid medication, which is common following surgery and increases opportunities for diversion.1 Given the high prevalence of dental opioid prescribing observed in our study, further investigation of prescribing for dental procedures is warranted to determine if targeted interventions are needed.

During this period, the MHS implemented policies to mitigate opioid over prescribing,23 and research has documented that opioid prescribing volume decreased.12 24 25 In the present study, purchased care prescribers represented over half (57%) of the providers prescribing IOPs. Civilian prescribers who treat service members may be aware of CDC guidelines and state requirements but may be less aware of Defense Health Agency policies that guide care for members. The language written into support care contracts for TRICARE26 should be examined to ensure that there are requirements for guideline-congruent opioid prescribing; separate monitoring of opioid prescribing among civilian providers may be warranted.

Oxycodone was the most frequently used therapeutic agent (44%), followed by hydrocodone (38%) and tramadol (10%); oxycodone was more prevalent in direct care programmes than in purchased care. It is unclear what factors influenced therapeutic agent choice, but another study of opioid prescriptions for children and young adults found 21% of prescriptions were for oxycodone.17 Hydrocodone has a 1:1 MME, whereas oxycodone has 1.5:1 MME. Therefore, ten 5 mg pills of oxycodone have 75 MME, compared with 50 MME for hydrocodone. Clinical studies on the comparative effectiveness are inconclusive. In response to a 2018 Defense Health Agency Procedural Instruction 6025.04, surgeons in the MHS appeared to increase tramadol prescribing while decreasing use of stronger opioids, while other specialists and primary care prescribers decreased their tramadol rates.25

The effectiveness of system-level policies may vary according to policy parameters (eg, target behaviours, metrics and enforcement). For example, Allen and colleagues recently determined that setting-specific duration limits were associated with reduced length of prescriptions for opioid-naïve patients.27 The present study’s finding that the majority of IOPs (64%) and initial opioid MME (67%) were written by 10% of prescribing clinicians suggests substantial potential benefit of policies and guidelines focusing on targeted care settings and scenarios.

This study has limitations. We found that 11% of prescriber NPIs and 8% of IOPs were either from outside the MHS or associated with an invalid NPI. These relatively small proportions increase our confidence in our matching approach; however, the non-matches may have impacted the analysis of prescription reason and prescriber specialty. Only 54% of IOPs originating from MTF encounters and written by non-dental providers had pain scores reported. Therefore, findings on reported pain levels on encounters associated with IOPs should be considered exploratory. Additional monitoring with more complete pain score data is needed to determine whether an opioid prescription is indicated or another analgesic would suffice for acute or chronic pain management, or if use of a non-pharmacological treatment is preferred, as recommended in the DoD/VA clinical practice guidelines.28–30 Navy members who spent time afloat during the study period could have had received opioids unobservable in MHS data. This may have contributed to the lowered prevalence of Navy members in the initial opioid analytic sample. Lastly, it is unclear whether study findings would be generalisable to patients in other healthcare systems, civilian populations or populations that are not predominantly in early adulthood.

In conclusion, in this study of a large population of relatively young adult service members with full dental insurance, we found that IOPs were received by a substantial proportion of patients, and that prescriptions for more than a 7-day supply and those for more than 50 daily MME were not uncommon. Further, the majority of IOPs and the majority of MME were prescribed by a small minority of providers, and a large proportion were provided by dental clinicians. Indeed, dental IOPs were more common than IOPs for musculoskeletal conditions within this population. This suggests that policies and guidelines that support dental providers in limiting opioid prescribing may be of particular value to young adult populations with dental insurance.
