Age-stratified optimal non-HDL cholesterol and all-cause mortality in a healthy Taiwanese population considering personal risk factors
Authors: Edy Kornelius, Tsuey-Hwa Hu, Chih-Hua Chiao, Jing-Shiang Hwang, Ta-Chien Chan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) is a key cardiovascular risk factor, yet its optimal range in healthy individuals remains unclear.MethodsThis study analyzed data from 134,343 adults aged 40–79 in the MJ Health Check-up Cohort in Taiwan (2000–2017). Participants with prior cardiovascular disease or extreme/incomplete BMI data (≥50 kg/m2) were excluded. Covariates included lifestyle, blood pressure, and diet. All-cause mortality was assessed using Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsThe mean age was 51.8 years, and 47.3 % were male. Most participants (62.7 %) had a BMI of 18.5–25. The average non-HDL-C level was 146.8 mg/dL. A U-shaped association was observed between non-HDL-C levels and all-cause mortality. Compared with the reference group (160–179 mg/dL), non-HDL-C levels of <100, 100–119, 120–139, and ≥200 mg/dL were linked to elevated mortality risk, with hazard ratios (HR) of 1.42 (95 % CI: 1.31–1.54), 1.19 (1.11–1.27), 1.11 (1.04–1.18), and 1.11 (1.03–1.20), respectively. The highest risk was noted in the 40–49 age group with non-HDL-C ≥200 mg/dL, particularly among females (HR 1.72, 95 % CI: 1.15–2.58). However, no significant increase in risk was observed for the same category in older age groups.ConclusionsA non-HDL-C range of 160–179 mg/dL was associated with the lowest all-cause mortality among healthy Taiwanese adults. Both lower and higher levels were linked to increased mortality risk, highlighting the importance of maintaining moderate non-HDL-C levels to support longevity and overall health.Keywords: Non-HDL-C, Mortality risk, Health behaviors

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains a global health challenge, accounting for approximately one-third of all deaths worldwide [1]. This alarming statistic highlights the critical need for continuous research and interventions to mitigate cardiovascular risk. Hyperlipidemia, a key modifiable risk factor, is a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Extensive research has documented low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) as a significant factor in cardiovascular events [2,3]. Consequently, numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have validated the effectiveness of statins in lowering LDL-C levels and reducing cardiovascular risk [4].

However, despite the established benefits of statin therapy and subsequent LDL-C reduction, a significant residual risk of cardiovascular events persists [5,6]. This observation has prompted the exploration of alternative hypotheses beyond solely focusing on LDL-C [7,8]. A prominent area of interest is the role of non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C). This broader category encompasses a spectrum of proatherogenic particles, including apolipoprotein B, very low-density lipoprotein, and intermediate-density lipoprotein [9]. Non-HDL-C levels are calculated by subtracting HDL-C from total cholesterol. Emerging evidence suggests that non-HDL-C may serve as a more reliable indicator of cardiovascular risk than LDL-C alone, potentially offering a more comprehensive assessment [[10], [11], [12]]. Additionally, other factors such as age, sex, blood pressure, smoking status, and lifestyle behaviors are also known to contribute to an increased risk of CVD [[13], [14], [15]].

One crucial limitation in current knowledge is the lack of well-defined reference ranges for non-HDL-C, particularly for specific populations like Asians and individuals without existing health conditions. To address this gap, we leveraged a large health examination database of healthy Taiwanese participants and followed them for up to 18 years. We aimed to clarify how non-HDL-C relates to all-cause mortality within various age groups, thereby contributing to a more refined understanding of cardiovascular risk stratification and prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Informed consent was obtained for data processing and analysis. This ethical review was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Biomedical Science Research, Academia Sinica (AS-IRB-BM-22008). Individually identified data were removed and anonymized throughout the study. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and followed an approved protocol. Clinical trial number is not applicable in this study.

Study population

This study utilized data from comprehensive health examinations conducted at the MJ Health Examination Center in Taiwan over 18 years (2000–2017). This extensive timeframe and large sample size of community residents provided a robust foundation for analyzing all-cause mortality in relation to various demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors.

Participants were aged 40–79 years and underwent their first health exam at the MJ Health Examination Center during the study period. This age range was chosen due to the higher prevalence of hypercholesterolemia in this group. A previous study using similar data from Taiwan (2009–2010) found an average prevalence of 43.96 % [16].

To ensure data accuracy and minimize confounding factors, we excluded participants with incomplete or missing body mass index (BMI) data, as well as those presenting with a BMI of 50 kg/m2 or above. Participants with a self-reported history of cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, or stroke, or those on long-term medication for cardiac conditions were excluded. Because ICD diagnostic codes were not systematically available in the MJ Health Examination database, exclusions were necessarily based on baseline self-reported history. This criterion was applied to focus on a relatively healthy population and to minimize the confounding effects of medications (e.g., statins, antihypertensives, or anti-arrhythmics) that could alter lipid levels and mortality risk. Furthermore, any participant lacking data on key covariates (blood glucose, blood pressure, lifestyle factors, etc.) was excluded. Although some individuals with baseline cancer (2.3 % of the sample) were retained for a real-world perspective, we acknowledge this may introduce residual confounding.

To assess the participants’ long-term health, we linked the MJ Health Examination Center database with the Taiwanese mortality database from 2000 to 2019. This process strictly adhered to privacy and security protocols. Trained personnel from the Health and Welfare Data Science Center, Ministry of Health and Welfare, and the MJ Health Research Foundation conducted the linkage using encrypted personal identification numbers. Encryption ensured researchers only accessed anonymized data, safeguarding participant confidentiality while allowing for accurate tracking. The successful integration enabled us to analyze survival status until December 31, 2019, providing valuable insights into the connections between non-HDL-C, lifestyle factors, and all-cause mortality in the Taiwanese population. All-cause mortality served as the primary outcome, measured using the cause-of-death database maintained by the Taiwanese Ministry of Health and Welfare (2000–2019). Cause-specific mortality data were not comprehensively available and therefore were not analyzed in this study.

Study covariates

Data were collected on demographic factors (age, sex, education), anthropometric measures (BMI), biochemical values (non-HDL-C, fasting blood glucose, systolic blood pressure), medical history (limited to conditions consistently recorded in the MJ Health Examination database, including cancer, asthma, nephritis, gout), medication use for hypertension, diabetes, or hyperlipidemia, and detailed lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol, betel nut, physical activity) and diet (frequency of eggs, beans, aquatic products, meat, vegetables, fruits, baked and fried foods). Fasting plasma glucose, rather than HbA1c, was used as a covariate because HbA1c values were not systematically available in the MJ Health Examination database during the study period. Non-HDL-C was categorized as <100, 100–119, 120–139, 140–159, 160–179 (reference), 180–199, and ≥200 mg/dL. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, self-reported history of hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medication.

Statistical analysis

We employed a Cox proportional hazards model, implementing using the coxph function in R version 4.2.1 to investigate mortality risk factors and assess survival differences across the seven non-HDL-C groups, stratified by sex and age. This mode incorporated all 26 covariates described earlier to comprehensively evaluate their potential mortality.

To understand the relationship between non-HDL-C levels and mortality risk, we used a restricted cubic spline (RCS) method, with 160 mg/dL of non-HDL-C as the reference point. Four knots were placed at appropriate percentiles of the non-HDL-C distribution. This approach provided a nuanced understanding of how varying non-HDL-C levels correlate with mortality risk across different populations. Prior to estimating survival rates within the non-HDL-C groups, a potential bias necessitated methodological intervention. Age and sex, which were uncontrolled covariates in the initial analysis, exhibited uneven distribution across the groups. This imbalance could lead to spurious results in the subsequent survival analysis.

To mitigate this potential bias, we employed the Weighted Kaplan-Meier Estimator. This established statistical technique facilitates the calculation of adjusted survival rates by incorporating the weights derived from a multinomial logistic model. Subsequently, we implemented the weighted log-rank test from the R package “survey” (Lumley 2024) to rigorously assess the presence of statistically significant differences in survival probabilities across the seven non-HDL-C groups within the three designated age categories. This comprehensive statistical approach culminated in the stratification of the data into 12 distinct combinations. These strata were formed based on a combination of four age groups (40–79, 40–49, 50–59, and 60–79) and three sex categories (all participants, males, and females). Our stratified analysis facilitated an in-depth examination of non-HDL-C hazard ratios (HRs) after adjusting for the influence of all covariates included in the model. We recognize that more granular RCS subgroup analyses and formal interaction tests could further elucidate subgroup differences, but these were not performed here.

Results

Our analysis began with 486,009 participants from the MJ Examination Center (2000–2017). To ensure participant privacy and accurate mortality linkage, we excluded records lacking the essential scrambled identification numbers (IDs). We then focused on the demographic most susceptible to hypercholesterolemia, restricting the sample to individuals aged 40–79 years (n = 290,188). After applying the exclusion criteria described in the Methods, we obtained a final cohort of 134,343 participants. In this manuscript, we describe this as a relatively healthy population, defined as individuals free of self-reported cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease and not on long-term cardiac medications at baseline, though variation in other conditions (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, or cancer) was still present. The U-shaped curve demonstrates that the lowest HRs for mortality fall within the reference non-HDL-C range of 160–179 mg/dL. This choice of reference group in our study is determined by this finding. It highlights that both very low (<100 mg/dL) and very high (≥200 mg/dL) non-HDL-C levels significantly increase all-cause mortality risk. The shaded area in the figure represents the 95 % CI, emphasizing the importance of maintaining optimal non-HDL-C levels and underlining the potential health risks associated with deviations from this range. This underscores the intricate relationship between cholesterol management and overall health outcomes.

Table 3 presents the adjusted HRs (with 95 % CIs) for all-cause mortality relative to the reference (160–179 mg/dL), with HR = 1.00. Notably, participants with non-HDL-C levels <100 mg/dL exhibited a significantly heightened mortality risk in the 40–79 age group for both sexes (combined HRs: 1.42; females: 1.41; males: 1.43). Participants with non-HDL-C levels in the range of 100–119 mg/dL showed a significantly increased mortality risk for both sexes (HR: 1.19), females (HR: 1.2), and males (HR: 1.17). Participants with non-HDL-C levels in the range of 120–139 mg/dL had a significantly increased mortality risk for both sexes (HR: 1.11) and males (HR: 1.12). However, the extent of this risk varied based on age and sex. An exception was observed in women aged 40–49 years: non-HDL-C <100 mg/dL was associated with a lower, non-significant mortality risk (HR 0.87; 95 % CI 0.62–1.21), which likely reflects limited events and imprecision in this younger subgroup. Among individuals with very high non-HDL-C levels (≥200 mg/dL), the data reveals a sex-specific pattern. Females in the youngest age group (40–49) exhibited a particularly notable increase in mortality risk (HR: 1.72), highlighting their potential vulnerability at this cholesterol level. Conversely, males in this category also showed a statistically significant increase in risk (HR: 1.36). However, the highest non-HDL-C group did not demonstrate significant mortality risk after 50 years old, while the lower non-HDL-C groups consistently showed an increased mortality risk. These findings should not be interpreted to mean that higher non-HDL-C is beneficial or acceptable in older adults. Rather, they highlight the complexity of lipid–mortality associations across age groups, which remain observational in nature and should not supersede established guideline recommendations for lipid management.

Table 4 details how various characteristics and lifestyle choices influence mortality risk across the entire age range (40–79 years), further differentiated by sex. This study explored the relationships between other factors and mortality. Being underweight (BMI <18.5) was correlated to a higher mortality risk (HR = 1.38, 95 % CI: 1.25–1.52). Conversely, higher educational attainment demonstrated a protective effect, with HRs decreasing as the educational level increased (0.86 for junior high/high school, and 0.73 for college or above; 95 % CI: 0.82–0.90, 0.69–0.77). Additionally, diabetes mellitus, defined by glucose levels >126 mg/dL, was significantly associated with an increased risk of mortality (HR = 1.43, 95 % CI: 1.34–1.53). The analysis revealed significant sex differences in mortality risk. Males inherently faced a higher baseline risk (HR = 1.45, 95 % CI: 1.38–1.52) further amplified by specific lifestyle choices. Current smoking substantially elevated mortality risk, with HRs of 1.66 (95 % CI: 1.57–1.75). Dietary choices such as fish and meat were modestly protective.

Discussion

This large-scale, community-based cohort of 134,343 healthy Taiwanese adults (aged 40–79) demonstrated a U-shaped relationship between non-HDL-C and all-cause mortality over a mean 14-year follow-up. Our results suggest that moderately high (160–179 mg/dL) non-HDL-C is associated with the lowest mortality risk, whereas levels below 100 mg/dL or above 200 mg/dL confer higher risk. This pattern was especially pronounced in younger adults (40–49), with females showing a particularly elevated hazard at ≥200 mg/dL. In contrast, we noted a non-significant trend toward lower mortality among women aged 40–49 years with non-HDL-C <100 mg/dL. Given the small number of deaths in this subgroup and the wide confidence intervals, this apparent protective association should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect chance variation or residual confounding rather than a robust biological effect. While estrogen-related cardiometabolic protection in premenopausal women could attenuate risk at lower lipid levels, the absence of statistical significance after full adjustment argues against a definitive conclusion.

This finding is particularly relevant in Asia, where diet and lifestyle may differ from Western countries, potentially impacting lipid metabolism and cardiovascular health. Region-specific guidelines and interventions are crucial for effectively managing non-HDL-C levels, improving cardiovascular health, and ultimately reducing mortality risk in Asian populations.

Our findings align with previous studies [17,18]. Specifically, a US analysis using data from the NHANES database (1999–2014) also identified a U-shaped relationship between non-HDL-C and mortality [19]. However, their optimal non-HDL-C range (136–163 mg/dL) differed from ours. The role of non-HDL-C in secondary prevention, where the focus is on managing existing conditions, presents a complex picture. Some studies observe a J- or U-shaped curve in higher-risk populations, while others note minimal associations in older adults [8,20,21]. Additionally, underlying health issues like malnutrition or chronic inflammation might confound interpretations at the very low range. Future studies could specifically analyze cause-specific mortality or exclude baseline cancer, but these were beyond our scope.

Our findings highlight a significant role for sex in the relationship between non-HDL-C levels and mortality risk. Males consistently exhibited a higher risk of mortality compared to females, and younger females (40–49) were disproportionately affected by ≥ 200 mg/dL, which possibly related to estrogen’s protective role [22]. Mechanistically, non-HDL-C includes LDL and other atherogenic particles that are implicated in atherogenesis [[23], [24], [25]]. Very low levels may reflect underlying diseases or cachexia, while very high levels promote plaque formation, leading to CVD events over time [[26], [27], [28]]. It is also possible that low non-HDL-C levels overlap with low BMI or body weight, as underweight status (BMI <18.5) was independently associated with higher mortality in our cohort (Table 4). This suggests that part of the increased risk in the lowest non-HDL-C category may be attributable to malnutrition, chronic illness, or systemic inflammation. A balanced range thus seems crucial for longevity. We acknowledge that multiple lifestyle variables (e.g., smoking, diet) may also interact with non-HDL-C, but we have deliberately minimized secondary findings to maintain focus on the main result.

While our stratified analysis revealed meaningful age-specific trends, we acknowledge that formal interaction testing or spline-based modeling of age and non-HDL-C would provide a more rigorous assessment of effect modification. Future studies should explore these approaches to better define optimal age-specific thresholds.

This study’s strengths lie in its large Asian population (134,343 participants), extended follow-up (up to 18 years), and comprehensive data on potential confounders. However, its observational nature precludes definitive causal conclusions, and self-reported data may incur recall bias. As an observational retrospective design, our study demonstrates associations but cannot establish causality, and residual confounding remains possible. Reverse causation is another concern, as individuals with very low cholesterol levels and early mortality may reflect underlying illness rather than a protective effect of low non-HDL-C. Although exclusion of early deaths would help address this, such sensitivity analyses were not feasible due to database constraints and limited event numbers in subgroups. In addition, cause-specific mortality data were not consistently available, restricting our analyses to all-cause mortality.

A limitation of our study is that cardiovascular disease was excluded based on self-reported history rather than ICD diagnostic codes, as systematic coding was not available in this dataset. This reliance on self-report may have led to some degree of misclassification or underreporting. Furthermore, while participants on long-term cardiac medications were excluded, we lacked comprehensive data on statin use and other treatments, which could influence cholesterol levels and outcomes. Dietary variables and socioeconomic indicators were also limited in scope, and these unmeasured or incompletely measured factors may contribute to residual confounding.

We also retained participants with baseline cancer (2.3 % of the cohort) to maintain the real-world generalizability of our sample; however, we acknowledge that their inclusion may have introduced residual confounding, given the elevated mortality risk associated with cancer. Additionally, by excluding individuals on long-term cardiac medications, we may have removed participants with a higher baseline burden of dyslipidemia or cardiovascular risk. This approach, while necessary to reduce confounding from treatment effects, may limit the generalizability of our findings to populations already receiving cardiac therapy.

Future work might refine these results by examining cause-specific mortality, excluding individuals with serious comorbidities, such as cancer at baseline, analyzing subgroups more deeply via RCS interaction terms, or exploring alternative lipoprotein measures. In addition, HbA1c data were not comprehensively available in this cohort, and fasting glucose was therefore used as the covariate for glycemic status. We acknowledge that this may not fully capture long-term glycemic control. Moreover, medical history in this cohort was restricted to conditions routinely captured in the MJ Health Examination database (cancer, asthma, nephritis, and gout). Data on other chronic diseases, such as chronic kidney disease or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, were not systematically available, which may have led to residual confounding.

Conclusion

In a healthy Taiwanese population aged 40–79, non-HDL-C levels below 100 mg/dL and above 200 mg/dL conferred heightened mortality risk, while maintaining levels around 160–179 mg/dL was linked to the most favorable survival. Our results emphasize the importance of identifying moderate, age-appropriate non-HDL-C targets in routine health management.
