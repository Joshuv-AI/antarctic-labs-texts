Neutrophils induce effective antibody responses to the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine by inhibiting T regulatory cells
Authors: Essi Y I Tchalla, Anagha Betadpur, Andrew Y Khalil, Elena Lopez, Manmeet Bhalla, Musea Chang, Elizabeth A Wohlfert, Elsa N Bou Ghanem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeutrophils are required for production of protective antibodies in response to the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV); however, the mechanisms behind that are not known. Using a mouse model, we found that depletion of neutrophils at the time of vaccination in female mice led to aberrant T-cell responses in the spleen, resulting in increased T regulatory cells (Tregs) that was accompanied by a dysregulated cytokine environment. We found that after vaccination, neutrophils influx into secondary lymphoid organs and increase expression of T-cell engaging or inhibiting markers. When we depleted Tregs in neutrophil-deficient mice at the time of vaccination, the opsonic activity of the antibodies and the ability of the sera to protect naïve hosts against pneumococcal infection significantly increased compared with control mice. Importantly, we examined responses after PCV administration in human female participants and found that PCV altered neutrophil phenotype. Additionally, in vitro coculture of donor neutrophils with their peripheral blood mononuclear cells at 1 wk after vaccination resulted in lower percentage and proliferation of FOXP3+ T cells, showing the clinical relevance of this phenotype. This work reveals a new mechanism by which neutrophils ensure protective Ab responses to vaccination by suppressing Tregs.

Introduction

Neutrophils or polymorphonuclear cells (PMNs) are innate immune cells that are indispensable for the clearance of many infections and have been traditionally studied as antimicrobial effector cells.1 However, in the past decade, PMNs have become increasingly appreciated for their ability to orchestrate adaptive immune responses.2,3 In fact, they can affect B and T cells directly or via control of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) such as dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages.4–6 They can engage and stimulate T cells in response to antigens via expression of major histocompatibility complex-II (MHC-II), CD80, CD86, and/or CD11c7–10 or inhibit T cells via production of IL-10, reactive oxygen species, arginase, or by expressing PD-L1.11–14 The ectonucleotidases CD73 and CD39 have been associated with T-cell inhibition via adenosine production and are also expressed on PMNs.15,16 PMNs can also suppress B-cell responses to protein antigens17–19 or activate B-cell responses to bacterial carbohydrates through secretion of B-cell activating factor, A Proliferation-Inducing Ligand, IL-21, or Pentraxin 3.20,21

Although most of the work examining PMN interaction with adaptive immune cells have used model antigens, a few studies have recently focused on the role of PMNs in vaccine responses.22 In an adenovirus–simian immunodeficiency virus vaccination model of Rhesus macaques, PMNs were found to migrate to the vaccine draining lymph nodes (vLNs) and acquire a B-cell helper phenotype as indicated by expression of B-cell activating factor, IL-21, and TNF-α among others.22 PMNs were found to be required for effective immune responses to both tuberculosis and leishmaniasis live-attenuated vaccine models.23,24 In the context of a clinically relevant vaccine, we showed that PMNs are required at the time of vaccination for induction of protective antibody responses to the licensed pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) Prevnar.25 In fact, depleting PMNs at the time of PCV vaccination led to a defect in antibody isotype switching to IgG2c and IgG3 and a decline in antibody function.25 Furthermore, mice that lacked PMNs at the time of vaccination had a significantly impaired ability to control and survive a subsequent pneumococcal infection.25 However, the mechanisms by which PMNs control antibody responses to PCV remain unclear. This is of high clinical relevance because PCV protects against infections caused by the bacterium Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus), which remain a leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia globally, particularly in vulnerable populations such as older adults.26 Understanding the mechanisms behind vaccine efficacy can lead to design of better vaccines.

Conjugate vaccines such as PCV induce T follicular helper cells (Tfhs) that aid the production of antibodies by B cells.27,28 In fact, PCV vaccination in humans induced the formation of Tfhs, which was linked to enhanced antibody responses.29 After vaccination with a conjugate vaccine, germinal centers (GCs) form and mature within secondary lymphoid organs such as the lymph nodes (LNs) and spleen.30,31 Inside GCs, polysaccharide-specific B cells acquire conjugate antigens from the surface of follicular DCs and process them intracellularly for presentation to Tfhs.30–32 Interaction between the Tfhs and GC B cells triggers B-cell differentiation into antibody-secreting cells and enhances antibody production, class switching, and affinity maturation.32,33 These antibodies are required for efficient host protection against infections because they facilitate binding, uptake, and killing of bacteria by phagocytes.34,35 Antibody responses by B cells are also highly influenced by cytokines, which are secreted by Tfhs as well as other T-cell subsets.27,36,37

A subset of T cells known to control immune responses are T regulatory cells (Tregs). Tregs have suppressive capacity and play an essential role in homeostasis and immune tolerance.38 In the context of vaccination, Tregs limit protective immune responses.39–44 In multiple HIV and cancer models of vaccination, Tregs dampen both cellular and humoral immune responses, leading to viral replication or tumor progression.45,46 In a pneumococcal polysaccharides model of vaccination, blocking the Treg effector molecule CTLA-4 enhanced antibody and cytokine responses to the polysaccharides.47 In humans, higher numbers ofTregs in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of older adults were associated with lower CD4+ T-cell responses to immunogenic pneumococcal proteins.48 A subset of Tregs that gain access to B-cell follicles are termed T follicular regulatory cells (Tfrs).49,50 Tfrs display phenotypic markers of Tfhs, in addition to their expression of FOPX3.49,51 They play a role in humoral responses to antigens that is context dependent, and they have been shown to inhibit B-cell responses and accelerate GC contraction.52–54 Several studies have looked at the relationship between Tregs and PMNs in models of inflammation and disease and found that Tregs modulate PMN recruitment to the site of inflammation.55–57 PMNs, in turn, also promote Treg recruitment in autoimmune and cancer models, and their depletion leads to a decrease in Tregs.58–61 The interaction of PMNs and Tregs during vaccine responses (including PCV) has not been studied, to our knowledge.

In this study, we tested the effect of PMNs on adaptive immune responses to PCV using a preclinical murine model of vaccination as well as by examining responses in vaccinated human participants. We found that after vaccination, PMNs acquire a T-cell interactive phenotype and suppress Tregs, which is required for the production of protective antibodies. In this study, we uncovered a mechanism, not before reported, to our knowledge, by which PMNs shape vaccine responses through the control of Tregs and that provides novel targets for the design of more effective vaccines against bacterial infections.

Materials and methods

Mice

Adult (10 to 12 wk old) female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine). Our study focused on female hosts because there is a well-documented sex-based difference in responses to PCV: female hosts elicit higher antibody responses and are better protected from subsequent infection than are male hosts.62–64 Therefore, we examined the mechanisms behind vaccine efficacy in the better protected hosts. Ongoing work in our laboratory is focused on sex-based difference in PMN responses in vaccinated hosts,63 which is beyond the scope of this study.

Adult (10 to 16 wk old) C57BL/6-Tg(Foxp3-DTR/EGFP)23.2Spar/Mmjax DEREG mice were a gift from Dr. Elizabeth Wohlfert (University at Buffalo) and have been previously described in detail.65,66 All mice were housed in a specific pathogen–free environment at the University at Buffalo Jacobs School of Medicine and Biomedical Sciences animal facility.

Human participants

Healthy unvaccinated female participants (24 to 29 yr old) were recruited. Individuals who were pregnant, had acute infections within the past 2 wk, took medication 48 h prior to initiating the study, had history of chronic infections, had conditions that altered immunity (eg autoimmunity, use of immune-modulating drugs, history of cancer) were excluded from the study. At the first visit (week 0), donors had blood samples collected and then were administered the PCV Prevnar 20 (Pfizer, Wyeth Pharmaceuticals) intramuscularly, per manufacturer's instructions. At weeks 1 and 4 after vaccination, donors had blood samples collected for subsequent PMN phenotyping. Blood samples from week 1 also were used for coculture assays. All blood was collected into tubes containing sodium citrate and visits occurred in the morning (8 AM to 10 AM) to control for circadian variations in PMN phenotype.67 All enrolled participants signed approved informed consent forms.

Study approvals

All work with mice was performed in accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the University at Buffalo Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval no. MIC33018Y). All work with human donors was approved by the University at Buffalo Human investigation Review Board (approval no. STUDY00007111).

Murine immunization and sera collection

Mice were vaccinated intramuscularly with 50 μL of the PCV into the caudal thigh muscle. At weeks 0, 2, and 4 after vaccination, 10 μL of blood from each mouse was collected from the tail and used for assessment of antibody titers. Sera samples from each mouse group were collected via cardiac puncture 4 wk after vaccination, then pooled and aliquoted to assess antibody quantity and functions in subsequent assays.

Bacteria

Streptococcus pneumoniae TIGR4 strain (serotype 4) was gift from Andrew Camilli (Tufts University). Bacteria were grown to mid-exponential phase in Todd–Hewitt broth (BD Biosciences) supplemented with Oxyrase at 37 °C and 5% carbon dioxide, aliquoted in growth medium supplemented with 20% glycerol, and stored at −80 °C, as previously described.25,63 Prior to use, bacteria were thawed on ice, washed, and enumerated by serial dilution in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by dribbled plating on tryptic soy agar plates supplemented with 5% sheep blood (Northeast Laboratory Services).

Murine neutrophil depletion

Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 50 μg of the Ly6G-depleting antibody IA8 or isotype IgG control on the day before vaccination and on days 1, 4, and 7 after vaccination and as previously described.25 We previously verified that this depletion protocol was specific to PMNs and did not change the number of circulating and splenic monocytes, DCs, or macrophages.25 PMN depletion in the spleen was assessed by a myeloperoxidase ELISA (Invitrogen), per manufacturer's instructions, and by flow cytometry.25

Murine T regulatory cell depletion

B6 mice were treated intraperitoneally with 300 μg of the CD25-depleting antibody PC-61.5.3 or isotype IgG1 control 2 days before vaccination, the day of vaccination, and on days 2, 4, 6, and 8 after vaccination. DEREG mice were treated intraperitoneally with 1 μg of diphtheria toxin (Millipore) or DMSO as vehicle control on the same schedule as the B6 mice. Depletion efficiency was assessed by flow cytometry.

Murine organ/tissue processing and flow cytometry

Blood, spleens, and/or vLNs (inguinal and popliteal) were harvested from euthanized mice. Single-cell suspensions from spleens and vLNs were prepared in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After red blood cell lysis with an in-house hypotonic solution (8.29 g ammonium chloride, 1 g sodium bicarbonate, 0.038 g trypsin/ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA], and 1 L of water), the remaining cells were enumerated, incubated with Fc block (2.4G2) and Live/Dead dye (Invitrogen), and stained with additional antibodies in separate panels.

PMNs were stained with Ly6G (1A8), CD11b (M1/70), CXCR4 (2B11), CCR7 (4B12), CD80 (16-10A1), CD86 (GL1), CD11c (N418), MHC-II (M5/114.15.2), PD-L1 (MIH5), CD39 (24DMS1), and/or CD73 (TY/11.8) antibodies followed by permeabilization with Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD) and intracellular staining with ARG1 (A1exF5).

B cells were stained with CD45R/B220 (RA3-6B2), CD138 (281-2), CD95 (SA367H8), and CD38 (90) antibodies followed by permeabilization with Fix/Perm buffer from the FOXP3/transcription factor staining buffer set (eBioscience) and intracellular staining with activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) (Polyclonal, Bioss).

T cells were stained with CD4 (RM4-5), TCR-β (H57-597), CD11a (M17/4), CD49d (R1-2), PD1 (J43), and/or CXCR5 (SPRCL5) antibodies followed by permeabilization with Fix/Perm buffer from the FOXP3/Transcription factor staining buffer set (eBioscience) and intracellular staining with FOXP3 (FJK-16 s), RORγT (Q31-378), Tbet (eBio4B10), and GATA3 (L580-823).

For intracellular cytokine staining in PMNs, cells were incubated in Brefeldin A in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (pen/strep) and stimulated with 9 × 106 CFU/well heat-killed S. pneumoniae TIGR4 strain, 10 μL/well PCV, 100 nM phorbol-12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), or medium alone for 3 h. Cells were then washed and stained extracellularly with Fc block, Live/Dead dye, Ly6G, and CD11b antibodies, followed by intracellular staining with IFN-γ (XMG1.2) after permeabilization with Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD). For intracellular cytokine staining in T cells, cells were incubated in Brefeldin A (BD) in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% pen/strep, and stimulated with a mix of 81 nM PMA and 1.34 μM ionomycin (cell activation cocktail; Biolegend) or medium alone for 2 h. Cells were then washed and stained extracellularly with Fc block, Live/Dead dye, and CD4, TCR-β, CD44 (IM7), and latency-associated peptide (LAP) (TW7-16B4) antibodies followed by intracellular staining with IFN-γ (XMG1.2), IL-17A (TC11-18H10), IL-4 (11B11), and IL-10 (JES5-16E3) antibodies after permeabilization with Fix/Perm buffer from the FOXP3/transcription factor staining buffer set (eBioscience). After staining, cells were washed with FACS buffer (Hanks’s balanced salt solution [HBSS], 1% FBS, and 0.1% sodium azide), and fluorescence was assessed using a BD Fortessa cytometer. At least 20,000 cells in the live, CD4+TCR-β+ and B220+ cells’ gates were collected and analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Murine antibody enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Antibodies in sera were assessed using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay as previously described.25,68 Briefly, Nunc-maxisorp plates (Thermo Scientific) were coated at 4 °C overnight with type 4 pneumococcal polysaccharide (ATCC) at 2 μg/well. The next day, plates were washed in PBS supplemented with 0.25% Tween, and serum, preabsorbed with a pneumococcal cell-wall polysaccharide mixture (CWP-multi, Cederlane), was added to the plates. After a 2-h incubation, plates were washed and incubated with diluted horseradish peroxidase conjugated anti-mouse IgM (Invitrogen), IgG (Millipore Sigma), or IgG1, IgG2b, IgG2c, or IgG3 (Southern Biotech). One hour later, plates were washed, tetramethylbenzidine substrate (Thermo Scientific) was added, and absorbance was read at 650 nm using a Biotek microplate reader for 10 min at 1-min intervals. Antibody units were calculated as a percentage of standard sera included in each assay. Standard sera samples were obtained from mice that were intranasally inoculated with S. pneumoniae serotype 4 weekly for 3 wk and immunized with PCV at week 4.

Murine IFN-γ ELISA

Single-cell suspensions of splenocytes were prepared in 2 mL of RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. IFN-γ concentrations were quantified using the Mouse IFN-γ ELISA kit (Invitrogen), per the manufacturer's instructions.

Murine IFN-γ blocking in vivo

Mice were treated intraperitoneally with 200 μg of the IFN-γ–blocking antibody XMG1.2 or isotype control following same schedule as neutrophil depletion at days −1, 1, 4, and 7 with respect to vaccination.

Murine cytokine/chemokine array

Pan cytokines were assessed using the Proteome Profiler Mouse XL Cytokine Array kit (R&D, ARY028) following the manufacturer's instructions as previously described.63 Briefly, single-cell suspensions of splenocytes were prepared in 2 mL of RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Cell suspensions were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. Samples were thawed on ice prior to use, lysed with Triton-X (VWR, 0694), and quantified for protein concentrations using the Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher) per the manufacturer's instructions. All samples were brought to the same protein concentrations and used for pan-cytokine assessment. Protein membranes were read using the BioRad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System, and the intensity for each dot on the blots was quantified using the QuickSpots Image Analysis Software. The intensity values of each blot were blanked using their respective buffer-only controls. Intensity values across multiple blots were normalized using the blot with the highest standard. Fold change of the values from PMN-depleted mice over those of PMN-sufficient mice were calculated and log transformed.

Murine splenocyte culture assays

Single-cell suspensions of splenocytes were prepared in 2 mL of RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS 1 d after vaccination. To remove PMNs from the obtained suspensions, splenocytes were stained with a phycoerythrin (PE)-labeled anti-Ly6G antibody and treated using the EasySep Release Mouse PE Positive Selection Kit (StemCell), following the manufacturer's instruction. One million cells per condition were used and treated with 5 × 107 CFU heat-killed S. pneumoniae or 1 μL/mL αCD3 (positive control). Cells were cocultured for 2 d before assessing percentages of Tregs and other T-cell subsets.

Antibody binding to the surface of S. pneumoniae

Serum samples collected 4 wk after vaccination were used to assess antibody binding to S. pneumoniae (TIGR4), as previously described.25,63 After 30 min of incubation at 37 °C, cells were washed with FACS buffer and labeled with an APC-tagged anti-mouse IgG (polyclonal) antibody for 30 min on ice in the dark. Cells were then washed, resuspended in FACS buffer, and fluorescence was assessed using a BD Fortessa cytometer. At least 10,000 cells were analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Murine opsonophagocytic killing assay

Neutrophils were isolated from the bone marrow of mice using density centrifugation with Histopaque 1119 and Histopaque 1077 (Sigma), as previously described.25,68,69 Briefly, neutrophils were resuspended in HBSS (without Ca2+ or Mg2+) medium supplemented with 0.1% gelatin and used in the assay. Sera samples collected 4 wk after vaccination were incubated with S. pneumoniae TIGR4 for 5 min and added to neutrophils in HBSS (with Ca2+ and Mg2+). After a 40-min incubation at 37 °C, reactions were plated on tryptic soy agar plates with 5% sheep blood. Percent killing was calculated relative to a no-neutrophils control for each serum condition.

Murine adoptive transfer of sera

Four weeks after immunization, naïve, vaccinated, neutrophil- and Treg-sufficient mice; neutrophil-depleted mice; neutrophils- and Treg-depleted mice; and Treg-depleted mice were euthanized. Blood was harvested via cardiac puncture; serum samples were obtained from the blood and pooled for each mouse group. A volume of 250 μL of serum was transferred intraperitoneally into naïve recipients, which were infected 1 h later.

Animal infection

Mice were infected intratracheally with 3 × 105 CFU of S. pneumoniae serotype 4 (TIGR4) using the tongue-pull method as previously described.25,70 Mice were monitored for survival for a total of 7 d after infection and assessed daily for bacteremia by plating blood collected in 50 mM EDTA on blood agar plates for enumeration of bacterial CFU.

Human PMN isolation and flow cytometry

From human participants, PMNs were isolated, using the EasySep Direct Human Neutrophil Isolation Kit (StemCell) following the manufacturer’s instructions, from peripheral blood of donors at vaccination and at weeks 1 and 4 after vaccination. For each donor and visit, 1 × 105 PMNs were added to each well and then stained with CD16 (eBioCB16), CD14 (61D3), CD10 (MEM-78), HLA-DR (L243), CD86 (BU63), PD-L1 (MIH1), CD73 (AD2), and/or CD39 (eBioA1) antibodies, followed by permeabilization with Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD) and intracellular staining with ARG1 (A1exF5). After staining, cells were washed with FACS buffer and fluorescence was assessed using a BD Fortessa cytometer. At least 5,000 cells in the CD16+ gate were collected and analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Human PMN flow cytometry–specific self-organizing map analysis

Analysis and data visualization of the PMN phenotype with and without vaccination over time were completed using software OMIQ software (Dotmatics). Traditional gating was done to differentiate the neutrophil population and all the markers. Dimensional reduction then was performed with optimized parameters for T-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (opt-SNE), which improves visualization of large datasets by reducing it to a 2- or 3-dimensional map while preserving local structure. Clustering into neutrophil subsets was done using the flow cytometry–specific self-organizing map (FLOWSOM) algorithm.71 Clustered heat maps were made based on meta-cluster gating to characterize the different PMN subsets based on the expression of different markers.

Human PMN and PBMC coculture assays

For human participants, PBMCs and PMNs were isolated from peripheral blood samples of 1-wk vaccinated donors, using the One-Step Polymorph (Accurate Chemical) and EasySep Direct Human Neutrophil Isolation Kit (StemCell), respectively, following manufacturers’ instructions. For each donor, 2 × 105 PBMCs were incubated in RPMI medium with 10% FBS with or without addition of 5 × 104 PMNs and stimulated with 10 μL of PCV. Cells were cocultured for 2 d before assessing percentages and proliferation of T cells. At 48 h after incubation, T cells were stained with CD11a (HI111), PD1 (EH12.1), CD4 (S3.5), CD49d (9F10), and CXCR5 (MU5UBEE), followed by permeabilization with Fix/Perm buffer from the FOXP3/transcription factor staining buffer set (eBioscience) and intracellular staining with FOXP3 (PCH101), RORγT (AFJKS-9), Tbet (eB4B10), GATA3 (L580-823), and Ki67 (B56). After staining, cells were washed with FACS buffer, and data were collected using a BD Fortessa cytometer. At least 10,000 cells in the live CD4+ gate were collected and analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was done using GraphPad Prism, version 11, software. Data were checked for normality distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Significant differences were determined by 1-way analysis of variance followed by Šídák's, Holm–Šídák's, Tukey's, or Dunnett's multiple comparisons tests; Kruskal–Wallis 1-way analysis of variance was followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test, Student's t test, or 1-sample t test, as appropriate. Survival was analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. All P values <0.05 were considered significant.

Results

PMNs home to secondary lymphoid organs in response to PCV

In prior work, we showed that PMNs are required at the time of PCV administration for the induction of effective antibody response and optimal host protection against pneumococcal disease.25 There is a well-documented sex-based difference in responses to PCV whereby females individuals elicit higher antibody responses and vaccine efficacy than male individuals64,72,73, and we recapitulated this in preclinical murine models.63 Therefore, to understand mechanisms of protection, we focused our studies on female hosts, which mount higher humoral and cellular responses to PCV and are better protected against subsequent pneumococcal infection compared with male hosts.63

To better understand how PMNs control PCV efficacy, we first asked whether they are recruited to secondary lymphoid organs (ie spleen and vLNs) in response to vaccination. We looked at expression of the migration markers CXCR4 and CCR7 on circulating PMNs 24 h after vaccination, because they are involved in PMN trafficking to the LNs via the lymphatic system or high endothelial venules (Figure S1 and Fig. 1A).74,75 We found that, at baseline, a low percentage of circulating PMNs expressed CXCR4 and CCR7 and, upon vaccination, CCR7+ PMNs significantly increased (Fig. 1A). This higher percentage of CCR7+ PMNs coincided with a decrease in the number of circulating PMNs 18 to 24 h after vaccination and an influx of PMNs to the spleen and vLNs at those time points, respectively (Fig. 1B). Following the peak of PMN influx into the spleen and vLNs, PMN numbers returned to baseline by 48 h after vaccination (Fig. 1B). These findings demonstrate that PMNs are rapidly recruited to secondary lymphoid organs in response to PCV.

PMNs do not affect B-cell numbers in response to PCV

To start investigating how PMNs induce protective antibodies to PCV, we first examined changes to the B-cell compartment. We depleted PMNs at the time of vaccination, as presented in Figure S2A and as previously described.25 The efficiency of PMN depletion in the spleen was assessed over the 28 d after vaccination. We observed >90% PMN depletion at day 7 by both myeloperoxidase ELISA (Figure S2B) and flow cytometry (Figure S2c), with a return to steady-state numbers in the spleen by day 28 (Figure S2B) as we had previously reported in prior studies.25 We then investigated B-cell responses after PMN depletion in the spleen on days 7, 14, and 21 after vaccination (Figure S3). Upon PMN depletion, there was no change in the percentage and numbers of total B cells (B220+) in the spleen compared with isotype-treated mice at any of the time points investigated (Figure S4A). Similarly, no effect of PMN depletion was observed on GC B cells (CD38loCD96+) and plasmablasts (CD138+) (Figure S4B and C).

In prior work, we had observed a defect in isotype switching and opsonic and binding capacities of antibodies in response to PCV in PMN-deficient hosts,25 indicating a possible defect on somatic hypermutation and/or class switch recombination. Therefore, in the present study, on days 7 after vaccination, we assessed B-cell expression of AID, an enzyme required for both somatic hypermutation and class switch recombination76,77 (Figure S3B). For both total and GC B cells, there was an increase in AID expression in mice after vaccination (Figure S5). However, depleting PMNs had no effect on the percentage of AID+ B cells or the expression of AID by B cells (Figure S5). These findings suggest PMNs are not directly acting on B cells during vaccination.

PMNs do not significantly affect macrophages and DCs in response to PCV

We then looked at the effect of PMNs on other immune cells important for vaccine responses, such as APCs.7,78 To do so, we depleted PMNs at the time of vaccination (Figure S2A) and analyzed the percentage of splenic macrophages (F4/80+) and DCs (F480−CD11c+) or their respective expression of antigen-presenting molecules MHC-II, CD80, and CD86 on days 7, 14, and 21 after vaccination (Figure S6). Overall, we found that depletion of PMNs had no effect on the percentages of either macrophages or DCs present in the spleen (Figure S7A). No substantial difference in the percentage of CD80-, CD86-, and MHC-II–expressing DCs and macrophages was observed between PMN-depleted and PMN-sufficient mice on days 7 to 14 after vaccination (Figure S7B and C). At 21 d after vaccination, PMN depletion resulted in increased CD86+ DCs and MHC-II+ macrophages (Figure S7B and C). These findings suggest PMNs are not significantly affecting macrophages or DCs immediately upon vaccination.

Absence of PMNs at the time of vaccination alters the cytokine environment in the spleen

The cytokine environment in secondary lymphoid organs controls the quality of antibodies produced.79 Therefore, we next looked more broadly at the overall inflammatory environment in the spleen in the presence or absence of PMNs in vaccinated mice using a cytokine protein array. We found that, overall, in the absence of PMNs at the time of vaccination, there was an increase in inflammatory mediators in the splenic milieu compared with when PMNs are present (Figure S8). This increase in inflammation was seen at day 7 and resolved by days 14 through 21 after vaccination (Figure S8), time points that coincided with return of PMNs after depletion (Figure S2B).25 Notably, unlike in vaccinated mice, depleting PMNs in naïve hosts did not lead to an increase in inflammation at day 7 (Figure S8). These data show that the absence of PMNs at the time of vaccination leads to an altered inflammatory response in the spleen, suggesting a regulatory role for PMNs during PCV.

We had observed a defect in isotype switching to IgG2 and IgG3 antibodies in the absence of PMNs25; therefore, we directly tested whether PMNs controlled effective antibody response to PCV through IFN-γ, because this cytokine is required for class switching to those isotypes.33,80 We measured the percentage of IFN-γ+ PMNs in the spleen 18 h after vaccination following ex vivo stimulation with PMA, heat-killed S. pneumoniae, or PCV (Figure S9). We found that more PMNs express IFN-γ when stimulated with PMA; however, there was no change in the percentage of IFN-γ+ PMNs after vaccination and upon restimulation (Figure S10A). Similarly, total IFN-γ levels in the spleen within the first 28 d of vaccination were comparable between PMN-depleted and PMN-sufficient mice (Figure S10B). Additionally, when we blocked IFN-γ in vivo at the time of vaccination using an αIFN-γ antibody following the same schedule as for the depletion of PMNs shown in Figure S2A, there was no effect on bacterial burden in the lung and blood at 24 h after infection (Figure S10C) or on host survival (Figure S10D) compared with the isotype-treated mice. To verify that the blocking antibody was functioning, we measured isotype switching to IgG2, which is controlled by IFN-γ,33 and found that blocking IFN-γ led to an increase in IgG1 antibodies and decrease in IgG2b and IgG2c (Figure S10E). In summary, these data indicate that, overall, the control of pneumococcal antibody responses by PMNs is not mediated by IFN-γ.

PMNs acquire a T-cell interactive phenotype upon entry into secondary lymphoid organs

To better understand how PMNs control PCV efficacy, we assessed how they respond to vaccination. PMNs acquire different phenotypes depending on entry into organs and tissues and the composition of the tissue microenvironment.81,82 Therefore, we examined PMN phenotype within the first 24 h after vaccination, when they influx into secondary lymphoid organs (Fig. 1B). Because the cytokine responses in the spleen suggested that PMNs play a regulatory role, we looked at the expression of various T-cell engaging (MHC-II, CD80, CD86, and CD11c) and suppressing (PD-L1, CD73, CD39, and ARG1) markers on PMNs (Figure S1B). In all sites investigated, overall, expression of both T-cell engaging and suppressing markers on PMNs did not change upon vaccination except for a decrease in PD-L1+ PMNs in the blood and spleen (Figures S11 and S12). However, there were tissue-specific differences regardless of vaccination status (Figures S11 and S12 and Fig. 1C). Expression of CD80, CD86, CD11c MHC-II, and ARG1 were higher on PMNs in secondary lymphoid organs compared with circulating PMNs in both naïve and vaccinated mice (Figures S11 and S12 and Fig. 1C). These data are consistent with the literature showing that entry into tissues instructs PMNs to acquire organ-specific phenotypes.81,82 Additionally, the data suggest that as PMNs influx into secondary lymphoid organs after vaccination (Fig. 1B), they acquire a tissue-specific T-cell interactive phenotype (Fig. 1C). As this PMN population decreases in the blood and enters secondary lymphoid organs (Fig. 1B), it may play a role in T-cell responses at those sites.

PMNs do not affect T helper cell responses to PCV

To test the effect of PMNs on T-cell responses, we depleted PMNs at the time of vaccination (Figure S2A) and measured the percentages and numbers of T helper 1 (Th1) (CD4+TCRβ+FOXP3−Tbet+), Th2 (CD4+TCRβ+FOXP3−GATA3+), Th17 (CD4+TCRβ+FOXP3−RORγt+), and Tfh (CD4+TCRβ+CXCR5+PD1+) cells and in the spleen 7, 14, and 21 d after vaccination (Figure S13). We found no difference in the percentages or numbers of any of the T-cell subsets in the spleen between vaccinated PMN-depleted and PMN-sufficient mice at any of the time points investigated (Figure S14).

Because examining total CD4+ T cells may mask changes within the smaller antigen-experienced population, we also looked at antigen-experienced cells by gating on CD49d+CD11a+ T cells (Figure S13A).83 Similarly, we found that PMN depletion had no effect on antigen-experienced Th1, Th2, Th17, and Tfh cells in the spleen (Figure S15).

Because the number of Th cells was not altered by depleting PMNs (Figures S14 and S15), we asked if their ability to produce cytokines was impaired. We examined cytokine responses ex vivo using PCV and PMA/ionomycin as positive stimulants for cytokine production. Using flow cytometry, we measured expression of IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-17, TGF-β, and IL-10 by splenic CD44hi-activated CD4+ T cells at day 14 after vaccination (Figure S16). We used the LAP as a proxy for TGF-β production because it acts as a chaperone for TGF-β and their levels correlate.84,85 The cytokine response was skewed toward production of IFN-γ and LAP, both in the presence and absence of PMNs, because the percentage of CD4+TCR-β+ T cells expressing these cytokines was higher than for the other cytokines investigated (Figure S17). Overall, PMA/ionomycin stimulation significantly increased LAP, IFN-γ, and IL-4 expression by CD44hi CD4+ T cells above baseline, whereas stimulation with PCV induced slight but not significant increases in these cytokines (Figure S17A to C). However, PMN depletion did not affect expression of any of the cytokines by T cells in comparison with PMN-sufficient mice in both stimulation conditions (Figure S17).

PMNs inhibit Treg expansion in the spleen in response to PCV

We next assessed the effect of PMNs on Tregs (CD4+TCRβ+FOXP3+) and Tfrs (CD4+TCRβ+CXCR5+PD1+FOXP3+) following PCV (Fig. 2). We saw an increase in the percentage of Tregs in the spleens of PMN-depleted mice compared with PMN-sufficient mice at day 14 after vaccination (Fig. 2A). In PMN-depleted mice, there was also an increase in the percentage and numbers of Tfrs at day 7 as well as an increase in percentage at day 21 after vaccination (Fig. 2B).

When looking at the antigen-experienced compartment, there was an increase in Tregs at days 7 and 14 after vaccination in the spleen of PMN-depleted mice compared with PMN-sufficient mice (Fig. 2C) and differences in the Tfr compartment at days 7 and 21 (Fig. 2D). No differences in Tregs or Tfrs were observed in the LNs (data not shown). Additionally, depleting PMNs in naïve hosts did not affect Tregs or any other T-cell subsets investigated (Figure S18). These data suggest PMNs inhibit the expansion of T regulatory cells in the spleen in response to PCV administration.

PMNs inhibit Treg expansion in response to PCV in vitro

To directly assess the effect of PMNs on Tregs, we used in vitro assays. For that, we performed 48 h culture assays of splenocytes isolated from day 1–vaccinated mice in the presence or absence of PMNs using heat-killed bacteria that contain bacterial polysaccharides or αCD3 as stimulants . PMNs were removed from splenocytes using positive isolation magnetic sorting. Although total CD4+ cells were not affected (Fig. 3A), similar to what we observed in vivo, in the absence of PMNs, there was an increase in the percentage of Tregs in response to stimulation with heat-killed S. pneumoniae (Fig. 3B). PMN removal did not alter other Th cell subsets (Fig. 3C–E). This recapitulates the in vivo findings and suggests that PMNs control Tregs’ response to bacterial polysaccharides.

Reducing Tregs in PMN-depleted hosts rescues antibody responses

Next, we tested whether the increase in T regulatory cells is responsible for the defective antibody responses seen in the absence of PMNs at the time of vaccination.25 To investigate this, we depleted Tregs using an αCD25 antibody86 either alone or in conjunction with PMN depletion using αLy6G (Figure S19A). We achieved ∼ 90% depletion of PMNs in the blood and ∼ 40% to 50% depletion of Tregs in both spleen and blood (Figure S19B and C). Moreover, αCD25 and αLy6G injections had no effect on percentages of Th1, Th2, Th17, Tfhs, and B cells in the spleen (Figure S19D). We then followed antibody levels upon Treg and/or PMN depletion in the blood of mice at weeks 0, 2, and 4 after vaccination. We found an increase in total IgG against pneumococcal polysaccharides in PMN- and Treg-depleted mice compared with PMN-depleted mice at 4 wk after vaccination (Fig. 4A).

We next investigated the ability of antibodies in sera to bind the surface of the pneumococcus (Fig. 4B). In all mouse groups, vaccination significantly increased binding of IgG to the bacteria compared with baseline (Fig. 4B). As previously reported, sera from vaccinated PMN-depleted mice had significantly lower IgG binding compared with sera from vaccinated PMN-sufficient mice (Fig. 4B).25 Interestingly, depletion of Tregs in PMN-depleted mice rescued IgG binding in the sera to similar levels to that of PMN-sufficient vaccinated mice, whereas depletion of Tregs alone did not affect IgG binding in the sera of PMN-sufficient vaccinated mice (Fig. 4B).

We also looked at the ability of antibodies in the sera to promote opsonophagocytic killing of the pneumococcus by PMNs isolated from naïve mice. As expected, there was an increase in bacterial killing by PMNs when sera from vaccinated versus naïve mice were used as opsonins (Fig. 4C). Similarly, depletion of Tregs in PMN-depleted mice rescued the ability of the sera to induce opsonophagocytic bacterial killing to levels similar to that in PMN-sufficient vaccinated mice, whereas depletion of Tregs alone did not affect the opsonic capacity of sera from PMN-sufficient vaccinated mice (Fig. 4C).

To confirm these findings, we used a different model of Treg depletion for assessment of antibody responses to PCV using diphtheria toxin–mediated depletion.66 For that, we used diphtheria toxin injections to deplete Tregs either alone or in conjunction with PMN depletion in DEREG mice65 (Figure S20A). We achieved ∼ 25% depletion of Tregs in the spleen (Figure S20B). Treg depletion in PMN-depleted DEREG mice increased their IgG levels 4 wk after vaccination compared with vehicle-treated PMN-depleted mice (Figure S20C). These data suggest the increase in Tregs seen in PMN-depleted mice contributes to the defective antibody response observed in these mice, because reduction of the Tregs rescues the levels and function of the antibodies to similar levels seen in PMN-sufficient vaccinated mice, whereas reduction of Tregs alone in PMN-sufficient vaccinated mice has no effect, possibly due to already high responses in young female mice.

Reducing Tregs in PMN-depleted hosts rescues the protective capacity of the sera against pneumococcal infection

Finally, we assessed the protectiveness of the sera from PMN- and/or Treg-depleted mice in vivo against infection by adoptively transferring sera from each group into naïve mice. Recipient mice were then infected 1 h later with 3 × 105 CFU of the S. pneumoniae TIGR4 strain, assessed for blood bacterial burden 48 h after infection, and monitored for survival. Recipients of sera from vaccinated PMN-sufficient mice were able to control bacterial numbers better than those that received sera from vaccinated PMN-depleted mice, with 50% and 87.5% of the mice, respectively, becoming bacteremic (Fig. 5A). Receiving sera from PMN- and Treg-depleted mice rescued the ability of the recipients to control infection: only 25% of the mice became bacteremic (Fig. 5A). Additionally, receiving sera from Treg-depleted mice did not affect control of blood bacterial burden (Fig. 5A).

Vaccination of human participants alters the phenotype of circulating PMNs

Because PMN responses vary significantly across species,87 we wanted to test the clinical relevance of these findings. To do so, we recruited unvaccinated adult female participants, administered PCV to them, and monitored the phenotype of PMNs in the circulation over time in response to vaccination by examining the expression of T-cell engaging and inhibiting markers using flow cytometry (Figure S21). To test if PMNs respond to PCV and alter their phenotype, we used the OMIQ software to perform opt-SNE analysis to visualize the data and cluster them using FLOWSOM. We found that PMNs separated into different clusters (Fig. 6A and B) with varying levels of maturity (based on CD10 expression). Clusters 3 and 6 expressed the highest levels of CD10, followed by clusters 2, 4, 1, 9, 7, 5, and 8 (Fig. 6C, Figure S22, and Table S1). Cluster 6 (most mature) was the only cluster that significantly expressed PD-L1 and had high levels of all the other markers, whereas cluster 8 (least mature) had very low expression of the markers we tested (Fig. 6C, Fig. S22, and Table S1). Upon vaccination, we observed a shift in the abundance of circulating clusters: clusters 1, 2, and 6 decreased in abundance, clusters 3, 4, and 5 increased, and clusters 7 and 8 did not change (Fig. 6B). The clusters that decreased from the circulation expressed the highest levels of CD39, ARG1, and HLADR (Fig. 6C, Figure S22, and Table S1) and may be homing to the tissues as we observed in mice.

When we examined the expression levels of the various markers at week 1 following vaccination, we found that expression of HLADR, CD86, CD39, and PD-L1 increased in all the clusters that expressed them. CD73 either increased or decreased depending on the cluster, whereas ARG1 decreased (Fig. 6C, Figure S22, and Table S1). Overall, these findings suggest that in response to PCV in humans, the phenotype of circulating PMNs changes, whereby particular clusters increase expression of T-cell engaging and suppressing markers and possibly exit from the circulation into tissues.

PMNs from PCV-immunized donors inhibit FOXP3+ T-cell expansion in vitro

Finally, we tested whether PMNs from vaccinated participants could suppress FOXP3+ CD4+ T cells. Because we found in the murine models that PMN depletion affected Treg numbers in vaccinated (Fig. 2), but not naïve, hosts (Figure S18), we focused here on assessing interactions after vaccination. Thus, at 1 wk after vaccination, a time point when we observed phenotypic changes in PMNs in response to vaccination (Fig. 6), we performed an in vitro assay in which PBMCs from each participant were cocultured with or without PMNs (from the same donor) and stimulated with PCV for 48 h before assessing T cells (Figure S23A and B). After culture for 48 h, PMN survival varied across donors, with ∼ 40% to 90% of PMNs showing viability (Figure S23C). Similar to what we found in the preclinical model, the presence of PMNs resulted in reduction of FOXP3+ T cells. Addition of PMNs to PBMC cultures led to a decrease in the percentages of total and antigen-experienced FOXP3+ T cells in 67% of participants (Fig. 7A). We also observed a decrease in Ki67+ antigen-experienced FOXP3+ T cells in the presence of PMNs in 100% of the participants (Fig. 7B), suggesting that PMNs suppressed proliferation. These data suggest PMNs inhibit FOXP3+ T-cell responses in humans after PCV administration.

Discussion

There is a growing number of studies investigating the effect of neutrophils on adaptive immune responses; however, few of them are focusing on vaccine responses.22–24 In this study, we report what is, to our knowledge, a new mechanism by which PMNs control antibody responses to the clinically relevant PCV by controlling Treg numbers. In fact, when neutrophils were depleted at the time of PCV vaccination, the number of Tregs increased that resulted in a defect in protective antibody responses.25 Reduction of Tregs in PMN-deficient vaccinated mice boosted antibody numbers, function, and protective efficacy in the sera. This work demonstrates that PMNs drive production of functional antibodies against pneumococci in response to PCV by limiting the expansion of immune-suppressive Tregs.

Although the effect of PMNs on Tregs has not been investigated in the context of clinically relevant vaccines, as far as we are aware, several studies have looked at the relationship between Tregs and PMNs in models of inflammation and disease.55–61 Here, we report that PMNs can inhibit Tregs, which is in contrast to prior work demonstrating that PMNs promote Tregs in models in which a high level of inflammation is induced.58,59,61 The exact way by which PMNs inhibit Tregs in response to PCV remains to be elucidated; however, multiple mechanisms could be involved. Some possibilities could be via control of the transcription factor FOXP3, a master regulator of Treg induction and function,88,89 or control of cytokines required for Treg differentiation and viability. In patients with traumatic brain injury, release of serine proteases (neutrophil elastase and proteinase 3) by infiltrating PMNs cleaved cytokine receptors on the surface of T cells, including IL-2Rα, the IL-2 receptor subunit that is important for IL-2 signaling in Tregs.90,91 In addition, those proteases can cleave cytokines themselves, including TGF-β and IL-2, leading to limited activation of T-cell response.92,93 In response to PCV, PMNs may similarly inhibit Treg induction by release of their proteases to deplete Treg-inducing cytokines and/or their respective receptors on the surface of Tregs. Indeed, the absence of PMNs at day 7 after vaccination resulted in increased levels of several cytokines. This was observed only in vaccinated, but not naïve, hosts, suggesting that PMN presence may control the inflammatory responses to PCV. Apart from direct degradation of compounds, PMNs may compete for factors with Tregs. For example, signaling of retinoic acid in naïve T cells enhances TGF-β–mediated induction of Tregs,94 and retinoic acid boosts PMN antibacterial functions.95 It is possible that retinoic acid sequestration by PMNs limits its availability for Treg induction. PMNs could also be inhibiting Tregs through release of their granule component cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide, the human form of which (LL-37) induces Treg apoptosis in vitro.96 PMNs could also be suppressing Tregs through the release of miRNA containing extracellular vesicles. PMN-derived extracellular vesicles containing miRNAs modulate responses in other cells, including macrophages and endothelial cells.97–99 The absence of miRNA-146a has been shown to promote Treg expansion, and other miRNAs are known to control Treg induction.100,101 PMNs, which express miRNA-146a, among others,102,103 could use such a mechanism to limit Treg expansion. Alternatively, the presence of PMNs at the time of vaccination may lead to Treg suppression by altering the strength of APC interaction with T cells. However, despite changes in several chemokines in the absence of PMN, we did not observe a change in total DC and macrophage numbers. We also only observed changes in MHC-II and CD86 expression on DCs and macrophages at day 21 after vaccination, after the observed changes in Treg numbers. One caveat is that we did not examine APC subsets in detail and could have missed differences in specific subsets important for PCV responses.30–32 All these potential mechanisms can be avenues of future research for this work.

The findings here suggest that the expanded Tregs in PMN-depleted mice are detrimental and contribute to defective antibody responses. In general, Tregs negatively affect immune responses to vaccines. Depletion of Tregs using αCD25 in vaccination models against herpes simplex virus (HSV) and Plasmodium berghei increased CD8+ T-cell responses to their respective vaccine epitopes and led to increased protection against subsequent challenge by those pathogens.41,104 In humans, responses to an experimental vaccine of DCs loaded with HIV peptides105 showed that patients with lower levels of HIV-specific Tregs displayed better T-effector cell responses after vaccination.46 In the context of S. pneumoniae, blocking of CTLA-4, an effector molecule on Tregs, enhanced levels of antibodies against pneumococcal polysaccharides in immunized mice, and higher Treg numbers in older adults were associated with decreased proliferation of CD4+ effector T cells in response to the pneumococcal protein AliB.47,48 Additionally, Tfrs, which we found are increased in PCV-vaccinated PMN-depleted mice, inhibit B-cell responses and promote the contraction of GC reactions.52,54 The findings here, showing an improvement of vaccine response upon depletion of the expanded Tregs in PMN-depleted mice, are in line with the reported inhibitory role of Tregs in vaccine responses.

There have been several studies examining how Tregs and Tfrs inhibit B-cell responses.106 They can affect B-cell number, differentiation, and antibody responses via release of soluble mediators such as the neuropeptide neuritin, IL-10, and TGF-β, or through contact-dependent mechanisms involving CTLA4, PD1, and granzymes.106–108 This suppression can lead to a decrease in B-cell expression of AID.109 However, B-cell numbers and AID expression were not affected in the present study. It is possible that the DNA-binding capacity or the enzymatic activity of AID are affected instead. AID activity is regulated by its phosphorylation status, which can either enhance or inhibit its enzymatic activity, depending on the amino acid being phosphorylated110,111 by protein kinase A.112–115 It is possible, therefore, that Tregs regulate AID phosphorylation in response to PCV, rather than overall expression through modulation of cAMP, as they have been shown to do in DCs and effector T cells.116

We found that the phenotypes observed in the preclinical model also were found in human participants, highlighting the clinical relevance of this work. Upon vaccination of healthy human donors, PMNs in the circulation changed phenotype, suggesting they are responsive to vaccination. Using peripheral blood collected from donors after PCV vaccination for in vitro cocultures, addition of PMNs to PBMCs led to a reduction in FOXP3+ T cells and their proliferation, recapitulating the Treg-inhibiting property of PMNs seen in the mouse models. However, further clinical studies are needed to assess the in vivo role of PMNs in protecting human hosts against pneumococcal disease, including linking PMN phenotype to antibody responses. Nevertheless, these findings can inform the design of improved pneumococcal vaccine formulations that offer better efficacy, especially in more vulnerable populations. Despite their availability, pneumococcal vaccines do not mitigate the growing global burden of S. pneumonia infections.26,72,117 This is due, in part, to the fact that the vaccines do not protect all populations equally; for example, male donors and aged individuals mount lower responses to S. pneumonia infection compared with their female counterparts. Interestingly, Tregs are reported to be higher in male individuals compared with sex-matched female individuals and higher in older adults than in younger people.118–120 In this work, we identified the role of the PMN–Treg axis in controlling antibody responses in female study participants that can be targeted for improvement of current vaccine formulations moving forward. Although more studies are needed to tease apart the specific mechanisms behind this inhibition of Tregs by PMNs, the findings have important implications for development of future vaccine strategies to improve efficacy to infections. Indeed, this PMN–Treg axis can be further studied in the context of sex- and age-driven differences in vaccine responses121,122 and can be targeted to increase vaccination efficacy in vulnerable older adults and male populations. The exact mechanisms of Treg inhibition by PMNs need to be elucidated to pinpoint what pathways or molecules to target specifically.

One limitation of this study is the focus on young adult female individuals, both in preclinical mouse work and in human participants. Neutrophil responses are affected by host age and biological sex.1,123–125 Therefore, their role in vaccine-mediated responses may be different in the more vulnerable male and aged hosts. Indeed, our prior work in preclinical models,63 as well as data from human studies,64,72,73 found that PCV efficacy is lower in male individuals compared with females and declines with host age. Therefore, we decided to first focus on the hosts that are the most protected after vaccination (young adult female individuals) and characterize PMN responses in them first and then move to less-protected hosts as a next step. Currently, ongoing studies in our laboratory are exploring the effect of aging and biological sex on PMN responses to PCV. Another limitation is that, for human participants, we are limited by access to cells in the circulation. Therefore, we are unable to examine the effect of PMNs on secondary lymphoid organs. We attempted to address that by performing PMN and PBMC cocultures and were able to recapitulate the inhibitory effect of PMNs on Tregs that we observed in vivo in preclinical studies. Furthermore, by being limited to responses in the circulation, we are unable to test if PMN phenotype is altered upon tissue entry in humans. However, unlike in mice, the phenotype of circulating PMNs in human participants did change in response to PCV administration, emphasizing differences in PMN behaviors across species. Finally, although we identified that control of Tregs by PMNs is 1 of the mechanisms by which they affect antibody responses to PCV, how they do so remains an open question. In summary, despite the discussed limitations, this study revealed new insights into how innate immunity controls adaptive responses, which has implications for future vaccine design, highlighting the importance of considering PMN responses.
