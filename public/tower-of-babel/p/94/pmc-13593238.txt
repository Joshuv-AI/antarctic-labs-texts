High risk of COVID-19 mortality in people living with advanced HIV disease in South Africa: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Seth Inzaule, Jassat Waasila, Ronaldo Silva, Nathan Ford, Soe Soe Thwin, Alimuddin Zumla, Meg Doherty, Janet Diaz, Silvia Bertagnolio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conflicting information exists in the literature regarding the relationship between HIV viral load suppression and immune suppression among people living with HIV (PLHIV) with coronavirus disease (COVID-19) comorbidity. We evaluated risk of in-hospital mortality among PLHIV with varying levels of immunosuppression and viral load in South Africa. We analyzed retrospectively collected individual-level cohort data from South Africa shared in the World Health Organization Global Clinic Platform comprising 3,10,522 hospitalized individuals, including 8191 PLHIV on antiretroviral therapy. Advanced disease was defined as CD4 counts of <200 cells/µL. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death within 28 days of hospital admission. Royston–Parmar survival models were used to assess the association of viral load and CD4 counts with in-hospital mortality among PLHIV on antiretroviral therapy. Models were adjusted for demographic factors, COVID-19 severity, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 variants, and comorbidities. Among the 8191 PLHIV with available CD4 and viral load data, 77.2% had viral suppression, and 35.9% had CD4 counts < 200 cells/μL. Compared to HIV-negative individuals, PLHIV with CD4 < 200 cells/μL and viral load ≥1000 copies/mL had a significantly higher risk of mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 2.73, 95% CI: 2.45–3.04). Elevated mortality risk was also observed in PLHIV with CD4 < 200 cells/μL and viral load < 1000 copies/mL (aHR 2.01, 95% CI: 1.84–2.20), and those with CD4 ≥ 200 cells/μL and viral load ≥ 1000 copies/mL (aHR 1.47, 95% CI: 1.20–1.81). PLHIV with CD4 ≥ 200 cells/μL and viral suppression had the lowest, but still significant, increased in-hospital mortality risk (aHR 1.27, 95% CI: 1.18–1.36). A substantial increased risk of in-hospital mortality occurs among PLHIV with advanced HIV disease and unsuppressed viremia hospitalized with COVID-19. These findings highlight the importance of prioritizing COVID-19 vaccination and COVID-19 therapeutics for PLHIV during the post-pandemic phase. Further research is however needed to understand the high risk of mortality in virally suppressed PLHIV with CD4 ≥ 200 cells/μL.

1. Introduction

People living with HIV (PLHIV) are predisposed to poor outcomes from coronavirus disease (COVID-19) due to compromised immune function and a high burden of comorbidities. The role of HIV infection in exacerbating COVID-19 outcomes has been debated, with some studies indicating that PLHIV are at an increased risk of severe disease and mortality, while others report conflicting results.[1-6] However over the past 4 years ever since the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, meta-analyses and large multi-country studies have demonstrated an increased risk of mortality among PLHIV hospitalized with COVID-19.[6,7] This evidence has informed World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines on the management of COVID-19 in PLHIV.[8,9] However, the specific role of viral suppression and CD4 counts in determining COVID-19 outcomes remains unclear. Some studies suggest that PLHIV with controlled viremia and high CD4 counts have outcomes comparable to those of HIV-negative individuals,[10,11] whereas others indicate higher mortality even among virally suppressed individuals compared to HIV negative individuals.[12,13] Some studies have found that even with viral suppression, PLHIV are still at a heightened risk of severe outcomes and mortality.[6,7,12] Data also conflict on COVID-19 responses between PLHIV and HIV negative individuals. While some have reported COVID-19 vaccine responses that are comparable to HIV negative individuals, others have shown reduced and short-lived immune responses among PLHIV.[14-20] We have also previously reported that although COVID-19 vaccine significantly reduces COVID-19 in-hospital deaths, mortality risk remains elevated when compared to HIV negative individuals.[21]

With 40.8 million PLHIV globally (21% living in South Africa), 9.8% of whom have advanced HIV disease, understanding the correlates of poor clinical outcomes in this population remains crucial to developing more accurate evidence-based strategies to mitigate these risks, including optimizing vaccine and therapeutic interventions. This study aimed to assess the effect of viral suppression (viral load of <1000 cps/mL) and immunosuppression (CD4 < 200 cells/µL) on in-hospital COVID-19 mortality among PLHIV, using individual-level data from South Africa submitted to the WHO Global Clinical Platform. By assessing a large dataset, we aimed to provide more definitive insights into the correlates of poor clinical outcomes in PLHIV, which could inform strategies for managing COVID-19 in this vulnerable population.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and variables

The WHO Global Clinical Platform include data from 59 countries, and has been described previously.[6,22] Our study focused on analyzing the effect of viral suppression and immunosuppression on in-hospital mortality among PLHIV hospitalized with COVID-19 based on data from South Africa where we had sufficient data on antiretroviral therapy (ART), viral load and CD4. We used individual-level data from the WHO Global Clinical Platform and analyzed the effect of viral suppression and immunosuppression on in-hospital COVID-19 mortality among PLHIV. This included data from 3,10,522 hospitalized patients, including children, adolescents, and adults from South Africa collected between January 3, 2020 and October 1, 2023. The WHO Global Clinical Platform design and methodology was used as described previously.[22] Briefly, the WHO Global Clinical Platform collects data through standardized case report forms or mappings from local databases. Participating countries submit data electronically, with local datasets standardized to the WHO case report form structure before analysis. The case report forms include standardized epidemiological and clinical patient data collected at hospital admission, throughout hospitalization, and at discharge. Data collected include demographic characteristics, medical history, vital signs, laboratory results, clinical treatments, hospital course, co-infections, immunization status, information on reinfection, use of oxygen or mechanical ventilation, and outcomes (e.g., hospital discharge, death, or intensive care unit admission).

2.2. Definitions and groupings

2.1.1. Viral load and CD4 counts

Following WHO guidelines, viral suppression was defined as a viral load (VL) below 1000 copies per milliliter (cps/mL), while advanced HIV disease was categorized as having a CD4 count below 200 cells/µL.[23]

2.1.2. Composite variables

We created a composite variable to capture the interaction between CD4 counts and VL, categorizing patients into 4 groups: CD4 < 200 cells/µL + VL ≥ 1000 cps/mL: Representing advanced HIV disease with unsuppressed viremia. CD4 < 200 cells/µL + VL < 1000 cps/mL: Advanced HIV disease with suppressed viremia. CD4 ≥ 200 cells/µL + VL ≥ 1000 cps/mL: HIV disease with preserved CD4 counts but unsuppressed viremia. CD4 ≥ 200 cells/µL + VL < 1000 cps/mL: HIV disease with preserved CD4 counts and suppressed viremia.

2.1.3. Outcome measure

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death within 28 days of hospital admission. Patients were hospitalized if they had laboratory-confirmed or clinically suspected COVID-19.

2.1.4. Statistical analysis

We used flexible parametric survival models (Royston–Parmar models) with natural cubic splines (3° of freedom) to model the baseline hazard. This approach relaxes the proportional hazards assumption while providing interpretable hazard ratios. The primary exposure was the compositite variable for viral load and CD4. Confounders were identified using directed acyclic graphs, with age, sex, and severity at admission included as essential adjustment variables. Additional covariates were selected based on their prevalence (>80% of cases), low collinearity with other variables (correlation coefficient < 0.8), and univariate association (P < .10) with in-hospital mortality or the primary exposure. This included severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) variants and comorbid conditions, Comorbidities considered for inclusion as covariates included: Chronic comorbidities (asplenia, asthma, chronic cardiac, kidney, liver, pulmonary, or neurological disease); lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking); Endocrine/metabolic conditions (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, obesity); malignant neoplasms; Tuberculosis (current or prior infection). Final models retained covariates with P-values < .05.

2.1.5. Sensitivity analysis

To contextualize the mortality risk for PLHIV, we included an HIV-negative reference group in our analysis. This comparison allowed us to assess relative mortality risk between HIV-positive and HIV-negative patients.

2.1.6. Ethical approval

The analysis plan was reviewed by the WHO Ethical Review Committee, which granted a waiver for ethical review clearance including consent as this was passive, anonymized clinical surveillance. Ethical clearance was obtained, where necessary, by relevant institutional or national bodies.

3.2. HIV co-infection

Of the 3,10,522 participants, 3,02,331(97.4%) were HIV negative while 8191 (2.6%) identified as people living with HIV (PLHIV) and on ART (Table 1). Of these, 77.2% (n = 6320) were virally suppressed, while 35.9% (n = 2937) had advanced HIV disease (CD4 cell counts below 200 cells/µL). Overall 57.4% (4709) were virally suppressed (VL < 1000 cps/mL) but with preserved CD4 cell counts, 6.7% (545) with viral non-suppression (VL ≥ 1000 cps/mL) and preserved CD4 cell counts, 19.7% (1611) virally suppressed (VL < 1000cps/mL) with advanced HIV disease, and 16.2% (1326) with viral non-suppression (VL ≥ 1000 cps/mL) and advanced HIV disease.

3.3. In-hospital mortality among PLHIV

Overall the rate of in-hospital mortality was higher among PLHIV compared to the HIV-negative population (Table 1). The risk of in-hospital mortality was 173% higher among PLHIV with advanced HIV disease with unsuppressed viraemia (CD4 < 200 cells/µL and VL ≥ 1000 cps/mL, adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 2.73, 95% CI: 2.45–3.04). This group was followed by PLHIV with advanced HIV disease with suppressed viraemia (CD4 counts < 200 cells/μL and VL < 1000 cps/mL, aHR 2.01, 95% CI: 1.84–2.20), PLHIV with preserved CD4 counts (≥200 cells/μL) and unsuppressed viremia (VL ≥ 1000 cps/mL (aHR 1.47, 95% CI: 1.20–1.81)), and PLHIV with preserved CD4 counts (≥200 cells/μL) and suppressed viremia (VL < 1000 cps/mL, aHR 1.27, 95% CI: 1.18–1.36; Table 2).

4. Discussion

Our study evaluated the risk of in-hospital mortality among PLHIV with varying levels of immunosuppression and viral load in South Africa. We observed a significant risk of in-hospital mortality among PLHIV hospitalized with COVID-19, especially those with advanced HIV disease (CD4 < 200 cells/μL) and unsuppressed viremia (VL ≥ 1000 cps/mL). Conversely, PLHIV with higher CD4 counts (≥200 cells/μL) and suppressed viremia had the lowest mortality risk among PLHIV but still faced a higher risk compared to HIV-negative individuals. Similar patterns were also observed across the different variant waves (Pre-Delta, Delta and Omicron waves) in a sensitivity analyses.

PLHIV with suppressed viremia but low CD4 counts remained at a significantly higher risk of mortality, indicating that immune recovery is a crucial factor in improving COVID-19 outcomes.[24,25] This aligns with previous research that has shown incomplete immune recovery in some PLHIV, despite effective ART, leaving them susceptible to opportunistic infections and other severe complications.[26,27] This suggests that even with effective ART and immune recovery, PLHIV may still have residual vulnerabilities to COVID-19, potentially due to chronic immune activation or other HIV-related factors. Notably, the observed mortality risk among PLHIV with low CD4 counts and unsuppressed viremia supports earlier research linking advanced immunosuppression with increased COVID-19 severity.[7,28] The increased in-hospital mortality risk indicates that people with advanced HIV and COVID-19 who are unwell enough to require hospital admission are likely to have healthcare needs that are substantially different, indicating that this group should be prioritized for more intensive care as recommended by WHO.[29] This finding also underscores the importance of early HIV diagnosis and the initiation of ART to prevent the progression to advanced HIV disease, which may exacerbate outcomes from COVID-19 or infections from other emerging and reemerging epidemics.

Interestingly, our study’s data show no significant difference in mortality between virally suppressed PLHIV with preserved CD4 counts and those with viral non-suppression but preserved CD4 counts, when compared with HIV negative individuals. This suggests that factors beyond viral replication, such as residual immune dysregulation or other unmeasured comorbidities, might contribute to COVID-19 severity in this population. Recent studies supports this view, indicating that comorbid conditions, socioeconomic factors, and barriers to healthcare access might heighten vulnerability of PLHIV to severe outcomes from COVID-19.[26,30]The observed mortality patterns in our study also corroborate findings from other studies which have consistently reported an increased risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes in PLHIV.[1,4,7,10] Our study adds to the literature by providing a more nuanced understanding of how combinations of viral load and CD4 count influence mortality risk. Notably, the lack of significant difference in mortality between PLHIV with higher CD4 counts regardless of viral suppression status suggests that factors beyond viral replication may contribute to COVID-19 severity in this population.

The lower mortality observed in PLHIV with relatively preserved immune function (CD4 ≥ 200 cells/µL) and viral suppression is consistent with studies that show comparable COVID-19 outcomes between these patients and HIV-negative individuals.[11,31] However, the residual risk observed even in these patients highlights the potential role of other factors such as compromised immunity, comorbidities, socioeconomic status, and access to care, which may also contribute to the increased mortality observed in this population.[26,30]

Our results need to be viewed in light of our study’s limitations. The observational nature of the study may introduce residual confounding despite adjustments. In addition, only documented covariates were assessed. Notably differences in key socioeconomic determinants of health related to HIV status, such as income, food insecurity, barriers to health care access could partly explain the outcomes, but this could not be assessed because appropriate indicators were not collected. Secondly, the fact that we used data from only South Africa may potentially limit generalizability to other settings particularly due to differences in hospitalization criteria among countries as well as differences in access to and quality of COVID-19 health care. Additionally, since the study focused on hospitalized patients, our findings may not be generalizable to all PLHIV, particularly those with mild COVID-19 or those who did not access hospitals. Lastly, the lack of detailed data on comorbidities, ART regimens, immunization and SARS-CoV-2 variant limits the ability to fully disentangle the effects of HIV-related factors from other risk factors for severe COVID-19 outcomes. Future research should explore the long-term outcomes of COVID-19 in PLHIV and the potential impact of newer ART regimens and COVID-19 vaccination in mitigating these risks.

A strength of this study was that it included individual level data for more than 8000 PLHIV across the different pandemic waves. Moreover the study findings were confirmed across the different SARS-CoV-2 waves.

Our study’s results have significant implications for clinical practice and public health strategies. Given the heightened risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes in PLHIV, particularly those with advanced HIV disease or unsuppressed viremia, targeted interventions are crucial. These include prioritizing PLHIV for COVID-19 vaccination and booster doses, as well as ensuring access to ART and regular monitoring of viral load and CD4 counts. Previous studies have shown reduced good immune response and reduced mortality among vaccinated PLHIV.[7,32] Moreover there is need to prioritize other interventions, including monoclonal antibodies and targeted antivirals, for hospitalized COVID-19 patients to minimize mortality.[33,34] Monitoring of CD4 counts and viral load in PLHIV, especially those hospitalized with COVID-19, is crucial:[23] early identification of those with advanced HIV disease or unsuppressed viremia can facilitate timely interventions, such as the administration of COVID-19 therapeutics or timely ART switch. Integrated care approaches that address both HIV management and COVID-19 treatment are essential. This includes ensuring that PLHIV have uninterrupted access to ART, as well as appropriate management of comorbidities that may exacerbate COVID-19 outcomes. Further research is needed to explore the mechanisms underlying the observed mortality risks, particularly the role of chronic immune activation and incomplete immune recovery despite ART as well as the influence of socioeconomic factors and barriers to healthcare access. Understanding these factors could lead to more tailored interventions for PLHIV.

5. Conclusions

Our study provides compelling evidence of the heightened risk of in-hospital mortality among PLHIV with advanced HIV disease and unsuppressed viremia when hospitalized with COVID-19. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive HIV management, including timely ART initiation and monitoring, as well as prioritizing PLHIV for COVID-19 vaccination. Addressing these vulnerabilities through targeted interventions and integrated care approaches can better protect this at-risk population from severe outcomes during the ongoing pandemic and beyond. Further research is however needed to explore the mechanisms underlying the observed mortality risks, as well as the influence of socioeconomic factors and barriers to healthcare access.
