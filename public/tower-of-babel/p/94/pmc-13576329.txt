Predictors of maternal and perinatal mortality among pregnant women with preeclampsia and eclampsia at Garissa County Referral Hospital: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Asha Abdi Nur, Amsalu Degu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreeclampsia and eclampsia are severe complications of pregnancy that significantly contribute to maternal and fetal mortality. A comprehensive understanding of the predictors of these conditions is essential for developing effective interventions. Despite preeclampsia and eclampsia being among the leading contributors to maternal and fetal mortality in resource-limited areas in Kenya, especially the arid and semi-arid regions such as Garissa, limited studies have specifically examined the predictors of maternal and perinatal mortality among women with preeclampsia and eclampsia. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the predictors of perinatal and maternal mortality among pregnant women with preeclampsia and eclampsia at Garissa County Referral Hospital.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was used to evaluate the records of 138 eligible pregnant women diagnosed with preeclampsia and eclampsia from 1st January 2019 to 31st December 2023. The study aimed to determine the predictors of maternal and perinatal mortality among pregnant women diagnosed with preeclampsia and eclampsia. A data abstraction tool capturing sociodemographic, clinical, treatment modalities, and outcome variables was used to collect data. Descriptive analysis was used to present mortality rates and management modalities, while binary logistic regression was used to determine the predictors of mortality among preeclampsia and eclampsia patients.ResultsThe mean age of the participants in this study was 26.86 ± 7.6 years. There were higher cases of preeclampsia (63.0%) than eclampsia (37.0%) observed. Maternal mortality was 13.0%, while the perinatal mortality was 42.7%. The findings indicated that 34.1% of patients had a postpartum haemorrhage and 14.5% had a placental abruption. Neonatal complications were observed in 26.0% of the fetuses, with respiratory distress being the most prevalent (20.0%). Diabetes mellitus (AOR = 8.9, 95% CI = 2.1–41.4, p = 0.003) and multigravida status (AOR 3.6, 95% CI = 1.6–8.5, p = 0.002) were the significant predictors of maternal and perinatal mortality, respectively.ConclusionThe maternal and perinatal mortality among mothers with preeclampsia and eclampsia was comparable to the rates reported in similar settings. The significant predictors of maternal and perinatal mortalities were diabetes and multigravida status, respectively, highlighting the need for timely antenatal care and close monitoring for multigravida women and those with pre-existing conditions like diabetes.

Background

Preeclampsia and eclampsia are pregnancy-induced hypertensive disorders. Preeclampsia is a condition characterized by high blood pressure of ≥ 140/90 mmHg and significant proteinuria, occurring after week 20 of pregnancy in previously normotensive women [1]. In the absence of proteinuria, it is diagnosed when hypertension is accompanied by signs of organ dysfunction, such as thrombocytopenia, impaired renal function, or liver dysfunction, pulmonary edema, or the onset of unexplained headache and blurred vision [2]. On the other hand, eclampsia manifests as seizures and, along with preeclampsia and HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelet count) syndrome, represents a spectrum of hypertensive disorders in pregnant women [3]. Eclampsia can present rapidly without preceding signs, but it can also develop from preeclampsia that is inadequately treated or monitored [4].

Preeclampsia and eclampsia are hypertensive disorders of pregnancy that can cause significant morbidity and mortality. Maternal morbidities due to preeclampsia and eclampsia are placental abruption, renal failure, and HELLP syndrome [2, 5]. Low birth weight, preterm delivery, and neonatal complications are common morbidities in the fetus and result in fetal mortalities [6–8]. Preeclampsia accounts for 5% to 7% of maternal morbidity and mortality and is the cause of over 70,000 maternal deaths and 500,000 infant deaths in the world yearly [1]. Preeclampsia is a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality in the United States [1]. In contrast to severe preeclampsia, eclampsia cases had twice the rate of severe maternal outcomes [9].

Pregnancy-related hypertensive diseases caused 16% of maternal fatalities worldwide and 9% of maternal deaths in Africa and Asia [7]. Around the world, 5% to 8% of pregnancies still result in preeclampsia [2]. The prevalence of preeclampsia in Kenya ranges between 5.6% and 6.5% [10]. In sub-Saharan Africa, high rates of maternal mortality attributable to preeclampsia and eclampsia are driven by inadequate access to care and delayed detection [11, 12].

The risk factors for preeclampsia and eclampsia tend to vary depending on various factors, ranging from race, age of the mother, maternal educational level, history of previous pregnancies, and maternal obesity [13]. Preeclampsia and eclampsia are more common in women with extremes of age, who are less than 20 years old, or women who are 36 years and above [14]. Previous studies have shown that there is an increased risk of preeclampsia in primigravida compared to multigravida women [15]. Furthermore, pre-existing comorbid conditions increase the risk of developing preeclampsia and eclampsia, and it has been reported that the combination of hypertensive disorders with additional comorbidities can lead to adverse outcomes [16].

Most studies on preeclampsia and eclampsia have been conducted in developed countries [10]. Despite preeclampsia and eclampsia being major contributors to maternal and fetal mortalities, adequate investigation on these cohorts within Kenya is still sparse. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the predictors of perinatal outcomes among pregnant women with preeclampsia and eclampsia at Garissa County Referral Hospital, Kenya.

Methods

Study design, setting, and period

A retrospective cohort study design was used to evaluate maternal and perinatal outcomes of preeclampsia and eclampsia, as well as the contributing factors, through the analysis of past medical records from 1st January 2019 to 31st December 2023. The study was conducted in Garissa County Referral Hospital, and data collection took place from October 2023 to March 2024. It is a level 5 government referral hospital in Garissa township, the highest rank of county-level healthcare in the Kenyan public health system. It receives referrals from 126 medical facilities in the county as well as the adjacent counties. Over the study period, the facility conducted an average of 4,184 deliveries annually, with caesarean section rates ranging from 21% to 34%. Maternal mortality rates ranged from 780 to 1,478 per 100,000 live births, and facility perinatal mortality rates ranged from 57 to 78 per 1,000 live births across the study period. The hospital laboratory provides a range of services such as hematology, blood transfusion services, viral load testing, microbiology, routine biochemistry, and maternal and newborn investigations. The hospital has an intensive care unit with a bed capacity of 6; however, a neonatal intensive care unit is not available.

Study population

The study population comprised pregnant women diagnosed with preeclampsia and eclampsia who attended Garissa County Referral Hospital in the last five years (1st January 2019 to 31st December 2023).

Sample size and sampling technique

A total of 530 patient files with a diagnosis of preeclampsia and eclampsia were retrieved from the hospital Records Department for the period 1st January 2019 to 31st December 2023. When retrieving the physical files, 342 were accessible while 188 had been damaged by water and termites and could not be used. No sampling technique was used since the study population was relatively small; consequently, all the patients who met the inclusion criteria and had complete records were included in the study. One hundred and thirty-eight eligible participants took part in the study. The exclusion due to incomplete records and damaged files reduced the final sample size, which may introduce selection bias and affect the generalizability of the findings (Fig. 1).

Data collection technique

To collect data for this study, medical records were used. First, approval was obtained from the ethical review board before collecting data. Subsequently, a research permit from the National Commission for Science, Technology & Innovation (NACOSTI) was acquired. The participants for the study were sourced from hospital medical records, and their eligibility was confirmed by meeting the inclusion criteria set for the study either through paper-based records, charts, or laboratory reports. A data abstraction form was developed, capturing sociodemographic characteristics, clinical presentations, treatment modalities, and maternal and fetal outcome data. The tool was populated by a comprehensive review of the medical records. Treatment modalities as documented in the medical records included anticonvulsants, antihypertensive agents, corticosteroids, and antibiotics. Socio-demographic data collected included age, marital status, and residence extracted from medical records as documented by clinical staff and were not recoded. Maternal and perinatal mortalities were the primary outcomes for the logistic regression analysis. Fetal birth weight, gestational age at birth, perinatal mortality, and neonatal complications were evaluated as fetal outcomes. Gestational age was determined by use of the last menstrual period (LMP) and early ultrasound for confirmation, and in cases where the date was unavailable or unreliable due to cycle irregularities. The data abstraction form was used to ensure that all required data were obtained in an accurate form. All collected data were checked for accuracy and completeness and securely stored.

Data analysis

The data collected were analyzed using R software version 4.3.1. Descriptive statistics were generated for all variables and were presented in frequency tables and figures. Multivariable binary logistic regression was used to determine the adjusted odds ratios (AORs) for the predictors of maternal and perinatal mortality in pregnant women with preeclampsia and eclampsia. All variables considered clinically relevant to maternal and perinatal outcomes in preeclampsia and eclampsia were included in the multivariable logistic regression model.

Operational definitions

Preeclampsia

High blood pressure of ≥140/90 mmHg during pregnancy after the 20th week with proteinuria in urine in previously normotensive women.

Eclampsia

The presence of seizures in pregnant women diagnosed with preeclampsia.

HELLP syndrome

A combination of hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count as a complication of preeclampsia or eclampsia.

Postpartum hemorrhage

Loss of blood of 500 ml or more from vaginal delivery or 1000 ml or more following caesarean section after birth.

Perinatal mortality

Death of a fetus or neonate from 28 weeks of gestation through the first 7 days of life.

Comorbid condition

One or more medical conditions that coexist alongside the primary condition.

Preeclampsia with severe features

Preeclampsia with any of the following: a blood pressure of ≥ 160/110 mmHg, serum creatinine > 1.1 mg/dL, thrombocytopenia below 100,000/mm³, pulmonary oedema, new onset of visual disturbances, or cerebral or impaired liver function.

Preeclampsia without severe features

Preeclampsia with blood pressure of ≥ 140/90 mmHg on two occasions, at least 4 h apart, with proteinuria ≥ 300 mg/24 hours or protein/creatinine ratio ≥ 0.3 after 20 weeks of gestation and no severe features (i.e., none of the signs of organ dysfunction seen).

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The mean age of the participants was 26.86 ± 7.6 years. The majority (52.9%) were aged 20–34 years, followed by the age group ≥ 35 years (24.6%). Most of the patients (93.5%) were married, and 54.3% resided in urban areas, while 45.7% were from rural areas of Garissa County (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics

There were higher cases of preeclampsia (63.0%) than eclampsia (37.0%) observed among the study participants. Preeclampsia with severe features (72.5%) was more prevalent than preeclampsia without severe features. Multigravida women comprised the majority (58.0%) compared to primigravida women (42.0%). Single pregnancies were recorded in 91.3% of the patients. Most patients were diagnosed antepartum (88.4%), while 11.6% were diagnosed postpartum. The majority of the pregnant women (63.0%) were diagnosed above 36 weeks of pregnancy. The study showed that 38.4% of the patients had pre-existing medical conditions, with chronic hypertension being the most prevalent (20.3%) (Table 2).

Maternal outcomes

The findings indicated that 14.5% and 6.5% of the patients experienced placental abruption and renal failure, respectively, while another 13.0% developed HELLP Syndrome. Among other forms of morbidities, postpartum haemorrhage was the most observed in 34.1% of the patients. Most of the mothers (52.9%) had a vaginal delivery. Most antepartum patients were delivered vaginally (54.9%), while postpartum patients had higher rates of caesarean delivery (62.5%). The maternal mortality was 13.0% in the study setting (Table 4).

Fetal outcomes

The study revealed that 51.3% of neonates had normal birth weight (2500–4000 g), while another 41.3% were born with low birth weights (< 2500 g). More than half (55.3%) of fetuses were born at term (37–42 weeks), and 38.0% were preterm (born below 37 weeks). Out of this, 34.7% were born in the third trimester. Neonatal complications were observed in 26.0% of the fetuses, with respiratory distress being the most prevalent (20.0%). Perinatal mortality was 42.7%. The other complications reported in the study were asphyxia, congenital hydrocephalus and congenital heart disease (Table 5).

Predictors of maternal mortality

In the multivariable analysis, diabetes was significantly associated with maternal mortality (AOR = 8.9, 95% CI = 2.1–41.4, p = 0.003) (Table 7). In addition, the odds of perinatal mortality were higher in multigravida women (AOR = 3.6, 95% CI = 1.6–8.5, p = 0.002) as compared to primigravida women. Nonetheless, the other factors were not significant predictors of maternal and neonatal mortality among preeclampsia and eclampsia patients (Tables 6 and 7).

Discussion

Preeclampsia and eclampsia are serious worldwide health issues and impact 2–10% and 0.03–0.05% of pregnant women, respectively [17]. The overall prevalence of preeclampsia ranges from 4.5% to 23% [18] and affects about 4% of pregnancies in the United States [19]. However, data on the predictors of maternal and perinatal mortality among pregnant women with preeclampsia and eclampsia at Garissa County Referral Hospital remain limited.

This study found a 13.0% maternal mortality rate among pregnant women diagnosed with preeclampsia and eclampsia. When stratified by diagnosis, maternal mortality was 4.6% for preeclamptic women and 27.5% for eclamptic women, and perinatal mortality was 31.0% and 72.5%, respectively. This suggests a notably higher mortality among women with eclampsia. This is in line with earlier research carried out in comparable environments, which found that pregnant women with preeclampsia and eclampsia had a high maternal mortality rate [9, 20–26]. In addition, about 16% of maternal mortalities were attributable to preeclampsia and eclampsia in low and middle-income countries [27]. A previous systematic review and meta-analysis in Sub-Saharan Africa showed that hypertensive disorders of pregnancy were associated with an increased risk of maternal mortality, preterm birth and neonatal mortality [28]. This may reflect barriers including insufficient prenatal care, inadequate access to hospital care, limited funding and incorrect diagnosis and management of preeclampsia and eclampsia patients in low- and middle-income countries [29].

The perinatal mortality due to preeclampsia and eclampsia in this population was 42.7%, which is higher than that reported in Ghana (20.4%) [30] and a rural Tanzanian hospital, where the perinatal mortality was 30% among women with eclampsia and 27% among those with severe preeclampsia [31]. Preeclampsia and eclampsia are well-established contributors to perinatal mortality [32]. This is in line with results from other studies that report preeclampsia and eclampsia are strongly linked to an increased risk of perinatal mortality, such as stillbirths and early neonatal deaths [33, 34]. This may be associated with uteroplacental vascular insufficiency leading to impaired fetal oxygenation and nutrition [35].

The findings revealed that 34.1% of patients had a postpartum haemorrhage, 14.5% had a placental abruption, and another 13.0% developed HELLP Syndrome. These rates are higher than those reported in the Netherlands, where 7.4% [36] and 10.4% [37] of preeclamptic women developed postpartum bleeding. In addition, the Indian study reported a lower rate of HELLP Syndrome (7.43%) and placental abruption (12.96%) as compared to the findings in our setting [30]. A Nigerian systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that acute kidney injury (6.37%), aspiration pneumonia (5.26%) and stroke (3%) were the most common complications among preeclampsia and eclampsia patients [25]. These differences may reflect the variations in the quality of antenatal and postnatal care across settings.

Low birth weight was observed in 41.3% of neonates, which is higher than the Ghanaian study (19%) [38]. Similarly, a previous study reported that preeclamptic mothers had a higher risk of neonatal complications [39], underscoring the importance of optimal antenatal and postnatal care in minimizing neonatal complications.

In the present study, diabetes was also a significant predictor of maternal mortality. Nonetheless, this finding is based on small numbers and may be influenced by residual confounding, and the results should be interpreted with caution. A study done in the USA reported that mothers with prior diabetes were three times more prone to in-hospital maternal mortality and cardiac arrest [40]. It has also been reported that preexisting diabetes in pregnancy significantly increases the risk for severe maternal vascular outcomes, including preeclampsia. This necessitates evaluation during preconception and early pregnancy screening for coronary artery disease and microvascular comorbidities [41]. Consequently, when this preexisting vulnerability meets the physiological changes that take place during pregnancy, including increased blood volume, cardiac output, and heart rate, additional strain is placed on the heart. The combined impact of these factors and the elevated risk of heart disease in diabetic mothers increases their risk for morbidity and mortality [42, 43]. Late diagnosis, limited access to antenatal care, inadequate sugar monitoring and resource constraints may worsen diabetes-related pregnancy complications in the Kenyan settings compared with high-income countries. However, additional studies with a sufficient sample size to account for confounders such as body mass index and other maternal characteristics are needed to assess the relationship of diabetes and preeclampsia and eclampsia with maternal mortality in Kenya.

Children of multigravida mothers had higher odds of perinatal mortality compared to children of primigravida mothers. This is consistent with prior studies reporting higher perinatal mortality in multigravida women [44–46]. The higher risk of perinatal mortality in multigravida women is probably associated with a higher risk of obstetric complications such as preeclampsia, antepartum haemorrhage, placenta previa and uterine rupture. These complications are directly associated with increased perinatal mortality [47]. However, other studies reported that perinatal mortality was higher in primigravida mothers [48, 49]. This can be attributed to primigravida mothers’ limited knowledge and experience in newborn care. Additionally, they may not know the significance of antenatal and postnatal care monitoring [49].

Limitations of the study

The retrospective nature of the study posed certain limitations, particularly a small sample size resulting from incomplete documentation of key medical information among the study participants, which may limit generalizability of the findings. In addition, a substantial number of files were excluded due to physical damage from water exposure and termite infestation of the medical records of the patients. This may have introduced selection bias and affected the generalizability and robustness of the findings.

As a referral hospital, Garissa County Referral Hospital receives more severe cases, which may have higher mortality rates in comparison to the general population of pregnant women with preeclampsia and eclampsia. Hence, our findings may not be fully generalizable to the broader community-based pregnant population and should be interpreted in the context of a tertiary referral setting. The current study could not assess the effects of specific treatment modalities on maternal and perinatal outcomes, necessitating future studies.

Conclusion

The maternal and perinatal mortality among mothers with preeclampsia and eclampsia in this study was comparable to the rates reported in similar settings. Multigravida status and pre-existing diabetes were significant predictors of perinatal mortality and maternal mortality, respectively. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions, including improved access to emergency obstetric care, enhanced antenatal surveillance of multigravida women and close monitoring of women with pre-existing comorbidities such as diabetes.
