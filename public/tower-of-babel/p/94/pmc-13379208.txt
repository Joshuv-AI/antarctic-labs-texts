Thin Liquid Sip Sizes in Outpatient Adults with Normal and Disordered Swallowing: Modifying Factors and Relationships with Swallowing Safety and Efficiency
Authors: James A. Curtis, Leyla E. Jimenez, Isabel M. Aberin-Angulo, Christine M. Clark, Valentina Mocchetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThin liquids are an important part of many swallowing assessment protocols, yet little is known about self-selected sip sizes in outpatient adults nor their relationship with functional swallowing outcomes. Therefore, this quality improvement study aimed to: (1) assess the influence of testing-specific factors on the size of self-selected sips in outpatient adults; (2) examine the relationships between self-selected sip sizes, patient characteristics, and degree of swallowing impairment; and (3) evaluate the relationship between the self-selected sip sizes, swallowing safety, and swallowing efficiency.MethodsConsecutive outpatient adults being evaluated for known or suspected swallowing impairments completed randomized self-selected sips of thin liquids varying by cup size, fill level, liquid color, liquid type, bolus delivery method, swallowing instruction, and endoscope presence. Self-selected sip sizes were measured for each trial. Multilevel statistical models were used to assess the relationships between self-selected sip size, testing conditions, patient characteristics, and pharyngeal residue, penetration, and aspiration as seen during flexible endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) and modified barium swallow studies (MBS).ResultsSeventy-four outpatient adults were recruited, yielding an analysis of 595 self-selected sip sizes. The median self-selected sip size was 24.0 mL during the most natural drinking condition. Colored liquids, smaller amount of liquid filling a cup, and straw use significantly decreased sip size, whereas transnasal endoscopy and barium significantly increased sip size (p < 0.05). Sex and height were the only patient characteristics significantly associated with sip size (p < 0.05). The size of self-selected sips of thin liquid was not associated with measures of pharyngeal residue, penetration, and aspiration (p > 0.05).ConclusionOutpatient adults typically select thin liquid sips of ∼24 mL under natural conditions, similar to what is reported in healthy community dwelling adults and far exceeding sip sizes frequently used during routine clinical assessments. Testing-induced alterations significantly alter natural sip size, suggesting that “natural” self-selected sips during clinical assessments, FEES, and MBS rarely reflect real-world drinking. Because sip size is not predictable from patient characteristics and is altered when being testing, clinicians should consider testing a range of fixed volumes to comprehensively assess swallowing function across patients.

Introduction

Thin liquids are routinely incorporated into clinical swallow evaluations, flexible endoscopic evaluations of swallowing (FEES), and modified barium swallow studies (MBS) [1–7]. From a physiologic perspective, thin liquids can help reveal impairments across multiple components of the swallow mechanism, including oral containment and transport (e.g., lip closure, tongue control), swallow timing (e.g., pharyngeal swallow initiation), and key pharyngeal events (e.g., soft palate elevation, hyolaryngeal excursion, epiglottic movement, laryngeal vestibule closure, pharyngeal constriction, and pharyngoesophageal segment opening) [8]. From a functional perspective, thin liquids are central to evaluating swallowing efficiency (e.g., pharyngeal residue) and swallowing safety (i.e., penetration and aspiration) [6, 8–14]. Because thin liquids are commonly consumed in daily life and meaningfully affect swallowing-related quality of life [15], protocols that use thin liquids should, as much as feasible, approximate real-world drinking conditions. This is important for ecological validity – so that observations made during the evaluation generalize to everyday swallowing and inform accurate clinical recommendations.

One important consideration when implementing protocols with thin liquids is the size of each sip being taken. Sip size can alter oropharyngeal biomechanics and, in turn, functional swallowing outcomes [16–19]. In healthy adults, self-selected thin liquid sips under natural drinking conditions typically fall between 17 and 29 mL [20–25]. By contrast, many standardized assessment protocols rely on fixed volumes of 5–10 mL, with relatively few including larger volumes (e.g., 20 mL) [1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 26–29]. Consistent with this, a recent survey found that the two most common thin-liquid volumes assessed in clinical practice were: (1) controlled, premeasured sips of ≤5 mL; and (2) self-selected (not fixed or premeasured) sips [30]. Together, these patterns suggest that commonly used fixed volumes may be several-fold smaller than typical everyday sips, raising concern that protocols may underestimate real-world drinking demands. However, it remains unclear whether outpatient adults with dysphagia self-select sip sizes comparable to non-dysphagic individuals or whether they systematically adopt smaller sips. Clarifying typical sip size in outpatient dysphagia populations could inform selection of fixed volumes that better reflect everyday drinking and improve the ecological validity of standardized assessments.

To improve ecological validity, some swallowing assessment protocols integrate self-selected sip sizes, with the assumption that these better approximate patients’ everyday drinking. However, whether sip sizes chosen during FEES or MBS truly reflect natural drinking remains unclear. In healthy adults, self-selected sip sizes during instrumental assessments typically range from 12 to 19 mL [17, 25, 27, 31] – substantially smaller than those reported in noninstrumental settings – suggesting that the testing context itself alters drinking behavior. Prior work has shown that sip size is influenced by testing-specific factors, including liquid type (e.g., sweet, sour, barium) [21, 23, 25], cup size and apparent fill level [24, 25], bolus delivery method (e.g., cup vs. straw) [24], and swallowing instructions [21–23]. In contrast, the role of patient-specific factors is less well defined: sex and upper aerodigestive morphology are commonly associated with sip size, whereas effects of age, height, and weight are inconsistent or unknown [17, 21, 23–25]. Data from mixed outpatient dysphagia populations are particularly limited. Clarifying how both testing- and patient-specific factors influence self-selected thin liquid sip size is therefore essential for designing assessment protocols that reflect typical drinking behavior rather than artifacts of the instrumental testing environment.

Beyond characterizing “typical” sip sizes, it is also critical to determine whether variation in thin liquid sip size meaningfully influences swallowing efficiency (pharyngeal residue) and swallowing safety (penetration, aspiration). In both clinical practice and research, bolus volume is routinely manipulated – by starting with small sips and titrating upward or by deliberately restricting volume in higher-risk patients – under the assumption that larger sips predictably worsen safety and efficiency. However, there is limited empirical evidence in outpatient dysphagia populations directly linking changes in self-selected thin liquid sip size to residue patterns or airway invasion events. In the absence of such data, clinicians risk using test volumes that fail to elicit clinically relevant impairments, potentially underestimating aspiration risk and mischaracterizing patients’ real-world swallowing function.

Accordingly, the goal of this quality improvement study was to characterize self-selected thin liquid sip sizes in outpatient adults undergoing dysphagia evaluation and to determine: (1) how common testing conditions influence sip size; (2) whether sip size varies with patient characteristics or swallowing impairment; and (3) whether sip size is associated with swallowing safety and efficiency outcomes. We hypothesized that sip size would vary across testing conditions, show modest associations with body size and sex, and that larger sips would be associated with worse swallowing safety and efficiency.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This was a prospective, repeated-measures, quality improvement study completed within an outpatient otolaryngology department at a tertiary academic medical center. Outpatient adults were consecutively recruited during routine dysphagia evaluations between August 2025 and December 2025. Two care settings were included: (1) a multidisciplinary dysphagia clinic where patients underwent an SLP-led FEES; and (2) a radiology suite where patients underwent an SLP-led MBS. Inclusion criteria required participants to be ≥18 years of age and to report current consumption of thin liquids at home, even if intake was reported to be minimal. Exclusion criteria included being <18 years of age, inability to follow verbal instructions, and complete study procedures due to clinical workflow/time constraints. Patients were planned for consecutive enrollment; however, scheduling demands occasionally prevented full test completion. All participants were informed that the purpose of this quality improvement project was to understand typical sip volumes of thin liquids to improve the ecological validity of the institution’s swallow-assessment protocols, but they were blinded to specific study aims and hypotheses.

Patient characteristic variables were recorded for each patient and included age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI). Self-reported perceived swallow function was obtained using the Eating Assessment Tool (EAT-10) screening questionnaire [32]. Clinician-rated, instrumentally observed swallow function was measured using DIGEST safety (DIGEST-S), efficiency (DIGEST-E), and total (DIGEST-T) grades, derived from FEES and MBS using a standard swallowing protocol [5, 29, 33]. The swallowing trials used in this study for the DIGEST and DIGEST-FEES were presented in a fixed order and included the following: 5 mL thin liquid, 10 mL thin liquid, 20 mL thin liquid, self-selected volume of thin liquid (two trials), 5 mL pudding, 10 mL pudding, 2 g cracker (two trials).

Study Procedures

All patients completed thin-liquid drinking trials in one or both clinical settings. Participants remained seated upright throughout testing. Sip volume was not fixed; instead, patients were instructed to take natural, comfortable, self-selected sip sizes unless otherwise specified. The standard instruction was: “Take a natural sip. It can be as big or small as you’d like—whatever you would normally do at home.” In trials requiring a “single-swallow” swallowing instruction rather than “natural swallow” swallowing instruction, wording was modified accordingly: “Take a natural sip. It can be as big or small as you’d like—whatever you would normally do at home. But take it in just one swallow–try not to do extra swallows.” All sips were self-administered. Instructions were given prior to the start of the sipping trial to avoid any potential effects of verbal cueing on swallowing function [34, 35]. Sip size was quantified using a high-precision digital food scale (capacity: 0.05–500 mL; accuracy: ±0.01 mL). Cups were weighed before and after each sip. Patients were blinded to measured sip sizes.

Sip size tasks

Both the 10-oz and 5-oz cups were clear plastic, allowing visual confirmation of fill level and liquid type. The large straw (6 mm ID × 19.5 cm length) and small straw (3 mm ID × 12.5 cm length) were also clear plastic. Green opaque water was prepared by mixing 1.0 mL of green food coloring (Chef-o-Van; Green Food Color) and 0.2 mL (4 drops) of white food coloring (AmeriColor; Soft Gel Paste Food Color Bright White) with 249 mL of room-temperature tap water. White coating water was prepared by mixing 6 mL of white food coloring (AmeriColor; Soft Gel Paste Food Color Bright White) with 244 mL of room-temperature tap water. These colorants were used since they are standard in our clinical practice based on current FEES research [36].

FEES exams were performed by an experienced SLP (>10 years dysphagia practice) or by a trained SLP graduate intern (i.e., completion of ≥10 healthy adult endoscopic passes and ∼15 supervised patient FEES examination by the time of study initiation) under the direct supervision of an experienced SLP. Instrumentation included a 2.4-mm distal-chip flexible laryngoscope (VNL8-J10, PENTAX Medical), DEFINA EPK-300 processor with xenon light source, and LCD display. Prior to endoscope placement, ∼0.1 mL of a 2% lidocaine/neo-synephrine topical mixture was aerosolized into each naris approximately 3 min before scope introduction [37]. Video clips were stored digitally for subsequent blinded analysis.

MBS-Associated Condition

A description of the MBS-related sip size tasks is outlined in Table 1. Patients completed one block of six randomized trials (n = 6). Experimental conditions varied by cup size (10 vs. 5 oz), fill volume (250 vs. 125 mL), liquid type (clear water vs. barium). The same cups used in FEES trials were used here. Water was room-temperature tap water; barium stimuli consisted of 40% w/v Varibar barium sulfate thin liquid (Bracco Imaging).

Videofluoroscopy was captured using a TIMS DICOM audiovisual system at 30 frames per second. Recording included the lips anteriorly, nasal cavity superiorly, cervical spine posteriorly, and extended inferiorly to the cervical esophagus and proximal trachea. Studies were acquired at 30 pulses per second and stored directly to TIMS Review Software.

Data Analysis and Outcome Measures

Flexible Endoscopic Evaluation of Swallowing

FEES were blindly analyzed using the visual analysis of swallowing efficiency and safety (VASES) [38, 39]. FEES outcomes were only measured for the green opaque and white coating water self-selected sips, whereas the clear water sips were not analyzed due to lack of endoscopic visualization. VASES provided standardized percentage-based perceptual quantification of residue within the oropharynx, hypopharynx, laryngeal vestibule, vocal folds, and subglottic space. Efficiency outcomes included oropharyngeal and hypopharyngeal residue ratings. Safety outcomes were defined as laryngeal vestibule, vocal fold, and subglottic residue ratings.

Oropharyngeal residue ratings were made by estimating how filled the valleculae would be with all residue contained within the oropharynx and on the epiglottis. Hypopharyngeal residue ratings were made by estimating how filled the piriforms would be with all residue contained within the boundary of the hypopharynx. Laryngeal vestibule residue ratings were made by estimating how covered the laryngeal vestibule was with residue. Vocal fold residue ratings were made by estimating how covered the superior surface of the membranous vocal folds was with vocal fold residue. Subglottic residue ratings were made by estimating how covered the subglottic shelf was with subglottic residue.

Modified Barium Swallow Studies

MBS video clips were exported from TIMS Review Software and analyzed in ImageJ (Version 2.14.0/1.54f), a free, open-source image quantification tool developed by the National Institutes of Health. All analyses were performed blinded to study condition. Pixel-based, frame-by-frame evaluations were completed using the freehand selection tool to outline bolus area of interest, supporting quantification of residue volume.

MBS were analyzed using: (1) Bolus Clearance Ratio (BCR) [40] – a fluoroscopic measure of swallowing efficiency; and (2) Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS) [41] – a fluoroscopic measure of swallowing safety. BCR is a ratio comparing the amount of bolus present in the pharynx at two time points: (1) during the swallow – when the bolus volume in the pharynx and proximal esophageal is maximal, and (2) after the swallow – immediately following pharyngoesophageal segment closure, but prior to full laryngeal descent [40]. Residue is quantified by tracing bolus area in both frames and calculating the clearance ratio. This measurement was completed after the first, primary bolus containing swallow (i.e., excluding any anticipatory swallows with none-to-minimal oral bolus propulsion). PAS is an ordinal 8-point perceptual scale describing depth of airway invasion and patient response. Scores range from 1 (no invasion) to 8 (silent aspiration). Scores 2–5 represent penetration events, whereas 6–8 represent aspiration.

Assessors

Each FEES and MBS functional swallowing outcome measure was independently rated by two assessors who were blinded to patient identity and self-selected sip size. Assessors were speech-language pathology graduate student interns who received standardized training from the first author in the application of all FEES- and MBS-based measurement methodologies described above. For each FEES-associated sip trial, raters recorded the number of swallows and assigned VASES residue ratings for the oropharynx, hypopharynx, laryngeal vestibule, vocal folds, and subglottic space. For each MBS-associated sip trial, raters recorded the number of swallows, BCR, and PAS.

A priori agreement thresholds were defined for categorical primary outcome measures, including PAS score (MBS) and the presence versus absence of laryngeal vestibule, vocal fold, and subglottic residue (FEES). Agreement thresholds were not applied to continuous outcome measures, including the amount of residue when present and bolus clearance ratio. Agreement was defined as: (a) exact agreement on PAS score; and (b) agreement on residue presence versus absence (0 vs. >0).

Ratings that met agreement criteria were retained for analysis. In cases of disagreement (i.e., any PAS mismatch or disagreement on residue presence vs. absence), ratings were adjudicated by the first author, who was blinded to the paired ratings as well as all participant identifiers and study condition information. The adjudicated ratings were then used for analysis. For continuous outcome measures without predefined agreement thresholds (i.e., amount of residue when present and bolus clearance ratio), values from the two raters were averaged and used for data analysis.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.3 [42]. A family-wise significance threshold of α = 0.05 was used for all inferential tests. When multiple comparisons were made within an analytic aim, a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied [43], to control the expected proportion of false discoveries at 5%. Raw (unadjusted) p values are reported in-text. Estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts were computed using the emmeans package to obtain model-derived sip-size estimates and group differences.

Aim 1 – Influence of Testing-Specific Factors on Self-Selected Sip Sizes

To determine effects of testing conditions on self-selected sip sizes, multilevel linear regression models were used, treating sip size (mL) as a continuous outcome and patient as a random intercept to account for repeated measures. Fixed-effect predictors included cup size (10 vs. 5 oz), fill volume (250 vs. 125 mL), delivery method (cup, large straw, small straw), swallowing instruction (natural vs single-swallow), liquid color (clear, green opaque, white), liquid type (water vs barium), and endoscope presence (yes vs. no). Marginal and conditional R2 were reported as standardized effect sizes. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied to seven predictor comparisons.

Aim 2 – Relationship between Patient-Specific Factors and Self-Selected Sip Sizes

Multilevel linear regression models were used to examine whether self-selected sip size varied as a function of patient-level factors. Fixed effects included age, sex, height, and body mass index (BMI) to represent patient characteristics (i.e., demographics and anthropometrics). Swallowing-related fixed effects included Eating Assessment Tool-10 (EAT-10) scores and DIGEST Safety, Efficiency, and Total grades. Given the observed distribution of DIGEST scores, Safety, Efficiency, and Total grades were modeled as three ordinal categories (0, 1, and 2–4). Self-selected sip size was modeled as a continuous outcome, with participant included as a random intercept to account for within-individual correlation across repeated trials. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied separately for patient-characteristic comparisons (four tests) and for swallowing-function comparisons (four tests).

Aim 3 – Relationship between Self-Selected Sip Size, Swallow Safety, and Swallowing Efficiency

Self-selected sip size was the fixed-effect predictor, with swallowing safety and efficiency outcomes as dependent variables. For models including FEES-based VASES residue outcomes, bolus color (green opaque vs. white coating) was included as a covariate [36, 44]. Standardized β coefficients are reported for continuous outcomes and odds ratios for ordinal outcomes. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied across eight outcome-level comparisons.

For FEES, the self-selected sips of green opaque and white coating water were analyzed, whereas the clear water sips were not. Oropharyngeal and hypopharyngeal residue were modeled using multilevel beta regression. Laryngeal vestibule and vocal fold residue were analyzed using zero-inflated beta models to account for: (1) presence vs. absence of airway invasion residue (i.e., ratings 0 vs. >0); and (2) amount of airway invasion residue when present (i.e., when >0). Subglottic residue was not analyzed due to only four observed aspiration events.

For MBS, the three thin-liquid barium sips were analyzed, whereas the clear water sips were excluded. PAS (ordinal 1–8) was analyzed using cumulative-link mixed-effects modeling, and BCR was analyzed using linear mixed-effects regression. All models included participant as a random intercept to account for repeated measures.

Intra-Rater Reliability

Inter-rater reliability was evaluated using 100% of paired dual ratings from the two independent assessors. Continuous outcomes – VASES residue ratings (percent-based) and bolus clearance ratio (BCR) – were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC; two-way mixed-effects model, single-measure, absolute-agreement). Ordinal outcomes were assessed using Cohen’s kappa, with quadratic weighting for PAS. We report ICC estimates with 95% confidence intervals for continuous outcomes and weighted kappa (κw) with 95% confidence intervals for PAS. Unweighted kappa (κ) was used for agreement on number of swallows.

Results

A total of 74 patients were included in this study (Table 2), yielding an analysis of 595 self-selected sips. The cohort was clinically heterogenous, with primary medical diagnoses including esophageal disorders (45.9%), spinal disorders (23.0%), neurologic disease (21.6%), respiratory disorders (16.2%), and head and neck cancer (12.2%). Based on DIGEST ratings from FEES or MBS, 44.6% had no swallow impairment (DIGEST 0) as seen on FEES or MBS, 28.4% presented with mild swallow impairment (DIGEST 1), 16.2% presented with moderate swallow impairment (DIGEST 2), 10.8% presented with severe swallow impairment (DIGEST 3), and 0% presented with profound impairment (DIGEST 4).

Assessor Agreement/Inter-Rater Reliability

A total of 27 PAS scores, 8 laryngeal vestibule residue ratings, 4 vocal fold residue ratings, and 1 subglottic residue rating disagreed and required reconciliation by the first author. Inter-rater reliability coefficients were observed to be ICC = 0.504 for oropharyngeal residue, ICC = 0.681 for hypopharyngeal residue, ICC = 0.727 for laryngeal vestibule residue, ICC = 0.784 for vocal fold residue, ICC = 0.840 for subglottic residue, ICC = 0.911 for bolus clearance ratio, and κw = 0.794 for PAS, and κ = 0.653 for number of swallows.

Aim 1: Influence of Testing-Specific Factors on Self-Selected Sip Size

Sip size varied significantly across several testing parameters (Fig. 2). Small amount of liquid filling a cup decreased sip size (25.3 mL vs. 22.8 mL; p = 0.001, marginal R2 = 0.011). Water with food coloring reduced sip size relative to water with no food coloring (24.2 mL clear water vs. 20.8 mL white coating and 19.7 mL green opaque; p = 0.011 and p < 0.0001; marginal R2 = 0.039). Delivery method showed the largest effect (25.1 mL cup; 19.6 mL big straw; 16.7 mL small straw; p < 0.0001 and p < 0.0001; marginal R2 = 0.109). Sip size was slightly larger with barium than clear water (27.2 vs. 24.8 mL; p = 0.007; marginal R2 = 0.006) and was greater when drinking with an endoscope in place (23.6 vs. 20.2 mL; p = 0.026; marginal R2 = 0.017). Cup size (22.5 vs. 23.9 mL for 10-oz vs. 5-oz) and swallowing instruction (25.1 vs. 23.3 mL for natural vs. single) did not significantly influence sip size (p = 0.079 and p = 0.212, respectively).

Aim 2: Relationship between Patient-Specific Factors and Self-Selected Sip Sizes

Patient-specific characteristics showed limited associations with sip size (Fig. 3). Sex was significantly related to sip size, such that men selected larger sips than women (25.0 vs. 19.7 mL; p = 0.022; marginal R2 = 0.050). Additionally, taller height was also associated with larger sips (β = 0.17 mL per 1 in; p = 0.022; marginal R2 = 0.045). However, age, BMI, and EAT-10 scores showed no statistically significant relationships with self-selected sip size (p > 0.05). Similarly, self-selected sip size did not differ across severity levels for DIGEST Safety, Efficiency, nor Total scores (p > 0.05), indicating that instrumentally confirmed swallow impairment was not a determinant of sip size in this study sample.

Self-selected sip size was not significantly associated with swallowing safety or efficiency outcomes in the current study sample (Fig. 4). Across FEES-based measures, sip size was not significantly related to oropharyngeal or hypopharyngeal residue (p > 0.05), nor to airway residue outcomes in the laryngeal vestibule or on the vocal folds (presence or amount when present; p > 0.05). Discussion

In this outpatient cohort, the median self-selected thin-liquid sip size under the most natural drinking condition was approximately 24 mL, closely aligning with values reported for healthy adults in similarly natural contexts [20–25]. This finding indicates that, when assessment conditions closely approximate everyday drinking, many patients – including those with perceived or instrumentally confirmed swallowing impairment – select sip volumes comparable to those of non-dysphagic adults. Notably, the cohort represented a clinically heterogenous outpatient population spanning esophageal, neurologic, respiratory, head and neck cancer, and spinal disorders, which supports interpretation of these findings as broadly reflective of real-word dysphagia referral rather than a single diagnostic group. Clinically, these data suggest that real-world thin-liquid sip volumes in outpatient adults commonly fall within a 20–30 mL range, substantially exceeding the 5–10 mL volumes that predominate in many standardized swallowing assessment protocols [30].

Recent evidence indicates that the self-selected sip is among the most common thin-liquid volume used during clinical swallow evaluations, FEES, and MBS. This practice likely reflects a combination of clinical ease (i.e., not needing to premeasure boluses) and an effort to enhance ecological validity by approximating patients’ natural drinking behavior. However, the present findings demonstrate that several features inherent to dysphagia assessment systematically alter self-selected sip size. Consistent with prior work, sip volume was sensitive to bolus appearance and delivery characteristics: the use of food coloring reduced sip size relative to clear water, straw drinking resulted in smaller sips than cup drinking, and partially filled cups elicited smaller sips than full cups [21–25].

Notably, swallowing instruction did not significantly influence sip size in the present study. This contrasts with previous reports and clinical observations suggesting that sip size varies as a function of clinician guidance and task demands [21]. One plausible explanation is the consistent emphasis in the current protocol on natural drinking (“as big or as small as you’d like—whatever you would do at home”), which may have attenuated instruction-related variability that is often present in routine clinical practice.

Both barium and the presence of a transnasal endoscope were associated with modestly larger self-selected sip sizes compared with clear water without an endoscope in situ. These findings run counter to prior work [45] and to our a priori hypotheses. Larger sips during transnasal endoscopy may reflect the need to coordinate cup drinking around the endoscope, whereas the mechanism underlying larger barium sips remains less clear. Both observations warrant further investigation.

Collectively, these findings underscore that common assessment modifications – including colored liquids or barium, altered cup fill levels, straw delivery, and endoscope presence – can meaningfully shift patients away from their usual sip sizes. As has been shown for pureed foods [46], these results caution against assuming that “self-selected” sips obtained during instrumental assessments accurately reflect everyday drinking behavior. In practice, sip sizes during FEES and MBS may either overestimate or underestimate the volumes patients typically consume outside the clinic.

Patient-specific factors accounted for relatively little variability in thin-liquid sip size. Sex and height emerged as the only significant predictors, whereas age, BMI, and patient-reported dysphagia severity (EAT-10) were not associated with sip volume. Because sex and height are physiologically correlated – men are, on average, taller than women – we conducted a post hoc exploratory analysis to assess whether these effects were independent or confounded by their intercorrelation. A multilevel model including both sex and height simultaneously as fixed-effect predictors revealed that neither variable remained statistically significant when mutually adjusted (sex: β = 4.47 mL, p = 0.084; height: β = 0.14 mL per inch, p = 0.070), suggesting that the individual associations observed in the primary analyses were likely attributable to shared variance between these two variables rather than independent effects on sip size. These findings further underscore the limited role of patient-level demographic and anthropometric factors in predicting self-selected thin-liquid sip size.

Moreover, self-selected sip size did not differ across DIGEST Safety, Efficiency, or Total grades. Although these demographic trends align with prior studies in healthy adults, the absence of an association between sip size and either patient-perceived or instrumentally confirmed swallowing impairment in an outpatient dysphagia cohort is novel. These findings challenge the common clinical assumption that patients with greater medical complexity or more severe dysphagia will naturally self-limit sip size. Reliance on this assumption may lead clinicians to underestimate real-world drinking volumes and, consequently, to select test conditions that fail to capture clinically relevant swallowing demands.

In the present study, self-selected thin-liquid sip size was not significantly associated with measures of swallowing efficiency, including oropharyngeal residue, hypopharyngeal residue, or bolus clearance ratio. This contrasts with prior work in pureed food textures, where larger bite sizes have been linked to increased residue among people with disordered swallowing [46]. Together, these findings suggest that relationships between self-selected volume and residue may be bolus-dependent and less pronounced for thin liquids in outpatient adults.

Similarly, self-selected thin-liquid sip size was not meaningfully associated with airway invasion during FEES or MBS. This stands in contrast to earlier studies reporting increased aspiration risk with larger bolus volumes [11, 13, 14, 47]. Potential explanations include differences in study populations, analytic approaches that model PAS as an ordinal rather than a linear outcome, and the focus on naturally self-selected sip volumes rather than experimentally constrained bolus sizes. Importantly, the absence of a detectable relationship between sip size and airway invasion suggests that smaller sips are not inherently safer under natural drinking conditions. Clinically, these findings support the routine evaluation of a range of thin-liquid volumes and caution against over-reliance on volume restriction alone as a strategy for mitigating airway compromise.

Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations. First, all data were collected at a single tertiary care center using specific cup types, straws, and food-coloring preparations. Variations in utensils, cup geometry, or liquid types may yield different sip size behaviors, which may limit generalizability to other clinical settings and assessment protocols. Second, although participants represented a range of DIGEST severity grades, most exhibited normal to moderate swallowing impairment; relatively few had severe dysphagia, and none met criteria for profound dysphagia as defined by DIGEST. In addition, aspiration events were infrequent in this cohort, precluding formal analysis of the relationship between sip size and aspiration. Larger studies including more severely impaired people are therefore needed to determine whether sip-size effects on swallowing safety and efficiency become more pronounced at higher levels of impairment.

Third, this quality improvement project was not designed or powered to compare diagnostic subgroups (e.g., neurodegenerative disease, head and neck cancer, pulmonary disease). As a result, etiology-specific differences in sip-size behavior and volume-outcome relationships remain unknown and warrant targeted investigation. Fourth, all “natural” sip sizes were observed in a clinical environment. Even under the most natural testing condition, participants were aware that their drinking behavior was being evaluated. Consequently, sip sizes in everyday home or community settings may differ from those observed here. Future studies incorporating ecological momentary assessment or ambulatory monitoring approaches may help bridge this gap and more directly capture real-world drinking behavior. Finally, although predictors were examined within predefined analytic aims using FDR correction, the number of testing- and patient-level variables relative to the sample size may limit precision of some estimates; larger studies with more targeted hypothesis-driven modeling are needed to confirm the stability and generalizability of these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, outpatient adults with both normal and mild to moderate disordered swallowing typically self-select thin-liquid sip sizes of approximately 24 mL. Features inherent to instrumental swallowing assessments – particularly the use of colored liquids or barium, straw delivery, altered cup fill levels, and the presence of a transnasal endoscope – systematically altered self-selected sip size. Because sip volume was not reliably predicted by age, body mass index, patient-reported dysphagia, or instrumentally confirmed swallowing severity, clinicians should avoid assuming that patients will “naturally” restrict sip size based on these factors alone. Future work should extend these findings to specific diagnostic populations, more severely impaired individuals, and naturalistic drinking contexts, and should further elucidate how thin-liquid sip size interacts with swallowing physiology to influence safety and efficiency in real-world settings.
