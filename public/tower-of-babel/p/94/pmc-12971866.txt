First record of natural helminth infection in early larval stages of two Anuran species
Authors: Maria Luiza Ribeiro Delgado, Murilo de Souza Queiroz, Hudson Alves Pinto, Tiago Leite Pezzuti, Luciano Alves dos Anjos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study reports trematode infection in tadpoles at early developmental stages under natural conditions. While most previous records describe infections in more advanced stages, we identified metacercariae of the diplostomid Neodiplostomum sp. in tadpoles of five anuran species at the Gosner stage between 25 and 28. A total of 207 tadpoles from eight species (Families Hylidae and Leptodactylidae) were collected from seasonal water bodies from Brazil, of which 39 were found parasitized with helminths. Within this group of infected tadpoles, 20 were specifically parasitized by trematodes, 12 of them at the earliest stages of tadpole development (Gosner stage 25). This record indicates that tadpole infection with trematodes may occur earlier than previously documented, providing new insights into the infection dynamics of larval trematodes.Keywords: Anurans, Infection dynamics, Natural infection, Neodiplostomum sp., Metacercariae

Introduction

Digenean trematodes are endoparasites with complex life cycles that include adults parasitizing vertebrate as definitive hosts and larval stages infecting obligatorily snails, in some cases, both vertebrates or invertebrates as second intermediate hosts (Szuroczki and Richardson 2009; Queiroz et al. 2021). Studies on trematode parasites of anurans are well documented in regions of the Northern Hemisphere; however, in the Neotropical region, there is still a lack of standard parasitological information (Cabrera-Guzmán et al. 2021). Moreover, most knowledge available for trematodes from anurans is based on studies of adult stages, and studies evaluating tadpoles are comparatively scarce. Nevertheless, trematode infection in amphibians has been considered an emerging concern, considering the impact the diseases caused by these agents in the context of recent urban development and environmental changes, such as the degradation of aquatic habitats (Skelly et al. 2006; VanAcker et al. 2019; Johnson et al. 2024).

As we observe a global decline in amphibian populations, it becomes increasingly crucial to analyze the elements that affect the survival of this animal group. This decline is the result of environmental changes, including both biotic factors, such as the introduction of species and the emergence of diseases, and abiotic factors, such as climate change and global warming (Gómez, 2023). Understanding how trematode infections affect the tadpole (health, fitness, body condition) assumes an extremely important role in the context of global changes (Kiesecker et al. 2004).

So far, trematode infections in tadpoles living under natural conditions have been recorded predominantly in more advanced stages of host development, from Gosner stage 27 onwards (Hamann and González 2009). This knowledge gap is significant, as understanding these processes can provide important insights into parasite ecology and the management of parasitic diseases in amphibians, as well as contribute to the understanding of ecological and evolutionary interactions between anurans and their parasites.

Materials and methods

Samplings were carried out in perennial ponds surrounded by Cerrado phyto physiognomies (savannah-type vegetation), located in the Central-West region of Brazil, state of Mato Grosso do Sul, in the municipalities of Selvíria (20.3400° S, 51.4039° W) and at the Pombo Municipal Natural Park in Três Lagoas (20.3388° S, 52.6470° W). Field campaigns occurred during the rainy season months, between October and March, in two consecutive years, 2024 and 2025. Specimens were collected using a capture net, a rectangular net measuring 50 cm x 75 cm with a 2 mm mesh; sweeps were performed through the water body with a sampling effort of 1 h, conducted during both morning and night periods for tadpole collection. The collected specimens were individualized in plastic bags containing water from the sampling site and transported to the Parasitism Ecology Laboratory (LECOP) at Unesp, Ilha Solteira, for classification based on their developmental stage according to Gosner (1960) and for taxonomic identification according to Rossa-Feres and Nomura (2006) and Schulze et al. (2015).

After classification, the animals were euthanized with eugenol solution and dissected in search of the presence of helminths (protocol number: SISBIO 91997-1). The helminths were killed with 70 °C water and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution for 24 h, preserved in 70% ethanol, then were stained with hydrochloric carmine, dehydrated through an ethanol series, and cleared using eugenol. Photomicrographs of the helminths were obtained using the computerized image analysis system LAZ V4 (Leica Application Suite), adapted to DM 2500-Leica microscope with phase-contrast interference system. Taxonomic identification of the trematodes was carried out based on Hamann and González (2009).

Results and discussion

A total of 207 tadpoles belonging to eight species, 20 Boana raniceps (Cope, 1862), 106 Boana albopunctata (Spix, 1824), 31 Dendropsophus nanus (Boulenger, 1889), 2 Dendropsophus cerradenses (Napoli & Caramaschi, 1998), 30 Scinax fuscovarius (Lutz, 1925) (Hylidae), and 11 Leptodactylus fuscus (Schneider, 1799), 4 Leptodactylus labyrinthicus (Spix, 1824), 3 Physalaemus cuvieri (Fitzinger, 1826) (Leptodactylidae), were collected. Among the sampled individuals, 39 tadpoles belonging to five species were parasitized by helminths, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 18.8%. Of the parasitized tadpoles, 20 were at early developmental stages (Gosner stages < 29; Gosner 1960), all of which were infected by trematodes. In these early-stage individuals, trematodes were found encysted in the musculature or infecting the kidneys. Among the infected species, Boana albopunctata exhibited the highest number of infected specimens across all developmental stages (Fig. 1), with 27 infected individuals (P 25%), followed by B. raniceps 1 (P 5%), Dendropsophus nanus 9 (P 29%), Leptodactylus labirinticus 1 (P 25%) e Physalaemos cuvieri with 1 individual (P 33%).

Lophosicyadiplostomum spp. (currently regarded as a synonym of Neodiplostomum; Achatz et al. 2025; and hereafter referred to as Neodiplostomum throughout the text) present a life cycle consistent with other diplostomids, with adults occurring in the intestine of piscivorous birds, where eggs are released via feces into aquatic environments (Channa et al. 2011). These eggs give rise to free-swimming miracidia that infect freshwater snails, although the specific mollusk taxa acting as first intermediate hosts remain unknown (López-Hernández et al. 2023). Amphibians, particularly anurans, serve as second intermediate hosts, with metacercariae developing mainly in the renal parenchyma of tadpoles and adult frogs (Hamann and González 2009; Aguiar et al. 2021). Unlike many digeneans, the metacercariae of Neodiplostomum spp. are unencysted within the kidney tissue, a feature emphasized in different studies (Hamann and González 2009; Queiroz et al. 2020). Despite these general patterns, important aspects of its biology, such as the identity of the snail hosts and detailed transmission dynamics, remain poorly resolved, highlighting the need for further experimental and molecular studies to clarify the complete life cycle of this genus.

This parasite had previously been reported parasitizing 14 species of adult anurans in Cerrado areas with the same physiognomy, and the collections were carried out in the same area (Queiroz et al. 2020; Aguiar et al. 2021). This is the first record of Neodiplostomum sp. parasitizing an anuran host at an early metamorphic stage (Gosner stages 25–28) (Fig. 2), specifically in Boana albopunctata and Dendropsophus nanus. The other three species infected did not present digenean infections at these developmental stages.

Previously, this trematode species had been used in experimental infections in more developed tadpoles (stages 31–34); the infection significantly affected tadpole behavior, with the more heavily parasitized individuals exhibiting increased swimming activity and higher food consumption, without gains in body mass (Rosa et al. 2024). Such behavioral and physiological changes throughout larval development may influence tadpole resistance to parasites and potential predators in the environment (Rohr et al. 2011).

Although the number of infected individuals was low, a clear trend is observable, which may be explained by our hypothesis that infections in juveniles at these stages cause significant damage and high mortality. Consequently, only the less heavily infected individuals survive to later developmental stages, resulting in a lower prevalence of infections among more advanced metamorphosing tadpoles. Studies have analyzed the relationship between tadpole developmental stage and susceptibility to cercarial infection, observing that hosts (at different Gosner stages) exposed to cercariae exhibited distinct responses, with tadpoles at stage 25 showing the highest mortality rates within the five days following exposure.

Schotthoefer et al. (2003), through laboratory-based experiments, demonstrated that the susceptibility of Rana pipienstadpoles to infection by echinostome cercariae varies according to developmental stage. The authors observed that infection-associated mortality occurred exclusively in tadpoles at Gosner stage 25, the earliest stage evaluated, and that survival at this stage decreased as cercarial dose increased. This heightened vulnerability during early developmental stages was primarily attributed to the site of cercarial encystment, which was concentrated in the pronephros—early functional renal organs with limited physiological capacity. In addition, the study revealed a progressive decline in susceptibility with advancing development, as encystment success was significantly higher at stages 25–26 (69.5%) than at more advanced stages, such as 37–38 (8.4%). Other experimental studies have similarly reported that early larval development constitutes a critical window of heightened risk, during which morphofunctional constraints, particularly those associated with the renal system, increase tadpole vulnerability to trematode infections (Fried et al. 1997; Queiroz et al. 2021; Rosa et al. 2024).

Experimental infections of tadpoles at different developmental stages have shown that the response to trematode infection varies with both age and infection intensity under controlled conditions (Rohr et al. 2011; Billet et al. 2020). It is possible to observe that infection prevalence decreases as tadpoles mature, supporting the idea that host development is associated with improved immune responses, as well as behavioral and morphological changes that reduce susceptibility to the parasite, such as reduced swimming activity or increased skin thickness (Rohr et al. 2011). Different from previous records, the present study describes for the first time the occurrence of this type of infection at such early developmental stages of tadpoles in a natural environment.

It is relevant to highlight that the time required for parasite infection can vary according to several factors, including host species and environmental conditions (Altman et al. 2016). This rapid infectious process had already been reported, and research indicated that younger tadpoles are more susceptible to rapid parasite infection in experimental infections (Schotthoefer et al. 2003; Rohr et al. 2011), a result consistent with the data presented here.

In Physalaemus cuvieri, variation in infection patterns should be interpreted in the context of the species’ rapid ontogeny and reproductive biology. Development is highly compressed, with hatching occurring ~ 48 h after oviposition and metamorphosis reached within 40–50 days (Barreto and Andrade 1995). This accelerated schedule affects stage-specific availability in field samples, favoring early larval stages and leading to underrepresentation of later stages. Accordingly, only one individual at Gosner stage 40 was recorded, suggesting that sampling occurred outside the peak abundance of advanced larval stages, possibly toward the end of the summer breeding season, contributing to the low representation of P. cuvieri in the dataset.

This study compiles records of Neodiplostomum sp. cercarial infections in tadpoles at early metamorphic developmental stages. These data provide a novel field-based record of trematode parasitism in tadpoles, indicating that trematode infections can occur at early larval stages, even though survival outcomes may differ among developmental stages, as most studies have reported infections predominantly in more advanced stages, while infections at earlier stages have been documented mainly under controlled laboratory conditions (Schotthoefer et al. 2003; Holland et al. 2007; Queiroz et al. 2020).

These findings increase the understanding of parasitic dynamics and the plasticity of the life cycles of these parasites. Considering the potential ecological implications and the survival of the hosts, it is recommended that future studies investigate the effects of these early infections on the development, behavior, and fitness of anurans.
