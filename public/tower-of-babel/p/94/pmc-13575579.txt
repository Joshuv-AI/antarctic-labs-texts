A Tale of Two Pathways: Same‐Surgeon Versus Different‐Surgeon Resection After Second Surgical Opinion
Authors: Areesh Mevawalla, Selamawit Woldesenbet, Azza Sarfraz, Qaidar Alizai, Meher Angez, Abdulaziz Elemosho, Odysseas P Chatzipanagiotou, Timothy M Pawlik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe role of second surgical opinions (SSOs) in gastrointestinal (GI) cancer care is not well‐defined. While SSOs are common, the impact may depend on whether patients ultimately undergo resection with the same surgeon or with a different surgeon after the SSO. We sought to characterize perioperative outcomes relative to SSO among older adults with GI cancers.MethodsUsing SEER–Medicare data (2000–2019), patients aged 66–90 with primary GI were identified. Cancer‐directed resections were categorized into three claims‐observed pathways: surgery without SSO, SSO with same‐surgeon resection, and SSO with different‐surgeon resection. Multivariable regression models assessed the association between operative pathway and perioperative outcomes including complications, extended length of stay (LOS), 90‐day readmission and mortality, discharge disposition, and achievement of a composite “textbook outcome.”ResultsAmong 40,603 surgical patients, 56.2% underwent surgery without SSO, 5.3% underwent SSO with same‐surgeon resection, and 38.5% underwent SSO with different‐surgeon resection. Compared with no SSO, SSO followed by resection with a different surgeon was associated with lower odds of 90‐day readmission (aOR 0.92, 95%CI 0.88–0.97), any complications (aOR 0.90, 95%CI 0.84–0.95), extended LOS (aOR 0.93, 95%CI 0.88–0.98), and mortality (aOR 0.67, 95%CI 0.58–0.78), as well as higher discharge‐home (aOR 1.05, 95%CI 1.01–1.12) and textbook outcome (aOR 1.12, 95%CI 1.07–1.17). In contrast, SSO with same‐surgeon resection was associated with higher complications (aOR 1.14, 95%CI 1.01–1.29), longer LOS (aOR 1.21, 95%CI1.09–1.35), and lower home discharge (aOR 0.77, 95%CI 0.70–0.85) with no survival advantage.ConclusionAmong older adults undergoing GI cancer surgery, SSO followed by resection with a different surgeon was associated with improved perioperative safety and recovery, whereas SSO followed by resection with the same surgeon was not associated with similar benefit. These findings suggest that the value of SSO may lie in its role as a pathway to a different surgical team when clinically appropriate.Keywords: gastrointestinal cancer, regionalization of care, second surgical opinion, surgical oncology

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) malignancies such as colorectal, pancreatic, hepatic, and biliary cancers impose an enormous healthcare burden, accounting for approximately 26% of all cancer diagnoses and 35% of cancer‐related deaths [1, 2]. In the U.S., colorectal cancer alone is projected to affect more than 171,000 individuals and cause over 65 000 deaths by 2030, representing a leading cause of cancer mortality [3, 4]. Pancreatic cancer, while less common, is among the most lethal malignancies and is projected to become the nation's second‐leading cause of cancer death by 2030 [5]. For most localized GI tumors, surgical resection is the cornerstone of curative‐intent treatment [6]. Yet in practice, fewer than half of patients eligible for curative‐intent surgery ultimately undergo resection, with outcomes varying substantially across patient populations and provider settings [7, 8]. These data underscore the need for timely referral and expert surgical judgment, as care by experienced teams maximizes curative potential.

Second opinions are increasingly integrated into modern cancer care pathways, particularly for technically complex GI operations [9, 10]. Approximately 1 in 6 patients with a new cancer diagnosis seek a second opinion, although reported rates range from 1% up to 88% [11]. Many newly diagnosed patients seek additional surgical input – often at cancer‐focused centers – motivated by diagnostic clarification, assessment of resectability, and treatment sequencing through multidisciplinary review [12]. Prior work suggests that obtaining a second surgical opinion (SSO) can increase the likelihood of definitive surgical resection; however, short‐term perioperative outcomes after an SSO have been mixed, with several analyses reporting comparable complication rates and length of stay (LOS) relative to single‐opinion care [13]. Other data suggest that surgeon and hospital selection reflect both patient agency and referral networks, and that organization of care (i.e., regional expertise, volume–outcome relationships) likely mediates the downstream impact of second opinions on safety and recovery [14].

Importantly, SSOs are often treated as a single exposure, obscuring whether patients ultimately proceed with the first surgeon or undergo resection with a different surgeon after the SSO. The objective of the current study was to examine older adults with GI cancers to determine whether claims‐observed SSO pathways were differentially associated with surgical outcomes, including complications, LOS, readmission, mortality, and discharge disposition. By characterizing differences between SSO with same‐surgeon resection and SSO with different‐surgeon resection, we sought to provide data to inform patient counseling, referral practices, and system design in GI surgical care.

Methods

Data Source, Study Population, and Cohort Selection

Linked Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results–Medicare (SEER–Medicare) files from 2000 to 2019 were used to identify older adults with GI cancers and to ascertain surgical care and outcomes [15]. The study population included individuals aged 66–90 years at cancer diagnosis with a first primary malignancy of the liver, biliary tract, pancreas, colon, and rectum/rectosigmoid identified via SEER site recodes and histology. Continuous enrollment in Medicare Parts A and B with no health maintenance organization participation was required for the 12 months preceding diagnosis and the 12 months following diagnosis (or until death). Patients diagnosed by death certificate or autopsy, those with implausible diagnosis–treatment dates, or missing county FIPS codes were excluded. Cancer‐directed resections (hepatectomy, pancreatectomy, colectomy, or rectal resection) were identified using validated codes; for patients with multiple operations, the earliest defined the index resection [16]. For patients who underwent surgery, continuous fee‐for‐service coverage from diagnosis through 90 days after discharge from the index hospitalization was additionally required. The Institutional Review Board at The Ohio State University waived informed consent because the data were analyzed under a SEER–Medicare Data Use Agreement.

Exposure and Covariates

The primary exposure was receipt of an SSO, defined as evaluation‐and‐management claims from ≥ 2 unique surgeon NPIs related to the index GI cancer. Encounters were identified in Carrier/Outpatient files using CPT visit codes and restricted to surgical specialties [17, 18, 19]. To avoid immortal‐time bias, the SSO window for operated patients spanned from diagnosis to the day before index resection; for non‐operated patients, from diagnosis to 180 days. Operated patients were then classified into three mutually exclusive claims‐observed pathways: surgery without an SSO (one unique surgeon NPI within the window), SSO with same‐surgeon resection (≥ 2 unique surgeon NPIs, with the operating surgeon matching the first surgeon), and SSO with different‐surgeon resection (≥ 2 unique surgeon NPIs, with the operating surgeon differing from the first surgeon). These categories were intended to capture patterns of surgeon use before resection rather than the clinical content of the SSO, the rationale for changing surgeons, or the capacity of the consulting institution. Among non‐operated patients, SSO status was recorded as present or absent using the 180‐day window. Covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, SEER tumor site and American Joint Committee on Cancer stage group (harmonized I–IV), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) derived from the 12 months preceding diagnosis, indicators of preoperative chemotherapy and preoperative radiotherapy, county‐level rurality (USDA Rural–Urban Continuum Codes), county Social Vulnerability Index (SVI; reported by CDC/ATSDR) categorized as tertiles, calendar year of diagnosis, and SEER registry [20, 21]. For descriptive context, travel distance to surgeons was calculated as the great‐circle distance between patient residential ZIP‐code centroid and surgeon practice ZIP on the claim; when surgeon ZIP was unavailable, the facility ZIP was used.

Outcomes of Interest

The primary utilization outcome was receipt of cancer‐directed resection within 6 months of diagnosis. Among operated patients, perioperative outcomes included a composite of major complications (i.e., respiratory failure/pneumonia, myocardial infarction, venous thromboembolism, acute kidney injury, hemorrhage/transfusion, surgical‐site infection, and gastrointestinal bleeding), 90‐day all‐cause mortality obtained from the Master Beneficiary Summary File, and 90‐day unplanned readmission to any acute‐care hospital with planned readmissions excluded using the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Planned Readmission Algorithm [22]. Index hospitalization LOS was measured in days; extended LOS was defined as greater than the 75th percentile within cancer site and procedure type [23]. Discharge disposition was categorized as home versus non‐home [24]. A textbook outcome composite was defined as no recorded complication, survival at 90 days, no readmission, and discharge home [25]. All outcomes were ascertained from MedPAR, Outpatient, and Carrier claims using established diagnosis and procedure code algorithms.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as median values with interquartile ranges for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables and compared across no SSO, SSO with same‐surgeon resection, and SSO with different‐surgeon resection groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test and χ2 test, respectively. The association between any SSO and undergoing resection within 6 months of diagnosis was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression adjusting for demographics, tumor characteristics, CCI, rurality, Social Vulnerability Index tertile, calendar year, and SEER registry fixed effects. Perioperative endpoints among operated patients were analyzed using multivariable regression models that included operative pathway as a three‐level categorical exposure, with surgery without SSO as the reference group. This approach allowed simultaneous evaluation of SSO with same‐surgeon resection and SSO with different‐surgeon resection relative to surgery without SSO within the same model. Binary outcomes were modeled with logistic regression using robust standard errors clustered at the hospital of surgery; LOS was modeled with a negative binomial regression with a log link, and incidence‐rate ratios were reported. Prespecified heterogeneity was examined by rurality and by cancer site using interaction terms; when interactions were significant (p < 0.10), stratum‐specific estimates were reported. Sensitivity analyses comprised restricting SSO to occur within 90 days of the first surgical consultation. Missing covariate data < 10% were handled by complete‐case analysis; variables with higher missingness (e.g., grade) included an “unknown” category. Two‐sided p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Basic Demographics

Among 70,750 patients with a GI cancer diagnosis, the most common sites were colon (n = 31 879, 45.1%), pancreas (n = 17 494, 24.7%), rectum (n = 10 878, 15.4%), liver (n = 5520, 7.8%), and biliary tract (n = 4979, 7.0%). Overall, 28 962 patients (40.9%) received an SSO and 41 788 (59.1%) did not. Compared with patients without an SSO, individuals with an SSO were younger (median age 75 vs. 76 years), more often White (79.5% vs. 78.2%), more frequently lived in metropolitan areas (85.8% vs. 81.1%), and had lower comorbidity burden (CCI > 2: 9.8% vs. 13.1%). Stage III disease was more common among patients with an SSO (22.9% vs. 19.4%), and patients with an SSO were more likely to be treated at teaching hospitals (58.6% vs. 55.5%) and undergo cancer‐directed surgery within 6 months (61.4% vs. 54.6%) (all p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Operative Pathways and Baseline Characteristics

Among 40 603 surgical patients, 22 825 (56.2%) underwent surgery without an SSO, 2157 (5.3%) underwent SSO with same‐surgeon resection, and 15,621 (38.5%) underwent SSO with different‐surgeon resection. When examined within each cancer site, operative pathway distributions differed across tumor types. SSO with different‐surgeon resection was most common among patients with rectal cancer (50.2%) and colon cancer (39.0%), but less common among patients with biliary tract (29.0%), pancreatic (21.7%), and liver cancers (19.7%). Conversely, surgery without SSO was most common among patients with liver (74.3%) and pancreatic cancers (71.5%). Full cancer‐site distributions are presented in Table 2. Compared with patients who underwent surgery without an SSO, individuals in the same‐surgeon and different‐surgeon SSO groups were younger (median 75 vs. 76 years) and more frequently metropolitan residents (n = 1839, 85.3% and n = 13 597, 87.0% vs. n = 18 195, 79.7%). Hospital characteristics at the site of definitive surgery also differed modestly across pathways; patients in the same‐surgeon and different‐surgeon SSO groups were more often treated at teaching hospitals (59.7% and 58.4% vs. 55.5%) and at hospitals with slightly larger median bed size (380 and 409 vs. 374 beds) than patients undergoing surgery without an SSO (all p < 0.001). Stage distribution also differed; patients in the same‐surgeon SSO group had a higher proportion of stage IV disease (n = 295, 13.7% vs. n = 1908, 8.4%), whereas patients in the different‐surgeon SSO group had more stage III disease (n = 4589, 29.4% vs. n = 5962, 26.1%) (p < 0.001). Median travel distance to the first surgeon also differed across pathways; 9.7 miles (IQR 4.4–22.0) for no SSO, 11.0 (4.7–25.9) for SSO with same‐surgeon resection, 11.2 (5.2–26.4) for SSO with different‐surgeon resection (p < 0.001). In contrast, median travel distance to the second surgeon was similar between the same‐surgeon and different‐surgeon SSO groups [11.0 (5.4–26.4) vs. 11.0 (5.4–26.6); p = 0.1] (Table 2).

Perioperative Outcomes and Adjusted Associations – Same‐Surgeon Versus Different‐Surgeon Resection After SSO

Home discharge was most frequent among patients who underwent SSO with different‐surgeon resection (n = 9702, 62.1%) compared with surgery without SSO (n = 13 455, 58.9%) and SSO with same‐surgeon resection (n = 1109, 51.4%). Similarly, unadjusted 90‐day mortality was lowest among patients who underwent SSO with different‐surgeon resection (n = 330, 2.1%), followed by SSO with same‐surgeon resection (n = 65, 3.0%) and surgery without SSO (n = 854, 3.7%) (both p < 0.001). Time to surgery was longer among patients with an SSO and shortest within the different‐surgeon SSO pathway [median 48 days (33–79) vs. 38 (24–57) vs. 54 (31–135); SSO with different‐surgeon resection vs. surgery without SSO vs. SSO with same‐surgeon resection; p < 0.001]. Index‐admission expenditure was lowest among patients who underwent SSO with different‐surgeon resection [$11 132 (8114–13 536) vs. $11 901 (8743–14 672) vs. $11 877 (8872–15 111); SSO with different‐surgeon resection vs. surgery without SSO vs. SSO with same‐surgeon resection; p < 0.001]. Consistent with the favorable components shown in Figure 1, textbook outcome was also most frequent among patients who underwent SSO with different‐surgeon resection (n = 8713, 55.8%) compared with surgery without SSO (n = 11 671, 51.1%) and SSO with same‐surgeon resection (n = 1046, 48.5%; p < 0.001) (Table 3, Figure 1).

On multivariable analysis, SSO with different‐surgeon resection, compared with surgery without SSO, was associated with lower odds of 90‐day readmission [aOR 0.92 (95%CI 0.88–0.97)], any complications [aOR 0.90 (95%CI 0.84–0.95)], extended LOS [aOR 0.93 (95%CI 0.88–0.98)], and 90‐day mortality [aOR 0.67 (95%CI 0.58–0.78)], as well as higher odds of home discharge [aOR 1.05 (95%CI 1.01–1.12)] and achieving a textbook outcome [aOR 1.12 (95%CI 1.07–1.17)]. In contrast, SSO with same‐surgeon resection, compared with surgery without SSO, was associated with higher complications [aOR 1.14 (95%CI 1.01–1.29)] and extended LOS [aOR 1.21 (95%CI 1.09–1.35)], lower odds of discharge home [aOR 0.77 (95%CI 0.70–0.85)], and no differences in 90‐day readmission [aOR 1.03 (95%CI 0.93–1.14)] or 90‐day mortality [aOR 0.86 (95%CI 0.66–1.13)]. Stage‐specific analyses of textbook outcome were consistently favorable with SSO with different‐surgeon resection [aOR, Stage I 1.09 (95%CI 1.01–1.19), Stage II 1.10 (95%CI 1.02–1.18), Stage III 1.15 (95%CI 1.06–1.25), Stage IV 1.22 (95%CI 1.05–1.41)], but not with SSO with same‐surgeon resection [aOR, Stage I 0.90 (95%CI 0.73–1.08), Stage II 0.99 (95%CI 0.85–1.17), Stage III 0.91 (95%CI 0.76–1.09), Stage IV 0.94 (95%CI 0.72–1.23)] (Table 4).

Sensitivity Analysis – Restricting Sso To ≤ 90 Days

When restricting SSOs to ≤ 90 days, SSO with different‐surgeon resection remained favorable compared with surgery without SSO; SSO with different‐surgeon resection was associated with lower odds of 90‐day readmission [aOR 0.91 (95%CI 0.87–0.96)], any complications [aOR 0.88 (95%CI 0.83–0.94)], extended LOS [aOR 0.91 (95%CI 0.86–0.96)], and 90‐day mortality [aOR 0.71 (95%CI 0.62–0.82)], as well as higher odds of home discharge [aOR 1.07 (95%CI 1.02–1.12)] and achieving a textbook outcome [aOR 1.14 (95%CI 1.08–1.19)] (eTable 1).

Discussion

The selection of a surgeon and surgical plan is critically important in oncologic cancer care, given that operations like pancreatic or esophageal resections are highly complex and outcomes vary widely [26]. In the US, seeking an SSO has become common – as over 40% of cancer patients pursue an SSO during their care – reflecting a broader shift toward multidisciplinary review of complex cancer cases [27]. Prior work in pancreatic cancer suggested that obtaining an SSO can alter management (e.g. increasing the likelihood of undergoing curative resection) without compromising short‐term outcomes [28]. In this context, we studied older adults undergoing major GI cancer surgery across three claims‐observed operative pathways: surgery without SSO, SSO with same‐surgeon resection, and SSO with different‐surgeon resection. The objective was to determine whether perioperative outcomes differed according to whether patients underwent surgery without SSO, SSO with same‐surgeon resection, or SSO with different‐surgeon resection. Notably, SSO followed by resection with a different surgeon was associated with improved perioperative safety and recovery, suggesting that second opinions may serve as a pathway through which selected patients are redirected to a different surgical team [19, 26].

SSO with different‐surgeon resection was associated with materially better perioperative safety and recovery. Compared with surgery without an SSO, this pathway was linked with lower odds of 90‐day readmission, postoperative complications, extended LOS, and mortality, alongside higher odds of discharge home and achieving a textbook outcome; these advantages persisted after adjustment and in sensitivity analyses. These findings suggest that the benefit associated with SSO may be less attributable to the second‐opinion encounter itself and more related to what the encounter enables: reassessment of operative candidacy, refinement of treatment sequencing, and potential redirection to a surgical team better aligned with the complexity of the patient's disease. Such interpretation is consistent with prior literature showing that outcomes after complex GI surgery vary substantially across care settings and are influenced by specialized technical expertise, perioperative infrastructure, multidisciplinary coordination, and failure‐to‐rescue capacity [29, 30]. In this context, SSO may function as a practical mechanism for connecting selected patients to surgical teams and systems capable of delivering complex oncologic care safely, although the current data cannot determine which specific system‐level factors mediated the observed associations [31, 32, 33].

Importantly, SSO with different‐surgeon resection did not appear to compromise care continuity or substantially increase measured travel burden among patients who accessed this pathway. Nevertheless, the relatively modest travel distances observed in this cohort likely reflect patients who successfully accessed surgical consultation and resection, and these findings may not fully capture barriers faced by patients in more remote areas. Despite involving a change in surgeon, this pathway was characterized by modest travel distances and similar or lower readmission rates, suggesting that surgeon change after SSO did not necessarily fragment perioperative care [34, 35]. This finding is clinically relevant because patients often balance perceived surgical expertise against concerns about travel, coordination, and continuity of care when deciding whether to pursue treatment outside their initial referral pathway [36, 37]. In fact, most patients choose surgeons through physician referrals or personal networks, and roughly one‐third of Medicare patients report that their doctor was the main decision‐maker related to where they underwent a major surgery [38, 39]. Such referral‐driven pathways may preserve continuity of care when an SSO is followed by resection with a different surgeon. Additionally, multidisciplinary cancer center networks can facilitate seamless transitions; coordinated tumor board review can modify treatment plans in a significant fraction of cases and impact the recommended surgical procedure or approach [19, 33]. Collectively, these data suggest that SSO followed by resection with a different surgeon may be associated with improved outcomes without necessarily fragmenting care or increasing patient burden among patients who are able to access such pathways.

At present, access to high‐volume centers remains uneven; despite decades of evidence linking volume to outcomes, many complex GI cancer operations in the US continue to be performed outside tertiary centers, and many patients – especially older adults – do not travel for specialty surgical care [36]. In this context, structured SSO pathways may offer a practical mechanism to facilitate additional surgical evaluation when clinical complexity, uncertainty regarding resectability, or patient preference warrants further review [40]. Dedicated navigation programs could support these referrals by assisting patients with logistics, education, and coordination between referring and receiving teams [36]. Transparency around surgical expertise, institutional resources, and multidisciplinary capabilities may also help patients and referring clinicians make informed decisions [36]. Payers and policymakers could further support these pathways by reducing logistical barriers—for example, through travel support, telemedicine‐enabled review, or centers‐of‐excellence networks for complex cancer surgery [41]. Importantly, such efforts should be designed to avoid widening disparities in access and help ensure that older adults and patients from geographically or socially vulnerable communities can benefit from SSO pathways when clinically appropriate [9].

This study has several limitations. SEER–Medicare lacks granular clinical details such as tumor biology, performance status, detailed imaging findings, surgeon‐assessed resectability, and patient preference. This limitation is particularly relevant to the association between SSO and receipt of surgery, as patients without a documented SSO had a higher proportion of stage IV disease and may have included individuals with clearly unresectable disease, prohibitive comorbidity, limited life expectancy, or preference against surgery. In addition, the intent, content, and rationale for obtaining an SSO could not be determined; therefore, claims‐observed pathways should not be interpreted as evidence that the second opinion changed the treatment recommendation or caused referral to higher‐volume care. Selection bias is also possible, as patients who underwent resection with a different surgeon after SSO may have differed in resources, health literacy, operative candidacy, or motivation to seek additional care. Despite these limitations, the study capitalizes on a large, population‐based sample with real‐world treatment variation.

In conclusion, among older adults with GI cancers, SSO followed by resection with a different surgeon was associated with improved perioperative outcomes, including lower morbidity, lower 90‐day mortality, shorter hospitalization, and greater likelihood of achieving a textbook outcome. In contrast, SSO followed by resection with the same surgeon was not associated with similar benefit. These findings suggest that SSOs may be most valuable when they facilitate appropriate transition to a different surgical team or care pathway, rather than functioning solely as a confirmatory encounter. Future work should identify which patients benefit most from facilitated SSO referral and how such pathways can be implemented without increasing geographic or logistical barriers.

Synopsis

Patients with gastrointestinal cancers who obtained a second surgical opinion experienced different outcomes depending on whether resection was performed by the same surgeon or a different surgeon. SSO followed by resection with a different surgeon was associated with lower perioperative complications, reduced readmissions and mortality, and a higher likelihood of achieving a textbook outcome, whereas SSO followed by resection with the same surgeon was not associated with similar benefit. Second surgical opinions may be associated with improved outcomes when they facilitate transition to a different surgical team when clinically appropriate.
