Predicting lifetime cervical cancer screening among sexually active women in low- and middle-income countries using machine learning models: evidence from Demographic and Health Surveys
Authors: Fentahun Bikale Kebede, Kusse Urmale Mare, Fetlework Gubena Arage, Ayele Tiyou, Getachew Teshale, Abenezer Zenebe Kebede, Nebyu Lakew Tefera, Amanuel Worku, Bewuketu Terefe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTIONCervical cancer poses a major health risk in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), with high morbidity and mortality among women. It is largely preventable through screening and early treatment, but underused programs lead to a heavy burden. The goal is to identify key factors influencing screening, reveal inequities, and improve policies in resource-limited areas.METHODSUsing recent Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) data from 19 LMICs, this study estimated lifetime cervical cancer screening among women aged 15 to 49 years. Missing data were removed, and class balancing was performed with synthetic minority oversampling technique. GridSearch optimized hyperparameters before dividing data into training and validation sets. Multiple machine learning algorithms predicted lifetime screening, including decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting, extra trees, XGBoost, LightGBM, and catBoost classifiers. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, recall, precision, F1 score, and receiver operating characteristics area under the curve.RESULTSThe analysis of 261,371 women aged 15 to 49 from the DHS found that random forest achieved 80% accuracy, 87% precision, 80% recall, and an 80% F1 score, while extra trees achieved 79%, 87%, 79%, and 83%. Key predictors included household size, wealth, women’s age, and education. Ensemble methods such as random forests and extra trees demonstrated strong generalization, making them useful for targeted interventions.CONCLUSIONEnsemble algorithms such as random forests and extra trees predict lifetime cervical cancer screening with ∼80% accuracy in LMICS. The study emphasizes targeted awareness, education, and age-sensitive screening. Findings will be tested in other populations to support clinical decision systems.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a significant public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where it is a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women. Globally, over 300,000 women die annually from cervical cancer, with approximately 94% of these deaths occurring in LMICs,1, 2, 3 The disease is largely preventable through effective screening and early intervention. Yet, the implementation and uptake of cervical cancer screening programs in these settings are persistently low, resulting in a disproportionate burden of disease.4,5

Screening for cervical cancer has been instrumental in reducing incidence and mortality in high-income countries. However, in LMICs, coverage remains alarmingly low, with studies reporting average screening rates of only 19% compared to 63% in high-income countries.5, 6, 7 Socioeconomic inequalities, limited access to healthcare, lack of awareness, and cultural barriers contribute to this gap.8 Evidence from Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) across multiple countries consistently shows that women in rural areas, those with lower educational attainment, and those in poorer wealth quintiles are less likely to be screened.5,9,10 These disparities underscore the urgent need for innovative and scalable solutions to improve screening uptake and equity.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) offer promising avenues to enhance cervical cancer screening, especially in resource-constrained settings.11 ML algorithms have demonstrated superior performance in predicting screening uptake and identifying at-risk populations by capturing complex, nonlinear relationships among sociodemographic and behavioral factors.1,4,12 Studies have applied a range of algorithms—including logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, support vector machines, and deep learning models—to both clinical and population-level data, achieving high accuracy in risk prediction and classification tasks.1,12, 13, 14

For instance, ensemble models such as Extra Trees and Random Forests have shown promise in predicting cervical cancer screening uptake among women aged 25 to 49 using DHS data from Sub-Saharan Africa.1 Similarly, deep learning approaches, including convolutional neural networks, have been effective in automating the classification of cervical images, thereby reducing reliance on scarce expert personnel in LMICs.14 These technological advancements have the potential to improve diagnostic precision, facilitate early intervention, and enhance the overall efficacy of screening initiatives.4,12

Despite these advancements, several critical gaps remain: many ML applications are developed in isolation and have not yet been integrated into existing clinical workflows or national screening programs.4,6,13 The effectiveness of ML models is often hampered by the limited availability of high-quality, representative data from LMICs, with most studies relying on small or nonpopulation-based datasets.4,14 Moreover, socioeconomic and geographic disparities in screening persist, and there is limited research on how ML can be leveraged to address these inequities at scale.5,10 The deployment of AI and ML in healthcare raises concerns about data privacy, algorithmic bias, and informed consent, particularly among vulnerable populations.4,15 Structural, cultural, and health system barriers—including lack of awareness, limited trained personnel, inadequate funding, and weak policy implementation—continue to impede the effectiveness of screening programs in LMICs.6,7

Recent studies using DHS data have provided valuable insights into the sociodemographic and behavioral determinants of cervical cancer screening among women in LMICs.1,5,8,9 By leveraging large, nationally representative datasets, researchers can develop and validate robust ML models that account for the diverse factors influencing screening uptake. These models can inform targeted interventions and policy decisions aimed at increasing coverage and reducing disparities.

Considering the persistent burden of cervical cancer and the promise of ML, this study aims to apply advanced ML algorithms to analyse lifetime cervical cancer screening among sexually active women in LMICs, utilizing evidence from DHS. The objective is to identify key predictors of screening uptake, uncover patterns of inequity, and provide actionable insights to inform policy and practice, ultimately contributing to the global effort to reduce cervical cancer incidence and mortality in resource-limited settings.1

Methods

Target variable

Lifetime cervical cancer screening uptake (Yes/No).

Independent variables

Socio-demographic variables: Age, educational status, marital status, types of marriage, employment status, access to media, sex of household head, family size, household wealth status, residence, and region.

Obstetrics and health-related characteristics: Age at first marriage, parity, using contraceptive methods, using contraceptive methods, decision maker on women’s healthcare, distance to a health facility, and getting money for medical help.

Operational definition

Lifetime cervical cancer screening uptake is classified as “Yes” or “No.” Women were asked whether they had ever been tested for cervical cancer by health professionals after explaining the screening procedure and techniques (cytology or visual inspection) to remember it.

Access to media is created by combining survey questions on the frequency of reading newspapers or magazines, listening to the radio, and watching television. Those who were exposed to at least one of these media at least once a week were considered to have access to mass media.

Multiple sexual partners: women who had more than one sexual partner in their lifetime were categorized as “Yes.”

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing, including data cleaning and balancing, reduces prediction errors and improves the efficiency of the ML models. Data impurities, including noise, outliers, redundancy, and missing values, can significantly impact outcomes. We removed variables with more than 30% missing values and used mean imputations for continuous variables and mode imputations for categorical variables with less than 30% missing values. After balancing the data using the synthetic minority oversampling technique, it was split into training and testing sets in an 80/20 ratio. Grid search hyperparameter tuning techniques with fivefold cross-validation were used to optimize each algorithm, and their accuracy was compared. High-accuracy algorithms were further evaluated using precision, recall, F1 score, and area under the curve (AUC).

Model selection

ML algorithms, such as decision tree classifiers, random forest classifiers, gradient boosting classifiers, extra trees classifiers, extreme Gradient Boosting Classifier (XGBoost), light gradient boosting machine classifiers (light GBM), and categorical boosting classifier (CatBoost), were used and evaluated using metrics such as accuracy, recall, precision, F1-score, and receiver operating characteristics (ROC) area under the curve.

Decision tree classifier

A decision tree is a supervised learning algorithm that constructs a model capable of predicting the class of the target variable by learning decision rules from the training data. It utilizes algorithms such as Gini impurity, entropy, gain ratio, and others to split and determine the structure of the tree.17

Random forest classifier

A random forest classifier is an ensemble ML method that combines tree predictors, such that each tree depends on the values of a random vector sampled independently and with the same distribution for all trees in the forest.18

Main steps in random forest classifier 1.Bootstrapping (bagging): A number of bootstrap samples are created by randomly sampling with replacement from the original data, and each sample is used to train a separate decision tree.2.Random feature selection: At each split of a decision tree, a random subset of m features is selected from the total n features. The split is made based on the best features from this subset, determined by metrics such as Gini impurity and entropy.3.Aggregation of predictions: Predictions from all decision trees are aggregated. For classification, majority voting is used, and for regression, the average of the predictions is used.

Gradient boosting classifier

It is an ensemble of ML algorithms used for classification tasks, building a strong classifier by combining multiple weak learners, with each new tree trained to correct the errors of the previous ones. It follows a step-wise manner using gradient descent to reduce the loss function.19

Extras tree classifier

Extremely randomized tree classifiers, also known as extra tree classifiers, are an ensemble ML method that builds many uncorrelated decision trees and combines their predictions. It is similar to a random forest, but it introduces more randomness by selecting split points randomly for each feature.20

eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)

XGBoost is a highly optimized, scalable, and efficient implementation of the gradient boosting framework, widely used for classification and regression. It is an ensemble method that constructs a series of decision trees, with each tree correcting the errors of the previous ones by performing gradient descent on the loss function. XGBoost improves upon traditional gradient boosting by incorporating regularization to reduce overfitting, enabling parallelization during training, offering fast execution, and scalability.21

Light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM)

LightGBM is an open-source, highly efficient, and high-performance gradient boosting framework developed by Microsoft, based on decision-tree algorithms. This algorithm is implemented using two key techniques: histogram-based decision tree learning, which discretizes continuous features into bins, and leaf-wise tree growth, which splits the leaf with the maximum loss reduction, resulting in faster convergence with better accuracy.22

Categorical boosting (CatBoost)

CatBoost is a high-performance gradient boosting framework developed by Yandex as an open-source tool for handling categorical features without extensive preprocessing. It is based on gradient boosting decision tree algorithms, particularly known for its accuracy and efficient handling of categorical data, which is a powerful tool for classification and regression tasks.23

Model evaluation

Evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and AUC score, were used to evaluate the models. Result

Socio-demographic characteristics

Out of 261,371 (weighted) sexually active women included in the analysis, 109,511 (41.9%) were between the ages of 35 and 49 years, and 215,258 (82.4%) were currently married. Nearly a quarter (25.7%) of the women had no formal education, while 28,316 (10.8%) had pursued higher education. Almost half (49.0%) of the women were employed, and more than half (52.9%) had exposure to mass media. Additionally, 158,916 (60.8%) of the women lived in rural areas, and 109,474 (41.9%) belonged to households with a low wealth index. Correlation of features

The correlation heatmap (Figure 1) provides insights into the relationships between key variables in the dataset. The results indicate that age at marriage, household wealth, family size, and education level exhibit weak correlations with other variables, suggesting that these factors operate independently in influencing the outcome. A notable negative correlation is observed between parity (number of children) and women’s age (−0.44), indicating that younger women tend to have fewer children, which aligns with demographic trends. Similarly, women’s education level and age (−0.15) show a weak negative correlation, suggesting that younger women may have attained higher education levels compared to older generations. Other variables, such as household wealth and family size, exhibit minimal correlation, suggesting that economic status does not strongly influence household size within this dataset. Developing and evaluating models

The evaluation (Table 4) of different ML models was conducted using five key performance metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC. The results indicate that ensemble-based models, particularly CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost, and Gradient Boosting, achieved the highest AUC scores, suggesting their superior ability to distinguish between classes. Random Forest and Gradient Boosting demonstrated balanced performance across all metrics, making them reliable choices for classification tasks. In contrast, the Decision Tree model exhibited the lowest AUC (0.59) and recall (0.75), indicating its limited effectiveness for this classification problem. Extra Trees and XGBoost showed relatively lower recall compared to their precision, which may indicate potential bias toward the majority class. Discussion

This study reveals alarmingly low cervical cancer screening rates among women in LMICs. Only 10.5%% of high-risk women (aged 35–49) were screened, leaving millions unprotected against a preventable disease (WHO, 2024). Screening disparities are stark: rural women (5.9% of the sample), those without education (4.2%), and the poorest households (4.7%) had the lowest uptake (Table 2).5,9 These gaps confirm that poverty, limited education, and geographic isolation remain critical barriers.6,8

ML exposed hidden drivers of low screening. Married (37.6% of women) was the top predictor of nonscreening, likely because women in such unions often lack autonomy over health decisions.24 Only 18.1% of women made health choices alone, but those sharing decisions with partners had higher screening rates (Table 2).8 Distance barriers (reported by 30.5%) further reduced access.6 SHAP analysis (Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 6) quantified these risks: low education, poverty, or high parity (many children) pushed screening probabilities down sharply. For example, one woman’s screening chance fell to 12% due to her lack of education and household poverty (Figure 5), echoing findings from Sub-Saharan Africa.1

The correlation heatmap (Figure 1) reveals that major demographic and socioeconomic variables, such as age at marriage, household wealth, family size, and education level, exhibit weak correlations with each other, indicating that these factors function largely independently in shaping cervical cancer screening uptake.25,26 This independence suggests that women’s health-related choices are often multifactorial and driven by complex, context-dependent influences.27 For instance, the lack of a strong correlation between household wealth and family size echoes the notion that economic status alone does not necessarily dictate household composition, especially in cultural contexts where fertility preferences or extended families prevail.28 The negative correlation between parity (number of children) and women’s age (r=−0.44) simply reflects the expected demographic pattern that younger women have had less time to bear children.28 Such patterns are foundational in demography and are consistent with reproductive behavior trends across numerous LMICs.29

Furthermore, a weak negative correlation between women’s education level and age (r=−0.15) may illustrate generational improvements in access to education for girls, with younger cohorts achieving higher educational attainment compared to older women.26,27 This is important because education is linked to better health-seeking behaviors, health literacy, and uptake of preventive care, such as cancer screening.30 The generally low inter-feature correlations, as shown in the heatmap, reinforce the value of utilizing a diverse set of predictors in predictive modeling, minimizing collinearity and improving model performance.31,32

The process of feature selection using RFE (Figure 2) prioritized marital status—particularly polygamous marriage—as the strongest predictor, with regional and country-specific variables also ranking highly.32,33 This finding highlights the deep influence of cultural, marital, and geographic factors in shaping preventive healthcare behaviors.34 Healthcare access variables, such as medical assistance, health insurance coverage, and healthcare decision-making, were found to be moderately important, reflecting the established role of structural healthcare barriers and facilitators in screening uptake.30,35 Conversely, broader socioeconomic and demographic factors (age at marriage, wealth, parity, family size) were ranked lower in predictive importance, suggesting that distal or structural features, although relevant, are less immediate determinants for cervical cancer screening compared to more proximal and context-bound variables.27,36,37 This insight aligns with research indicating that healthcare access is a stronger driver for preventive services than pure economic resources, especially where primary barriers are logistical or sociocultural.38

Model evaluation through cross-validated ML approaches showed that boosting-based ensemble algorithms, such as CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost, and Gradient Boosting, achieved the highest accuracies and AUC values, confirming their superior ability to distinguish screeners from nonscreeners in complex health datasets (Figure 3).19,21 This agrees with the broader ML literature, which demonstrates that boosting models outperform basic tree-based or bagging-based methods in classification problems with potentially imbalanced or high-dimensional data.39 Overall, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting balanced performance across all metrics, emphasizing their reliability for health outcome prediction.18 In contrast, the poor recall and low AUC of the Decision Tree model (AUC=0.59) indicate limitations in simpler, unstable classifiers, likely due to overfitting and a lack of ensemble error correction.40 The observation that models like Extra Trees and XGBoost have relatively lower recall but higher precision indicates potential overfitting or sensitivity to class imbalance, a well-documented challenge in health analytics.41,42

The ROC curve comparison (Figure 3) further elucidates these trends: boosting algorithms such as Gradient Boosting (AUC=0.77), LightGBM (0.77), XGBoost (0.76), and CatBoost (0.76) all perform markedly better than bagging approaches like Random Forest (AUC=0.68) and Extra Trees (0.65), while the Decision Tree model underperforms (AUC=0.59).19,21 This superiority arises from the iterative, bias-minimizing nature of boosting, which makes these models particularly powerful in healthcare prediction contexts.40 High AUC values (>0.75) indicate useful discriminatory ability, and the dramatic gap between ensemble (boosting) and single-tree methods underscores the need for advanced model selection in public health applications.32 The overall pattern confirms extensive studies showing the dominance of ensemble boosting methods for complex, nuanced prediction tasks.18,43

Model explainability, facilitated by SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analyses, deepens understanding of key variable contributions to screening uptake (Figure 4, Figure 5).44 The SHAP beeswarm plot (Figure 4) demonstrates that household weight, household wealth, women’s education, women’s age, and regional origin (ncountry_India) are the highest impact predictors, paralleling previous findings that socioeconomic advantage and older age are consistently linked with higher preventive service usage.35,36 Notably, being from India and having a larger family size reduces the likelihood of screening, which may potentially reflect regional disparities, infrastructure gaps, or broader systemic barriers to access.29,35 The beeswarm’s red (high) values for household weight, wealth, and education, which are associated with positive SHAP values, are consistent with the concept that social determinants of health operate additively and synergistically.27,38,45

The SHAP waterfall plot (Figure 5) for an individual case exemplifies how cumulative disadvantages—absence of women’s education, high parity, low household wealth, and low weight- produce a downward shift in screening prediction from the baseline, reflecting the literature’s perspective that disadvantage accrues across multiple domains.38,44 Even potential positive contributors, such as being from India and having an older woman’s age, do not offset the predominant negative influences, underlining the challenge of single-lever interventions in complex public health inequities.45 Similarly, the SHAP force plot (Figure 6) reveals that negative determinants—such as low age, high parity, low wealth, moderate weight, and no insurance—collectively drive the predicted probability near zero.44,45 These SHAP-based visualizations not only align with the social determinants of health framework but also offer transparency and individual-level interpretability, which are critical for the practical adoption of ML in healthcare.46, 47, 48

In summary, this analysis demonstrates that, although population-level socioeconomic and demographic variables explain some variation in cervical cancer screening uptake, region- and household-level factors, as well as healthcare access, are far more potent determinants.35,37 State-of-the-art ensemble boosting models provide the best predictive accuracy, as reflected in AUC and other key metrics, consistent with current best practice in health analytics.21,39 Model explainability, enabled by SHAP, brings unique value by exposing how distinct combinations of social and individual attributes interact to drive individual predictions—illuminating population risk profiles as well as targeted intervention opportunities.44,47 This multidimensional approach underscores the importance of not only developing robust risk models but also adopting intervention strategies that comprehensively address access, education, and regional disparities to accelerate progress toward cervical cancer elimination.28

Strengths and limitations of the study

One major strength of the study is its comprehensive analytical approach, which combines traditional statistical methods with advanced ML techniques. The use of various ensemble models, such as CatBoost, LightGBM, and XGBoost, enables robust evaluation and comparison, ensuring that the predictive findings are not model-dependent. The study also benefits from employing explainability tools, such as SHAP, which provide transparent insight into the contributions of individual predictors at both global and local levels. By assessing a wide range of demographic, socioeconomic, and healthcare access variables, the study provides a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing cervical cancer screening uptake. The use of feature selection methods ensures that the model is both effective and parsimonious, reducing the risk of overfitting while maintaining interpretability. Lastly, performance was assessed using a suite of relevant metrics, which strengthens the appropriateness and generalizability of the results.

Despite its strengths, the study has several limitations. The analysis is based on a specific dataset, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or regions with different sociocultural or healthcare contexts. Potential confounding variables not included in the dataset may influence the results, and unmeasured biases could affect the interpretation of the associations. The study’s cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causality between predictors and outcomes. There is also a risk of information bias due to self-reported variables, especially on sensitive topics such as health-seeking behavior or socioeconomic status. Imbalanced class distributions may have impacted model performance, particularly for metrics like recall, and could have led to overfitting in some ensemble algorithms. Finally, while SHAP improves interpretability, complex interactions between features may still be missed or oversimplified by model-based explanation methods

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that advanced ensemble ML models, combined with explainability techniques, can provide significant insights into the factors influencing cervical cancer screening uptake. The results indicate that regional and healthcare access factors, more than broader socioeconomic or demographic indicators, are primary determinants of screening participation in the study sample. The findings underscore the importance of targeted interventions that address structural barriers and contextual differences rather than relying solely on economic or demographic targeting. By integrating explainability tools, the study bridges the gap between complex analytics and actionable policies, highlighting at-risk groups and informing more equitable public health strategies. Overall, the study contributes to the evidence base guiding policy and intervention design aimed at accelerating the uptake of preventive healthcare services.
