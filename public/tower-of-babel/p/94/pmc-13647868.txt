Developing a framework for primary health care resilience in pandemic conditions for Iran
Authors: Naser Derakhshani, Erfan Kharazmi, Seyedeh Narges Pouyan, Azam Choopani, Yousef Pashaei Asl, Nasrin Moradi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPrimary Health Care (PHC) services are people's first point of contact with the health system. PHC helps to ensure health system resilience and health community, playing a vital role in preventing and detecting diseases early, especially during pandemics. By doing so, PHC helps reduce mortality rates significantly. This study aimed to explore the challenges affecting the resilience of PHC during pandemics and to develop a Framework for strengthening that resilience.MethodsThis qualitative research was conducted in three phases in 2024. In the first phase, semi-structured interviews were held with 14 experts in PHC that originated from IRAN to identify key challenges in PHC service delivery during pandemics. In the second phase, an initial resilience Framework was designed through an expert panel involving five specialists. In the final phase, the Framework was validated using a one-round Delphi process with 21 experts. Data were analyzed using MAXQDA 2020.FindingsThe analysis revealed six main themes: processes, human resource management, financial resource management, infrastructure and equipment, sociocultural factors, and policy and planning. From the data, 20 sub-themes and 102 initial codes were extracted. Based on expert feedback, a proposed PHC resilience Framework was developed and validated through the Delphi method, achieving a consensus score of 0.90.ConclusionHealth system managers and policymakers can apply the identified components of the proposed Framework to improve PHC resilience. Key strategies include optimizing financial resource allocation, expanding human resource capacity, enhancing workforce preparedness for pandemic response, promoting public awareness, strengthening social support systems, and advancing digital health and telemedicine infrastructure. These measures can help ensure the continuity of PHC services and enhance preparedness for future pandemics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01389-5.

Introduction

Health system includes all the activities whose primary purpose is to promote, restore and maintain health [1, 2]. Primary Health Care (PHC) is a cornerstone of health systems, playing a crucial role in disease prevention and early detection. By minimizing unnecessary visits to emergency departments and hospitals, PHC increases system efficiency, reduces healthcare costs, and improves public health and well-being [3–7]. Ensuring equitable access especially for vulnerable populations to essential services such as immunization, family care, tuberculosis and HIV/AIDS screening, and non-communicable disease management is a key priority in PHC delivery.

During pandemics, PHC strategies such as rapid screening, timely case identification, patient follow-up, and appropriate referrals are essential for disease control [8, 9]. However, effective PHC implementation often faces barriers such as poor intersectoral coordination between levels of health service providers, inadequate funding, inequitable payment systems for staff, limited use of modern technologies and unfairly distribution human resources [9–12–13]. The emergence of novel infectious diseases has further disrupted PHC operations, shifting service demands and altering disease patterns and health outcomes [14–16]. At the same time, the broad social, economic, and political impacts of pandemics highlight the urgent need to strengthen health system resilience to ensure uninterrupted essential services [17, 18]. Therefore, during pandemics, governments should be protective to continue providing essential care [19] and policymakers should invest more in PHC systems to continuously provide the health services [20, 21]. Evidence from prior studies indicates that robust social support mechanisms play a pivotal role in enhancing the resilience of primary health care systems during pandemics, primarily by fostering effective teamwork among health professionals and strengthening coordination between individuals and communities regarding their respective roles in crisis response [22–24].

Pandemic-related shocks can overwhelm PHC facilities by creating a sudden surge in demand. If these centers lack sufficient resilience, they may struggle to meet the population’s basic health needs. A global study during the COVID-19 pandemic reported that nearly 90% of countries experienced disruptions in essential health services [25]. For instance, in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, over one million infants missed BCG and first-dose measles vaccinations, and more than 80 million children were left vulnerable to diseases like diphtheria, rubella, and polio during the pandemic [25, 26]. Such service disruptions reduce access to critical care and create serious gaps in continuity [27–29]. Public fear of infection has also contributed to delayed or avoided care-seeking behavior [30, 31]. Also, COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated burnout of staff in primary care centers [32].

Primary health care in Iran faced challenges as coordinated national and international efforts, as well as the cultivation of public trust, the need for innovation and optimal utilization of existing resources to improve community health services and safety, ensuring psychological and financial support for healthcare personnel, maintaining adequate reserves and timely access to essential medications, treatments, and protective equipment, the importance of updated, reliable communication systems to disseminate information effectively; and securing sustainable financial resources for medical supplies, protective equipment, and staff remuneration [33, 34].

Because PHC centers serve as the foundation of health systems, they must be capable of maintaining essential services even during crises. This requires the capacity to respond to a wide range of health needs, emergencies, and social changes [35].Resilience refers to a health system’s ability to adapt, absorb shocks, and respond effectively under pressure [36]. In a resilient health system, health actors, institutions, and populations have capacity to prepare and effectively respond to crises; maintain core functions; and, informed by lessons learned during the crisis, and reorganize if conditions require it. In a resilient health system, human life protected and health outcome is produced in good manner for everyone during the crisis [18].

However, in many countries, health systems lack resilience due to weak leadership, fragmented structures, resource limitations, poor coordination, and insufficient performance monitoring [20]. Moreover, pandemic response efforts have often focused on hospital preparedness, overlooking the critical preventive and continuity roles of PHC [37]. As a result, PHC centers have faced shortages in medical equipment, personal protective gear, staff, and financial resources needed for essential care delivery [8].

In Iran, studies have shown that neglecting resilience in PHC has disrupted service delivery chains. These disruptions may increase mortality among vulnerable groups such as pregnant women, young children, and individuals with chronic illnesses like diabetes and hypertension [38] and impaired liver and renal function [39]. Some studies have confirmed an increase in the number of stroke cases and cardiovascular events during the COVID-19 pandemic due to lack of visits to health care facilities [40–42]. Overall, pandemics can substantially undermine the delivery of essential health services [16].

Since PHC centers play a central role in delivering basic health services, any disruption in their operations during crises can have significant public health consequences [43]. Therefore, protective and supportive measures from governments to maintain uninterrupted service delivery during pandemics are crucial [19]. In this context, health policymakers must prioritize and invest in strengthening PHC systems to ensure the continuous and reliable provision of services [20]. Accordingly, this study was designed to assess the resilience of essential service delivery in PHC settings during pandemics and to develop a framework for its enhancement.

Data collection

An interview guide was shared with participants in advance, either in person or electronically (Appendix 1). Interviews were held in person or by phone, based on participant preference, and lasted between 45 and 60 min. Written informed consent was obtained from participants who were face to face interview and via email for virtual participants. Verbal consent was obtained to record the sessions, and each participant was assigned a unique code to maintain confidentiality.

Data analysis

Content analysis was used to identify, analyze, and interpret recurring themes within the text [47, 48]. Two researchers collaborated throughout the analysis process—particularly in reviewing transcripts, extracting codes, categorizing themes, and ensuring consistency.

Rigor and trustworthiness

To ensure the credibility and reliability of the findings, Guba and Lincoln’s four criteria—credibility, confirmability, dependability, and transferability—were applied [49]. At the end of each interview, the participants’ opinions were recounted to them to ensure that there was no ambiguity in their opinions and to confirm the accuracy of their statements.

Phase two

Expert roundtable and panel discussions

The second phase involved synthesizing the qualitative findings into a preliminary framework through collaborative discussions among the research team and a panel of experts.

Initially, the research team held a roundtable discussion to consolidate findings from the interviews, remove redundancies, and develop a draft Framework. This Framework was then refined through two expert panel sessions.

Study sample

Five experts in PHC were selected using purposive sampling [44]. The panel included two faculty members in health services management, one in health policy, one in disaster health, and one in public health. Feedback and suggestions provided during the sessions were used to iteratively revise the framework.

Data collection

Expert panels are well suited for gathering expert perspectives and building consensus [50]. Each component of the draft Framework was discussed individually. Ambiguities and disagreements were addressed, and consensus was reached on which elements to retain or modify.

Two expert panel sessions were held, each lasting approximately 75 min. With participant consent, sessions were audio recorded. Approved revisions to the Framework were applied during the meetings in real-time.

Phase three

Delphi technique – framework validation

In the final phase, the Delphi method was used to assess and validate the proposed framework by achieving expert consensus (Appendix 2).

Study sample

Out of 23 invited experts, 21 responded (95.4%). Participants included 11 men (54.5%), and 10 held PhDs in health-related fields (45.4%). Participants were given two weeks to respond, with follow-up reminders sent as needed. A stratified purposeful sampling method was used to ensure diversity in expertise and position [44]. This method involves the deliberate selection of information-rich participants who possess in-depth knowledge of the phenomenon under investigation and are able to articulate their experiences and insights effectively. Moreover, this strategy integrates two established purposive sampling techniques (typical case sampling and maximum variation sampling) thereby enabling the inclusion of participants who are representative of common cases while also capturing a broad range of perspectives and contextual variations [45].

Data analysis

The Delphi responses were analyzed using Content Validity Index (CVI) and modified kappa (k*) statistics to assess agreement. Kappa values were calculated based on the proportion of agreement CVI and chance-adjusted agreement probability. According to Polit and colleagues, kappa values > 0.40 indicate fair agreement, 0.60–0.74 indicate good agreement, and values > 0.74 indicate excellent agreement [51].

Findings

The analysis of semi-structured interviews with PHC personnel across various levels identified six main themes, 20 sub-themes, and 102 primary codes related to the factors influencing and challenges affecting the resilience of PHC services during the COVID-19 pandemic. The main themes included: health service processes, human resource management, financial resource management, infrastructure and equipment, sociocultural factors, and planning and policymaking (Table 2).

Health service processes

The pandemic significantly disrupted core services such as maternal care, vaccination programs, and monitoring of communicable and non-communicable diseases. Public fear reduced clinic visits, leading to delays in routine care like Pap smears and gaps in postpartum services—highlighting structural weaknesses in service delivery. Vaccination programs faced issues such as inconsistent data recording, vaccine shortages, and underprepared staff.

“We struggled to provide postpartum care—many mothers avoided visits due to fear of infection at the clinic…” (P1), “During the peak of the crisis, we couldn’t even track empty vials properly due to high workload and lack of training…” (P4).

Human resource management

Participants emphasized the need to adapt human resource strategies during crises. Flexible work schedules, staff redeployment, and the involvement of volunteers and less-experienced personnel in non-clinical roles helped sustain operations. Supportive interventions, including staff training and psychosocial support (e.g., counseling, stress management, symbolic appreciation), were also critical.

“Without flexible shifts, burnout and resignations would have been much higher…” (P6), “Psychological support really helped reduce anxiety and fear among the staff…” (P14).

Financial resource management

Unstable funding and weak financial planning were major concerns. Participants cited the lack of emergency financial provisions, reliance on inconsistent funding sources, and inadequate staff compensation as demotivating factors. Payment delays and a lack of transparency in financial management during the crisis further hindered service continuity.

“Costs increased significantly, but staff salaries didn’t reflect the added pressure…” (P1), “Some months we had no idea when funding would come—we had to rely on local donations…” (P5).

Infrastructure and equipment

Participants described limitations in physical infrastructure and equipment, including outdated devices, low-quality protective gear, and a lack of dedicated spaces for suspected COVID-19 patients. However, some innovative solutions were implemented, such as using schools for vaccination and deploying mobile units.

“We used school buildings for vaccinations because our health center lacked the space…” (P6), “Internet access was poor in some villages, but the university improved bandwidth to help us cope…” (P11).

Sociocultural factors

Social capital, public trust, and community participation were essential for maintaining services. Successful strategies included involving local volunteers, engaging families, and using trusted community leaders for public outreach. However, misinformation, conflicting advice, and cultural tensions between traditional and modern medicine posed serious challenges.

“We couldn’t have maintained services without support from volunteer groups…” (P8), “People were confused between traditional and modern medicine—we had to address rumors constantly…” (P10).

Planning and policymaking

Participants stressed the importance of adaptable policies, evidence-based decision-making, and localized guidelines. Involving PHC representatives in planning meetings, coordinating with local authorities, and conducting emergency preparedness exercises were identified as key strategies. The COVID-19 experience was seen as a catalyst for revising health policies and improving crisis management structures.

“We tailored national guidelines to fit local needs—the standard versions weren’t effective…” (P1), “COVID showed us the need to strengthen PHC crisis management structures…” (P3), “No one had planned for a crisis like this…” (P2), “Different organizations gave conflicting instructions—we were overwhelmed…” (P6).

Results from expert panel and roundtable discussions

The expert panel developed a conceptual framework (Fig. 2) consisting of two interrelated cycles: The inner cycle includes six core components: human, financial, and physical resources, sociocultural factors, processes, and planning and policymaking. The outer cycle acts as an umbrella and includes: management and leadership, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation. When these components function cohesively, they support the continued delivery of PHC services and contribute to improving overall public health.

Delphi technique

The initial framework for the resilience of PHC services during pandemics consisted of six main domains: service processes, human resource management, financial resource management, infrastructure and equipment, sociocultural factors, and planning and policymaking, encompassing 20 sub-themes. These elements were selected to be evaluated in the Delphi process.

In this stage, the Delphi questionnaire was distributed to 23 national-level health system experts, and 21 participants completed and returned the form.

After analyzing the Delphi results, all 11 evaluation criteria in the questionnaire scored above 0.74, indicating a high level of agreement among experts. Consequently, the Delphi process was concluded after the first round (Table 3), and the proposed framework was finalized.

Qualitative study – semi-structured interviews

The first phase, among the various approaches of qualitative studies, the conventional Content-Analysis approach was selected. This approach is an appropriate approach to collect valid and reliable results; that can be used to obtain and develop rich information, insights and a guide to performance [52]. Conventional Content analysis is a method that has become popular in health studies recently. The content analysis is a flexible method for analyzing qualitative data. It describes a group of analytic approaches that differ from impressionistic, intuitive, interpretive analyses to systematic, strict textual analyses. Advantage of the conventional approach to content analysis is getting direct information from the participants without imposing preconceived categories or theoretical perspectives [53].

Discussion

This study aimed to develop a resilience framework for PHC services. The final Framework consisted of key components including governance and leadership, monitoring and evaluation, implementation, planning and policymaking, human resources, infrastructure and equipment, financial management, sociocultural support, and service delivery processes. These findings are consistent with the framework proposed by Mosadeghrad et al., which outlines structural and procedural elements of PHC resilience such as governance, financing, staffing, equipment, medications, information systems, and service delivery [20].

Management and leadership were identified as essential elements for building resilience. Effective crisis management requires timely responses, transparency in decision-making, and coordination across sectors. Health managers must possess the skills and flexibility to adapt to rapidly changing conditions, define clear roles, supervise tasks, and facilitate inter-organizational collaboration. Studies by Mahmoudi et al. and Jafari et al. also emphasized the critical role of leadership in organizational resilience [54, 55]. Moreover, coordinated efforts among government bodies, public health institutions, PHC systems, and hospitals are vital not only during crises but also throughout preparedness planning [56].

Monitoring and evaluation enable health managers to assess existing challenges and implement process improvements. According to Mosadeghrad et al., assessing the health system’s resilience is necessary to identify vulnerabilities and guide corrective actions [57]. Clinical audits, which evaluate structures, processes, and outcomes, can enhance service quality and effectiveness—ensuring that PHC systems remain responsive to ongoing challenges [58].

Human resources are central to PHC resilience. Adequate numbers of trained professionals such as physicians, midwives, and health workers are critical, especially during health emergencies [59]. Well-prepared staff can improve service delivery and contribute to a more adaptive, responsive health system. Godoi et al. noted that during pandemics, expanding the workforce, training personnel, and ensuring their protection is essential [60, 61]. Other studies confirm that PHC resilience depends on continuous investment in human resources, infrastructure, and service systems [62].

Infrastructure and equipment were also highlighted as essential for resilient PHC systems. The study emphasized the importance of sufficient physical space, access to medicines and medical supplies, and reliable information systems. Effective pandemic response depends on the availability of medical equipment and drugs. WHO reports have also recognized the significant role of vaccines and medications in building resilience—for example, the Ebola vaccine played a crucial role in containing that outbreak [63]. Additional studies confirm that improving health infrastructure, providing diagnostic tools, distributing vaccines, and developing digital systems are all vital for strengthening PHC capacity [20]. During COVID-19, access to personal protective equipment (PPE) was particularly critical for ensuring staff safety [64].

The study found that financial stability plays a crucial role in maintaining PHC services during crises. Inadequate funding can disrupt service delivery and negatively impact health outcomes. In India, increasing PHC budgets allowed the government to offer free diagnostic and treatment services [65]. Similarly, Thomas et al. emphasized that strategic allocation and protection of financial resources are fundamental to ensuring health system resilience during emergencies [66].

Sociocultural support was identified as another key factor in strengthening PHC resilience. Community participation, public education, and local engagement help build support networks that enable communities to better cope with health crises [67]. Walker et al. highlighted the role of organizational culture in enhancing institutional resilience [68]. Involving civil society and community-based groups in PHC governance increases responsiveness and helps reduce service fragmentation [69]. Other research has shown that without active participation and collaboration, even well-designed national health programs may fall short in real-world crises [70]. Developing guidelines to strengthen community participation for content transfer by health team members to the community and target groups in Iran could be effective.

Finally, the Framework highlighted the importance of maintaining continuity of care during pandemics. The findings suggest that expanding digital health tools can support PHC resilience by enabling remote communication, staff training, and patient support. Hearnshaw’s study confirmed that digital platforms such as phones, emails, and social media were effectively used for remote consultations and maternal health support during the pandemic [71]. Service delivery care for pregnant mothers was facilitated by the use remote counseling or telephone calls.

Limitations

One of the main limitations of this study was the exclusive participation of Iranian experts, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Due to restricted access to international experts and specialists, the proposed framework has been shaped primarily around the context of Iran’s health system, which reduces its applicability to other countries. However, in an effort to enhance the framework’s generalizability, multiple methods including qualitative study, expert panel discussions, and the delphi technique were employed for identifying and categorizing the key resilience factors.

Conclusion

Resilience in PHC is critically important, especially during pandemics, as PHC serves as the first point of contact between the public and the health system. The COVID-19 pandemic revealed both strengths and vulnerabilities in PHC resilience across many countries. Given the crucial role of PHC in disease prevention and early detection, as well as its contribution to reducing unnecessary hospital visits, improving system efficiency, lowering healthcare costs, and enhancing overall public health, its resilience and flexibility in the face of pandemics and crises is of great significance. Despite extensive efforts in this area, PHC service providers still face numerous challenges, highlighting the urgent need for comprehensive preparedness planning for future pandemics. The Framework developed in this study can serve as a practical guide for health system managers and policymakers in strengthening the resilience of PHC services during pandemics.

It is recommended that health policymakers utilize the components of this Framework to focus on evidence-based policymaking, increasing the recruitment and retention capacity of human resources, enhancing staff knowledge and skills, rational budgeting, improving public awareness, strengthening social support, and expanding telemedicine infrastructure. These measures are essential to ensuring the resilience of PHC systems and maintaining the continuity of essential services during future health crises.
