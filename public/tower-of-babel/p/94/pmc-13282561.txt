Reduced Cerebral Blood Flow in the Early Chronic Phase of Recurrent Concussion Among Female Collegiate‐Aged Athletes
Authors: Mitchell J Andersson, Bryna D Goeckner, Lezlie Y España, Andrew R Mayer, Benjamin L Brett, Timothy B Meier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe susceptibility of athletes to long‐term adverse outcomes following recurrent concussion remains controversial. Acute concussion precipitates a transient reduction in cerebral blood flow (CBF) that typically returns to pre‐injury levels. Recent work suggests that long‐term CBF alterations may be sex‐specific. We assessed whether concussion history was associated with chronic CBF alterations and whether sex moderated this relationship in otherwise healthy, active collegiate‐aged athletes. Collegiate‐aged athletes from the community (N = 204, 37.8% female, M age = 21.0 ± 1.7) underwent magnetic resonance imaging with pseudo‐continuous arterial spin labeling to quantify global and regional CBF and spatial coefficient of variation. Participants also completed measures of depression, anxiety, and cognition. Multiple linear regressions incorporating prior concussion‐by‐sex interaction terms adjusted for relevant covariates were fit to estimate the extent to which sex moderated the concussion history—CBF relationship. Exploratory analyses further assessed whether oral contraceptive use or pill formulation moderated this association in females. The number of prior concussions was negatively associated with global and regional gray‐matter CBF in female but not male athletes, with the strongest associations localizing to frontotemporal regions. CBF metrics were not related to cognitive performance or affective symptom burden. Contraceptive use did not appear to substantially moderate the concussion history—CBF association in female athletes. Female‐specific associations between concussion history and cerebral hypoperfusion in early adulthood suggest potential sex differences in pathways and trajectories of adverse long‐term outcomes. Early chronic CBF alterations may represent one vulnerability to future risk in a sex‐specific manner.Keywords: arterial spin labeling, mild traumatic brain injury, oral contraceptives, sex differences, sport

Introduction

Contact and collision sport participation places athletes at an elevated risk of sustaining recurrent sport‐related concussions (SRC; Maas et al. 2022; Rabinowitz et al. 2014), also known as mild traumatic brain injuries (mTBI; Silverberg et al. 2023). While substantial research has clarified the acute outcomes and recovery trajectories following single‐event SRC (Broglio et al. 2022; Caccese et al. 2024), less is known about the cumulative effects of recurrent concussions. Acutely, SRC‐inflicted forces can induce widespread neuronal and cerebrovascular dysfunction, contributing to the typically transient sequelae experienced by athletes in the hours and days following injury (Giza and Hovda 2014; Kontos et al. 2019). Meanwhile, accumulating evidence suggests that some athletes who appear clinically recovered from previous concussions may nevertheless exhibit subtle, enduring neurobiological changes beyond the acute phase of injury (Brett et al. 2022, 2025; Patricios et al. 2023).

Although a constellation of neurobiological processes may be impacted by SRC, an alteration in cerebral blood flow (CBF) and its impact on long‐term health may be particularly relevant (Baker et al. 2022). CBF—defined as the volume of blood perfusing a given mass of brain tissue per unit time—is primarily driven by perfusion pressure, vascular resistance, and blood viscosity, and is regulated by metabolic demand and autoregulatory mechanisms (Fantini et al. 2016; Sweeney et al. 2018). Many of these processes are potentially affected by brain injury, resulting in differences in perfusion that are well documented during the acute and subacute phases of SRC recovery (Churchill et al. 2025; Di Battista et al. 2018; Len et al. 2011; Meier, Bellgowan, et al. 2015; Neill et al. 2024; Purkayastha et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2019; Wright et al. 2022).

While some evidence suggests that CBF often returns to baseline within 1 month following a single SRC (Churchill et al. 2025; Maugans et al. 2012; Meier, Bellgowan, et al. 2015), a growing body of research indicates that this system may be susceptible to cumulative insults and incomplete recovery in the context of recurrent SRC, though results have been mixed and based on relatively small samples. Churchill, Hutchison, Richards, et al. (2017) compared 21 university athletes with concussion history (CHx) at least 9 months post‐injury to matched peers without CHx and reported patterns of both hypoperfusion and hyperperfusion. Specifically, athletes with CHx exhibited reduced CBF in superior frontal cortical regions, alongside increased perfusion in the right hippocampus and posterior cingulate. Furthermore, a higher number of prior concussions was linked to hypoperfusion in the frontal and temporal regions. In contrast, Brooks et al. (2019) investigating 37 adolescents with CHx relative to 18 orthopedic controls identified a pattern of anterior hyperperfusion and posterior hypoperfusion. Notably, however, the only region showing a statistically significant association between CHx and CBF was the left inferior frontal gyrus (orbital), which demonstrated hyperperfusion.

While these earlier studies offer valuable insights, relatively little attention has been given to potential sex differences in the relationship between CHx and CBF, which may help explain the observed subtle disparities in neurobehavioral and neuroimaging outcomes between males and females during the early chronic phase of injury (Andersson et al. 2024; Brett et al. 2022; Gupte et al. 2019; McGroarty et al. 2020; Meier et al. 2025; Turner et al. 2024). Two recent case–control studies comparing athletes with and without CHx reported persistent global and regional CBF alterations, with sex emerging as a key moderator. In a study of 12 athletes with persisting symptoms attributed to recurrent SRC, all of whom were at least 6 months post‐injury, Vedung et al. (2022) found no global CBF differences compared to 12 healthy age‐ and sex‐matched controls; yet, stratified analyses revealed that female athletes exhibited reduced CBF across 16 gray matter regions, with particularly pronounced effects in the right temporal lobe and right thalamus. Additionally, female athletes with recurrent SRC exhibited diminished CBF to the right frontal lobe and right thalamus in comparison to male athletes with recurrent SRC. On the other hand, Hamer et al. (2020), studying 122 athletes regardless of symptom status, reported that only male athletes with CHx displayed altered CBF compared to controls. However, among athletes with multiple prior concussions, females demonstrated lower CBF in the left anterior cingulum, right cerebellum, and right middle occipital gyrus compared to those with a single concussion, while males showed no such differences.

The cited moderating effect of biological sex on the relationship between CHx and CBF measures in these studies lends to the continuing debate into whether the neurobiological responses to SRC/mTBI and subsequent acute and chronic clinical outcomes differ by sex at the population level (Caccese et al. 2024; Covassin et al. 2018; Hannah et al. 2021). Still, it remains unclear whether the observed effects are attributable to differences in baseline characteristics—such as the presence of SRC‐related persisting symptoms in Vedung et al. (2022)—or whether similar CBF alterations can emerge resulting from cumulative effects of repeated head injuries, even in otherwise healthy athletes with a history of recurrent SRC/mTBI. Given that cerebrovascular irregularities (e.g., hypoperfusion) can precede neurocognitive decline and neurodegenerative disorders (Sweeney et al. 2018), it remains imperative we investigate whether cumulative concussion can lead to chronic abnormalities in perfusion as these may represent a mechanistic pathway linking recurrent SRC to long‐term adverse outcomes reported in some athletes (Brett et al. 2025; Iverson et al. 2023).

Hence, the present study aimed to investigate the association between CHx and CBF in a cohort of collegiate‐aged athletes without recent injury and examine whether this relationship was moderated by sex. Based on prior research, we hypothesized that (1) CHx would be associated with global and regional hypoperfusion in both male and female athletes and that (2) these associations would be particularly pronounced in female athletes with CHx (Hamer et al. 2020; Vedung et al. 2022). In addition, we explored whether cognitive or affective functioning showed overlapping associations with CHx or CBF. Finally, given growing interest concerning how both short‐ and long‐term outcomes in females may be influenced by hormonal contraceptive use (Chen et al. 2021; Goeckner et al. 2024; Heller et al. 2025; Wunderle et al. 2014), additional exploratory analyses sought to characterize the modifying effect of oral contraceptive use and pill formulation on the CHx–CBF relationship in female athletes.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Healthy, collegiate‐aged athletes from the community were recruited to enroll in a study designed to investigate the long‐term effects of prior concussion and contact sport exposure (Project ChronEx). Eligible participants were aged 18–26 years and had participated in organized sports within the past year. Exclusion criteria included self‐reported psychiatric, neurodevelopmental, neuroendocrine, severe neurological, or autoimmune disorders; a history of moderate‐to‐severe TBI; recent (within 6 months) or unresolved concussion; alcohol or substance misuse based on the CAGE‐AID questionnaire; current use of neuroactive medication; contraindications to study procedures; or any injury or illness that would interfere with participation. Of the 1217 individuals initially contacted, 204 athletes competing across 13 unique sports were included in the present study. Among them, 101 (49.5%) reported no CHx, 41 (20.1%) reported one previous concussion, and 62 (30.4%) reported multiple previous concussions (maximum = 8). American football was most often reported as male participants' primary sport (44.1% of males), while soccer was the most prevalent among female participants (37.7% of females). Primary Exposure: Concussion History and Exposure to Contact Sports

Self‐reported concussion histories are subject to recall and interpretation biases, while limiting reports to medically diagnosed SRCs likely underestimates true incidence due to under recognition and psychosocial barriers to reporting (Ferdinand Pennock et al. 2023; Meier, Brummel, et al. 2015). To mitigate these limitations, our team employed semi‐structured interviews designed to extract detailed accounts of head and neck injuries. A comprehensive description of our method has been previously described (see Meier et al. 2021). In the present study, we utilized the definition of concussion aligned with criteria from the American Congress of Rehabilitation Medicine (ACRM 1993), requiring clinical indicators of concussion, such as loss of consciousness, post‐traumatic amnesia, or altered mental status. In addition, a semi‐structured interview was used to quantify the total number of years each participant participated in any sport associated with exposure to repetitive head impacts (American football, soccer, rugby, combat sports, wrestling, men's ice hockey, and men's lacrosse).

Demographic and Clinical Measures

We administered a short survey to collect relevant demographic data. Waist‐to‐hip ratio (WHR) was calculated as a proxy for adiposity using the participant's waist circumference and dividing it by his or her hip circumference. Socioeconomic status (SES) was estimated using the modified Four Factor Index of Social Status (Hollingshead 1975). The iPad version of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Toolbox Cognition Battery was used to assess cognitive performance. This battery includes a wide range of tasks designed to assess fluid cognitive abilities—such as cognitive flexibility, attention, inhibitory control, episodic and working memory, and processing speed—as well as crystallized abilities including picture vocabulary and single word reading. Uncorrected standard scores for the fluid and crystallized composite measures were used in the current work. Participant contraceptive use was assessed via self‐report, including type, brand, dosage, and duration of use. Hormone activity estimates were then derived from oral contraceptive users' pill formulation following Dickey (2021), incorporating variation in the pharmacological potency of synthetic progestins and the corresponding ethinyl estradiol dose within each formulation.

Participants also completed a series of validated self‐report surveys to measure current affective symptom burden. Depressive symptoms were measured using the Beck Depression Inventory‐II (BDI‐II; Beck et al. 1996), a 21‐item questionnaire rated on a four‐point Likert scale, yielding cumulative scores ranging from 0 to 63. Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI; Beck et al. 1988), which similarly consists of 21 items rated on a four‐point Likert scale, with cumulative scores ranging from 0 to 63.

MRI Acquisition and Processing

MRI data were collected using a GE SIGNA Premier 3 T scanner (GE HealthCare, Waukesha, WI, USA) equipped with a 32‐channel receive‐only head coil (Nova Medical Inc., Wilmington, MA, USA). High‐resolution T1‐weighted (T1w) anatomical images were collected using a 3D magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo sequence (MPRAGE) with the following parameters: 240 × 240‐mm field of view, 240 × 240 image matrix, 188 axial slices (1‐mm3 isotropic voxels), 2279‐2356 ms repetition time (TR), 2.84–2.97 ms echo time (TE), 900 ms inversion time, and 8o flip angle. CBF data were collected using the GE 3D pseudo‐continuous arterial spin labeling sequence with the following parameters: TR = 4636‐4830 ms, TE = 10.56–52.80 ms, post‐label delay = 1525 ms, flip angle = 111 degrees, FOV = 240, image matrix 128 × 128, spiral readout of 8 arms and 512 samples, excitations = 3, label duration = 1450 ms, in‐plane resolution = 1.875 × 1.875 mm, slice thickness = 4 mm, number of slices = 36, scan length ~4.5 min. Participants were instructed to passively fixate on a crosshair and think of nothing in particular during CBF data collection.

Data were processed using the ExploreASL pipeline (v1.11.0) within MATLAB (v2024b; MathWorks Inc., Natick, Massachusetts, USA), which is described thoroughly elsewhere (see Mutsaerts et al. 2020). Briefly, CAT12 (v1615; Gaser et al. 2024) and SPM12 (v7219; Ashburner 2012; Flandin and Friston 2008) were used to process and segment T1w scans into gray matter (GM), white matter, and CSF to generate partial volume (PV) images. Geodesic‐shooting normalized these tissue maps and the T1w image to the MNI template, and the resulting deformation fields and modulated PV maps were saved in standard space. M0 images were intensity masked, eroded, smoothed and scaled to mean GM. CBF was quantified using the single‐compartment model (labeling eff. = 0.85 × 0.75; λ = 0.9; blood T1 = 1650 ms) and warped to MNI template space. CBF data were partial‐volume‐corrected using linear regression within extracted regions‐of‐interest (ROI) with PV images that were smoothed and down sampled to ASL resolution. All group analyses were restricted to an analysis mask accounting for field of view and vascular and susceptibility artifacts. Bilateral partial‐volume‐corrected CBF (CBFpvc) measured in mL/100 g/min and spatial coefficient‐of‐variation (sCoV) ROI statistics were extracted based on Harvard–Oxford cortical and subcortical atlases (Desikan et al. 2006) using the following equation:

This measure has been shown to approximate arterial transit time in single post‐label delay paradigms (Mutsaerts et al. 2017). All outputs underwent visual inspection in AFNI v25.1.08 and quantitative evaluation in R using measures derived from ExploreASL/CAT12 reports.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R v4.4.1 (R Core Team 2025) using the car (Fox and Weisberg 2019), emmeans (Lenth et al. 2025), and performance (Lüdecke et al. 2021) packages. Visualizations were created using ggplot2 (Wickham 2016), ggseg, and ggseg3d (Mowinckel and Vidal‐Piñeiro 2020). Continuous data are presented as means ± standard deviations or medians alongside quartiles, depending on distribution, while categorical data are reported as frequencies and percentages. To examine the relationship between CHx and global CBFpvc or sCoV in gray matter, we generated separate multiple linear regression models for each outcome. CHx was included as the primary explanatory variable, while mean‐centered age, WHR, SES, and years of exposure to contact sports were included as a priori covariates. Based on potential qualitative sex differences in CBF functioning (Beltz et al. 2019; Kawano et al. 2024) and neurobiological response to brain injury (Churchill et al. 2021; Gupte et al. 2019; McGroarty et al. 2020), models included CHx × Sex interaction terms:

When indicated, simple main effects of CHx on CBFpvc by sex and their difference (interaction effect) are reported as unstandardized betas (B) with 95% confidence intervals. Post hoc ROI analyses were conducted across cortical and subcortical ROIs to characterize associations of CHx with CBFpvc and sCoV in male and female athletes separately as indicated by interaction effects in the global models. We deployed the Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) procedure to maintain the false discovery rate (FDR) at 5% across ROIs. Assumptions of linearity, normally distributed residuals, and homoscedasticity were met through visual assessment of posterior predictive, residual‐fitted and Q–Q plots.

We also conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of our results. Global and regional analyses excluding participants presenting with any extreme outliers across all quality control and CBFpvc metrics (±5 SDs from the sample mean) were performed. Then, to assess potential nonlinearity in the relationship between CHx and CBFpvc, we stratified CHx into a three‐level ordinal variable: no CHx (no exposure), one prior concussion (single exposure), and two or more concussions (recurrent exposure). A multiple linear regression model was fitted with global GM CBFpvc as the outcome, including the CHx × Sex interaction term along with the same covariates. The interaction was evaluated using an F‐test based on Type II sums of squares. Sex‐stratified analyses and pairwise contrasts of estimated marginal means were performed only if the interaction reached statistical significance. Next, to determine whether cognitive and affective functioning share variance with CHx or CBFpvc/sCoV, we conducted exploratory Spearman (ρ) correlations FDR‐corrected for multiple comparisons. This approach enabled us to assess potential overlapping relationships and to determine whether CBF alterations are present even in the absence of differences in clinical symptomatology.

Lastly, a series of exploratory analyses were conducted to assess whether oral contraceptive use (OC) or hormone activity modulated the relationship between CHx and CBFpvc among females. Differences in mean global GM CBFpvc between oral contraceptive users and those not using hormonal contraception were tested using an independent‐samples t‐test without additional covariates. Multiple linear regression models examined CHx × OC interactions and CHx interactions with estimated sex hormone pill‐derived activity (estrogenic, progestational, and androgenic). Models were designed to assess the moderating effect of oral contraceptive use and hormone activity on the relationship between CHx and CBFpvc, respectively. Activity levels were natural log‐transformed and because these analyses were exploratory, p‐values were not corrected for multiple comparison.

Results

Concussion History—Cerebral Blood Flow Association by Sex

There was a significant prior concussion‐by‐sex interaction on global GM CBFpvc when controlling for covariates, B CHx×Female = −3.2 mL/100 g/min [−5.8, −0.6], p = 0.017. Follow‐up analyses showed that this was driven by a significant negative association between CHx and global GM CBFpvc among female athletes, B CHx = −2.9 mL/100 g/min [95% CI −5.1, −0.6], p = 0.012, whereas no evidence of such was found among male athletes, B CHx = 0.3 mL/100 g/min [95% CI −1.1, 1.8], p = 0.64 (Figure 2). In contrast, there was no main effect of CHx when averaged by sex (B CHx = −0.02, p = 0.17) nor a CHx‐by‐sex interaction (B CHx×Female = −0.01, p = 0.79) for global GM sCoV.

Sensitivity Analyses

Across all 15 quality control GM‐related metrics and 57 ROI CBFpvc measures, only one participant exhibited values exceeding 5 SDs on any measure. Excluding this participant (due to excessive T1‐weighted asymmetry) did not meaningfully alter the results (Table S2). Stratification of CHx into groups (0, 1, 2+) including the same covariates yielded a significant prior concussion‐by‐sex interaction, F(2, 194) = 3.096, p = 0.047. Subsequent split‐by‐sex pairwise comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences between groups, although the 0 versus 2+ contrast approached significance for global GM CBFpvc in female athletes (B = −8.37 mL/100 g/min [95% CI −16.90, 0.17], p = 0.055; Table S3).

Conducting Spearman correlations among our exposure, outcomes, and other potential confounders, we identified several significant correlations in female athletes. CHx was significantly positively correlated with depression (p adj = 0.017) and anxiety (p adj = 0.02). However, no additional measures were significantly associated with both our exposure (CHx) and outcomes (CBFpvc/sCoV). Moderation of Oral Contraceptive Use on Female Cerebral Blood Flow

Comparing global GM CBFpvc, oral contraceptive users (n = 30) exhibited 6.5 mL/100 g/min [95% CI −13.7, 0.6] less perfusion than those not using hormonal contraception (n = 38), though this was not significant, p = 0.072. When including a CHx × OC interaction in a multiple linear regression model, OC use did not modify the relationship between CHx and global CBFpvc, B OC×CHx = −2.52 [95% CI −9.79, 4.76], p = 0.49. In a subset of oral contraceptive users where we were able to estimate pill‐derived estrogenic, progestational, and androgenic activity (n = 25), none of our models including the Hormone × CHx interactions reached statistical significance (ps = 0.10–0.37; Table S5).

Discussion

In a healthy sample of active collegiate‐aged athletes, we measured gray matter CBFpvc and sCoV as a function of concussion history, controlling for relevant confounding factors. Our results suggest sex‐specific associations of cumulative concussion with CBFpvc. Global and regional GM CBFpvc in female athletes decreased subtly with increasing concussion history, whereas no associations between concussion history and CBFpvc were observed in male athletes. Regional effects in female athletes were most apparent in frontotemporal regions, and these findings were independent of age, socioeconomic status, waist‐hip ratio, and years of exposure to contact sports. Notably, cognitive performance and current affective symptomology were not associated with these changes. Finally, exploratory analyses demonstrated that oral contraceptive use did not significantly moderate the relationship of concussion history with CBFpvc measures in female athletes.

These results expand upon extant work reporting widespread TBI‐related hypoperfusion following a single‐event concussion acutely and additional emerging data that suggest that such effects may persist or reemerge in the early chronic phase of injury (Churchill, Hutchison, Richards, et al. 2017; Churchill et al. 2025; Ware et al. 2020). Specifically, our study extends this literature by revealing that the relationship between cumulative concussion and CBFpvc appears to be sex‐specific, as previously described (Hamer et al. 2020; Vedung et al. 2022). Vedung et al. (2022) reported widespread hypoperfusion in female athletes with a history of recurrent concussion but not males, while Hamer et al. (2020) found greater posterior hypoperfusion in female athletes with multiple concussions versus a single concussion. The slight discrepancy with Hamer et al. may stem from how prior concussions were operationalized/collected or potential analytic differences (Hamer et al. compared one vs. multiple concussions). In our sensitivity analyses, we did not observe global CBFpvc differences when comparing female athletes with one versus multiple concussions when categorizing athletes into groups based on concussion history. Because treating concussion history as a continuous variable uses all available information, our primary analyses should allow the detection of subtle, dose‐dependent influences of prior concussion on CBFpvc.

Associations were observed globally, though regions surviving correction were predominately located in frontotemporal areas, reflecting the known vulnerability of this region to primary injury (Bigler 2007; Isokuortti et al. 2018). These regions are known to serve as a key hub in networks supporting cognition, executive function, and emotion regulation (McAllister 2011). Reduced CBF to these areas post‐SRC has been associated with poor cognitive performance acutely (Churchill, Hutchison, Graham, and Schweizer 2017). Further, structural and functional changes in these regions among individuals with greater history of repetitive neurotrauma have been reported to correlate with cognitive and neurobehavioral symptoms later in life (Brett et al. 2022, 2025). In our relatively young, healthy sample, however, we did not observe robust associations between CBFpvc and cognitive or affective measures.

Overall, these findings add to evidence that recurrent concussion may be associated with latent neurovascular and metabolic changes in addition to previously described structural and behavioral changes specifically in otherwise healthy female athletes (Meier et al. 2021, 2025; Turner et al. 2024). Effect sizes were small, though consistent with our sensitivity analyses, not associated with neurocognitive performance or affective symptoms. In our primary analyses, the largest regional hypoperfusion per additional concussion was observed in the frontal pole among female athletes. This reduction corresponded to an average estimated drop of −6.4 mL/100 mL/min—a −5.7% change from the sample mean—commensurate with half the estimated decrease in global CBF observed after acute caffeine intake (Clement et al. 2018). This underscores the subtlety of these effects while emphasizing that cumulative concussion may be impactful.

Our understanding of the mechanisms driving these neurobiological and behavioral sex‐differences in concussion‐related outcomes is still evolving, but numerous biological factors have been identified. A frequently cited explanation involves reduced relative cervical girth and neuromuscular strength in female athletes as biomechanical modifiers of head acceleration and injury severity; although, this seems to only explain a small fraction of the variance seen in TBI outcomes (Cooney et al. 2022). Another factor relates to differences in brain anatomy. Over and above the overall sex differences seen in brain volumetrics (V male > V female), a range of micro‐ and macrostructural distinctions may have important implications for TBI (DeCasien et al. 2022). For example, Dollé et al. (2018) demonstrated that female axon diameters were significantly smaller and contained fewer microtubules than their male counterparts, which was associated with increased susceptibility to mechanical injury. Another emerging area of study relates to sex hormones and their paradoxical relationship with TBI. Not only do these hormones shape baseline brain morphology and physiology (Brønnick et al. 2020), some research suggests that endogenous and exogenous sex hormones may confer a degree of protection against TBI (Wunderle et al. 2014). However, TBI can also dysregulate the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis that regulates hormone production—a disruption frequently manifested in post‐injury menstrual irregularities (Heller et al. 2025). Previously, our group demonstrated that progesterone and estradiol are acutely altered in female athletes' post‐SRC and that these hormone changes are associated with exacerbated acute sequelae (Goeckner et al. 2024). Our present analyses were inconclusive regarding the moderating effects of oral contraceptive use and estimated pill‐derived sex hormone activity on the concussion history–CBFpvc relationship. Although the concussion history‐by‐progesterone interaction did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.10), the direction of the association suggests progestational activity may influence the concussion history–CBFpvc relationship. Given the limited sample size, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and revisited in future work.

We emphasize that these modest CBFpvc alterations may not manifest as clinically deleterious neurological sequelae in the early chronic phase. However, they may represent an early modest risk factor that—when combined with other comorbidities (e.g., cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndromes) and genetic vulnerabilities—could elicit the development of adverse neurobiological or behavioral outcomes later in life (Sweeney et al. 2018). It is well‐established that females over the age of 60 years are disproportionately affected by dementias—both in terms of more rapid disease progression and increased pathological burden relative to males (Ferretti et al. 2018). In this context, modifiable cardiovascular and cerebrovascular factors (e.g., hypoperfusion) may become especially relevant as potential female‐specific targets for early detection and intervention in neurodegenerative disease processes associated with recurrent concussion (Baker et al. 2022).

Strengths and Limitations

Our study's validity is bolstered by its extensive exclusion criteria, use of comprehensive interviewing to assess number of prior concussions, and large sample size including both male and female athletes relative to similar studies. Regardless, appreciation for the study's limitations and their influence on our interpretations is crucial. First and foremost, the cross‐sectional design precludes any inference about the causal influence of number of concussions on CBFpvc. It is imperative future studies employing longitudinal designs with extended follow‐up periods after recurrent concussions be conducted to confirm and extend our findings. Although the present study applied extensive exclusion criteria, several factors that can influence CBF were not fully controlled for, such as acute use or abstinence from alcohol, caffeine, and nicotine (Clement et al. 2018). Furthermore, sCoV was used as a proxy for arterial transit time (Mutsaerts et al. 2017), but sequences incorporating multiple post‐labeling delays would provide more accurate estimates and should be prioritized in future work. Finally, the current work was limited to a relatively homogenous group of collegiate‐aged athletes, with relatively few participants with an extensive concussion history, and it is unclear how well results would generalize to more diverse populations or individuals with more prior concussions.

Conclusions

Our results suggest that reductions in cerebral blood flow associated with repeated concussion, most strongly observed in frontotemporal regions, can be observed in female athletes in early adulthood. These findings add to the nascent literature indicating that sex is an important moderator of the chronic and cumulative effects of concussion on brain health, and they further underscore the importance of effective injury‐prevention strategies. Future longitudinal research is needed to determine how chronic perfusion differences observed in young, active athletes may interact with other comorbidities to create biological conditions that may contribute to poorer long‐term clinical outcomes.
