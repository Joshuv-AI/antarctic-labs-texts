Investigation of secondary hyperparathyroidism and its contributing factors in hemodialysis patients: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Mahdieh Jamshidi, Golamreza Bazmandegan, Zahra Kamiab, Mohammad Rasool Dehghani Firouzabadi, Mohammad Ali Zakeri, Alaa Hamza Hermis, Saadya Hadi Humade, Mahmood Kahnooji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Hemodialysis may lead to changes in serum levels of important parameters such as calcium and phosphorus, which can also affect the level of parathyroid hormone. The present study aimed to determine the prevalence and some causes of secondary hyperparathyroidism (SHPT) in hemodialysis patients.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The population of this descriptive-cross-sectional study included 60 hemodialysis patients admitted to the hospital in the southeast of Iran, all of whom were enrolled in the study through convenience sampling. Sociodemographic characteristics, hematologic and biochemical indices, and serum parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels were examined. A PTH hormone cut-off level of 300 picograms per deciliter was considered. Data were analyzed using an independent t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, Chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test at a significance level of less than 0.05.RESULT:The mean age of the participants was 53.16 ± 8.60 years. Among these, 34 (56.7%) were diagnosed with SHPT, and 58 (96.7%) had underlying diseases. Additionally, 17 (28.3%) had abnormal calcium levels, 40 (66.7%) had abnormal phosphorus levels, four (6.7%) had abnormal alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and 19 (31.7%) had abnormal Ca × P. The mean levels of PTH (P < 0.001), median ALP (P = 0.002), and Ca × P (P = 0.026) were significantly higher in individuals with SHPT compared to those without.CONCLUSION:Based on the results of this study, the higher levels of ALP in hemodialysis patients with SHPT suggest that regular monitoring of laboratory tests, particularly alkaline phosphatase, can be beneficial in the follow-up of these patients.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive condition affecting more than 10% of the global population (over 800 million individuals). CKD is more prevalent among the elderly, women, racial minorities, and individuals with diabetes and hypertension.[1] Recognized as a global health issue and a major non-communicable disease,[2] CKD is associated with adverse clinical and economic outcomes, including an increased risk of mortality, cardiovascular diseases, and high healthcare costs.[3] Most CKD patients suffer from diabetes, hypertension, and/or cardiovascular diseases, which complicates their management due to the interrelationship among these chronic conditions.[45]

Hemodialysis is one of the three treatment modalities for kidney failure.[6] Long-term complications of hemodialysis include amyloidosis, neuropathy, and various forms of cardiac disease, including left ventricular hypertrophy.[7] Thus, CKD and subsequent hemodialysis are associated with significant complications.[89]

SHPT and skeletal involvement are among the major challenges in CKD.[10] In CKD, parathyroid gland function compensatorily increases due to several factors, including reduced phosphate excretion by the nonfunctioning kidneys, impaired calcium absorption from the intestines (resulting from inadequate conversion of vitamin D to its active form in the kidneys), bone resistance to parathyroid hormone, increased serum calcitonin levels, and the use of aluminum-containing compounds in these patients. As kidney function declines, disturbances in various body systems, including the endocrine glands, emerge, highlighting the importance of recognizing these changes.[11] When the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) falls below 50%, PTH levels begin to rise. This increase is primarily driven by a deficiency of active vitamin D, decreased ionized calcium, and elevated serum phosphate levels. Therefore, SHPT is a compensatory response to electrolyte imbalances and hormonal dysregulation. Elevated PTH levels lead to disturbances in bone formation, anemia, calcium deposition in blood vessels and skin, muscle weakness, and pruritus. Consequently, controlling PTH levels and managing hyperparathyroidism in hemodialysis patients are crucial for preventing calcium deposition and its associated complications.[12]

This study provides new insights into the prevalence and contributing factors of SHPT in hemodialysis patients, particularly in understudied populations and regions that have received less attention. While previous research has explored SHPT in CKD patients,[131415] our study specifically focuses on severe hemodialysis-related disorders. The interplay between biochemical markers (e.g. calcium, phosphate, and vitamin D levels) and clinical outcomes offers a comprehensive analysis that can inform targeted interventions.

Therefore, understanding the complications of chronic kidney failure and its treatment methods, including hemodialysis, is essential for prevention, control, and improved management. This understanding should be a priority for every healthcare system. Hence, the present study was conducted with the following specific objectives: (a) to examine the association of demographic variables with SHPT, (b) to investigate the association of clinical characteristics with SHPT, and (c) to explore the association of hematological indices with SHPT.

Materials and Methods

Study design and settings

This descriptive-cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the prevalence and contributing factors of SHPT in hemodialysis patients (May–September 2022). The inclusion criteria comprised all hemodialysis patients in the dialysis unit of Ali ibn Abi Talib Hospital in Rafsanjan who had a minimum of three months of dialysis history, complete medical records, and provided consent to participate in the study. A convenience sampling approach was used to recruit hemodialysis patients from a single center during the study period.

Study participants and sampling

The study population included 80 hemodialysis patients in the dialysis unit of Ali ibn Abi Talib Hospital in Rafsanjan, all of whom were enrolled through census sampling. After reviewing the inclusion criteria and explaining the study objectives to the patients, 60 patients agreed to participate in the study.

Data collection tools and techniques

Demographic and Clinical Forms: A designed checklist containing demographic and clinical information including age, gender, weight, underlying diseases (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular diseases), duration of dialysis, and number of dialysis sessions per week was used.

Hematological Indexes: Hematological indices included tests for PTH levels, calcium, phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), creatinine, and hemoglobin levels. It is noteworthy that these hematological indices were routinely measured on a monthly basis, and the most recent test results were extracted from the patients’ medical records for review. The target PTH level in hemodialysis patients was set between 150–300 pg/mL. Therefore, a PTH cut-off level of 300 pg/mL was established, and patients with PTH levels exceeding this threshold were included in the study as individuals with SHPT, following consultation and diagnosis by an internal medicine specialist. Additionally, the target calcium level in hemodialysis patients was 4.8–5.9 mg/dL, the target phosphorus level was 3–5 mg/dL, the target ALP level was 5–13 mg/dL, the target hemoglobin level was above 11 g/dL, and the target Ca*P ratio was above 55.

The demographic and clinical data of patients who met the inclusion criteria and consented to participate in the study were reviewed. Data collection took place from August to October 2021. To examine hematological indices, a 5 cc blood sample (one clotted sample and one citrated sample) was collected from patients by one of the nurses in the dialysis unit. The obtained samples were sent to the laboratory of Ali ibn Abi Talib Hospital in Rafsanjan for hematological and PTH index analysis using immunoassay methods.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Rafsanjan University of Medical Sciences (IR.RUMS.REC.1400.132), the project ID: 98230. The research was carried out following the guidelines of the ethics committee listed in the ethics statement. Before commencing the study, an informed consent form was designed, and hemodialysis patients who met the inclusion criteria were asked to sign this form.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS software version. Results for quantitative variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and for qualitative variables, they were reported as frequency (percentage). Independent t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used to compare the means of quantitative variables in two groups under study, while Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were utilized to compare the frequencies of qualitative variables. A significance level of 0.05 was considered in all tests.

Results

Out of the 60 hemodialysis patients studied, 29 (48.3%) were male, and 31 (51.7%) were female. The mean age of the participants was 60.85 ± 60.85 years. The mean duration of illness among the participants was 2.37 ± 1.85 years. Thirty-four patients (56.7%) were diagnosed with SHPT, among whom 18 (52.9%) were male and 16 (47.1%) were female.

There was no significant difference in terms of gender distribution (P = 0.412) between patients with and without SHPT. Additionally, there was no significant difference in mean age (P = 0.112), weight (P = 0.153), duration of illness (P = 0.365), and duration of dialysis (P = 0.946) between patients with and without SHPT. However, the median ALP level was significantly higher in patients with hyperparathyroidism compared to those without it (P = 0.002). Furthermore, the mean ca*P level was significantly lower in patients with hyperparathyroidism compared to those without it (P = 0.026) [Table 1 and Figure 1].

Discussion

The present study aimed to determine the prevalence and potential causes of SHPT in hemodialysis patients. Out of 60 dialysis patients, 34 (56.7%) were diagnosed with SHPT. In this study, no significant difference was found in gender distribution between individuals with and without SHPT, which is consistent with the findings of Bures et al.[16] and Levy et al.[17] However, Xu et al. reported a significant association between gender and PTH levels in adults with CKD. Gender has been shown to influence the pathophysiology and treatment of certain diseases, including renal diseases and hormonal disorders such as hyperparathyroidism.[16] Women with end-stage kidney disease have been reported to exhibit higher PTH levels compared to men. The discrepancy between the results of the current study and previous research may be attributed to the sample size.

The findings of the present study also indicated no significant association between age and SHPT, aligning with the results of Levy et al.,[17] Bures et al.,[16] and Fitzpatrick et al.[18] In contrast, Xu et al.[14] reported that younger individuals exhibited higher PTH levels. Although PTH concentrations appear to increase with age, no significant relationship was observed in this study after multivariate adjustment. A large US study found higher PTH levels in individuals aged <60 years compared to those aged <30 years, independent of renal function.[19] Similarly, Ho et al.[20] demonstrated a significant association between age and PTH levels, which contrasts with our findings. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in demographic characteristics and geographical factors.

Females, particularly postmenopausal women, are at a higher risk for SHPT due to estrogen deficiency and its effects on bone and mineral metabolism.[17] Older adults, especially those with CKD, are also at an increased risk of SHPT due to age-related declines in kidney function, calcium absorption, and vitamin D levels.[21] Understanding these associations is critical for the early identification, prevention, and management of SHPT in at-risk populations. Appropriate interventions, such as calcium and vitamin D supplementation and hormone replacement therapy, may help mitigate the risk and progression of SHPT in older adults and postmenopausal women.

Furthermore, the study found no significant associations between underlying diseases—such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases—and the presence of SHPT. This finding is consistent with Bures et al.[16] Similarly, Xu et al. (2021) found no significant association between hyperparathyroidism and conditions such as hypertension in CKD patients. In a study investigating the prevalence of SHPT among Nigerians with CKD, no significant correlation was observed between comorbid diseases and serum PTH levels,[22] aligning with the findings of the present study. However, Genctoy et al.[23] reported a significant association between the severity of hypertension and PTH levels in hemodialysis patients. These inconsistencies may be attributed to differences in population characteristics and geographic factors. Although SHPT has been reported as a major component of bone mineral metabolism disorders in CKD and is closely linked to cardiovascular disease, fractures, and an increased risk of mortality in end-stage renal disease (ESRD),[24] no association between underlying diseases and SHPT was observed in the present study. Understanding these associations is crucial for the early diagnosis, prevention, and management of SHPT.

The findings of this study revealed that the mean ALP level among participants was 310 U/L, with the median ALP level being significantly higher in individuals with SHPT compared to those without the condition. This observation aligns with the results reported by Zou et al.[25] (2021), who also identified elevated ALP levels as a key marker of SHPT. Similarly, Gimba et al.[22] (2018) demonstrated a significant association between SHPT and increased ALP levels, further supporting the role of ALP as an indicator of bone turnover and disease activity in SHPT.

In addition to ALP, other hematological and biochemical abnormalities are commonly observed in SHPT. For instance, Amjad et al.[26] (2024) reported that patients with hyperparathyroidism exhibited significantly lower calcium and hemoglobin levels, alongside elevated phosphate levels. These findings highlight the complex interplay of mineral and bone metabolism in SHPT. Furthermore, Wojtowicz et al.[27] (2024) observed significant reductions in baseline PTH, calcium, and phosphate levels over a two-year follow-up period, emphasizing the dynamic nature of these biomarkers in response to treatment.

The pathophysiology of SHPT involves the overproduction of PTH by the parathyroid glands, often driven by CKD, vitamin D deficiency, or imbalances in calcium and phosphate metabolism. Persistently elevated PTH levels can lead to adverse outcomes such as bone resorption, vascular calcification, and osteodystrophy.[28] Elevated ALP levels in SHPT are indicative of increased bone turnover, which can result in bone loss, fractures, and other skeletal complications. Similarly, the Ca × P serves as a critical marker of mineral balance, with elevated Ca × P levels increasing the risk of soft tissue and vascular calcification, particularly in CKD patients.[29]

The marked elevation of PTH, ALP, and Ca × P in individuals with SHPT underscores the intricate relationship between mineral and bone metabolism in this condition. These biomarkers are not only essential for diagnosing SHPT but also play a crucial role in monitoring disease progression and guiding therapeutic interventions. Regular evaluation of these parameters is vital for preventing complications such as bone disease and cardiovascular calcification. Despite advances in understanding SHPT, its management remains challenging due to its multifactorial nature and gaps in existing clinical guidelines.[30] However, clinical standards for the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of SHPT vary significantly across countries, highlighting the need for more unified and evidence-based guidelines.[31] Future research should focus on addressing these disparities and developing targeted therapies to improve outcomes for patients with SHPT.

Limitations and recommendations

Limitations of this study include the small sample size due to the limited number of dialysis patients in the hospital, as well as existing shortcomings in the files. Therefore, caution should be exercised in generalizing the results. It is recommended that future studies with larger sample sizes be conducted in a multicenter manner.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, it is recommended that health policies incorporate the measurement of PTH and ALP levels into routine screening programs for patients with CKD in stages 3 and 4. Additionally, efforts should be made to raise awareness among physicians and healthcare professionals regarding the importance of early diagnosis of SHPT and its potential consequences. Furthermore, the development of standardized treatment protocols for managing PTH, calcium, and phosphate levels in CKD patients should be prioritized. Equally important is enhancing patient awareness of the necessity of regular follow-up tests and adherence to prescribed treatment regimens. These measures should be key areas of focus for healthcare administrators and policymakers to improve patient outcomes and prevent complications associated with SHPT.
