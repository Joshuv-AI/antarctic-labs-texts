In spine surgery, compression is not an issue and decompression is not the treatment
Authors: Atul Goel
Published: 2024

Identification of site and extent of neural compression and neural deformation on imaging and decompression of the “compressed” neural structures by resection of the offending compressor or making space to accommodate the intruder forms the basis of spine surgery. Neural compression by tumors and related space-occupying lesions are not included in the present discussion.

Radiographic imaging has progressed and improved over the years. Plain radiographs formed the backbone of investigations for several decades. Identification of spinal deformation, reduction in the disc space height, identification of osteophytes, abnormal bone formation and fusion, reduction in the spinal and neural canal dimensions and alterations of spinal curvatures, and configuration on static and dynamic imaging helped in understanding of pathological basis of the clinical entity and in designing the treatment strategy. As disc space reduction was the prominent radiographic observation, the entire subject of spinal degeneration evolved on the understanding that reduction in the “water” content of the disc probably due to “old” age or its herniation into the spinal canal formed the basis of spinal degeneration. Such disc alterations formed a nodal point of pathogenesis, and it triggered a cascade of secondary spinal alterations that were grouped under the umbrella term of “spinal spondylosis.” Spinal instability was identified and confirmed on dynamic imaging.

Initially myelography and subsequently computer-based imaging of computerized tomography and magnetic resonance helped direct observation of neural tissues and neural compression. The primary issue in the understanding of the subject of spinal degeneration and its diagnosis and treatment was based on identification of the site of neural compression and nature of offender that directed the “decompressive” surgical procedure. Anterior compression meant anterior decompression was the general dictum. When the number of levels of neural compression was at more than 3–4 levels and when anterior surgery was wrought with potential for complications, posterior decompression was by laminectomy or laminoplasty was the selected mode of treatment. Surgical stabilization of the treated spinal segments was considered when multilevel bone/soft-tissue resection for decompression was considered to have a potential for delayed instability and deformity. Such a format of surgical treatment continues to enjoy universal acceptance.

In 2010, we identified for the first time in the literature the entity of “vertical” spinal instability.[12345] Standing on two legs, a position unique to humans, lays lifelong stress on the muscles that affect this posture. Majority of muscle bulk of the spine is located in the extensor compartment and their activity is focused at the facets that forms the fulcrum of all movements. Only thin muscle groups are located in relationship to the vertebral bodies and the intervertebral discs. Our articles identify disc spaces to “air” in the tires and muscles as pump and motor of the running car. We also observed that discs (and odontoid process) are the brain of the spinal movements and muscle pump is the heart of its activity.[678] Our articles identify intervertebral discs as “divine,” gives “birth” to the spine and a structure that can only be protective, reparative, or adaptive and can “never go wrong.”[678]

Weakness of the muscles of the spine related to their disuse, abuse, or injury leads to subtle listhesis of the facet of the rostral vertebra over the facets of caudal vertebra. Such listhesis is a chronic process and progressively worsens with the increasing weakness of the muscles. Our observation is that facetal listhesis or vertical spinal instability is the nodal point of genesis of the entire process of spinal degeneration.[59] Such facetal listhesis is difficult or impossible to identify even on advanced computer-based dynamic imaging at least in the initial stages and has therefore been entirely ignored in the understanding of the pathogenesis of spinal degeneration.[10] Chronic pain and muscle spasm that is naturally aimed at reduction in spinal movements and protection of the neural structures in the face of an unstable spinal segment is the basis of secondary spinal alterations that include bulging of the discs, buckling of the ligaments, reduction in the intervertebral disc dimensions, and osteophyte formation in the intervertebral spaces that include vertebral bodies and facets.[11] The eventual outcome of the secondary spinal alterations is reduction in the spinal and neural canal dimensions and spinal deformities. Essentially, it is the muscle weakness that leads to instability that is initially focused at the facets and is the primary or nodal clinical event and all the so-called pathological processes are secondary natural alterations that are protective or adaptive in their function.[9] Radiological evidence of new bone formation outside or inside the spinal canal and fusion is not an evidence of enhanced stability but is an evidence of pathological instability and showcases the attempts of Nature to protect the neural structures from instability-related neural damage. All the secondary alterations that are seemingly compressive in their function are in fact secondary and protective, indicate unstable spinal segment, and more importantly, are reversible following stabilization of the unstable parts of the spine. Clinical entities such as single- or multiple-level cervical spinal degenerative radiculopathy or myelopathy; single-level lumbar radiculopathy or lumbar canal stenosis; radiculopathy or myelopathy related to ossification of posterior longitudinal ligament; intervertebral disc herniation at cervical, dorsal, or lumbar spinal levels; Hirayama disease; and a host of other clinical entities present a neural compressive picture on imaging. For all these entities, “decompression” is currently the accepted norm of treatment. Our several articles on the subject discuss that identification of the unstable spinal segments and their solid stabilization forms a rational treatment option.[1213141516171819202122]

Craniovertebral junction is the hallmark of stability and mobility. Occipitoatlantal joint is the center of stability and atlantoaxial joint is the center of mobility. While atlantoaxial joint is the most mobile joint of the spine, it is also the most unstable joint of the spine. Our literature survey suggests that atlantoaxial instability is the most under-recognized and under-treated clinical entity. Unlike the subaxial facetal articulation, the atlantoaxial facets are horizontally placed. We discussed the clinical entity of central or axial atlantoaxial dislocation (CAAD). There can be atlantoaxial instability even in the absence of any pathological alteration of the atlantodental interval or any evidence of compression of the dural tube or neural structures. CAAD is usually a chronic and longstanding process.[2324] Several naturally protective neural and musculoskeletal alterations are the hallmarks of CAAD. These include short neck, torticollis, basilar invagination, platybasia, Chiari formation, syringomyelia, assimilation of atlas, C2–3 fusion, Klippel–Feil alteration, and a host of other alterations, which are the hallmarks of chronic atlantoaxial instability.[24] All these secondary alterations are naturally protective or adaptive, indicate the presence of atlantoaxial instability, and are reversible following atlantoaxial stabilization. These alterations appear to be neural compressive on imaging, and the contemporary treatment for all these alterations is neural decompression. Identification and treatment of atlantoaxial instability provides an opportunity for quick resolution of symptoms and a possibility of reversal of neurological deficits.

Chronic atlantoaxial and subaxial instability showcases the powers of Nature. A number of secondary alterations in both these clinical situations present a neural compressive picture on imaging. Even modern computer-based imaging can have difficulty in demonstrating instability in a number of cases. To formulate a rational treatment strategy, it is crucial to understand the point of origin of these alterations and stabilize the unstable segments. Any form of decompressive bone or soft-tissue resection in the presence of an unstable spine can only have negative clinical implications. More than treating the images, it is important to treat the cause. Presence of more than normal amount or in abnormal locations of bones, water, fat, disc or neural tissue are naturally protective or adaptive manifestations of chronic instability, their presence indicates spinal instability and following stabilization of the affected spinal segments there is a potential for reversal of these alterations.[25]
