Mohs Micrographic Surgery: A Narrative Review of Current Practices, Emerging Trends, and Case-Based Insights
Authors: Magdalena Maciejewska, Aleksandra Bętkowska, Joanna Czuwara, Lidia Rudnicka, Laura Banciu, Tiberiu Tebeica, Mihaela Leventer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMohs micrographic surgery (MMS) is a method used to treat skin cancers. It ensures a complete microscopic examination of the tumor margins with the conservation of surrounding tissue, leading to high cure rates and favorable cosmetic outcomes. In each Mohs stage, the excised specimen is frozen and sectioned horizontally, and its margins are evaluated for the presence of malignant cells. A variety of staining methods can be employed, including immunohistochemical. The process is repeated until all margins are negative. The most common indications for MMS are non-melanoma skin cancers, but it is increasingly utilized for other skin lesions, such as melanoma, lentigo maligna, and dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans. A growing body of evidence indicates significantly higher cure rates and a lower risk of local recurrence associated with MMS compared to other surgical modalities. Numerous modifications of the Mohs surgical technique have been developed to enhance the accuracy of margin control and to tackle specific challenges associated with various tumor types. Such alternative approaches include the “spaghetti technique,” the “slow Mohs” technique, and other variations such as the Munich method, the square method, the muffin technique, or the perimeter technique. Complications are rare and include infections, bleeding, or impaired wound healing. The increasing popularity of noninvasive imaging, digital pathology, and artificial intelligence models will likely enhance the efficiency of MMS in the future. Machine learning can predict diagnoses, recommend treatment options, predict responses to treatment and potential drug interactions, or help plan surgical procedures, enabling dermatologists to tailor therapies to individual patient characteristics. In addition to presenting the latest trends in Mohs micrographic surgery, this article includes a selection of clinical cases, with an overview of the treatment protocols followed by our institution, as well as data on recurrences based on our clinical experience.Keywords: Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, Melanoma, Mohs micrographic surgery, Skin cancer, Skin surgery

Key Summary Points

The study was undertaken to summarize the current evidence supporting the use of Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS) in treating various skin tumors and to explore how recent advancements, including technique modifications and the application of new technologies, may contribute to improved patient outcomes We synthesize the fundamental principles of MMS and recent developments, including modifications to the technique reported in the available literature, its expanding applicability to various tumor types, postoperative care, and integration of novel imaging studies and AI-powered tools We also share our clinical experience in Mohs micrographic surgery through case presentations and our dataBy discussing its widening indications, improved cure rates, and the incorporation of emerging technologies, this review highlights the potential for MMS to result in more precise, efficient, and personalized surgical interventions These insights will hopefully guide clinicians in optimizing patient selection, surgical planning, and postoperative care

Introduction

Mohs surgery, also known as Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS), is a method used to treat a variety of skin tumors, mainly basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). It ensures a comprehensive microscopic assessment of both peripheral and deep surgical margins with complete cancer removal and maximal sparing of the surrounding uninvolved tissue [1]. The technique, developed by Frederic E. Mohs at the University of Wisconsin in the 1930s, has undergone several modifications over the years [2]. Mohs initially named the method ‘chemosurgery’ as it involved in situ tissue fixation with zinc chloride paste, followed by surgical excision and microscopic examination of specimen margins. The paste could cause pain, inflammation, and fever with lymphadenopathy, and the whole process took a few days before the fixative took effect, especially when more complex reconstruction was needed. These issues led to a shift to using fresh tissues that were then frozen, without fixation, under local anesthesia. The fresh tissue technique was performed by Mohs himself and was further solidified as a viable alternative by Theodore Tromovitch and Sam Stegman, whose contributions helped establish a new standard and ultimately marked the transition from ‘chemosurgery’ to ‘Mohs micrographic surgery’ [3, 4]. The surgical technique introduced by Mohs was mostly appreciated by dermatologists and has since become widely used for the treatment of several skin cancers [2, 5, 6]. In the USA, Mohs micrographic surgery is now used to treat > 876,000 tumors each year [7].

Recognized indications for MMS are primarily non-melanoma skin cancers (NMSCs) with a high risk of recurrence or in areas of cosmetic or functional importance [7]. The main advantages are precise tumor removal due to a complete microscopic evaluation of surgical margins, high cure rates achieved for both primary and recurrent skin tumors, smaller postoperative skin defects compared to standard excision with a safety margin, and a better functional outcome. For a long time, MMS has been considered of great benefit in tumors that are likely to recur, recurrent tumors, and lesions with indistinct clinical borders [8]. Over the course of 6 years, 1356 cutaneous tumors were treated using Mohs surgery at the Dr Leventer Centre. There included treatment of cases with high-risk BCC, high-risk SCC, and rare non-melanoma skin cancers, including adnexal tumors (mucinous carcinoma, sebaceous carcinoma, malignant cylindroma, etc.), based on tumor characteristics, patient characteristics, and anatomic location. The exclusion criteria were represented by locally advanced squamous cell carcinoma, metastatic squamous cell carcinoma, locally advanced basal cell carcinomas, or some cases of syndromic basal cell carcinomas. These tumors were not microscopically curable by surgery and were amenable to systemic drug treatment or radiation therapy. The follow-up period was established depending on each tumor type, initially at 2 months for scar evaluation, then every 1 year for low-risk BCC and SCC or 3–6 months for high-risk SCC.

During the period under review, only four cases of post-MMS recurrence have been recorded, with a cure rate > 99.7%. Those patients were noted to have undergone multiple treatments prior to the intervention, including complex reconstructions using skin flaps [9].

Patients who developed recurrences had initially had high-risk tumors, with a history of multiple wide local excisions prior to MMS, mainly located in the periorbital area. These patients had who missed follow-up visits after surgical treatment and presented with new papules/nodules developed in the reconstruction flaps, subsequently confirmed as carcinomas by skin biopsy. The management of these patients is more complex, considering the characteristics of the recurrent tumor and the patient. Subsequent treatment consisted of MMS, systemic treatment, or radiation therapy.

Recommended indications for MMS depend on the type of tumor, tumor aggressiveness, anatomical location, and individual patient characteristics [10].

To become a Mohs surgeon, one has to complete additional fellowship training [11]. Dermatologists can apply for a Micrographic Surgery and Dermatologic Oncology fellowship training program accredited by the American College of Mohs Surgery (ACMS), the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education, or the Australasian College of Dermatologists. This competitive program lasts 1–2 years and follows a structured curriculum ensuring the acquisition of essential knowledge and hands-on experience performing Mohs surgery procedures under professional supervision [12]. Of note, in the US, there are two professional organizations dedicated to the field of Mohs surgery, which, although similar in name, are separate entities with distinct roles and membership requirements (see Table 1) [13, 14].

In contrast to the US and Australia, the availability of MMS in Europe is limited. The European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology (EADV) aims to increase the number of European Mohs surgeons and offers dermatology specialists the opportunity to participate in a 6-month fellowship at five training sites: Ambroise Paré University Hospital (Boulogne-Billancourt, France), Valencian Institute of Oncology (IVO), University Hospital Zurich, Erasmus University Medical Center (Rotterdam, The Netherlands), and most recently, in Eastern Europe, the Dr Leventer Centre in Bucharest, Romania [15].

In this review, we aim to familiarize the reader with the Mohs micrographic surgery technique and some of the current modifications, its applications in both common and rare tumors, and the principles of postoperative care following MMS. We also discuss its complications and limitations and point to possible future developments.

Methods

A literature search of the PubMed and Google Scholar databases was conducted for relevant articles published until September 2024. Only articles written in English or Polish were included. Titles and abstracts were screened initially, followed by a review of relevant full-text publications, including citation searches. No additional filters or restrictions regarding study design were applied.

The patients in this article have given written informed consent to the publication of their case details. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Discussion

Technique

MMS consists of a variable number of stages depending on the tumor size of the tumor, and each stage follows several steps (see Fig. 1). A skin biopsy is recommended before excision to establish the diagnosis [16]. The surgical area is first cleaned. The tumor is marked under dermatoscopic guidance. A 1–3-mm margin is commonly utilized during the initial excision [17]. However, for aggressive histopathologic types such as micronodular, infiltrating, and sclerosing BCC, along with Bowen’s disease and lentigo maligna, which have subclinical extension, the margin extends to ≥ 2–5 mm. In cases of dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, the margin may be extended up to 1–2 cm [18–21]. Table 2 lists the initial margin sizes commonly used in MMS for selected tumor types [17, 22–26].

The procedure is performed under local anesthesia, typically using a combination of lidocaine and epinephrine. Slow administration of the anesthetic helps reduce patient discomfort during injection [27]. Some studies suggested using bicarbonate to buffer lidocaine, which might reduce pain associated with the injection [28, 29]. Figure 1 presents a simplified algorithm summarizing the Mohs surgery technique.

Before the proper excision, debulking of the tumor with a curette may be performed. Most studies on the efficacy of curettage analyzed its application in BCC and SCC. Light presurgical curettage may better delineate tumor margins and increase the specimen size removed in stage one of MMS, without the unnecessary excision of healthy tissue. Moreover, it can reduce the operative time and costs [30–32]. In SCC, although the frozen examination of the entire specimen is faster, the gold standard is still debulking of the tumor before the first stage of MMS, with paraffin examination for tumor staging. This tumor staging is mandatory for placing the patient in a risk group. Although there are studies that showed there is no significant difference in up-staging between MMS and debulking and paraffin examination, in our institution, we prefer using paraffin sections [33].

The first incision is performed with a margin of healthy tissue. The scalpel is held at a 45° angle, allowing proper alignment of the edges. Appropriate specimen orientation is ensured by placing small marks that extend into the surrounding tissue, usually at the 12 o’clock position [27].

The excised tissue is then marked with dyes along the edges. The features of the specimen (or specimens) are transferred onto a detailed diagram of the surgical site, known as the Mohs map, further ensuring the specimen’s precise orientation [27, 34]. The tissue may be divided into smaller fragments for processing depending on tumor size and technician’s preferences [34, 35]. Colored inks are used to properly orient the segments with color codes simultaneously drawn on the Mohs map [27, 34].

Next, the specimen is embedded in a special medium and frozen in the cryostat. Rapid freezing is necessary to avoid the formation of cytoplasmic and interstitial ice crystals in the tissue [27]. The tissue block is then cut horizontally into thin (usually 5–6 μm) sections on a microtome. This allows the analysis of 100% of the surgical margins, both lateral and deep in the same plane [34, 35].

Slides are usually stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), but other dyes may also be utilized [34]. Toluidine blue has been successfully used in BCC as it stains the mucinous stroma around the tumor metachromatically. It has also been reported to help visualize SCC perineural involvement, a feature associated with higher recurrence and metastasis rates [36]. Combining H&E with toluidine blue staining has improved Mohs surgery trainees’ diagnostic accuracy, thus suggesting that teaching dual staining techniques should be included in the MMS training curriculum [37]. Moreover, immunohistochemical staining techniques are becoming increasingly popular among Mohs surgeons to improve the accuracy of tumor margin assessment. These stains can be particularly useful in complex cases, e.g., with neuronal or vascular invasion or severe inflammation [38]. Once staining has been completed, the slides are examined under a microscope by the Mohs surgeon [34]. If the margins are found positive for tumor cells, the surgeon returns to the operating room to perform another excision during the second Mohs stage. The process is repeated until all margins are clear [1].

There are several options for wound repair following MMS, including linear closure, second intention healing, skin flap, or skin graft [47]. Linear closure is the most widely used approach and has been associated with the fewest Mohs stages [48].

Useful resources—including a video summary illustrating the consecutive steps of MMS for both healthcare provider and patient education purposes—are provided on the website of the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) Dermatology (available at: https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamadermatology/fullarticle/2764796).

Indications

MMS is commonly performed for skin tumors with a high risk of recurrence or in areas where tissue preservation is paramount, such as the head and neck, genital area, hands, and feet. This method provides better functional and cosmetic outcomes by maximizing the conservation of healthy tissues [1].

In 2012, the American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) developed appropriate use criteria for MMS in collaboration with the American College of Mohs Surgery, the American Society for Dermatologic Surgery Association, and the American Society for Mohs Surgery. In addition to BCC, SCC, lentigo maligna, and melanoma in situ, other less common types of skin tumors were considered, including dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans [49].

Keratinocyte Cancers

BCC diagnoses have been on the rise in Europe and the US for the past few decades [50, 51]. A randomized trial by van Loo et al. compared the results of MMS and surgical excision (SE) in the treatment of high-risk facial BCC. After a 10-year-long follow-up, 4.4% and 12.2% recurrence rates were observed in the MMS and SE groups, respectively, among patients with primary BCC (n = 408). For patients with recurrent BCC (n = 204), the tumor recurrence rates were 3.9% in the MMS group and 13.5% in patients who underwent SE [52].

SCC is the second most diagnosed type of skin cancer and is the leading cause of non-melanoma skin cancer-related metastases and mortality [53, 54]. A 2013 systematic review analyzed a pooled population of 1144 patients who underwent different treatments for SCC and showed that the average recurrence rate after MMS was 3%, compared with 5.4% after standard SE [55]. Similar results were later observed in a Dutch study of 579 patients showing that MMS was associated with lower recurrence rates than SE, 3% and 8%, respectively [56].

The aforementioned appropriate use criteria, which evaluate 270 clinical scenarios where MMS is often considered, offer a structured approach to help select patients based on the anatomical location of lesions, as well as both patient and tumor characteristics [49]. By combining the available evidence with clinical expertise and judgment, the panel of experts assigned each scenario a rating (appropriate, uncertain, inappropriate), with most (212) related to keratinocyte cancers. The document emphasizes several features generally associated with a better suitability for MMS: aggressive histologic findings, moderate-to-high-risk locations (head and neck, genitalia, hands, feet, ankles, pretibial surface, or nipples), the presence of certain genetic syndromes (e.g., basal cell nevus syndrome, xeroderma pigmentosum), immunocompromised patients, those whose lesions occurred within skin exposed to radiotherapy or within post-traumatic scars, chronic ulcers/osteomyelitis/inflammation, or individuals previously diagnosed with aggressive tumor types [49].

For BCC, in areas other than the trunk and extremities, recurrent tumors, as well as primary aggressive, primary nodular, and primary superficial lesions, were considered appropriate. For tumors on the trunk and extremities, only recurrent aggressive, recurrent nodular, or larger primary lesions were deemed suitable for MMS [49].

For SCC, MMS was deemed most appropriate for both primary and recurrent tumors, almost irrespective of histologic subtype, when occurring in high-risk anatomical locations. In low- or moderate-risk areas, indications considered the patient’s immunity status, the presence of aggressive histology (e.g., perineural/perivascular involvement, sclerosing, small cell poorly/undifferentiated lesions), tumor size, recurrence status, or distinct scenarios, such as keratoacanthoma-type SCC, or in situ SCC/Bowen.

Given the complexity of the clinical scenarios discussed, the AAD has developed a free mobile application designed to assist dermatologists and other healthcare professionals in determining when Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS) is appropriate [57]. Such apps could potentially streamline referral processes and also serve as a valuable tool for patient education and shared decision-making. Melanoma and Lentigo Maligna

MMS is recognized as an appropriate treatment option for melanoma in situ and lentigo maligna [49]. A recent review showed that MMS improved survival rates in invasive melanoma compared with wide local excision. MMS may be particularly beneficial in patients with a superficial spreading type of melanoma and melanoma with a Breslow depth < 0.8 mm in sites with greater cosmetic and functional importance [58]. MART-1 (also known as Melan A) immunostaining has been suggested in MMS for melanoma, as it has been associated with improved diagnostic accuracy and a lower recurrence rate [59, 60].

Lentigo maligna (LM) is a type of melanoma in situ on sun-damaged skin with distinct clinical features and the potential to progress to lentigo maligna melanoma (LMM). Surgical therapies are considered the treatment of choice for LM/LMM and include wide local excision (WLE), staged excision, and MMS, the last of these particularly useful for large, poorly defined lesions or lesions located in difficult anatomic locations [61]. A meta-analysis of 41 studies involving 6278 patients conducted by Elshot et al. compared different surgical modalities for LM/LMM. The authors demonstrated that microscopically controlled excisional techniques, namely staged excision and MMS, yield better outcomes than wide local excision (WLE) [62]. The overall local recurrence rates were 11.8% (146/1236) for WLE compared to 2.3% (29/1270) for staged excision, and 1.3% (32/2428) for MMS. Among all margin-controlled techniques analyzed, MMS combined with immunochemistry specifically achieved the lowest recurrence rate of < 1% (11/1916). While data heterogeneity in this meta-analysis was moderate to high, it is worth noting that lesions treated with MMS combined with immunostaining tended to have larger mean diameters and longer follow-up periods and included more recurrent tumors—factors that further solidify its superior efficacy. Additionally, the authors highlighted the potential of reflectance confocal microscopy to reduce the number of surgical stages and minimize the risk of recurrence [62]. These conclusions align with findings from a recent meta-analysis by Mesquita et al., which evaluated the role of confocal microscopy in mapping LM/LMM before surgery. They found that compared to standard histopathology, reflectance confocal microscopy had a negative predictive value of 89.15% in detecting LM/LMM-negative margins, suggesting it could be a valuable non-invasive adjunct in presurgical margin estimation [63].

A variant of MMS known as “slow Mohs” has been recommended for lentigo maligna. In this technique, permanent formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections are used in each Mohs stage instead of the traditional frozen ones. While this method prolongs the process, it facilitates the accuracy of the microscopic interpretation of melanocytic atypia by eliminating freezing artifacts in melanocytes [64–66]. Careful examination of lentiginous melanocytic hyperplasia is paramount in patients with LM/LMM to distinguish between transformed cells at the lesion’s periphery and non-malignant melanocytes resulting from chronic sun damage [65].

Considering the subclinical extensions of LM/melanoma in situ lesions, previously recommended 5-mm surgical margins have proved insufficient for a substantial number of primary and recurrent lesions, particularly those affecting the head and neck or acral sites [62, 67, 68]. Acral lentiginous melanoma has a wide subclinical extension, and the Mohs intervention helps to avoid amputations and preserve as much healthy tissue as possible [69]. This further supports the potential superiority of MMS over other surgical modalities. Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans

Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a rare form of soft tissue sarcoma with locally destructive properties. It typically presents as a slow-growing, firm, asymptomatic dermal plaque or subcutaneous thickening that progresses insidiously to a protuberant lesion, often resulting in a delayed diagnosis [70].

Diagnosis is confirmed by pathology, preferably by a deep biopsy from the central part of the lesion. The spindle-shaped cells comprising the tumor typically show strong CD34 immunostaining, but it should be noted that CD34 expression is not exclusive to DFSP (e.g., spindle cell lipoma, fibroma can stain positively) and may also be reduced or even absent in DFSP with a fibrosarcomatous component [70].

DFSP is considered locally aggressive, but metastases are rare. Due to its infiltrative nature, surgical management of DFPS is notoriously challenging, and recurrence rates remain high, while recurrent tumors resulting from incomplete excision are associated with a poorer prognosis. Treatment options for resectable lesions include MMS, WLE, or, rarely, amputation in lesions involving the digits [70].

The characteristics of cutaneous sarcomas, including DFSP, make them suitable candidates for MMS. The neoplastic cells tend to form subclinical tentacle-like extensions going deep into the surrounding tissue, far beyond what is visible or palpable, and there are no curative treatments other than a complete excision. WLE, although technically simpler and quicker, requires a larger safety margin (usually 2–4 cm), including the underlying fascia, which may still be insufficient [70, 71]. More extensive excisions lead to significant, sometimes disfiguring surgical defects requiring more difficult reconstructions, and the process is further complicated if wound edges are found positive for residual tumor. In addition, WLE’s ‘bread loaf’ sectioning evaluates approximately 1% of the margins, whereas MMS provides a complete evaluation of the margins, both deep and lateral.

Current literature points to significantly lower recurrence rates of MMS than of WLE, suggesting that MMS may be the more efficient approach in the treatment of DFSP. To date, however, no randomized controlled trials have compared the outcomes of MMS vs WLE for DFSP. Lowe et al. reviewed the records of 186 patients with DFSP treated between 1955 and 2012. Among the 158 patients for whom recurrence information was available, 91 underwent WLE, while 67 were treated with MMS, and the reported recurrence rates were 27.7% and 3% after WLE and MMS, respectively [72]. Another retrospective analysis by Durack and colleagues found that out of 362 primary DFSPs treated with WLE, only 6 recurred during the median follow-up time of 25.5 months, whereas there were no recurrences after MMS [73]. Zhou et al. compared their results in treating 80 patients diagnosed with DFSP, 52 of whom were treated with WLE, and 13 with slow MMS. The recurrence rate after wide local excision was 9.6%, and no recurrences were reported in the MMS group [74]. Similar results were obtained by Snow et al., who observed no recurrences in 29 patients with DFSP treated with MMS in a > 5-year follow-up period [75]. A scoping review involving 117 patients performed by Sanabria et al. compared the patient outcomes following MMS and WLE in the treatment of DFSP of the head and neck, a location where it is notoriously difficult to obtain negative margins, and it has therefore been associated with a higher risk of recurrence compared to other anatomical sites. They found MMS was associated with a significantly lower recurrence rate (2%) than WLE (19%) [76]. A systematic review with meta-analysis by Malan et al. included an impressive total of 684 patients from five studies and found that the recurrence rates of DFSP after WLE and MMS (after a mean follow-up period of 5.32 years) were 9.1% and 2.72%, respectively [77]. Comparable results were observed by Loghdey et al., who analyzed the charts from 67 patients with DFSP treated with frozen-section MMS between 1996 and 2013. They found a 1.5% recurrence rate during the average 50-month follow-up [78]. These findings are reflected in interdisciplinary consensus-based guidelines issued in 2015 by European experts who recommended MMS as the optimal treatment for dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans [71].

Many modifications of the Mohs surgical technique have been devised to improve the accuracy of margin control and to address specific challenges associated with different tumor types.

One such alternative approach is the “spaghetti technique,” a two-stage method in which a narrow band of tissue beyond the lesion margins is resected, leaving the lesion itself intact [79]. The defect is sutured immediately, and the specimen is divided into smaller sections, which are then sent for histopathologic examination. If the excised “spaghetti” fragment is found positive for malignant cells, the process is repeated around this particular segment with residual tumor cells until the resected tissue strips are clear. The second phase involves tumor resection and simultaneous wound reconstruction [79]. Recently, a similar technique has been proposed for DFSP, called “the Saturn’s rings technique,” which utilizes ring-shaped frozen biopsies taken from the lesion’s periphery at fixed intervals. The authors praised it as less time-consuming (especially for larger lesions on the trunk and limbs) than conventional frozen MMS and reported no recurrences within 3 years with this novel method, although long-term results are unknown [80].

Another modification is the already mentioned “slow Mohs.” This technique uses paraffin-embedded permanent sections instead of the conventional frozen ones. The obvious disadvantage of the paraffin sections is the inevitable prolongation of the entire process, but despite this inconvenience, slow Mohs is increasingly becoming the method of choice for DFSP [21, 81]. The reason for this widespread appreciation lies in its ability to overcome several significant drawbacks of the frozen technique, namely tissue tearing, freezing artifacts causing changes in tissue morphology, and, perhaps most importantly, inadequate assessment of tumor cells infiltrating surrounding tissues. Many of these deferred analysis methods use incisions perpendicular to the skin surface (instead of the conventional 45° angle), which simplifies surgical wound closure [81, 82]. Randomized controlled trials are needed to establish which technique yields better results, paraffin or frozen MMS. To date, the available observational data do not support one method over the other [81, 82].

To name just a few of the remaining modifications, variations that have been developed include the Munich method [83–85], the square method [86–88], the muffin technique [85, 89], and the perimeter technique [85, 90] (see Table 4).

MMS Mohs micrographic surgery, WLE wide local excision, DFSP dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans

The preferred treatment method for localized DFSP at the Dr Leventer Centre is slow Mohs. This approach is considered the safest for a precise and comprehensive pathologic examination of the margins. According to the center's experience, the entire process, including histopathologic processing, typically takes 1 week, and the immunohistochemical staining requires an average of 48 h. Patients with resectable primary or recurrent tumors were included in the surgical treatment. Exclusion criteria were unresectable tumors or tumors that would have unacceptable functional or cosmetic outcomes after resection. These patients have undergone either systemic drug treatment or radiotherapy. Patient follow-up was initially performed after 2 months to assess the scar, and then annually.

For patients with unresectable or metastatic disease, the treatment of DFSP is systemic. Imatinib, an oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor, is considered the gold standard for patients who are not eligible for surgery [91]. In some cases, an initially inoperable DFSP has been shown to regress into a resectable tumor following neoadjuvant therapy with imatinib [92–95]. Notably, imatinib therapy has its limitations. Although it is widely regarded as safe and well-tolerated, adverse events are common and include edema, anemia, fatigue, nausea and vomiting, cutaneous reactions, hematologic toxicity, and resistance to imatinib, which can occur over time [96, 97]. Finally, some patients may not respond to targeted therapy at all. DFSP with fibrosarcomatous transformation may lack the characteristic chromosomal translocation targeted by imatinib, rendering the lesion resistant to treatment [71, 98]. Other proposed treatment options for patients with DFSP who are not eligible for surgery include radiation and chemotherapy. Still, cytotoxic chemotherapy and radiotherapy alone have very limited use in patients with DFSP [94, 99]. A recent review by Fionda et al. investigated the role of postsurgical radiotherapy in patients with DFSP. They found it may be considered as adjuvant therapy in the presence of risk factors (e.g., recurrent tumors, aggressive histology) or serve as a last resort in cases with positive margins where re-excision is unachievable [100]. Postoperative Care

In the postoperative period, pain, swelling, erythema, and bruising are to be expected. The healing time varies depending on the patient's characteristics (e.g., age, overall health, use of medications, preexisting vascular diseases, smoking status) and the course of the MMS procedure (e.g., the type of repair, tumor site, and size) [27, 101].

Postoperative pain is usually mild to moderate and typically persists for up to 1 day after the surgery. In a 2012 study, the patients rated peak postoperative pain as on average 1.99 out of 10 on a visual analog scale (VAS) [102]. Procedures on the scalp were associated with more discomfort than in other anatomical locations. Pain relief can be achieved with over-the-counter analgesics, such as paracetamol, and the need for prescription drugs is low [103].

MMS procedures are usually performed as same-day surgeries. However, same-day surgery is not always possible for various reasons. Studies comparing the postoperative course in patients treated on the same day vs. with delayed repair concluded that delayed reconstruction (but no more than 2 days) did not increase the risk of complications [101, 104]. Perhaps surprisingly, one study showed that delaying repair while using full-thickness skin grafts or composite grafts is associated with lower complication rates [105]. It has been argued that when using full-thickness or composite grafts, especially when cartilage is exposed, one can achieve better graft survival since the recipient wound has had time to improve its vascular supply [106].

Wound care should be practiced for 7–14 days after the surgery. Plain emollients such as petroleum jelly are recommended right after the procedure and should be applied by the patient at home. Topical antimicrobials frequently cause allergic reactions and should be avoided in the postoperative period on clean surgical wounds [107].

Complications and Additional Considerations

MMS is generally considered safe and is well tolerated by patients. The most common complications include infections, bleeding or hematoma formation, and impaired wound healing [108]. In a multicenter cohort study analyzing the complications of MMS in 1550 patients, no major postoperative complications were reported, and the rate of minor complications was only 2.6% [102]. An even larger analysis included 20,821 MMS surgeries and reported a 0.72% rate of adverse events, most of which were infections [108]. Skin grafts and surgery on the forehead were associated with a higher risk of complications [109].

The most common complication of MMS is surgical site infection (SSI). The risk of SSI depends on the surgical location (e.g., lower extremities) and type of repair used (such as skin flaps or grafts) [110]. Carriers of Staphylococcus aureus have been found to benefit from decolonization using intranasal mupirocin for 5 days before surgery. Compared to pre- and postoperative administration of oral antibiotics, those who underwent topical decolonization with mupirocin developed significantly fewer SSIs [111, 112]. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that decolonization reduces the risk of SSI in non-carriers as well [113].

In 2008, an advisory statement was published in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology (JAAD) providing updated recommendations for antibiotic prophylaxis in dermatologic surgery, including Mohs surgery [114]. Given the low rates of surgical site infections with MMS, experts state that routine antibiotic prophylaxis is generally unnecessary in most patients. Prophylaxis is recommended for patients at high risk of infective endocarditis, as well as selected patients with joint prostheses in whom antibiotics should be administered if the oral mucosa is likely to be breached. Furthermore, prophylaxis should be given in procedures involving the lower limbs and groin area, wedge excisions of the ear or lip, skin flaps on the nose, or skin grafting. In these groups, oral cephalexin 2 g or, in patients allergic to penicillin, oral clindamycin 600 mg should be used. Importantly, the statement emphasizes an individualized approach and advises careful consideration of the risk-to-benefit ratio in each case [114]. Notably, antibiotic prophylaxis is not required for patients with mitral valve prolapse, bicuspid aortic valve, calcified aortic stenosis, and certain congenital heart defects (e.g., atrial septal defect) [115].

Active bleeding requiring medical attention is more common in older patients with larger tumors and therefore more extensive postoperative wounds [102]. Most cases of postoperative bleeding and impaired wound healing after MMS procedures occur in patients receiving anticoagulants, and these individuals are estimated to constitute up to 38% of patients undergoing cutaneous surgery [108, 116]. Therefore, a detailed history should be obtained from the patient before the surgery concerning the use of anticoagulants and antiplatelet medications.

There are no standardized guidelines regarding the perioperative use of anticoagulants in patients undergoing MMS specifically. Preoperative assessment of the international normalized ratio (INR) is sometimes suggested for dermatologic surgery with an increased risk of bleeding. It is recommended to continue vitamin K antagonists (VKA) with INR within the therapeutic range, as INR < 3.5 is unlikely to pose significant bleeding in most procedures [117, 118]. When a higher risk of bleeding is anticipated, prescribers may be asked to adjust the dosing regimen beforehand to keep the patient at the lower end of their individual therapeutic range [118]. A survey conducted among British Mohs surgeons showed that direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) were continued by most [119]. This is in line with the 2023 guidance issued by the British Society for Dermatological Surgery (BSDS), which recommends the continuation of most medically necessary anticoagulants (including DOACs and VKAs) and antiplatelet medications for most dermatologic surgical procedures [118]. For procedures associated with a high risk of bleeding, surgeons may consider maintaining a 12–48-h interval between the last dose of DOACs and surgery, depending on the patient’s renal function and the anticoagulant used, or scheduling the procedure just before the next dose [119, 120]. However, caution should be exercised in all cases, for it has been argued that even brief interruption of clinically indicated antithrombotic therapy may lead to serious, potentially life-threatening thrombotic complications, which outweigh the risk of postoperative bleeding [121, 122].

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and keloid formation following MMS are more frequent in skin of color. To minimize hyperpigmentation and keloid formation, wound edge manipulation should be gentle, and high-tension closure should be avoided [123].

Facial lesions, such as DFSP, may require careful postoperative functional assessment, especially because these locally invasive tumors can infiltrate the underlying facial nerve fibers. Neural damage may also result from the surgical procedure itself. Therefore, a multidisciplinary approach should be considered in these patients to ensure safety and optimal functional outcomes [124].

Even though wound reconstructions after MMS can generally be less complex than those following standard surgical excisions performed without margin control, the anatomic sites involved and large postoperative defects can prove challenging, as Mohs surgeons tend to operate mostly on areas of great cosmetic and functional sensitivity. The type of repair used depends upon a multitude of factors, including the lesion (e.g., tumor type, size, exact location), patient (e.g., age, preference, skin redundancy, and laxity), and surgeon (e.g., experience, preference). Commonly utilized reconstructive techniques include linear closure, regional skin flaps, or grafts of varying thickness [125].

In challenging locations, when extensive and advanced repairs are necessary to address post-excisional defects, a multidisciplinary approach, such as referral to ENT specialists or plastic surgeons, may be warranted [126]. For facial or genital lesions, each area presents specific pitfalls and may require unique reconstructive techniques, including an array of local flap repairs such as H-plasty, O-T, V–Y, or rotation advancement flaps. For example, poor reconstruction of the eyelids can lead to contractures, keratitis, and impaired vision, severely worsening the patient’s quality of life. Scar placement has to be strategic within the natural creases, shadows, and subunit borders while preserving symmetry and functionality [127–130]. A thorough understanding of the anatomy and careful preoperative planning are imperative in all instances.

Several innovative wound management strategies have been proposed for treating post-MMS defects, including collagen skin substitutes, synthetic nanofiber matrices, or amniotic membranes [131–133]. Biologic wound healing agents have been suggested in nasal and scalp reconstructions when other closure methods are not viable. They can be used as both permanent and temporary solutions, followed by delayed skin grafting if necessary [134].

If, despite scrupulous wound management and meticulous repair, postoperative complications arise, laser therapy may prove useful. A recent systematic review of the applications of lasers following MMS demonstrated their positive effects on wound healing, graft resurfacing, telangiectasias, and unfavorable scarring [135]. Other scar revision techniques include steroid injections, dermabrasion, and surgical revision [136].

Limitations

Contraindications

There are no absolute contraindications to MMS [1]. According to data from a Spanish registry, most patients were disqualified from MMS because of their general health. The second most reported reason was a low-risk tumor, likely indicating inadequate referral [137]. Advanced age alone does not disqualify patients from MMS, as it is safe and effective in elderly patients, including those aged ≥ 90 years [138, 139].

Accessibility

According to a UK survey, the waiting times for MMS varied from < 4 weeks to > 12 weeks, and so did the travel distances to the closest center offering MMS [140].

Adequate patient education is essential to obtain informed consent before surgery. The process of MMS can be difficult for patients to comprehend, resulting in anxiety, formal complaints, poor cooperation, and dissatisfaction with the results. Several educational techniques and resources clinicians can use to support their patients’ understanding have been proposed, including videos, 3D models, pamphlets, professional online resources, and preoperative teledermatology consultations [141].

Costs

The costs associated with MMS are often raised as a potential barrier to its widespread use [142]. Contradicting results have been reported in the literature regarding the relative costs of MMS compared to other treatment options [143–145]. However, the lower recurrence rates, smaller excisions that reduce costly wound management, and the surgeon examining the slides—eliminating additional pathologist fees—contribute to the cost-effectiveness of MMS [146].

Future Considerations

MMS is an evolving field, and the future will likely bring new solutions to facilitate workflow and further improve clinical outcomes. A few exciting amendments include the increasing use of non-invasive imaging, digital pathology, and artificial intelligence (AI) models.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) and multispectral optoacoustic tomography (MSOT) are advanced but not yet widely used non-invasive imaging modalities. These tools may enable accurate visualization of tumor dimensions preoperatively, possibly reducing the number of Mohs stages [6]. Another non-invasive real-time imaging technique currently investigated as a potential adjunctive tool in MMS is the already mentioned reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM). RCM has been gaining popularity because of its ability to better delineate lesion margins and detect the presence of residual cancer cells in both non-melanoma skin cancers and LM/LMM [147–149].

Digital pathology is becoming increasingly used by dermatologists. The slide scanning takes an additional 10–30 min but enables surgeons to remain in the operating room while waiting for the pathologist to report the result back digitally. This could facilitate the sharing of images between professionals and allow for postoperative review. On the other hand, high-resolution files can take up much of the server storage [150].

The widespread computerization of healthcare and facilitated data sharing among medical personnel have also enabled the transition from traditional paper-based Mohs maps to digital photographic mapping of surgical sites. These digital maps, created in the operating room, can be stored securely in a dedicated cloud. Pathology laboratory workers can then access and download the maps almost immediately, allowing them to annotate and report results back directly on the computerized images [151].

Machine learning models have been proposed to assist clinicians in evaluating dermoscopic images, facilitate microscopic examination of histologic slides for the presence of tumor cells, and reduce the financial burden on the healthcare system [152, 153]. In terms of histologic slide analysis specifically, these models can help identify whether tumor cells are present in an image, mark areas suspicious for malignancy, generate digitally enhanced images that mimic standard histopathology slides through advanced coloring and image enhancement algorithms, and even suggest potential diagnoses [152, 154–156]. AI tools have also shown promise in predicting the complexity of MMS procedures based on patient demographic data and tumor characteristics. Such predictions could positively affect patient preparation and counseling and enable more efficient operating room scheduling and staffing [157].

One particularly promising application of AI in MMS is the analysis of fresh-frozen sections. Some of the models created to date have demonstrated the ability to accurately differentiate histologic structures despite the presence of common freezing artifacts [152, 158–160]. Although further validation studies and development of larger, more diverse datasets are imperative to ensure the models’ generalizability across varying clinical settings, emerging reports suggest that both models and physicians tend to excel in slightly different aspects of the diagnostic process. By integrating AI-generated preliminary assessments into the workflow, clinicians could benefit from an additional analytical output, paving the way for a future where the two could complement each other to boost diagnostic accuracy and efficiency and ultimately optimize clinical outcomes [158].

Finally, there is a growing worldwide trend to use MMS as the first-line treatment for diverse skin lesions. Recent research shows that it is now increasingly recommended for patients with rare non-melanoma skin cancers such as DFSP, as well as for the treatment of lentigo maligna and melanoma. With its many benefits, the range of conditions suitable for MMS is only expected to expand.

Conclusions

MMS is a specialized surgical method that continues to evolve, with emerging technique modifications to better adapt it to different tumor types and characteristics. Our clinical experience aligns with and adds to the growing body of evidence emphasizing the effectiveness of MMS regarding its high cure rates and fewer recurrences. While it is significantly more established in the US, European dermatology associations are working to expand MMS accessibility across Europe, with the hope that the number of accredited Mohs surgeons will soon increase. Looking ahead, advancements in the fields of non-invasive imaging and digital pathology, together with the implementation of artificial intelligence models, may further refine MMS. With the evolution of these technologies, MMS is likely to contribute to more personalized and cost-efficient care, leading to improved outcomes.
