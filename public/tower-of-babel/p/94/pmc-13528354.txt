Impact of acoustic and informational noise on AI-generated clinical summaries
Authors: Thomas C Draper, Jason Leake, Kathryn Lamb-Riddell, Timothy Cox, John McCormick, Stephen Trowell, Janice Kiely, Richard Luxton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo investigate the effect of introducing environmental noise, microphone identity and position, and informational noise on the accuracy of a commercial Clinical AI Scribe (CAIS).Methods and analysisConsultations on five medical conditions (memory loss, diarrhoea, headaches, skin rash or prostate symptoms) were recorded in a simulated primary care setting. A ground-truth audio file was produced, along with a ground-truth clinical summary via the CAIS. Different types of ‘noise’ at different levels were then investigated by (i) varying the type and distance of the microphone, (ii) introducing background noise (rain, baby, construction, toddler) at various levels and (iii) introducing informational noise (irrelevant discussions, medical and non-medical). These were then compared with the ground-truth summaries. The degradation of clinical summary accuracy was measured (omissions, hallucinations and inclusions), relative to the ground-truth summary.ResultsErrors increased for all noise levels and noise types, principally being omissions. At 4.5 m from the consultation, for all microphones tested, all facts were omitted. At up to 2 m, all microphones, except the laptop, only introduced a small number (<5) of omissions. For background noise, the type of noise was found to correlate with omissions, with toddler and heavy rain being particularly deleterious. The CAIS was remarkably apt at rejecting additional informational noise.ConclusionsThe CAIS generally excelled at capturing a doctor-patient consultation and producing accurate clinical summaries; however, error rate (especially omissions) increased notably when acoustic noise was introduced. The system was better able to handle informational noise.

Introduction

Recent studies have highlighted the transformative potential of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare.1 2 Several reviews highlight potential advantages of the technology together with risks and challenges, for example, data security, regulatory issues and acceptance by patients and clinicians.3–5 Recently, there has been a surge in the development of AI-powered scribe technologies, which transcribe clinician-patient consultations into structured medical summaries. There are now a significant number of such software packages available commercially.6 Reviews of some of these products are available via the KLAS website (https://engage.klasresearch.com/).

The internal processing undertaken by AI medical scribes tends to be a two-step process: (i) automatic speech recognition (ASR) produces a text transcript from the digital audio input, which in turn is (ii) processed by a large language model (LLM) to produce a clinical summary.7 This generated summary is then input to the patient’s electronic health record (EHR) by the consulting clinician. As there can be errors present in these summaries, it is currently mandated by commercial suppliers that the clinician should check the summary and correct for any errors prior to input to the patient’s electronic health record (EHR). Clinical AI Scribe (CAIS) utilisation can improve consultations by reducing the need for clinician note-taking (allowing for more eye contact and attention to the patient),8 decreasing clinician time spent on administrative tasks and associated burnout9 10 and reducing errors introduced by manual clinician input.11

Significant errors may be introduced by AI scribes; see, for example, Kernberg et al,12 who report significant errors for a chatGPT-4-based scribe. Methods to determine and classify errors have recently been reviewed by Gebauer.6 These include automated approaches, for example, ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) and BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers)13–16 and also manual approaches,12 in which the clinical notes are compared with the original audio file, often by clinically trained personnel. A number of other metrics are also described, for example, a modified Physician Documentation Quality Instrument-917 and the Sheffield Assessment Instrument for Letters18 19 approaches.

Despite the potential advantages, significant additional challenges exist in a real-world clinical scenario in obtaining an accurate clinical summary of the consultation. The investigation of these challenges forms the focus of the current paper: (i) first, the placement (and quality) of the microphone is critical to obtain an optimal audio input to the ASR; (ii) second, the clinical environment can be noisy with background noise present, for example, from building work outside or the presence of a crying baby (iii) and third, informational noise may also be present during the consultation, for example, a long and irrelevant diatribe about a hobby, or medical discussion which is not relevant to the consultation. These and other challenges have been described by Quiroz et al.20

The effect of these additional challenges is investigated here experimentally. The approach that we have adopted is first to establish a ground-truth clinical summary with a good quality, noise-free audio file recording of a consultation, which is input to the commercial CAIS. Noise, either acoustic (microphone or background) or informational (medical or non-medical), is then introduced at different levels to the audio file for the three challenges outlined above. The altered audio file is then presented to the CAIS to produce a modified clinical summary, which is then compared with the ground-truth summary to establish the additional errors introduced by the presence of the introduced noise.

Note that this study does not seek to evaluate the performance of a specific CAIS, but rather to examine issues pertinent to the integration of such technologies within primary care. Although a single CAIS was used as an illustrative case, the types of errors identified are likely to reflect patterns across comparable systems.

Methods

Study design

All medical consultations used, recorded or referred to in this study were performed using actors and simulated medical conditions. For full protocol details, see the Protocols file in the online supplemental information.

This research was conducted in a simulated primary care setting at the NHS South West Centre of Digital Excellence (CoDE). This provided a simulated primary care environment, allowing for increased realism in the positioning of persons and equipment, background noise and acoustic profile. A controlled experimental framework was implemented to assess the impact of microphone cost (recommended retail price £25–£120, specifics in the Protocols file in the online supplemental information and online supplemental table S1) and placement, background noise and varying informational noise (both medical and non-medical) on the accuracy of a CAIS. Heidi Free (Heidi Health) was selected as a representative CAIS for this study due to its widespread use in UK Primary Care at the outset of the study. No additional development, fine-tuning or prompting of the AI was undertaken; the CAIS was used as-is, with the default settings and the Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan (SOAP) template.

A repeated-measures approach was adopted, enabling systematic variation of these factors while maintaining as consistent an input to the AI as possible. Specifically, simulated consultations (typically 7 min in length) were recorded and presented to the CAIS, which generated a clinical summary (containing typically 25–30 points of information). The original audio files were then modified (eg, by including construction noise or a prolonged conversation about tennis) and re-presented to the same CAIS, which generated a new clinical summary, which could be compared with the original summary. A total of 160 clinical summaries were generated using this methodology (see online supplemental tables S3, S4 and S5). These processes are displayed in figure 1.

Patient and public involvement

A public/patient group (Citizens Reference Group) has been set up for the NHS Healthcare Centre of Digital Excellence. They were consulted in the design phase of the study and were provided with early stage results to which they gave feedback to further inform the study.

Results

Impact of microphone placement (acoustic noise)

The distance between the participants of the consultation and the recording microphone is a key factor in ensuring high-quality output from the clinical AI. Four microphone positions were looked at: control, 0.5 m, 2.0 m and 4.5 m (m=metres), chosen to simulate a seated conversation, small movement (eg, to do test) and larger movement (eg, moving to couch or behind examination screen). As can be seen in figure 2, the total points of information reduce as the distance is increased. This is expected because as the distance is increased, the intensity of the sound signal at the microphone decreases. This might be expected to follow an inverse square law, I ∝d-2 , where I is the audio intensity and d is the distance. The decrease in the intensity results in a reduction in the bit depth of the recorded speech, while the bit depth of the background noise remains constant, ultimately resulting in a sharp decrease in the signal-to-noise ratio, and so the accuracy of the text produced by the ASR.

The median points of information, as a percentage, included in a clinical summary produced by an artificial intelligence with different microphones (having different costs, see Protocols file in the online supplemental information), as a function of distance and relative to the original summary, for all five medical conditions combined. The blue dashed lines are to guide the eye. Medians are shown to preserve clarity and the device-level variability is shown in the box plot in online supplemental figure S1.

Across non-laptop microphones, device-level interquartile ranges (IQRs) overlapped at each distance, indicating similar performance. By contrast, the integrated laptop microphone exhibited greater loss of information at all distances (see online supplemental figure S1).

This decrease in the points of information as the distance increases correlates with the increase in the numbers of errors, as shown in figure 3. The number of hallucinations and inclusions remains relatively constant across the distances, although the number of omissions rapidly increases, and in many cases, the number of omissions equates to the original number of points of information (thus the complete loss of information at 4.5 m, shown in figure 2). These omissions occur because of a failure of the speech-to-text (ASR) process, which is unable to accurately recognise the English speech. A lack of inclusions is anticipated, as the introduction of additional truthful information is unlikely when the original information has already been compromised. Of note, however, is the small but visible peak in hallucinations at the 2 m position. This is primarily caused by the cheapest of the tested microphones, which had a disproportionate increase in hallucinations at this distance, despite overall error rates remaining comparable to other non-laptop microphones. At 0.5 m, the signal is sufficiently robust to permit operation with minimal errors, whereas at 4.5 m the signal is so weak that the AI is unable to continue processing; however, at 2 m, an ‘anti‐Goldilocks zone’ is observed. In this region, the LLM assumes a sufficient quantity and quality of information to proceed, but is overconfident.

The number and types of errors at different microphone positions, d: 0.5 m, 2.0 m and 4.5 m from the midpoint of the source, for all microphones and medical conditions combined. Numbers indicate the p values: Wilcoxon test between distances and analysis of variance within error type.

It is interesting to note that, in most cases, the cost/brand of the microphone has little impact on the performance of the clinical AI, with all but one sharply dropping off after the distance is increased beyond 2 m. The sole exception to this is the laptop integrated microphone, which deteriorated at a much greater rate. This can also be seen in online supplemental figure S1.

Impact of background (acoustic) noise

In a real-world primary care setting, there could be significant confusing sounds present, for example, generated by those present in the consultation and also outside the consulting room. Four different typical background noises were investigated at varying volumes: baby crying, construction noise, heavy rain and toddler chatter (for source details, see online supplemental information). The noise types investigated were chosen because they were considered to be realistic and plausible, as discussed with experienced primary care clinicians. It is, unfortunately, not always possible to wait for a toddler to stop babbling or for the rain to cease before being able to conduct a clinical consultation, especially if the volume is deemed low to human ears. It is conceivable that the CAIS may be more sensitive to background noise than a human, potentially due to a poorer ‘cocktail-party effect’, which permits humans to filter out background noise and speech subconsciously21; an area still in active development for computers.22

All four resulted in the introduction of errors, with the number of errors typically increasing as the volume of the background noise, relative to the consultation sound level, was increased. For this study, decibels (dB) were used to quantify the relative sound volumes. The increase in errors can be seen in figure 4 and online supplemental figure S2. The volumes used were 0 dB (approximately the same volume as the consultation), −10 dB (about half the perceived loudness, or ~10% of the sound pressure intensity), −5 dB (~30% of the sound pressure level, ie, noticeably quieter) and +5 dB (~3 times sound pressure level, ie, noticeably louder). For reference, the background noise could be clearly discerned at −10 dB, while at +5 dB, the speech was still comprehensible, although the consultation would be unlikely to proceed until the noise had abated. Moderate rainfall producing noise levels around −5 dB in an office is within the realm of possibility; however, rainfall sufficient to reach +5 dB is less likely. In contrast, internal noise contributions (eg, from a toddler or baby) have a higher probability of reaching the +5 dB threshold.

The number (and type) of errors introduced when different volumes of the four background noises are introduced. Four different sources of background noise are presented, with results for all medical scenarios combined. Hal, hallucination; Inc, inclusions; Omi, omission. Values shown are the mean, and error bars are SE.

When comparing directly between volumes, across all background noise sources, there was no statistically significant increase in error rate between the −10 dB and −5 dB samples (p=0.1175), nor between the −10 dB and 0 dB samples (p=0.0782). However, the difference between −10 dB and +5 dB was significant (p=0.0055). At this highest level of noise, the AI system struggled to isolate the clinician’s and patient’s speech, leading to degraded accuracy and incomplete clinical summaries.

It is interesting to note that there is a strong correlation between the type of background noise and the number of errors introduced. The introduction of the toddler’s continuous, although unstructured chattering, caused a great number of errors.

At the other end of the spectrum, both the baby and construction noise caused far fewer errors. As there was no recognisable speech in these noises, the only mechanism for disruption was by literally drowning out the speech. Intermittent background sounds (eg, loud crashes) were also better tolerated than continuous noise. The introduction of heavy rain at high volumes caused the greatest number of errors, suggesting that the frequency profile of certain background noises affects AI recognition differently. Heavy rain is a common background sound in consultation rooms, and also happens to approximate pink noise (1/f), a sound similar to white noise, but which distributes equal power across the used frequencies,23 that is, the conversational speech range. This sound profile therefore effectively masks speech,24 making heavy rain particularly mechanistically relevant for CAIS performance and helping to explain the larger degradation we observed relative to other non-speech noises.

Impact of informational noise

The AI-generated summary should capture only the important and relevant information. Examples of potentially confounding information are: (i) irrelevant medical-themed monologues by the patient, which should not dilute the clinician’s professional view and should not appear in the clinical summary and (ii) casual conversations (eg, about the weather), which should also be omitted from the clinical summary.

Medical and non-medical informational noise was added either as a short snippet or as a longer rigmarole. Full details are provided in the online supplemental information, but examples include:

When superfluous information was introduced into the audio file, either as a patient-derived medical or conversational non-medical form, the CAIS was remarkably apt at reporting only the relevant details. Conversational pieces (eg, about a tennis club) were entirely left out, while irrelevant medical details (or speculative self-diagnoses) were either omitted or in some way tagged as being a patient opinion or concern.

Nevertheless, while this informational noise was usually not included (or was at least appropriately labelled), some errors still occurred. Figure 5 shows the absolute number of errors introduced into the summaries, while the relative error rate can be seen in online supplemental figure S3. The presence of a small number of inclusions is expected, as there may be new information to report (eg, ‘patient worried about brain cancer’). However, the presence of omissions indicates that the proportion of medically relevant information captured has reduced. Throughout all experiments in this section, only a single hallucination was observed (see online supplemental figure S3). This hallucination was simply a note to follow up on a blood pressure check, which had been discussed, but the specific follow-up had not been mentioned.

Interestingly, there was no significant difference between the number of errors produced from irrelevant medical versus irrelevant non-medical information (p=0.6806, W=477—Wilcoxon rank sum test with continuity correction). This suggests that the AI scribe is equally capable of rejecting both types of informational noise. Likewise, the difference between shorter and longer segments of informational noise was not significant when looking at omissions (p=0.846, W=463—Wilcoxon rank sum test with continuity correction).

Discussion

Interpretation of findings

The findings of this study show that this commercial CAIS can be highly sensitive to environmental conditions, particularly acoustic noise, such as that caused by microphone positioning or certain background noises. It should be noted, however, that the errors produced add onto what is already a (mostly accurate, though still) imperfect summary. We have observed that the commercial CAIS produces on average around 0.5 hallucinations and one omission per consultation under ideal audio conditions. For the interested reader, error counts for the original ‘ground-truth’ CAIS-generated clinical summary can be found in online supplemental table S2. Further details on this will be presented in a future publication focusing on the accuracy of various CAIS products.

The positive correlation between increased microphone distance and the rise in errors (specifically omissions) highlights the importance of microphone placement, set-up and testing in clinical settings. An unexpected discovery was that more expensive microphones did not necessarily provide better performance than cheaper microphones; and in fact, cheaper microphones could actually outperform them (however, one low-cost microphone did display a distance-specific shift towards hallucinations at 2 m). The data were clear, however, that the integrated laptop microphone tested was particularly poor. It is suggested that a possible reason for this is a combination of the low sensitivity of the microphone (which could have been tuned to work optimally at typical laptop user distances), combined with the innate directionality of that microphone. It should also be noted that the audio quality from different microphones in different laptops is, of course, highly variable.25

The impact of background noise on accuracy emphasises the need for AI algorithms capable of filtering out environmental disturbances. It is evident that some types of noise, such as a baby crying, have minimal effect on the clinical summary generated. However, vocalisations, such as toddler chatter, caused notable degradation of the clinical summary. As the transcript does not record the toddler’s words, it appears that the errors are due to the CAIS struggling to isolate different voices when the toddler spoke over either the patient or the clinician. Additionally, and of particular interest, the pink noise generated by heavy rain also caused a particularly large error rate, even at lower (and more realistic) volumes. Due to its acoustic profile, pink noise is often used for sound masking,24 which may have contributed to the CAIS’s difficulty in distinguishing the speech.

Informational noise, in the form of either irrelevant casual conversation or patient-derived medical terms (eg, a speculative patient self-diagnosis) was treated comparatively well by the CAIS. Only a single hallucination was observed (with informational noise), although there were a number of omissions. As the content of the informational noise (medical or non-medical) did not have a statistically significant correlation with the number of omissions, it appears that (either through context, voice-identifying or some other method) the CAIS is able to distinguish between the clinician and patient, and record their vocalisations with appropriate weighting. This is important because a clinical note should be weighted to a clinician’s thoughts, and not the patient’s.26

Throughout the study, by far the most prevalent type of error was omissions. This demonstrates the conservative nature of this commercial CAIS and suggests that the LLM has been designed, when presented with a difficult audio transcription, to err on the side of caution (producing omissions) rather than take a ‘best guess’ (which could produce hallucinations). It is generally accepted that it is better to omit than to have incorrect information. Omissions observed included:

With the exception of the informational noise section, it was interesting to note the presence of inclusions (accurate points of information present in the new summary, but not the original summary). It was anticipated that the addition of either type of noise would cause a decrease in the number of points of information, but instead, in several cases, the CAIS noted additional truthful points, which it had previously ignored. For example, the addition of a conversation in which the clinician and patient, who are socially friends, discuss babysitting, mother-in-laws and arranging a playdate (ie, no additional mention of smoking) caused the AI to accurately report that the patient is a smoker in the clinical summary (the original, and only, smoking discussion was temporally far removed from the inserted casual conversation). While this information was accurate (it was mentioned in the original audio), it was not reported in the original summary. The mechanism behind this remains unclear, although it demonstrates LLMs’ unpredictable sensitivity to user input.

Implications for clinical practice

Given the rapidly increasing international uptake of AI-powered documentation tools in primary care (and elsewhere in clinical workflows), it is essential to ensure the accuracy and reliability of these systems. These findings underscore several key considerations for the integration of AI scribe technology into primary care settings.

First, microphone placement should be optimised within the consultation environment, with recommendations favouring a dedicated microphone positioned within 1 m of the clinician, to reduce omission errors. In particular, integrated laptop microphones should be avoided. It is possible that lapel microphones could offer an improvement, although this could impact user acceptance. Additionally, an additional microphone located at an examination couch (which could be upwards of 2 m away27) may be required to ensure all conversation is captured. Second, while the AI clinical scribe was generally able to accommodate low levels of background noise, interference from other vocalisations (such as toddlers) or pink noise (such as heavy rain) should be carefully minimised. This is especially important when the noise volume is equal to or greater than the volume of the speech. Third, when non-medically relevant social conversations are present in a consultation, additional care should be taken in confirming the generated summary, to ensure that no omissions have occurred.

The issue of hallucinations and omissions in AI-generated clinical summaries reinforces the fact that (as required by all commercial providers) clinician oversight remains a necessary safeguard. While AI has the potential to streamline documentation,1 its current limitations indicate that all generated summaries should undergo manual review before being integrated into patient records. Clinicians must be trained to identify potential AI errors and to verify the accuracy of recorded information. It must be emphasised that user overconfidence in the accuracy of the AI-generated summary (also known as automation bias) should be guarded against, as errors occur regularly enough to be problematic, but infrequently enough that users may become habituated, resulting in reduced critical scrutiny. And while the effect of such an event would be variable, it is quite possible that delays in diagnosis, impacts on treatment or even a fatal consequence may occur.

The aim of this study was not to evaluate the performance of any specific CAIS, but rather to explore broader considerations relevant to the deployment of such technologies in primary care settings. While one CAIS product was used as an exemplar, the patterns of error observed are likely to be generalisable across similar systems. It remains the responsibility of individual clinicians, organisations and regulatory bodies to ensure that any CAIS in use complies with relevant professional, organisational and statutory requirements.

Limitations

This study was conducted within a simulated clinical environment using actors to portray a limited range of medical scenarios, which may constrain the generalisability of the findings. While the controlled setting allowed for precise manipulation of noise variables and consistent input to the CAIS, it cannot fully reflect the complexity and unpredictability of real-world primary care consultations. Additionally, the study focused exclusively on a single CAIS product, and although care was taken to use it in a representative manner, findings may not be directly transferrable to other systems or future iterations of the same product, particularly given the pace of development in this field. Finally, the error analysis in this study, while comprehensive in terms of characterising hallucinations, inclusions and omissions, did not assess the clinical significance of these errors in terms of their potential to affect patient outcomes or decision-making. Certain errors, such as the absence of medication history or symptom onset, may carry far greater clinical risk than others.

Future directions for research

While this study provides significant insights into the challenges associated with CAISs, further research is needed to understand and enhance the robustness and usability of these systems. In particular, real-world studies (with real patient consultations) will be vital to determine their effectiveness in diverse and unpredictable scenarios. It is important to acknowledge that these tools are undergoing swift advancements, and considerable enhancements in performance are anticipated in the future.

One important area for future study is the development of AI models trained on datasets that incorporate a greater variety of real-world clinical noise conditions. There are currently very few publicly available databases that also include the audio file.13 Additionally, investigations into multimodal CAIS approaches, which integrate audio, text and contextual patient data, may yield improvements in accuracy and relevance.

Another avenue of exploration is the impact of CAIS on clinician workload and efficiency. While CAIS offer the potential to reduce administrative burden, it remains to be seen whether their implementation leads to measurable improvements in workflow and patient outcomes.4 28 Longitudinal studies assessing the long-term effectiveness of AI-assisted documentation, including its influence on consultation dynamics and medico-legal considerations, will be essential for guiding future AI adoption in healthcare.

Conclusion

AI-powered clinical scribes have great potential to improve clinical documentation and alleviate administrative burdens for healthcare professionals.1 2 This study highlights the challenges associated with the use of CAISs, particularly their sensitivity to microphone quality/positioning and environmental noise.

The findings reinforce the importance of microphone placement, with close-proximity, dedicated microphones yielding the most reliable results. Increased distance and the use of integrated microphones were associated with higher omission rates, suggesting that optimised audio capture strategies should be a priority in clinical settings. Background noise, particularly comprehensible speech (eg, toddler chatter) and heavy rain (pink noise), also significantly impacted accuracy. While both non-medical and medical informational noise (eg, a conversation about tennis or speculative patient self-diagnosis) did contribute to an increase in errors, they were far less pronounced than those caused by acoustic noise.

Ultimately, clinician oversight remains a key factor in ensuring accuracy of both the summary and the EHR. While AI has the potential to streamline documentation, human validation is necessary to correct potential errors. Structured review workflows will be crucial to maintaining data integrity and supporting safe clinical decision-making.
