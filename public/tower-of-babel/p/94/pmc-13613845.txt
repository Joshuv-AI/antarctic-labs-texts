Community-based chest X-ray screening versus symptom-based case presentation in the elderly: evidence from three consecutive years of data
Authors: Kui Liu, Ziyang Fang, Bin Chen, Yaling Feng, Zhicheng Yu, Yi Yu, Tao Jiang, Jianmin Jiang, Hui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity-based chest X-ray (CXR) screening has been widely performed to improve tuberculosis identification in elderly populations, but comparative evidence against mode of symptom-driven case finding remains scarce.MethodsWe conducted three annual rounds of mass CXR screening among individuals aged over 60 years in Lanxi City, China, from 2021 to 2023. Tuberculosis cases identified through CXR screening were compared with concurrent, symptom-driven case presentations via routine surveillance. Restricted cubic spline and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to examine factors associated with distinct detection pathways.ResultsAt the individual level, a total of 831 tuberculosis cases were identified through two modes. CXR mass screening detected more patients with less typical symptoms, while symptom-driven detection was more effective in females (aOR = 0.59, 95% CI 0.39–0.88), bacteriologically confirmed cases (aOR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.27–0.63), and those aged greater than 80 (aOR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.30–0.82). Age showed a nonlinear association with mass screening yield, with peak effectiveness between 60 and 66 years while no significance was found for BMI. In population-level analysis, CXR mass screening outperformed symptom-driven detection for case identification in the first round but declined and reversed by the third round. Subgroup analyses revealed potential benefits among individuals with diabetes or prior tuberculosis history, particularly within two years post-diagnosis or treatment.ConclusionsCommunity-based CXR screening could effectively detect incremental tuberculosis cases with atypical or mild symptoms. The young-old would benefit from mass CXR screening, while more accessible services were needed for the very elderly. Elderly individuals with diabetes or prior tuberculosis history should be prioritized, with intensified monitoring within two years after diagnosis or treatment completion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07931-8.

Introduction

As a chronic infectious disease, tuberculosis causes huge challenges in low- and middle-income countries globally, especially among the 30 high burden countries [1]. According to the latest report released by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2025, there were 10.7 million new tuberculosis cases globally, with an estimated incidence rate of 131 per 100,000 population [2]. In recent years, with the enhancement of global surveillance capacity and improvements in diagnostic technologies, the gap in undetected tuberculosis cases has been gradually narrowing. However, a disparity of approximately 2.7 million persists between estimated data and reported results [3]. To address this gap, active screening interventions, particularly community-based active case finding have been implemented to improve case detection in populations with limited healthcare access and well-confined regions [4].

Globally, passive case detection (PCF), where patients seek care based on their symptoms, remains the dominant tuberculosis detection strategy [5, 6]. Recent studies showed that a clinically significant proportion of active pulmonary tuberculosis cases, particularly among elderly populations, presented with nonspecific or asymptomatic manifestations, substantially compromising the sensitivity of conventional PCF approaches [7, 8]. In this context, mass screening such as using chest radiography has gained prominence as a viable case-finding modality in resource-constrained settings, owing to its optimal balance between diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for case identification [9–11].

Since 2021, we have conducted three annual rounds of active pulmonary tuberculosis case detection using Chest X-ray (CXR) screening among elderly populations in Lanxi City, China. This study aims to evaluate the difference between community-based CXR screening and concurrent symptom-driven group at both the individual and population levels. It would help identify effective patient demographic profiles of CXR screening for further optimal screening intervention and clarify key target populations for screening in elderly communities as well.

Methods

Setting

During 2021–2023, three rounds of CXR screening for tuberculosis among all community-dwelling elderly residents (aged ≥ 60 years, with median age of approximately 70 years) in Lanxi City, Zhejiang Province were performed. Lanxi City, located in eastern Zhejiang Province, was a high-burden area of tuberculosis. Elderly individuals aged over 60 years accounted for 27% of the total population. The tuberculosis notification rate among this elderly population was 205 per 100,000 in 2020, and the prevalence of latent tuberculosis infection among those aged over 65 years reached 26.78% [12]. The serial mass chest X-ray screenings were implemented in 16 local community health service centers. The location was presented in Figure S1.

Study design

A previous study described the similar study design of the first round of CXR screening in community [13]. The screening period for each round was one year, targeting local elderly individuals aged 60 and above. Each round of mass screening covered two stages: preliminary community mobilization and CXR screening in local community health centers. CXR screening underwent two rounds of review by community-based radiologists and peers from higher-level institutions. Presumptive tuberculosis cases were referred to designated hospitals for further evaluation. We defined tuberculosis cases identified through community-based CXR screening as presumptive tuberculosis cases detected through CXR screening in local community and confirmed via referral within three months after this specific mass screening. Symptom-driven tuberculosis cases in this study referred to pulmonary tuberculosis patients aged 60 or above who were notified in Lanxi City from 2021 to 2023 after seeking care at designated hospitals due to tuberculosis-related symptoms. Information on these patients was first obtained from the tuberculosis-specific reporting data in the China Disease Control and Prevention Information System and subsequently verified through field checks at designated hospitals to confirm symptom-driven Tuberculosis cases. Two scenarios were excluded: (1) tuberculosis cases who did not participate in community-based active screening but were identified through routine physical examinations at local general hospitals and subsequently referred. (2) tuberculosis cases who were diagnosed during medical examinations or hospitalization for other diseases. For both community-based mass screening and symptom-driven tuberculosis cases identification, field verification at designated hospitals was conducted to ultimately confirm diagnosis and patient-reported information, while relevant variables were collected. All diagnosed pulmonary tuberculosis cases received standardized treatment regimens, and their closed contacts received comprehensive interventions in accordance with national guidelines.

Besides, the annual surveillance data for individuals with prior tuberculosis were constructed based on the time interval between treatment completion or cure with case closure in the Tuberculosis Surveillance System and their subsequent re-diagnosis with tuberculosis. The annual surveillance data on tuberculosis among diabetic patients were derived from the time interval between the patient’s initial diagnosis in the diabetes surveillance system and the diagnosis of tuberculosis in its specific surveillance system.

Study definitions

In this study, we classified patient detection methods into two categories: community-based CXR screening and symptom-based case presentation. The former referred to cases identified through systematic community-based CXR screening of presumptive individuals, followed by referral and diagnostic confirmation. Symptom-based case presentation was defined as tuberculosis cases detected when patients proactively sought medical care due to TB-related symptoms. It should be noted that among passively detected cases, those identified incidentally—for instance, through routine hospitalization check-ups—were excluded from the definition of symptom-based case presentation in this study. The definition of bacteriologically confirmed and clinically diagnosed pulmonary tuberculosis cases in both groups referred to the Diagnostic Criteria for Pulmonary Tuberculosis (WS 288–2017) in China [14]. Smoking statuses were categorized into 3 groups as nonsmoker, former smoker, and current smoker. Former smokers were defined as people with a previous history of smoking but now quit; current smokers were defined as people who consumed any tobacco product within the past 30 days and had a lifetime cigarette use of 100 or more. Alcohol use was also categorized into 3 subgroups as nondrinkers, occasional drinkers who drink less than twice a week, and frequent drinkers who drink at least twice a week [12]. Body mass index (BMI) was generally categorized according to Asian-specific criteria as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–23.9 kg/m²), and overweight (≥ 24 kg/m²) [15]. People with a prior history of tuberculosis were defined as having any tuberculosis treatment experience including completed course or cured before this round of tuberculosis treatment [16]. Previous tuberculosis treatment records or diabetes diagnosis records were obtained through the online specialized illnesses reporting system or local patient self-reports.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Categorical variables were presented using frequencies/percentages, and the Chi-square (χ²) test and Fisher’s Exact Test were used to compare the differences between subgroups in both groups. Binary logistic regression identified factors for patients’ difference between CXR screening group and symptom-driven tuberculosis group, calculating adjusted ORs and 95% CIs, with P < 0.05 indicating significance. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to explore the potential nonlinear or linear association between the continuous variables and the ratio of the CXR mass screening group to the symptom-driven group [17]. Data processing and analysis were performed using R 4.5.

Results

General features of tuberculosis cases identification in both community-based CXR screening group and symptom-driven group

From 2021 to 2023, we conducted three rounds of screening in the Lanxi area, Zhejiang Province. The first round involved 49,339 participants, the second round involved 78,121 participants, and the third round involved 87,488 participants. A total of 374 active pulmonary tuberculosis cases were identified through mass screening in these rounds. Among the 374 tuberculosis cases detected, 278 were bacteriologically positive, accounting for 74.33%. In comparison, during the same period, the symptom-driven group identified 457 tuberculosis patients aged 60 and above, with a bacteriological positivity rate of 87.1% (398/457). A comparison of demographic characteristics between the two groups revealed that the community-based CXR screening group had a higher proportion of tuberculosis identification in the 60–69 age group, while the average age of this specific group was lower than that of the symptom-driven group. Additionally, the community-based screening group identified more tuberculosis cases with drinking habits. In terms of symptoms, cases in this group showed less pronounced symptoms such as any cough, cough lasting more than two weeks, expectoration, chest pain, fever, and appetite loss compared to the symptom-driven group. Restricted cubic spline analysis for age and BMI

Regarding the continuous variables of age and BMI, we further explored the relationship between these variations and the ratio of tuberculosis case finding through community-based CXR screening to symptom-driven case finding, respectively. For the RCS analysis, age showed a highly significant nonlinear association with the odds ratio (overall P < 0.001, nonlinear P < 0.001). As shown in Fig. 1, between the ages of 60 and 66, the odds ratio increased rapidly with age, while after age 75, the odds ratio gradually declined. Besides, the multivariable-adjusted RCS model revealed no significant association between BMI and the odds ratio of two modes (overall P = 0.219, nonlinear P = 0.205). As illustrated in Figure S2, across the entire BMI distribution range, the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) estimates were close to 1.0, and their 95% confidence intervals consistently included the null value.

Multifactorial analysis of community-based CXR screening versus symptom-driven group

Further multivariate analysis revealed that patients in the older age group (age ≥ 80), females, and those with bacteriologically confirmed tuberculosis were more susceptible to being identified through symptom-driven group (Fig. 2). Additionally, patients presenting with symptoms such as cough, hemoptysis, chest pain, fever, and appetite loss were more likely to be diagnosed with tuberculosis via symptom-driven pathways. Conversely, symptoms including cough lasting more than two weeks, expectoration, night sweats, and weight loss were consistently observed in both the community-based screening group and the symptom-driven group.

Population differences in tuberculosis case detection between community-based CXR screening and symptom-driven group

At the population level, we compared the yield of tuberculosis detection between serial CXR screenings and concurrent symptom-driven presentations. In the first round, the CXR screening identified significantly more active pulmonary tuberculosis than the symptom-driven group with risk ratio (RR) 1.63 and its 95% CI 1.28 to 2.07. By the second round, no significant difference was observed between the two models. However, in the third round, the CXR screening group was associated with significantly lower tuberculosis detection among older adults compared with the symptom-driven group (RR = 0.70, 95% CI 0.55–0.89). Sex-specific analyses revealed that the overall trend among male participants was consistent with the general population, whereas no statistically significant differences were detected between both groups among females across any round. When stratified by age, the CXR screening group detected significantly more tuberculosis cases in the 60–69 year age group than the symptom-driven identification in the first two rounds; however, this difference was no longer significant in the third round. Among participants aged 70–80 years, the symptom-driven group outperformed the CXR screening group in the latter two rounds. Among individuals with a history of diabetes, the CXR screening improved tuberculosis detection across all rounds compared with the symptom-driven group. Among those with prior tuberculosis, the CXR screening exhibited a gradually increasing advantage in tuberculosis case detection across screening rounds, with a marginally higher detection yield than the symptom-driven presentation in the second round and a statistically significant superiority in the third round of mass screening. Analysis of surveillance data for populations with prior tuberculosis or diabetes

We further examined the surveillance data on prior tuberculosis group and diabetes group. Among elderly patients in Lanxi City who completed the tuberculosis treatment course and were cured between 2014 and 2020, 47 cases developed tuberculosis again within the following five years. Notably, 65.96% (31/47) of these recurrence cases occurred within two years after completing the treatment. Additionally, among elderly patients diagnosed with diabetes, 121 developed tuberculosis within five years of their diabetes diagnosis. Of these, 65.29% (79/121) occurred within the first two years after diabetes diagnosis. It was shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

In this study, we compared case detection via community CXR screening with those found through symptom-driven presentations. RCS analysis clarified the key individual attributes in improving the effectiveness of CXR screening among the elderly. Meanwhile, our findings revealed significant differences in the demographic characteristics and clinical manifestations of tuberculosis identified by both strategies, providing a more detailed profile of targeted objectives by CXR screening. Finally, from a population perspective along with historical surveillance data, our findings indicated that active screening should be prioritized or intensified for individuals within two years following completion of tuberculosis treatment or cure, as well as for those within two years after a diabetes diagnosis. Our findings offer important data support and decision-making basis for optimizing tuberculosis control strategies in the elderly population, particularly for implementing targeted screening in high-risk subgroups.

RCS analysis revealed a significant non-linear relationship between age and case finding in different modes. Between the ages of 60 and 66, the odds ratio of patients in active cases findings increased rapidly, indicating that the relative yield of CXR screening improved with age during this interval. Some reasons might explain this result. Within this age interval, they maintained a certain level of social mobility and possessed discretionary time after retirement, resulting in higher accessibility to community-based screening [18]; meanwhile, the clinical symptoms of tuberculosis in the old population tended to be more insidious or atypical [19–21]. CXR screening could increase the chance of detecting more “hidden cases” and simultaneously raise the possibility of detecting more asymptomatic or atypical tuberculosis cases [7, 12]. This makes it a critical window for achieving early tuberculosis control. Consequently, investing community-based CXR screening resources in this group yielded incremental marginal returns with each additional year of age. After the age of 75, the odds ratio gradually declined, implying that case findings from community-based CXR screening diminished in the older population. This shift may be explained by some reasons below. Firstly, the accessibility of community-based CXR screening declined with age. Older adults faced physical functional decline and mobility challenges, making it difficult to participate in centralized community screening [7]; secondly, their frequent medical visits due to multiple comorbidities increased the likelihood of tuberculosis detection during routine care, thereby partially offsetting the relative benefits of mass screening [22, 23]. This indicated that for individuals aged 75 and above, CXR screening strategies in community required corresponding adjustments. That is to say, sole reliance on centralized CXR screening for tuberculosis in this specific age group may face limited yield, and alternative approaches such as door-to-door screening and integration into multi-chronic disease co-management should be explored to improve tuberculosis detection among the elderly population [24–27].

A notable negative finding in our study among the elderly subgroup was that BMI was not a predictor of the suitable pathway through which tuberculosis cases should be detected. From the perspective of optimizing resource allocation, prioritizing case finding strategies based on BMI may be inefficient in the elderly population. Whether driven by symptom‑based healthcare-seeking or active participation in mass screening, the decision‑making process among older adults was likely influenced more by factors such as symptom perception, functional status, and socioeconomic determinants, rather than anthropometric indices alone [28, 29]. In future, greater emphasis should be placed on the relative importance of refined age stratification and comorbidity management in guiding targeted screening. Concurrently, we should explore efficient indicators, particularly behavioral and social determinants, to better differentiate the pathways of patient identification.

Third, multivariate analysis further identified important independent factors influencing the pathways through which patients were detected. Older age group (greater than 80 years), female gender, bacteriologically confirmed positivity, and the presence of typical symptoms such as cough, hemoptysis, chest pain, fever, and appetite loss all significantly increased the likelihood of patients being detected through the symptom-driven pathway. These findings align with clinical observations and suggested that, for specific subgroups such as senior women, it should strengthen tuberculosis awareness campaigns, enhance their vigilance and proactive healthcare-seeking behavior toward atypical symptoms [29, 30]. It also helped to reduce diagnostic delays [31]. Although not an unbiased approach to detecting asymptomatic tuberculosis, community-based mass screening using CXR played an indispensable role in identifying individuals with suggestive tuberculosis—particularly when patients presented only with atypical or minimal symptoms [32–35].

At the population level, stratified annual comparisons across different subgroups revealed dynamic variations in the effectiveness of screening strategies. Overall, the detection rate of the first round of community-based CXR screening was significantly higher than that of symptom-driven detection. However, as the number of screening rounds increased, its effectiveness gradually declined and reversed by the third round. This may reflect the substantial “depletion” of the pool of tuberculosis cases among individuals inclined to participate in mass screening after multiple rounds caused by self-selection bias [36]. Additionally, the possible decline in tuberculosis cases within the age groups, changes in population structure, and the potential impact of screening during the COVID-19 period may all have played a role. Notably, the benefits of CXR mass screening existed in some specific subgroups such as individuals with diabetes and individuals with prior tuberculosis. Regarding the latter, CXR mass screening demonstrated higher detection rates than symptom-driven group starting from the second round. These findings are further validated by local surveillance data. About 65% of TB recurrences in cured patients and new TB cases among diabetic patients happened in the first two years after treatment or diagnosis. Thus, both high-risk groups should intensify more comprehensive interventions such as regularly collecting sputum samples and concentratedly implementing tongue swab screening [37, 38]. It could facilitate early detection and treatment of recurrent or new tuberculosis cases, thereby curbing transmission and improving patient prognosis.

Some limitations should be mentioned. Firstly, the symptoms reported in some patients’ chief complaint during their initial registration in designated hospitals may be inconsistent with those reported during the mass screening, potentially due to participants' stigma at the screening site or improper questioning techniques by community healthcare workers. Although we adjusted it based on chief complaints, potential bias may still exist. Secondly, due to the insidious onset of diabetes, we used the date of the patient’s first diabetes diagnosis in the monitoring data, which may have some impact on the subsequent duration of tuberculosis onset.

In conclusion, within the context of a high-burden city in Eastern China, a community-based CXR screening can effectively identify tuberculosis cases with mild or asymptomatic status compared with symptom-driven case detection at the individual level. Meanwhile, the current mass screening protocol should be maintained for the younger elderly group to improve case detection, while more convenient and precise services should be developed for the very elderly population. At the population level, elderly individuals with diabetes or a history of tuberculosis should be prioritized as core screening populations, with an intensified monitoring mechanism established within two years post-treatment or post-diagnosis.
