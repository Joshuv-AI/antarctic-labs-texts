Immune-related adverse event profiles and survival outcomes in non–small cell lung cancer: a cluster analysis from a long-term real-world study
Authors: Yijiao Xu, Zhisheng Chen, Haiyan Chen, Jiaxin Liu, Jianying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune-related adverse events (irAEs) are heterogeneous in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). This study aimed to characterize post-treatment irAE phenotypes and explore their temporal and survival associations.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 202 patients with pathologically confirmed NSCLC who received at least two cycles of ICI therapy. Mixed irAE features were integrated using factor analysis of mixed data (FAMD) and clustered by K-means. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier and landmark analyses. Time-dependent Cox models were used to account for delayed irAE-cluster formation, and Fine–Gray models evaluated baseline predictors of irAE occurrence with death treated as a competing event.ResultsFAMD-based clustering identified five descriptive post-treatment irAE phenotypes: multiorgan non-hepatic/gastrointestinal, hepatic/gastrointestinal-enriched multiorgan, irAE-negative, other/atypical, and thyroid-dominant endocrine phenotypes. Conventional Kaplan–Meier analysis showed longer observed OS in patients with any irAE than in those without irAEs (median OS, 30.5 vs. 20.0 months; hazard ratio, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.47–0.99). However, survival patterns differed across irAE phenotypes, and PFS did not differ significantly by irAE occurrence or across clusters. In time-dependent Cox analyses, the hepatic/gastrointestinal-enriched multiorgan phenotype was consistently associated with poorer subsequent OS, highlighting heterogeneity beyond overall irAE occurrence. Fine–Gray analyses identified radiotherapy and older age as baseline factors associated with a higher cumulative incidence of irAEs. Cluster stability analyses showed acceptable overall reproducibility, although smaller clusters required cautious interpretation.ConclusionsData-driven irAE phenotypes captured heterogeneity in the timing and organ distribution of immune toxicity among ICI-treated NSCLC patients. Background

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, and the overall prognosis for patients with advanced or recurrent disease remains unsatisfactory [1, 2]. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have substantially improved survival outcomes in patients with locally advanced and metastatic NSCLC and have become an integral component of treatment strategies [3]. Monotherapy or combination regimens targeting programmed death receptor 1/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) have demonstrated durable responses and significant overall survival benefits across multiple clinical trials [4–6].

ICIs exert their antitumor effects by releasing T-cell inhibition and activating antitumor immune responses; however, they can also trigger immune-related adverse events (irAEs), which frequently involve multiple organ systems, including the skin, gastrointestinal tract, endocrine glands, lungs, and liver [7]. Although most irAEs are reversible with appropriate management, a minority of severe or visceral toxicities may lead to permanent discontinuation of therapy or even death [8, 9]. With the ongoing expansion of ICIs indications and increasing adoption of combination regimens, the precise identification and risk-stratified management of irAEs has emerged as a critical challenge in clinical practice.

Previous studies have suggested that the occurrence of irAEs is often associated with higher objective response rates and prolonged survival in patients with NSCLC, and is considered, to some extent, a surrogate marker of effective immune activation [10, 11]. However, their prognostic significance is influenced by multiple factors, including toxicity grade [11, 12], number of irAEs [13], involved organs [14, 15] and timing of onset [16]. Current research on the prognostic implications of irAEs is gradually shifting from a simplistic dichotomy based on their presence or absence toward more refined stratification incorporating organ specificity and toxicity grading. Nevertheless, systematic investigations into the overall toxicity patterns formed by combinations of multi-organ and multi-grade irAEs, as well as the resulting prognostic heterogeneity, remain relatively limited [17].

Against this background, the present study was conducted in a cohort of patients with advanced or recurrent NSCLC receiving ICI therapy. An unsupervised clustering approach was applied to construct immune-toxicity phenotypes based on multiple irAE domains, including organ involvement, number of events, and severity. Clinical and laboratory characteristics were compared across different phenotypes, and their associations with overall survival were evaluated. In addition, the relationships between baseline patient characteristics and the occurrence of irAEs were explored. This study aims to provide new evidence to support risk stratification and individualized management of NSCLC patients undergoing ICI treatment.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study included patients with pathologically confirmed NSCLC who received ICI therapy at our tertiary hospital between January 2020 and December 2024.

Eligible patients met the following inclusion criteria: (1) histologically or cytologically confirmed NSCLC; (2) disease stage IV, unresectable stage III, or postoperative recurrence confirmed by imaging; (3) receipt of at least two cycles of ICI-based treatment (including PD-1, PD-L1, or CTLA-4 inhibitors, alone or in combination); and (4) completion of at least one radiologic efficacy evaluation after treatment initiation.

Patients were excluded if they: (1) had incomplete medical or laboratory records; (2) lost to follow-up within 3 months after ICI initiation; (3) had severe baseline hepatic or renal dysfunction; or (4) participated in other interventional clinical trials during the same period.

The first ICIs administration was defined as the index date (baseline) for all analyses.

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No. B2025-026). The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and anonymized data processing.

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records using standardized case-report forms. Collected variables included, demographics: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), and smoking history; clinical characteristics: tumor type, disease stage, ECOG performance status (PS), PD-L1 expression, treatment line, radiotherapy, target or chemotherapy, and comorbidities (hypertension(HBP), diabetes mellitus (DM), coronary artery disease (CAD), pulmonary disease); laboratory parameters: baseline hematologic and biochemical indices (WBC, neutrophil (Neu), lymphocyte (Lym), monocyte counts (MO), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), C-reactive protein (CRP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH); nutritional indices: nutrition risk screening (NRS) score, etc.

Immune-related adverse events (irAEs): type, organ involvement, onset time, and grade (per Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [CTCAE] v5.0) [18]. All variables were reviewed independently by two investigators to ensure consistency, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Follow-up, efficacy and prognosis assessment

Patients were followed from the initiation of ICI therapy until death or the last clinical contact. Toxicity was assessed every 3–4 weeks and imaging/tumor marker evaluations were conducted every 2 months. Follow-up information was obtained through inpatient and outpatient medical records, structured telephone interviews, and death registry verification. Treatment efficacy was evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 [19] (RECIST 1.1). Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from ICI initiation to death from any cause or last follow-up, and progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from ICI initiation to disease progression, death, or last follow-up. IrAEs were identified from clinical documentation and graded based on the Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC) [20]and American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) [21] Guideline. The primary endpoint was OS. Secondary endpoints included PFS and the occurrence and temporal dynamics of irAEs. Data collection and follow-up were conducted through July 31, 2025.

Statistical analysis

To characterize the heterogeneity of irAEs, factor analysis of mixed data (FAMD) was applied to integrate mixed irAE-related variables, including maximum irAE grade, number of involved organs/systems, and organ-system involvement patterns. FAMD is a dimension-reduction method suitable for datasets containing both continuous and categorical variables [22]. In this analysis, “Dim” refers to the latent dimensions generated by FAMD, which summarize the main variation patterns in the mixed irAE data. The number of retained dimensions was defined by eigenvalues > 1 and scree-plot inspection. The retained FAMD dimensions were then subjected to K-means clustering. The number of clusters was determined based on elbow and silhouette analyses together with clinical interpretability, yielding distinct immune-toxicity phenotypes. Cluster robustness was evaluated using sensitivity and stability analyses. Clustering was repeated with different numbers of retained FAMD dimensions, and agreement with the main solution was assessed using the adjusted Rand index (ARI). Bootstrap resampling was used to estimate cluster-specific Jaccard stability, and repeated 70:30 split-sample analyses were performed to assess reproducibility across random data partitions. Sensitivity to the number of clusters was also examined across candidate K values. These analyses were used to assess the reliability of the FAMD-derived clustering structure and potential small-cluster instability.

Baseline characteristics were compared among clusters using the χ² or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables (presented as n [%]) and the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables (expressed as median (Q1:1st Quartile, Q₃: 3rd Quartile)). Organ-specific irAE distributions were visualized with clustered heatmaps, and global differences were assessed by χ² or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. OS was estimated by the Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve and compared using the log-rank test. To address immortal-time bias, landmark analyses were conducted at 3 and 6 months. Density plots and cumulative incidence curves illustrated temporal patterns of irAEs onset.

The prognostic relevance of immune-toxicity phenotypes was evaluated using time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models, in which the original irAE clusters were modeled as time-updated exposures rather than baseline-fixed covariates. A start–stop data structure was constructed. All patients initially contributed person-time to the no-irAE and were assigned to their corresponding final cluster only after the predefined exposure-entry time. In the primary analysis, exposure entry was defined as the time when the complete cluster phenotype had formed. Sensitivity analyses were performed using the first irAE onset time and the second involved organ-system time as alternative exposure-entry definitions. The models were adjusted for age, targeted therapy, disease stage, ECOG-PS, treatment line, radiotherapy history, NRS, and baseline ALB. Disease stage, ECOG-PS, and treatment line were modeled as categorical variables. Robust standard errors clustered by patient ID were used to account for multiple observation intervals contributed by the same patient. Results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models were used to evaluate baseline predictors of irAE occurrence, with death before irAE treated as a competing event. Candidate variables were first screened using univariable Fine–Gray models before multivariable modeling. Disease stage and immunotherapy treatment line were prespecified clinical covariates and were forced into the multivariable model. Treatment exposure was further assessed using ICI treatment cycles as an alternative analysis time scale. Results were reported as subdistribution hazard ratios (SHRs) with 95% CIs. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.3). Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons where appropriate.

Results

Identification of immune-toxicity phenotypes by FAMD-based clustering

This study ultimately included clinical data from 202 patients, with a median follow-up time of 17.3(11.0, 29.4) months.

To delineate the heterogeneity of irAEs, FAMD was performed to integrate both categorical and continuous irAE-related features, followed by K-means clustering. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the first two FAMD dimensions accounted for 35.0% of the total variance (Dim1: 23.2%; Dim2: 11.7%), summarizing the major variation patterns in the mixed irAE data. Dim1 primarily reflected irAE count and maximum irAE grade, corresponding to the global intensity of immune toxicity, whereas Dim2 was mainly driven by organ-specific irAEs, particularly thyroid, hepatic/renal, and gastrointestinal events (Fig. 1B). These axes respectively represented toxicity severity and pattern.

FAMD-based clustering of immune toxicity phenotypes. A Scree plot showing explained variance of each dimension; B Variable contribution map of irAE features on the first two FAMD axes; C Five immune-toxicity clusters identified by K-means, with Cluster 3 representing the irAE-negative group

Clustering based on the first five FAMD dimensions identified five distinct immune-toxicity phenotypes (Fig. 1C):

Cluster 1 was labeled as the multiorgan phenotype without hepatic/GI enrichment(non-visceral), characterized by multisystem irAE involvement without enrichment of hepatic–renal or gastrointestinal toxicity. Cluster 2 was labeled as the hepatic/GI-enriched multiorgan irAE phenotype(visceral enriched), reflecting multisystem toxicity with a higher frequency of hepatic–renal and gastrointestinal events. Cluster 3 was defined as the irAE-negative phenotype, with no observed irAEs across organ systems and therefore served as the reference phenotype. Cluster 4 was labeled as the other/atypical irAE phenotype, predominantly characterized by less common or atypical immune manifestations classified as “other-type” events. Cluster 5 was labeled as the thyroid-dominant endocrine irAE phenotype, with thyroid involvement accounting for the majority of events. These labels were assigned descriptively to improve readability and should not be interpreted as biologically definitive subtypes.

Collectively, the FAMD-based clustering revealed heterogeneous immune-toxicity patterns shaped by both irAE burden and organ-involvement profiles. The irAE-negative Cluster 3 emerged as a clearly separable subgroup, supporting the clinical interpretability of this clustering framework and forming the foundation for subsequent outcome analyses.

Organ-specific immune-toxicity patterns across clusters

To further characterize these phenotypes, the distribution of organ-specific irAEs was compared among the five clusters (Fig. 2). The heatmap (Fig. 2A) visualized the proportional representation of each irAE type, and the bar plot (Fig. 2B) identified those with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05).

Organ-specific distribution of immune-related adverse events across clusters. A Heatmap showing the proportion of each irAE type in five FAMD-derived clusters, with statistical significance indicated (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001); B Bar plot summarizing significant between-cluster differences

Between-cluster variation was highly significant across multiple irAE categories. These findings suggest that irAE heterogeneity was not limited to severity, but also involved distinct organ-involvement profiles across clusters.

Cluster-specific baseline, irAE, and laboratory characteristics

Among 202 participants, the distribution was: the multiorgan phenotype without hepatic/GI enrichment (n = 41, 20.3%), the hepatic/GI-enriched multiorgan irAE phenotype (n = 25, 12.4%), irAE-negative phenotype (n = 111, 55.0%), the other/atypical irAE phenotype (n = 9, 4.5%), and the thyroid-dominant endocrine irAE phenotype(n = 16, 7.9%).

Demographic and hematological variables (age, WBC, Neu, Lym, MO, ALB, GLB, CRP, LDH, BMI) were comparable across groups (P > 0.05). Similarly, baseline clinical parameters—including gender, smoking, tumor type, stage, PD-L1 expression, ECOG performance score, comorbidities, and nutritional status—did not differ significantly. Significant differences were observed in radiotherapy history (P = 0.018) and multiple irAE features, including AEcount, maximum grade, and specific organ toxicities (all P < 0.001, except cardiac P = 0.005; Table 1).

Overall survival according to irAE occurrence and immune-toxicity phenotypes

In conventional KM analyses: patients experiencing any irAE had significantly longer OS than those without irAEs (mOS 30.5 vs. 20.0 months; HR 0.68, 95% CI [0.47–0.99]; P = 0.040; Fig. 3A), suggesting a potential association between irAE occurrence and favorable survival in this cohort.

Overall survival according to irAE occurrence and immune-toxicity clusters. A KM curves comparing OS between patients with and without irAEs (HR = 0.68, P = 0.040). B OS among five immune-toxicity clusters derived from FAMD analysis (global log-rank P = 0.029). Cluster 2 showed the shortest median OS among the five phenotypes; although it was not significantly different from Cluster 3, it was significantly shorter than Cluster 1 in descriptive pairwise comparison(adjusted P=0.018)

When stratified by immune-toxicity cluster (Fig. 3B), OS differed significantly among the five groups (global log-rank P = 0.029). Pairwise log-rank comparisons were then performed using Cluster 3, the irAE-negative phenotype, as the reference group. Compared with Cluster 3, Cluster 1, representing the multiorgan phenotype without hepatic/GI enrichment, showed significantly longer OS after correction (mOS 45.1 months, 95% CI [27.0 – NA]; raw P = 0.008; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.032). In contrast, Cluster2, the hepatic/GI-enriched multiorgan phenotype; Cluster 4, the other/atypical phenotype; and Cluster 5, the thyroid-dominant endocrine phenotype did not differ significantly from Cluster 3 after correction (Table 2).

Temporal heterogeneity of irAEs and their survival implications

Landmark analyses were performed to reduce immortal-time bias when evaluating the association between irAE occurrence and subsequent OS (Fig. 4A–B). At the 3-month landmark, patients who had developed irAEs showed longer subsequent OS (P = 0.044), whereas the association was attenuated and did not reach statistical significance at the 6-month landmark (P = 0.11).

Landmark analyses of overall survival (OS) according to irAE occurrence. Patients who experienced irAEs (“Yes”, red lines) demonstrated significantly longer OS compared with those without irAEs (“No”, teal lines) at the 3-month landmark (A), while the survival difference remained favorable but not statistically significant at the 6-month (B)

Temporal analysis of the FAMD-derived irAE phenotypes showed heterogeneous onset patterns across clusters (Fig. 5A–B). The median irAE onset times were 6.4 months for the multiorgan non-hepatic/GI-enriched phenotype phenotype, 5.5 months for the hepatic/GI-enriched multiorgan phenotype, 7.6 months for the other/atypical phenotype, and 3.5 months for the thyroid-dominant endocrine phenotype. Consistent with the density distributions, the cumulative incidence curves showed relatively earlier irAE accumulation in the hepatic/GI-enriched multiorgan and thyroid-dominant endocrine phenotypes, whereas the multiorgan non-hepatic/GI-enriched phenotype and other/atypical phenotypes displayed broader and more delayed onset patterns. These findings suggest that irAE timing and organ distribution may help characterize heterogeneous post-treatment immune-toxicity profiles. Because these phenotypes were defined using post-baseline irAE events, these temporal patterns were considered descriptive and exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in view of potential survivorship and treatment-exposure effects.

As an additional secondary outcome, PFS was analyzed according to overall irAE occurrence and irAE clusters. No significant PFS difference was observed between patients with and without irAEs (log-rank P = 0.420; HR, 0.878; 95% CI, 0.638–1.209), or across the five irAE clusters (log-rank P = 0.409), Supplementary FigureS1.

Prognostic relevance of immune-toxicity clusters in time-dependent analysis

To address the time-dependent nature of irAE occurrence and reduce post-baseline information leakage, the original immune-toxicity clusters were analyzed as time-updated exposures in Cox regression. In the primary analysis, exposure entry was defined as the time when the complete cluster phenotype had formed. Before cluster formation, patients contributed person-time to the no-irAE state, which served as the reference state.

As shown in Table 3, after adjustment for age, targeted therapy, disease stage, performance status, treatment line, radiotherapy, NRS, and baseline ALB, Cluster 2 was significantly associated with poorer subsequent OS compared with the no-irAE state (adjusted HR = 2.91, 95% CI: 1.72–4.92, P < 0.001). In contrast, neither Cluster 1 nor Cluster 4 was significantly associated with OS (Cluster 1: adjusted HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 0.69–2.32, p = 0.445; Cluster 4: adjusted HR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.33–2.10, p = 0.695). Cluster 5 showed a numerically increased mortality risk, but this association did not reach statistical significance (adjusted HR = 1.84, 95% CI: 0.90–3.75, P = 0.093).

Fine–Gray competing risk model for irAE occurrence

A Fine–Gray competing-risk analysis was performed to identify baseline predictors of irAE occurrence, with death before irAE treated as a competing event. Candidate variables were first screened using univariable Fine–Gray models, and radiotherapy, age, CRP, and GLB were selected for multivariable modeling according to the prespecified screening criterion. Disease stage and immunotherapy treatment line were forced into the multivariable model as prespecified clinical covariates to account for disease burden and baseline treatment context (Fig. 6).

In the multivariable Fine–Gray model, prior or concurrent radiotherapy (SHR = 2.119, 95% CI 1.290–3.480, P = 0.003) and older age (SHR = 1.306, 95% CI 1.037–1.645, P = 0.023) were associated with a higher cumulative incidence of irAEs, whereas CRP, GLB, stage IV disease, and further-line immunotherapy were not significant after adjustment. Treatment exposure in the Fine–Gray framework was further assessed by using the number of ICI treatment cycles as an alternative analysis time scale, rather than by directly adjusting for cumulative cycles as a conventional covariate.

Sensitivity and stability analyses

Sensitivity and stability analyses were performed on the FAMD-derived five-cluster solution. ARI values remained high across 2–8 retained FAMD dimensions, ranging from 0.834 to 1.000. In 100 repeated 70:30 split-sample analyses, the validation ARI ranged from 0.777 to 1.000, with a median of 0.943. Bootstrap analysis showed that two clusters were stable and three were moderately stable, with no cluster showing a mean Jaccard similarity below 0.60 (Supplementary Table S1, Figure S2). These findings supported the overall reproducibility of the clustering structure, although smaller clusters were interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

In this long-term follow-up study of NSCLC patients receiving immunotherapy, unsupervised clustering identified five clinically heterogeneous immune-toxicity phenotypes characterized by distinct patterns of organ involvement, event burden, and severity. In the Kaplan–Meier analysis and the 3-month landmark analysis, the occurrence of any irAE showed a borderline association with prolonged survival, which was broadly consistent with several previous studies suggesting that irAE development may be linked to better outcomes in patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors [11, 12, 23]. However, this pattern changed after accounting for the time-dependent nature of irAE occurrence. In the time-dependent Cox analysis, different toxicity phenotypes showed distinct associations with subsequent OS. The cluster enriched for hepatic/GI-enriched multiorgan irAE phenotype was most consistently associated with poorer subsequent OS, whereas the multiorgan phenotype without hepatic/GI enrichment, the other/atypical irAE phenotype, and the cluster predominantly characterized by mild thyroid and thyroid-dominant endocrine irAE phenotype did not differ significantly from the no-irAE.

Previous studies in NSCLC have shown that the prognostic impact of irAEs may vary by affected organ system, toxicity severity, and treatment context [23–25]. By integrating organ involvement, event burden, and severity into a phenotype-based framework, our study extends these observations. In our cohort, the visceral-enriched phenotype was associated with poorer subsequent OS, whereas other non-visceral-enriched or relatively mild irAE phenotypes did not show a similar increase in risk. Similarly, target trial emulation evidence in metastatic urothelial cancer showed that the apparent survival advantage of irAEs was no longer evident after immortal-time bias was addressed [26]. These findings suggest that the prognostic information carried by irAEs may depend more on toxicity phenotype than on their simple presence or absence.

From a temporal perspective, the occurrence of irAEs may also convey prognostic information. Previous studies have indicated that most irAEs arise within weeks to months after treatment initiation and that severe toxicities often occur relatively early [27–29]. In the present study, time-density analysis showed distinct onset patterns across toxicity phenotypes. Clusters 1 and 4 tended to occur later in the treatment course, with broader and more gradual distributions, whereas Clusters 2 and 5 showed early concentrated onset patterns. Considering the observed survival outcomes, these findings allow us to propose the hypothesis that early-onset visceral irAEs may serve as a marker of a clinically unfavorable immune-toxicity phenotype. Although treatment interruption, corticosteroid exposure, and second-line immunosuppression were not fully captured or formally modeled in this analysis, early-onset visceral irAEs such as hepatitis, renal injury, and gastrointestinal toxicity may reflect greater systemic inflammatory vulnerability and more complex toxicity management needs, which could partly explain their association with poorer subsequent survival.

This inference is generally consistent with the findings of several recent studies. Zablonski et al. reported in a real-world melanoma cohort that the overall presence of irAEs was not consistently associated with improved survival, whereas grade ≥ 3 irAEs and immune-related hepatitis were associated with higher mortality risk [30]. Studies focusing on hepatic and gastrointestinal irAEs have further shown that early-onset or higher-grade immune-related hepatitis may be associated with poorer outcomes, while ICI-related colitis often requires prolonged corticosteroid therapy, infliximab, or other immunosuppressive agents, particularly in patients with more severe histologic inflammation [31, 32]. Overall, the prognostic implication of irAEs is not uniformly favorable, particularly after accounting for their time-dependent occurrence; early-onset or organ-specific irAEs, especially clinically significant visceral toxicities, may correlate with poorer survival. This association is biologically and clinically plausible. Immune checkpoint blockade has been proposed to disrupt peripheral immune tolerance and increase T-cell activity against normal tissues, contributing to inflammatory injury across multiple organ systems; when such injury involves vital visceral organs, it may result in substantial organ dysfunction, diagnostic complexity, treatment interruption, and the need for intensive immunosuppression [33]. Similar concerns have also been raised for other severe visceral irAEs, including checkpoint inhibitor pneumonitis, in which intensive immunosuppression and ICI discontinuation may compromise therapeutic benefit [34, 35]. From the therapeutic-management perspective, high corticosteroid peak dose and second-line immunosuppressants used for irAE treatment have also been associated with impaired survival, supporting the possibility that severe visceral toxicity and its management may partially offset the therapeutic benefit that irAEs would otherwise signify [36].

Based on the above findings, the immune-toxicity phenotyping framework established in this study may provide an exploratory framework for interpreting irAEs during follow-up. For patients who develop irAEs, assessment should not be limited to a binary “presence or absence” determination. Instead, a comprehensive evaluation incorporating the pattern of organ involvement, event burden, toxicity severity, and timing of onset is warranted. Toxicity profiles similar to Cluster 2—characterized by early-onset hepatic/GI-enriched multiorgan irAEs—were associated with shorter survival and may warrant closer monitoring and individualized toxicity management. In contrast, except for Cluster 2, the remaining immune-toxicity phenotypes did not show a favorable prognostic signal compared with the no-irAE, suggesting that non- hepatic/GI, atypical, or mild endocrine/dermatologic irAEs should not be simply interpreted as indicators of better prognosis. Finally, these findings support further exploration of irAE phenotypes as dynamic post-treatment indicators rather than definitive prognostic biomarkers. This may facilitate their integration with imaging assessments, blood-based biomarkers, and clinical scoring systems, thereby supporting a more comprehensive framework for follow-up and clinical decision-making. This approach may help clarify the balance between toxicity control and preservation of therapeutic efficacy.

This study also has important limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective analysis with a limited sample size and inherent heterogeneity in treatment regimens. Although the time-dependent Cox models were designed to reduce post-baseline information leakage and guarantee-time bias, residual confounding and selection bias cannot be fully excluded. In particular, differences in treatment duration, toxicity management, corticosteroid exposure, treatment interruption, and clinical decision-making may still have influenced the observed associations. The small number of cases in Clusters 4 and 5 also resulted in wide confidence intervals for the corresponding effect estimates. Second, the FAMD and clustering results were derived from variables specific to this cohort and have not yet been validated in external datasets; the optimal number of clusters and phenotype boundaries may differ across centers and populations. Finally, this study primarily included a single-center Chinese population receiving PD-1/PD-L1-based combination therapy, and caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to other treatment modalities, ethnic groups, or healthcare systems.

Objective response data were not consistently available in a standardized form; therefore, correlations between irAE phenotypes and objective response rate or disease control rate could not be reliably assessed.

Conclusions

This retrospective analysis of 202 patients with NSCLC treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors identified five descriptive post-treatment immune-toxicity phenotypes using factor analysis of mixed data combined with K-means clustering. These phenotypes showed substantial heterogeneity in organ-involvement patterns, timing of onset, and survival associations. Although irAE occurrence was associated with longer observed OS in conventional KM and landmark analyses, PFS did not differ significantly by irAE occurrence or across clusters, supporting a cautious interpretation of survival differences. After accounting for the time-dependent nature of irAE occurrence, the hepatic/GI-enriched multiorgan phenotype remained consistently associated with poorer subsequent OS, whereas the other immune-toxicity phenotypes did not show a clear favorable prognostic signal compared with the no-irAE state. Temporal analyses suggested earlier irAE accumulation in the hepatic/GI-enriched multiorgan and thyroid-dominant endocrine phenotypes, while the multiorgan non-hepatic/GI-enriched phenotype and other/atypical phenotypes showed broader and more delayed onset patterns. Fine–Gray competing-risk analyses indicated that radiotherapy exposure and older age were associated with a higher cumulative incidence of irAEs, including in treatment-cycle time-scale sensitivity analyses. Cluster stability analyses supported acceptable overall reproducibility, although smaller clusters required cautious interpretation. Overall, these findings suggest that integrating irAE organ distribution and timing may provide exploratory prognostic information in ICI-treated NSCLC. However, because irAE phenotypes were defined using post-baseline events and may be influenced by treatment exposure and survivorship, these results should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger, multicenter prospective studies.
