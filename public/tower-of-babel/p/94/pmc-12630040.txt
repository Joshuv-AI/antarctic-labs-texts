Numerical study of macroscopic thermal diodes: Influence of interface topography and contact resistance
Authors: Katja Vozel, Andrej Kitanovski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMacroscopic conductive solid-state thermal diodes, composed of two dissimilar materials, are approaching limits in rectification performance and operating range. Beyond material choice, alternative design strategies have been explored. This study numerically investigates two passive mechanisms affecting macroscopic solid-state thermal diodes: interface topographies between materials and thermal contact resistance at the interface. Results reveal key insights. First, while interface topographies have little effect on the rectification factor when contact resistance is negligible, they can extend the temperature range of rectification by up to 10 K. Second, a constant thermal contact resistance generally reduces rectification, but introducing temperature-dependent contact resistance at low temperatures can significantly enhance it. In one case, rectification improved from nearly zero to nearly unity. These findings provide guidelines for further research and optimization of solid-state thermal diodes for practical thermal management applications.

Introduction

Thermal diodes, also known as thermal rectifiers, enable passive control of heat flux. In most cases, thermal diodes are either fluidic or solid state.1,2 Fluidic thermal diodes, which regulate heat flow using a working fluid, come in various forms. Heat pipes are the most prominent type, leveraging phase change to achieve high performance, with rectification ratios over 170.3,4 Simpler single-phase devices serve passive heating or cooling in buildings.1 Other theoretical concepts include systems based on nanoscale particle flux,5 liquid crystals,6 and chemical reactions.7

Solid-state thermal diodes are appealing because their lack of moving parts makes them compact and reliable.8 They can be used in applications ranging from on-chip thermal control9 and nanoscale technologies such as thermal logic10,11 to macroscopic systems like building thermal management12 and power generation.13

Solid-state thermal diodes transfer heat via conduction or radiation. Radiative diodes often rely on phase-change materials (PCMs), limiting operation to temperatures near the PCM transition, while near-field devices require nanometer-scale separations.14,15

Conductive diodes have mainly been studied at the nanoscale,16,17,18 where effects like asymmetric scattering19 and interfacial phonon coupling20 enable rectification. Nanoscale asymmetries, such as selective doping,21 junction design,22,23 or geometric defects,24 yield devices a few nanometers to micrometers in size. The thermal biases used are below 100 K.18,19 Scaling to the macroscale is challenging because bulk effects dominate, and maintaining the desired behavior requires precise alignment of asymmetries across the entire heat flux area, increasing fabrication difficulty and cost.

Macroscopic conductive solid-state thermal diodes (MSTDs) consist of junctions between materials with differing thermo-physical properties. This study focuses on steady-state thermal rectification, which results from differences in the temperature dependence of thermal conductivity (Figure 1A). In contrast, specific heat may affect only transient behavior. MSTDs, also called two-segment or heterojunction diodes,2,25 can be made of phase-invariant materials (PIMs)26, PCMs27, or a combination of the two28,29 (Figures 1B–1D). The key performance metric of a thermal diode’s efficiency is its rectification factor (RF), usually defined as the ratio of 1D heat fluxes in the forward and reverse directions at steady state, q˙for and q˙rev,30,31,32(Equation 1)RF=q˙for−q˙revq˙rev,

where |q˙for|≥|q˙rev|. In most studies, this calculation involves reversing the temperatures at the MSTD terminals in the forward and reverse bias or maintaining at least one of the terminal temperatures (either hot or cold) constant.26,28,32

Other important factors include the magnitude of the forward- (or reverse-) direction heat flux, which is a critical parameter for practical applications, and the time response of the device, which determines its suitability for dynamic thermal management scenarios. Device size and geometry significantly affect the thermal performance, fabrication cost, and integration potential. Material abundance impacts scalability, while toxicity and environmental effects must be considered for sustainability and safety.

For PIM-based MSTDs, RF depends on the slope and monotonicity of the thermal conductivity functions. For example, different RFs are expected on either side of the peak thermal conductivity of LaCoO3 due to changes in slope, as shown in Figure 1B. The thermal conductivity is often modeled as a power-law function30,31,32:(Equation 2)k=k0(TT0)n,

where T0 is a reference temperature and k = k0 at T0. The exponent n (−4 ≤ n ≤ 5)30 characterizes the power-law dependence of thermal conductivity on temperature and is typically treated as constant within a limited interval when calculating RF, with nA and nB for materials A and B. Thus, RF depends on the operating temperature interval, increasing with both the difference |nA − nB| and the thermal bias ΔT.30 Theoretical studies predict maximum RF values of 2–3 for materials with linear temperature dependence of k30,31,32 and up to 14 for quadratic dependence.31 However, experimental RFs are typically lower due to insufficient differences in n over usable temperature ranges. For example, Kobayashi et al.26 experimentally achieved an RF of 0.43 at a thermal bias of 59 K between 40 and 99 K using lanthanum oxides (Figure 1B). Near room temperature, Zhang et al.33 achieved an RF of 0.51 at a thermal bias of 97 K using a thermal diode composed of Ni0.85Fe0.15S and Al2O3.

Current MSTD designs fall short of the desired efficiency in RF and other performance metrics. To improve RF in steady-state operation, several approaches have been proposed.

One strategy is to modify the interface topography between constituent materials. Wang et al.34 introduced a zigzag interface and showed that RF increases when the MSTD combines two PIMs with large differences in the k-function exponent n. Their study assumed a fixed ratio of material segment lengths and total interface length. Using theoretical analysis with fictitious material properties, they reported an RF of 9 at ΔT = 300 K, while calculations with real materials yielded an RF of 2 at ΔT = 75 K, with mean temperature Tm = T0 for both materials (see Equation 2). The mean temperature is defined as the arithmetic average of the temperatures at thermal diode terminals.

Li et al.35 proposed a fractal-like interface between liquid metal and an insulator, achieving an RF of 0.47 at an 18 K thermal bias due to asymmetric electron transport. Multi-junction MSTDs using different materials have also been explored. Swoboda et al.36 modeled a multilayer structure with PCMs and reported an RF of 1.36 at a 200 K bias. Some concepts go beyond pure conduction, such as an asymmetric embedding of staggered copper and polystyrene sheets in stainless steel, with transverse air convection considered.9 This setup, while not a conventional MSTD, yielded an RF of 1.6 near room temperature at a 30 K bias.

Zhao et al.37,38 numerically studied stress- and temperature-dependent thermal contact resistance (TCR) for ΔT> 100 K, showing that RF is highly sensitive to initial interface gap, TCR, and thermal expansion mismatch. Combining a small TCR model index, optimal gap placement, and materials with opposite thermal expansion coefficients can yield RFs up to 20. However, this requires maintaining optimal spacing in reverse MSTD orientation. In a separate study,39 transient behavior in an Al-Fe MSTD showed that moderate TCR improves rectification during both heating and cooling phases, while excessive TCR is detrimental. Shen et al.40 developed a fast numerical method for estimating the TCR of rough surfaces, considering thermo-mechanical coupling and elastoplastic deformation. They found that TCR is sensitive to roll-off wavenumber but not to the Hurst exponent. Zhang et al.41 introduced a peridynamic model to predict heat flow across imperfect interfaces and cracks. Dong et al. showed that torsional angle and load can tune interface thermal conductance. Yuan et al.42 reported that soft, deformable materials can reduce TCR under sufficient pressure.

Existing studies on MSTDs lack a comprehensive and systematic investigation of novel concepts. Specifically, they fail to account for several key factors: the relationships between thermal conductivity functions of material pairs, segment length ratios, interface lengths, temperature intervals, and mean operating temperatures. These factors are essential for fully assessing the performance potential of MSTDs.

In MSTDs without gaps between material segments, constant TCR is often neglected despite its influence on performance. Above 50–100 K and over small temperature intervals, TCR differences between forward and reverse bias from thermal expansion are usually within an order of magnitude.37 However, interface TCR strongly depends on surface roughness, ranging from 10−8 m2kW−1 for well-mated flat surfaces43,44 to ≥10−3 m2kW−1 in rougher contacts.43,44,45,46,47,48 This variability underscores the need to account for TCR in device modeling.

Moreover, the effects of TCR have not been thoroughly studied at cryogenic temperatures below 50–100 K, where its temperature dependence becomes pronounced and TCR increases sharply with decreasing temperature.43,44 Namely, at cryogenic temperatures, the mean free path of the heat carriers can become comparable to the roughness of the contact surface and ballistic transport or even scattering at the interface can occur.45 According to the acoustic mismatch model,49 the TCR has a very high value near absolute zero and decreases with temperature with an exponent ν,(Equation 3)R|T<100K≡RLT(T)=R0T−ν,

where R is the value of the TCR and R0 is the TCR at T = 1 K. Previous studies have shown that ν ≤ 3 below 50 K and gradually decreases at higher temperatures.43,44,50 In MSTDs with flat interfaces, the interface temperature differs between forward and reverse bias under the same thermal load. This indicates that temperature-dependent TCR may significantly influence RF at low temperatures—a factor that has not yet been investigated.

Most MSTD studies report RF only at fixed terminal temperatures and thermal biases,30,32,33,51 offering limited practical insight.35,52 In cyclic energy conversion systems, where Tm and ΔT fluctuate over time (see Figure 2A), efficient operation requires not only high RF but also stability across varying conditions.52,53,54,55Figure 2Classification of thermal diodes and example application(A) Schematic representation of a cyclic energy conversion system with embedded MSTDs. Finally, to our knowledge, a systematic study to identify optimal material pairs that provide the highest RFs across different temperature intervals has yet to be undertaken.

This study addresses two key gaps in the current understanding of MSTDs outlined above. Specifically, it systematically investigates the impact of modified interface topography for selected material pairs on MSTD performance and examines the role of TCR across different temperature ranges, both under steady-state conditions. In doing so, it accounts for the mentioned relevant factors, including the relationships between thermal conductivity functions of material pairs, segment length ratios, and mean operating temperatures.

The RF is analyzed as a function of the mean temperature Tm. In this context, the concept of an operating range for thermal diodes is introduced and formally defined. This motivates the consideration of operating range as a supplementary criterion for evaluating the effectiveness of thermal diodes.

First, a systematic numerical analysis is conducted to evaluate the influence of non-planar interface topographies relative to flat interfaces. This analysis assumes ideal thermal contact (i.e., negligible TCR). Three representative material pairs are selected, chosen to reflect diverse thermal conductivity relationships. Various length ratios between the constituent material segments and different interface lengths are considered to explore geometric effects.

Subsequently, the influence of constant TCR on the performance of MSTDs with flat interfaces is numerically assessed. TCR values of varying magnitude are applied at the interface for different temperature intervals, based on the selected material pairs.

Additionally, the effect of temperature-dependent (non-constant) TCR on the RF of MSTDs is investigated at cryogenic temperatures (below 100 K), using TCR functions drawn from existing literature.

At the end of this work, examples of potential device applications are presented, with two of the considered devices evaluated under varying boundary conditions at low temperatures.

Results and discussion

This study addresses the following analyses: (1) the dependence of MSTD RF on Tm and material segment length ratio for flat interfaces, (2) the impact of interface topography on MSTD performance, (3) the influence of constant TCR, (4) temperature-dependent TCR effects at low temperatures, and (5) potential applications of the considered MSTDs.

The primary aim of this study is to qualitatively examine how variations in material interface topography and TCR influence the RF and operating range of MSTDs, rather than to identify optimal material combinations that yield the highest RF values. For each study case, the steady-state RF is computed and plotted as a function of either Tm for selected thermal biases or ΔT for selected cold terminal temperatures. In all cases, the forward and reverse biases are analyzed under matched conditions, ensuring that both Tm and ΔT are identical in each direction. Although a more general analysis might include asymmetric thermal conditions between forward and reverse modes, this topic lies beyond the scope of the present work and is only briefly discussed at the end of this section (in the section application analysis of presented macroscopic solid-state thermal diodes).

This section begins by introducing the key definitions, including the mean temperature (Tm) and the operating range of a thermal diode. It then delineates the cases considered in analyses (1)–(5). Detailed descriptions of the employed methods are provided in STAR Methods. The subsequent sections present and critically discuss the simulation results corresponding to analyses (1)–(5).

The orientation of an MSTD is defined by the arrangement of its material segments relative to the hot and cold terminals, specifically A-B or B-A. The forward orientation exhibits a higher heat flux compared to the reverse orientation.

Analysis of potential applications of presented macroscopic solid-state thermal diodes: Study parameters

The operating temperature range of the presented MSTD examples falls below room temperature, rendering them potentially valuable for integration into low-temperature systems. These include, for example, spacecraft applications61,62 or thermal management systems of low-temperature electronics.63,64

In the application examples, two of the considered MSTDs are evaluated under varying boundary conditions at low temperatures. When switching the direction of heat flux, one MSTD terminal is maintained at a constant temperature, while the other alternates between higher and lower temperatures. This contrasts with the general analysis, where the temperatures at both MSTD terminals are interchanged.

Two possible scenarios for the utilization of the MSTDs are considered, as illustrated in Figure 8. In both cases, an electronic device or component is maintained at a constant temperature TX. In the first scenario (scenario (a)), the MSTD is oriented to hinder parasitic heat transfer from the non-operational cooler to the electronic component. The forward direction of the MSTD points away from the electronic component. This arrangement is intended to reduce the heat flux toward the electronic component compared to a setup using only a thermal interface material (TIM) between the device and the cooler. The appropriate orientation—whether material A faces the electronic component and material B faces the cooler, or vice versa—depends on the temperatures of the component and the cooler. When the cooler is operational, its temperature is TC < TX; when it is not, its temperature increases to TH > TX. In the second scenario (scenario (b)), the MSTD is oriented to hinder subcooling of the electronic component. The forward direction of the MSTD points toward the component. This setup is designed to enhance heat flux from the warmer surroundings toward the component, relative to a configuration employing only an insulating material. As before, the appropriate placement of materials depends on the temperature of the component, TX, and the temperature of the surroundings, which fluctuates between TC and TH.Figure 8Two possible scenarios for the utilization of the MSTD(A) The MSTD hinders heat transfer from the non-operating cooler to the component.(B) The MSTD hinders subcooling of the component.

In this analysis, the temperature of the cooler or the surroundings fluctuates between TC = TX − ΔT and TH = TX + ΔT. Target electronic component temperatures TX (between 10 and 70 K) and thermal biases ΔT (between 1 and 50 K) are selected. A key distinction between this analysis and the previous analyses is that the mean temperature Tm differs between the forward and reverse bias, owing to the specific configurations of the applications considered. For the same reason, temperature biases ΔT below 50 K are considered. Last, asymmetrical temperature fluctuations, where TH − TX ≠ TX − TC, are also considered for MSTD 2. Results of the analysis are presented in the section application analysis of presented macroscopic solid-state thermal diodes.

As illustrated in Figures 12, 13, and 14, the function RF(Tm) changes with the addition of the zigzag interface. The larger the LZ, the more pronounced the change in RF(Tm) compared to the flat interface. A comparison between Figures 12, 13, and 14 with Figure 11 reveals that adding a zigzag interface of any length does not lead to an improvement in the maximum RF value for any of the materials investigated. The highest RF value consistently occurs with a flat interface, along with optimal ρL and Tm (Table 1). An exception is observed for material pair 3 (Be–BeO) under conditions of ρL = 0.3:0.7 and ΔT = 100 K, where introducing a zigzag interface with LZ ≤ 0.2 L enhances the RF by approximately 4%, increasing it from 0.140 to 0.145. This change is assumed to fall within the error margin of discretized segment length values.

The obtained result is not surprising, as the zigzag interface only alters the spatial distribution of the materials in the MSTD. However, in one instance, an improvement in the operating range (see section the mean temperature and the operating range of a macroscopic solid-state thermal diode) of an MSTD is observed with the addition of the zigzag interface. This occurs for material pair 1 (Co3V2O856 and CAXM-5Q57) at ΔT = 50 K (Figure 12E). The highest RF value RFmax = 0.93 is achieved when the interface is flat, namely, at Tm = 60 K. With the flat interface, the RF value already drops to 0.65 at Tm = 40 K, which is less than 90% RFmax (0.9 RF max = 0.84). However, with a zigzag interface of length LZ = 0.3L, the RF values remain above 90% RFmax at both Tm = 60 K (RF = 0.88) and Tm = 40 K (RF = 0.86). For mean temperatures above Tm = 60 K, the RF value for both the flat interface and the zigzag interface decreases at a similar rate. Thus, the operating range, defined as the temperature range within which the RF is greater than 90% of the highest RF value, improves by 10 K (from 54 K ≤ Tm ≤ 67–40 K ≤ Tm≤ 63 K) by adding a zigzag interface with LZ = 0.3L.

The operating range is enhanced only in the case where the materials have opposing thermal conductivity slopes. This behavior can be partially explained by examining the effective thermal conductivity in the interfacial region, as discussed by Wang et al.34 The authors provide an expression for the effective thermal conductivity in the region surrounding the interface (whether flat or zigzag, Equation 14 of Wang et al.34), which depends on the ratio of the thermal conductivities of the two materials, assuming they are constant.

Although the thermal conductivities vary with temperature, average values can be taken near the interface, for each side of the interface. Assuming an average temperature TA of material A near the interface and a constant temperature drop across the contact line, the effective thermal conductivity can be expressed as a function of TA and the interface angle θ. Here, θ = π/2 for a flat interface and θ < π/2 for a zigzag interface. The details of this calculation are provided in the STAR Methods section. Therefore, the effective thermal conductivity as a function of TA differs between flat and zigzag interfaces. When the k(T) functions have opposing signs and significantly different slopes, the effective conductivity curve tends to flatten over a range of lower temperatures for the zigzag case, as is qualitatively depicted in Figure 15. Although a more detailed analysis is needed for a general conclusion, this may explain the more stable RF observed across a broader range of mean temperatures at the lower end of the temperature spectrum.Figure 15Qualitative explanation of the flattening of the RF curve, for a material pair with opposing slopes in their k(T) functions(A) The interface region where keff is defined, with average temperatures TA and TB on either side. The heat-flow illustration for the MSTD is adapted with permission from Wang et al.34(B) The non-dimensional effective thermal conductivity keff curve shows less curvature for the zigzag interface than for the flat interface, leading to a corresponding flattening of the RF curve. At the higher end of the temperature range, the addition of the zigzag interface appears to improve the RF for the material pair with opposing slopes of the k(T) functions. This region corresponds to temperatures far from the intersection of the k(T) functions, where RF is typically smallest; therefore, this phenomenon is not investigated further.

The improvement in RF stability while maintaining the largest possible RF value holds as long as LZ is not too large. As LZ increases, the system approaches the limiting case of parallel strips of materials, which ultimately leads to zero rectification.

In most cases, the TCR, similar to the zigzag interface, does not improve the highest RF value of an MSTD. The RF decreases once the TCR exceeds a certain threshold. The maximum allowable TCR value that does not negatively impact rectification depends on the desired heat flux through the MSTD. Similar to the zigzag case, for a material pair with opposing k(T) slopes, the RF improves in the presence of TCR at higher temperatures.

The results in Figures 18 and 19 indicate that when the MSTD operates at low temperatures, where the TCR function is very steep, a significant increase in the RF value is observed due to the rise in TCR at these low temperatures. For the material pair RhFe-Al2O3 (Figure 18), this leads to an improvement in the RF value for cold terminal temperatures of 0+, 2, and 4 K and for thermal biases below 30 K. The most significant improvement in the RF value occurs when it increases from 6 × 10−3 (in the case of a constant R100 K) to 0.94 (in the case of temperature-dependent TCR) at a thermal bias of only 5 K, just above absolute zero, where the RLT(T) function is steepest.

With the exception of the RhFe-Al2O3 material pair, there is a lack of literature data on the values of RLT(T) just above absolute zero. This results in missing information about the steepest region of the TCR function, which is critical because it is where the greatest influence of the TCR on the RF values can be observed (Figure 18). Consequently, the results for Al2O3-Au, Al2O3-Al, and C-Al material pairs in Figures 19A, 19B, and 19D show no improvement or even a decrease in the RF value compared to the case with constant TCR with R100 K. However, at slightly higher temperatures (10 K < Tcold terminal < 20 K), a moderate increase in the RF value can be observed for the C-Au pair (Figure 19C). In this case, the RF value increases by up to 27% (from 0.73 to 0.92 for Tcold terminal = 16 K and ΔT = 45 K).

The present analysis of temperature-dependent TCR effects at low temperatures demonstrates that this factor cannot be neglected. In certain cases, it may even be leveraged to enhance device performance. To achieve the most significant improvement in RF, one should seek a steep temperature dependence of the TCR, which typically occurs at temperatures below 20 K. However, each scenario must be evaluated individually, as the outcome depends on the specific TCR and thermal conductivity functions of the materials, device dimensions, and exact temperatures at MSTD terminals.

Conclusions

The existing concepts for solid-state thermal diodes do not fulfill the desired operating characteristics and efficiency in terms of RF and operating temperature range. This study aims to fill some gaps in research that addresses these limitations, particularly by systematically exploring the impact of utilizing effects at the interface between the materials on the RF and operating range of MSTDs. Two different passive mechanisms are numerically evaluated independently from each other, with MSTDs consisting of pairs of selected materials from the literature.

The first mechanism involves the modification of interface topography between the materials. A multiparametric analysis is performed considering three interface topographies (zigzag, with rectangular teeth, with round teeth), with varying segment lengths and interface lengths. Simulations were performed across different temperature intervals (i.e., at different mean temperatures Tm), without considering TCR, to assess the impact of interface geometry on the RF. The second mechanism explored is the effect of TCR between material segments on MSTD performance. In this analysis, MSTDs with a flat interface between the materials were used. Different constant TCR values are investigated at different temperature intervals with two different thermal biases. Separate pairs of materials are also selected to investigate the effect of temperature-dependent TCR on the performance of the MSTDs at low temperatures compared to constant TCR.

Conclusions of the study

Summary of key findings for MSTD design and optimization. Opposing k(T) slopes are favorable, with optimal mean temperatures just below the k(T) intersection. Choosing the right segment length ratio is crucial. Interface modifications (e.g., zigzag) can extend operating ranges. TCR is critical: even small constant values reduce RF, while temperature-dependent TCR at low T can yield large improvements. Application analysis shows that MSTDs are generally more effective for passive heating than cooling, with orientation and Tm asymmetries influencing performance.

Notably, whenever possible, material pairs with opposing thermal conductivity slopes should be selected. Moreover, the desired operating mean temperature should be slightly lower than the temperature at which the thermal conductivity curves intersect.

Careful selection of segment lengths is crucial for achieving the desired RF, and modifications in interface topography, such as introducing a zigzag interface, may be beneficial for extending the operating range when the material pair exhibits opposing k(T) slopes. In one case considered, the operating range is extended by 10 K (from 54 K ≤ Tm≤ 67 K–40 K ≤ Tm≤ 63 K) at ΔT = 50 K.

In addition, TCR at material interfaces should be taken into account when developing MSTDs, regardless of whether they operate at low temperatures or above 100 K. Even small constant values of TCR can reduce the RF. The highest permissible constant TCR value that does not affect the RF depends on the desired heat flux through the MSTD.

Neglecting the temperature dependence of the TCR at low temperatures can result in inaccurate device evaluations. At temperatures just above absolute zero, significant performance improvements may be achievable due to the pronounced temperature dependence of TCR. In one case considered, the RF value increases from 6 × 10−3 (for constant TCR) to 0.94 (for temperature-dependent TCR) at a thermal bias of only 5 K.

The analysis of selected application configurations shows that, in practice, the considered MSTDs perform more effectively when facilitating heat flux from a warmer environment to the object and impeding heat flux from the object to a colder environment (passive heating), rather than the reverse (passive cooling). These configurations, which involve temperature fluctuations on only one side of the MSTD, result in different values of Tm for the two heat flux directions. This makes it non-trivial to determine the optimal physical orientation of the device or whether the desired rectification will occur in any orientation.

Further research is needed to experimentally evaluate the accuracy of the results from this study, particularly concerning TCR at low temperatures. Additionally, a broader range of material pairs should be explored to identify generalizable conditions where modifications to interface topography and TCR can enhance MSTD performance. Furthermore, a combined analysis of both effects could provide valuable insights into how they interact, potentially leading to optimized MSTDs for various applications.

Limitations of the study

This study considers only macroscopic heat transfer in solids. Numerical models are limited by 2D spatial discretization, as finer grids significantly increase computational time. Experimental validation of the results is still pending.

Analysis considering constant thermal contact resistance at a flat interface between materials: method details

The curve RLT(T) flattens at temperatures between approximately 50 K and 100 K (see Figure 7A). Above 100 K, the TCR remains constant under constant contact pressure. Changes in contact pressure resulting from thermal expansion may lead to differences in TCR values between the forward and reverse directions. However, over small temperature intervals above 50-100 K, this variation is typically within an order of magnitude or less.37 Therefore, this effect is neglected for simplicity. Under these conditions, the TCR is considered temperature-independent and, consequently, direction-independent. It is assumed to depend primarily on the quality of surface contact, such as the degree of surface roughness at the material interface, and may vary over several orders of magnitude. The potential influence of electric contact resistance on heat transfer - particularly through its effect on charge carrier dynamics65 - is not explicitly analyzed in this work, but it is presumed to be implicitly accounted for as a component of the total TCR.

In this part of the study, the TCR at the interface between the materials is treated as constant with respect to temperature in all cases studied, even when the temperature drops below 100 K. This is partly because the data for RLT(T) is not known for the selected materials, but also because the purpose of this section is to illustrate the effect of non-zero TCR resulting from surface roughness and other design constraints on the performance of MSTD.

When selecting the lengths of the MSTDs for each material pair, several factors must be considered. The total heat flux q˙ through the MSTD is inversely proportional to the MSTD length for a fixed thermal bias. The TCR can affect the performance of an MSTD when R>≈q˙−1ΔT. This suggests that the MSTDs should have a shorter length. On the other hand, it should be noted that heat fluxes of the order of 10 MWm-2 are considered extremely high.66,67 This leads to a downward limitation of the MSTD length. The length of the MSTDs for each material pair is therefore determined so that the heat flux at the selected mean temperatures and thermal biases, and without TCR is between 104 and 107 Wm−2 for all test cases.

Applications of macroscopic solid-state thermal diodes: rationale for case study selection

The presented MSTD examples are potentially valuable for integration into low-temperature systems. In spacecraft applications, for instance, MSTDs could be employed between successive cooling stages, facilitating heat transfer from a colder stage to a cold thermal radiator while simultaneously impeding heat flow from a hot thermal radiator.61,62

Another promising application is the direct integration into the thermal management systems of low-temperature electronics. This field encompasses semiconductor materials, devices, circuits, and systems that operate at cryogenic temperatures, commonly defined as ranging from 123 K down to 4.2 K, and in some cases, extending into the milli-Kelvin regime.63 Low-temperature electronics are essential across numerous disciplines,68 including astronomy,6 experimental cosmology,69 and in the detection of particles,70 photons, and antimatter.71 They also play a critical role in quantum computing, where cryogenic conditions are required to maintain quantum coherence. In this context, low-temperature electronics are employed for signal amplification and qubit control.64,72

Cooling analog circuits - for example, as demonstrated in Conway Lamb et al.68 - can substantially reduce thermal noise73 and enhance the performance of specific components, such as CMOS transistors. These transistors remain functional at temperatures as low as 100 mK, exhibiting improved electrical characteristics.74 In digital systems, cryogenic operation increases carrier mobility, decreases interconnect resistance, and lowers the subthreshold swing, collectively enabling higher clock frequencies and reduced power consumption.75 However, not all components retain operability at such low temperatures; for instance, bipolar transistors undergo silicon freeze-out and typically cease functioning between 70 K and 90 K.76

MSTDs could be strategically positioned between cryocoolers and electronic components to prevent parasitic heat loads on adjacent coolers.77 This is crucial for reducing overall energy consumption and minimizing the capacity requirements of cryocoolers, which are typically costly and resource-intensive.61

In addition, MSTDs may serve to protect temperature-sensitive components. Certain electronics, for instance, are only operable above 77 K or 30 K.78,79 In the presence of temperature fluctuations, MSTDs placed between different system components can offer thermal protection at critical interfaces while allowing heat regulation through designated thermal pathways.

Numerical models

The numerical simulations are based on time-dependent Fourier heat conduction, and are implemented using the finite volume method in conjunction with the backward difference scheme.

The governing equation is expressed as(Equation 5)ρcp∂T∂t=∇(k∇T),

where T stands for temperature, t for time, ∇ represents space gradients, ρ stands for density, cp stands for specific heat capacity, and k is thermal conductivity tensor. In general, properties ρ, cp, and k are considered to be temperature-dependent.

Transient models are employed due to their enhanced numerical stability, particularly in handling discontinuous variations in TCR values across iterations. Moreover, these models are well-suited for future extensions involving transient operating conditions in MSTDs. The boundary conditions are prescribed as constant temperatures at both terminals. Simulation results are analyzed once a steady-state thermal distribution is achieved.

A 2D numerical model is required to simulate the interface region of the MSTD in cases where the topography of the interface is altered, although the cumulative heat flux between the MSTD terminals remains effectively 1D. In the interface region, a fine discretization is applied (more than 100 control volumes in both directions) to ensure high accuracy. In the other parts of the MSTD, a coarser discretization is used in the x-direction (fewer than 40 control volumes), while the discretization in the y-direction is kept the same throughout for simplicity. The resulting system matrix is large but sparse, and is solved using sparse matrix algorithms from the SciPy library.80 A time step of 0.1 s is used in the simulations.

For the 2D simulations, the thermal conductivities of material pairs 1, 2, and 3 are approximated using polynomial functions (shown as dashed lines in Figure 3) over selected temperature intervals, as runtime interpolation of the conductivity data is computationally expensive. The polynomials used to represent the thermal conductivity k(T) of selected materials were generated using NumPy’s81 polyfit function, based on thermal conductivity data from the literature. The resulting coefficients are provided in Table S4.

A 1D model is used to analyze the TCR. Each MSTD is discretized using 200 control volumes. The resulting tridiagonal system matrix is solved using a tridiagonal matrix algorithm to reduce computational time. The time step is varied to ensure numerical stability and convergence, with a typical value of 1 ms.

The validation of the 1D model can be found in Vozel et al.82 The implementation of the TCR model is validated using Figure 8 and Table 2 from Zhao et al.37 The thermal conductivities and other parameters used are consistent with those reported in the cited study. A comparison between our results and those from Zhao et al.37 is provided in Table S5 and Figure S1. The 2D model is validated with Figure 2A of Wang et al.34 and is shown in Figure S2. The parameters for the simulation are the same as in Wang et al.34: L = 0.3 m, H = 0.1 m, ρL = 0.5:0.5, LZ = 0.1 m, kA=k0(T/T0)−3, kB=k0(T/T0)3, T0 = 200 K, k0 = 1 Wm−1 K−1, constant temperature boundary conditions Tcold terminal = 100 K and Thot terminal = 300 K.

Each of the boundary conditions is either adiabatic, constant heat flux, constant temperature, or convective. With the constant temperature boundary condition, a constant temperature is defined at the outermost surface of the CVs at the selected boundary. In this case, the conductivity k between the surface and the centre point of a boundary CV is determined by the temperature of the boundary CV, with the corresponding distance being half the length (for x-direction) or height (for y-direction) of the boundary CV. The convective boundary condition takes into account the defined convection coefficient h, which determines the heat transfer rate between the boundary CV and the environment. Together with the heat transfer coefficient, the outer boundary temperature Tb,o is given and the heat flux through the boundary surface is calculated as h(Tb,o − Tb,i), where Tb,i is the temperature at the inner side of the interface, linearly interpolated from the temperatures of the boundary CV and its nearest neighbour, Tb,i=32T−12Td.

The Equation 6 is written for each CV of the system, resulting in a system of N by M linear equations, where N and M are discretization numbers in x and y directions, respectively. The system of linear equations is solved for each successive time step until the steady-state is reached.
