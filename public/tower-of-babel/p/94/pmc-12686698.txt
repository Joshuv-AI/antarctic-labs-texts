A Scoping Review of Large Language Models in Personal Sleep Wellness
Authors: Hamid Mansoor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs sleep health becomes increasingly central to personal well-being, individuals are turning to digital tools for education, tracking, and behavior change support. Large language models like ChatGPT and Gemini have recently emerged as promising components of these tools, capable of generating personalized, conversational, and context-aware sleep guidance. This scoping survey synthesizes findings from 21 papers that explore the use of large language models in nonclinical, everyday user-focused applications for sleep health. We organize the literature into 4 core use cases: educational question answering, condition-specific support (eg, obstructive sleep apnea), personalized recommendations and coaching, and cognitive behavioral therapy–based self-help systems. We analyze the diverse data sources involved—including wearable sensor data, self-reported metrics, and synthetic benchmarks—as well as model architectures, fine-tuning techniques, and personalization strategies. Finally, we examine evaluation frameworks ranging from expert review to pilot user studies and LLM-based scoring. The review highlights current capabilities, methodological challenges, and open opportunities for advancing trustworthy, personalized sleep support using generative artificial intelligence, while emphasizing that much of the evidence remains preliminary, often short-term, expert-rated, or proxy-based, which limits external validity and generalizability.

Sleep is a foundational component of human health, intricately tied to cognitive function, emotional regulation, and the risk of chronic illness.1 Yet, many individuals experience persistent sleep issues without adequate clinical support or access to personalized guidance.2 The recent emergence of large language models (LLMs) offers a promising avenue to fill this gap by delivering scalable, intelligent, and conversational support tools.3,4 This article presents a scoping survey of how LLMs are being used to promote personal sleep wellness, synthesizing findings across applications such as education, behavioral coaching, condition-specific interventions, and therapeutic support.

Large language models have demonstrated strong performance in delivering medically accurate, emotionally supportive responses across multiple ailments and domains.5 In sleep health, LLM have been shown to answer sleep-related queries, often rivaling sleep specialists in perceived helpfulness and clarity.6, 7, 8, 9 Cheong et al10 found that ChatGPT responses to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) questions were more understandable and actionable than those from Google Bard, supporting its role in educational question answering. Bilal et al11 also argued for ChatGPT’s potential to raise awareness and support self-diagnosis of OSA, particularly in underinformed populations.

Beyond general layperson education, more specialized applications have emerged, such as supporting patients with OSA through tailored education on treatment options,8,9,12 with Campbell et al9 showing that chatbot-based OSA explanations were preferred over static web resources and aligned well with clinical domains or providing Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) through empathetic chatbot interactions.13,14 These systems increasingly integrate physiological data from wearables, self-reported symptoms, and behavioral patterns to personalize feedback and improve user engagement.15, 16, 17, 18 Corda et al19 proposed a smartphone application that combines machine learning–based sleep forecasting and LLM-generated explanations to deliver context-aware behavior suggestions, which improved user trust and engagement.

This scoping review organizes the literature into several thematic categories: key use cases (eg, education, condition-specific coaching, and cognitive behavioral therapy [CBT] delivery), data sources (eg, wearable, self-reported, and synthetic), technical strategies (eg, fine-tuning, chain-of-thought [CoT] reasoning, and personalization frameworks), and evaluation methods (eg, expert review, user studies, and indirect and proxy evaluation). We cover a range of models from proprietary platforms like GPT-47,16 to open-source tools like ChatGLM14 and Qwen,13 often fine-tuned using lightweight techniques such as low-rank adaptation (LoRA)14 or CoT distillation.20

By synthesizing this emerging landscape, our survey aims to guide future research and development in leveraging LLMs for accessible, trustworthy, and personalized sleep wellness support. At the same time, the reviewed studies must be interpreted cautiously, as many rely on expert ratings, small pilot trials, or indirect proxies such as chatbot logs vs wearable-derived sleep metrics. These factors introduce risk of bias and limit the extent to which findings can be generalized beyond controlled or short-term settings.

Methods

To identify relevant studies for this scoping review on LLMs in personal sleep wellness, we followed the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews)21 (Figure; checklist provided in Supplemental Appendix 7, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). A comprehensive search was conducted across 4 major academic databases (Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and IEEE Xplore), and the reference lists of retrieved articles were also hand-searched to identify additional studies. The search string used was as follows: (“generative artificial intelligence” OR “large language models” OR “chatbot”) AND (“sleep” OR “apnea” OR “insomnia”), which was designed to capture a wide range of studies intersecting generative artificial intelligence technologies and sleep-related topics. Full database strategies are provided in Appendix A. The search was limited to publications dated between January 1, 2020, and June 1, 2025, to ensure relevance to the recent wave of generative model development. After duplicates were removed, the primary author independently screened all titles, abstracts, and full texts for eligibility using the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure). Both the title/abstract and full-text stages were therefore done by a single reviewer. Uncertainties were resolved by revisiting the inclusion criteria and erring on the side of inclusion, with systematic documentation of all decisions. Although dual independent screening is often recommended, it is not mandatory for scoping reviews under the PRISMA-ScR framework. Given the exploratory and mapping nature of this review, and in line with prior scoping methodologies, a single reviewer approach was used to balance rigor with feasibility. Importantly, reasons for exclusion were systematically recorded and transparently reported to mitigate bias. No formal critical appraisal was undertaken, consistent with scoping review methodology; however, we explicitly note this omission.

PRISMA-ScR flow diagram illustrating the search, screening, and inclusion process. Screening at both title/abstract and full-text stages was performed by a single reviewer, with reasons for exclusion systematically recorded.

The scope was limited to nonclinical and everyday-use contexts, including personal wellness, self-management, and condition-specific education where the focus was on user-facing support. We included OSA- and positive airway pressure (PAP)/hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HGNS)-related educational applications under a personal wellness lens, as these tools target patient empowerment and self-guided understanding rather than clinical diagnostics. We excluded papers that centered on purely clinical, diagnostic, or research-oriented uses of LLMs or that did not involve or envision direct personal interaction by everyday users. The final selection emphasized systems or evaluations where LLMs were designed to assist individuals in managing their own sleep health, rather than being tools for health care professionals or researchers.

Limitations of the Review

It should also be noted that the strength of evidence across included studies is limited: many relied on expert review, short-term pilot testing, or proxy-based measures (eg, chatbot logs compared with wearable data). This introduces potential bias and constrains the external validity of the findings synthesized in this review. A key limitation is that screening was performed by a single reviewer rather than by 2 independent reviewers with reconciliation, as recommended by PRISMA-ScR. This choice was driven by feasibility constraints and the scoping purpose of the review, which aimed to broadly map the emerging literature rather than make definitive judgments on study quality or effect sizes. Single reviewer screening is an accepted, although less rigorous, practice under PRISMA-ScR guidelines when resources are limited. We mitigated this limitation by applying explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria, erring on the side of inclusion when uncertain, and systematically recording reasons for all exclusions. We believe this transparent approach maintains the integrity and reproducibility of the review while acknowledging its constraints.

Use Cases for LLMs in Personal Sleep Wellness

In this section, we provide an overview of a few trends in sleep-based use cases that were being targeted by LLMs:

Sleep Education and Question Answering

Large language models like ChatGPT have shown promise in delivering accurate, readable sleep-related answers, often matching or surpassing specialists28 in layperson-rated helpfulness and emotional support6 and achieving high alignment in myth debunking.7 For OSA-related content, ChatGPT agreed with experts in over 75% of item-level cases8 and delivered mostly correct answers across prompting styles.9 For example, ChatGPT-4’s responses were rated higher by the public than those by specialists’ for helpfulness and clarity.6

Recent evaluations further affirm ChatGPT’s educational utility: its responses to insomnia-related queries were judged clinically accurate and adaptable across patient- and physician-oriented prompts, although caution was advised regarding reference validity and statistical precision.24 In a head-to-head comparison with Google Bard, ChatGPT also achieved higher scores in understandability and actionability across condition, investigation, and treatment domains for OSA-related education materials, reinforcing its role as a superior source for patient-facing information.10

Condition-Specific Support

Large language models have also been shown to be helpful in condition-specific support across other domains such as mental health issues,29 elderly care,30 and even conditions like diabetes.31 Moreover, LLMs like ChatGPT have been evaluated for supporting patients with OSA, particularly in educating users about treatment options such as PAP and HGNS therapy.8,12 ChatGPT-4o provided medically adequate, coherent, and comprehensible responses to frequently asked questions on both PAP and HGNS, with PAP answers judged complete in 88% of expert ratings.12 It achieved high accuracy in answering common OSA treatment questions8 and mirrored physician-level detail in explaining treatment procedures.9 However, performance was weaker on newer HGNS therapies owing to limited training data, highlighting the need for updated and domain-grounded models.12,32

A head-to-head study found that ChatGPT outperformed Google Bard in both understandability (90.8%) and actionability (73.04%) of treatment information, underscoring its suitability as a source of patient education for OSA across multiple domains.10 Despite occasional gaps, LLMs show clear potential as adjunctive tools for patient-facing OSA education.

Personalized Recommendations and Coaching

Large language models increasingly support greater personalization through integration with wearable sensors and user profiles.33 Models such as PhysioLLM incorporate sleep metrics (eg, efficiency and restlessness) and behavioral patterns to produce contextualized insights and advice.15 Subramanian et al16 used feature importance values to create a graph-augmented LLM framework for personalized sleep. Cosentino et al17 developed personal health (PH)-LLM, fine-tuned on 857 expert-curated case studies, showing human-level performance in generating sleep recommendations grounded in longitudinal sensor data. Similarly, HealthGuru system by Wang et al18 combines a multiagent architecture with behavioral theory to guide users through interactive goals using both LLM reasoning and rule-based logic.

Corda et al19 presented an LLM pipeline that generates individualized insights from users’ self-reported information including input through ecological momentary assessment questionnaires (short surveys) and smartphone sensor data summaries, emphasizing practical personalization needs for sleep coaching at scale. Jang et al26 demonstrated how integrating subjective chatbot-based sleep logs with Fitbit data can expose discrepancies in perceived vs measured sleep, offering opportunities for models to calibrate personalized advice using both perspectives. Ajovalasit et al23 introduced NightCare Assistant, which tailors daily routine suggestions based on nighttime sleep data for elderly users, integrating caregiver feedback to adjust personalization outputs dynamically. These hybrid models demonstrate that augmenting LLMs with structured health inputs and behavioral frameworks can significantly enhance personalization and actionability of sleep wellness advice.

CBT and Guided Self-help

Large language models are increasingly used to deliver CBT-I through conversational agents.34 Tang et al13 designed ZzzMate, a sleep chatbot incorporating self-conscious emotion awareness and CBT principles to foster emotional engagement and habit formation. Their multicomponent framework combines empathy modeling, and dynamic response selection to simulate human-like therapeutic interactions.13 Chen et al14 developed a mobile application powered by ChatGLM-LoRA, fine-tuned on 764 sleep-related dialogues. Their model offers personalized advice, relaxation guidance, and CBT-based sleep interventions via an Android interface. The system showed encouraging results in a 1-week clinical trial, with over 50% of users reporting improved sleep.

Chan et al25 piloted a chatbot-based tool for guiding users through digital CBT-I (dCBT-I)-based reflection exercises and psychoeducation, finding high engagement levels, especially when users received tailored follow-up responses across multiple sessions. Together, these studies highlight the potential of LLM-based systems to deliver accessible, emotionally intelligent, and evidence-backed insomnia treatment, especially in contexts where access to human therapists is limited.13,14,25

Data Modalities and Sources

In this section, we describe the types of data used to feed or evaluate the LLMs.

Sensor and Wearable Data

Large language models are increasingly integrated with sensor-derived data to generate personalized wellness insights.33 Physiological signals from Fitbit devices—tracking heart rate, sleep duration, and stress—were used to tailor actionable suggestions for adolescents and adults.15,22 Oura ring data were also explored for detecting sleep stages and correlating behavioral patterns with cognitive states.16 Further, LLMs were also used to combine data streams such as Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) survey scores, academic reports, and wearable logs to generate holistic profiles and adaptive recommendations.22 PhysioLLM further processed time-aligned sensor data to uncover correlations—for example, between daily step count and sleep quality—and used LLMs to explain these relationships through conversations.15 In a large-scale study, chatbot-collected sleep logs were compared with Fitbit data to quantify the gap between subjective and objective measures, revealing individual differences in perceived sleep time and showing that self-reported quality influenced time perception.26 Corda et al19 proposed an LLM-powered feedback pipeline that combines user prompts and smartphone data summaries to generate personalized wellness guidance, without relying on predefined templates or static advice. Across these studies, sensor data served both as input for context-aware personalization and as validation tools to measure recommendation relevance and goal actionability.

Self-reported or Survey Data

Self-reported data through application-administered surveys or personal journaling are often used for gauging sleep wellness.35 LLMs for sleep wellness often incorporate self-reported data to contextualize or evaluate recommendations. Ahmed et al22 used PSQI surveys36 alongside Fitbit data to gauge subjective sleep experiences and detect discrepancies with wearable-derived metrics. The PSQI scores, collected before and after study, assessed sleep duration, latency, and disturbances, helping refine the model’s feedback.22 Sano et al27 combined self-reports and behavioral self-assessments with electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings to link cognitive states and sleep quality in real-time, supporting dynamic, user-aware LLM outputs.27

In a large-scale cohort study, Jang et al26 compared chatbot-based sleep self-reports with Fitbit recordings across 543 participants, revealing systematic gaps in perceived vs measured sleep durations, particularly among users who reported better subjective sleep quality. Chan et al25 similarly leveraged conversational self-reports in a longitudinal sleep agent to support proactive, LLM-guided feedback loops based on daily mood and sleep input patterns. These subjective inputs enable a richer understanding of user well-being by capturing mood, stress levels, or attention that wearables might miss. They also serve as ground truth for validating LLM-generated advice. Despite variability in self-report accuracy, such data remain critical for nuanced personalization, especially when merged with physiological streams.

Synthetic and Simulated Data

Synthetic data generation is increasingly used to train and evaluate LLM-based health models when real-world data are limited or privacy sensitive.37 Zheng et al20 introduced SleepCoT, a lightweight model for personalized sleep health guidance, trained via CoT distillation from GPT-4o outputs. SleepCoT leverages CoT distillation reasoning to simulate structured user profiles, including synthetic physiological attributes (eg, stress levels and sleep onset latency) grounded in known behavioral-sleep correlations. These synthetic profiles were generated using rule-based prompts and health constraints, enabling diverse, controllable, and privacy-preserving training data. The authors demonstrated that models trained on these data could generalize well to real-world queries, supporting both education and recommendation tasks. This approach highlights the promise of combining LLM reasoning with structured simulation to bootstrap health models in low-data settings.

Benchmark Data Sets

Benchmark data sets are essential for training and evaluating LLMs in health-based applications.38 Cosentino et al17 developed PH-LLM using Gemini, a data set of 857 structured, long-form health behavior records that span sleep patterns, physical activity, diet, and mood.17 Each entry is annotated with expert-written recommendations and contextual tags, enabling supervised fine-tuning and rigorous evaluation of LLM outputs. The data reflect realistic, longitudinal behavior patterns and include metadata for explainability and personalization. This data set represents a valuable step toward standardized, high-quality training corpora for PH-focused LLMs.

Technical Implementation and Model Variants

Model Families

A wide variety of LLM architectures have been used across personal sleep wellness applications. Proprietary models like GPT-3.5/4/4o are frequently used for general-purpose prompting, offering strong performance in sleep question and answering and personalized coaching tasks.7,16 Gemini-based PH-LLM, fine-tuned on sleep and fitness data, demonstrates near-expert performance in generating long-form insights and recommendations from wearable data, with tailored evaluation rubrics and case studies for benchmarking.17 Open-source models are also gaining traction. ChatGLM with LoRA fine-tuning supports structured CBT-I dialogues, enabling contextualized and emotionally intelligent support.14 Similarly, Qwen1.5 powers ZzzMate, which integrates self-conscious emotion awareness to enhance user engagement in sleep interventions.13 Ahmed et al22 used Meta-Llama-3-Instruct as their model of choice for real-time recommendations, showcasing the flexibility of foundation models in health scenarios.

Comparative studies confirm the effectiveness of proprietary models like ChatGPT, which outperformed Google Bard on patient education tasks for OSA in both understandability and actionability metrics.10 Other work shows ChatGPT’s utility in supporting user awareness and self-diagnosis through natural dialogue on OSA symptoms and management strategies.11 These efforts highlight a shift from general-purpose LLMs to domain-specialized, often fine-tuned, variants designed to interpret multimodal health data and deliver contextualized, trustworthy sleep wellness support.

Fine-Tuning and Adaptation Techniques

Several approaches have been used to adapt general-purpose LLMs to the sleep wellness domain. One prominent method is LoRA, which inserts low-rank trainable matrixes into frozen pretrained models, enabling efficient fine-tuning with minimal parameter overhead. Chen et al14 fine-tuned the ChatGLM model using LoRA for a CBT-I mobile application, training on a curated data set of 764 sleep-related dialogues. Their setup used 450 training epochs, the AdamW optimizer, and achieved strong performance on BLEU-4 and ROUGE metrics, demonstrating the feasibility of deploying LLMs in mobile mental health tools.14

Another technique is CoT distillation, as used by Zheng et al.20 They synthesized labeled CoT examples using GPT-4o and distilled reasoning traces into a smaller Qwen2.5-based model. This lightweight model showed strong personalization and logical consistency in generating structured sleep recommendations, balancing interpretability with efficiency.20 These examples highlight how lightweight tuning and reasoning-aware methods are key enablers for making LLMs clinically relevant and deployable in real-world sleep wellness settings.

Personalization Techniques

Personalization in LLM-based sleep wellness systems involves tailoring outputs to individual user data, goals, and behavioral contexts. Campbell et al9 evaluated ChatGPT’s ability to personalize responses about OSA through well-constructed prompts, showing improved relevance and accuracy when queries included patient-specific details. Fang et al15 developed PhysioLLM, which integrates Fitbit-derived physiological metrics with behavioral patterns and demographics to generate adaptive sleep insights. The model’s prompting strategy aligns LLM outputs with user profiles and contextual trends, enhancing the actionability of suggestions.15 HealthGuru by Wang et al18 advances this by embedding behavioral theory into a multiagent system that uses a bandit algorithm to select which LLM-driven coach should respond. Each coach has a distinct motivational style (eg, emotional and logical) and uses real-time user feedback and historical performance to adapt messaging strategies. HealthGuru demonstrated higher user engagement and goal adherence in sleep-related tasks, especially when matched with the user’s preferred interaction style.18

Recent work has also explored real-time, context-aware LLM responses grounded in behavioral and sensor data. Corda et al19 presented a mobile system that forecasts nightly sleep quality and uses LLMs to generate personalized, interpretable tips based on environmental and behavioral conditions, enhancing user trust and adherence.19 Similarly, Chan et al25 evaluated an LLM-powered assistant capable of offering tailored guidance to users with anxiety and sleep issues, showing that personalized feedback significantly improved user-reported sleep outcomes. Collectively, these techniques highlight diverse strategies including prompt engineering, behavioral modeling, and agent-based adaptation, for aligning LLMs with personalized health support.

Evaluation Methods

Expert Review

Multiple studies have used expert evaluation to assess the quality and safety of LLM responses in the context of sleep health. Kim et al6 compared ChatGPT-4’s answers with those of 140 common sleep questions against sleep disorder specialists, with both experts and laypeople rating the outputs. Experts slightly preferred the specialists’ responses (56%), but ChatGPT was rated higher on emotional supportiveness and detail by laypeople.6 Pordzik et al12 evaluated ChatGPT-4o’s responses on PAP therapy and found them generally helpful and comprehensible, although lacking nuance in clinical appropriateness.12 Mira et al8 conducted a multinational study where 350 otolaryngologists and 10 superexperts compared ChatGPT-3.5’s diagnostic suggestions for OSA with those from expert consensus. Although ChatGPT agreed with individual experts in more than 75% of items, full-response consensus occurred in only 4 of 10 cases, and superexperts rated its answers significantly lower (P=.0009).8 Campbell et al9 also involved sleep experts to review ChatGPT responses to OSA-related queries, noting adequate accuracy but cautioning against unsupervised use.

Cheong et al10 conducted a head-to-head expert-rated comparison of ChatGPT and Google Bard for patient education on OSA, finding that ChatGPT outperformed Bard on understandability and actionability across 46 questions, with no incorrect content identified. Similarly, Alapati et al24 evaluated ChatGPT’s responses to insomnia-related queries using sleep physicians and trainees, noting high accuracy and detail, although with moderate interrater reliability.

User Studies

Largr language model–based sleep wellness systems have been evaluated using a range of small-scale, real-world user studies. Tang et al13 evaluated ZzzMate with a pilot involving 4 young adults, showing improved self-efficacy and sleep adherence through empathetic handling of self-conscious emotions like guilt and pride. PhysioLLM was assessed in a controlled study with 24 Fitbit users; participants found it more effective than commercial applications or generic LLMs in generating actionable, personalized sleep insights, supported by expert review.15 Ahmed et al22 analyzed LLM-generated recommendations for high school students using Fitbit and academic data, validated by expert raters for clarity, actionability, and data alignment. Ajovalasit et al23 explored caregiver acceptability of NightCare Assistant for elderly sleep support via a survey-based study, finding audio-based LLM feedback most trustworthy and usable.

Chan et al25 conducted a randomized controlled trial comparing dCBT-I with and without chatbot-based or human coaching. Their results showed that both coaching conditions improved adherence and ISI outcomes compared with those by the control group, demonstrating the feasibility of LLM-style coaching for behavior change.25 Corda et al19 developed a smartphone system combining machine learning and LLMs to deliver personalized, context-aware sleep tips. A preliminary user study found that the LLM explanations enhanced user trust and engagement.19 These evaluations provide preliminary evidence of usability, personalization, and user engagement, although most are short-term and exploratory in nature.

Indirect and Proxy Evaluation Strategies

Beyond expert and user evaluations, several studies explore scalable, nontraditional methods to assess LLM performance in sleep health. Subramanian et al16 used GPT-4 as a meta-evaluator to rate sleep guidance generated by other LLMs across relevance, actionability, comprehensiveness, and personalization. They found that richer prompts—especially those using patient-specific data and graph-based retrieval—produced higher-rated outputs.16

Jang et al26 compared chatbot-recorded sleep logs with Fitbit data from over 500 users. Although average values like total sleep time were similar across methods, individual-level differences were large, highlighting the complexity of aligning subjective reports with objective wearable metrics. This approach suggests the potential of wearable validation as a proxy for ground truth.26 Bilal et al11 described ChatGPT’s potential to raise awareness and support self-diagnosis of OSA. Although not an empirical study, they propose using user behavior shifts (eg, increased help-seeking) as a possible evaluation signal for public-facing LLM tools.11 Together, these studies reflect emerging interest in automated and real-world evaluation strategies—leveraging artificial intelligence models, sensor data, or user behavior—to complement expert review and expand how LLM-driven sleep tools are assessed.

Quantitative Metrics

Large language model–based systems for sleep wellness are increasingly evaluated using structured quantitative metrics. Studies commonly apply Likert scales to assess response quality, particularly for dimensions such as medical accuracy, comprehensibility, and emotional supportiveness.6,7,12 For example, Bragazzi and Garbarino7 used 5-point Likert scales to gauge ChatGPT’s ability to debunk sleep myths, revealing strong alignment with expert ratings (intraclass correlation coefficient, >0.8). Pordzik et al12 used 6-point Likert scales to rate ChatGPT-4o’s answers to sleep apnea frequently asked questions, finding high mean scores across categories such as coherence and conciseness, with interrater agreement metrics like Fleiss κ indicating reliable evaluations. In the study by Kim et al,6 both experts and laypeople rated ChatGPT and specialists on clarity and medical helpfulness, with statistically significant differences in preference depending on evaluator type. Some studies also use binary evaluations for completeness and risk or compare response length and comprehensiveness.

Cheong et al10 used the PEMAT tool and Flesch-Kincaid readability scores to evaluate ChatGPT and Google Bard across 46 OSA-related questions.10 Alapati et al24 also applied structured binary metrics and readability scores to rate ChatGPT’s insomnia responses, reporting high levels of clinical accuracy despite moderate interrater reliability. Together, these metrics provide structured validation of LLM outputs in sleep health contexts, revealing not just accuracy but also usability and risk potential.

Open Challenges and Future Directions

Accuracy and Hallucinations

Large language models can generate fluent responses, but concerns remain about their factual reliability and potential to hallucinate medically inaccurate or misleading information.39,40 Although most responses are generally accurate, studies have found occasional errors or omissions, particularly when addressing newer or specialized therapies, such as HGNS, where LLM training data are sparse or outdated.12 For instance, ChatGPT-4o confused medical equipment terminology owing to language ambiguity, which could mislead patients if not carefully supervised.12 Similarly, Campbell et al9 reported that although GPT-3.5 produced mostly correct answers to sleep apnea queries, few responses were perfect, and the inclusion of references improved only when specifically prompted.

Cheong et al10 found that ChatGPT’s responses to OSA questions were consistently rated more understandable and actionable than those from Bard, with no dangerous errors detected, yet the study reinforced the need for structured evaluation frameworks to ensure reliability in diverse sleep domains. Alapati et al24 also reported generally accurate and informative insomnia-related responses from ChatGPT, although expert raters showed only moderate agreement, pointing to ongoing concerns about consistency in clinical interpretations.

These findings not only underscore the need for domain-specific fine-tuning and regular reevaluation to maintain accuracy but also highlight the risk of overconfidence in systems that may produce fluent but occasionally misleading responses. Despite improvements in newer models, hallucinations remain a nontrivial risk that warrants expert oversight and further research.9,12 Future work should incorporate standardized outcome instruments, such as fact-checking benchmarks and structured medical quality assurance data sets, to systematically track hallucination rates. Larger user studies across diverse populations (eg, older adults and individuals with limited health literacy) could reveal how hallucinations differentially affect trust and adherence in real-world contexts.

Personalization at Scale

Delivering personalized sleep insights at scale presents major challenges involving data complexity, computational demands, and privacy concerns. Subramanian et al16 highlight that typical LLM pipelines like RAG and fine-tuning are insufficient for integrating rich, temporal, and multimodal wearable data, leading to generic or low-actionability outputs. Their proposed graph-augmented framework mitigates this by leveraging intrapatient and interpatient similarity graphs and dynamically generated feature importance scores to tailor LLM prompts, significantly improving personalization without retraining models on every new user. Meanwhile, Cosentino et al17 stress the need for models like PH-LLM that are explicitly fine-tuned on sleep and fitness data. They show that fine-tuning enables the system to contextualize raw time-series sensor input and produce recommendations nearly on par with expert clinicians. However, they also caution that scaling such personalization requires not only technical advances in multimodal reasoning but also ethical handling of sensitive data and fairness across user demographics.17 Future systems must balance personalization quality with real-time responsiveness, cost-efficiency, and rigorous safeguards for user data.

Future systems must therefore balance personalization quality with real-time responsiveness, cost-efficiency, and rigorous safeguards for user data, while recognizing that current personalization efforts remain preliminary and often untested in diverse, real-world populations. Concrete next steps include testing personalization frameworks in populations beyond early adopters, such as adolescents, shift workers, and elderly users. Rigorous evaluation in randomized or crossover study designs could clarify how personalization impacts adherence and health outcomes. There is also a gap in outcome measures that combine both behavioral change (eg, validated adherence indices) and clinical sleep improvements.

Long-term Efficacy

Most existing systems for sleep wellness using LLMs are still in early developmental stages, typically validated through short-term pilot studies. For instance, ZzzMate demonstrated promising results over a 2-week trial, showing improved emotional intelligence and sleep adherence through empathetic interactions with just 4 participants.13 Similarly, the ChatGLM-LoRA–based insomnia intervention application underwent only a 1-week trial with 16 users, yielding positive feedback but lacking rigorous statistical power or extended observation periods.14 Chan et al25 conducted a randomized controlled trial comparing dCBT-I with and without chatbot coaching over several weeks, showing that LLM-style digital coaching improved insomnia outcomes and adherence metrics compared with the control group coaching.25

These systems highlight encouraging feasibility and user engagement, yet their findings remain preliminary. The heavy reliance on short-term and small-scale studies limits external validity. There is a critical need for larger, longitudinal studies with diverse user populations to assess not only behavioral outcomes but also clinical sleep improvements over time. Such studies must also account for variability in sleep disorders, user adherence, and evolving interaction patterns to evaluate sustained impact and generalizability across real-world contexts. Bilal et al11 also propose evaluating LLM-driven sleep tools based on their ability to promote awareness and encourage self-diagnosis in real-world users, suggesting a potential behavioral impact pathway beyond traditional clinical trials.

Future studies must move beyond short-term pilots by using longitudinal randomized controlled trials lasting several months, with validated instruments like the ISI and PSQI. Research should also target heterogeneous user groups (including individuals with comorbid conditions) to assess generalizability and long-term safety.

Integration of Multimodal Inputs

Integrating diverse physiological signals like EEG, wearable-derived activity data, and self-reported text presents both technical and conceptual challenges in sleep wellness applications. Sano et al27 demonstrated that although LLMs can generate personalized sleep feedback using EEG features and actograms, their ability to accurately interpret and integrate complex data types remains limited without fine-tuning or specialized prompts. Further, LLMs often struggle with high-dimensional inputs such as EEG spectrograms, requiring structured preprocessing and context-aware prompting for meaningful outputs. Similarly, Fang et al15 showed that combining statistical summaries from wearable sensors with LLM-driven conversations led to deeper user understanding and more actionable sleep goals. However, meaningful multimodal integration requires aligning temporally disjointed data (eg, daytime activity with nighttime sleep) and translating correlation patterns into behaviorally relevant insights.

The user study by Corda et al19 highlighted that LLM-generated explanations boosted trust and engagement in behavioral suggestions. Jang et al26 analyzed over 500 users’ chatbot-based sleep logs against Fitbit data, revealing substantial individual-level discrepancies despite population-level alignment, illustrating the difficulty of reconciling subjective and sensor-derived sleep data in real-world contexts.

Overall, although promising prototypes exist, scalable and reliable multimodal pipelines remain an open challenge. Moreover, many current evaluations are proxy-based (eg, chatbot logs vs Fitbit records), which introduces risk of bias and raises questions about ecological validity. Advancing multimodal integration will require new evaluation protocols that combine objective (eg, EEG and actigraphy) and subjective (eg, ecological momentary assessments) measures. Target populations such as adolescents or patients with irregular sleep patterns should be prioritized because they highlight the limitations of current proxy-based approaches. A major gap remains in developing outcome instruments that capture both crossmodal alignment and behavioral impact.

Conclusion

This survey underscores the emerging promise of LLMs in personal sleep wellness, spanning education, condition-specific support, personalized coaching, and CBT delivery. Although early results show strong potential for emotionally intelligent and context-aware interventions, key challenges persist—including hallucination risks, limited personalization at scale, and lack of long-term validation. Technical barriers around integrating multimodal data further constrain current systems. Nonetheless, innovations in fine-tuning, prompt engineering, and hybrid architectures suggest a viable path forward. Future research should prioritize rigorous evaluation, privacy-conscious personalization, and multimodal reasoning to ensure that LLM-driven tools become safe, effective, and equitable companions in everyday sleep health management.
