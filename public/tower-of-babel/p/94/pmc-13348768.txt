Q fever in Kazakhstan: seroepidemiology, molecular evidence, and spatial distribution across livestock and ticks
Authors: Yuliya V Perfilyeva, Akzhigit S Mashzhan, Moldir M Kuatbek, Yekaterina O Ostapchuk, Zhaniya M Dosmagambet, Andrey V Zhigailov, Artyom V Kuligin, Akerke O Bissenbay, Saltanat A Kuatbekova, Sofiya A Kan, Anzhelika V Lushova, Nurshat Abdolla, Anna S Nizkorodova, Zhanna Zh Shapiyeva, Zaure Z Sayakova, Anastassiya V Perfilyeva, Zhanna A Berdygulova, Elina R Maltseva, Yuriy A Skiba, Andrey M Dmitrovskiy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundQ fever, caused by Coxiella burnetii, remains a neglected zoonosis in Kazakhstan, with limited epidemiological data available. This study assessed the occurrence, spatial distribution, and risk factors of Q fever nationwide.ResultsBetween 2019 and 2024, 1,314 cattle and 1,689 small ruminants were sampled from 115 to 149 villages/cities, respectively, and 3,073 ticks were collected from 39 districts across Kazakhstan. Blood serum samples were screened for anti-C. burnetii antibodies by ELISA, while DNA from sheep milk and ticks was analyzed by qPCR, followed by partial sequencing of the 16 S rRNA and IS1111 genes. Multiple spacer sequence typing was performed on selected C. burnetii - positive samples. Risk factors were evaluated in a GEE model, and spatial risk maps were generated via inverse distance weighting interpolation.The apparent individual seroprevalence was significantly higher in small ruminants (30.6%; 95% CI: 28.4 - 32.9) than in cattle (6.6%; 95% CI: 5.3 - 8.1; p < 0.0001). Herd-level prevalence was 12.9% (95% CI: 8.4 – 17.6) in cattle and 60.2% (95% CI: 52.5 - 67.6; p < 0.0001) in small ruminants. Pavlodar oblast exhibited the highest individual and herd-level seroprevalence in both species, whereas low seroprevalence (≤ 11%) was observed in Atyrau, Mangystau, East Kazakhstan, and North Kazakhstan oblasts. Herd size was identified as a significant risk factor in both cattle and small ruminants. Coxiella burnetii DNA was detected in three of 77 (4%; 95% CI: 0.8 - 10.9) tested sheep milk samples. Overall, 7.6% of ticks (234/3,073; 95% CI: 6.7 - 8.6) tested positive for Coxiella spp., primarily H. scupense and D. niveus, likely including non-pathogenic endosymbionts. Thirteen samples were successfully sequenced and showed 99.7%-100% nucleotide identity in the IS1111 gene with reference C. burnetii isolates. The allele profile identified in the present study for the loci Cox2 - Cox5 - Cox18 was 3-8-15.ConclusionQ fever should be regarded as a potentially underrecognized and underreported infection in Kazakhstan. The exceptionally high prevalence observed in Pavlodar oblast in small ruminants and cattle underscores the urgent need to initiate monitoring of Q fever in the human population.Keywords: Coxiella burnetii, Kazakhstan, Livestock, Spatial distribution

Background

Q fever is a significant zoonotic disease with a global distribution, caused by the bacterium Coxiella burnetii [1, 2]. Coxiella burnetii is a strictly intracellular gram-negative bacterium. The pathogen is extremely infectious, with only a few organisms needed to initiate infection. According to Brooke et al. [3], the dose for 50% infection in humans is estimated at just 1.18 bacterial cells.

Numerous mammalian, avian, and arthropod species serve as natural reservoirs of the pathogen. Coxiella burnetii has been detected in approximately 40 tick species, which may serve as vectors of Q fever for domestic animals but rarely for humans [4]. Domestic ruminants, especially sheep and, to a lesser degree, cattle and goats, represent the primary sources of human Q fever outbreaks [5, 6]. Infection in animals is typically latent, yet they may continuously shed C. burnetii into the environment. Indirect transmission via windborne dispersal of contaminated aerosols is recognized as the most common route of human infection [4]. In female animals, bacterial shedding intensifies dramatically during parturition, when millions of organisms can be released per gram of placental tissue [7]. This intermittent but massive contamination of the environment makes Q fever outbreaks difficult to predict and facilitates rapid spread to large numbers of people, posing a substantial public health challenge [4].

In humans, the infection may present as either an acute illness - most commonly a self-limiting febrile syndrome, atypical pneumonia, or hepatitis - or as a chronic infection, typically manifesting as endocarditis [6]. Chronic disease occurs predominantly in individuals with pre-existing valvular heart conditions and, to a lesser extent, in immunocompromised patients and pregnant women [8]. Chronic Q fever is often associated with substantial long-term morbidity, requiring prolonged combination antibiotic therapy, and many patients ultimately require surgical valve replacement. Despite appropriate treatment, reported mortality rates for chronic Q fever are high ranging from 18% to 38% [9, 10].

Analysis of the available literature indicates that Q fever is persistently present in many countries worldwide, with recurrent outbreaks underscoring the need for surveillance of the infection. In a systematic review, Tan et al. reported 81 distinct human Q fever outbreaks across 27 countries between 1990 and 2022. These outbreaks varied widely in size, ranging from 2 to 4,107 cases. Of particular concern, more than half of the outbreaks occurred in community settings outside of traditional high-risk occupational groups [6]. This finding contrasts sharply with the long-held assumption that Q fever predominantly affects individuals with direct livestock exposure - such as farmers, veterinarians, abattoir workers, livestock transporters, shearers, and wool classers - or that it is confined mainly to rural environments. A Q fever outbreak associated with non-occupational exposure was confirmed in March 2022 in a semi-urban area of Croatia. Among 23 identified cases, three patients required hospitalization, and one fatal case was reported [11]. A total of 805 confirmed Q fever cases were reported in the European Union in 2023, corresponding to a notification rate of 0.19 cases per 100,000 population [12]. Between February and October 2024, four Q-fever outbreaks were reported across three districts of Nepal [13], urging national public health and veterinary authorities to initiate surveillance of the infection.

In Kazakhstan, Q fever is included in the official state statistical reporting system, indicating that the infection is formally under epidemiological surveillance. However, active surveillance and routine laboratory diagnostics remain largely absent. This gap persists despite several studies demonstrating the presence of C. burnetii infection in both human and domestic animal populations. Head et al. [14] reported a weighted seroprevalence of 1.3% among the human population in the southern region of Kazakhstan. Tsoi [15] demonstrated seroprevalences of 13.2% in cattle and 17.6% in small ruminants in 1984. Antibodies to C. burnetii were also detected in saiga, a species of antelope that inhabits the Ural steppes, Ustyurt Plateau and Betpak-Dala areas in Kazakhstan [16]. In addition, C. burnetii DNA was identified in ticks collected from farm animals in the southern region of the country [17]. In our recent study, animal seroprevalence varied widely across districts in southern Kazakhstan, ranging from 0% to 55.5% [18]. Collectively, these studies have focused almost exclusively on the southern region of the country, which is historically considered endemic for several zoonotic infections. In contrast, the prevalence of Q fever in other regions of the country remains largely uninvestigated, despite reports of very high Q fever incidence rates of up to 9.31 per 100,000 population in some regions of Russia neighboring Kazakhstan [19]. In this study, we aimed to study the spatial distribution of C. burnetii infection across the country and genotype the detected C. burnetii strains. To achieve this, we tested cattle and small ruminants for C. burnetii antibodies and screened ticks and milk samples for the presence of C. burnetii DNA.

Methods and materials

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the local ethics committee of the National Center for Biotechnology (approval No. 4, October 24, 2023). All animals were handled carefully to minimize discomfort during tick, blood and milk collection, in accordance with good animal practice.

Study area

Kazakhstan is the ninth-largest country in the world, occupying a territory of approximately 2.7 million km². It is located in Central Asia and borders China, Kyrgyzstan, Russia, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan. Administratively, the country is divided into 17 regions (oblasts) and three cities of republican significance: Astana, Almaty, and Shymkent. Kazakhstan’s vast territory includes steppe, desert, semi-desert, and mountainous regions that support extensive livestock grazing and pastoralism. Livestock production plays a central role in the agricultural sector. According to official statistics, Kazakhstan’s livestock sector comprises 21,700.7 thousand small ruminants (sheep and goats) and 8,376 thousand cattle. Cattle are distributed among households (45.1%), peasant/farm enterprises (44.3%), and agricultural enterprises (10.6%). A similar distribution is observed for small ruminants, with sheep and goats also kept largely in households (39.4%) and farm enterprises (54.0%), highlighting the importance of non-industrial production in the national livestock profile [20].

Sample size calculation and sample collection

Due to the absence of a comprehensive and up-to-date national registry of livestock operations, true simple random sampling was not feasible. Therefore, a convenience-based sampling strategy was employed to select villages and herds across all 17 oblasts of Kazakhstan. Field veterinarians collaborated with local veterinary authorities at the district or oblast levels to obtain available lists of farms and livestock owners. Farms were subsequently contacted and invited to participate in the study, with selection influenced by accessibility and logistical considerations.

The required sample size was calculated assuming an expected infection prevalence of 50%, a confidence level of 95%, and a maximum allowable error of 5%, resulting in a minimum sample size of 384 animals [21]. The obtained sample size was further adjusted for design effects (DE) to accommodate the clustering of observations: DE = 1 + ρ (m-1), where ρ denotes the intra-cluster correlation coefficient and m indicates the cluster size (number of animals sampled per herd) [22]. Using a ρ - value of 0.2 [23] and an average herd size of 16 small ruminants and 10 cattle, the total sample size was estimated to be 1,536 small ruminants and 1,075 cattle.

Blood samples were drawn by a trained veterinarian from the jugular vein, and serum was separated prior to freezing. Mid-flow milk samples were manually collected from the udder into sterile 5-mL tubes. All samples were transported at − 20 °C in a mobile freezer to the Almaty Branch of the National Center for Biotechnology for further analysis. During sampling, data were also recorded on herd size as well as the age, sex and breed of the animals.

Ticks were collected from vegetation by flagging. Ticks were also collected opportunistically from rodents and saiga antelope during authorized wildlife monitoring and veterinary activities conducted by official services. Wild rodents were captured using Sherman-type live traps and examined for ectoparasites using the standardized combing method. Saiga were individually herded into a raised net using motorbikes, then briefly physically restrained for sampling. To minimize stress, each captured animal was examined as quickly as possible, and only two to three attached ticks were removed using specialized forceps in accordance with established safety procedures, after which the animals were immediately released. All procedures were performed by trained personnel. No wild animals were captured or handled specifically for this study. All ticks were placed into plastic vials and transported to the laboratory at 4 °C for further examination.

Tick identification and homogenization

Hard ticks were identified to species and sex using a published taxonomic key [24] and a binocular microscope Mikromed MC-2-Zoom (version 2 A) with a ToupCam video eyepiece (1.2 M). Individual ticks were then homogenized using a Mixer Mill MM 400 (Retsch) for 3 min at 30 Hz after adding 3 mm stainless steel grinding beads (Retsch) and 500 µl of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to each tube. After homogenization, the tubes were centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was frozen and stored at − 20 °C for further analysis.

DNA extraction

Tick DNA was extracted by selective precipitation using the MagnoPlus-NK-Bio DNA/RNA extraction kit (form F+) (Intifika, Russia) on the automated AutoMag96 workstation (Allsheng, China). DNA from milk samples was extracted using the DNeasy® Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Netherlands) following the protocol described by Basanisi et al. [25]. Isolated DNA was stored at -20 °C until further processing.

Detection and characterization of C. burnetii

Molecular screening of DNA extracted from ticks and sheep milk for Coxiella spp. DNA was performed by real-time PCR using the AmpliSens®Coxiella burnetii-FRT kit (AmpliSens, Russia), which targets the IS1111 transposase gene. Amplification was carried out on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) under thermocycling conditions specified by the manufacturer. Tick samples were processed both individually and in pools. Pooling was applied to increase testing efficiency. Pools were formed based on the collection site rather than tick species or sex. All ticks from PCR-positive pools were subsequently tested individually to confirm positive findings.

To differentiate zoonotic Coxiella pathogens from Coxiella endosymbionts, partial sequencing of the 16 S rRNA and IS1111 genes was performed for PCR-positive samples with a cycle threshold (Ct) value ≤ 25 using a DNA sequencing kit for C. burnetii (Vector-Best, Russia), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Conventional PCR products were resolved by electrophoresis using the EasyCast D2 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in 1.5% agarose gels containing 0.2 mg/mL ethidium bromide (Sigma, USA) in 1× TAE buffer. DNA bands were visualized using a gel documentation system (Bio-Rad, USA). DNA fragments were excised from gels and purified using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, USA). Purified amplicons were sequenced bidirectionally using the BigDye® Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, USA) on an ABI 3500XL genetic analyzer (Applied Biosystems). The obtained IS1111 and 16 S rRNA genes were analyzed using BLAST comparison in the NCBI database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

For genotyping of detected C. burnetii strains, all positive samples were subjected to conventional PCR using three primer pairs - Cox2F/Cox2R, Cox5F/Cox5R, and Cox18F/Cox18R - targeting three intergenic spacers [26]. These spacers were used for multispacer sequence typing (MST). Individual spacer sequences of C. burnetii obtained in this study were concatenated, and the MST genotype was determined by comparing the results with the international C. burnetii MST database [27]. Multiple sequence alignments were performed using the MUSCLE algorithm. A model with the lowest BIC scores (Bayesian Information Criterion) was considered to describe the substitution pattern the best. Phylogenetic relationships among MST genotypes were inferred using the Maximum Likelihood method implemented in MEGA version 11 software [28]. Statistical support for internal nodes was assessed by nonparametric bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates [29].

Serologic testing

Blood serum samples from cattle and small ruminants were tested for antibodies against C. burnetii using the ID Screen® Q Fever Indirect Multi-species ELISA kit (IDVet, France), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. This assay detects C. burnetii - specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies against both phase I and phase II antigens. According to the manufacturer, the test has a reported sensitivity and specificity of 100%. Each assay run included appropriate positive and negative controls.

Spatial analysis

The spatial distribution of Q fever prevalence across Kazakhstan was assessed by interpolating seroprevalence data obtained from sampled villages using the Spatial Analyst extension of ArcMap v10.8 (Esri Inc., Redlands, CA, USA). Interpolation was first performed separately for cattle and small ruminants based on animal-level seroprevalence estimates derived from serological surveys, with villages treated as the spatial aggregation unit. The inverse distance weighting (IDW) method with a power parameter of 2 was applied to predict Q fever prevalence in unsampled areas and to generate continuous seroprevalence surfaces across the territory of the Republic of Kazakhstan. IDW was selected due to its minimal assumptions and suitability for unevenly distributed data [30]. To further identify districts with high and low risk of Q fever infection, mean seroprevalence values combining data from both cattle and small ruminants were extracted from the interpolated surfaces at the administrative district level using the Zonal Statistics tool within the Spatial Analyst module of ArcMap v10.8.

Spatial autocorrelation analysis was performed to assess whether the observed distribution of Q fever seroprevalence in livestock exhibited random, dispersed, or clustered spatial patterns. Spatial dependence was evaluated using Moran’s I statistic, which measures the similarity in attribute values across adjacent spatial units [31]. A statistically significant Moran’s I value (|Z| > 1.96; p < 0.05) was interpreted as evidence against the null hypothesis of spatial randomness, indicating the presence of significant spatial autocorrelation in Q fever seroprevalence [32].

Statistical analysis

A generalized estimating equations (GEE) model was used to analyze factors associated with C. burnetii infection in cattle and small ruminants, as multiple observations (sampled animals) from the same herd are not statistically independent [33]. Apparent animal-level seroprevalence was selected as the dependent variable. Odds ratios (ORs) and robust standard errors were estimated using an exchangeable working correlation structure to account for within-herd (farm-level) clustering, implemented in STATA v14.2 (StataCorp). In the first step, univariate analyses were conducted to assess associations between seropositivity and each independent variable. Independent variables included age, sex, animal species, production category/breed, and herd size. To avoid sparse categories, cattle breeds were grouped as follows: dual-purpose breeds (crossbred, Simmental), dairy breeds (Holstein Friesian, Black-and-White, Red Steppe), and beef breeds (Aberdeen Angus, Hereford, Kazakh White-headed, Kalmyk, Charolais, Santa Gertrudis, Auliekol). Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis and no evidence of collinearity were included in the multivariate GEE model. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Associations between characteristics and results of molecular screening of ticks were examined in a logistic regression model in STATA v14.2 (StataCorp) software.

Results

Q fever spatial distribution

Between 2019 and 2024, a total of 1,314 serum samples from cattle and 1,689 serum samples from small ruminants (1,660 sheep and 29 goats) were collected across 115 and 149 villages/cities (Fig. 1), respectively. In addition, milk samples were obtained from 77 lactating sheep. The mean number of animals sampled per herd (farm) was 10 for small ruminants (range: 1–31) and 7 for cattle (range: 1–32). The surveyed farms varied markedly in size, ranging from small private household holdings with a single animal to large commercial operations housing up to 6,500 head of cattle and 3,900 head of small ruminants.

Serological testing of cattle demonstrated an overall animal-level seroprevalence of antibodies against C. burnetii of 6.6% (87/1,314; 95% CI: 5.3–8.1). At least one seropositive animal was detected in 12.2% of surveyed herds (24/196; 95% CI: 8.4–17.6). No seropositive cattle were identified in Jetisu, Mangystau, West Kazakhstan, Aktobe, North Kazakhstan, or Ulytau oblasts. Across oblasts, animal-level seroprevalence ranged from 1.6% to 44.2%, with the highest seroprevalence observed in Pavlodar oblast (Table 1).

Small ruminants exhibited significantly higher individual and flock seroprevalence than cattle (p < 0.0001), with an animal-level prevalence of 30.6% (517/1,689; 95% CI: 28.4–32.9) and a herd-level seroprevalence of 60.2% (103/171; 95% CI: 52.5–67.6). Seropositive small ruminants were detected in all the oblasts of the country. Consistent with the findings in cattle, the highest animal-level seroprevalence among small ruminants was recorded in Pavlodar oblast (84%). Across most oblasts, animal-level seroprevalence ranged from 19.9% to 71%. Lower rates of Q fever seroprevalence (5% − 8.3%) were only observed in Atyrau, Mangystau and East Kazakhstan oblasts (Table 1).

The spatial trends of Q fever seroprevalence among cattle and small ruminant populations across the territory of Kazakhstan are illustrated in Fig. 2. The conducted spatial analysis predicted a high prevalence of Q fever (mean district-level seroprevalence in livestock ranging from 38 to 65%) in the western region of Kazakhstan (Zhanibek, Martuk, Kargaly, Aktobe, Alga, Ayteke Bi and Khromtau districts), the southern region (Sauran, Baidibek and Turar Ryskulov districts), the central region (Karkaraly district), and the northern region (Atbasar, Valikhanov, and Birzhan Sal districts, as well as most of Pavlodar oblast) (Fig. 3). In contrast, districts classified as having low Q fever prevalence, characterized by district-level seroprevalence values ranging from 0% to 8% in livestock, included Mangystau oblast, the majority of districts in Atyrau, Kostanay, North Kazakhstan, Ulytau, and Karagandy oblasts, as well as several districts within Abai oblast. Global Moran’s I analysis at the village level demonstrated weak-to-moderate but highly significant positive spatial autocorrelation of Q fever seroprevalence (Moran’s I = 0.15, Z = 4.3, p = 0.000013).

Q fever risk factor analysis

In the univariate GEE model, seropositivity to C. burnetii in cattle was significantly higher in the northern region of the country compared with other regions (p < 0.001), as well as in large herds (p < 0.001). Sex, age, and production category were not significantly associated with C. burnetii infection in cattle (Supplementary Table 1). In the final multivariate model, herd size of > 1,000 cattle emerged as the strongest risk factor, increasing the odds of C. burnetii infection by 7.6-fold (p < 0.0001). The overall model was highly significant (Wald chi² (2) = 37.61) (Table 2).

In small ruminants, sex, age, breed, and region were not significantly associated with Q fever seroprevalence in the univariate analysis. Herd size of > 100 animals emerged as the only factor significantly associated with Q fever seroprevalence (Supplementary Table 2).

Molecular detection of C. burnetii in sheep milk

To assess C. burnetii shedding, DNA was extracted from milk samples collected from lactating sheep in three regions of Kazakhstan (Table 1). Quantitative PCR screening detected C. burnetii DNA in three of the 77 analyzed milk samples, corresponding to an animal-level prevalence of 4.0% (95% CI: 0.8–10.9) and a herd-level prevalence of 8.7% (2/23) (Fig. 1). The cycle threshold (Ct) values of the PCR-positive samples were 34, 30, and 26. We subsequently attempted partial sequencing of the IS1111 and 16 S rRNA gene fragments for all three PCR-positive samples. Sequencing was successful for only one sample with a Ct of 26, which was obtained from a seronegative sheep in the Turkistan oblast. Partial sequencing of the 16 S rRNA gene confirmed the presence of C. burnetii (Supplementary Table 3). The Q fever seroprevalence in the two herds where PCR-positive animals were detected was 31% in the Turkestan oblast and 100% in the Almaty oblast.

Molecular detection of C. burnetii in ticks

Between March 2019 and September 2024, a total of 3,073 ticks were collected across 39 districts and administratively independent cities in 14 oblasts of Kazakhstan (Fig. 4). The vast majority of ticks (n = 3,018) were collected from vegetation, while smaller numbers were obtained from animal hosts, including saiga antelope (n = 30), sheep (n = 4), and great gerbils (Rhombomys opimus) (n = 21). Of the ticks collected, the vast majority were adults (82.7%; 2,542/3,073); nymphal stages represented a small proportion of the total sample (1.9%; 59/3,073). Life stage was not identified for 472 ticks collected due to human error. Females were more abundant (51.4%; 1,579/3,073) than males (41.7%; 1,284/3,073), while the sex of 210 ticks was not determined. The collected ticks belonged to five genera, including Hyalomma spp., Dermacentor spp., Ixodes spp., Haemaphysalis spp., and Rhipicephalus spp. The most frequently collected species was H. scupense (n = 1,102), followed by D. marginatus (n = 592) and D. niveus (n = 439). Other species included D. reticulatus (n = 416), H. asiaticum (n = 191), R. pumilio (n = 111), H. anatolicum (n = 107), H. excavatum (n = 55), I. persulcatus (n = 55), and H. punctata (n = 5) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Homogenates of 2,834 ticks were combined into 290 pools, with 2–10 ticks per pool (mean = 10 ticks per pool); a total of 239 ticks were tested individually. Ticks from PCR-positive pools were subsequently tested individually. A small number of pools (n = 6) screened positive, but individual retesting of constituent ticks was negative. This discrepancy could be due to trace amounts of pathogen DNA in pooled samples that were below the detection limit upon individual testing. These samples were therefore considered unconfirmed and excluded from individual-level prevalence estimates. As a result of the analysis, Coxiella spp. DNA was detected in 234 ticks, yielding an overall prevalence of 7.6% (95% CI: 6.7–8.6), which likely includes a substantial proportion of non-pathogenic Coxiella-like endosymbionts. The highest prevalence of Coxiella spp. infection in ticks was observed in the southern region of Kazakhstan (p = 0.0001) (Table 3). PCR-positive ticks were identified in four of the 14 tested oblasts, specifically in West Kazakhstan, Zhambyl, Jetisu, and Mangystau oblasts (Table 3; Fig. 4). The highest prevalence was observed in the Talas and Moiynkum districts of Zhambyl oblast, where 50 of 52 (96.2%) and 49 of 54 (90.7%) collected ticks, respectively, tested positive for Coxiella spp.

Coxiella spp. DNA was detected predominantly in D. niveus (41.7%; 99/237) and H. scupense (11.7%; 129/1,102), which together accounted for the majority of positive samples. Coxiella spp. bacteria were also detected in R. pumilio and H. excavatum ticks. All PCR-positive ticks were questing ticks, with the exception of samples from West Kazakhstan oblast, which were collected from saiga antelope; among these ticks, 37% tested positive for Coxiella spp. (Table 3). All PCR-positive ticks were identified at the imago stage; no nymphal ticks tested positive. Females tested positive for Coxiella spp. DNA (9.7%; 153/1,579) more often than males (6.3%; 81/1,284, p = 0.001).

The mean Ct value of the PCR-positive samples was 30.1 ± 4.2. Samples with Ct values ≤ 25 (n = 26) were selected for subsequent sequencing. Of the analyzed samples, only 19 were successfully sequenced. Partial 16 S rRNA gene sequences predominantly yielded closest matches to Coxiella-like endosymbionts. However, partial 16 S rRNA sequencing is often insufficient to reliably discriminate between pathogenic C. burnetii and Coxiella endosymbionts, due to the high sequence conservation of this gene within the Coxiella spp., particularly when shorter amplicons are used [34–36]. Only one sample showed the closest similarity to C. burnetii based on the 16 S rRNA gene (98.92%). IS1111 insertion element sequences were obtained for 13 of the 19 sequenced samples. BLAST searches of the obtained partial IS1111 gene sequences demonstrated complete or near-complete identity (99.7–100%) with reference C. burnetii strains for all 13 samples (Supplementary Table 2). Positive samples originated from the Talas and Moiynkum districts of Zhambyl oblast, the Panfilov district of Jetisu oblast, and Mangystau oblast.

Genetic characterization of C. burnetii

We then performed MST-based genetic characterization of selected C. burnetii strains. A Maximum Likelihood phylogenetic tree based on concatenated sequences is shown in Fig. 5. The resulting topology suggested the presence of two major lineages broadly consistent with previously described MST-associated clusters. All six isolates (black circles) shared an identical allele profile (Cox2.3-Cox5.8-Cox18.15) and clustered within a single lineage, indicating limited genetic variability among the analyzed samples. Within this lineage, the isolates were positioned alongside reference sequences from Europe and the Middle East, including those related to MST16, MST20, and MST61. Bootstrap support for the broader lineage was moderate, while internal branching among the study isolates was weak (less than 20%), indicating limited phylogenetic resolution. The Cox2.3-Cox5.8-Cox18.15 profile of the detected strains was consistent with partial patterns reported for MST92/93. However, as only three of the ten MST loci were analyzed, this classification should be considered provisional.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the nationwide extent of Q fever infection in farmed ruminants and ticks across Kazakhstan.

Q fever prevalence in domestic ruminants in Kazakhstan

Samples from farmed ruminants for this study were collected from all oblasts of the country, allowing the study population to be considered broadly representative of the national livestock. It should be noted that livestock production in Kazakhstan is dominated by sheep farming, with cattle raised to a lesser extent and goats being relatively rare. Therefore, although few samples were obtained from goats, the seroprevalence estimates for small ruminants reported in this study largely reflect Q fever infection in sheep.

The seroprevalence of Q fever in cattle in Kazakhstan observed in this study is much lower or comparable to that reported in several European and Asian countries, including Italy [37, 38], Estonia [39], Slovenia [40], India [41], and China [42]. In contrast, the seroprevalence observed in small ruminants is markedly higher than that reported in many European countries, such as Italy [43], Greece [44], Switzerland [45], Great Britain [46], and Estonia [39], as well as in parts of Asia such as Turkey [47] and northeastern China [42]. Comparable or higher levels of exposure in small ruminants have been reported only in some countries, including Iran [48], Saudi Arabia [49], and several African countries [50–54].

We observed a remarkable spatial variation in Q fever seroprevalence among cattle across Kazakhstan. Seropositive cattle were predominantly concentrated in the northern region of the country, with the highest animal- and herd-level seroprevalence recorded in Pavlodar oblast, while seropositive cattle herds were rare or absent in other regions. Similarly to cattle, the highest herd-level and animal-level seropositivity among small ruminants was also recorded in Pavlodar oblast. Notably, cattle samples from Pavlodar oblast were collected in 2024, whereas samples from small ruminants were collected in 2023, suggesting active circulation and possible persistence of infection in this area. One possible explanation for the elevated prevalence of Q fever in Pavlodar oblast is the intensive importation of breeding livestock, particularly cattle, which may contribute to the higher C. burnetii seroprevalence observed in cattle in this oblast compared with other oblasts. In 2024 alone, approximately 2,000 cattle were imported into Pavlodar oblast from European countries [20]. However, their C. burnetii infection status at importation is unknown, as routine testing of imported animals for Q fever is not performed in Kazakhstan. Therefore, a direct link between importation and the observed prevalence cannot be established. Implementation of routine screening of imported livestock for Q fever would be essential to assess the risk of pathogen introduction and strengthen control measures.

Elevated seroprevalence of Q fever in small ruminants was also evident in southern Kazakhstan. Overall, small ruminants exhibited substantially higher seroprevalence than cattle across all surveyed oblasts. The higher seroprevalence of Q fever observed in small ruminants compared with cattle in Kazakhstan can be attributed to several factors. Cattle typically shed lower quantities of C. burnetii and for shorter periods, resulting in reduced environmental contamination [55]. Small ruminants in Kazakhstan are predominantly raised in extensive or semi-nomadic production systems characterized by large flock sizes and communal grazing and lambing areas. This may facilitate efficient horizontal transmission and increase exposure to tick-infested pastures. Cattle, by comparison, are more commonly kept in semi-intensive systems without communal grazing or lambing areas, which may limit opportunities for pathogen spread.

Kazakhstan is a geographically vast country, which poses substantial challenges for comprehensive surveillance of infectious diseases. Q fever is currently not included in routine national surveillance programs, and only sporadic human cases have been officially reported since 2011: one case in West Kazakhstan oblast in 2017, one case in the same oblast in 2018, and one case in Zhambyl oblast in 2020 (data were obtained upon special request from the Scientific and Practical Center for Sanitary and Epidemiological Expertise and Monitoring, National Center for Public Health, Ministry of Health of the Republic of Kazakhstan). The nonspecific clinical presentation of Q fever further increases the likelihood of underdiagnosis [56]. In such settings, geographic information system (GIS) - based models provide valuable tools for disease prediction and support the prioritization of control measures by veterinary and public health authorities [57, 58]. In this study, we applied IDW interpolation to serological data from both cattle and small ruminants to identify districts with elevated predicted risk of Q fever, resulting in the identification of 22 priority areas. These findings were supported by the high Z-score and strong statistical significance observed in the Global Moran’s I analysis (p = 0.000013), which provides robust evidence of non-random spatial clustering of villages with similar prevalence levels and indicates the presence of localized transmission foci. Consequently, intensified surveillance for potential human Q fever cases should be considered in the identified districts. Surveillance for potential human Q fever should also be warranted in the Karasai district of Almaty oblast and the Sairam district of Turkistan oblast, where active shedding of C. burnetii in sheep milk was detected (all PCR-positive sheep were sampled in 2024).

We further investigated potential risk factors for Q fever in cattle and small ruminants in Kazakhstan. Sex, age, and breed/production category were not significantly associated with Q fever seroprevalence in either host species. These findings are consistent with previous studies that also reported no association between Q fever prevalence and animal gender [43, 53, 59–62] or age [59, 62, 63]. While most studies report higher Q fever seroprevalence in dairy cattle compared with beef cattle [39, 59, 64, 65], we did not observe such an association due to the small sample sizes within these production categories, as the majority of cattle in Kazakhstan are raised for dual purpose. Herd size emerged as the most significant risk factor for Q fever both in cattle and sheep. Animals originating from herds comprising more than 1,000 cattle and 100 small ruminants showed significantly higher seroprevalence compared with those from smaller herds. The association between herd size and an increased risk of C. burnetii infection has been well-established in studies from Europe [38, 43, 46, 66] and Africa [60, 67]. Larger herds typically include a greater number of breeding females, which represent a major source of C. burnetii shedding during parturition and consequently contribute to enhanced environmental contamination within the herd [68]. In addition, larger herd size reflects more intensive management practices, including higher animal density, increased animal movement, and more frequent introduction of new animals, all of which may potentially facilitate pathogen transmission. Also, the probability of pathogen introduction and persistence is inherently higher in larger populations, which may further contribute to the observed increase in seroprevalence.

Notably, goats exhibited a much higher seroprevalence (44.8%) than sheep (30.4%); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the smaller sample size of goats included in the study. Additional studies focusing on Q fever prevalence in goats are therefore needed to better define their epidemiological role as potential reservoirs of this infection in the country.

Q fever prevalence in ticks

Coxiella spp. DNA was detected in 7.6% of all tested ticks. This estimate should be interpreted with caution, as it likely reflects the presence of both pathogenic C. burnetii and non-pathogenic Coxiella-like endosymbionts [36, 69]. Coxiella-like endosymbionts are generally considered apathogenic, but sporadic studies showed pathogenic potential in particular cases. Fatal infections with Coxiella-like endosymbionts have been observed in birds [70–72]. Furthermore, human clinical manifestations such as skin-associated inflammation, scalp eschar, and regional lymphadenopathy caused by Coxiella-like bacteria have been described [73, 74]. Low DNA concentrations in most PCR-positive samples prevented successful sequencing, making discrimination between Coxiella-like endosymbionts and C. burnetii impossible. Despite these limitations, the overall prevalence of Coxiella spp. observed in this study is broadly consistent with C. burnetii detection rates reported in molecular surveys of ticks from other regions. Reported prevalence of C. burnetii in ticks ranges from 2.9% to 13.9% in Africa, from 2.5% to 10.5% in Europe, and from 4.8% to 12.5% in the Middle East [75].

In this study, Coxiella spp. were detected in several tick species, including D. niveus, H. scupense, H. excavatum, and R. pumilio, consistent with previous studies demonstrating that Coxiella bacteria have been identified in more than 40 tick species worldwide and are not restricted to a single tick species [8]. Further sequencing confirmed the presence of C. burnetii in D. niveus, H. scupense, and H. excavatum, collected in the southern and western regions of the country. In Kazakhstan, C. burnetii has previously been reported only in H. anatolicum and D. marginatus in Kazakhstan [17]. Outside Kazakhstan, the pathogen has also been documented in H. scupense in Pakistan, D. niveus in China, and H. excavatum in Egypt [76–78].

Female ticks were more frequently positive for Coxiella spp., which may be related to their more aggressive host-seeking behavior and longer blood-feeding periods compared with males. Of the 234 PCR-positive ticks, 11 were collected directly from animal hosts, whereas the majority were questing ticks. Notably, 11 positive ticks collected in West Kazakhstan originated from saiga antelope, suggesting the potential involvement of wildlife as an additional reservoir of Q fever. This finding is consistent with earlier serological studies reporting the presence of anti-C. burnetii antibodies in saiga antelope [16]. The saiga population amounts to 4.1 million animals as of April 2025 (updated population data for 2026 are expected to be released later this year) [20]. Given their large population size and extensive long-distance migratory routes, including transboundary movements between Kazakhstan and neighboring regions of Russia, saiga antelope may play a role in the wide geographic dissemination of C. burnetii. This highlights the potentially “borderless” nature of Q fever transmission, where wildlife movement may facilitate pathogen spread across national boundaries.

Multiple spacer typing is a widely applied method for genotyping C. burnetii [26], enabling identification of region-specific genotypes and comparison of strain profiles across studies for source tracing purposes [78, 79]. To date, 93 MST genotypes have been described in the MST database. Among these, MST23, MST25, MST27, and MST28 have previously been reported in Kazakhstan, while additional MST types, including MST2, MST7, MST31, and MST85, have previously been found in neighboring countries [27]. Sequencing of three of the ten spacers in this study yielded an allelic profile (3-8-15) consistent with patterns previously reported for MST92/93. However, as only a subset of loci was analyzed, this finding represents a preliminary indication rather than a definitive genotype assignment. Notably, this profile shares two spacer alleles with previously reported Kazakhstani genotypes (e.g., 3–8–1 corresponding to MST23/25), differing at a single locus, which may suggest local microevolution within circulating strains. At the same time, given the lack of published data on the geographic distribution of MST92/93 and the known movement of livestock and wildlife across borders, the introduction of genetically distinct strains from other regions cannot be excluded. In this study, the identified profile was detected in the southern region of Kazakhstan, a region characterized by active livestock trade and transboundary animal movement. Overall, the presence of this allelic pattern, in addition to those previously reported in the country, likely reflects a combination of local evolutionary dynamics and ongoing strain introduction. Complete MST profiling or whole-genome sequencing is required to confirm genotype assignment and resolve evolutionary relationships of detected C. burnetii strains.

A limitation of this study is the use of a convenience-based sampling strategy, which may have introduced selection bias and affected the representativeness of the dataset. Sampling was influenced by accessibility and logistical constraints, and the absence of a comprehensive national livestock registry further limited the ability to perform random sampling. In addition, sample sizes varied across regions, with some oblasts overrepresented and others underrepresented. These factors may have influenced both prevalence estimates and the spatial patterns observed, and therefore the results should be interpreted with caution, particularly when extrapolating findings to the oblast levels. The spatial analysis based on IDW interpolation also has inherent limitations. This approach assumes spatial continuity and may oversimplify complex and heterogeneous epidemiological patterns. When combined with uneven sampling density, this may reduce the accuracy of predicted risk surfaces, particularly in under-sampled regions. Therefore, the identified high- and low-risk areas should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive. The interpretation of molecular findings in ticks should also be approached cautiously. Detection of Coxiella spp. DNA does not necessarily indicate the presence of pathogenic C. burnetii, as ticks commonly harbor Coxiella-like endosymbionts. Due to low DNA concentrations in many samples, sequencing success was limited, preventing reliable differentiation between pathogenic and non-pathogenic Coxiella species in a substantial proportion of cases. In addition, pooling of tick samples, while improving testing efficiency, may have reduced analytical sensitivity and led to underestimation of infection prevalence. Similarly, the results of PCR detection of C. burnetii in sheep milk are based on a small number of positive samples and should be considered preliminary. Further studies with larger sample sizes are required to better assess the role of milk shedding in the epidemiology of Q fever in Kazakhstan. Finally, conclusions regarding MST-based genotyping should be interpreted with caution. Only three of the ten standard spacers were analyzed, and therefore the assignment of the detected allelic profile to MST92/93 remains provisional. Comprehensive MST typing or whole-genome sequencing will be necessary to confirm genotype classification and to more accurately resolve the evolutionary relationships and origin of circulating C. burnetii strains.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study provides the first nationwide evidence of Q fever presence across all regions of Kazakhstan and demonstrates that small ruminants constitute the primary reservoir of C. burnetii throughout the country. Q fever appears to be rare or absent in cattle in most regions of the country, with the notable exception of Pavlodar oblast, which is characterized by exceptionally high seroprevalence in both small ruminants and cattle. From both a public health and an animal health perspective, this study is important as it identifies priority districts where Q fever should be suspected in humans and where intensified, active surveillance is strongly recommended. Molecular screening of ticks followed by sequencing further supported the circulation of C. burnetii in some parts of the country. Taken together, these results highlight the importance of further investigations of Q fever.
