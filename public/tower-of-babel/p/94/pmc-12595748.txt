Associations of metabolic syndrome and its components with complex renal calculi and stone composition: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Siyuan Bu, Tinghong Zhang, Huaida Xu, Xiang Huang, Peng He, Jingxian Gao, Ruiji Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic syndrome (MS) is a risk factor for renal calculi development. This investigation intended to explore the associations of MS and its components with complex renal calculi and stone composition.MethodsThe study involved 465 patients with renal calculi who underwent surgical treatment at Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital. Patients were classified into non-complex and complex renal calculi groups. In the complex renal calculi group, patients were divided into subgroups based on the presence of MS, and separately based on abnormal MS components. Differences in stone size and composition among these subgroups were evaluated. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify key MS components associated with the progression of complex renal calculi, based on comparisons between non-complex and complex renal calculi patients.ResultsA total of 465 patients with renal calculi were included, of whom 197 had complex renal calculi. In patients with complex renal calculi, MS, high fasting blood glucose (FBG), and dyslipidemia were associated with larger stone size. Patients with MS had higher ammonium magnesium phosphate hexahydrate (MAP) and uric acid (UA) stone proportions. Patients with high body mass index (BMI) had a higher UA stone proportion. The carbonate apatite and MAP stone proportions were higher, while the calcium oxalate monohydrate stone proportion was lower in patients with high FBG. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that BMI (OR = 1.214, 95% CI: 1.033–1.295), FBG (OR = 1.795, 95% CI: 1.359–2.285), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol × 10 (HDL-C × 10; OR = 0.301, 95% CI: 0.210–0.421) were independently associated with complex renal calculi.ConclusionElevated BMI and FBG, along with reduced HDL-C, are associated with the presence of complex renal calculi. Monitoring these factors may improve disease management.Keywords: Complex renal calculi, Stone composition, Metabolic syndrome, Metabolic syndrome components, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MS), defined by the WHO as a pathological condition characterized by abdominal obesity, insulin resistance, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia, affects about one quarter of the global population [1]. Its components include excessive waist circumference, high blood pressure, increased fasting blood glucose (FBG), elevated triglyceride (TG), and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [2, 3]. Genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and neurohormonal activation all contribute to the complex pathogenesis of MS [4]. Studies have shown that MS and its components are associated with an increased risk of various diseases, such as ischemic stroke, depression, hyperuricemia, and chronic kidney disease [5–8].

Renal calculi are common urological conditions that result from mineral supersaturation in the urine, and their typical symptoms are reno-ureteral colic, while some patients experience loin pain [9]. The related manifestations include gross hematuria, vomiting, and fever, although certain patients present as asymptomatic. Kidney stones > 2.5 cm in diameter, staghorn stones, infectious stones, multiple stones, and stones in kidney with anatomical and functional abnormalities that cause difficulty in stone removal are considered complex renal calculi [10]. These challenging cases may lead to deterioration of kidney function, which directly increases the burden on medical and social services [11]. The complexity of the stones clinically makes the surgery more challenging and increases the risk of complications [12]. Recent studies have highlighted the different compositions of complex renal calculi and emphasized their roles in recurrent urinary tract infections and renal function impairments [13]. Therefore, analyzing stone composition is essential to understand the pathogenesis of the disease and provide individualized treatment for patients.

The causes of stone formation have shifted from infectious to metabolic over time, with MS identified as an independent risk factor for renal calculi development [14, 15]. Kim et al. found that the prevalence of MS in patients with renal calculi was 15.9%, which was higher than that in patients without renal calculi [16]. Studies have shown that metabolic and dietary risk patterns in patients with MS increase the risk of stone formation, which may be linked to elevated protein intake and acidic urine [17]. Although numerous studies have uncovered the correlation between renal calculi and MS, the metabolic factors that influence the composition and progression of complex renal calculi have not been explored.

This research investigated the relationship between stone composition and MS, as well as MS components, in patients with complex renal calculi. Differences in MS components between patients with non-complex and complex renal calculi were analyzed to explore the associations between MS components and complex stone formation.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study screened 571 patients with renal calculi who underwent surgical treatment at the Urology Department of Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital between January 2015 and October 2021. The study received ethical approval from Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent.

The inclusion criteria were those who were over 20 years old, demonstrated renal calculi-related symptoms, had confirmed renal calculi by imaging and surgical exploration, were fully informed about this investigation and signed informed consent, met the relevant requirements of the International Ethics Committee, exhibited high compliance and could actively cooperate with clinical treatment and nursing.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) hypercalcemia (serum calcium > 2.58 mmol/L), including hyperparathyroidism, lacto-alkali syndrome, sarcoidosis, vitamin D toxicity, hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma, increased cortisol levels, adrenal insufficiency, and vitamin A toxicity; (2) other potential causes of urinary calculi, such as renal tubular acidosis (characterized by polyuria, polydipsia, nocturia, fatigue, delayed growth, decreased blood pH, and increased urine pH; imaging may show multiple scattered large stones in both kidneys), gout, and certain genetically determined diseases (such as primary cystinuria and hyperuricemia); (3) partial congenital anatomic abnormalities that may directly or indirectly affect urinary dynamics within the renal collecting system, such as bladder neck stenosis, sponge kidney, ureteral stomatocele, renal cyst, neurogenic bladder, and congenital megaloureter; (4) a history of taking triamterene, drugs for HIV infection (such as indinavir), magnesium silicate, sulfonamides, acetazolamide, or corticosteroids within 2 years before admission; (5) refusal to undergo stone component analysis and follow-up after surgery; (6) incomplete stone removal according to surgical standards; and (7) complicated by malignant tumor. Based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, 465 participants were ultimately included in the study (Fig. 1).

Definition for complex renal calculi and MS

According to the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Urological and Andrological Diseases in China (2019 Edition) [18], those who met one of the following conditions were defined as having complex renal calculi: stones with a diameter of > 2.5 cm, staghorn stones, infectious stones, multiple stones, and stones in kidney with anatomical and functional abnormalities that cause difficulty in stone removal. According to this definition, 268 participants were excluded, and 197 participants with complex renal calculi were identified.

According to the revised diagnostic criteria suggested by the Chinese Diabetes Society (2004 Edition) [19], those who met at least three of the following four conditions were defined as having MS. (1) body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2 or waist circumference ≥ 90 cm; (2) fasting blood glucose ≥ 5.6 mmol/L and/or diagnosed with diabetes and receiving treatment. (3) systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, and/or diagnosed with hypertension and receiving treatment; (4) dyslipidemia, defined as fasting triglyceride ≥ 1.7 mmol/L or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol < 1.03 mmol/L. Notably, due to the close correlation between BMI and waist circumference, BMI is considered a reliable substitute indicator for waist circumference, so it is also used as a research indicator in this study.

Data collection

This study collected detailed data from 465 participants, including demographic data, present medical history, past medical history, SBP, DBP, weight, height, and BMI. Those previously confirmed to have hypertension were asked about their previous maximum blood pressure and whether they had taken antihypertensive drugs. The maximum diameter of the stone at horizontal plane was measured according to the CT imaging data of participants within 2 weeks before surgery. Blood routine, urine routine, urinary bacteria culture, liver and kidney function, coagulation function, nine tests before transfusion, procalcitonin (PCT), FBG, TG, and HDL-C measurements of the blood and urine samples from participants were conducted by the Clinical Laboratory Department. The renal calculi composition, including calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM), calcium oxalate dehydrate (COD), carbonate apatite (CAP), ammonium magnesium phosphate hexahydrate (MAP), and uric acid (UA) stones, was analyzed by infrared stone component analysis, in which the detected cystine and purine stones were excluded according to exclusion criteria.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 20.0 software. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are expressed as percentages. The normality of the data was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and the independent-samples t-test was employed to compare differences between groups. The χ2 test was applied to categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was utilized to calculate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) to assess the risk factors for progression from renal calculi to complex renal calculi. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Our study confirmed that larger stones in complex renal calculi were associated with MS and its components (high FBG and dyslipidemia). In addition, MS, FBG, and BMI were correlated with changes in stone composition. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that elevated BMI and FBG, along with reduced HDL-C, were independently associated with the presence of complex renal calculi.

Increasing evidence suggests that MS is a risk factor for stone formation and is closely associated with renal calculi development [20]. The definition of MS revolves around four components, including obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes, and all features of MS have been independently associated with urolithiasis [21]. A large-scale study involving 487,860 participants who had no renal calculi at baseline identified MS as an important independent risk factor for the development of renal calculi, with risk increasing as the number of MS traits increased [15]. The study by Dassanayake et al. also supports this conclusion, revealing a statistically significant association between MS and renal calculi, with hypertension and impaired glucose tolerance among the MS traits showing the strongest correlations [22]. Another cross-sectional study involving 694 elderly Taiwanese men demonstrated that MS components were highly correlated with renal calculi, with abnormal blood pressure identified as the key risk factor [23]. A retrospective study involving 820 untreated asymptomatic renal calculi patients found that MS, FBG ≥ 100 mg/dL, and young age were positively correlated with changes in kidney stone size [24]. These previous studies mainly focused on the correlation between MS and renal calculi, but did not pay attention to its association with complex renal calculi. We found that MS and its components (high FBG and dyslipidemia) were associated with larger stones in patients with complex renal calculi. Consistent with this, a retrospective study spanning from 2019 to 2022 found that MS was associated with the formation of bilateral staghorn calculi and was a major predictor of complications from percutaneous nephrolithotomy [25]. This evidence indicates the significant role of MS and its components in complex renal calculi.

Exploring metabolic factors leading to stone formation is crucial, as stone composition affects the treatment strategies for renal calculi [26]. Kadlec et al. analyzed 590 patients with renal calculi, and they found that calcium oxalate stones were the most common type of stone, followed by UA stones, and that MS patients were more likely to develop UA stones than calcium phosphate stones [27]. Chen et al. found that 48.7% of 712 patients with kidney or ureteral stones were diagnosed with MS, and the proportion of UA stones was higher in patients with MS than in patients without MS [28]. However, the association of MS with the composition of complex renal calculi remains unclear. We found that the proportion of MAP and UA stones in MS patients was significantly higher than in non-MS patients. Notably, urine in MS patients is often acidic, and UA and cystine tend to form in acidic urine, which may be responsible for the increased formation of UA stones [29, 30]. Individual MS components may independently affect stone composition. Wan et al. reported in a retrospective study of 267 patients with urinary tract calculi that the urinary tract UA stone group had a higher BMI and included more obese and diabetic patients compared to other types of stone groups [31]. Another study also reported that obese people have a higher incidence of UA renal calculi as a result of obesity lowering urine pH and increasing the excretion of calcium salts, oxalate, and UA [32]. Similarly, we found that patients with complex renal calculi with BMI ≥ 25 had a significantly higher proportion of UA stones than those with BMI < 25. Further studies revealed that the formation of UA stones may be related to the susceptibility of obese patients to insulin resistance, which increases urine acidity, thus inducing the formation of UA stones [33]. Additionally, we found that patients with complex renal calculi combined with hyperglycemia had a lower proportion of COM stones and a higher proportion of CAP and MAP stones than those with normoglycemia. This may be due to the poor general condition and long disease duration in diabetic patients, leading to urinary tract infections. The similarity of symptoms between diabetes and urinary tract infections may lead to patients not receiving timely treatment, resulting in repeated urinary tract infections and the formation of infectious stones [34]. We further compared MS component differences in patients with non-complex and complex renal calculi. Results indicated that BMI, FBG, and HDL-C significantly differed between the two groups. Multivariate logistic regression analysis found that BMI, FBG, and HDL-C were independently associated with the presence of complex renal calculi. Similarly, a cross-sectional study suggested that high BMI significantly increases the risk of renal calculi, especially in middle-aged and older adults [35]. Almuhanna et al. found that type 2 diabetes was markedly correlated with heavy stone burden, and high FBG was a key predictor [36]. Masterson et al. found that HDL-C was linked to lower nephrolithiasis [37].

The associations of MS components with complex renal calculi may involve multiple mechanisms. Obesity-related inflammation inhibits the transporter SLC26A6-mediated intestinal oxalate secretion and induces hyperoxaluria, while minor changes in urine oxalate affect the risk of renal calculi [38]. Obesity-associated insulin resistance affects the Na⁺/H⁺ exchange of renal tubules, leading to decreased urine pH, which is conducive to the formation of UA stones and calcium oxalate stones. In addition, the poor dietary structure closely related to obesity increases the concentration of substances that form stones in urine [39]. Glucose, as an allosteric modulator of calcium-sensitive receptors, increases the risk of renal calculi by promoting the excretion of urinary calcium and magnesium [40]. Moreover, the urinary sugars may act as crystal growth regulators, promoting the formation and aggregation of calcium oxalate and accelerating the development of renal calculi [41]. The decreased HDL-C levels observed in subjects are associated with increased oxidative stress, which accelerates crystal deposits in the kidneys [42, 43]. Lipid metabolism disorders may promote the formation of kidney stones by affecting fatty acid oxidation, sphingolipid metabolism and phospholipid metabolism [44].

The strength of this investigation is its in-depth exploration of the association of MS and its components with complex renal calculi and stone composition, rather than simply analyzing the relationship between MS and complex renal calculi. However, the research still has certain limitations. First, this study only included patients treated in our hospital, and the actual results may be affected by regional and ethnic factors. Moreover, the study may have been subject to bias, such as incomplete or incorrect medical records. Additionally, age and gender are directly related to the incidence of renal calculi. However, due to factors such as the duration of disease progression, hospital level, and the tiered medical treatment system, patients included in this study were predominantly between 50 and 60 years old. Moreover, the limited sample size resulted in a very small number of female patients with certain abnormal metabolic factors (such as hypertension and dyslipidemia). Therefore, subgroup analyses based on age and gender could not be performed, and further studies with more samples are needed to address this issue. Furthermore, a cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Patients’ lifestyle factors (such as dietary patterns, exercise volume, and alcohol consumption), metabolic factors (such as levels of trace elements or vitamins), and genetic factors may act as potential confounders and affect the observed results.

Conclusion

MS and its components (BMI, FBG, blood lipids) may influence stone size and/or composition in patients with complex renal calculi. Elevated BMI and FBG, along with reduced HDL-C, are independently associated with the presence of complex renal calculi. Monitoring these factors may help in the early detection and prevention of complex renal calculi in clinical practice.
