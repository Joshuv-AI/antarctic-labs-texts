Trends in Mortality Among People With HIV in Mexico: Analysis of National Death Registries (1998–2022)
Authors: Isaac Núñez, Yanink Caro-Vega, Jesús Alegre-Díaz, Brenda Crabtree-Ramírez, Raúl Ramírez-Reyes, Pablo Kuri-Morales, Malaquías López-Cervantes, Juan Sierra-Madero, Pablo F Belaunzarán-Zamudio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to describe the frequency of deaths related to advanced HIV disease (AHD) and its associated characteristics, as well as the frequency of very early and early mortality after HIV diagnosis, among people with HIV in Mexico between 1998 and 2022.MethodsWe used data from the Mexican National Death Registry. We defined deaths related to AHD as those listed in the AIDS-defining conditions of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, very early mortality as deaths occurring within the first month after HIV diagnosis, and early mortality as those occurring between the first month and the first year after HIV diagnosis. We used logistic regression models to describe trends of deaths related to AHD, early mortality, very early mortality, and specific causes of death over time.ResultsThere were 106 056 registered deaths among people with HIV, ranging from 507 to 5287 annually. The most common cause of death was related to AHD throughout the study period (54%). The predicted probability of death related to AHD decreased from 75% to 35% between 2000 and 2012 but increased after 2014 to 52% in 2022. Overall, 22% of deaths occurred very early and 25% early after HIV diagnosis. Calendar year was the only strong predictor of death due to AHD.ConclusionsA high and increasing proportion of deaths were attributed to advanced HIV disease in Mexico; a fifth of them occurred in the first month of HIV diagnosis and almost a half in the first year. In the context of universal access to antiretroviral therapy, these results suggest that additional components of the public health HIV response could be lacking.Keywords: causes of death, death certificates, HIV/AIDS, Mexico

Mortality among people with HIV (PWH) decreased dramatically in many low-, middle-, and high-income countries after the introduction of combined antiretroviral therapy (ART) [1–4]. Widespread use of ART also has caused a shift on the causes of death. The proportion of deaths due to advanced HIV disease (AHD) has generally decreased, followed by increases in deaths due to chronic diseases (eg, cardiovascular disease) [5–8]. In Mexico, the overall HIV mortality of PWH did not follow that pattern after the expansion of ART access, which started in the year 2000. It rather had a minimal reduction from 2000 to 2009 and might have slightly increased since 2010 [9]. Late diagnosis [10, 11], high frequency of advanced disease when starting ART [12, 13], heterogeneity in regional medical resources, and lack of adherence to ART [14–16] are potential explanations for the poor impact of universal ART access on mortality in Mexico.

Analyses of specific causes of death in PWH may provide insights to identify failures in the continuum of care [17, 18], find health inequalities [19], and help to elucidate specific needs on prevention to prioritize the allocation of resources in the health system. By 2023, 380 000 people with HIV were estimated to live in Mexico, of which 64% were undergoing ART [20, 21]. However, there are no studies in Mexico describing the frequency and changes in causes of death along time. In this study, we aimed to describe the frequency of deaths related to AHD among PWH, the frequency of early and very early mortality (EM and VEM) and trends over time, and the characteristics associated with deaths related to AHD in PWH in Mexico between 1998 and 2022.

METHODS

Study Design, Settings, and Data Sources

We performed an observational study using data from the Mexican National Death Registry between 1998 and 2022. The registry is managed by the National Institute of Statistics and Geography, which generates official population statistics [22]. Data are collected from all death certificates at the national level, systematized, and annually reported according to international codes [23, 24]. The General Directorate of Epidemiology at the Ministry of Health has access to the original raw death certificates data, to which we had access. The database contains data on age, sex, geographic location, and social security affiliation at death. It also contains the direct cause of death, which is considered the condition directly leading to the demise, and a list of up to 6 additional diagnoses: 4 preceding and causally associated to the direct cause of death, ordered sequentially in time, and up to 2 other significant conditions not necessarily contributing to the death. HIV infection, any associated diagnosis, or both might be registered in this list as a direct cause of death, as a contributing cause of death, or as a comorbidity. For more details, see the Supplementary material. In this analysis, we included every death certificate with an ICD-10 code compatible with HIV diagnosis (codes B20–B24) as either a cause of death or a comorbidity.

Definitions and Identification of Causes of Death

The main cause of death was defined as the diagnosis listed in the death certificate as the direct cause of death. Direct causes of death attributed to conditions unlikely to cause death or coded with very broad ICD-10 codes were considered nonacceptable as causes of death (Supplementary Table 1). For example, if the direct cause of death was attributed to unspecific events (eg, hypotension, I95; anemia, unspecified, D649) or to events unlikely to cause death (eg, pediculosis and phthiriasis, B852), these were flagged as “nonacceptable.” We used an algorithm to assign the first specific condition likely to cause death listed in the direct, antecedent, or contributing causes of death registered in the death certificate (Supplementary Figure 1). If the algorithm was completed and nonacceptable causes of death were identified in the 6 antecedent and contributing conditions listed in the death certificate, we kept the originally registered direct cause of death and recoded as described in Supplementary Table 2 [25]. After reassigning direct causes of death, we grouped all direct causes of death using categories developed by the CoDe Working Group and the D:A:D Study Group, which reclassify ICD-10 codes into categories of causes of death according to organ system or etiology/pathology, in addition to grouping certain causes of death as being related to HIV [26]. The code system has detailed categories for specific diseases unrelated to advanced HIV. However, it groups all “AIDS-defining” diagnoses in 2 broad categories: neoplasia and infection. We modified the CoDe classification to include more detailed categories for events considered to be AIDS defining, using the 1993 AIDS classification of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Supplementary Table 2); we considered these to be specific causes of deaths due to AHD [27, 28]. For other causes of death, we included the following: other infectious diseases (including pneumonias with specific etiologic diagnoses not considered AIDS defining), liver diseases, cerebrovascular and cardiovascular disorders, central and peripheral neurologic disorders (nonvascular related), mental health related, cancer, chronic lung disorders, gastrointestinal disorders, endocrine disorders, renal failure, violent deaths, unclassifiable and unknown causes, hematologic disorders, and other noninfectious disorders. Most nonacceptable direct causes of death were regrouped as “other AIDS-related conditions” (B22, B24) or as non–AIDS-related “unclassifiable and unknown causes” according to the procedure summarized in the Supplementary material to assign as the main cause of death. We finally grouped these categories in 2 large categories: deaths related and unrelated to AHD (Supplementary Table 2).

The dataset contains the estimated time before death when the diagnosis listed in the death certificate was identified but only from 2016 to 2022. We used the time specified for codes directly related to HIV diagnosis (B20–B24) for this purpose. If the time specified for an HIV-related condition (eg, pneumocystis pneumonia) was longer, we used the longer one. Using the time between HIV diagnosis and death, we defined VEM as deaths occurring within the first month of HIV diagnosis, EM as those occurring after the first month but within the first year after HIV diagnosis, and late mortality as deaths occurring at least a year after HIV diagnosis.

Statistical Analysis

We described the frequency of causes of death related to AHD, the specific causes for each year, the temporality of mortality (ie, VEM), and the sociodemographic characteristics of the individuals, using descriptive statistics. We also analyzed the observed percentage of deaths related to advanced HIV stratified by sex assigned at birth. We examined mortality trends using the predicted probability of death attributed to advanced HIV throughout the study period, which was obtained with an adjusted logistic regression model. First, a logistic regression model was run while adjusting for calendar year, biological sex, age, calendar year of death, education level, and social security affiliation. Then, its results were used to predict the probability of death attributed to advanced HIV during the years 2000 to 2022 (reporting practices did not allow this for 1998–1999) while holding the other covariates fixed. We used similar models for the outcome of very early, early, and late mortality. Finally, we used similar models to evaluate trends of specific causes of death over time. For example, we fit a model to estimate the adjusted probability of death due to tuberculosis as compared with all other causes of death, and we used it to predict the probability of dying due to tuberculosis during each year while holding the other covariates fixed. Bootstrap resampling with 200 iterations was used to compute 95% CIs for the predictions. Continuous variables were included in all the models as linear and quadratic terms to avoid assumptions of linear response between these covariables and the outcome [29].

We used R software version 4.1.2 for the analyses. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Instituto Nacional de Ciencias Médicas y Nutrición Salvador Zubirán (approval INF-2154-18-19-1).

Characteristics Associated to Deaths due to AHD

Most variables were not predictors of death due to AHD. Death occurring during the early study period was associated with an increased odds of death attributed to AHD, while older age was associated with a decreased odds of death due to AHD (Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, sex, education, and social security status were not associated with increased odds of deaths due to AHD.

Frequency of Very Early, Early, and Late Mortality

Temporality of death was available for 22 431 of 27 294 (82.2%) individuals from 2016 to 2022 (Table 1). During this period, 22.1% of deaths occurred within the first month after HIV diagnosis and 25.2% between 1 and 12 months after the HIV diagnosis. The percentage of very early deaths throughout the study period was lower among those who died due to AHD as compared with those who died due to another cause (19.6% vs 24.5%). The percentage of early deaths was higher in those who died due to AHD as compared with those who died due to another cause (28.4% vs 22.1%). Finally, the percentage of late deaths was slightly lower among those who died due to AHD vs those who died due to another cause (33.8% vs 35.6%). The adjusted probability of early and late mortality slightly decreased from 2018 to 2022 (31.7% to 26.3% and 43.6% to 37.4%), while that of VEM increased (24.6% to 36.2%; Supplementary Figure 3).

Among those with information on temporality of death (n = 22 431), the most common causes of death among those with VEM were AHD (46.6%), non-AIDS infectious diseases (37%), and unknown (3.8%). Of those who had VEM due to AHD, the most common specific causes were unspecified HIV conditions (30.7%), tuberculosis (15.2%), and unspecified pneumonia (13.5%).

Among people with EM, the most common causes were AHD (55.9%), non-AIDS infectious diseases (29.7%), and unknown (2.8%). Of those who had EM due to AHD, the most common specific causes were unspecified HIV conditions (30.8%), tuberculosis (22.4%), and wasting syndrome (8.4%). Among people with late deaths, the most common causes were AHD (48.3%), non-AIDS infectious diseases (30.5%), and cardiovascular diseases (4.8%). Of those who had late mortality due to AHD, the most common specific causes were unspecified HIV conditions (35.4%), tuberculosis (17%), and wasting syndrome (10.3%). DISCUSSION

In this study, we described the frequency and characteristics of all registered deaths in PWH, using death certificate–based National Death Registry in Mexico during a 24-year period. The study extends from 1998 to 2022, which comprises the period immediately before to the implementation of the universal access program to ART at the national level (2000–2004) and its expansion and maturation up to 2022, including the years of the COVID-19 pandemic. We identified that a high percentage of deaths (54%) were attributed to AHD, a high percentage (22%) occurred within the first month of the HIV diagnosis, and almost half (47%) happened within the first year of the HIV diagnosis, although information on the temporality of death after HIV diagnosis was available for only the 6 most recent years. The main cause of death throughout the study period was due to AIDS-defining conditions present in AHD.

AHD was the most frequent cause of death during the study, with no significant changes along 24 years; this starkly contrasts with causes of death in the general Mexican population, which are presently led by heart disease, diabetes, and cancer [22]. In a country with universal access to ART, it would be expected that causes of death among PWH started to mirror those of the country's general population. Our findings also contrast with reports of mortality elsewhere. In a recent observational cohort study in Europe and North America, only 24% of deaths were attributed to AIDS [5]. Similarly, in a recent report from the Swiss HIV Cohort, 9% of individuals were reported to have died due to AIDS [6]. In both cases, specific causes of death in PWH had transitioned from mostly AIDS-defining conditions to other causes similar to those in the general population (eg, cardiovascular disease) after the introduction and distribution of combination ART.

We found that not only does AHD continue to be the most common cause of death, but it has also increased in recent years. Since we cannot ascertain whether people were receiving care or treatment for HIV around their death, we do not have evidence to support a clear explanation for this concerning trend. However, we and others have described a persistently high frequency of late diagnosis [10, 11], delays in linkage to care after diagnosis [12], AHD when prescribed ART [12, 13], and ART interruptions [30] in Mexico and Latin America. Studies from Europe, North America, and Africa have reported a higher risk of death among individuals who interrupted ART [31, 32]. In addition, we previously identified that most deaths in our region are attributable to late enrollment in care and ART initiation [33].

Our finding of a persistently high percentage of deaths occurring either very early (within a month) or early (within a year) after HIV diagnosis is consistent with such studies and supports our hypothesis that AHD at diagnosis and late enrollment in care and ART initiation could be severely limiting the potential benefits of the universal access to ART program in our country. Other factors proposed as explanations for the underwhelming effect that universal access to ART in Mexico has had in mortality in PWH are variability in medical resources across regions and lack of adherence to ART [14–16]. Steps have been undertaken to improve accessibility to care in some regions during the COVID-19 pandemic, but a concentrated effort throughout the country is needed [18].

There might be an underreporting of deaths among PWH. We rely on the information that physicians report in the death certificate. Deaths among people who were undiagnosed at death would not appear, potentially underestimating the proportion of deaths due to advanced HIV. Conversely, it is theoretically possible that physicians might not put HIV diagnosis in the certificate if the death was unrelated to HIV (eg, car accident), even if death certificates do have a section on unrelated comorbidities. Studies in Brazil have shown that deaths among individuals with HIV included in hospital-based cohorts may not have HIV as a diagnosis listed in their death certificates, particularly among older individuals [34, 35]. Similar dedicated studies are needed to determine if this is a problem in Mexico, where HIV is deemed a mandatory reporting condition by the general epidemiology directorate. In addition, while there is a possibility that physicians might leave out HIV as a cause in the death certificate if, for example, the family requested it due to perceived stigma, it remains a hypothetical concern and likely very rare, as physicians are not allowed to do so.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Data on temporality of death (very early, early, or late) were not routinely available throughout the study period, and there was a nonnegligible percentage of missing data during the years that they were available (17.8%). Another limitation is that our analysis relies on the accuracy of the National Death Registry. While mortality datasets in Mexico are broadly deemed of high quality, we observed a higher probability of death due to other AIDS-defining conditions during the earlier years of our study period and again close to the end (Figure 3) [30]. The “other AIDS-defining conditions” category includes ICD-10 codes related to HIV that are relatively unspecific (eg, B227 for “HIV disease resulting in multiple diseases classified elsewhere”). Along with the high frequency of unknown or unclassifiable direct causes of death, a worrying possibility is that these causes represent people with AHD in whom a diagnostic workup was not feasible. To evaluate this, we would require data on the clinical picture when the fatality occurred, including any diagnostic workup.

We do not have data on ART, viral suppression status, CD4 cell count, engagement to care, and other variables that could help us improve our understanding of the specific causes of death. Nonetheless, we included relevant sociodemographic variables that are often considered predictors of care, and the descriptive analyses show consistent trends in mortality due to AHD: a very high percentage during early years, followed by a decrease and a slight increase in recent years. We caution against a causal interpretation of the estimates from the regression models, as we used them exclusively to explore prediction [36].

Also, a smaller number of deaths among PWH was observed in 2020 as compared with the rest of the study period. We suspect that the strain and oversaturation of health care services during the early COVID-19 pandemic [37] led to death certificates consistently being filled with “COVID-19” as the only cause of death in the entire document, which in turn led to this underregistration of mortality among PWH.

In conclusion, our study provides robust data at the national level consistent with previous evidence showing that a very high percentage of death in Mexican PWH is due to AHD despite national availability of ART. These results contrast with those of high-income countries. The very high frequency of late diagnosis, late presentation to care, and late ART initiation can partially explain our results. Our results provide evidence that universal access to ART is not the only intervention necessary to reduce mortality due to AHD. Policy efforts should be directed to expand HIV diagnosis and early linkage to care and ART initiation to further reduce mortality in PWH and the frequency of deaths due to advanced HIV.
