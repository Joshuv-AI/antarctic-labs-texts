Pediatric anti-NMDAR encephalitis: Neuroradiological presentation, cognitive outcome and volumetric MRI analysis
Authors: Fabio M Doniselli, Anna Paola Savoldi, João Ramos, Alessandra Erbetta, Chiara Foschini, Riccardo Pascuzzo, Carlo Antozzi, Francesca Ragona, Federica Zibordi, Tiziana Granata, Marina Grisoli, Elena Freri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (anti-NMDAR) encephalitis is the most frequent autoimmune encephalitis in children and may cause long-term sequelae. Although MRI plays a key role in diagnosis and follow-up, its correlation with cognitive outcome remains unclear. We retrospectively reviewed pediatric cases of anti-NMDAR encephalitis diagnosed from 2007 to 2024, including only antibody-positive patients with at least one MRI. Clinical data encompassed presenting symptoms, EEG, therapies, relapses, and the latest neuropsychological assessment. MRI scans were evaluated by three neuroradiologists using a structured atrophy scale, and volumetric T1-weighted sequences, when available, were analyzed with Volbrain. Twenty-two patients (9 males, 13 females; median onset age 11.9 years) were included. The most frequent presenting symptoms were seizures (82%) and behavioral disturbances (59%), while EEG abnormalities were detected in 95%. At onset, 9 patients (41%) showed MRI abnormalities, most commonly fronto-parieto-occipital atrophy. Long-term cognitive follow-up (median 4.8 years), available for all patients, demonstrated that 80% of patients had normal or only mild deficits, but those with abnormal baseline MRI were more likely to develop long-term cognitive impairment, although the limited sample size precluded robust statistical analysis. Long-term MRI was available in 8 patients (median follow-up 10 years); volumetric analysis revealed parietal lobe volume loss in 6, including some with visually normal scans. Overall, baseline MRI alterations appeared associated with higher risk of subsequent cognitive deficits, while volumetric data highlighted subtle parietal atrophy even in patients with normal-appearing scans and, in some cases, without neuropsychological impairment. These findings support the complementary role of quantitative MRI and systematic neuropsychological monitoring in pediatric anti-NMDAR encephalitis.Keywords: Anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis, Epilepsy, Magnetic resonance imaging, Cognition disorders, Long-term care, Neurology

Introduction

Anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (anti-NMDAR) encephalitis in pediatric patients presents unique challenges due to its heterogeneous clinical manifestations and potential impact on cognitive development. This neurological disorder, first elucidated by Dalmau et al. in 2007 (Dalmau et al., 2008, Dalmau et al., 2019), is associated with autoantibodies against the GluN1 subunit of NMDA receptors on the neuronal surface, leading to inflammation and dysfunction and can be associated with ovarian teratoma (Nha et al., 2025) or other neoplasms (Kosek et al., 2024). Symptoms include behavioral disorders, seizures, and cognitive impairment; besides that, speech disorders, movement disorders and autonomic dysfunction represent the most common presentations (Bartels et al., 2020, Granata et al., 2018, Matricardi et al., 2016, Freri et al., 2015).

This condition is increasingly recognized in pediatric populations and is considered the most common autoimmune encephalitis in children (Bartels et al., 2020, Titulaer et al., 2013).

Diagnostic workup typically includes an extensive assessment: neuroimaging, electroencephalography, and CSF analysis to confirm the presence of antibodies (Titulaer et al., 2013). Treatment strategies aim to suppress autoimmune response while managing symptoms effectively. Immunotherapy with corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulins, and plasma exchange, is commonly employed (Titulaer et al., 2013).

MRI, an essential tool in diagnosing and monitoring anti-NMDAR encephalitis, can be normal in up to 60% of patients (Shen et al., 2024, Gong et al., 2024). Common MRI manifestations include cortical T2-hyperintensities and cortical atrophy, notably in limbic regions (Titulaer et al., 2013, Irani et al., 2010, Bassal et al., 2021). When MRI abnormalities are present, they are most found in the temporal, frontal, and parietal lobes (Hou et al., 2024).

Few studies have investigated long-term MRI changes in patients with anti-NMDAR encephalitis and correlated them with clinical outcomes (Iizuka et al., 2010), hampering a comprehensive understanding of the disease’s impact on brain structure and function over time. Given the marked variability in radiological presentation and the potential prognostic value of these findings, our study sought to systematically review neuroimaging features from disease onset and to characterize long-term sequelae. When volumetric data were available, quantitative assessments were performed to complement visual evaluation and provide more sensitive markers of subtle structural alterations.

Material and methods

This study was done in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the Helsinki declaration. Patients signed an informed consent, accepting the anonymous collection of their clinical data for research purposes. We retrospectively searched the databases of our Pediatric Neurology department for patients from 2007 to 2024 with the following inclusion criteria: (I) diagnosis of anti-NMDAR encephalitis; (II) at least one MR performed during the first event or a relapse, acquired in our Institute or re-evaluated by one of our neuroradiologists; (III) age under 18 years. The following clinical information was retrieved: clinical onset symptoms, neurological status using modified Rankin score for children as done by our group in previous study (Matricardi et al., 2016), first EEG localization, therapy (including steroid administration), relapse, last cognitive assessment (summarized as normal, mild cognitive delay, or moderate/severe cognitive delay). Neuropsychological tests included the Wechsler Intelligence Scale, assessments of language, memory, planning, attention, and visuomotor integration as previously reported by our group (Matricardi et al., 2016).

Symptoms at onset were categorized as seizures, behavioral disorders, movement disorders, sleep disorders, cognitive (language or memory) disorders, perceptual disturbances or psychiatric symptoms.

Radiological analysis

MRI scans were performed on different scanners: a 1.5 T Siemens Avanto Fit, a 1.5 T Philips Achieva or a 3 T Philips Achieva Dstream. The acquisition protocol changed over time due to technical improvements but always included T1-weighted images (from 3 mm thickness to volumetric images) before and after contrast administration, axial or coronal T2-weighted images, axial, coronal or 3D FLAIR images. Patients who were unable to remain still during the MRI scan were sedated in the presence of an anesthesiologist.

All MRI scans were analyzed by two neuroradiologists (A.S. and J.R., both with five years' experience in neuroimaging) and any discrepancies were resolved by a third neuroradiologist (F.M.D.) with nine years' experience in neuroimaging.

The trophism of the different brain regions was assessed using the Global Cortical Atrophy (GCA) scale (Pasquier et al., 1996); scores ranging from 0 to 3 were semiquantitatively assigned for both the degree of sulcal and ventricular dilation (0: normal volume/no ventricular enlargement; 1: opening of sulci/mild ventricular enlargement; 2: volume loss of gyri/moderate ventricular enlargement; 3: 'knife blade' atrophy/severe ventricular enlargement).

For patients with volumetric T1-weighted imaging available at the last follow-up (acquired in our center), an automated online tool (Volbrain, (Manjón and Coupé, 2016) was used to quantify subtle deviations from normative volumetric data. Volbrain performs automated segmentation of brain structures using advanced probabilistic algorithms and compares individual volumes to an age- and sex-matched normative database. The subset of patients with long-term follow-up was imaged on a 1.5 T Siemens Avanto Fit scanner, using volumetric T1-weighted TSE acquisitions with isotropic voxels. Prior to Volbrain processing, all volumetric T1-weighted datasets underwent quality control to confirm adequate image quality and absence of motion artifacts, for reliable segmentation.

In addition to brain atrophy, the presence of midbrain and cerebellar atrophy, as well as signal abnormalities in brain parenchymal structures, was also systematically evaluated and recorded for each patient.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as absolute frequencies with corresponding percentages. Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate the association between the use of corticosteroids within 1 month prior to MRI and the presence of abnormalities in baseline MRI; Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to evaluate the differences in neurological status between patients with normal and abnormal baseline MRI. Moreover, to explore the association between baseline MRI abnormalities and long-term cognitive outcome, Fisher’s exact test was used, considering cognitive outcome either as a three-class variable (normal, mild, moderate/severe) or dichotomized (normal vs impaired). Associations were quantified using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1).

Results

A total of 22 patients were included in the study (9 males, 13 females). The median age at disease onset was 11.95 years (interquartile range [IQR] 7.25–15.53). The most common presenting symptom was seizures, occurring in 18 patients (82%). A detailed list of symptoms is reported in Table 1.

Electroencephalography (EEG) revealed slow-wave abnormalities—either diffuse or focal—in 21 out of 22 patients (95%). One patient presented with ictal activity in the absence of background slowing.

Two patients died during follow-up: one from complications of disseminated intravascular coagulation as a complication of immunotherapy (patient 16), and one by suicide (patient 14), during relapse of disease 10 years after the first episode.

Most patients had slight-to-moderate disability with a modified Rankin score of 2 (8/22, 36%) and 3 (8/22, 36%); fewer had severe disability (score 5: 4/22, 18%).

All patients were treated with corticosteroids. Ten out of 22 patients (45%) received corticosteroids within 1 month prior to MRI. None of the patients required admission to the intensive care unit.

Radiological evaluation

A total of 54 brain MRIs were analyzed, corresponding to an average of 2.45 MR scans per patient (range: 1–9). Of the 22 patients, 16 underwent first MRI at our institution, while 6 had imaging performed at external centers.

The first MRI was performed at a median of 40 days from symptom onset (IQR 22–158). In 11 patients (50%), the first MRI was unremarkable. Among the 11 patients with abnormal initial MRI, 9 showed cortical atrophy while two (patient 10 and 21) showed only white matter alterations. Among the included patients with atrophy and signal intensity abnormalities, patient 6 exhibited periventricular T2 hyperintensities; patient 11 demonstrated abnormalities adjacent to the frontal horns and in the parietal periventricular region; patient 15 presented a right frontal malacic area, possibly due to a previous herpetic encephalitis and developed anti-NMDAR encephalitis some weeks later; and patient 22 showed a pontine signal alteration. Two patients showed only signal changes on the first scan; one of these (patient 15) later developed cortical atrophy, as confirmed by a follow-up MRI one week later.

One patient (patient 10), whose first MRI (performed 36 days after symptom onset) was normal, developed signal abnormalities without evidence of atrophy on repeat imaging one month later. One patient (patient 21) showed bilateral white matter alterations also without atrophy at the follow-up.

Cortical atrophy of the cerebral hemispheres was seen in 9 patients. Of these, 8 had diffuse involvement (i.e., more than two lobes), while one showed focal atrophy. In 7 cases MRI abnormalities were bilateral and in 2 unilateral.

The parieto-occipital lobes were the most atrophic regions (in 8/9 cases, 89%), followed by the temporal lobes (6/9, 67%) and frontal lobes (5/9, 55%).

Atrophy was graded as mild in 7 patients (78%), moderate in 1 (11%), and severe in 1 (11%).

No patient showed midbrain atrophy. However, 2 of the 9 patients with brain or supratentorial cortical atrophy also demonstrated cerebellar atrophy (patients 5 and 11).

We observed that the group of patients with abnormal baseline MRI had a significantly (p = 0.0004) higher modified Rankin score (median: 2; range 1–3) than those without (median: 4; range: 3–5). Moreover, the use of corticosteroids within 1 month prior to MRI was significantly associated with the presence of abnormalities at baseline MRI (p = 0.0274), with an OR = 11.67 (95% CI: 1.53–89.12).

Long-term follow-up

Eight patients underwent a follow-up MRI after a prolonged interval (median 10.3 years, IQR 9.5–11.75). In one case (patient 6, Fig. 1), periventricular T2-FLAIR hyperintensities were still present, and in another case (patient 15), a right frontal cortico-subcortical clastic lesion was observed (previous herpes encephalitis). In all remaining cases, visual inspection by the neuroradiologist did not reveal any significant abnormalities in parenchymal signal. Volumetric analysis revealed brain lobe volumes below the 1st percentile in at least one lobe in six out of eight patients (75%). All six patients exhibited parietal lobe volume reduction: three bilaterally, with values below the 1st percentile, and three unilaterally, with contralateral reduction down to the 3rd percentile in two cases. Frontal lobe volumes were also decreased relative to age-matched normative data in four of the six patients with parietal involvement, although to a lesser degree. In one patient, reduced volume of the right occipital lobe was also observed. A detailed summary of these findings is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Cognitive assessments were conducted after a median follow-up of 4.75 years (IQR 1.4–7.63). Approximately 80% of patients showed normal cognitive function or only mild deficits.

Regarding the correlation between radiological alterations at the first MRI and the cognitive assessment, 8 patients with normal MRI findings showed normal cognitive profiles (Fig. 4). Five patients with normal MRI presented mild cognitive delay (4 cases) or moderate/severe delay (1 case). Among the 9 patients with alterations at onset, 3 showed normal neuropsychological follow-up, while the remaining 6 demonstrated concordances between MRI abnormalities and cognitive deficits (in particular in language, working memory and behavior; 3 mild and 3 moderate/severe).

Although patients with abnormal baseline MRI were more frequently impaired at follow-up, the association did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.32 for the three-class analysis; p = 0.39 for the dichotomized comparison). In the dichotomized analysis (impaired vs normal),the estimated OR for long-term cognitive impairment in the abnormal MRI group was 3.2 (95% CI: 0.54–18.98).

Discussion

In this retrospective case series of 22 pediatric patients with anti-NMDAR encephalitis, we provide a long-term neuroradiological and clinical follow-up spanning over 17 years. Our findings confirm the variable radiological presentations at onset, with emphasis on the potential for long-lasting structural sequelae, even in the absence of intensive care admission or prolonged altered consciousness.

MRI at disease onset was normal in more than half of the cases, consistent with previous literature reporting negative conventional MRI findings in 50–65% of pediatric patients with anti-NMDAR encephalitis (Shen et al., 2024, Gong et al., 2024, Irani et al., 2010). However, when abnormal, we observed cortical atrophy in the majority of cases, often involving posterior regions, especially the parieto-occipital lobes. This distribution differs from the classical frontal and temporal predilection reported by previous articles in pediatric population (Bassal et al., 2021, Hou et al., 2024). It has to be noted that none of the patients in our cohort required intensive care, even in the presence of seizures, movement disorders, and severe behavioral alterations. This finding may reflect differences between our cohort and previously published reports, although additional evidence is required to confirm this hypothesis.

Patients with cerebellar abnormalities (patients 5 and 11) both presented at onset with movement disorders, and only patient 11 also exhibited behavioral disturbances and seizures. At the last clinical follow-up, patient 5 had a globally normal psychological evaluation, whereas patient 11 showed a more impaired profile. All other patients showed no cerebellar abnormalities.

Steroid administration is a known factor associated with reduced brain volume (De Alcubierre et al., 2023). All our patients underwent steroid therapy for variable durations; however, some patients received steroid therapy in the month before first MRI. Our analysis showed that steroid administration was associated with atrophy presence, but a stronger association was found between clinical status and atrophy. It is not possible to establish a precise causal relationship between clinical status, corticosteroid administration, and MRI alterations within a retrospective study design such as the present one. However, it is important to acknowledge the potential contribution of treatment effects, in addition to the underlying disease itself.Of note, the evolution of MRI findings over time in our cohort revealed a dynamic process. While some patients exhibited improvement of cortical atrophy at follow-up, others demonstrated progressive volume loss, particularly in the parietal lobes as demonstrated by volumetric analysis. Such variability raises the hypothesis that both the intensity of the autoimmune process and the time to initiation of immunotherapy may contribute to the degree and persistence of structural damage. However, we do not have sufficient data to substantiate this assumption, which therefore remains theoretical. Volumetric analysis proved useful in identifying subtle changes not always visible on qualitative review. The presence of volumetric reductions, particularly in the parietal lobe, had not been reported in previous studies. This finding highlights the importance of quantitative assessment in brain morphometry beyond the routinely qualitative evaluation. Further studies on larger cohorts are needed to confirm this observation.

Despite these radiological abnormalities, long-term cognitive outcomes were favorable in most patients. Approximately 80% demonstrated normal or only mildly impaired cognitive function at follow-up, supporting the growing recognition that structural MRI alterations do not correlate with clinical deficits (Shen et al., 2024, Gong et al., 2024, Hartung et al., 2023). Iizuka and colleagues reported that cognitive functions (in only two patients in their cohort) improved over the following 4–5 years, with a marked reduction in atrophy. Therefore, they did not associate acute-phase atrophy with long-term outcomes. By contrast, in our cohort we observed that long-term cognitive deficits were numerically more frequent among patients with baseline MRI alterations (Fig. 4). However, this association was not significant, and the estimated OR of 3.2 had substantial uncertainty likely due to the small sample size. Therefore, this finding should be considered exploratory and requires confirmation in larger cohorts. Moreover, patients who underwent volumetric analysis were variably distributed across different groups, and their limited number does not allow for a more detailed analysis aimed at correlating cognitive deficits with volume loss.

It has to be noted that none of the patients in our cohort required intensive care, even in the presence of severe neurological condition which included seizures, movement disorders, and behavioral alterations. This observation is in line with previous reports highlighting that early recognition and timely immunotherapy are crucial to prevent life-threatening complications and reduce the need for ICU support. On the other hand, it is possible that the cohort is biased towards a less severe clinical phenotype if more severely affected children were unable to obtain an MRI.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and heterogeneous MRI protocols on different scanners over time may have introduced variability in detection sensitivity. Automated volumetric analysis was feasible only in a subset of patients, and the absence of a matched control group limits interpretation of subtle volume differences. Some baseline examinations performed externally could not be re-evaluated and may have been falsely considered negative. The use of Volbrain for volumetric analysis is appropriate and well recognized in the literature; however, we acknowledge its main limitations, such as dependence on image quality and the relatively small normative pediatric dataset. Finally, the small sample size precludes generalizable statistical associations between radiological findings and clinical outcomes.

In summary, our 17-year institutional experience highlights the variable MRI presentations and the potential for enduring structural changes in pediatric anti-NMDAR encephalitis. The presence of radiological alterations on the first MRI was more likely to be associated with long-term cognitive deficits. However, volumetric analysis revealed parenchymal volume loss even in patients whose conventional MRI was reported as normal, independently from cognitive impairment presence. These findings suggest that early MRI abnormalities may represent a risk factor for unfavorable cognitive outcome but also highlight the sensitivity of quantitative volumetric techniques in detecting subtle structural changes that may remain clinically silent. Comprehensive neuropsychological monitoring combined with advanced imaging tools is therefore crucial to better understand the interplay between structural damage and functional outcomes in this population.
