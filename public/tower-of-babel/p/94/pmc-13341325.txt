Impact of User Responses to Smart MDI Notifications: Insights From a Longitudinal Real‐World Study of 2604 Smart MDI System Users
Authors: Martin Cuesta, Marie Strivay, Alessandro Rizzi, Sandra Schluter, Tim van den Heuvel, Shannon N. Edd, Fabio di Piazza, Ohad Cohen
Published: 2026

Background

With approximately 60% of insulin users using insulin pens, they remain the most widely used method of insulin delivery globally [1]. Missed or delayed bolus dosing remains a major contributor to suboptimal glycaemic control in people with diabetes [2]. Connected insulin pens aim to address this gap in people with diabetes using multiple daily injections (MDI) by integrating insulin dosing data with continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), but evidence for improved glycaemic outcomes has been inconsistent, with improvements reported from modest [3, 4] to non‐significant [5, 6], compared to conventional MDI + CGM therapy.

Unique in the connected pen landscape is the MiniMed Smart MDI system, which combines dose logging, insulin and CGM data, a dose calculator and real‐time actionable alerts, including missed dose alerts (MDA) and correct high glucose alerts (CHGA). Unlike traditional CGM alerts, which are based solely on glucose thresholds or trends, these alerts are designed to prompt specific user actions. Previously published real‐world data have demonstrated that higher response rates to such alerts are associated with greater time in range (TIR), but causal relationships remain uncertain [7].

We conducted a longitudinal real‐world analysis to evaluate whether changes in user responsiveness to Smart MDI alerts over time are associated with changes in glycaemic outcomes.

Methods

The Smart MDI system consists of the Bluetooth‐connected InPen insulin injector, the Simplera CGM and the InPen app. Together, they provide dosing guidance with its built‐in dose calculator function and notify the user when a correction is needed (CHGA), or a meal is missed (MDA). The InPen can deliver rapid‐acting insulin boluses from 0.5 to 30 units per injection.

This retrospective longitudinal real‐world study collated data from Europe, the Middle East and Africa, starting from 22 OCT 2024 to 15 AUG 2025. The study included people with any type of diabetes, with ≥ 10 days of InPen bolus and CGM data for a minimum of 6 months consecutively.

The study participants were stratified into four quartiles based on TIR at month 1 (with Q1 having lowest average TIR), and their glycaemic control from month 1 to month 6 was analysed. Users from Q1 who had a response rate increase by > 10%, and users from Q4 with a response rate decrease by > 10% over 6 months, were included. The 10% was based on the observed distribution of changes in response rate (e.g., among those who showed an increase, the median change was 8.8% for MDA and 13.1% for CHGA, while among those with a decrease, this was 10.9% and 14.1%). Response to alerts was defined as a rapid‐acting bolus delivery within 1 h of alert. Change in TIR from month 1 to month 6 was assessed using a two‐sided t‐test (α = 0.05), testing whether the mean change in TIR differed from 0.

Results

The study included 2604 Smart MDI system users, and they were followed up for 250.2 ± 44.1 days. The largest proportion of the participants was in the age group of 29–49 years (19.5%), and 34.7% of participants did not report their age. System use was high, with InPen use 94.9% (i.e., proportion of days with ≥ 1 InPen bolus), and CGM use 96.8% of the time. The mean TIR was 58.8% ± 17.7%, with a mean glucose management indicator (GMI) 7.4% ± 0.7%. User characteristics at month 1 are presented in Table S1.

In Q1, representing users with the lowest average TIR at month 1, a > 10% increase in response rate to MDA from month 1 to 6 improved average TIR by 8.6% ± 13.4% (N = 127, 95% CI: 6.2 to 10.9, p < 0.0001). An increase in TIR was also observed for those who increased response rate to CHGA by > 10%; TIR improved by 9.1% ± 14.3% (95% CI: 5.7 to 12.6, p < 0.0001). GMI was reduced over time by 0.3% ± 0.6% for MDA (95% CI: −0.3 to −0.2, p < 0.0001), and 0.4% ± 0.6% for CHGA (95% CI: −0.5 to −0.2, p < 0.0001).

In Q4, representing those with the highest TIR at month 1, an opposite pattern was observed. Participants with a > 10% decrease in response rate to MDA showed 3.1% ± 9.2% reduction in TIR (N = 191, 95% CI: −4.4 to −1.8, p < 0.0001). Similarly, a > 10% decrease in response rate over time to CHGA was associated with 3.9% ± 9.2% decrease in TIR (N = 184, 95% CI: −5.2 to −2.5, p < 0.0001). GMI increased by 0.1% in both instances (for MDA: 95% CI: 0.0 to 0.1, p = 0.0064; for CHGA: 95% CI: 0.0 to 0.2, p = 0.0009). Figure 1 shows a visual representation of the results.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that changes in user responsiveness to Smart MDI alerts were associated with changes in glycaemic outcomes; among Smart MDI users with poor glycaemic control, a 10% increase in engagement with alerts resulted in meaningful and statistically significant improvements in TIR, while users with best TIR at month 1 showed a significant reduction in TIR when their response to alerts decreased by 10%. Based on the mean 1.3 MDA and 4.5 CHGA per day at month 1, such a 10% increase in response rate corresponds to just 1 additional bolus response to MDA and 3 additional bolus responses to CHGA per week.

The observed improvement in TIR in Q1 (> 8.5%) with these behavioural changes is clinically relevant, as this corresponds to 120 additional minutes spent in range per day. A 10% increase in TIR is reported to be associated with an ~0.5% improvement in HbA1c [8], reduction in diabetes‐related complications [9, 10] and reduction in complication‐related healthcare costs [10].

Our findings from the study also align with the recently published cross‐sectional study on the Smart MDI system from Laurenzi et al. [7]. Both studies report sub‐optimal engagement with the bolus calculator and also sub‐optimal response rates to alerts. This implies that the smart features of the smart MDI system remain underutilised, indicating the need for effective education on proper system use, and the need to further explore why users did not respond to alerts (e.g., alert fatigue, or the role of HCP guidance).

Our study has some limitations. First, demographic variables were self‐reported and may be subject to misclassification and partial missingness. Information on education, motivation, lifestyle changes, physical activity and exposure to diabetes previous technology was not available. Second, the Smart MDI system automatically records only rapid‐acting insulin. The reliance on manual reporting of long‐acting insulin may result in missing or incomplete dosing data, and the extent to which these gaps affect study outcomes is unknown. Third, the study population consisted largely of early adopters whose limited familiarity with new features and clinical workflows may have led to suboptimal use, potentially underestimating the system's true effectiveness. Fourth, although this data shows that a causal relationship between alert response rate and glycaemic outcomes is plausible, this should be confirmed through interventional studies. Fifth, regression to the mean cannot be excluded, as users starting with very low TIR are more likely to improve over time, regardless of intervention.

Strength of this study includes the use of real‐world data from a well‐established database and the consistency of results at both ends of the TIR spectrum (Q1 and Q4). In addition, the consistency in results from a sensitivity analysis (Q2 and Q3) demonstrated similar, though less pronounced, patterns compared with Q1 and Q4, respectively.

In conclusion, this real‐world study demonstrates that among users with low baseline glycaemic control, TIR improves as response rates to alerts increase. This relationship is clinically meaningful and can help improve glycaemic control.
