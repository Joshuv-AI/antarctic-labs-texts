Validation of the Mayo Clinic Percutaneous Coronary Intervention Risk Prediction Score in Patients With Acute Coronary Syndrome
Authors: Antonio Landi, Andrea Zito, Mandeep Singh, Dominick J Angiolillo, Davide Capodanno, Enrico Frigoli, Andrea Milzi, Sunil V Rao, Philip Urban, Marco Valgimigli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA contemporary, comprehensive risk score for the prediction of in‐hospital fatal and nonfatal adverse events after percutaneous coronary intervention has been recently developed, albeit its external validation in patients with acute coronary syndrome is lacking. The objective of this study is to validate the Mayo Clinic integer risk score in a large cohort of patients with acute coronary syndrome undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention from the MATRIX (Minimizing Adverse Haemorrhagic Events by Transradial Access Site and Systemic Implementation of Angiox) trial.MethodsThe risk score is composed of a common set of 23 clinical and procedural variables (model A) and 18 preprocedural variables (model B). The primary objective was to appraise the receiver‐operator characteristics of both models for in‐hospital mortality, bleeding, contrast‐associated acute kidney injury, and stroke. The impact of risk score categories on 1‐year outcomes was also investigated.ResultsAmong the 6724 patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention in the MATRIX trial, the receiver‐operator characteristics—area under the curves for in‐hospital mortality, bleeding, contrast‐associated acute kidney injury, and stroke were 0.88 (95% CI, 0.85–0.91), 0.66 (95% CI, 0.63–0.69), 0.70 (95% CI, 0.67–0.72), and 0.65 (95% CI, 0.52–0.78) for model A and 0.88 (95% CI, 0.84–0.92), 0.63 (95% CI, 0.60–0.66), 0.66 (95% CI, 0.63–0.69), and 0.65 (95% CI, 0.52–0.78) for model B, respectively. Mortality (model A), bleeding, and stroke risks were well calibrated, whereas mortality (model B) and contrast‐associated acute kidney injury risk were underestimated. There was a gradual risk increase for 1‐year mortality and bleeding as a function of the risk score tertile using both models.ConclusionsThe risk score identifies patients with acute coronary syndrome at incremental risk of in‐hospital mortality, bleeding, contrast‐associated acute kidney injury, and stroke, representing a valuable tool for mitigation of modifiable risk factors.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT01433627.Keywords: acute coronary syndrome, acute kidney injury, bleeding, mortality, percutaneous coronary intervention, risk score, strokeSubject Categories: Percutaneous Coronary Intervention, Stent

The prediction of adverse events after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is paramount for guiding the adoption of preventive measures aimed at mitigating modifiable risk factors. The serious clinical implications of adverse events after PCI and the large variation in their prevalence explain the large number of prediction risk scores developed in the past 20 years. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 However, the majority of these scoring systems predict only a single outcome per score (eg, not multiple outcomes) assessed at different time frames, requiring the use of different models for the prediction of multiple events after PCI. Additionally, some risk scores included only preprocedural variables, whereas others require very detailed angiographic and procedural features that make their implementation in practice difficult and time consuming. Finally, some of these risk scores were developed and externally validated in noncontemporary patient cohorts, before the wide adoption of radial access, newer‐generation drug‐eluting stent and tailored pharmacological strategies. Recently, a contemporary risk score (the Mayo Clinic Integer Risk Score) has been developed to predict the occurrence of in‐hospital mortality, postprocedural bleeding, acute kidney injury (AKI), and stroke from a large real‐world cohort of patients undergoing PCI showing high discriminatory performance. 9 Although overcoming some limitations of previous risk scores, including limited practical use or poor performance in specific clinical scenarios, this risk score underwent a temporal but not yet an external validation. 9 Furthermore, in the derivation and temporal validation data set, patients with myocardial infarction (MI) represented only a minority (24%); hence, the score performance in this high‐risk patient cohort remains unknown.

We sought to externally validate this novel comprehensive risk score in a large cohort of patients with ACS undergoing invasive management from the MATRIX (Minimizing Adverse Haemorrhagic Events by Transradial Access Site and Systemic Implementation of Angiox) trial. 10 , 11

METHODS

Study Design and Population

The MATRIX trial is a program of 3 randomized controlled, multicenter, superiority trials (NCT 01433627) in patients with ACS undergoing invasive management. 12 The MATRIX‐Access compared radial versus femoral access in 8404 patients with ACS. 11 , 13 The MATRIX‐ Antithrombin and Treatment duration compared bivalirudin versus unfractionated heparin and prolonged post‐PCI bivalirudin infusion versus short‐term bivalirudin administration in patients undergoing PCI. 10 The inclusion and exclusion criteria of the MATRIX trial are reported in the Supplemental Material.

All patients enrolled in the MATRIX trial were eligible for this study if they underwent PCI. In case of missing values for score predictors, formal imputation was made. The MATRIX trial was approved by the institutional review board at each participating site, and all patients gave written informed consent. Data from this study will be made available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Objectives

The primary objective of the present study was to appraise the receiver‐operator characteristics (ROC) of the risk score models for all‐cause mortality; Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) type 2, 3, or 5 bleeding; contrast‐associated (CA)‐AKI; and stroke up to hospital discharge. Secondary objectives were the rates of all‐cause mortality; BARC type 2, 3, or 5; and stroke at 1 year. The discriminative performance of the Mayo Clinic risk score was also compared with other risk scores for mortality (GRACE [Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events]) 14 ; BARC type 2, 3, or 5 bleeding (ACUITY [Acute Catheterization and Urgent Intervention Triage Strategy]) 15 ; and CA‐AKI (Mehran II risk score). 16 CA‐AKI was defined according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes criteria as an absolute (≥0.3 mg/dL) or a relative (≥1.5 times) increase of serum creatinine (sCr) levels within hospitalization compared with baseline values. Details about the other outcomes definitions are presented in the Supplemental Material. An independent clinical events committee, blinded to treatment allocation, adjudicated all clinical events.

Calculation of the Mayo Clinic Integer Risk Score

The Mayo Clinic integer risk score includes up to 23 demographic, clinical, angiographic, and procedural variables (models A) assigning 0 to 100 points to each variable: age, sex, MI within 24 hours, ST‐segment–elevation MI (STEMI) at clinical presentation, cardiogenic shock, unstable angina, Canadian Heart class ≥3, diabetes, prior PCI, prior coronary artery bypass grafting, peripheral arterial disease, chronic lung disease, cerebrovascular disease, sCr, metastatic cancer, cardiac arrest, anemia, congestive heart failure, femoral access, left main stem disease, number of diseased vessels, left ventricular ejection fraction ≤40%, and contrast media volume. Model B included only precatheterization variables, thus excluding the latter 5 variables. Canadian Heart class and metastatic cancer were not systematically collected in the electronic case report form and therefore not considered in the present analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Differences across groups were assessed using the Student's t test in case of continuous variables and the chi‐square or Fisher exact test in case of categorical data. The discriminative ability of the Mayo Clinic risk score for the primary end point was assessed through the ROC area under the curve (AUC) with 95% CIs on the overall study population for models A and B. Missing values for score variables (creatinine [44 missing], congestive heart failure [244 missing], total contrast medium [120 missing], arterial pressure [61 missing], heart rate [71 missing], white blood count [124 missing]) were imputed using a multiple imputation model with chained equations. Twenty imputations were performed and effect estimates combined using Rubin's rules. A sensitivity analysis in the nonimputed population was also performed.

The discriminative ability of the 2 models for all‐cause mortality; BARC type 2, 3, or 5 bleeding; and CA‐AKI were compared with other well‐established scoring systems for the prediction of in‐hospital adverse events 14 , 15 , 16 using DeLong's test P values for pairwise AUC comparisons. Calibration was assessed comparing predicted and observed risks using calibration plots. 17 Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit test was also appraised. The discriminative performance of the 2 scores was also appraised in subgroups of interest by sex, age, and diabetes status. Net reclassification improvement was calculated to determine how model A reclassified patients into various risk categories compared with model B. Kaplan–Meier cumulative curves for all‐cause mortality; BARC type 2, 3, or 5 bleeding; and stroke at 1 year were constructed for time‐to‐event variables with comparisons across score tertiles groups performed using the log‐rank test. Associations of each score tertile with clinical outcomes were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards model adjusted by age (continuous), sex, body mass index (continuous), arterial hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, and renal failure.

All analyses were 2 sided and P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Missing data were not replaced. All statistical analyses were performed with Stata release 18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Of the 8404 patients enrolled in the MATRIX trial, 6724 patients were treated by PCI and were included in this analysis.

In the overall study population (n=6724 patients), mean age was 65.5±11.7 years, 77% were men with a significant prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors (arterial hypertension in 62.3%, hypercholesterolemia in 44.2%, diabetes in 22.3%) and comorbidities (peripheral arterial disease in 7.9%, prior PCI in 14.5%) (Table 1). Nearly 41.0% and 54.4% presented with non‐STEMI and STEMI, respectively. Other baseline characteristics are shown in the Table 1. Overall, mean amount of used contrast medium was 173±83 mL and procedural success was achieved in 93.6% of all treated lesions (Table 2). Left anterior descending artery was the most common treated vessel (49.3%), followed by the right coronary artery (32.9%), left circumflex (26.8%), left main (4.0%), and bypass graft (0.8%) (Table 2).

Validation of the Mayo Clinic Integer Risk Score

In the overall study population, in‐hospital all‐cause death occurred in 95 (1.4%) patients; BARC type 2, 3, or 5 bleeding in 402 (6.0%) patients; CA‐AKI in 367 patients (5.6%); and stroke in 20 patients (0.3%). The observed and predicted risks of in‐hospital mortality, bleeding, CA‐AKI, and stroke showed a stepwise increase through the risk categories by both models A and B (Figures 1 and 2). The Mayo Clinic integer risk score showed good discriminatory power for all‐cause mortality with a ROC AUC of 0.88 (95% CI, 0.85–0.91) for model A and 0.88 (95% CI, 0.84–0.92) for model B. The risk of mortality was well calibrated with model A (Figure S1; Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit: P=0.348) and underestimated with model B (Figure S2; Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit: P=0.002). Sensitivity and specificity for each risk score threshold for mortality are shown in the Tables S1 and S2.

Subgroup Analyses and Comparison With Other Risk Scores

The discriminative ability of the Mayo Clinic risk score remained largely consistent across subgroups of interest, except for a higher risk score performance of both models for bleeding in male than in female patients (Table S11).

The discriminative ability of both models was compared with other validated risk scores for in‐hospital mortality (GRACE); BARC type 2, 3, or 5 bleeding (ACUITY); and CA‐AKI (model 1 and 2 of the Mehran II risk score) (Table S12). The discriminative power of the Mayo Clinic risk score for mortality was comparable to the GRACE score using both the models A (DeLong P=0.582) and B (DeLong P=0.660). Compared with the ACUITY score, the score showed similar discriminative power using both models A (DeLong P=0.139) and model B (DeLong P=0.999). Both models of the Mayo Clinic risk score showed a comparable discriminative power for CA‐AKI compared with model 1 (DeLong P=0.354 for model A; DeLong P=0.456 for model B), but model B had a lower discriminative power compared with model 2 (DeLong P=0.005) of the Mehran II risk score.

The risk of 1‐year mortality, bleeding, and stroke was evaluated as a function of risk score tertiles for both models. DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study aimed at externally validating a newer scoring system for the prediction of fatal and nonfatal in‐hospital adverse events in a large cohort of patients with ACS undergoing invasive management. The main findings of the present analysis can be summarized as follows:

The Mayo Clinic Risk Score Performance

The Mayo Clinic risk score is based on a single‐center, large (n=19 322 patients from 2000 to 2016), real‐world cohort of patients undergoing PCI and included a comprehensive model with precatheterization clinical and procedural variables (model A) and a simplified model excluding procedural variables (model B). 9 Using machine learning methods, the risk score was derived from a common set of variables and internally validated for predicting the risk of multiple outcomes (in‐hospital death, bleeding, CA‐AKI, and stroke) after PCI. In our study, the score performance remained largely consistent across subgroups of interest except for the prediction of bleeding by sex. It is worth noting that an improvement in reclassification for bleeding and AKI was noted with model A versus model B.

The present study demonstrated a good discriminatory power for mortality and a moderate performance of the risk score for other outcomes in patients with ACS undergoing invasive management, although slightly lower than in the original derivation and internal validation cohort. 9 Possible explanations for this lower discriminative ability might reside in the different baseline risk of the considered patient populations. The MATRIX trial included an unselected, relatively low‐risk population of patients with ACS treated with PCI undergoing invasive management, of whom only 5% with unstable angina. 18 Patients included in randomized trials usually exhibit a lower burden of comorbidities compared with “real‐world” populations. Despite the data set used for the development of the Mayo Clinic risk score encompassed patients with higher prevalence of risk factors and concomitant comorbidities, it included 46.5% with unstable angina whereas only 17.4% had a STEMI at clinical presentation. 9 Additionally, in the derivation and internal validation data set, the vast majority (83.1%) of patients underwent catheterization via the femoral access and did not receive newer‐generation drug‐eluting stent. Although patients with ACS usually exhibit higher risks of fatal and nonfatal ischemic and bleeding events due to the concomitant presence of hemodynamic instability, advanced coronary artery disease (eg, multivessel and calcified disease, left main stem involvement) requiring complex interventions 19 , 20 and more extensive left ventricular dysfunction, subjects included in randomized trials are usually younger and exhibit a lower burden of comorbidities compared with real‐world populations.

In line with the original derivation cohort 9 and other reports, 21 , 22 the risk score performance was not significantly affected by the exclusion of procedural variables in the model B. Procedural features such as left main stem involvement or other features included in the SYNTAX score have been repeatedly shown to carry prognostic information in patients undergoing PCI. 23 Nowadays, technological advancements in PCI materials and techniques (eg, newer‐generation drug‐eluting stent, calcium modification techniques, intracoronary imaging) could have made less relevant procedural variables than in the past, emphasizing the importance of clinical features in PCI risk determination. Other than clinical and procedural variables such as biomarkers should be appraised in prediction models and potentially integrated in future risk scores development.

Comparison With Other Risk Scores

The Mayo Clinic risk score performed well compared with other well‐established risk prediction tools for in‐hospital mortality (GRACE) 14 and bleeding (ACUITY). 15 The GRACE score is a simple prediction tool for mortality at 6 months after ACS that includes 8 readily available items: age, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, sCr, cardiac arrest at admission, ST‐segment deviation on ECG, abnormal cardiac enzymes, and Killip class. 14 The performance of the GRACE score has repeatedly shown excellent discrimination ability and calibration in patients with ACS, 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 making it one of the most widely used risk scores for mortality in practice. The ACUITY score has been derived and validated for the prediction of noncoronary artery bypass grafting‐related major bleeding within 30 days after ACS and includes 7 clinical variables: age, sex, sCr, white blood cell count, anemia, clinical presentation (non‐STEMI or STEMI), and antithrombotic medications. 15 Although several risk scores have been developed to predict the risk of bleeding after PCI, 3 , 28 , 29 , 30 the ACUITY is one of the few scores estimating the risk of short‐term bleeding after PCI.

The Mayo Clinic risk score showed a similar discriminative ability for CA‐AKI compared with the model 1 but lower than the model 2 of the Mehran II risk score. 7 , 16 The model 1 of the Mehran II CA‐AKI risk score includes 8 preprocedural variables, and the model 2 also included 4 procedural variables. The inclusion of a more contemporary cohort of patients undergoing PCI (from 2012 to 2020) and the adoption of the Acute Kidney Injury Network definition in the derivation data set of the Mehran II risk score may account for the higher discriminative power of the latter compared with the Mayo Clinic risk score. The risk of CA‐AKI was apparently underestimated using the Mayo Clinic risk score. Future studies should assess whether this reflects the selection of patients at higher risk of AKI in our data set or whether recalibration of the risk score is warranted.

It should be acknowledged that, despite the good performance of the Mayo Clinic risk score for the prediction of fatal and nonfatal adverse events, the score calculation at the bedside might be time demanding and automated systems for risk score calculation are warranted.

Mortality and Bleeding Risk After PCI

Our analysis further extended previous findings showing that the Mayo Clinic risk score is also able to predict the risk of long‐term (eg, 1 year) adverse events, including mortality, bleeding, and stroke. Patients in the highest risk score tertile had >9‐fold higher risk of mortality, >2‐fold higher risk of bleeding, and >4‐fold higher stroke risk at 1 year compared with lower risk groups. These findings carry important clinical implications since the accurate and comprehensive estimation of risks after ACS may prompt the adoption of tailored preventive strategies in high‐risk patients during (eg, radial access, glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor avoidance, lower intensity of heparin treatment) and after PCI (eg, more intensified lipid‐lowering therapies, tailored antithrombotic strategies).

Study Limitations

Some limitations of this study should be considered. First, although the MATRIX trial included a large, unselected population of patients with ACS with broad inclusion and minimal exclusion criteria, patients with history of recent (<30 days) PCI, with severe renal dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min per 1.73 m2) or on dialysis treatment were excluded from the trial. Therefore, our results might not apply to these high‐risk patient cohorts. Second, metastatic cancer and cardiac arrest at presentation were not included in the score calculation, because data on the former were not systematically collected in the electronic case report forms and the latter was an exclusion criterion for trial participation. Third, renal output data were not available, thus the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes classification solely relied on the absolute or relative changes in sCr values. Finally, sCr was not longitudinally appraised during follow‐up, thus the impact of this comprehensive scoring system on postdischarge AKI events could not be established.

CONCLUSIONS

The present study externally validated a contemporary, comprehensive risk score for the prediction of in‐hospital fatal and nonfatal events (bleeding, CA‐AKI, and stroke) in a large cohort of patients with ACS undergoing invasive management. The simplified risk score excluding procedural features (model B) favorably compares with other well‐established scoring systems, carrying the advantage of estimating the risk of all 4 outcomes using a common set of clinical variables. The score also successfully identifies patients at higher risks of mortality and bleeding at 1 year after PCI, representing a valuable tool for mitigation of modifiable risk factors and tailored adoption of preventive measures.
