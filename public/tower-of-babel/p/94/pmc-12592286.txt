Intelligent design and synthesis of nucleobase and amino acid-based ligands to inhibit the JAK2 enzyme to treat rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Ameneh Zarei, Alireza Fatahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatoid arthritis is the most prevalent form of autoimmune arthritis. In modern drug development, computer-aided drug design plays a crucial role not only in discovering new therapeutic compounds but also in assessing their potential efficacy. This study employs ligand-based pharmacophore modeling to identify novel drug candidates without requiring an initial target protein structure. Essential chemical features were extracted from nucleotides, amino acids, and sulfonylaziridine derivatives to construct potential drug scaffolds. Ligands exhibiting similar structural characteristics were then screened for promising chemical interactions. Using MATLAB software, the initial pool of 4,000 candidates was narrowed down to 330, which was further reduced to 58 and finally to 8 through AutoDock screening. These top candidates were further analyzed using molecular docking (static analysis) and molecular dynamics simulations (dynamic analysis). Among them, the compound CPROSCP demonstrated favorable dynamic stability, an appropriate hydrodynamic radius, minimal distortion of protein structure, and advantageous binding energy. The synthesized drug candidate achieved a yield of approximately 92%, with IR and NMR spectroscopy confirming its successful synthesis and high purity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00726-025-03487-w.Keywords: Ligand-based drug design, Rheumatoid arthritis, Janus kinase 2, Nucleobase synthesis, Molecular dynamics simulation

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is the most common type of autoimmune arthritis. In RA, your body’s immune system attacks its healthy tissues, causing significant inflammation in the joints and various other organs. This can cause joint pain, stiffness, swelling, and reduced joint flexibility (Aletaha and Smolen 2018; Bullock et al. 2019). It typically starts in small peripheral joints, is usually symmetric, and progresses to involve proximal joints if left untreated. Joint inflammation over time leads to the destruction of the joint, characterized by loss of cartilage and bone erosions (Bullock et al. 2019; Klareskog et al. 2020). Other organs, such as the eyes and lungs, may also be affected. It’s a long-term illness. RA symptoms can occur at any age, but are more common in middle age (30s to 50 s). Women are 2 to 3 times more likely to develop RA than men (Bereda 2023). RA is a chronic disease, and while there is currently no cure, there are many ways to treat it and alleviate symptoms. Early diagnosis and appropriate treatments can reduce symptoms and prevent joint damage or disability (Anderson et al. 1985; Conigliaro et al. 2019). A disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) such as methotrexate, leflunomide, hydroxychloroquine, and sulfasalazine is often prescribed in the early stages of RA. If DMARDs alone do not control RA inflammation, then drugs such as tofacitinib, Upadacitinib, or Baricitinib will be prescribed (Sparks 2019; Smolen et al. 2016; Fraenkel et al. 2021; Mori et al. 2021). Baricitinib (Olumiant®) is a synthetic oral DMARD that targets Janus kinase (JAK) inhibition (Al-Salama and Scott 2018; Ahmad 2022). JAKs are tyrosine protein kinases that play an essential role in pro-inflammatory signaling pathways (Yeh and Pellegrini 1999). This new small molecule, by inhibiting the actions of JAK1 and JAK2, attenuates JAK-mediated inflammation and immune responses and was approved by the European Commission for use as monotherapy or in combination with methotrexate for the treatment of adults in February 2017 (Markham 2017). However, biological and synthetic agents have uncommon but serious side effects, such as bacterial, viral, and fungal infections, skin cancer, skin reactions, allergic reactions, heart failure, and nerve damage. The decrease in white blood cells was not as noticeable, and researchers are seeking to replace them with new drugs that have fewer side effects (Mori et al. 2021; Genovese et al. 2016).

Today, computational-aided drug design (CADD) methods have rapidly replaced traditional methods that relied entirely on laboratory studies. CADD is a process that not only leads to the discovery of new drugs but also provides an opportunity to measure optimal drug effectiveness by examining factors such as bioavailability, toxicity, biocompatibility, appropriate drug half-life, distribution, and absorption (Yu and Adedoyin 2003; Tollenaere 1996; Stromgaard 2009). These issues play a crucial role in reducing production costs and accelerating progress. In general, the drug design process begins with the identification of a target for the drug and a lead compound. It encompasses two primary methods: receptor structure-based drug design (SBDD) and ligand-based drug design (LBDD) (Merz 2010). Based on these methods, computational chemistry in drug design includes QSAR, 3D-QSAR, Pharmacophore, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics methods (Young 2009; Sarkar and Kellogg 2009; Wolber et al. 2008). Numerous experimental studies have been conducted on the treatment and management of rheumatoid arthritis. However, since this project aims to utilize computational methods for designing a novel drug, a review of previous work in this field is warranted.

In 2014, Lee et al. In one study, potential candidates from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) were identified that inhibit the interleukin-6 receptor (IL-6R) in the treatment of RA. The final candidate compound for TCM, calycosin, obtained through the virtual screening method, exhibited a strong binding affinity to the IL-6R protein. Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulations were used to confirm the stability of the protein complex interaction with calycosin. Analysis showed that the protein complex with calycosin was more stable. Therefore, calycosin is a potential candidate for further investigation as a lead drug in the development of RA-targeting therapies that inhibit the IL6R protein (Lee et al. 2014). In 2017, Li et al. investigated cytokines as therapeutic targets in a broad panel of natural compounds for the treatment of RA. Here, an SBDD approach, including virtual screening and molecular docking, was used to target the IL-6R. A total of 48,531 natural compounds were screened, and only two of these compounds were shortlisted for molecular binding simulation and tested for inhibition of gp130 dimerization in human macrophages. Their results showed that Lead5 (CID5329098) has significant inhibitory effects (Chen et al. 2017). In 2020, Choi et al. used CADD methods to demonstrate that, given the crucial role of Toll-like receptors, particularly TLR4, in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including inflammatory, autoimmune, and rheumatoid arthritis, only a few therapeutic modulators targeting TLR4 expression are currently in clinical trials (ul Ain et al. 2020). In 2022, according to the research by Li et al., Wang et al. identified 432 potential substances (active ingredients) with apparent pharmacological effects when using AutoDock Vina in the treatment of RA. Absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) have also been studied. On the other hand, their effects were confirmed on mouse synovial cells using the cell proliferation method. Using bioinformatics and CADD, potential anti-RA small molecules and their mechanisms of action were comprehensively identified (Sun et al. 2022). Additionally, in 2022, an in-depth study was conducted by A. Baig et al. using a CADD approach on various herbal, natural, and synthetic inhibitors, examining their effects on receptors involved in RA stimulation, including IL-6R, the Histocompatibility of the human leukocyte antigen receptor (HLA-DR), and CD20. Additionally, drug adjustment is performed using Lipinski’s rule, taking into account the ADME of the ligands. The results suggest that calycosin-7-O-glucoside and angeliferulate are possible ligands for IL6R and HLA-DR, respectively. However, pharmacokinetic properties suggest that angeliferulate is an effective ligand for biological systems compared to calycosin-7-O-glucoside (Imran et al. 2022). In 2023, Thelma et al. used MD simulations with a force field including polarization to investigate and model protein solubilization and ligand binding to mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAP3K8). The advantage of this method lies in the reliable classification of MAP3K8 inhibitors in structure-activity relationship studies in the nanomolar range (Sandhu et al. 2023). It is noteworthy that due to the lack of a transparent mechanism for RA disease, studies in this regard are still ongoing (Raj et al. 2015; Faris et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2023; Polykovskiy et al. 2018).

In this study, we aim to utilize ligand-based pharmacophore modeling as a key computational strategy for discovering new drugs in the absence of a target protein. Specifically, our designed ligands differ from Baricitinib in three significant ways that modify the interaction network: (a) the added nucleobase fragment provides additional π-systems and oriented H-bonding vectors that can interact with aromatic and H-bonding residues; (b) the sulfonyl-azetidine unit offers a geometrically constrained sulfonyl acceptor and a compact, angle-defined motif; and (c) the proline/pyrrolidine carboxamide linkers introduce a chiral, H-bond-donating/accepting junction that stabilizes multi-point contacts. These alterations enable the new scaffolds to establish further, spatially distinct hydrogen-bond and van der Waals interactions compared to Baricitinib. For this purpose, first, several drug candidates are introduced by extracting suitable chemical features from nucleotides, amino acids, and various sulfonylazitidine derivatives that are well-matched to the structure of the target drug. Then, a set of known ligands that also provide suitable structural features is screened, and in the next step, a screening is also performed in terms of suitable chemical interactions. In the next step, a more detailed investigation is conducted on the screened candidates using non-dynamic simulations, such as molecular docking, and dynamic simulations, such as molecular dynamics. And in the final step, a suitable synthesis method for the new drug and its characterization are presented to ensure the correct synthetic steps.

Computational methods and experimental details

Virtual screening

Considering the various binding configurations of nucleobases, which include five types of nucleotide bases and twenty amino acids, alongside dipeptides formed from two groups of twenty amino acids, a large pool of candidate ligands is generated when interacting with eight sulfonylazitidine derivatives. These candidates are screened using MATLAB R2024a software (Inc 2022), with a focus on their physical attributes. The ligands identified in the previous stage are then ranked based on their interaction energy and affinity using the AutoDock Vina 1.2.0 package (Eberhardt et al. 2021). To ensure a more thorough screening process. The OSIRIS Property Explorer software (Sander and Property Explorer 2001) was also used to calculate pharmacokinetic properties, and the MOE software (Molecular 2024) was utilized to examine the details of the interaction between the drug candidate components and the receptor protein.

Molecular Docking simulation

Molecular docking is a practical computational approach utilized in drug design. This simulation helps identify the optimal binding mode and interaction sites between a small molecule (ligand) and a macromolecule (Young 2009). The AutoDock4.2.3 package (Hou et al. 2013) was applied to set up docking calculations between the ligands and the protein. The PDB file of the protein (ID: 6WTO) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. The PDB file of the ligands was then optimized using a DFT study and loaded into AutoDock. AutoDockTools1.5.6 (Morris et al. 2009). The preparation of coordinate files for the ligand and protein (PDBQT format) was applied. The docking simulation was enclosed in a box with dimensions of 126 × 126 × 126 points along all axes, respectively, and a spacing of 0.375 Å. The grid maps were generated using AutoGrid4.2.3. For docking calculations, a Lamarckian genetic algorithm (LGA) was implemented with a total of 300 runs for the binding site. All other parameters were set to default settings. For each docking case, the lowest-energy docked conformation, as determined by the AutoDock scoring function, was selected as the binding mode.

Molecular dynamics simulation

In this research, the primary and stable protein structure of JAK2, represented in PDB format by ID 6WTO, was obtained from the RCSB web database (RCSB 2022). The initial configurations of the drug target and its candidates were illustrated using SPARTAN14 (Shao et al. 2006), and subsequently optimized via density functional theory (DFT) (Parr 1983) at the B3LYP level (Yanai et al. 2004), employing a 6-31G+(d, p) (Rassolov et al. 1998) basis set. The GROMACS 2020.6 software package (Van Der Spoel et al. 2005) was used to conduct all-atom molecular dynamics simulations, employing the GROMOS 54a7 force field [Schmid et al. 2011; Huang et al. 2011], a commonly adopted force field in many bioresearch studies, such as those involving proteins, peptides, and biopolymers (Huang et al. 2011; Salomon-Ferrer et al. 2013; Salehi et al. 2022; Rezaeisadat et al. 2024). The GROMACS package directly supplies protein parameters through the utilization of the pdb2gmx command, and the other parameters for the target drug and candidates in the GROMOS54a7 force field were extracted from the Automated Topology Builder (ATB) web-accessible server (Malde et al. 2011). The simulation box was defined with dimensions of 8.6 × 8.6 × 8.6 nm³. The 3-point water molecule model with enhanced charge correlation, referred to as SPC/E, was applied (Vega and Abascal 2011). To maintain charge equilibrium in the system, chlorine ions (Cl¯) were incorporated into the simulation box. The simulations employed periodic boundary conditions (PBC) in all three dimensions. The system was simulated at a constant temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1 bar, facilitated by the Nose–Hoover thermostat (Braga and Travis 2005) and the Parrinello-Rahman barostat (Bussi et al. 2007), which were configured with time constants of 1.0 and 2.0 ps, respectively. The LINCS algorithm was employed to restrict the vibrations of bonds that include hydrogen atoms (Hess et al. 1997). Long-range electrostatics were addressed via the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) approach (Essmann et al. 1995). A cutoff radius of 1.2 nm was implemented for nonbonded interactions, short-range electrostatics, and the short-range neighbor list. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm (Deift and Zhou 1993), which consisted of 50,000 steps. The simulations lasted for 400 ns, using a time step of 2 fs throughout the process. The movements of the system (Trajectories) were logged at 100 ps intervals. The acquisition of all images was performed using the VMD (Humphrey et al. 1996) and Chimera (Pettersen et al. 2004) software tools.

Materials and instruments

DCM (Dichloromethane), DIEA (N, N-Diisopropylethylamine), DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide), and Pyridine were bought from Merck. Solvents and common reagents are used in analytical grade. Fmoc-proline (N-9-Fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl-L-proline), TBTU (N-[(1 H-Benzotriazol-1-yl) (dimethylamino)methylene]-N-methylmethanaminium tetrafluoroborate N-oxide), Cytosine, and 1-(ethylsulfonyl)−3-(prop-2-yn-1-ylidene) azetidine were received from Sigma-Aldrich. The drug candidate was synthesized and specified according to the method reported in part 3.3. The final drug candidate was purified, dried, and then tested using FTIR and NMR for identification. Recording FTIR spectrum was performed by a Bruker-vertex70 spectrometer within the 4000–400 cm-1 range. 1HNMR (400 MHz) spectra were obtained in a DMSO-d6 solution in a Bruker-Avance 400 spectrometer.

Results and discussion

Computational and virtual screening

The ligand-based pharmacophore model aims to develop a drug structure that aligns with the physicochemical characteristics of the drug Baricitinib, derived from new nucleobase-amino acid structures. In essence, the target drug must possess physical attributes, including molecular mass, surface area, hydrophilicity, and hydrophobicity, that correspond to the requirements of the receptor protein’s active site. Additionally, it should possess the correct chemical properties and functional groups to facilitate the necessary interactions with the active site. To achieve this goal, potential drug candidates have been identified that result from the integration of five nucleotide bases and twenty amino acids, in addition to eight recognized derivatives of sulfonylazitidine (See Table S1). Furthermore, through structural rearrangement, one can explore the pairing of two series of amino acids (dipeptide structures) with sulfonylazitidine derivatives. This combination of various possible configurations leads to approximately 4,000 distinct drug candidate structures.

The preliminary research operates under the assumption that the dimensions of the receptor protein’s active site are unknown. To expedite the initial screening process, a MATLAB script was utilized to analyze all drug candidate structures. These structures were then compared and selected based on a 10% variance in volume and surface area relative to the original lead drug, Baricitinib. This script is founded on the molecular mass of fundamental structures, including basic nucleotides, amino acids, and various sulfonylazitidine derivatives. It employs a nested loop to generate all potential configurations for the three-part components required to resemble the lead drug’s structure.

Additionally, since the densities of these proposed structures are nearly identical, their volumes and surface areas are approximated. As a result of this initial analysis, 332 structures were identified that exhibit only a 10% difference in volume and surface area from the lead drug. The structures were subsequently refined using Spartan software and density functional theory (DFT) to obtain the optimal structural parameters, including bond lengths and angles. Additionally, physical properties such as volume, surface area, polarizability, and distribution coefficient were evaluated for both the lead drug, Baricitinib, and the proposed structures (see Table 1).

The binding energy (affinity) of the designed drug candidates with receptor proteins

In the initial phase, a screening focused on physical and structural properties was conducted using MATLAB software. However, in this current phase, the emphasis shifts to a screening based on chemical-structural properties. These chemical-structural properties pertain to the interactions between various function-al groups of the lead drug and the drug candidates with the active site of the receptor protein. To achieve this, AutoDock Vina has been employed, utilizing an empirical scoring function to assess and rank the binding affinity between the ligand and receptor in molecular docking simulations. Specifically, these simulations do not involve dynamic changes, and the scoring function is designed to estimate the binding free energy, serving as a quantitative measure of the docking process’s effectiveness. The scoring approach of AutoDock Vina integrates various energy components, considering both the intermolecular interactions between the ligand and receptor and the intramolecular interactions within the ligand. The binding energy (or affinity) of the lead drug Baricitinib with receptor protein (PDB-ID: 6WTO) was determined to be approximately − 8.4 kcal/mol. Taking into account a 10% variation in affinity between the lead drug and the target drug candidates, 58 structures were identified as energetically favorable. These findings are detailed in Table 1.

From the 58 drug candidates listed in Table 1 and 8 candidates exhibiting the highest similarity in binding affinity (binding energy) to the receptor protein, in comparison to the lead drug, were chosen for further calculations. As mentioned earlier, AutoDock Vina does not consider dynamics for ligands and receptors, but in AutoDock, this dynamic is considered for ligands. In simpler terms, the receptor is not dynamic, but the ligand has dynamics inside its active site. Therefore, this technique was used in the following to more accurately find the active site in the protein for interaction with the proposed structures and the degree of compliance of this site concerning the original drug and new candidates (overly similarity). The degree of similarity between the active site of the lead drug and the drug candidate structure with the highest binding energy is predicted by AutoDock software to be approximately 0.9, which seems reasonable given a 10% difference. In the next step, four of the eight drug candidates that show the highest similarity are selected for further detailed examination through molecular dynamics calculations. According to the results of the dominance analysis in Fig. 1, although it is predicted that drug candidate CPROSCP has the highest alignment with the lead drug, for a more detailed examination, the four drug candidates with the highest alignment were considered for molecular dynamics simulation.

The radius of gyration (Rg) is a parameter that indicates flexibility (elastic stability) or, conversely, the resistance to bending. The RMSD parameter assesses the dynamics of a specific structure, whereas the radius of gyration or hydrodynamic radius quantifies its size or volume (Pollack 2009; Rezaeisadat et al. 2021a, b). In this research, the radius of gyration was used to assess the degree of aggregation or flexibility of the drug candidates. Therefore, in this study, we expect that the hydrodynamic radius, or in other words, the distribution of atoms of a drug candidate structure around its axis in the active site of the protein, will be similar to that of the lead drug. Following the RMSD for drug stability, the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) is a crucial parameter for evaluating protein stability. This parameter was determined to assess the variation of residues in protein. In other words, this metric indicates the extent to which a residue deviates from its average location within a set of structures, typically represented by the spatial coordinates of its alpha-carbon (Cα) as part of the protein backbone. The analysis focused on the last 20 ns of the simulation, as these parameters are indicative of protein stability, with the results illustrated in Fig. 4. Synthetic route study

Given that one of the most agreements and alignments is related to CPROSE, the synthesis of this drug candidate was considered. In the initial stage of the synthesis pathway, in a volumetric flask, we accurately weigh 3.33 g of fmoc-proline (N-9-Fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl-L-proline) and 3.29 g of TBTU (N-[(1 H-Benzotriazol-1-yl) (dimethylamino)methylene] -N-methyl methan aminium tetrafluoroborate N-oxide), which we then dissolve in 30 mL of DCM (Dichloromethane). Following this, we introduce DIEA (N, N-Diisopropylethylamine) to adjust the pH to approximately 9.0. The contents of the flask are then stirred at 30 °C for one hour. Subsequently, we add 1.1 g of cytosine, previously dissolved in 10 mL of DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide), and allow it to be stirred at 40 ℃ for 3 h. Add 50 mL of water to the sample, decant the organic phase, and subsequently add 20 mL of pyridine (stirred at 35 ℃ for an hour). Then, the contents of the flask were connected to a vacuum rotary at 40 °C, and the solvent removal process was continued for 45 min until a precipitate was formed. The precipitate was washed with ether to remove the remaining deprotected fmoc-proline. In the next step, to completely dry the obtained precipitate, we placed it in an oven at 40 ℃ for 6 h. In the final step, dissolve 1.0 g of 1-(ethylsulfonyl)−3-(prop-2-yn-1-ylidene)azetidine with the precipitate from the previous step in 30 mL of DCM and stir for 24 h at 40 °C. Subsequently, the final precipitate was rinsed with ether and dried, followed by the execution of identification tests, including IR and NMR. Conclusion

This study aimed to develop novel drug candidates that mimic Baricitinib by integrating nucleotide bases, amino acids, and sulfonylaziridine derivatives, resulting in approximately 4,000 potential compounds. Initial filtering using MATLAB reduced this pool to 332 candidates based on a 10% allowable deviation in volume or surface area relative to Baricitinib. These candidates were further refined using Spartan software with Density Functional Theory (DFT) to optimize bond lengths, angles, and physicochemical properties. AutoDock Vina was employed to assess binding affinities, using Baricitinib’s score of − 8.4 kcal/mol as a reference. Sixty compounds met the energy threshold within a 10% variance. From these, eight candidates with binding energies most closely matching those of Baricitinib were selected for further analysis.

Drug-likeness was evaluated using OSIRIS, while MOE software was used to examine protein-ligand interactions. Flexible docking with AutoDock identified the best-performing candidate, which was aligned with Baricitinib’s binding site, exhibiting 90% structural similarity and a 10% difference in binding strength, indicating comparable binding behavior. Four top candidates were selected for advanced computational studies to assess long-term binding stability.

Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) analysis identified CPROSCP as the most stable candidate (0.34 ± 0.02 nm), surpassing Baricitinib (BCT, 0.44 ± 0.02 nm). CPROSE showed the least stability (0.53 ± 0.02 nm), with overall RMSD stability ranking: CPROSCP > CPROSM > CPROSMP > BCT > CPROSE. Radius of gyration (Rg) analysis showed that CPROSCP (1.97 ± 0.01 nm) and CPROSMP (1.98 ± 0.01 nm) most closely resembled Baricitinib in structural compactness, while CPROSE (2.05 ± 0.01 nm) showed the least similarity. Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) analysis revealed CPROSCP caused the least structural deviation in protein residues, whereas CPROSE resulted in the most. Residue flexibility ranked: CPROSE > CPROSM > CPROSMP > CPROSCP. MM-PBSA free energy calculations confirmed CPROSCP as the strongest binder and CPROSM as the weakest, in agreement with earlier docking results.

Finally, CPROSCP was synthesized with a yield of approximately 92%. The structure and purity of the final product were confirmed using IR and NMR (¹H, ¹³C) spectroscopy.
