Zincophilic Porous Carbon Interface with Dendrite‐Free Zinc Deposition via Selective Ion Regulation for Aqueous Zinc Ion Batteries
Authors: Yanghyun Cho, Chanho An, Yeonjin Lee, Sang Wan Seo, Dayeong Gang, Junsu Son, Gyuri Kim, Minsu Gu, Tae Kyung Lee, Chi‐Ju Kim, Ji Sun Im, Seulki Song, Woo‐Jin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe development of a protective layer that not only blocks parasitic reactions but also actively regulates Zn2+ transport and nucleation behavior is critical for the advancement of aqueous zinc‐ion batteries (AZIBs). In this study, we propose a novel interfacial engineering strategy based on a zincophilic porous carbon (ZPC) coating, which provided selective Zn2+ adsorption and directional Zn2+ flux regulation. The ZPC layer composed of poly (acrylic acid) (PAA)‐grafted carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) (CLP) and activated carbon (AC) synergistically integrates zincophilic functional groups and a porous structure. This design enables rapid Zn2+ desolvation and effectively suppresses dendrite formation. The selective Zn2+ affinity of the ZPC layer minimizes hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and corrosion, while promoting preferential Zn deposition along the (002) crystallographic plane. As a result, ZPC@Zn exhibits an extended lifespan exceeding 3600 h at 4 mA cm−2 and stable Zn plating/stripping at a high depth of discharge (DOD, 43%). Full cells paired with an iodine cathode demonstrate excellent rate capability and outstanding cycle stability, maintaining approximately 90% capacity retention over 5000 cycles at 10 C. This work establishes a new paradigm in interfacial layer design and paves the way for dendrite‐free, high‐performance AZIBs.

Introduction

Recently, commercial lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) have been used in large‐scale energy storage systems (ESS) and small‐scale devices such as portable electronics due to their high energy density (265 Wh kg−1 and 670 Wh L−1) and stable cell voltage (>4.2 V) [1, 2]. However, challenges remain, including fire hazards associated with organic electrolytes and rising raw material costs due to resource depletion [3, 4]. To address these issues, there is a growing need for the next generation of rechargeable batteries that are not only affordable and safe but also environmentally friendly [5]. Aqueous zinc‐ion batteries (AZIBs) have emerged as one of the most promising alternatives, employing zinc (Zn) metal as the anode [6, 7, 8]. Zn metal offers high theoretical capacities (820 mAh g−1 and 5855 mAh L−1), is cost‐effective (0.5–1.5 USD lb−1 compared to 8–11 USD lb−1 for Li metal), and is more abundant in the crust of Earth (79 ppm for Zn versus 17 ppm for Li) [5]. Additionally, Zn is relatively stable in water compared to Li, Na, Mg, and Ca, enabling the use of aqueous electrolytes [9].

However, the low reversibility of Zn metal due to uncontrollable dendrite formation and side reactions (corrosion, hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), and passivation) in aqueous electrolytes remains a major obstacle for applying AZIBs to grid‐scale electrochemical ESS [10, 11]. Dendrites that grow vertically on the Zn metal surface during the plating/stripping process are typically non‐uniform and create “dead Zn”, which not only causes short circuits but also leads to low Coulombic efficiency (CE) [12, 13, 14]. Moreover, since the redox potential of H2O/H2 (−0.41 V versus SHE) in aqueous electrolytes is higher than that of Zn/Zn2+ (−0.76 V versus SHE), Zn reduction is accompanied by water decomposition during cycling [15, 16]. This leads to the production of byproducts due to the high concentration of OH− at the Zn surface, raising the electrolyte pH and promoting continuous electrolyte consumption through the formation of inactive Zn4(OH)6SO4∙xH2O (ZHS) [17, 18]. Passivation of the Zn surface further hinders Zn2+ diffusion, resulting in capacity degradation [19, 20]. Therefore, controlling the reactions at the electrode/electrolyte interface is essential for improving Zn reversibility [21, 22].

Various strategies have been explored to overcome these challenges, including 3D structure design [23], electrolyte optimization [7, 24, 25], separator modification [26, 27, 28], and interface coating [29, 30, 31]. Within these strategies, interface coating offers a simple, effective way to stabilize the electrode/electrolyte interface [32, 33, 34]. Isolating the Zn anode from the electrolyte reduces side reactions and suppresses dendrite formation. For example, polymer‐based protective layers can regulate Zn2+ flux and suppress side reactions through ion‐coordinating functional groups [35]. However, low electronic conductivity and strong polymer/electrolyte interactions often increase polarization and hinder Zn2+ transport during cycling [36]. On the other hand, carbon‐based materials exhibit high conductivity and chemical stability, which mitigate charge accumulation and improve interfacial kinetics [37, 38]. Nevertheless, strong cohesion within carbon materials and weak adhesion to Zn metal compromise the stability of the protective layer. This may block ions and prevent many active sites from interacting with Zn2+ in the electrolyte. Thus, understanding how electrode geometry affects electrochemical mechanisms is crucial for designing advanced electrodes [39, 40]. To address these challenges, forming polymer/carbon composites can synergistically combine the ion‐regulating ability of polymers with the conductivity and stability of carbon [41]. This integration enables effective Zn2+ flux control and enhanced interfacial charge transport, promoting stable Zn electrode interfaces [42].

In this study, we design a zincophilic porous carbon (ZPC) coating with abundant active sites for the Zn anode interface. The ZPC layer consists of hydrophilic polyacrylic acid (PAA)‐grafted sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) (CLP) and activated carbon (AC). The porous structure of AC shortens the diffusion distance of Zn2+ and enhances ion transport, leading to uniform Zn deposition [43]. The CLP reduces interfacial resistance and improves adhesion between the Zn surface and coating layer, thereby facilitating stable interface formation [44]. In addition, zincophilic active sites (e.g., ester groups and carboxyl groups) in CLP lower the Zn nucleation energy barrier, thus suppressing dendrite growth. Consequently, the symmetric cell exhibits a long lifespan of 3,600 h under a current density of 4 mA cm−2 with (002) plane deposition. Full‐cell tests with an iodine cathode further demonstrate impressive performance, maintaining ≈89.85% capacity retention over 5000 cycles at 10 C. This study introduces a new perspective on interface coating design and validates its applicability in AZIBs.

Results and Discussion

Structural and Physicochemical Characterization of Zincophilic Porous Carbon Layer

The interfacial adhesion between the protective layer components and the Zn anode surface is essential for ensuring structural stability [45]. Therefore, rational binder design is crucial for enhancing the integrity of the coating layer. Over the past few decades, AC has been successfully commercialized across research such as electronics, transportation, and renewable energy [46]. During electrode film fabrication, AC forms a composite structure in which the polymer matrix and AC particles are uniformly dispersed [47]. This architecture is mechanically robust and physically stable, and it exhibits high microstructural uniformity [48]. Although PAA is a widely used aqueous binder, and CMC serves as an effective dispersant, both exhibit inherent limitations. PAA tends to form intermolecular hydrogen bonds in water, resulting in polymer aggregation and poor adhesion to large particles such as AC [49]. Furthermore, CMC undergoes swelling due to Na+ dissociation, which weakens particle contact and makes the structure prone to mechanical failure under pressure [50]. To overcome these limitations, a CLP binder was developed to improve coating stability (Figure S1). Comparative experiments demonstrated that CLP formed a more uniform and continuous coating than either PAA or CMC alone (Figure S2). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images confirmed that ZPC particles exhibited no aggregation (Figure S3). Electrochemical performance tests further indicated that CLP provided excellent structural stability (Figure S4). The weak acidity of PAA can protonate the AC surface, promoting OH− attraction and unwanted side reactions [51]. Meanwhile, the volumetric expansion of CMC increases interparticle distances, reducing electrical conductivity. In contrast, CLP contains abundant polar functional groups and ester linkages, which facilitate strong interactions with Zn2+ and enhance electrochemical performance. These results highlight CLP as a promising binder for stable Zn anode protection layers (Figure 1).

Schematic illustration of the Zn plating/stripping process during cycling for bare Zn and ZPC@Zn electrodes.

To evaluate the effect of surface area and functional groups on electrochemical performance, carbon black was selected as a control material. Widely used as a conductive agent due to its excellent electrical conductivity [52], zincophilic carbon black (denoted as ZC) was compared with ZPC. Their surface areas and pore size distributions were assessed using N2 adsorption‐desorption isotherms (Figure S5). The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas were 60 m2 g−1 for ZC and 1206 m2 g−1 for ZPC. The hierarchical pore structure and high surface area of ZPC facilitate Zn2+ transport and deposition while regulating ion flux equilibrium [53]. This porous, functionalized host also provides active sites for the initial deposition of Zn2+. ZC and ZPC coatings were fabricated using a sequential two‐step strategy. First, ozone (O3) treatment was applied to the Zn anode to enhance surface adhesion (Figure S6). The O3 treatment increased the surface energy of the Zn anode by introducing polar functional groups, which improved adhesion with the coating material. Next, ZC and ZPC were applied onto the Zn surface with the CLP binder using a doctor‐blade casting method to achieve a 20 µm thickness (denoted as ZC@Zn and ZPC@Zn; for details, see Figures S7–S12) [54]. The average adhesion force of the ZPC@Zn was 2.8 N, significantly higher than that of ZC@Zn at 0.8 N (Figure S13), confirming that high surface area and rich functional groups of ZPC enhance interfacial interactions with the O3‐treated Zn anode.

SEM images showed that ZC@Zn consisted of densely packed nanoparticles forming compact layers across the surface (Figure 2a). This dense arrangement may induce localized Zn growth, increasing local stress during cycling. In contrast, ZPC@Zn exhibited a morphology composed of micro‐sized particles with internal porosity and interconnected pores (Figure 2b). Although both ZC@Zn and ZPC@Zn had comparable coating thicknesses (Figure 2c,d), their surface architectures differed markedly. The hierarchical porous structure of ZPC promotes Zn2+ diffusion and enhances interfacial interactions, while providing abundant nucleation sites that reduce local current density and suppress dendrite growth during repeated cycling. x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyse the chemical composition and bonding characteristics of the passivation layers on ZC@Zn and ZPC@Zn. The C 1s spectrum of ZC@Zn showed peaks at 284.5 and 286.1 eV, corresponding to C─C and C─O─C/C═O bonds, respectively (Figure 2e). In ZPC@Zn, the intensity of the C─O─C/C═O peak at 286.3 eV was notably increased, suggesting that the oxygen‐containing functional groups on ZPC contributed to a broader contact area with the Zn substrate. Additionally, residual carboxyl groups were converted into more stable carboxylate, indicating a shift in the electronic environment that strengthens interfacial interactions and improves the binding stability of the coating [55]. The O 1s spectrum of ZPC@Zn also showed a more pronounced O═C─O signal, further confirming stronger chemical bonding to the Zn surface, which is critical for enhancing structural integrity and electrochemical stability (Figure 2f).

To elucidate the synergistic mechanism between AC and the CLP polymer, additional XPS and Raman spectroscopy analyses were performed on pristine AC (Figures S14 and S15). Upon interactions with the CLP polymer, the AC surface become enriched with oxygen‐containing and polar functional groups, accompanied by charge transfer in which carbon acts as an electron acceptor [56, 57, 58]. These results indicate that strong interfacial adhesion is established via hydrogen bonding and that the modified interface selectively adsorbs Zn2+ (Figure S16). For long‐term operation, the coating layer must remain intact throughout Zn plating and resist cracking or delamination. This stability depends not only on interfacial interactions but also on the mechanical robustness of the coating itself. Nanoindentation analysis showed that ZPC@Zn (creep: 19 mm) exhibited better structural retention under mechanical stress than ZC@Zn (creep: 23 mm) (Figure S17). These results indicate that the ZPC layer maintains its integrity even under volume expansion during Zn deposition, effectively supporting long‐term cycling stability [59].

To investigate the effects of oxygen‐containing functional groups on the adsorption characteristics of Zn ion under both vacancy‐free and vacancy‐containing conditions, the density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the six model systems; bare graphene (bare (w/o vacancy) and bare (w/ vacancy)), hydroxyl‐functionalized graphene (hydroxyl‐functionalized (w/o vacancy) and hydroxyl‐functionalized (w/ vacancy)), and carboxyl‐functionalized graphene (carboxyl‐functionalized (w/o vacancy) and carboxyl‐functionalized (w/ vacancy)) on the Zn (002) surface. We calculated the adsorption energies of the Zn ion on the different interface systems (Figure S18). In the bare, hydroxyl‐functionalized, and carboxyl‐functionalized systems, the adsorption strength was slightly enhanced in the interface systems with a vacancy compared to the corresponding interface systems without a vacancy. Importantly, the adsorption energies of Zn ion on hydroxyl‐functionalized (w/o and w/ vacancy) (−0.350 and −0.357 eV) and carboxyl‐functionalized (w/o and w/ vacancy) (−0.342 and −0.371 eV) systems were significantly lower than that on bare (w/o and w/ vacancy) system (−0.264 and −0.276 eV), indicating that the oxygen‐containing groups can enhance the zincophilicity of interface system (i.e., graphene@Zn) and facilitate Zn nucleation. Moreover, the analysis of the atomic charge of the Zn ion adsorbed on different interface systems was conducted (Figure 2g). These results reveal a more pronounced interfacial charge transfer between adsorbed Zn ion and interface system (i.e., graphene@Zn) in the hydroxyl‐functionalized (w/o and w/ vacancy) (0.047 and 0.051 e) and carboxyl‐functionalized (w/o and w/ vacancy) (0.039 and 0.058 e) systems compared with bare (w/o and w/ vacancy) system (0.026 and 0.028 e). This enhanced charge transfer suggests that the introduction of an oxygen‐containing functional group can promote stronger Zn ion adsorption.

Stabilization of Interfacial Chemistry and Electrochemical Properties

Electro‐splitting reactions locally increase the pH near the Zn anode surface. In this process, the abundant OH− ions react with dissolved Zn2+ to form byproducts such as ZHS, which deposited on the anode surface [60]. The ZHS that insulating compounds with a loose structure increases the surface area and facilitates side reactions such as HER and corrosion [61, 62]. Experimental results indicate that ZPC@Zn exhibited the most effective suppression of pH fluctuations and ZHS formation induced by HER (Figure S19). To verify the protective effect of the ZPC layer on the Zn anode, its chemical and electrochemical corrosion resistance was investigated using polarization curves. As shown in Figure 3a, Tafel analysis revealed that ZPC@Zn had a lower corrosion current density (0.73 mA cm−2) than both bare Zn (1.05 mA cm−2) and ZC@Zn (0.93 mA cm−2), indicating superior corrosion resistance. To confirm the corrosion inhibition effect of ZPC@Zn, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was conducted to assess HER activity (Figure S20). The onset potential for H2 evolution on ZPC@Zn was significantly higher than bare Zn, demonstrating the effective suppression of HER by the ZPC layer. This performance can be attributed to the ZPC layer physically blocking corrosive interactions between the Zn anode and the electrolyte [63].

When bare Zn was immersed under the same conditions, a strong Raman peak corresponding to the S─O bond (967 cm−1) was observed on its surface. In contrast, this peak was absent on the ZPC@Zn surface (Figure 3b), suggesting that ZHS formation is effectively suppressed by the ZPC layer [64]. After 24 h of aging in 2 m ZnSO4, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) revealed that ZPC@Zn exhibited the lowest charge transfer resistance (Rct) of 407 Ω (Figure 3c). This indicates that the ZPC layer enhances interfacial stability and efficiently suppresses parasitic reactions [18]. As shown in the x‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure 3d; Figure S21), bare Zn displayed new peaks corresponding to ZHS, while ZPC@Zn showed no such byproducts, highlighting a distinct surface composition. However, the surface charge of ZC@Zn attracts anions from the electrolyte, promoting additional reactions within the coating layer and failing to prevent byproduct formation. Bare Zn exhibited random ZHS deposition, whereas ZPC@Zn retained the original structure of the coating with minimal surface byproduct formation. In comparison, ZC@Zn displayed fine precipitates and surface salt residues (Figure 3e–g). These results confirm the superior protective function of the ZPC layer on the Zn anode.

To elucidate the mechanism behind the suppression of HER and the enhancement of Zn2+ flux in ZPC@Zn, we compared the electrochemical behavior of CLP and ZPC. The CLP layer exhibited a low EDL capacitance of 2.83 mF cm−2 and a corrosion potential of −0.011 V. These results indicate limited Zn2+ adsorption and accelerated side reactions, which are key contributors to HER and ununiform Zn plating (Figure S22). Moreover, the SEM image after Zn deposition revealed an uneven morphology and ZHS formation, directly illustrating the poor interfacial stability associated with these electrochemical properties [8]. In contrast, the ZPC composite effectively mitigated these issues by regulating ion flux and suppressing HER. This improvement originated from the mildly acidic pH of CLP (6.1) [65] and highly negative zeta potential (−69.4 mV), which enhanced Zn2+ adsorption while repelling anions (Figure S23). When combined with the porous carbon network of ZPC, these effects synergistically improve Zn2+ transport and charge transfer kinetics, thereby enhancing interfacial reversibility. To further validate these electrochemical advantages, intermittent symmetric cell tests were performed (Figure S24). During the rest period, bare Zn and CLP@Zn experienced a short circuit due to ZHS accumulation and HER. In contrast, ZPC@Zn maintained stable cycling, confirming superior interfacial stability (Figure S25).

During Zn plating/stripping, accelerated interfacial desolvation can hinder the migration of undesired electrolyte components (e.g., OH−, SO4 2−). To investigate the Zn2+ flux regulation and surface charge of ZPC, zeta potential measurements were carried out, confirming its strong Zn affinity. In a 2 m ZnSO4 electrolyte, ZPC exhibited a zeta potential of −4.61 mV, indicating anion repulsion and suppression of the HER from active H2O (Figure 3h) [66]. Notably, this potential was more pronounced in ZnSO4 than in deionized water (DW), suggesting spontaneous electrostatic interactions with Zn2+. To compare electrode wettability, contact angles were measured using both DW and 2 m ZnSO4 (Figure 3i). In DW, ZPC@Zn showed the lowest contact angle (34°) compared to bare Zn (50°) and ZC@Zn (46.4°), attributed to the large surface area and abundant polar functional groups of the ZPC layer. When the solution was changed to 2 m ZnSO4, contact angles increased for bare Zn (64.3°) and ZC@Zn (54.2°), while ZPC@Zn retained the lowest angle (19.5°). The surfaces of bare Zn and ZC@Zn, which are less interactive with Zn2+, maintain more spherical water droplets, resulting in larger contact angles. In contrast, the reduced contact angle on ZPC@Zn reflects its zincophilic property. This strong zincophilicity suggests that Zn2+ preferentially forms an electric double layer (EDL) rather than associating with water molecules [67]. As shown in Figure 3j, ZPC@Zn exhibited a high EDL capacitance of 162.68 mF cm−2, indicating its ability to suppress active water molecules and reduce interfacial polarization via enhanced charge transfer.

Development of Promoting Zn2+ Desolvation and Uniform Deposition

Zn2+ in aqueous electrolytes is typically coordinated with six water molecules to form [Zn(H2O)6]2+ [68]. This coordination occurs before the ion reaches the electrode/electrolyte interface and hinders ion diffusion, resulting in sluggish electrochemical reactions [69]. To assess the interfacial energy barrier at the ZPC@Zn interface, the activation energy (Ea) was evaluated using EIS measurements of Zn‖Zn symmetric cells at various temperatures based on the Arrhenius equation (Figure S26 and Table S1). The Ea of ZPC@Zn (16.40 kJ mol−1) was significantly lower than that of bare Zn (30.84 kJ mol−1), indicating that the ZPC layer inhibited anion adsorption and promoted rapid Zn2+ transport (Figure 4a). Previous studies have shown that ─COO− groups can accelerate the Zn2+ desolvation, thereby reducing Ea [70, 71]. Notably, both CLP@Zn and ZPC@Zn showed lower Rct values than metallic Zn, which is more conductive. This comparison indicates that interfacial charge transfer is primarily governed by facilitating Zn2+ desolvation and optimizing Zn2+ flux at the interface, rather than by simple electronic conductivity (Figure S27) [72, 73]. The Zn2+ transference number (tZn 2+) was determined using Nyquist plots and chronoamperometry (CA) measurements (Figure S28 and Table S2). While bare Zn exhibited a tZn 2+ of 0.48, values for ZC@Zn and ZPC@Zn increased to 0.74 and 0.92, respectively (Figure 4b). The enhanced tZn 2+ of ZPC@Zn is attributed to the interaction between Zn2+ and zincophilic functional groups (e.g., ─COO−, ─COOH), which facilitates rapid and uniform Zn2+ migration. Additionally, electron‐affinity groups (e.g., ─C═O, ─COOH) repels SO4 2 anions, further improving tZn 2+ [74].

The initial nucleation and growth of Zn2+ significantly influence subsequent Zn plating behavior, making the surface of Zn anode condition critical. To investigate the Zn nucleation process, CA was performed at −150 mV for 100 s (Figure 4c) [75]. For bare Zn, current density increased sharply with the applied overpotential, indicating 2D surface diffusion and rough deposition over 150 s, as Zn2+ aggregated to minimize surface energy. Similarly, ZC@Zn exhibited dendrites growth due to 2D diffusion, leading to nonuniform deposition (left inset of Figure 4c). Zn2+ initially occupied energetically favorable surface sites, resulting in uneven charge accumulation. In contrast, ZPC@Zn showed a stable 3D diffusion process over 100 s. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis of the asymmetric cell further confirmed fast and uniform nucleation, attributed to the capacity to guide Zn2+ flux and provide uniform nucleation sites of the ZPC layer (Figure S29).

To analyze Zn deposition morphology more thoroughly, plating was conducted at current densities of 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 mA cm−2, followed by SEM images. As shown in Figure 4d, ZC@Zn surfaces showed sharp dendrites after deposition at 0.5 mA cm−2, which became more pronounced as the current density increased. This behavior is consistent with nonuniform 2D nucleation, where Zn2+ preferentially deposits on protrusions. In contrast, ZPC@Zn displayed smooth surfaces across all current densities, suggesting Zn2+ deposited uniformly on ZPC particles. Even at higher current densities, Zn remained interconnected without dendrites formation, indicating that the ZPC layer supported fast Zn2+ diffusion and regulated interfacial Zn2+ flux to promote uniform 3D growth (Figure 4e). To further understand the role of ZPC porosity in Zn plating/stripping behavior, electrode performance was evaluated while systematically varying ZPC porosity (Figure S30). When the ZPC particle size decreased, internal short circuits occurred after approximately 50 h (Figure S31). These results suggest that adequate porosity in the ZPC layer is essential for enabling interconnected Zn2+ deposition throughout the coating.

Electrochemical Performance of Zincophilic Porous Carbon Structures

To assess the impact of the ZPC layer on the cycling stability of Zn anodes, symmetric cells were assembled and evaluated galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 with an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2. Remarkably, the ZPC@Zn exhibited long‐term operational stability exceeding 1,800 h while maintaining an exceptionally low average overpotential of 23 mV (Figure 5a). This minimal polarization indicates that the ZPC layer effectively lowers the nucleation energy barrier, promoting more uniform Zn deposition [76]. In contrast, the bare Zn cell exhibited rapid voltage fluctuations and failed after approximately 100 h, while the ZC@Zn cell failed prematurely at approximately 900 h, suggesting uncontrolled dendritic Zn growth during cycling. The superior performance of the ZPC@Zn is attributed to the synergistic effects of strong Zn2+ binding and increased interfacial surface area, which facilitate desolvation and lower the kinetic barriers to Zn nucleation (Figure S32). Furthermore, the ZPC@Zn symmetric cell sustained stable operation for over 3,600 h and 300 h at higher current densities of 4 mA cm−2 and 10 mA cm−2, respectively, highlighting the critical role of the ZPC interfacial layer in stabilizing Zn plating/stripping dynamics (Figure S33).

Figure 5b presents the rate capability measured at an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 as the current density increased from 0.5 to 16 mA cm−2. Bare Zn and ZC@Zn exhibited noticeable voltage fluctuations with increasing current density, attributed to the rapid growth of randomly oriented dendritic Zn. In contrast, ZPC@Zn maintained significantly lower voltage hysteresis even at 16 mA cm−2, demonstrating robust interfacial stabilization. As shown in Figure S34, the ZPC@Zn exhibited an exchange current density of 7.37 mA cm−2, substantially higher than that of bare Zn (2.14 mA cm−2) and ZC@Zn (6.00 mA cm−2). This enhancement indicates markedly accelerated Zn plating/stripping kinetics in the presence of the ZPC layer. To further confirm the Zn2+ selective adsorption capability at the interface, a high DOD test was performed (Figure 5c) [77]. The ZPC@Zn maintained low polarization throughout long cycling, suggesting that the ZPC layer enhances Zn2+ utilization and charge transfer efficiency. The interfacial morphology during cycling was further investigated using XRD after 50 cycles at 1 mAh cm−2 (Figure 5d; Figure S35). The intensity ratio of the (002) diffraction peak relative to the (100) and (002) planes was significantly higher for ZPC@Zn (0.67) compared to bare Zn (0.54) and ZC@Zn (0.29). Furthermore, the ZPC@Zn exhibited the highest (002) relative texture coefficient (RTC) value of 25.81% compared to bare Zn (19.40%) and ZC@Zn (9.42%), indicating that the initial nucleation process is also preferentially oriented toward the (002) plane (Table S3). This preferential (002) crystal orientation facilitates Zn2+ deposition parallel to the substrate surface, suppressing dendrite growth and promoting lateral grain expansion (Figure S36).

After cycling, XPS spectra revealed that the surface chemical states of C 1s and O 1s remained unchanged, indicating that the ZPC layer maintained structural stability (Figure S37) [78]. Notably, the Zn 2p peaks of ZPC@Zn shifted toward lower binding energies compared with those of bare Zn and ZC@Zn, suggesting interactions between Zn and the polar functional groups in the ZPC layer (Figure S38) [79]. This shift confirms that the ZPC coating effectively directs Zn nucleation, thereby enabling uniform plating [80]. Asymmetric Zn‖Cu electrodes were also assembled using bare Cu, ZC@Cu, and ZPC@Cu to evaluate the reversibility of Zn plating/stripping in AZIBs [81]. As shown in Figure 5e, the bare Cu exhibited highly unstable and failed via short circuit after 50 cycles. The ZC@Cu failed an average CE of 98.35% over 1,500 cycles, though dendrite growth and non‐uniform deposition limited its performance. In contrast, the ZPC@Cu maintained an average CE of 99.45% over 1,500 cycles, attributed to the uniform deposition and regulation of the Zn2+ flux afforded by the porous ZPC layer (Figure S39). In the in situ optical microscopy (OM) analysis, bare Zn showed highly heterogeneous nucleation, while ZPC@Zn provided homogeneous deposition and suppressed dendrite formation (Figure 5f). These results indicate the capability of the ZPC@ layer in improving Zn stripping/plating reversibility (Table S4).

Practical Application of Zn–Iodine Full Cell

The advantages of the ZPC layer were validated in full cell configurations to assess its effectiveness under practical operating conditions. Full cells were assembled using iodine (I2) loaded AC [82] (denoted as AC@I2) (Figure S40). CV performed at 0.1 mV s−1 revealed that ZPC@Zn possesses a significantly larger electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of 694.51 cm2, compared to bare Zn (582.66 cm2) (Figure 6a). This enhancement is attributed to the hierarchical porosity introduced by the ZPC layer, which increases the number of electrochemically accessible active sites and facilitates charge transfer kinetics [83, 84]. GCD profiles at 0.2 A g−1 further demonstrated that ZPC@Zn‖I2 achieved an initial discharge capacity of 150.94 mAh g−1, approximately 30% higher than that of bare Zn‖I2 (116.21 mAh g−1) (Figure 6b). Additionally, the high electrochemical activity of the ZPC layer contributes to improved rate capability across current densities ranging from 2 to 8 C (Figure 6c; Figure S41), consistent with the increased ECSA and more efficient active site utilization for Zn2+ storage and reaction kinetics.

A key challenge in Zn‐I2 batteries is the migration of polyiodide (I3 −) intermediates to the anode, leading to rapid battery degradation [85, 86]. As shown in Figure 6d, ZPC@Zn displayed higher I3 − absorption peaks at 290 and 351 nm than bare Zn, indicating more effective trapping of polyiodide species. Visual inspection (Figure S42) supported these results, as the ZPC@Zn electrolyte showed no significant color change, suggesting corrosion prevention at the anode due to I3 − ion blocking [87]. Following self‐discharge testing, ZPC@Zn‖I2 maintained 82.12% capacity retention, outperforming bare Zn‖I2 (75.53%) (Figure 6e; Figure S43). After 500 cycles, the bare Zn electrode exhibited I3 − adsorption and byproduct formation on its surface, resulting in coating degradation. In contrast, ZPC@Zn retained a stable and intact protective layer (Figure S44). At 10 C, bare Zn exhibited capacity decay, whereas ZPC@Zn maintained approximately 89.85% of its initial capacity over 5,000 cycles with a CE of 97.25% (Figure 6f; Table S5). Additionally, at a high current density of 20 C, the bare Zn anode experienced a short circuit (70.02%@931st), whereas the ZPC@Zn anode demonstrated long‐term cycling stability (71.62%@2,700th) (Figure S45). The ZPC@Zn maintained stable performance, confirming the ability of the ZPC coating to chemically isolate I3 − ions and enhance anode durability at a low N/P ratio of 5.5 (Figure S46).

To further examine the feasibility of practical application, a pouch cell measuring 3 cm x 4 cm was fabricated (Figure S47). When cycled at a current density of 1 A g−1, the ZPC@Zn pouch cell exhibited a capacity retention of 94.30% over 1000 cycles (Figure 6g). In addition, despite the use of a large‐area iodine cathode, the ZPC@Zn remained stable after 24 h of rest (Figure S48). Based on this result, we further verified the applicability of ZPC by assembling a full cell with a Z0.25V2O5 (ZVO) cathode (Figure S49) [88, 89]. The ZPC@Zn‖ZVO cell showed a high capacity of 335.30 mAh g−1 at 0.1 A g−1 and exhibited excellent cycling stability at 5 A g−1 (Figure S50). Overall, these results clearly demonstrate that the ZPC layer not only effectively suppresses corrosion but also represents a practical strategy for advancing AZIBs.

Conclusion

In summary, a zincophilic and high surface area ZPC interfacial layer was developed to stabilize Zn anodes by selectively adsorbing Zn2+ while suppressing inert to parasitic side reactions. The introduction of zincophilic functional groups promotes efficient Zn2+ desolvation, enhances ion transport kinetics, and reinforces the EDL, effectively suppressing Zn dendrite formation. Benefiting from these structural and chemical features, the ZPC@Zn electrode demonstrated improved electrochemical performance, including an average CE exceeding 99.4%, excellent rate capability (up to 16 mA cm−2), and extended cycling stability of over 3,600 h in symmetric cells. ZPC@Zn‖I2 exhibited long‐term cyclability exceeding 5,000 cycles at 10 C. Furthermore, the ZPC layer facilitates Zn deposition along the (002) crystal plane, leveraging its high surface area and active functional groups to achieve uniform nucleation. Collectively, this study provides insight into constructing protective layers that enhance Zn anode stability and high‐performance AZIBs by fabricating polymer composites from commercially available materials.
