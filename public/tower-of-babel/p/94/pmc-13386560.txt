Development and validation of a risk prediction model for functional constipation in school-aged children
Authors: Dan Cai, Haiyan Lei, Wen Zheng, Tian Xu, Chengyu Zhou, Qionghong Wang, Muchi Wang, Huan Li, Jun Yang, Hongxi Guo, Jingjing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify independent risk factors for functional constipation (FC) in school-aged children, and to develop and internally validate a nomogram-based risk prediction model for early screening of high-risk populations.MethodsIn this single-center, retrospective, cross-sectional case-control study, we enrolled 467 school-aged children who presented to the Constipation Clinic or Department of Child Health Care at Wuhan Children’s Hospital between January 2024 and May 2025. Participants were randomly allocated into a derivation cohort (n = 326) and an internal validation cohort (n = 141) in a 7:3 ratio. Children in the derivation cohort were classified as FC or healthy controls based on Rome IV criteria. Independent risk factors were identified through multivariable logistic regression. A nomogram prediction model was then developed using the R software based on the results of the multivariable analysis. Model discrimination was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), and calibration was assessed with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. For internal validation, the predictive performance of the established nomogram was assessed on an independent validation cohort, which comprised 141 school-aged children (68 with FC and 73 healthy controls). This assessment was based on a receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis, with performance quantified by the area under the curve (AUC).ResultsMultivariable analysis identified the following independent risk factors for FC in school-aged children: shorter duration of exclusive breastfeeding, absence of any toilet training, child’s concern over lack of privacy during defecation, complete unwillingness to defecate at school, frequent complaints of school-related stress, and suboptimal daily parent-child interaction time (all P < 0.05). The nomogram demonstrated excellent discrimination in the derivation cohort (AUC 0.88, 95% CI 0.85–0.92; sensitivity 74%, specificity 86%) with good calibration. Performance remained robust in the validation cohort (AUC 0.84, 95% CI 0.78–0.90; sensitivity 70%, specificity 82%).ConclusionsIncorporating key modifiable factors from type of infant feeding, toilet training, school environment, psychological adaptation, and parent-child interaction, the developed nomogram provides a simple, intuitive, and feasible clinical tool. It demonstrates good discriminative ability and predictive accuracy, offering a practical and actionable guide for the early identification of school-aged children at high risk of FC and for informing targeted preventive interventions.

Introduction

Functional constipation (FC) ranks among the most prevalent functional gastrointestinal disorders in children and adolescents. Symptoms are often chronic or recurrent, characterized by infrequent defecation (typically fewer than three times per week), passage of hard stools, prolonged or incomplete evacuation, and painful defecation [1]. The reported prevalence of FC in pediatric populations varies widely across regions, ranging from 0.3% to 32%, with a global incidence estimated at approximately 3%. In China, cross-sectional studies indicate a childhood prevalence of 7.5%, which has shown a consistent upward trend in recent years [2, 3]. Neglecting childhood constipation and delaying intervention can exacerbate symptoms, impair children’s self-esteem and quality of life, and increase the risk of later psychological symptoms and mental disorders, thereby imposing substantial economic and emotional burdens on families [4, 5]. The school-age period represents a critical stage of rapid physical and psychological development, during which children are particularly susceptible to specific health issues due to physiological, psychological, and environmental influences. Notably, FC in school-aged children tends to be more severe and persistent, requiring extended treatment and highlighting an urgent need for greater clinical attention from pediatric healthcare providers [6]. Consequently, identifying risk factors and enabling early intervention have remained central research priorities in the field of FC. As a nomogram can incorporate multiple key factors into a single predictive model and visually display the probability of a specific outcome, it serves as an effective tool for individualized risk assessment in various diseases [7]. However, to our knowledge, effective methods for predicting the individual risk of developing FC in school-aged children have been rarely reported in the literature, both domestically and internationally [8]. Dietary patterns, toilet training practices, socio-environmental factors, and family history are recognized as established contributors to childhood constipation [8, 9]. Conversely, specific nutrients and food types have been identified as protective factors against constipation in preschool and school-aged children, as well as in adolescents [6, 10]. This study aims to identify independent risk factors for FC in school-aged children across multiple domains including feeding practices, toilet training, school environment, psychological adaptability, and parent-child interaction. Based on these factors, we will develop and internally validate a nomogram-based risk prediction model, to assist the early clinical screening of children at high risk of FC and provide a practical reference for individualized prevention and management of this condition.

Materials and methods

Research objects

This is a retrospective, cross-sectional case-control study conducted at a single center. A total of 467 school-aged children who were seen at the Constipation Clinic or the Child Health Care Department of Wuhan Children’s Hospital between January 2024 and May 2025 were enrolled. Among them, 235 children diagnosed with FC in the Constipation Clinic constituted the FC case group. The diagnosis of FC was strictly based on the Rome IV criteria [1]. Inclusion criteria for the FC group were: (1) meeting the Rome IV diagnostic criteria for FC; (2) age between 6 and 12 years; and (3) provision of informed consent by a parent or legal guardian. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) constipation with an identifiable organic cause (e.g., Hirschsprung’s disease); (2) presence of other severe comorbidities (e.g., psychiatric or autoimmune disorders) that could confound the study; and (3) inability to cooperate or presence of significant cognitive impairment. Following the methodology of a prior study [11], a healthy control (HC) group was established by matching for age. Using a 1:1 case-to-control ratio, 232 children who underwent routine health examinations in the Child Health Care Department of our hospital during the same period were selected as normal controls. Within each cohort, the case‑control ratio was approximately 1:1 (164 FC vs. 162 HC in derivation; 68 FC vs. 73 HC in validation). The 1:1 matching was chosen to maximize statistical power. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Children’s Hospital (Approval No. 2025R163-E01).

Questionnaire

To minimize potential inaccuracies in children’s self-reported information, parents or guardians were designated as the primary respondents for the survey. Each participating parent or guardian was provided with a self-administered questionnaire titled “Functional Constipation Survey for School-Aged Childre”. The questionnaire was developed by our research team based on literature review and clinical expertise; it was pilot-tested for clarity but not formally validated. The questionnaire was distributed and collected face-to-face in the Constipation Clinic and the Child Health Care Department by nurses who had undergone uniform training. Prior to completion, the purpose and significance of the study were explained in detail. All questionnaires were filled out by parents on-site and collected immediately. Each returned questionnaire was reviewed individually, and those that did not meet the inclusion criteria or had missing essential data were excluded. After the survey, all completed questionnaires were securely stored by a designated research staff member. The questionnaire comprised the following sections: ① Basic Demographic and Anthropometric Data: sex, weight, height, and BMI category;② Early Growth and Development: duration of breastfeeding and age at initiation of toilet training༛③Defecation‑Related Characteristics: frequency and duration of defecation, perceived straining, pain during defecation, and the number of episodes with such symptoms༛④ Dietary and Lifestyle Habits: regularity of breakfast consumption, daily water intake, fruit and vegetable consumption, frequency of whole‑grain and milk intake, and regularity of sleep–wake schedule༛⑤ School‑Related Factors: child’s report on whether break time is sufficient for toileting, evaluation of school toilet hygiene and its perceived impact on defecation, concerns about lack of privacy when using school toilets, discomfort with squat toilets at school, and complaints about school‑related stress༛⑥ Parent–Child Interaction: quality of parent–child relationship, daily duration of parent–child interaction, and parental response when the child reports abnormal defecation or withholding behavior༛⑦ Family Health History: family history of constipation, history of food allergies, and the child’s general health status༛⑧ Parental Knowledge of Constipation: parental recognition of constipation‑related symptoms and their perception of the impact of constipation on the child’s health and daily life. These domains were chosen because they represent modifiable factors during the critical school-age period, have shown associations with gastrointestinal function in previous studies, and are amenable to intervention in clinical and school settings [8, 9]. Height and weight were measured by trained nurses during the clinic visit. For school-related questions, children were asked directly whenever possible; if the child was unable to answer, parents provided proxy responses – this potential bias is acknowledged in the limitations.

Diagnostic criteria for FC

The diagnosis of FC was based on the Rome IV criteria [1], which require, for at least one month in a child > 4 years of age, two or more of the following: (1) two or fewer defecations in the toilet per week; (2) at least one episode of fecal incontinence per week; (3) history of retentive posturing or excessive volitional stool retention; (4) history of painful or hard bowel movements; (5) presence of a large fecal mass in the rectum; and (6) history of large-diameter stools that may obstruct the toilet. Additionally, after appropriate medical evaluation, the symptoms cannot be fully explained by another medical condition (e.g., irritable bowel syndrome).

Analytical methods

Data were entered independently by two researchers using Microsoft Excel. Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS (version 21.0) and R (version 4.3.3). Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s) and were compared between groups using independent-sample t‑tests. Categorical data are expressed as number (percentage) and were compared using the chi‑square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent risk factors for FC in school‑aged children. Variable selection was performed using stepwise forward logistic regression with AIC as the criterion. Subsequently, the final regression model was translated into a visual nomogram using R software (version 4.3.3) to quantify individual risk. The nomogram’s performance was evaluated through calibration curves and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‑of‑fit test. Its discriminative ability was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC‑ROC). To account for potential overfitting, we performed internal validation using 200 bootstrap resamples. The optimism‑corrected AUC was calculated, and the calibration curve was adjusted for optimism. All statistical tests were two‑sided, and a p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic information of the enrolled patients

A total of 467 school‑aged children were enrolled in this study. Among them, 235 were diagnosed with FC and 232 served as HC without constipation. A study flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1, showing that 51 questionnaires were excluded due to incomplete data or failure to meet inclusion criteria. The mean age was 8.22 ± 1.61 years in the FC group and 8.37 ± 1.72 years in the HC group, both ranging from 6 to 12 years. For model development, 326 participants (164 FC and 162 HC) were assigned to the derivation cohort. The 7:3 split was performed using simple random assignment with a fixed seed (123) in R software to ensure reproducibility. No statistically significant differences were observed in baseline clinical characteristics between the derivation and validation cohorts (all P > 0.05) (Table 1). The observed age difference in the derivation cohort (P = 0.002) was due to random sampling variability and was adjusted for in all regression models.

Multivariable analysis of FC in school-aged children

A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent risk factors for FC. The presence of FC served as the binary dependent variable (0 = no, 1 = yes). All demographic characteristics and variables significant in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05) were entered as candidate independent variables. The analysis revealed five independent risk factors (all P < 0.05): a shorter duration of breastfeeding, delayed initiation of toilet training, the child’s concern over lack of privacy in school toilets, the child’s complaints about school-related stress, and suboptimal daily parent-child interaction time(Table 3).

Development of a risk prediction model for FC in school-aged children

Based on the identified risk factors for FC in school-aged children, a nomogram was developed using R software to provide an individualized risk prediction (Fig. 2). The nomogram assigns points to each risk factor as follows: a duration of breastfeeding < 6 months contributes 62.5 points; absence of toilet training contributes 95 points; the child being “very concerned” and completely unwilling to defecate at school contributes 75 points; frequent complaints about school-related stress contribute 65 points; and daily parent–child interaction time < 30 min contributes 100 points. The total points are calculated by summing the individual scores. This total is then aligned with the probability axis at the bottom of the nomogram to obtain the corresponding predicted risk of FC. For example, a child with breastfeeding < 6 months (62.5 points), no toilet training (95 points), very concerned about privacy (75 points), frequent school stress (65 points), and parent‑child interaction < 30 min (100 points) has a total of 397.5 points, corresponding to a predicted FC risk > 90% .

Validation and evaluation of the risk prediction model for FC in school-aged children

In the derivation cohort, the nomogram demonstrated excellent discriminative ability, with AUC of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.85–0.92). At the optimal cutoff, sensitivity was 74% and specificity was 86% (Fig. 3). The calibration curve closely approximated the ideal 45-degree line, indicating good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes and supporting the model’s predictive accuracy (Fig. 4). When applied to the independent validation cohort, the model maintained strong predictive performance, yielding an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.78–0.90), with a sensitivity of 70% and a specificity of 82% (Fig. 5). These results confirm the model’s robust predictive value and suggest its potential clinical utility.

Discussion

The pathogenesis of FC in children is multifactorial and not fully elucidated. Current research indicates that its development is influenced by a range of interconnected factors, including dietary habits, physical activity levels, genetic predisposition, gut microbiota composition, and psychosocial and behavioral factors [1].

Analysis of influencing factors for FC in school-aged children

Type of infant feeding

Shorter duration of exclusive breastfeeding emerged as an independent risk factor for FC in our cohort. The nomogram assigned considerable weight to this factor, with a duration of less than 6 months contributing 62.5 points and a duration of 6–12 months contributing 55 points. This finding aligns with existing literature. A study by Sangalli [12] reported a decline in constipation prevalence following improvements in infant feeding practices, including prolonged breastfeeding. Compared with other feeding modes, exclusive breastfeeding promotes stool frequency and increases fecal bulk. This effect has been attributed to human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs), the third most abundant component of human milk, which function as natural prebiotics by selectively promoting the growth of beneficial gut bacteria, particularly Bifidobacterium longum, during the critical window of neonatal intestinal colonization. Importantly, this early-life microbial programming is not transient; the gut microbiota composition established in infancy can exert sustained effects on intestinal motility, immune function, and visceral sensitivity through the gut-brain axis, potentially extending into school age. Emerging evidence from the gut-brain axis literature supports that early-life nutritional exposures can produce long-lasting “programming” effects on gastrointestinal function. Moreover, a large nationwide birth cohort study (n = 70,078) demonstrated that a breastfeeding duration of ≥ 7 months was inversely associated with functional constipation at 3 years of age (adjusted OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.65–0.88), further supporting the long-term protective effect of breastfeeding on constipation [13]. In contrast, cow’s milk contains higher levels of calcium and casein and relatively lower amounts of sugars and starches, factors that may predispose to constipation. De Oliveira [14] further demonstrated that infants fed formula before 6 months of age had a significantly higher risk of developing FC compared with breastfed infants. Therefore, breastfeeding should be encouraged and, where possible, sustained for 6–12 months to help prevent FC in early childhood. The high proportion of early milk cessation observed in our cohort may reflect local cultural practices and should be interpreted with caution.

Toilet training

Delayed initiation of toilet training was identified as a significant risk factor for FC in school-aged children. The nomogram quantified its impact, with initiation after 2 years of age contributing 47.5 points and the complete absence of training contributing 95 points. Human defecation evolves through distinct developmental stages: reflexive in the neonatal period, voluntary control in infancy and toddlerhood, and finally, regulated, habitual defecation in the school-aged years. The successful transition from reflexive to voluntary and ultimately to habitual bowel control is not solely dependent on dietary maturation but critically relies on parental guidance through timely and structured toilet training to establish a consistent defecation reflex. Apart from organic, endocrine, and metabolic causes, current research emphasizes the role of diet, toilet training, and bowel habits in pediatric constipation [14, 15]. For instance, Fedele [16] demonstrated that education and reward-based toilet training form the cornerstone of non-pharmacological recovery in constipated children. Furthermore, the Chinese Guidelines for Urinary and Bowel Training and the Rational Use of Diapers in Infants and Young Children (2025 Edition) recommend initiating basic toileting practices around 6–9 months of age and formal toilet training around 1 year [17]. Therefore, healthcare providers should counsel parents to initiate toilet training early, ideally between 1 and 2 years of age, tailoring the approach to the child’s temperament and comprehension. Training at fixed times daily, such as after meals, can leverage the gastrocolic reflex to facilitate habit formation, thereby supporting the primary prevention of FC in early childhood.

School environment

Our study identifies a child’s expressed concern over lack of privacy and unwillingness to defecate at school as a potent, independent psychosocial risk factor for FC. The nomogram assigned a considerable weight of 75 points to strong concern and complete avoidance, and 35 points to moderate concern and attempted avoidance. Currently, school restrooms often lack adequate privacy safeguards. Common issues include simple partitions without doors, doors that are too short or have wide gaps, and a lack of visual barriers at the entrance, allowing direct visibility into stalls. During peak hours, crowding and waiting can exacerbate the problem. Fearing being rushed, watched, or having a door suddenly opened, a child’s natural physiological response is to contract the pelvic floor muscles—a direct antagonist to the relaxation required for defecation. This suppression of the urge leads to prolonged colonic transit, excessive water absorption from stool, and the passage of hard, painful stools, thereby initiating a vicious cycle of withholding, constipation, and defecation anxiety. Psychologically, school-aged children are in a critical period of developing self-esteem and autonomy, with a heightened need for privacy. Exposure or fear of exposure during such a vulnerable act can evoke profound feelings of shame and social anxiety. This not only inhibits the defecation reflex but also conditions a strong behavioral aversion to using school toilets, fostering a pattern of habitual stool withholding. This risk is particularly pronounced in introverted or less communicative children, who may internalize their discomfort and are less likely to seek help from teachers or parents, allowing the problem to persist undetected. Our findings are consistent with the work of Naenen [18], which identified irregular bowel habits, frequent stool withholding, and fear of defecation as key contributors to chronic childhood constipation. Therefore, healthcare providers should encourage parents to proactively discuss their child’s toileting experience at school and advocate for “privacy-friendly” restroom improvements through school-home collaboration. The primary intervention should focus not merely on laxative use but on helping the child rebuild a sense of safety and control over the toileting process. Parental understanding, support, and positive guidance are essential for improving school-based toileting behavior, establishing healthy bowel habits, and preventing FC.

Psychological adaptation

Self-reported, frequent school-related stress was identified in our model as a significant psychosocial risk factor for FC, contributing 75 points in the nomogram. This finding underscores the critical link between psychological well-being and gastrointestinal function in school-aged children. The school-age period represents a pivotal developmental window characterized by rapid physiological, cognitive, and emotional maturation. In contemporary society, academic performance has become a predominant source of pressure, often intensified by parental expectations and competitive educational environments. This sustained psychological stress can manifest as chronic anxiety or tension. The pathophysiological connection is mediated by the brain-gut axis. Prolonged or intense negative emotional states activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and dysregulate the autonomic nervous system (ANS).The colon is richly innervated by the ANS. A stress-induced shift toward sympathetic dominance can inhibit colonic motility and disrupt the coordinated relaxation of the pelvic floor and anal sphincter muscles necessary for normal defecation, thereby precipitating or exacerbating constipation. Furthermore, children with poorer social adaptability or interpersonal skills may experience heightened feelings of isolation, frustration, or dysphoria, which are also established risk factors for functional gastrointestinal disorders, as supported by prior research [19]. Therefore, healthcare providers should guide parents to pay closer attention to their child’s emotional well-being. Enhancing children’s mental health and resilience to stress may help reduce the incidence of FC in this vulnerable population.

Parent-child interaction

Our analysis reveals a nuanced, non-linear relationship between daily parent-child interaction time and the risk of FC, identifying both insufficient and excessive interaction as significant risk factors. The nomogram assigned the highest weight among all factors (100 points) to an interaction time of less than 30 min per day, while an interaction time exceeding 120 min per day was also weighted substantially (90 points). Parent-child interaction constitutes the primary and most formative social relationship for a child, exerting a profound influence on psychological and physiological development. Our findings corroborate the work of Yamada [20], who established a clear association between parent-child dynamics and FC, noting that regular family meals and positive interactions are protective, whereas infrequent interaction is a risk factor. This link is likely mediated through the quality of the parent-child relationship and the resulting family emotional climate, which are foundational to a child’s overall well-being and stress resilience [21]. The mechanisms underlying this U-shaped risk curve are distinct for each extreme: Interaction Deficit (< 30 min/day): Inadequate interaction time severely limits parental awareness of the child’s daily experiences, including school-related anxieties and toileting concerns. This can foster a sense of neglect in the child, making them less likely to confide in parents about sensitive issues like defecation difficulties. Consequently, problems remain unrecognized and unaddressed, allowing stool withholding and associated anxiety to become entrenched. Interaction Excess (> 120 min/day): Conversely, an overly intensive focus, particularly if it involves persistent questioning about bodily functions or school performance, can be perceived as intrusive and pressuring by the child. This may provoke resistance, increase anxiety surrounding toileting, and paradoxically reinforce the child’s reluctance to discuss or acknowledge related problems, thereby exacerbating the condition. Therefore, maintaining daily parent–child interaction within an optimal range of 60 to 120 min is recommended. This duration ensures meaningful communication without creating psychological pressure, enabling parents to accurately monitor their child’s emotional state and effectively encourage them to express toileting concerns, which is crucial for the prevention of FC.

Clinical applicability

The nomogram can be easily implemented in pediatric outpatient clinics or school health screenings. Clinicians can input the five factors (breastfeeding duration, toilet training, privacy concern, school stress, parent‑child interaction time) to obtain an individual’s FC risk probability, facilitating early referral and targeted behavioural interventions. Because the model was developed using cross‑sectionally collected data, it should be considered a screening tool to identify children at high risk of current FC, rather than a true prospective predictor.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the inclusion of psychosocial and environmental factors, rigorous model development with bootstrap validation, and the provision of an intuitive nomogram. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference and the model’s “predictive” ability should be interpreted as screening for current FC. Second, this was a single‑center study, which may limit generalizability. Third, recall bias is possible for early feeding practices; parents of constipated children may over‑report shorter breastfeeding duration. Fourth, school‑related factors were partly reported by parents as proxies, which may not perfectly reflect the child’s actual experience. Fifth, we did not perform external validation in an independent cohort. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to confirm our findings and assess the model’s predictive performance over time.

Conclusion

This study developed and internally validated a novel risk prediction nomogram for FC in school-aged children, incorporating five modifiable factors across developmental, environmental, and psychosocial domains: duration of exclusive breastfeeding, timing of toilet training initiation, child’s concern over school toilet privacy, self-reported school-related stress, and daily parent-child interaction time. The model demonstrated robust performance. It exhibited excellent discriminatory ability, with a high AUC in both the derivation and validation cohorts. The calibration plot showed strong agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, which was confirmed by a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, indicating the model is well-calibrated across the spectrum of risk. This nomogram provides clinicians with an intuitive tool to visually assess the individual contribution of each factor to FC risk, facilitating the identification of high-risk children. It offers a straightforward and practical reference for designing targeted interventions in this population. Future studies should expand the sample size and conduct multicenter prospective validations to further refine the model and enhance its clinical applicability.
