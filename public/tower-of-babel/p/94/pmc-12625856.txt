Amino acid insufficiency impairs hepatic vitamin A mobilization in mice
Authors: Chintan T. Bhavsar, Youn-Kyung Kim, Flavio C. Rodriguez-Polanco, Brian A. Zalma, Esther M. Lopez, Saad A. Farooq, Maria Ibrahim, Eileen White, Tracy G. Anthony, Loredana Quadro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceVitamin A is an essential fat-soluble micronutrient that controls critical biological functions. Many organs depend on circulating vitamin A in the form of retinol bound to retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4) secreted in the bloodstream from the liver. Our data demonstrate that amino acid (AA) insufficiency prevents hepatic retinol mobilization via RBP4 without affecting liver vitamin A stores. We further provide evidence of AA sensing as a control point regulating vitamin A availability to the periphery. Together, these results point to additional layers of regulation beyond vitamin A status in controlling its distribution from the liver to other tissues.

Vitamin A is an essential fat-soluble micronutrient known for its roles in vision, cell differentiation, growth, immunity, and metabolism. Insufficient vitamin A intake and/or tissue utilization can reduce lifespan and life quality, especially in children (1–7). The liver is the major body reserve of retinoids (vitamin A and its derivatives), with retinyl esters stored primarily in hepatic stellate cells (8). In times of need, upon retinyl ester hydrolysis, retinol (vitamin A alcohol, ROH) is transferred from hepatic stellate cells to hepatocytes which synthesize retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4 or RBP), the sole specific carrier of vitamin A in the bloodstream (9). ROH–RBP4 is secreted from the hepatocyte into the circulation where it exists in a complex with transthyretin (TTR) (10) to prevent renal filtration of RBP4 (11). RBP4 and TTR are both synthesized by the hepatocyte (9). Once bound to ROH, forming holo-RBP4, it is secreted into plasma. Many health conditions, such as infection, inflammation, liver injury, and protein malnutrition, report reduced circulating ROH–RBP4–TTR and reduced liver RBP4 synthesis is the presumed cause (12–14). On the other hand, if ROH is not available to bind apo-RBP4 due to overt vitamin A deficiency, then it cannot be secreted and instead accumulates in the hepatocyte, resulting in reduced ROH–RBP4 levels in the circulation. To date, vitamin A status is the only factor known to regulate hepatic secretion of vitamin A (15–17).

We previously identified a protective role for vitamin A in patients receiving the antileukemic agent, asparaginase (ASNase) (18). In that report, ASNase reduced serum and liver ROH levels in mice by an unknown mechanism (18). ASNase induces amino acid (AA) insufficiency in the liver by depleting serum asparagine and glutamine. AA insufficiency induced by drugs or dietary deprivation activates the integrated stress response (ISR) via phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 on its alpha subunit (eIF2α) by the general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2) protein kinase (19–21). GCN2 is one in a family of four eIF2α kinases that include heme regulated inhibitor (HRI), protein kinase R (PKR), and PKR-like ER resident kinase (PERK) (21). ISR activation by GCN2 directs the ribosome to preferentially translate select mRNAs while limiting bulk protein synthesis. A few selectively synthesized proteins, such as activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), execute broad changes to the transcriptome in an effort to regain homeostasis (21). To what extent the ISR is involved in regulating circulating ROH–RBP4–TTR complex is unstudied.

Protein deficiency per se does not deplete liver reserves of vitamin A (22), and this led us to ask whether ASNase hinders mobilization of ROH–RBP4–TTR independent of nutritional vitamin A status and whether this regulation extends to AA insufficiency more broadly. To this end, we compared three AA insufficiency types then focused our efforts using ASNase because of its clinical relevance as a chemotherapy agent and its potential for translational applications beyond oncology. We hypothesized that AA insufficiency regulates hepatic mobilization of vitamin A independent of vitamin A status. In the liver, we found that during AA insufficiency RBP4 accumulated despite no increase in Rbp4 mRNA and reduced RBP4 protein synthesis. In contrast, liver TTR protein abundance was reduced by AA insufficiency via lower Ttr transcript levels. Reduced serum levels of ROH by ASNase corresponded with perturbed retinoid levels in the periphery of the body in an organ-specific manner. To test ISR involvement in the mechanism of effect, we administered ASNase to global Gcn2 knockout mice (Gcn2−/−) and pretreated wild-type (WT) primary hepatocytes with a chemical inhibitor of GCN2 kinase activity before ASNase exposure. Both models prevented RBP4 protein accumulation and TTR protein reduction, while mobilization of both into the circulation or media remained low. Interestingly, hepatocyte-specific deletion of the ISR gene target autophagy-related 7 (Atg7), which functions in autophagosome formation and secretion, fully rescued reductions in circulating ROH, RBP4, and TTR, as well as the changes in abundance of hepatic RBP4 and TTR in ASNase-exposed mice. These data suggest the involvement of ATG7-containing compartments in the mobilization of RBP4 and TTR. We conclude that AA insufficiency serves as an independent regulatory input in the control of vitamin A mobilization, affecting whole body retinoid metabolism.

Results

ASNase Reduces Circulating ROH–RBP4–TTR Complex and Perturbs Hepatic RBP4 and TTR Protein Levels.

To examine how ASNase exposure perturbs whole body vitamin A metabolism, we administered to C57BL6/J WT male and female mice a pegylated form of ASNase at a dose that matched previous reports by us and others (ASNase 1.5) (23, 24) or a higher dose (ASNase 3.0) that matched our earlier work using a native form of ASNase (19, 20). A separate group of mice received phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as vehicle control. Mice were killed 7 h or 5 d after a single injection of PBS, ASNase 1.5, or ASNase 3.0. All mice remained weight stable 7 h after a single injection of drug or vehicle. Mice injected with either dose of ASNase but not PBS lost a significant amount of body weight over 5 d (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and D) regardless of biological sex, agreeing with previous reports (23, 24). To confirm enzymatic depletion of asparagine, we performed reverse phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to measure circulating AAs. As expected (25), asparagine concentrations were significantly reduced by ASNase regardless of time and dose, indicating sustained enzyme activity that was confirmed by increased serum aspartic acid (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). Serum glutamine was also decreased by ASNase 3.0 but only at 7 h (P = 0.051) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Nevertheless, serum glutamic acid concentrations were significantly elevated at both doses and timepoints, suggesting additional, persistent glutaminase activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D), as previously reported (25).

We next measured serum and tissue retinoid concentrations by reverse-phase HPLC (26). In male mice, serum ROH was significantly decreased by ASNase 1.5 at 7 h (Fig. 1A) and further reduced to ~37% of PBS mice by ASNase 3.0. Both doses of ASNase significantly reduced serum ROH relative to PBS at 5 d. To measure relative abundance of RBP4 and TTR in the serum, we then conducted immunoblotting and showed that RBP4 and TTR were both substantially reduced relative to PBS at 5 d, regardless of ASNase dose (Fig. 1 B and C). We also observed that the ratio of apo- vs. holo-RBP4 remained unchanged upon ASNase (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). In the liver, ROH concentrations remained unchanged upon ASNase, whereas levels of retinyl esters trended upward (Fig. 1D). However, when expressed relative to whole organ weight, ASNase did not change total liver retinoid content (SI Appendix, Figs. S1C and S4A). Interestingly, while liver TTR protein abundances were either unchanged or decreased by ASNase (P = 0.055 in ASNase 1.5 vs. PBS), both ASNase doses significantly increased hepatic RBP4 protein levels (Fig. 1 E and F).

In female mice, serum ROH was significantly reduced by ASNase 3.0 at 7 h, and ASNase 1.5 and 3.0 at 5 d relative to PBS (Fig. 1G). Like in the males, serum RBP4 and TTR protein levels were significantly reduced by ASNase at 5 d regardless of dose (Fig. 1 H and I). The ASNase 3.0 dose increased retinyl esters at 7 h and significantly reduced liver ROH levels at 5 d (Fig. 1J). These differences persisted when total retinoid content was assessed (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Regardless of dose, ASNase reduced liver TTR protein and increased liver RBP4 protein at 5 d relative to PBS (Fig. 1 K and L).

Taken together, ASNase administration, at both timepoints and doses, decreased circulating ROH–RBP4–TTR complex levels with a concomitant reduction in liver TTR and a paradoxical increase in hepatic RBP4 protein in both male and female mice. Hepatic retinoid levels remained in a range reflecting nutritional sufficiency but were altered in a sex- and time-dependent manner and in the female mice confirmed the reduction in hepatic ROH we reported earlier (18).

ASNase Perturbs mRNA Levels of Specific Liver Retinoid–Related Genes.

We next asked to what extent ASNase may alter hepatic retinoid metabolism. Vitamin A–dependent transcript abundances reflect retinoid fluxes within tissues (27). So, we measured messanger RNA (mRNA) abundances of key genes in retinoid metabolism via real-time qPCR (RT-qPCR) in the livers of WT male and female mice administered ASNase or PBS for 5 d. In male mice, ASNase significantly decreased retinol dehydrogenase 10 (Rdh10) which controls ROH oxidation (28), but we did not detect changes in the expression of dehydrogenase/reductase 3 (Dhrs3), an enzyme responsible for retinaldehyde reduction to ROH (29, 30), or retinaldehyde dehydrogenase (Raldh1), which converts retinaldehyde to retinoic acid (31), or lecithin retinol acyltransferase (Lrat), the enzyme which synthesizes retinyl esters in the liver (32–34) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Instead, relative to PBS, ASNase significantly reduced the mRNA abundance of Cyp26a1, a canonical retinoic acid target gene, which catabolizes retinoic acid (35–37) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). In female mice, Rdh10, Dhrs3, Raldh1, and Lrat mRNA levels did not significantly change in response to ASNase exposure (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). In female mice given ASNase 1.5 but not ASNase 3.0, Cyp26a1 mRNA was significantly downregulated relative to PBS (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). We also measured gene expression of retinol-binding protein receptor 2 (Rbpr2), a transmembrane receptor thought to facilitate hepatic ROH–RBP4 reuptake (38). Rbpr2 mRNA levels in the 5 d ASNase 3.0 male group were significantly reduced relative to PBS controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), whereas Rbpr2 mRNA in female mice was unaltered by ASNase exposure (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Overall, these data suggest that ASNase may perturb retinoic acid homeostasis in the liver.

ASNase Differentially Perturbs Retinoid Levels in Extrahepatic Tissues during VAD Feeding.

We next asked to what extent decreased serum ROH–RBP4–TTR levels during ASNase exposure reduced retinoid content in extrahepatic tissues. Both hepatic mobilization of vitamin A via the ROH–RBP4–TTR complex and postprandial vitamin A via chylomicrons maintain adequate supply of the vitamin to the peripheral tissues, and these two pathways can compensate for each other (8, 39, 40). Therefore, to exclude the contribution of postprandial vitamin A delivery to extrahepatic tissues, we placed a cohort of WT male mice on a vitamin A–deficient diet (VAD) starting 1 wk prior to a 5 d ASNase 1.5 exposure. Importantly, this acute dietary adjustment does not cause vitamin A deficiency (41, 42). In these mice, ASNase 1.5 decreased serum ROH alongside reduced serum RBP4 and TTR levels (Fig. 2 A and B). ASNase 1.5 lowered liver ROH concentrations while retinyl ester concentrations increased (Fig. 2C). While hepatic total retinyl ester levels (i.e., adjusted to organ weight) did not increase in these ASNase-exposed mice, total liver retinol was significantly reduced (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C), a finding that was not apparent in ASNase-exposed male mice fed a vitamin A–sufficient chow diet (Fig. 1D) but that confirmed our earlier report (18). Furthermore, ASNase 1.5 increased hepatic RBP4 protein and decreased TTR protein levels similar to mice fed the vitamin A–sufficient chow diet (Fig. 2D).

A relatively common ASNase-associated adverse metabolic event is pancreatitis (43). Our previous work suggested a correlation between ASNase exposure, the development of pancreatitis, and perturbation of vitamin A homeostasis in this organ (18). In the pancreas, ASNase 1.5 exposure resulted in a significant reduction in ROH with directional elevations in retinyl ester content (Fig. 2E). We next examined retinoids in the eye, an organ specifically dependent on circulating ROH–RBP4 for its supply of vitamin A to maintain the visual cycle (39, 44). ASNase caused a ~27% reduction in eye retinyl ester levels with no changes in ROH levels (Fig. 2F). A survey of additional peripheral organs revealed that ASNase did not alter total retinoid levels in the heart or the lung (Fig. 2 G and H). However, ASNase significantly increased gonadal white adipose tissue (gonWAT) and inguinal white adipose tissue (ingWAT) retinoid concentration (Fig. 2 I and J). This is remarkable because ASNase triggers significant weight loss over 5 d. We previously reported that ASNase-exposed mice lose ~30 to 40% fat mass over 5 d (24). When we account for a 40% fat mass loss, gonWAT retinoids, but not ingWAT retinoids, remain significantly elevated (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). To assess to what extent ASNase exposure may have altered retinoid homeostasis in the gonWAT, we measured mRNA abundances of key genes in retinoid metabolism via RT-qPCR in this organ. This analysis showed that ASNase significantly elevated diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 (Dgat1), which functions in retinol esterification (45). No other genes involved in retinol metabolism were altered (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). We conclude that ASNase-induced perturbations in retinoid levels are tissue specific.

Leucine-Devoid Diet and Low-Protein Diet Feeding Reduce Circulating ROH–RBP4–TTR Complex Levels and Increase Hepatic RBP4.

To discern whether the reduction in circulating ROH–RBP4–TTR complex by ASNase extends to other models of AA insufficiency, we analyzed serum and liver samples from mice fed leucine-devoid (46) or low-protein diets that were both vitamin A sufficient. We first analyzed serum and liver from WT mice maintained on a leucine-devoid diet (LeuD) for 8 d. LeuD feeding reduced serum ROH (Fig. 3A) as well as RBP4 and TTR levels (Fig. 3B). In the liver, ROH concentrations remained unchanged whereas retinyl ester concentrations increased upon LeuD diet feeding (Fig. 3C). Moreover, RBP4 accumulated while TTR had a modest, nonsignificant reduction (Fig. 3D). RNA-sequencing on the liver of these mice showed minimal changes in both Rbp4 and Ttr mRNA levels upon LeuD feeding (Fig. 3 E and F).

Low-protein feeding when severe enough induces AA insufficiency. Mice fed a severely low-protein diet (4% protein by energy) for 4 d showed similar reductions in circulating ROH (Fig. 4A), RBP4, and TTR levels (Fig. 4B). In the liver, ROH and retinyl ester concentrations were significantly elevated upon low-protein diet feeding (Fig. 4C). Similar to the LeuD group, an accumulation of RBP4 protein in the liver was further noted upon low-protein feeding, but hepatic TTR protein was not significantly decreased (Fig. 4D). Total mRNA levels of Rbp4 and Ttr measured by RT-qPCR also were not significantly affected by low-protein feeding (Fig. 4 E and F). These findings show that different dietary forms of AA insufficiency interfere with circulating ROH–RBP4–TTR complex and hepatic RBP4 abundance similar to ASNase.

AA Insufficiency Activates the ISR and Decreases the Translation of RBP4 But Not TTR.

To explore the mechanism by which AA insufficiency impairs systemic ROH–RBP4–TTR transport, we considered regulation of protein synthesis via activation of the GCN2-ISR. To test this, we first measured phosphorylation of the GCN2 protein kinase and its target, eIF2α in livers of WT mice exposed to ASNase and saw expected increases in the phosphorylation of both proteins regardless of dose (Fig. 5 A–C) (24). We then conducted polysome profiling to measure protein synthesis and observed a reduction in the area under the polysomes relative to the monosome peak and free ribosomal subunits, reflecting a reduction in global translation (Fig. 5D). We also measured mRNA abundance of ISR target genes Atf4, Atf5, Atf3, and fibroblast growth factor 21 (Fgf21) and observed increased mRNA abundance of Atf5, Atf3, and Fgf21 at both ASNase doses (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, we saw a reduction in translationally active Rbp4 mRNA with no change in total mRNA levels in the liver of the mice exposed to ASNase (Fig. 5 F and G). These findings cannot explain the observed RBP4 protein accumulation in the liver as shown in Fig. 1 E, F, K, and L. In contrast, hepatic Ttr showed no reduction in translationally active mRNA and a significant decrease in total mRNA levels measured by RT-qPCR (Fig. 5 H and I), suggesting reductions at the transcript level directed the decline in hepatic TTR protein abundance.

Loss of GCN2 Activity Alleviates Liver RBP4 Accumulation In Vivo and Ex Vivo.

GCN2 is the primary eIF2α kinase that is activated in response to AA insufficiency (19–21, 47, 48). We therefore sought to directly test the contribution of GCN2 activation to the effects on serum and liver retinoid, RBP4, and TTR levels by AA insufficiency. Gcn2−/− female mice were administered a single injection of ASNase 1.5 or PBS and killed 5 d later as described above for the WT mice. Similar to WT mice, Gcn2−/− mice lost significant body weight (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A) as previously reported (20, 49, 50) Notably, we observed increased hepatic phosphorylation of eIF2α and the eIF2α kinase PERK (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B), which is typically not phosphorylated in WT mice exposed to ASNase (19, 24). Although loss of Gcn2 did not normalize mobilization of ROH–RBP4–TTR into serum (Fig. 6 A and B), liver RBP4 and TTR protein levels in ASNase-exposed mice remained similar to PBS controls and no change in liver retinoid concentration was noted (Fig. 6 C and D). When normalizing to whole organ weight, total liver retinyl esters significantly increased (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). We also assessed the cell-autonomous role of acute GCN2-ISR activation by exposing primary hepatocytes isolated from WT mice to ASNase or PBS for 16 h in the presence or absence of a chemical GCN2 kinase inhibitor [GCN2iB, (51)]. Immunoblot analysis of cell lysates from vehicle-treated cells confirmed increased RBP4 protein upon ASNase exposure (Fig. 6E) and a downward trend in RBP4 and TTR in the cell culture media as compared to PBS-exposed hepatocytes (Fig. 6F). In agreement with our Gcn2−/− results, GCN2iB blocked RBP4 accumulation in ASNase exposed hepatocytes (Fig. 6E) but failed to rescue reduced RBP4 and TTR protein in the cell culture media (Fig. 6F).

Loss of ATG7 during ASNase Rescues ROH–RBP4–TTR Mobilization from the Liver.

We then examined serum and liver samples from mice with a liver (hepatocyte)-specific deletion of autophagy-related 7 (ls-Atg7−/−). ATG7 is an ISR gene target (52) that is upregulated during AA insufficiency. Importantly, we recently reported that ls-Atg7−/− mice displayed reduced phosphorylation of hepatic GCN2 and failed to upregulate several hepatic ISR gene targets during ASNase (24). ATG7 is essential for formation of autophagosomes and also plays less understood roles in regulating a noncanonical form of secretion (53, 54). Male ls-Atg7−/− mice were administered a single injection of ASNase 1.5 or PBS vehicle and killed 5 d later, as described above. Serum ROH measured by HPLC (Fig. 7A) as well as serum RBP4 and TTR levels assessed by immunoblotting were elevated in ASNase-exposed ls-Atg7−/− mice (Fig. 7B). Moreover, hepatic ROH concentrations were reduced while retinyl esters remained unchanged (Fig. 7C) and the results remain the same after accounting for organ weight change (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). Liver RBP4 and TTR levels in ASNase-exposed ls-Atg7−/− mice were similar to PBS controls (Fig. 7D). While Rbp4 mRNA levels were unaltered by ASNase exposure, Ttr mRNA levels were modestly reduced. Overall, these data show that loss of Atg7 in the hepatocyte prevents the reduction of circulating ROH, RBP4, and TTR as well as liver RBP4 accumulation and TTR reduction in mice exposed to ASNase.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to determine the effects of AA insufficiency on whole body vitamin A metabolism and identify mechanisms underlying these effects. We showed that AA insufficiency significantly reduced circulating ROH–RBP4–TTR levels in mice. Importantly, this is also evident in humans in the form of protein malnutrition (55) and in acute lymphoblastic leukemia patients administered ASNase (56); both patient populations have reduced plasma RBP4 and TTR concentrations. ASNase also increased hepatic RBP4, without affecting Rbp4 mRNA levels, and decreased both TTR protein and mRNA abundance in the liver of mice. Moreover, feeding mice a LeuD or a low-protein diet recapitulates the reductions in circulating ROH–RBP4–TTR and increase in hepatic RBP4. Overall, our results indicate that hepatic ROH–RBP4 mobilization is highly and specifically responsive to AA insufficiency. From a clinical perspective, unraveling the molecular basis for how AA insufficiency regulates whole body vitamin A homeostasis may improve treatment strategies for patients receiving ASNase (18) and identifies opportunities for influencing vitamin A metabolism outside of dietary vitamin A intake.

Vitamin A status is a well-recognized direct regulator of hepatic secretion of ROH–RBP4. Indeed, apo-RBP4 accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum of the hepatocytes of vitamin A–deficient animals (57–59) implying that secretion of the complex occurs upon binding of ROH to RBP4 (15–17, 60). AA insufficiency induced by drugs or dietary manipulation did not lead to vitamin A deficiency in our experimental animals that displayed abundant hepatic retinoid stores well above defined vitamin A deficiency cutoff of 0.1 µmol/g liver (61). Thus, AA insufficiency regulates hepatic mobilization of the ROH–RBP4–TTR complex independent of vitamin A status.

The mechanism whereby protein malnutrition lowers serum vitamin A was generally ascribed to attenuated liver protein synthesis (14). With the anticipated inhibition of bulk protein synthesis induced by ASNase (25), we observed RBP4 translation to be reduced without any change in total hepatic Rbp4 mRNA levels. Given the increased hepatic RBP4 protein levels, reduced hepatic secretion could explain plasma RBP4 levels during AA insufficiency. However, this effect appears specific to RBP4 because many acute phase and other proteins including the hepatokines FGF21 (25) and GDF15 (24) are substantially increased in the circulation during ASNase. Previous literature suggested that binding to TTR facilitates RBP4 secretion as Ttr knockout mice show a ~60% increase in hepatic RBP4 levels and substantially attenuated, but still detectable serum ROH–RBP4 levels (62). While in mice given ASNase hepatic TTR protein levels were decreased, the reduction is not shared across all our models of AA insufficiency, arguing against decreased hepatic TTR as a driving factor impairing RBP4 secretion during AA insufficiency. Importantly, hepatocyte-specific ablation of the downstream ISR gene target Atg7 rescued the effects of ASNase on serum and liver retinoid, RBP4, and TTR. As ISR activation by ASNase is attenuated in mice lacking hepatic Atg7 (24), these data suggest the involvement of ISR in hepatic vitamin A mobilization during ASNase exposure. ATG7 is required for the formation of autophagosomes which, during macroautophagy, carry engulfed cargo such as damaged organelles to the lysosome for degradation (63). During AA insufficiency, autophagy is increased to help maintain proteostasis (64, 65). More recently, autophagy and autophagy-related proteins have been implicated in regulating canonical and noncanonical protein secretion (54). For instance, insulin secretion was enhanced during glucose tolerance testing in mice with heterozygous deletion of Atg7 in pancreatic beta cells (66). Additional studies are needed to explore intracellular sorting and trafficking more broadly in mediating hepatic mobilization of vitamin A.

Our work explored the direct connection between the GCN2-ISR and the hepatic mobilization of the ROH–RBP4–TTR complex. Even though ASNase robustly activates GCN2-ISR (19) global loss of Gcn2 failed to rescue the defect in ROH–RBP4–TTR mobilization from the liver. However, ASNase activated PERK and increased eIF2α phosphorylation in livers of mice lacking Gcn2. This indicates that additional studies are needed to clarify the redundant or auxiliary ISR inputs involved in regulating ROH–RBP4 mobilization. For example, we note other ISR regulators are activated by stress conditions which are also associated with reductions in circulating ROH–RBP4 in human and animal models, such as iron deficiency (HRI) (67, 68) and viral infection (PKR) (69).

The ROH–RBP4–TTR complex specifically transports vitamin A in the bloodstream to maintain proper supply of vitamin A to most of the peripheral organs (39). We showed that ASNase-induced reduction in circulating vitamin A perturbed the retinoid status of the eye, pancreas, and adipose tissue. The dependence of the eye on the ROH–RBP4 pathway for its supply of vitamin A is well established. Many reports have shown that absence of the ROH–RBP4 complex rather than low serum ROH levels per se is the reason for the visual impairment in human and animal models (62, 70, 71). In agreement with these findings, we showed that ASNase exposure significantly decreased eye retinyl ester levels, which would predict visual defects (72). There is a longstanding association between protein malnutrition and ocular defects; however, the mechanisms behind this connection are unknown (73). Our work provides potential mechanistic insights into how AA insufficiency may impair vision health through inhibition of the hepatic ROH–RBP4–TTR secretion. We also found it interesting that retinoid levels were perturbed in the pancreas of mice administered ASNase. Maintaining retinoid homeostasis, specifically retinoic acid, is crucial for pancreas health and may help mitigate ASNase-induced acute pancreatitis (18, 74, 75). Colvin et al. (76) found retinoic acid signaling significantly increases immediately upon cessation of cerulean treatment, an agent known to induce pancreatic injury, suggesting retinoids to assist in the healing process. Finally, we noted that ASNase substantially increased gonWAT retinoid levels. Perhaps, this could be a protective mechanism to store retinoids during stress conditions or to support WAT function (77). The origin of increased WAT retinoids in mice fed VAD requires further study.

In conclusion, we propose a model wherein AA insufficiency suppresses hepatic mobilization of ROH–RBP4–TTR, leading to perturbations in retinoid homeostasis in specific extrahepatic tissues, without depleting vitamin A stores. This model identifies opportunities for influencing vitamin A status outside of dietary vitamin A intake.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Experimental Design.

Animal protocols were approved by the Rutgers Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee in compliance with the NIH guide for care and use of laboratory animals and in accordance with ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines (78). All mice were housed at the Bartlett animal facility, a 12 h light–dark cycle in a temperature-(23 °C) and humidity-controlled (40 to 60%) facility with free access to food (LabDiet, 5001) and water.

Experiment 1: ASNase administration during vitamin A–sufficient diet feeding.

Male and female C57BL/6J mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). At 15-wk old, mice were single housed on soft bedding for at least 24 h then administered an intraperitoneal (IP) injection of 1.5 international units per gram bodyweight (IU/g/BW) or 3.0 IU/g/BW pegylated (PEG)-ASNase, or equivolume PBS as control and killed 7 h or 5 d later.

Female Gcn2−/− mice (15-wk old) on the C57BL/6J background (20) were IP injected 1.5 IU/g/BW PEG-ASNase and killed 5 d later.

Tissues and serum were from male mice carrying a hepatocyte-specific deletion of Atg7 (ls-Atg7−/−) that were previously described (24).

Experiment 2: ASNnase administration during VAD feeding.

Male C57BL/6J mice (16 to 20 wk old) bred in-house were switched from a chow diet to a purified VAD (Research Diets, D09093004). After 1-wk, mice were injected with PBS as control or 1.5 IU/g/BW PEG-ASNase and killed 5 d later.

Experiment 3: Leucine-devoid diet feeding.

Tissues and serum were from male C57BL/6J mice fed a control or LeuD for 8 d as previously described (46).

Experiment 4: Low-protein diet feeding.

Male C57BL/6J mice (10 to 18 wks old) were switched from a chow diet to either a semipurified control diet (Research Diets D11051801, 20% protein/total kcal) or low-protein diet (Research Diets D11092301, 4% protein/total kcal) for 4 d.

All mice were killed by decapitation, and trunk blood was collected to generate serum. Tissues were rinsed in PBS and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further analyses.

ASNase Activity.

Two brands of pegylated-ASNase were used, Oncaspar® (pegaspargase) and Asparlas® (calaspargase). ASNase activity was determined by the Nesslerization technique as described previously (20).

Reverse-Phase HPLC Analysis of Serum AAs and Retinoids.

Measurements of serum AAs were performed using reverse-phase HPLC (Agilent 1200 HPLC instrument) as previously described (25). The concentrations of retinol and retinyl esters in serum, liver, eyes, lung, heart, and adipose tissues were measured using reverse-phase HPLC (Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC system) as previously described (26).

Polysome Profiling and RNA Isolation from Sucrose Fractions.

Sucrose density gradients were generated as previously described with minor modifications (24, 25) as detailed in the SI Appendix, Text, Extended Materials and Methods. Sucrose gradients were fractionated into 1 mL aliquots. Aliquots containing light (40S, 60S, monosome) and heavy (disome and greater) polysomes were pooled and RNA was extracted, converted to cDNA, and subsequent RT-qPCR was performed as described below. The relative abundance of the heavy fraction was calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method normalized to Actb and expressed as a fold change relative to the light fraction of each sample.

SDS-PAGE and Immunoblotting.

Protein was extracted from liver tissue or cells as previously described (24). For serum protein measurements, serum was diluted with PBS, added to sample buffer, and boiled for 3 min prior to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). To detect apo- and holo-RBP4, unboiled diluted serum samples were electrophoresed using a nondenaturing gel in the absence of SDS. Cell medium was concentrated using ultracentrifugal filters (Millipore Sigma, UFC500308) and then added to sample buffer and boiled prior to SDS-PAGE. Serum proteins were normalized to albumin by ponceau stain. Antibodies are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1.

RNA Sequencing.

Rbp4 and Ttr mRNA expression in mice fed a leucine-devoid diet were derived from a secondary analysis of a published RNA sequencing dataset (46).

RT-qPCR.

RNA was isolated using the Zymo Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, R2052) for liver tissue or RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74804) for adipose tissue. RNA integrity was confirmed by rRNA agarose gel electrophoresis and contamination by the Nanodrop fluorospectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). One microgram of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using Applied Biosystems High-capacity Reverse Transcription cDNA Synthesis Kit (Applied Biosystems, 43-688-14). RT-qPCR was run in biological duplicates (PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix, Applied Biosystems, A25918). Data were quantified using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method and results were expressed as a fold change from the reference group. Actb or Tbp was used as a housekeeping gene. Forward and reverse primers for each gene are listed in SI Appendix, Table S2.

Primary Hepatocyte Isolation and Treatments.

Primary murine hepatocytes from female C57BL6/J mice (18 to 20 wk old) were isolated via a modified protocol of the two-step collagenase perfusion method (79) as detailed in the SI Appendix.

Statistical Analysis.

All analyses and data visualization were done using GraphPad Prism. Unpaired t tests were conducted to compare a single treatment group to the control group. Multiple treatment groups were analyzed using one-factor ANOVA. Datasets were analyzed for main and interaction effects using two-factor ANOVA. Tukey’s test was used to discern multiple comparisons. If data violated normality, as calculated by Shapiro–Wilk test, data were log10 transformed. If data still violated normality, a nonparametric test was used (Kruskal–Wallis followed by Dunn’s multiple corrections test or Mann–Whitney test). If the variance between groups was significantly different, then a Welch’s t test or Welch’s ANOVA was used. A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant. All data are shown as mean ± SD or as box plots showing median, hinges representing the 25th and 75th percentiles, and ends of whiskers representing the lowest and highest values.
