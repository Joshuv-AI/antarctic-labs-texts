Dimorphic regulation of time-restricted feeding effects by hepatocyte Period 1
Authors: Jiameng Sun, Cassandra B. Higgins, Joshua A. Adams, Yiming Zhang, Shannon C. Kelly, Hyo-Jin Kim, Brian J. DeBosch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesObesity is linked to metabolic disorders including type 2 diabetes, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, and cardiovascular disease. Lifestyle interventions, such as time-restricted feeding (TRF), have proven to be effective for long-term weight management. The metabolic effects of TRF are closely associated with circadian clock function in the liver. We previously demonstrated that the circadian gene Period 1 (Per1) mediates responses to acute fasting in both sexes. We therefore hypothesized that hepatocyte Per1 contributes to the long-term adaptations to repeated fasting exposure in the form of TRF, and investigated its role in diet-induced obesity in both sexes.MethodsMale and female mice with or without hepatocyte Per1 (Per1fl/fl and Per1LKO) were subjected to either ad libitum feeding (ALF) or TRF restricted to the active phase (8 h/day).ResultsTRF attenuated Western diet-induced weight gain and peripheral and hepatic lipid accumulation, and improved heat production, metabolic substrate flexibility, and glucose homeostasis in Per1fl/fl and Per1LKO males. In contrast, hepatocyte Per1 was required for TRF-induced improvements in energy expenditure and peripheral and hepatic lipid accumulation in females. Surprisingly, TRF failed to significantly attenuate diet-induced weight gain or glucose and insulin tolerance in females independent of genotype. Transcriptomic data revealed sex-specific transcriptional responses to TRF and to hepatocyte-specific Per1 deletion. Specifically, genes involved in lipid metabolism were differentially regulated when comparing TRF-treated Per1fl/fl and Per1LKO female mice.ConclusionsHepatocyte Per1 mediates the energy, lipid, and glucose homeostatic effects of TRF, and this regulation is almost completely sex-dependent.

Introduction

Obesity is a global challenge, with prevalence rising at an unprecedented pace and affecting more than 40% of the population worldwide [1]. Although several FDA-approved medications are clinically available for obesity treatment, the National Institutes of Health guidelines emphasize that pharmacological approaches cannot replace lifestyle interventions such as physical activity and healthy dietary habits [2]. To establish sustainable prevention of weight gain and promote long-term health benefits, recent studies have identified time-restricted feeding (TRF) as an effective strategy to reduce adiposity in both mice and humans [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Given the alignment between daily activity/rest cycles and feeding/fasting metabolic cycles, TRF has been investigated for its impact on the rhythmic expression of key hepatic metabolic genes. Restricting feeding to the night phase in mice induces the greatest number of rhythmic hepatic transcripts compared with ad libitum or arrhythmic feeding [7]. Among major metabolic regulators, mTOR, ERK1/2, AKT, and CREB display rhythmic phosphorylation changes in response to TRF [7,8].

The 24-hour rhythm that governs the transition between active/feeding and inactive/fasting states in mammals is maintained by an intricate circadian clock network. Core circadian genes, including Period (Per1/2/3), Cryptochrome (Cry1/2), Clock, and Arntl (Bmal1), form a transcriptional-translational feedback loop to sustain cyclic expression patterns [9]. Although the hypothalamus is thought to serve as the master pacemaker, several peripheral tissues maintain intrinsic circadian oscillations under the regulation of these core circadian genes [10,11]. The liver, a central hub of glucose and lipid metabolism, exhibits widespread diurnal gene expression [12,13]. Consistently, disruption of hepatic circadian genes leads to profound metabolic defects [14]. For example, mice lacking Bmal1 in hepatocytes display impaired mitochondrial function and oxidative phosphorylation [15], and disruption of hepatic clock function broadly disturbs lipid and glucose homeostasis [16].

Despite the established role of circadian networks in metabolism, emerging evidence suggests that nutrient-sensing pathways can regulate hepatic transcripts independently of the core clock. In TRF-treated mice, many rhythmic hepatic transcripts remain intact even in the absence of functional circadian genes [7,8,17]. For instance, mice lacking Cry1/2 exhibit physiological responses to TRF similar to wild-type controls [8]. Moreover, although hepatic clock deficiency predisposes mice on a high-fat diet to hyperlipidemia, TRF effectively prevents metabolic disease development in both wild-type and clock-deficient animals [17]. However, the contributions of specific circadian genes to the effects of both acute fasting metabolism and chronic repeated fasting in the form of TRF remains unresolved. In particular, the functions of the Period gene family have yet to be systematically examined, presenting an important gap in the field.

We recently identified the circadian gene Period 1 (Per1) as a Period family member that is uniquely upregulated in the liver during acute fasting [18]. We further examined how Per1 responds to fasting signals across the 24-hour cycle and in mice lacking hepatocyte Bmal1. Unexpectedly, fasting consistently induces Per1 gene expression throughout the circadian cycle, and independently of Bmal1. Hepatocyte Per1 deletion impairs β-oxidation and prevents the activation of hepatokine Fgf21 and the metabolic regulator Pdk4 [18]. Given this novel function of Per1 apart from other core circadian genes, we sought to quantify the contributions of hepatocyte Per1 to metabolic benefits of TRF.

Here, we demonstrate sex-dependent responses to TRF in mice challenged with a Western diet. Male mice derived metabolic benefits from TRF largely independent of hepatocyte Per1, whereas female mice exhibited partial dependence on Per1 for TRF-mediated improvements. These results reveal a previously unrecognized, sex-specific role of hepatocyte Per1 in modulating TRF responses and identify downstream targets that may contribute to the observed phenotypic differences.

Results

Time-restricted feeding (TRF) improves glucose homeostasis in male but not female mice under western diet

We previously demonstrated that hepatocyte Per1 regulates key fasting responses in both male and female mice [18,19]. We therefore examined how Per1 mediates the metabolic effects of time-restricted feeding (TRF) under Western diet (WD) challenge in both male and female mice. We used Per1fl/fl mice with Cre recombinase expressed under Albumin promoter (Alb-Cre) to induce hepatocyte-specific Per1 knockout (Per1LKO). At 8 weeks of age, mice were switched to WD and assigned to either ad libitum feeding (ALF) or TRF restricted to Zeitgeber Time (ZT)14–22 (8 h during the dark phase) for twelve weeks (Figure 1A). Glucose and insulin tolerance tests (GTT and ITT) were performed in both sexes 10 weeks-post diet change. Male Per1LKO mice exhibited a trend of higher glucose levels than Per1fl/fl mice under ALF during GTT (Figure 1B). Area under curve (AUC) analysis revealed a lower blood glucose in Per1LKO mice under TRF treatment, which indicates that TRF improves glucose homeostasis in the absence of Per1 (Figure 1C). Similarly, both Per1fl/fl and Per1LKO male mice under TRF treatment exhibited lower blood glucose throughout the ITT. ITT analysis normalized to baseline suggests TRF is sufficient to improve insulin sensitivity in Per1LKO mice (Figure 1C, S1C). By contrast, female mice did not show any improvements in either GTT or ITT under TRF treatment (Figure 1D-E, S1C-D). TRF reshapes body composition in male mice independent of hepatocyte Per1

We also measured body weight changes during the first 10 weeks of WD feeding. Male Per1fl/fl and Per1LKO mice under TRF gained significantly less weight than their ALF controls (Figure 2A). Consistent with their attenuated response to TRF in GTT and ITT, female mice did not exhibit a similar degree of body weight loss when compared with males (Figure 2B). We observed no significant difference in total food consumption between genotypes in either male or female mice (Fig. S1A). We then measured body composition in both sexes. In male mice, TRF treatment significantly lowered the percentage of fat and increased the percentage of lean mass. This difference was also reflected in the fat-to-lean mass ratio (Figure 2C). However, female mice showed no significant changes in fat or lean mass percentage, or in the fat-to-lean mass ratio (Figure 2D). Sexually dimorphic responses in serum and hepatic lipids mediated by TRF

To evaluate lipid accumulation in male and female mice under ALF or TRF treatment, we measured hepatic and serum lipid level. Male mice showed a significant treatment effect in serum triglyceride (TG) level. No change was found in serum non-esterified fatty acid (NEFA) level (Figure 3A). Most importantly, TRF treatment significantly reduced serum cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLc) independent of genotypes in male mice (Figure 3A). Two-way ANOVA analysis revealed a significant treatment effect driven by TRF in male mice serum glucose level (Figure 3B). TRF treatment in female mice significantly increased serum TG level, and Per1LKO mice had significantly higher serum TG level than Per1fl/fl mice under TRF treatment (Figure 3C). Unlike male mice, female mice under TRF treatment had significantly higher serum NEFA independent of hepatocyte Per1. Hepatic lipid level measurements revealed both Per1fl/fl and Per1LKO male mice gained less hepatic TG content, indicating that TRF-driven reduction in liver TG is independent of hepatocyte Per1 in males. Liver NEFA level was increased in TRF-treated male mice in both genotypes, and no significant difference was found in liver cholesterol level (Figure 3E). Similarly, female Per1fl/fl mice showed a decrease in liver TG level under TRF treatment (Figure 3F). However, Per1LKO females failed to show any improvement, and TRF-treated Per1LKO females exhibited significantly higher hepatic TG level compared with Per1fl/fl TRF mice (Figure 3F). In contrast to males, TRF decreased hepatic NEFA level in females of both genotypes, with no significant change in hepatic cholesterol level (Figure 3F). Taken together, hepatocyte Per1 mediates TRF-induced reductions in serum cholesterol, LDLc and hepatic TG in females, whereas Per1 is dispensable for these effects in males.

TRF reinstates day–night fuel utilization patterns in Western diet–fed male mice independent of Per1

Previous studies have shown that TRF can restore diurnal metabolic rhythms in obese male mice [17]. To examine how male and female mice respond to TRF in the presence or absence of hepatocyte Per1, we performed indirect calorimetry to quantify gas exchanges and energy expenditure. In male mice, TRF significantly separated the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) between ZT6 and ZT18, indicating improved metabolic flexibility and the ability to switch between fatty acid and glucose utilization across resting and active phases (Figure 4A–B, Figure S2A). Analysis of caloric expenditure revealed that Per1LKO mice under ALF produced less heat during the dark phase, but TRF treatment rescued this deficit and increased heat production in both genotypes. TRF also increased heat production during the light phase, although Per1LKO mice remained lower than Per1fl/fl controls (Figure 4C–D, Figure S2B). Consistent with these findings, TRF enhanced both CO2 production and O2 consumption across light and dark phases, reflecting an overall increase in metabolic rate (Figure 4E–H, Figure S2C-D). Under ALF, Per1LKO males exhibited reduced VCO2 and VO2 during the dark phase, which was rescued by TRF. However, VO2 during the light phase remained lower in Per1LKO mice even with TRF treatment (Figure 4H). Correlation analysis revealed stronger associations between body weight and heat production in both TRF groups (Figure S2E). Two-way ANOVA further demonstrated a significant effect of TRF on dark-phase activity, with no effect during the light phase (Figure S2F). TRF increases heat production in Per1fl/fl but not Per1LKO female mice

We next assessed gas exchanges and heat production in female mice by indirect calorimetry. RER measurements showed non-significant differences between ZT6 and ZT18 in both ALF and TRF groups (Figure 5A–B, Figure S3A). TRF significantly increased heat production in Per1fl/fl females during both dark and light phases; however, Per1LKO mice failed to elevate heat production during the light phase (Figure 5C–D). Notably, Per1LKO females under ALF displayed reduced heat output in both light and dark cycles, and during dark phase, Per1LKO mice under TRF treatment did not reach the level observed in Per1fl/fl TRF mice (Figure 5D, Figure S3B). Consistent with these findings, TRF increased CO2 production and O2 consumption in Per1fl/fl females, but Per1LKO females maintained lower exchange rate even under TRF condition (Figure 5E–H, Figure S3C–D). No significant correlation was observed between body weight and heat production (Figure S3E), and total activity did not differ among groups (Figure S3F). TRF drives divergent transcriptional responses in male and female mice

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that samples clustered tightly within groups, with a clear separation between sexes along the y-axis (Figure 6A). Along the x-axis, both genotype and feeding condition contributed to sample separation. In male mice, TRF clustered Per1fl/fl and Per1LKO samples together, suggesting that TRF reduced transcriptional differences between genotypes. We next quantified the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs; false discovery rate or FDR <0.05, absolute value of log2 fold-change or |log2FC| > 1). In male mice, TRF induced more DEGs in Per1fl/fl than in Per1LKO mice, with 204 of 346 DEGs in Per1LKO TRF mice overlapping with those in Per1fl/fl mice, suggesting a shared, Per1-independent transcriptional shift (Figure 6B). In contrast, female Per1LKO mice showed nearly 5-fold more DEGs than Per1fl/fl mice after TRF (993 vs. 248), with only 103 genes shared. The data indicate transcriptional divergence that correlates with an attenuated response to TRF in Per1LKO female mice (Figure 6B).

Gene identity analysis revealed that TRF suppressed inflammatory genes (Ccl2, Ccl5, Cxcl9, Cxcl10) in male Per1fl/fl mice, along with circadian regulators (Dbp, Per1, Per3, Wee1) (Figure 6C). Several metabolic genes were also downregulated, including Cidec (a lipid droplet regulator previously shown to be suppressed by TRF [17,20]) and Vldlr (very low-density lipoprotein receptor, whole-body deletion of which protects against obesity [21]). In female Per1fl/fl mice, TRF induced far fewer DEGs, though circadian genes were suppressed similarly to males (Figure 6C). Direct sex comparisons under TRF revealed higher Vldlr expression in Per1fl/fl females (Figure 6C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis highlighted suppression of inflammatory and circadian pathways in male Per1fl/fl TRF mice, and suppression of circadian and hormone-related pathways in females (Figure 6D). Comparison of Per1LKO TRF versus Per1fl/fl TRF mice showed minimal genotype differences in males (only one DEG), but substantial differences in females, including induction of lipogenic genes (Acly, Elovl6) (Figure S4A).

We validated these findings by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). In male mice, TRF significantly suppressed Per1, Per3, and Wee1, while Dbp showed treatment-driven suppression and Bmal1 remained unchanged (Figure 7A). TRF suppressed lipogenic genes Elovl6 and Scd1, whereas Fasn and Acly were unchanged (Figure 7B). Discussion

The liver integrates intrinsic circadian signals with external nutritional cues [16]. Proper alignment of these pathways is essential for metabolic health, as disruption of circadian rhythms and feeding patterns increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, obesity, diabetes, and cancer [[22], [23], [24]]. Time-restricted feeding (TRF) has emerged as an effective strategy to realign circadian sleep–wake cycles with feeding–fasting metabolic cycles [25]. Female human participants and female models of diet-induced obesity have a distinct response to TRF when compared with males [26,27]. Despite this long-held observation, protective mechanisms mediating TRF - and still further its dimorphism - remain unclear. We recently identified hepatocyte Per1 as a critical factor in the metabolic switch from glucose to fat oxidation during acute fasting by inducing a Per1-dependent Fgf21-Pdk4 axis in both sexes [18]. The unique position of hepatocyte Per1 in integrating both circadian and nutritional signals in the liver prompted us to quantify the function of Per1 in the metabolic benefits of TRF under the setting of diet-induced obesity [8,18]. We now identify hepatocyte Per1 as a mechanism by which TRF induces energy expenditure and reduces hepatic lipid accumulation in WD-fed female but not male mice, and therefore as a determinant of the dimorphic effects of TRF. Together, the data reveal several non-exclusive possibilities: i) a novel function for Per1 in the chronic adaptive response to repeated bouts of fasting, ii) a greater dependence on dynamic substrate plasticity in the metabolic effects of TRF in females versus males, and iii) the existence of additional Per1-independent adaptive pathways that mediate TRF effects in males, such as TRF-induced Vldlr suppression.

Sexually dimorphic responses to TRF treatment mediated by hepatocyte Per1

A sex-dependent response to TRF treatment has been characterized in mice [28,29]. TRF treatment during 9 h-window during dark phase significantly attenuated body weight gain in male mice under Western diet but not in female mice [28]. In line with the previous study highlighting a more pronounced response to TRF in male mice compared to females [28], our work uniquely identifies hepatocyte Per1 as a critical mediator of TRF effects, particularly in females. In Per1fl/fl females, TRF significantly lowered serum cholesterol, serum LDLc, serum glucose and hepatic TG level, whereas no such reductions were observed in Per1LKO mice (Figure 3C,F). Moreover, female Per1LKO TRF mice had higher serum TG levels than their Per1fl/fl counterparts (Figure 3C). In contrast to male mice—where metabolic defects in glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, and energy expenditure in Per1LKO mice were fully rescued by TRF—female mice showed no such improvements. Specifically, TRF did not enhance oxidative metabolic flexibility in females (Figure 4B), and Per1LKO females continued to exhibit lower heat production and gas exchange during both light and dark phases despite TRF treatment (Figure 5C–H). Taken together, these findings indicate that hepatocyte Per1 is largely dispensable for TRF-mediated benefits in male mice, but is essential for females to mount a proper metabolic response.

Changes in lipid metabolism as the main outcome of TRF mediated by Per1

Previous work has shown that TRF in male mice suppresses genes involved in lipid metabolism largely independent of an intact circadian network [17]. Consistent with this, we found that TRF significantly lowered expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism including Acot3, Cidec, and Vldlr (Figure 7B) [17,20]. Notably, Vldlr has been implicated in the development of fatty liver: elevated hepatic Vldlr expression in Pparβ/δ knockout mice is associated with increased lipid accumulation, whereas Vldlr deficiency improves glucose and insulin tolerance and reduces adipose tissue inflammation [21,30]. In our study, Vldlr exhibited clear sex-dependent response to TRF. In males, TRF robustly suppressed Vldlr expression in both genotypes. In contrast, in females, Vldlr levels remained unchanged under either feeding condition (Figure 7B,E). Although sex differences in Vldlr expression have been documented in both mice and humans [31], our findings reveal a previously unrecognized sex-specific divergence in the regulation of Vldlr by TRF, which may contribute to the sexually dimorphic metabolic responses we observed.

In addition to Vldlr, we also identified that female mice significantly suppress circadian genes in response to TRF treatment independent of hepatocyte Per1. This included Per2, Per3 and Dbp. In contrast, male mice showed no significant changes in the expression of these genes (Figure 7A,D). Notably, Bmal1 was significantly upregulated in both Per1fl/fl and Per1LKO female mice under TRF treatment, but no changes were found in male mice. Previous studies have demonstrated sex-dependent differences in circadian rhythms in both mice and humans [32,33]. Our findings, which reveal distinct circadian gene expression changes under TRF between male and female mice, further support the existence of sex dimorphism in circadian regulation. This dimorphism may contribute to the differential physiological responses to TRF observed between sexes. In addition, we did observe trends in Per2 and Per3 in male mice subjected to TRF that were not statistically significant. In light of known interactions between Bmal1 and the Period genes, we cannot fully rule out the possibility that Per2 and Per3 compensation contributes to the protective phenotypes observed in Per1LKO males. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms by which circadian genes mediate female-specific responses to TRF and to better understand the underlying basis for the observed sex differences.

The main differences we identified at transcriptional level between male and female mice are genes that are involved in lipid metabolism (Figure 6C). Through qRT-PCR validation, we noticed that male mice under TRF treatment suppressed key lipogenic genes in both genotypes (Figure 7B). However, TRF-treated female Per1LKO mice had significantly higher expression of lipogenic genes including Elovl6, Fasn and Acly, which may contribute to higher hepatic TG level than the Per1fl/fl TRF mice (Figure 3, Figure 7E).

Differences between acute fasting in regular chow and chronic fasting in Western diet

We previously reported that a single 16 h overnight fasting induces Per1 expression across the circadian cycle [18]. In contrast, after 12 weeks of Western diet, TRF suppressed Per1 expression in both sexes relative to ALF (Figure 7A,D). These opposite outcomes suggest distinct mechanisms underlying adaptations to acute versus chronic fasting. Beyond Per1, other key differences emerged. Under TRF, male mice exhibited elevated serum TG levels with reduced hepatic TG accumulation (Figure 3A,E), whereas acute fasting decreased serum TG and increased hepatic TG. Transcriptional responses also diverged. During acute fasting in chow-fed lean mice, both sexes showed similar defects in activating Fgf21 and Pdk4 transcription, but TRF revealed pronounced sex-specific differences in lipid metabolism genes. Collectively, these findings indicate that the molecular adaptations to acute and chronic fasting are mechanistically distinct, and results from one condition may not be directly extrapolated to the other.

Limitations and future directions

In this study, we demonstrate a role of hepatocyte Per1 in mediating responses to TRF in both male and female mice. Most phenotypic differences in males were found independent of hepatocyte Per1. Given prior work showing that mice lacking other circadian genes regulators remain responsive to TRF [17], we did not further pursue how Per1 influences rhythmic expression of hepatic transcripts. It is important to note that all tissues were harvested at ZT4; therefore, the transcriptional changes identified here primarily reflect this time point and may not capture dynamic oscillations across the circadian cycle.

One notable finding from our work is the sex-specific regulation of Vldlr. Differences in Vldlr expression between male and female mice highlight a potential mechanism by which TRF exerts sex-dependent effects. In addition to Vldlr, Per1LKO female mice subjected to TRF exhibited higher expression of lipogenic genes compared with Per1fl/fl controls (Figure 7E). Further studies are required to elucidate the mechanisms by which hepatocyte Per1 regulates metabolic responses in females during TRF, with particular emphasis on de novo lipogenesis.

Moreover, our RNA-sequencing data indicate that TRF in female mice lead to alterations in hormone-related pathways (Figure 6D), and Per1 mediates female responses to TRF. These observations raise the possibility that Per1 may interact with sex hormone signaling to shape metabolic responses to TRF. Future studies dissecting how Per1 integrates circadian, nutritional, and hormonal cues could provide new insights into the molecular basis of sex-dependent metabolic phenotypes under TRF.

Materials and methods

Animals

C57BL/6 J-strain mice expressing Cre recombinase under Albumin promoter (Jackson Laboratory, 003574) were obtained directly from the Jackson Laboratory. Per1fl/fl mice were generated by Genome Engineering & iPSC Center (GEiC) at Washington University. Upon arrival, mice were equilibrated for a minimum of 7 days in the specific pathogen-free vivarium prior to initiating metabolic measurements. Mice were kept under a 12 h alternating light/dark, temperature-controlled facility throughout the experimentation. All in vivo experimental procedures were performed in strict accordance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines at Washington University School of Medicine. Western diet was purchased from Inotive (TD88137).

Body composition measurement

Mice 10 weeks after diet change were assessed for body composition using EchoMRI (EchoMRI™ Body Composition Analyzer, USA), and fat and lean mass were recorded.

Indirect calorimetry

Mice 10 weeks post diet change were transferred to single-housed chambers in an indirect calorimetry system (TSE PhenoMaster, Germany) after recovering from tolerance tests for at least 72 h. Data were collected following a 24-hour equilibration period. CO2 and O2 volumes, heat production, and activity were recorded, and RER was calculated by the system software. A maximum of two mice per group were transferred into the indirect calorimetry with single housing for each round of measurement, and the remaining mice were kept in their original cage. This ensured that only a minority of the group was singly housed at any given time, and that single housing exposure was minimized. After the data collection, mice were put back to their original cage along with other mice to recover for at least a week before the tissue collection.

Tissue harvest

All mice were synchronized with regard to feeding/fasting and light/dark timing. After mice were recovered from indirect calorimetry for at least a week, we harvested tissues from these mice for downstream analysis. On the day of harvest, we collectively fasted all mice at ZT22 and harvested the tissues from both male and female mice simultaneously at ZT4 ± 30 min. Tissues were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in −80 °C until processing. Serum samples were collected using serum separation tube (BD Microtainer, 365,967) after centrifugation at 13000g for 15 min. Serum lipid and glucose measurements were made prior to storage in −80 °C.

Serum and hepatic lipids

For all serum analyses, submandibular blood was collected immediately prior to sacrifice and serum was separated. Triglycerides (ThermoFisher, TR22421), free fatty acids (Wako Diagnostics, 999-34691, 995-34791, 991-34891, 993-35191), cholesterol (ThermoFisher, TR13421) and glucose (Cayman, 10,009,582) quantification were performed using commercially available reagents according to manufacturer's directions. Hepatic lipids analyses are done as previously reported [34].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was prepared by homogenizing snap-frozen livers or cultured cells in Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, 15,596,026) according to the manufacturer's protocol. cDNA was prepared using Qiagen Quantitect reverse transcriptase kit (Qiagen, 205,310). Real-time qRT-PCR was performed with Step-One Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Green master Mix Reagent (Applied Biosystems) and specific primer pairs. Relative gene expression was calculated by a comparative method using values normalized to the expression of the internal control gene β-Actin. All primers were custom-synthesized oligonucleotides obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies. Primer sequences are included in Supplementary Table 1.

Glucose and insulin tolerance testing

Glucose tolerance test was performed in mice fasted for 6 h from ZT0-ZT6 for all groups. Mice were weighted and injected with 30% glucose solution dissolved in saline 2 g/kg body weight. Blood glucose level was measured using glucometer every 30 min. Similarly, insulin solution was injected in mice fasted for 4 h from ZT0-ZT4 for all groups, 0.75 units/kg body weight (Humulin R, NDC 0002-8215-17). Blood glucose level was measured using glucometer every 30 min.

RNA-sequencing analysis

RNA-sequencing was performed through Center for Medical Genomics Core at the Indiana University School of Medicine in Indianapolis using Illumina NovaSeq X Plus. Expression analysis was performed using edgeR package [35].

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SEM unless specified in the figure legends. The number of independent biological samples (n) in each experiment is detailed in the figure legends. The data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism 9 software as specified in the figure legends.
