Determination of morphine sulfate anti-pain drug solubility in supercritical CO2 with machine learning method
Authors: Gholamhossein Sodeifian, Chieh-Ming Hsieh, Farnoush Masihpour, Amirmuhammad Tabibzadeh, Rui-Heng Jiang, Ya-Hung Cheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Accurate solute solubility measuring and modeling in supercritical carbon dioxide (ScCO2) would address the best working conditions and thermodynamic boundaries for material processing with this type of fluid. Theory- and data-driven methods are two general modeling approaches. Using theory-driven methods, the solubility is estimated based on the principles of thermodynamics, while data-driven methods are developed by training the algorithms. Despite acceptance of each of these methods, more experimental solubility data are still needed to promote modeling performances. In this study, for the first time, solubility of morphine sulfate is determined and modeled by a set of 13 semi-empirical (theory-driven) and random forest (data-driven) models. Using a laboratory system with an ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy, the experimental solubilities including 48 data points were obtained at different temperatures (308–338 K) and pressures (12–27 MPa). The minimum (0.806 × 10−5) and maximum (5.902 × 10−5) equilibrium mole fractions were observed at working pressures of 12 and 27 MPa, respectively, both at the same temperature of 338 K. It was indicated that random forest model (with AARD% of 1.29%) had an excellent predictive performance against semi-empirical models (with AARD% from 9.33 to 19.76%). The results showed that solute molecular weight had the highest effect on random forest modeling. Using modeling results from Chrastil and Bartle models, total and vaporization enthalpies of dissolution of morphine sulfate in ScCO2 were found to be 35.12 and 59.04 kJ/mole, respectively.

Introduction

Nowadays, different production processes involved in the pharmaceutical industries can be assisted by supercritical fluids (SCFs) such as particle formation1, synthesis of medical compounds2, encapsulation in solid carriers3, crystallization4, and targeted extraction from complex matrices5. The great advantages accompanied by the use of SCF-assisted processes include providing noteworthy characteristics and controlled specifications with the final products.

Among different candidates as supercritical media, carbon dioxide (CO2) presents some processing superiorities over the others, such as non-toxicity and high availability. Furthermore, mild critical temperature and pressure, i.e., 304.1 K and 7.38 MPa, respectively are needed to have supercritical carbon dioxide (ScCO2). The advantageous properties of ScCO2 are reported in the literature6.

In order to achieve the best processing outcomes, thermodynamic information on the phase behavior of the system at a wide range of working temperature and pressure is important. One of the critical factors is the solubility of the materials to be processed by ScCO2, and also the solubility of ScCO2 in those materials. The latter is especially important for processing of polymer compounds7. To study the solute solubility in the ScCO2, experimental practices are generally categorized into two methods including static and dynamic. In static method, a constant-volume vessel containing compound of interest is filled with ScCO2 at the given temperature and pressure. When the equilibrium state is reached, a small volume of the saturated fluid is taken from the fluid phase and analyzed. In dynamic method, SCF is continuously passed over the compounds and dissolved particles are collected from the outlet stream. Due to small quantities of dissolved compound, the use of instrumental analysis such as spectrometric or chromatographic methods is necessary, to provide high sensitivity with the equilibrium concentration of the solute. With respect to what was mentioned, it is apparent that experimental determination of solid solute solubility at wide conditions is not only often laborious, but also needs too much cost for accomplishment. Instead, there are several computational approaches, which are developed to estimate the solubility of the solute in the supercritical phase. These approaches are generally categorized into theory- and data-driven methods. Among theory-driven methods, some are based on thermodynamic fundamentals and some others are developed empirically8. Equations of state (EoSs) and activity coefficient methods have been long the well-known thermodynamic approaches for estimating equilibrium properties and phase behavior of pure and mixture systems. Successful application of EoSs for solid-SCF equilibrium is highly dependent on the type of mixing rules utilized for calculations and also on the accurate determination of physical properties of the solute (i.e., critical constants, molar volume, vapor pressure etc.). To employ activity coefficient methods for high pressure systems, they should be coupled at first with another model. In this regard, conductor-like screening models (COSMO) coupled with Peng-Robinson (PR) EoS is one of the most studied methods. For increased correlative abilities of EoSs or activity coefficient methods, calculations would be accompanied by more extended complexities.

Semi-empirical models include several adjustable parameters (from three in Chrastil9, Bartle10, or MST11 models to ten in Haghbakhsh model12) which are often determined by minimization of errors between experimental and calculated results. It is believed that the correlative ability of a semi-empirical model depends on its number of adjustable parameters. The companion coefficients of each adjustable parameter has also considerable influence in this respect13. One of important assumptions for employing semi-empirical models is that the fluid density equals to the density of the pure SCF at those conditions. Compared to EoSs, it is indicated that semi-empirical models can be applied more simply and achieve comparable results14–16. In addition, semi-empirical models can provide some physical properties connected with the dissolution process of the solute. However, there has been to date no theory-driven approach that gives the best correlation results for experimental solubility of all compounds.

Recently, machine learning methods are considered as suitable alternatives for simulation with high accuracy. They present several advantages such as robustness to noisy or incomplete data, resistance to overfitting, reduced time consumption, and better correlation with the nonlinear variations. Among machine learning methods, the effectiveness of tree-based models such as Gradient Boosting (GB), Extra Trees (ET), and Random Forest (RF) has been proved for predicting the solute solubility in ScCO217–19. However, machine-learning methods do not present physical parameters for the simulated process. To promote modelling performances to the desired level of accuracy, more experimental solubility data are still needed.

Morphine sulfate is an anti-pain drug and is widely utilized in pain management, for example after surgical operations or for patients who suffer from cancer20. By providing therapeutic effects through showing affinity with the opioid receptors in the central nervous system, morphine sulfate gives significant relief to the patients who experience moderate to severe pain.

The scope of this research is to determine the solubility of morphine sulfate at different working pressures and temperatures of ScCO2. Thereafter, it is aimed to correlate the solubility dataset by using two approaches namely semi-empirical (theory-driven) and random forest (data-driven) models. By calculating statistical criteria, i.e., R-square (R2) and average absolute relative deviation (AARD%), the accuracies of these models were compared to each other. Furthermore, total and vaporization enthalpies of dissolution of morphine sulfate in ScCO2 were determined.

Apparatus and procedure

According to the schematic diagram presented in Fig. 1, the solubility experiments were conducted at different pressures and temperatures by using a laboratory integrated system and a double-beam ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometer setup (photonix Ar 2015, Teifsanje Company).

Fig. 1Experimental setup for supercritical solubility measurement.

Experimental setup for supercritical solubility measurement.

All components of the system, made of stainless steel 316, including valves, tubes, and connections were capable of working at pressures up to 40 MPa. To bring morphine sulfate and ScCO2 into direct contact, a stainless steel cell (with wall thickness of 0.8 inch) was utilized. At first, 500 mg of morphine sulfate powder was transferred into the cell. After delivering the fluid into the cell, the desired pressure and temperature based on the experimental conditions of each test were adjusted by a pressure gauge (WIKA, Germany, Code: EN 837-1) with accuracy of 1 MPa and by an oven (Froilabo, France, AE-60) with accuracy of 0.1 K, respectively. To increase the dissolution rate of morphine sulfate in ScCO2 and thus decreasing the time required for equilibration, the mixture inside the cell was agitated continuously. For this purpose, the cell was placed on a magnetic stirring setup. Following equilibration at the operating conditions of each test, a given volume of the saturated fluid was discharged from the sampling tube into a graduated cylinder containing an appropriate solvent. According to the literature21, morphine sulfate is soluble in water (64.51 mg/mL at 25 °C). Therefore, deionized (DI) water with the conductivity of 5 µS.m−1 was used as the collection solvent. At last, by injecting an amount of DI water, the solute particles those possibly left in the sampling tube were also carried into the graduated cylinder. Detailed description of the sampling method has been provided by other research groups22. Concentration of morphine sulfate in each test solution was determined by considering its wavelength of maximum absorbance (λmax = 290 nm) in the UV-Vis spectrum (see Table 1). To have standard solutions for spectrometric analysis, an aquatic stock of morphine sulfate was prepared at first with the concentration of 100 µg/mL, from which diluted solutions were then obtained sequentially. Using the absorbance calibration line established by the standard solutions (with the correlation coefficient of 99.61%), mole fraction and mass concentration of morphine sulfate in the test solutions were calculated. Each solubility experiment was performed twice and the mean value from absorbance measurements was used for calculations. Results and discussion

Experimental data

With respect to the experimental procedure, a set of 48 experiments was conducted to determine the solubility of morphine sulfate in ScCO2. For this purpose, the combinations of six pressure points (from 12 to 27 MPa) and four temperature points (from 308 to 338 K) were studied. Using the relationships above, the experimental results in terms of equilibrium mole fraction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{2}$$\end{document}) and equilibrium mass concentration (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{2,T}$$\end{document}) of the solute were calculated. The obtained data are summarized in Table 2, each of which represents the mean value of two distinct experiments. Furthermore, the mole fraction values are shown versus pressure and density of ScCO2 in Fig. 2(a) and (b), respectively. In these figures, certain shapes and colors have differentiated the data points of each solubility isotherm. According to these results, the minimum (0.806 × 10−5) and maximum (5.902 × 10−5) equilibrium mole fractions are observed at working pressures of 12 and 27 MPa, respectively, both at the same temperature of 338 K.

Fig. 2Comparison of experimental solubility data and correlation results of three semi-empirical models for morphine sulfate in terms of (a) Solubility-pressure and (b) solubility-density diagrams.

Comparison of experimental solubility data and correlation results of three semi-empirical models for morphine sulfate in terms of (a) Solubility-pressure and (b) solubility-density diagrams.

The experimental results indicate that the solubility of morphine sulfate increases with increased pressure of ScCO2 (see Fig. 2(a)), because increasing pressure enhances fluid density and subsequently improves the solvent power of carbon dioxide23. On the other hand, temperature has a more complicated role. It can concurrently affect vapor pressure of morphine sulfate and density of ScCO2. Disregarding how high the melting temperature is, vapor pressure of solids changes directly with temperature. By increasing the temperature from 308 to 338 K, higher vapor pressure of morphine sulfate is expected. Higher vapor pressure of morphine sulfate is linked to higher solubility in the fluid. On the other hand, decreasing fluid density by increasing temperature from 308 to 338 K (see Table 2) lowers the solubility of morphine sulfate. Therefore, the result of variation of temperature depends on which factor becomes dominant24. According to Fig. 2(a), there is a crossover pressure point within 15–18 MPa for the solubility of morphine sulfate in ScCO2. Above the crossover point, an increase in temperature and pressure results in increased equilibrium mole fraction. However, below the crossover point, equilibrium mole fraction increases with increasing pressure, but decreases with increasing temperature. Similar results have been often observed in the solid solute solubility experiments in ScCO225. Crossover point has also been reported for the solubility of nystatin (at about 24 MPa)26, dapagliflozin propanediol monohydrate (at about 17 MPa)27, chlorothiazide (at about 17 MPa)28, and sildenafil citrate (at 24 MPa)16.

Solubility correlation using semi-empirical equations

Examining the self-consistency of the newly measured experimental data with semi-empirical models is crucial to validate their reliability. This study applied 13 semi-empirical models to correlate the measured solubility data for morphine sulfate. Although some semi-empirical models are unable to assist the self-consistent test, good agreements between the experimental data and correlation results are also considered evidence for reliable solubility data. The equations of these studied models with different number of adjustable parameters (denoted as a0 to a7) are provided in Table 3. Table 4 presents the correlation results of the semi-empirical models and their corresponding AARD%. Belghait (with eight adjustable parameters) and Bian (with five adjustable parameters) models provide the most accurate correlation results with AARD% of 9.33% and 9.80%, respectively, whereas the remaining models show similar correlation results with a slightly higher AARD% ranging from 10.15% (for Gordillo model) to 19.76% (for Khansary model). Figure 2 shows the comparison of experimental and correlated data from three selected models: Kumar and Johnson (K-J), Sodeifian, and Belghait models. As mentioned before, Fig. 2 reveals an increasing solubility trend with rising pressure at a given temperature and with rising temperature at a given CO2 density. Furthermore, a good agreement is observed between experimental and correlated data, indicating the robustness of the three semi-empirical models. It seems that, when it comes to the correlation of experimental data, semi-empirical models with more adjustable parameters perform more accurately29. Self-consistent tests using MST (see Fig. 3(a)) and K-J (see Fig. 3(b)) models were performed to validate the reliability of the solubility data. The phenomenon of collapsing experimental solubility data under varying temperatures into a single line confirms the self-consistency of the dataset.

According to the experimental solubility data for morphine sulfate, total, vaporization, and solvation enthalpies were found to be 35.12 kJ/mole, 59.04 kJ/mole, and − 23.92 kJ/mole, respectively. These calculated enthalpy values provide insights into the energy requirements for solid solute dissolution in ScCO2. A higher total enthalpy implies that solute molecules necessitate more energy to dissolve into ScCO2 and exhibit a greater heat release during recrystallization. Moreover, a higher vaporization enthalpy suggests stronger intermolecular interactions between solute molecules. It is important to note that these values are not experimentally measured but estimated through Chrastil and Bartle models with assumptions employed in model development.

Representation of solubility using random forest

Random forest (RF) is composed of a certain number of decision trees, and each decision tree is trained based on different random subsets of samples and features, promoting diversity among the trees. The ensemble of these trees enables the model to effectively capture intricate nonlinear relationships and interactions within the data, mitigating the risk of overfitting. Random forest exhibits good capability in handling diverse input variables and fitting real-world data robustly30–32. A few case studies have demonstrated that random forest is able to correlate and predict solid solute solubility in ScCO233–37. Thus, we applied random forest to correlate and represent the solubility data measured in this study together with the solubility data of 19 chemicals collected from the literature (Table 5). These solubility data were partitioned into training and testing sets with a ratio of 8:2. To mitigate overfitting, a five-fold cross-validation was implemented. The performance of the random forest model is assessed through the average absolute relative deviation (AARD%) and absolute logarithmic deviation (ALD-y).

The input features for the random forest model are temperature (T), pressure (P), and CO2 density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({\rho\:}_{{\text{C}\text{O}}_{2}})$$\end{document} at experimental conditions, as well as molecular weight (MW) and melting temperature (Tm) of the studied drugs.

The values of hyperparameters can significantly influence the performance of machine learning models. In this study, the values of four hyperparameters in the random forest model are determined from the grid search algorithm with the following range: 10 ~ 100 for tree depth and tree number, 2 ~ 3 for minimum sample split, and 1 ~ 2 for minimum sample leaf. The range of each hyperparameter is chosen based on our previous experience, aiming to balance computational cost and model complexity. The final values of the hyperparameters are 50 for tree depth, 80 for tree number, 2 for minimum sample split, and 1 for minimum sample leaf. The above approach aims to mitigate the risk of overfitting and uncover the most suitable hyperparameter values for the random forest model to well correlate the training data and effectively predict the testing (or unseen) data.

Figure 4(a) shows the parity plot of experimental and calculated solubility from the random forest model. The majority of data points cluster around the diagonal line, i.e., the calculated solubility data in both training and testing sets closely mirrors the experimental results. Figure 4(b) illustrates deviations between predicted and experimental solubility. Most data points cluster below ALD-y = ± 0.1, confirming accurate estimation results. The calculated R-square (R2), AARD%, and ALD-y of the random forest model are 0.9916, 5.23%, and 0.0223 for the training set, respectively, while those are 0.9735, 11.04%, and 0.0468 for the testing set, respectively. In the case of morphine sulfate, the overall deviations are 1.29% in AARD% and 0.005 in ALD-y, and the calculation results at various temperatures and pressures are depicted in Fig. 5, demonstrating excellent predictive performance.

Figure 6 presents the result of feature importance analysis for the model inputs. Based on the permutation feature importance algorithm developed by Fisher et al.38, the feature importance is assessed by calculating the increase in model prediction error after permuting the features. If reshuffling the values of a feature leads to an increase in model error, then that feature is considered “important.” In these cases, the model relies on that feature for predictions. The analysis results show that the molecular weight (MW) of these drugs is the most important characteristic among the five input features, indicating that the molecular size of a drug strongly influences its solubility in ScCO2 since CO2 is considered a non-polar solvent. Pressure (P) ranks second in terms of importance in influencing the calculation results. As can be seen in experimental solubility data, the solubility in ScCO2 typically increases with elevating pressure at a given temperature. The melting temperature (Tm) comes next since it signifies the internal interactions between drug molecules, i.e., a substance with a higher melting temperature has a larger resistance for molecules to escape from its crystalline structure and become free molecules of liquid state. Density of carbon dioxide (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\rho\:}_{{\text{C}\text{O}}_{2}}$$\end{document}) is the fourth important feature since the solvent power of ScCO2 is considered proportional to its density. Temperature (T) is the least important feature, and this may be attributed to that CO2 density is a function of temperature and pressure, and the influence of temperature on solubility has already been taken into account through density. It should be noted that the effect of temperature and pressure on the solubility has been established in these papers29,62–70. Furthermore, in general, adjustable parameters in EoSs or other models such as semi-empirical models may be determined by various methods including different algorithms like nonlinear regression methods71–73.

Conclusion

In this study, correlative abilities of a set of 13 semi-empirical models and random forest model were compared for the solubility of morphine sulfate in supercritical carbon dioxide (ScCO2). For this purpose, the solubility experiments were conducted at first at different pressure and temperature ranges of 308–338 K and 12–27 MPa, respectively, by using a laboratory system and an ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometer setup. Experimental results indicated that an increase in temperature and pressure of the system, above crossover point (within 15–18 MPa) resulted in increased solubility. However, below the crossover point, solubility of morphine sulfate increases with increasing pressure, but decreases with increasing temperature. Among semi-empirical models, Belghait and Bian models had the highest accuracies, with AARD% of 9.33% and 9.80%, respectively. Modeling results indicated that random forest with AARD% of 1.29% is more superior to correlate solubility data.
