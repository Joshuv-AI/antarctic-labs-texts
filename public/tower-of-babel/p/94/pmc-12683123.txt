Dietary Spirulina platensis enhances immune responses and modulates gut microbiota and microbial function in laying hens
Authors: Md Salahuddin, Ahmed AA Abdel-Wareth, Kayla G Stamps, Trahmilla L Carr, Cassandra D Gray, Adrian MW Aviña, Jayant Lohakare
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrowing restrictions on antibiotic use in poultry nutrition have heightened the need for natural feed additives that promote health and performance. Spirulina platensis (SP) has gained attention for its immunomodulatory and gut health benefits. This study evaluated the effects of SP supplementation on immune responses, gut microbiota composition, and microbial functional potential in White Leghorn laying hens. A total of 192 White Leghorn laying hens (39 weeks old) were randomly assigned to four dietary groups: Control (0g SP/kg feed), T1 (2.5g SP/kg feed), T2 (5g SP/kg feed), and T3 (10g SP/kg feed). Each group received its respective diet for a six-weeks duration. Serum biomarkers, including cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, were analyzed, and microbial profiling was performed using 16S rRNA sequencing followed by PICRUSt2-based functional predictions. Immune data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by orthogonal polynomial contrasts and Duncan’s multiple test (p < 0.05). Microbial data were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis and UniFrac distances with Tukey’s HSD. LEfSe (LDA>4.0, p < 0.05) was used for identifying differentially abundant taxa. SP supplementation, particularly at 10g SP/kg, significantly enhanced (p < 0.05) pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. Microbial shifts included increased Firmicutes and Lactobacillus, and reduced Bacteroidota and Fusobacterium, with distinct clustering of microbial communities in SP-treated groups. Functional predictions showed significant upregulation (p < 0.05) of glycolysis, amino acid, and nucleotide metabolism, and downregulation (p < 0.05) of teichoic acid and lipid metabolism. These findings underscore the potential of SP, particularly at 10g SP/kg, as a sustainable feed additive to enhance immunity and gut health in antibiotic-free poultry production.Keywords: Spirulina platensis, Gut microbiota, Immune responses, Laying hens, Microbial function

Introduction

The global poultry industry is facing growing pressure to enhance animal productivity and health while reducing dependency on antibiotic growth promoters due to rising concerns over antimicrobial resistance, food safety, environment impact, and consumer demand for antibiotic-free animal products (Samad, 2022; Fonseca et al., 2024; Azizi et al., 2024). This has been prompting a paradigm shift toward safer, more sustainable nutritional strategies that can simultaneously support performance, immunity, and welfare in poultry production. In this context, the use of functional feed additives has garnered significant attention as a strategy to improve host immunity, optimize gut health, and enhance performance in poultry (Broderick et al., 2021; Fonseca et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2024; Gadzama et al., 2025). Among various alternatives, Spirulina platensis (taxonomically classified as Limnospira platensis; Nowicka-Krawczyk et al., 2019, but widely known, marketed, and reported in poultry nutrition literature as Spirulina platensis [SP]), a filamentous cyanobacterium, has emerged as a promising natural supplement due to its exceptional nutritional profile and bioactive properties. (El-Shall et al., 2023; Emam et al., 2025).

SP is significantly rich in high-quality protein containing most of essential amino acids, as well as a broad spectrum of vitamins (including B-complex and provitamin A), essential minerals (such as iron, calcium, and magnesium), and potent bioactive compounds (Liestianty et al., 2019; Abdel-Wareth et al., 2024; Marjanović et al., 2024). These include phycocyanin, β-carotene, polysaccharides, γ-linolenic acid, and phenolic compounds, each known to confer specific health benefits. Collectively, these constituents endow SP with strong antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and antimicrobial properties, positioning it as an effective dietary tool for enhancing poultry health and production efficiency (Mirzaie et al., 2017; El-Shall et al., 2023; Lestingi et al., 2024; Spínola et al., 2024a; Salah et al., 2025). Previous studies have demonstrated that SP supplementation can stimulate immune function by activating both innate and adaptive immune responses, enhance antioxidant status by mitigating oxidative stress, and improve growth performance in broilers and laying hens (Mirzaie et al., 2017; Kolluri et al., 2022; Lestingi et al., 2024; Abdelfatah et al., 2024; Salahuddin et al., 2024; Salah et al., 2025). Furthermore, it has been shown to beneficially modulate the gut microbiota by enriching beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium and suppressing pathogenic taxa including Fusobacterium, Bacteroides, Clostridium perfringens, and Escherichia coli (Satyaraj et al., 2021; El-Shall et al., 2023), which in turn supports better nutrient utilization, gut integrity, and resistance to enteric infections.

Despite the growing body of evidence supporting SP supplementation in poultry, most studies have focused primarily on performance or antioxidant indices, with limited emphasis on the intricate interactions between immune responses, gut microbiota, and microbial functional potential (Zahroojian et al., 2013; Mirzaie et al., 2017; Selim et al., 2018; Omri et al., 2019; Salah et al., 2025). Given the central role of the gut–immune axis in regulating host health, where the intestinal microbiota closely interact with the immune system to maintain barrier integrity, modulate inflammatory responses, and support systemic immunity, a mechanistic understanding of how SP modulates these interactions is essential for its strategic incorporation into commercial layer diets (Wlaźlak et al., 2023; Ayalew et al., 2025). Our recent study has highlighted the potential of SP as a functional feed additive to improve poultry production, egg quality, shelf life during storage, and blood biochemical parameters, supporting its role in promoting overall health and productivity (Salahuddin et al., 2024). Although many of these research gaps have been emphasized in broiler studies, the evidence in laying hens is even more limited, underscoring the importance of the present investigation. Building upon these findings, the present study aims to further investigate the effects of SP on immune responses, gut microbiota composition, and microbial functional pathways in laying hens, providing deeper insight into its mechanisms of action in antibiotic-free production systems.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

This research was conducted at the Poultry Center, Cooperative Agricultural Research Center, Prairie View A&M University, Prairie View, TX, USA, in compliance with the ethical guidelines set forth by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). The experimental protocol received official approval under the identification number AUP# 2023-046.

Birds, diet management, and experimental design

The overall experimental setup, including bird allocation, housing, dietary treatments, and management practices, was conducted following the same protocol as described in our previously published article (Salahuddin et al., 2024). Briefly, a total of 192 White Leghorn laying hens at 39 weeks of age, with an initial average body weight of 1,676.34 ± 29.583 g, were used. The birds were randomly assigned to four dietary treatment groups: Control (0 g SP/kg feed), T1 (2.5 g SP/kg feed), T2 (5 g SP/kg feed), and T3 (10 g SP/kg feed). Each treatment group had eight replicates, with six hens per replicate. The experiment was conducted over six weeks following a 7-day acclimatization period, during which all hens were fed the control basal diet under the same housing and management conditions before starting the dietary treatments. SP was incorporated into the basal diet during feed mixing to ensure uniform distribution and precise supplementation levels in the treatment groups. The basal diet contained 2882 kcal/kg of apparent metabolizable energy (AME) and 165 g/kg of crude protein (CP) (Salahuddin et al., 2024). The experimental diets were formulated according to NRC (1994) recommendations for laying hens to meet the requirements for energy, CP, essential amino acids, calcium, phosphorus, and vitamins/minerals necessary. The Arthrospira platensis (commercially known as Spirulina platensis) powder used in the diets was obtained from Earthrise Nutritionals LLC, USA. The product is produced under GMP and ISO-certified conditions and undergoes testing for heavy metals, microcystins, pesticides, and microbial contaminants, ensuring food-grade quality and safety. The proximate analysis of SP revealed a high protein content (69.3 g/100 g), along with both essential and non-essential amino acids. It also contained substantial mineral quantities and a variety of phytonutrients, including c-phycocyanin, β-carotene (0.23 g/100 g), and zeaxanthin (0.15 g/100 g). Feed was offered ad libitum to ensure continuous availability, and fresh drinking water was provided throughout the study. The birds were housed in a semi-open poultry facility with floor-based housing, using wood shavings as bedding material. Standard poultry management practices were implemented, including temperature maintained between 22-26°C, relative humidity of 55-65%. Ventilation was provided to maintain air quality and prevent heat or moisture buildup, ensuring optimal laying conditions. Routine biosecurity measures were strictly followed to prevent disease transmission and ensure a hygienic environment.

Blood collection and analysis

At the end of the experimental period, sixteen birds from each treatment group were selected based on their average body weight within each replicate to ensure representative sampling. The birds were not subjected to feed restrictions before sampling. Blood sample (5 mL) was collected from the wing vein of each hen using sterilized syringes. The collected blood was allowed to clot naturally before centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 minutes using a Sorvall ST Plus centrifuge (Thermo Electron LED GmbH, Langenselbold, Germany). The separated serum was carefully pipetted into 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes and stored at −20°C until further biochemical analysis. The biochemical parameters analyzed from the serum included interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-21 (IL-21), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and interleukin-16 (IL-16), macrophage inflammatory protein-3 alpha (MIP-3α), regulated upon activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted (RANTES), macrophage inflammatory protein-1 beta (MIP-1β), growth factors like macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). These immunological analyses were performed at the Immune Monitoring Shared Resource Laboratory, Georgia Cancer Center, Augusta University, Augusta, GA.

Excreta sample collection

Excreta samples were collected from eight hens per treatment group, with one bird selected randomly from each replicate. To ensure a sterile collection process, chickens were separated and housed individually in clean cages at the end of the experiment until fresh excreta were obtained, after which they were returned to their original housing. Immediately upon excretion, the excreta samples were carefully collected using sterilized tubes and placed on ice to preserve microbial integrity. The samples were then promptly transferred to a −80°C freezer for long-term storage and subsequent microbiome analysis.

Microbial DNA extraction, 16S rRNA sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis

Microbial genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from excreta samples collected from all treatment groups using the FastDNA™ Spin Kit for Feces (MP Biomedicals, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted gDNA were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WI, USA). The amplification of the V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was performed using the universal primers 5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′ (forward) and 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′ (reverse). PCR amplicons were subsequently purified, quantified, and sequenced on an Illumina platform following standard protocols for high-throughput sequencing.

The raw sequencing data were processed to remove low-quality reads and chimeric sequences, ensuring the generation of high-quality clean data. The DADA2 pipeline was employed for denoising, dereplication, and identification of Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) to enhance resolution and accuracy. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed using QIIME2′s classify-sklearn algorithm with the Silva 138.1 database.

Alpha diversity was evaluated using Observed Features, Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou’s Evenness (Pielou_e) indices to measure species’ richness and evenness. Statistical significance was determined using the Kruskal-Wallis test, and boxplots were generated for visualization. Beta diversity was analyzed using Unweighted UniFrac, Weighted UniFrac, Bray-Curtis, and Jaccard indices to compare microbial community composition across groups. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) and Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) were used for ordination, and statistical significance for Unweighted and Weighted UniFrac was assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Tukey’s HSD test for multiple comparisons. Dendrograms were generated based on UniFrac distances to illustrate phylogenetic relationships. LEfSe analysis was conducted to identify differentially abundant microbial taxa. The analysis included the Kruskal-Wallis test (p<0.05), pairwise Wilcoxon test, and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA score > 4.0). Results were visualized as LDA score plots and cladograms to highlight enriched taxa and their phylogenetic relationships. All analyses were performed using QIIME2, and results were visualized using ggplot2 in R.

Functional profiling of the microbial communities was conducted using PICRUSt2 to predict gene family abundances and explore the metabolic potential of the microbiota. The analysis included the annotation of functional pathways using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, KEGG Orthology (KO), Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers, and Pfam protein families (PFAM). The results were visualized using heatmaps to display enriched KEGG pathways, volcano plots to highlight differentially abundant functions, and 95% confidence interval plots to assess statistical significance. Data visualization and statistical analyses were carried out using QIIME2, PICRUSt2, and ggplot2 in R.

Statistical analysis

The normality of data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test in Excel 2019 (Version 16, Data Analysis Toolpak add-in). A completely randomized design (CRD) was used for statistical analysis, and data were analyzed using the General Linear Models (GLM) procedure in SAS 9.2 (SAS Institute, 2009). The model included only the level of SP supplementation as the main effect. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts were performed to determine linear and quadratic effects of increasing supplementation levels. Duncan’s multiple range test was used to compare treatment means, with significance set at p<0.05. All immune marker graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism (Version 9, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Effects of SP on Serum Biomarkers

Pro-inflammatory Cytokines. The effects of SP on pro-inflammatory cytokine levels are presented in Fig. 1. The concentration of IL-2 (Fig. 1A) did not differ significantly among treatments (p > 0.05), and no significant linear (p = 0.107) or quadratic (p = 0.871) trends were observed. In contrast, IL-16 level (Fig. 1B) exhibited a significant linear increase (p < 0.001), with all SP-treated groups showing higher concentration compared to the control (p < 0.05). Similarly, IFN-γ level (Fig. 1D) demonstrated a significant linear response (p < 0.05), with the highest level observed in the T3 group, which was significantly greater than that of the control (p < 0.05). However, IL-21 levels (Fig. 1C) remained unchanged among groups.

Effects of SP on gut microbiota composition, diversity, and functional potential

Taxonomic Composition of Gut Microbiota. The microbial composition at the phylum level revealed the dominance of Firmicutes, which increased from 79.25% (Control) to 89.79% (T3 group), while Bacteroidota decreased from 7.98% to 3.79% (Fig. 5). Proteobacteria declined in T3 (0.26%) compared to the control (0.47%). Additionally, Actinobacteria increased in the T3 (13.04%) group compared to the control (11.28%), while Euryarchaeaota remained relatively stable. Cyanobacteria showed a minimal presence across groups but were slightly higher in the T3 group. Verrucomicrobiota exhibited an increase in the T3 group compared to the control. At the genus level, Lactobacillus significantly increased from 25.67% (Control) to 45.75% (T3 group), while Bacteroides decreased (Fig. 6A). Romboutsia also increased in T3 group, whereas Fusobacterium was lower in the T3 group than in the control. Phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 6B) demonstrated distinct microbial clustering between the control and SP-treated groups, confirming compositional changes. Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 7) showed a reduction in unique amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) in the T3 group, with 598 ASVs shared across all groups.

Functional prediction of microbial communities

KEGG Pathway Analysis. KEGG pathway analysis revealed distinct metabolic shifts in the microbial communities of SP-supplemented hens, particularly in the T3 group. Heatmap visualization (Fig. 11) showed a consistent increase in pathway enrichment in T3 compared to the control, especially in pathways associated with carbohydrate metabolism, nucleotide biosynthesis, and microbial cell wall synthesis. Key metabolic pathways such as glycolysis, glycolysis II (from fructose 6-phosphate), and homolactic fermentation were notably upregulated, indicating enhanced microbial energy metabolism. Additional enrichment was observed in peptidoglycan biosynthesis and coenzyme A biosynthesis, along with fatty acid-related pathways like gondoate and cis-vaccenate biosynthesis. Furthermore, nucleotide biosynthetic pathways, including guanosine deoxyribonucleotides de novo biosynthesis II, guanosine nucleotide biosynthesis, and the superpathway of pyrimidine nucleobases salvage, were significantly more abundant in T3. Lipid-related pathways, such as UDP-N-acetylmuramoyl-pentapeptide biosynthesis II (lysine-containing) and CDP-diacylglycerol biosynthesis I, also exhibited elevated activity in the T3 group. Volcano plot analysis (Fig. 12A-C) identified several pathways significantly enriched or depleted between groups. Notably, teichoic acid (poly-glycerol) biosynthesis was significantly downregulated (p = 0.026), suggesting a reduction in microbial components potentially linked to inflammation. In contrast, multiple biosynthetic pathways were upregulated in T3, including the superpathway of histidine, purine, and pyrimidine biosynthesis (p = 0.034) and pyrimidine deoxyribonucleotide de novo biosynthesis (p = 0.030). Other significantly enriched pathways included anhydromuropeptides recycling, the superpathway of heme biosynthesis from glutamate, and degradation of inositol isomers (myo-, chiro-, and scillo-inositol), each contributing to enhanced microbial biosynthetic and catabolic activity. Amino acid metabolism was also significantly affected, with L-histidine degradation I (p = 0.015) and the superpathway of heme biosynthesis from uroporphyrinogen-III (p = 0.047) being upregulated. Additionally, pyruvate fermentation to butanoate (p = 0.048) and the superpathway of L-methionine biosynthesis showed increased activity in T3. These findings were further supported by bar plot analysis (Fig. 13A-B), which confirmed statistically significant upregulation of pathways related to nucleotide metabolism, including guanosine deoxyribonucleotide de novo biosynthesis II common to both T2 and T3 groups. In addition, the T2 group showed enrichment of pyrimidine deoxyribonucleotide de novo biosynthesis I, pyrimidine ribonucleotide biosynthesis, and the pyrimidine salvage pathway, whereas the T3 group exhibited additional upregulation of guanosine nucleotide biosynthesis and PRPP biosynthesis. Carbohydrate metabolism and microbial cell wall biosynthesis pathways were also notably elevated in the T3 group. In contrast, pathways associated with lipid metabolism and oxidative stress modulation were significantly downregulated, indicating a functional microbial shift toward enhanced biosynthetic efficiency and reduced inflammatory potential in response to SP supplementation.

KEGG Orthology (KO) Analysis. KEGG Orthology (KO) analysis revealed significant alterations in microbial functional profiles between the control and SP-treated groups. Heatmap analysis (Fig. 14) indicated significant enrichment of several KO terms in the T3 group, with hierarchical clustering clearly distinguishing it from the control group. Notably, genes associated with membrane transport and transcriptional regulation, such as the putative ABC transport system permease protein, LacI family transcriptional regulator, 23S rRNA pseudouridine synthase, and CAAX protease family protein, were markedly upregulated in T3. Additional enrichment was observed for protein-tyrosine phosphatase, putative transposase, fumarate reductase flavoprotein subunit, and probable phosphoglycerate mutase, suggesting enhanced microbial metabolic activity. Other upregulated KO terms included dipeptidase, acetate kinase, ATP-dependent DNA helicase RecG, and polar amino acid transport system permease protein. Transporters such as the basic amino acid/polyamine antiporter (APA family) and amino acid transporter (AAT family) were also elevated, indicating improved nutrient assimilation and microbial adaptation in response to SP supplementation. Volcano plot visualization (Fig. 15A-C) identified numerous KO terms with statistically significant changes in abundance. Several were significantly downregulated, including triphosphoribosyl-dephospho-CoA synthase (p = 0.0036), citrate lyase subunits alpha, beta, and gamma, citrate (pro-3S)-lyase ligase, lactocepin (p = 0.0054), and sugar-phosphatase (p = 0.0056). These reductions may reflect a shift away from lipid catabolism and protein degradation. In contrast, several KO terms were significantly upregulated, including sulfate permease (p = 0.0100), aspartate ammonia-lyase (p = 0.0104), phosphonoacetaldehyde hydrolase (p = 0.0106), porphobilinogen synthase (p = 0.0110), glutamyl-tRNA reductase (p = 0.0463), 5′-nucleotidase (p = 0.0118), inorganic pyrophosphatase (p = 0.0124), and imidazolonepropionase (p = 0.0120). These enzymes are associated with amino acid metabolism, nucleotide synthesis, microbial growth and adaptability. Bar plot analysis (Fig. 16A-B) confirmed these findings, showing that genes involved in nucleotide metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, glycan degradation, and signal transduction were significantly upregulated in the T1 and T3 groups. These changes suggest that SP supplementation promotes enhanced microbial biosynthesis and regulatory activity. Conversely, genes associated with lipid metabolism and oxidative stress modulation were significantly downregulated in T3, indicating a possible reduction in inflammation-related pathways and a metabolic shift toward improved gut health and functionality.

Enzyme Commission (EC) Analysis. The EC analysis revealed significant alterations in microbial enzymatic activity in response to SP supplementation, particularly in the T3 group. Heatmap visualization (Fig. 17) demonstrated increased abundance of several key enzymes in T3 compared to the control. Among these, acetyl-CoA carboxylase, phenylalanine–tRNA ligase, exodeoxyribonuclease VII, and 2-C-methyl-D-erythritol 4-phosphate cytidylyltransferase were notably elevated, suggesting enhanced lipid biosynthesis, aminoacylation, DNA repair, and isoprenoid biosynthesis, respectively. Additional enzymes, such as glycerol-3-phosphate 1-O-acyltransferase and calcium-transporting ATPase, also showed higher expression in T3, indicating increased membrane lipid synthesis and ion transport. Furthermore, enzymes involved in nucleotide metabolism, including 23S rRNA pseudouridine synthase, Type I site-specific deoxyribonuclease, tRNA (uracil-5-)-methyltransferase, and GTP diphosphokinase, exhibited distinct changes in abundance, reflecting modulation of RNA modification and replication processes. Volcano plot (Fig. 18A-C) showed statistically significant differences in enzyme expression. Several enzymes were significantly upregulated in T3, suggesting increased microbial metabolic activity in amino acid and nucleotide pathways. These included aspartate ammonia-lyase (p = 0.044), phosphonoacetaldehyde hydrolase (p = 0.021), porphobilinogen synthase (p = 0.034), glutamyl-tRNA reductase (p = 0.016), and glutamate-1-semialdehyde 2,1-aminomutase (p = 0.026). Enzymes related to histidine metabolism-such as histidine ammonia-lyase (p = 0.011), urocanate hydratase (p = 0.015), and imidazolonepropionase (p = 0.032)-were also significantly elevated, indicating enhanced amino acid turnover and nitrogen assimilation. Upregulation of carbonic anhydrase (p = 0.034) and epoxyqueuosine reductase (p = 0.016) pointed to altered pH regulation and RNA modification, respectively. Conversely, several enzymes were significantly downregulated in the T3 group, reflecting decreased activity in specific biochemical pathways. These included triphosphoribosyl-dephospho-CoA synthase (p = 0.003), citrate lyase holo-[acyl-carrier protein] synthase (p = 0.004), citrate CoA-transferase (p = 0.004), and citrate (pro-3S)-lyase ligase (p = 0.004), suggesting suppressed citrate and lipid metabolism. Additional reductions were observed for cephalosporin-C deacetylase (p = 0.004), sugar-phosphatase (p = 0.006), lactocepin (p = 0.005), aminoglycoside N(3′)-acetyltransferase (p = 0.007), deoxynucleotide 3′-phosphatase (p = 0.006), and chloride peroxidase (p = 0.007), indicating diminished lipid hydrolysis, antimicrobial resistance, and oxidative stress response. Bar plot analysis (Fig. 19A-B) further confirmed significant differences in enzyme profiles among groups. In particular, homoserine kinase, a key enzyme in the aspartate pathway for essential amino acid biosynthesis, was significantly increased in the T1 group, indicating enhanced microbial capacity for amino acid. In the T3 group, enzymes associated with carbohydrate metabolism, glycan degradation, signal transduction, metal ion homeostasis, and anaerobic respiration were notably upregulated, supporting improved microbial energy production and environmental adaptation. In contrast, enzymes linked to nucleotide metabolism, lipid metabolism, and oxidative stress modulation were significantly downregulated, suggesting microbial metabolic reprogramming toward efficient biosynthesis and reduced oxidative burden in SP-supplemented hens.

Protein Families (PFAM) Analysis. The PFAM analysis provided further insight into the functional shifts in microbial protein domains. Heatmap visualization (Supplementary Fig. 2A) revealed clear hierarchical clustering patterns among the control and treatment groups, indicating distinct treatment-induced changes in the microbial functional landscape. Notably, the T3 group exhibited a pronounced enrichment of specific PFAM domains compared to the control, suggesting enhanced functional capacity in microbial communities exposed to the highest level of SP. The volcano plot (Supplementary Fig. 2B), which presents differentially abundant PFAM domains based on log2 fold change and adjusted p-values, identified a total of 189 significantly altered domains (p < 0.05), of which 146 were upregulated and 43 were downregulated in the T3 group. These shifts reflect broad functional adaptations in response to dietary intervention. Additionally, the bar plot (Supplementary Fig. 2C) illustrated the mean relative abundance of selected PFAM domains between the control and T3 groups, with 95% confidence intervals, further confirming significant differences. While some PFAM domains were more prevalent in the control group, a significant number exhibited increased abundance in T3, emphasizing a dietary SP-driven restructuring of microbial functional potential.

Discussion

This study provides comprehensive evidence that dietary SP supplementation exerts multi-dimensional effects on laying hens, particularly enhancing immunological responses and beneficially modulating gut microbial composition and function. Our findings support the hypothesis that SP, as a rich source of bioactive compounds, has the potential to fine-tune host-microbial interactions through immunomodulation and metabolic reprogramming. These effects were most pronounced at 10g SP/kg group, emphasizing the dose-dependent nature of SP's impact on overall health, disease resistance, and productive performance in laying hens.

Modulation of serum immune markers by SP

The immune system represents the first line of defense against pathogens, and dietary interventions that strengthen immune competence are of considerable interest in poultry production (Wlaźlak et al., 2023: Ayalew et al., 2024). The present study found that SP significantly influenced both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine profiles. The observed changes in pro-inflammatory cytokines indicate the immunostimulatory potential of SP. While IL-2 and IL-21 levels remained unaffected, IL-16 and IFN-γ exhibited significant increases with increasing SP inclusion. IL-16, a cytokine involved in T-cell activation and immune cell recruitment (Cruikshank and Little, 2008; Glass et al., 2006), suggests an enhanced immune response, potentially improving the host’s ability to counteract infections and maintain immune homeostasis (McFadden et al., 2007; Yosri et al., 2024). The significant increase in IFN-γ supports the idea that SP enhances immune function. IFN-γ is a key regulator of Th1 immune responses (Sala et al., 2022). It plays an important role in activating macrophages. This activation helps improve antimicrobial defenses (Wang et al., 2019). IFN-γ also helps regulate adaptive immunity, making the immune system more effective (Frazão et al., 2019; Artamonov et al., 2022). This aligns with earlier reports indicating that phycocyanin and other polysaccharides from SP can stimulate macrophage activity and cytokine production through TLR and NF-κB signaling pathways (Wu et al., 2020; Piovan et al., 2022). The increase in these pro-inflammatory cytokines suggests that SP may enhance immune preparedness against pathogenic challenges, contributing to a more robust immune system in laying hens.

Interestingly, SP supplementation also induced significant changes in anti-inflammatory cytokines, specifically IL-10 and IFNα. The significant increase in IL-10 suggests that SP promotes immune regulation by mitigating excessive inflammatory responses. IL-10 is a key anti-inflammatory cytokine that limits immune-mediated tissue damage while maintaining immune surveillance (Carlini et al., 2023; York et al., 2024). The elevation of IFNα, a cytokine known for its antiviral properties, indicates that SP may enhance the birds’ ability to mount effective antiviral defenses, thereby reducing susceptibility to viral infections. This dual immunomodulatory effect, balancing both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses, is likely mediated by SP’s bioactive compounds such as phycocyanin, β-carotene, and polysaccharides, which have been shown to modulate cytokine secretion and immune cell activity (Yoshimoto et al., 2019). The ability of SP to fine-tune immune responses suggests that it could serve as a functional feed additive, enhancing disease resistance while minimizing the risk of chronic inflammation in poultry.

The significant alterations in chemokine levels further reinforce SP’s role in modulating immune responses. The reduction in MIP-3α levels in SP-treated groups indicates a decrease in inflammatory cell recruitment to the gut, which may contribute to lower levels of gut inflammation and improved gut integrity. In contrast, the increased levels of RANTES and MIP-1β suggest enhanced recruitment of monocytes and lymphocytes, which are essential for maintaining immune surveillance and tissue repair (Rodero et al., 2015). These findings suggest that SP optimizes immune cell trafficking, potentially reducing the risk of chronic inflammation while enhancing host defense mechanisms. By promoting a well-regulated immune response, SP supplementation may help mitigate inflammatory disorders commonly observed in poultry production systems, such as necrotic enteritis and coccidiosis.

The elevated levels of M-CSF and VEGF in SP-treated groups point to enhanced hematopoiesis and angiogenesis, which are crucial for maintaining immune and vascular health during stress or infection. M-CSF is a hematopoietic growth factor that not only drives the proliferation and differentiation of monocytes and macrophages but also enhances their phagocytic and antigen-presenting capacities (Motoyoshi, 1998). These functions are central to mounting an effective immune defense and maintaining homeostasis in tissues undergoing inflammatory or oxidative stress. Meanwhile, VEGF is a multifunctional cytokine that regulates angiogenesis by promoting the survival, proliferation, and migration of endothelial cells (Azimi-Nezhad, 2014). It also increases vascular permeability and supports the formation of new capillaries, which is essential for nutrient delivery and tissue repair following immune challenges. Therefore, the SP-induced upregulation of M-CSF and VEGF suggests an immunometabolic adaptation promoting tissue resilience and rapid recovery during physiological stress (Ferrara, 2004).

Gut microbiota modulation by SP

SP supplementation profoundly reshaped the cecal microbial community structure, favoring beneficial taxa and reducing potentially pathogenic ones. At the phylum level, a dose-dependent increase in Firmicutes and decrease in Bacteroidota and Proteobacteria were observed. This shift is generally associated with improved gut health, as Firmicutes include many SCFA-producing bacteria, while Proteobacteria and Bacteroidota can be markers of dysbiosis and inflammation (Turnbaugh et al., 2006; Shin et al., 2015). At the genus level, the enrichment of Lactobacillus and Romboutsia suggests that SP promotes probiotic populations. Lactobacillus, known for its role in maintaining gut pH, enhancing mucin production, and modulating immune responses, likely benefited from the polysaccharide and amino acid profile of SP (Górska et al., 2016; Valeriano et al., 2017). The reduction of Fusobacterium, often linked to mucosal inflammation, supports SP’s anti-inflammatory effects on the gut ecosystem (Engevik et al., 2021).

Alpha diversity metrics, including observed features, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices, showed a non-significant reduction in microbial richness and diversity in SP-treated groups, this trend may reflect ecological specialization and a shift toward dominance of health-promoting bacterial taxa. Such a microbial profile may not compromise functionality but instead enhance community stability and resistance to pathogen colonization. Importantly, beta diversity analysis using Weighted UniFrac distances revealed distinct clustering patterns between the control and SP-treated groups, particularly the T3 group. These confirm that SP induces microbiome restructuring, likely contributing to improved gut stability and resilience against perturbations. Moreover, the differential microbial composition analysis using LEfSe identified significant enrichment of Lactobacillaceae and Limosilactobacillus in the T3 group, further confirming that SP enhances the population of beneficial bacteria. These genera are known for their role in producing antimicrobial peptides and SCFAs, which support gut health and immune function (Montgomery et al., 2024). This indicates that SP induced significant shifts in microbial community structure, driven by alterations in phylogenetic composition and relative abundance rather than total richness alone. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that dietary interventions can modulate microbial composition more markedly than diversity metrics alone reveal (Lozupone et al., 2012; McCullough et al., 2025). Therefore, the restructuring observed in the SP-treated birds reflects a beneficial remodeling of the gut ecosystem, supporting improved nutrient utilization and immune modulation.

Beyond compositional changes, SP supplementation induced pronounced shifts in microbial metabolic potential. KEGG pathway enrichment in the T3 group revealed upregulation of glycolysis, pyruvate fermentation, amino acid biosynthesis (e.g., histidine and methionine), and coenzyme A production. These changes suggest enhanced microbial energy metabolism and biosynthetic activity, likely supporting mucosal health and immune modulation (Louis and Flint, 2009; Rooks and Garrett, 2016). Increased microbial biosynthesis of nucleotides and amino acids could contribute to epithelial renewal and tight junction integrity, while upregulation of SCFA-related pathways like butanoate metabolism is associated with anti-inflammatory effects and improved gut barrier function (Ramos Meyers et al., 2022). Conversely, the downregulation of teichoic acid biosynthesis and lipid metabolism pathways indicates a metabolic shift away from pro-inflammatory components and lipid peroxidation (Andersen, 2022).

The KO and EC analyses further substantiated the microbial compositional shifts by highlighting functionally relevant changes in microbial gene expression and enzymatic activity. Key KEGG Orthologs related to nutrient transport, transcriptional regulation, and redox balance were significantly enriched in the T3 group, indicating that SP supplementation promotes microbial functions associated with enhanced adaptability and metabolic efficiency. For instance, the upregulation of sulfate permease, acetate kinase, and ATP-dependent helicases implies increased capacity for energy metabolism, nutrient uptake, and DNA stability under oxidative stress (Chia et al., 2018). Simultaneously, the downregulation of citrate lyase subunits and oxidative stress-related enzymes reflects a potential reduction in lipid catabolism and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (Migita et al., 2013; Quijano et al., 2016), likely facilitated by the antioxidant properties of SP-derived compounds such as phycocyanin and β-carotene.

In line with these findings, PFAM domain analysis revealed significant enrichment of protein families involved in DNA repair mechanisms, signal transduction pathways, and membrane transport systems. Such alterations not only support the microbial community's resilience under dietary modulation but also enhance host-microbe interactions essential for gut homeostasis and immune regulation (Rooks and Garrett, 2016). Together, these functional adaptations suggest that SP supplementation fosters a more efficient, less inflammatory, and metabolically active gut microbiota, thereby creating a microbiological environment conducive to improved poultry health and performance.

In addition to the biological findings, it is important to acknowledge the economic and production-scale aspects that influence the commercial viability of SP as a feed additive. At present, SP remains more expensive than conventional feed proteins such as soybean meal, with economic analyses indicating that inclusion beyond about 5% of poultry diets is not yet financially viable (Nambahu et al., 2024). Current production relies mainly on open pond and photobioreactor systems, and while technological advances and integration with low-cost substrates or waste streams are gradually reducing costs, further scaling is needed for widespread feed use (Saratale et al., 2022; Razzak et al., 2024). Commercially, SP can be applied either as whole biomass, providing a broad spectrum of nutrients and bioactive compounds, or as concentrated extracts of functional molecules such as phycocyanin or carotenoids, which may deliver similar benefits at lower dietary inclusion levels (Liang et al., 2009; Spínola et al., 2024b). A promising pathway is the biorefinery model, where high-value compounds are extracted for nutraceutical markets and the remaining protein-rich biomass is incorporated into animal diets, improving both sustainability and cost-effectiveness (Dutta et a., 2025). Thus, while large-scale substitution of staple feed proteins is unlikely in the near term, moderate inclusion of SP biomass or its concentrated actives represents a practical and commercially feasible strategy in antibiotic-free poultry production.

Conclusion

This study shows the practical potential of SP as a functional feed additive for laying hens, offering a sustainable solution to enhance health and immunity. The immunomodulatory effects and the restructuring of gut microbiota, including enrichment of beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus, collectively contribute to improved disease resistance and intestinal stability. Functionally, the upregulation of metabolic pathways related to energy, nucleotide, and amino acid metabolism further supports enhanced nutrient utilization and gut homeostasis. These benefits are particularly valuable in commercial egg production systems where birds are frequently exposed to environmental stressors and enteric challenges. In the current era of increasing restrictions on antibiotic use, heightened consumer demand for residue-free animal products, and the need for sustainable feed inputs, SP emerges as a natural, protein-rich, and bioactive ingredient that aligns with industry trends. Its integration into layer diets not only supports bird health and welfare but also contributes to safer, more resilient, and eco-conscious poultry production practices. Thus, SP supplementation, especially at 10g SP/kg, offers a promising strategy to meet the evolving demands of modern poultry farming while reducing dependence on synthetic additives. Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects and evaluating the long-term benefits of SP supplementation on disease resistance, intestinal integrity, and production performance under commercial conditions.
