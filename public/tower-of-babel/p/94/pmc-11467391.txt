HPV16 E6-induced M2 macrophage polarization in the cervical microenvironment via exosomal miR-204-5p
Authors: Xiaohang Chen, Yanan Liu, Xiaojin Luo, Teng Pan, Tong Zhang, Liang Hu, Buling Wu, Weiqiang Liu, Fengxiang Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The persistent infection of high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) and the progression of cervical cancer necessitate the involvement of microenvironmental immunity. As cervical lesions advance, there is an observed increase in the infiltration of type 2 (M2) macrophages. However, the precise mechanism driving this increased infiltration of M2 macrophages remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the role of exosomes in polarising M2 macrophages in cervical lesions associated with HPV E6. Through the analysis of bioinformatics data and clinical specimens, we discovered a positive correlation between HPV E6/E7 mRNA copy number and the level of M2 macrophage infiltration. Exosomes derived from HPV E6 overexpressed (HPV E6+) cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC) cells were found to induce the polarisation of macrophages towards M2 type. Specifically, miR-204-5p, enriched in HPV E6 + CESC exosomes, was transported into macrophages and triggered M2 macrophage polarisation by inhibiting JAK2. The clinical relevance of exosomal miR-204-5p in the progression of cervical lesions was validated through serum samples from 35 cases. Exosomal miR-204-5p emerges as a critical factor influencing M2 macrophage polarisation and is correlated with the severity of cervical lesions. Consequently, miR-204-5p could be used as a potential treatment and a candidate biomarker for cervical lesions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74399-0.

Introduction

Cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC) ranks among the most prevalent cancers affecting women, with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) identified as the primary pathogenic factor. Over the past two decades, the incidence of CESC has increased, accompanied by a downward shift in the age of onset1. The widespread adoption of combined HPV genotype screening and liquid-based cytology has significantly enhanced the early detection and prevention of CESC at the cervical squamous intraepithelial lesion (SIL) stage. Consequently, the incidence of invasive cervical cancer has shown a gradual decline2. SIL is categorised into low-grade (LSIL) and high-grade (HSIL) lesions. Persistent HPV infection is a critical risk factor for SIL recurrence after treatment. However, only a minority of HPV-infected individuals ultimately progress to CESC, suggesting the involvement of additional internal and external cofactors in the progression of cervical lesions3,4. Given that persistent HPV infection can modulate immune cell function to evade immune clearance, it is necessary to explore the influence of HPV on the microenvironmental immunity of cervical lesions5.

The expression of HPV oncogenes E6 and E7 plays a crucial role in the development of CESC6. Extensive research has documented that E6 and E7 affects the biological behaviour of host cells, yet the direct impact of HPV E6 and E7 on immune cells remains poorly understood. Previous studies conducted by our group have shown that HPV can induce changes in macrophage polarisation7. Therefore, this study aims to further investigate whether HPV oncogenes are involved in these processes.

Macrophages constitute the largest proportion of immune cells within the cervical microenvironment. Influenced by neighbouring cells and cytokines, macrophages can adopt various phenotypes and display distinct functional states. Tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) are primarily categorised into two types: classically activated M1 and alternatively activated M2 macrophages. M1 macrophages are pivotal in Th1 immune response and anti-tumour activities, whereas M2 macrophages facilitate tissue repair and promote tumour growth8. Although TAMs-targeted immunotherapies have entered clinical trials, the direct effects of HPV on macrophage behaviour remain underexplored9. Understanding how HPV influences macrophage polarisation could elucidate the interplay between cervical cells and immune cells, providing new therapeutic strategies for HPV persistent infection. Consequently, this study investigates the impact of HPV oncogenes on macrophages in CESC.

Exosomes (EXOs), vesicular bodies ranging from 30 to 150 nm in diameter, were first identified by Pan JS in 198310. Numerous studies have shown that exosomal miRNAs modulate the immune microenvironment by targeting various immune cells and cytokines11. Nevertheless, the specific effects of exosomal miRNAs on immune cells within the cervical lesion microenvironment, particularly in the context of HPV persistent HPV infection, are not well defined.

In this study, we observed that infiltrating macrophages exhibit distinct functionalities associated with varying grades of cervical lesions. Notably, HPV E6 was found to induce macrophage polarisation towards the M2 type, which correlated with the expression of exosomal miR-204-5p. Additionally, variations in serum exosomal miR-204-5p levels were noted among patients with different grades of cervical lesions. Our findings suggest that miR-204-5p could serve as a potential marker for prognosticating SIL progression and targeting M2 macrophage-based immunotherapies.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatic analyses

The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with various HPV statuses in human cervical lesions were analysed. Our study utilised bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA database, which included 278 HPV-positive (HPV+) cancer samples, 19 HPV-negative (HPV-) cancer samples and three normal cervical samples. Additionally, the GEO database provided RNA-seq data from 13 CESC, six HSIL, four LSIL and two normal cervical epithelia samples (GSE223804 and GSE215744). For correlation analyses among different groups in the TCGA data, the online tool THInCR (https://thincr.ca/) was utilised12. The infiltration of immune cells in the bulk RNA-seq data were analysed using R package xCell. Moreover, the single-cell RNA-seq data from nine cases of cervical lesions, including five CESC, two HSIL, two HPV + and two HPV- normal cervical tissues, were sourced from the GEO database (GSE208653). Cells that failed quality control criteria (≤ 200 genes or ≥ 20,000 genes, > 10% mitochondrial genes) were excluded. Data normalisation involved principal component analysis (PCA) based on the top 2000 highly variable genes using R package Seurat. R package Harmony was then applied to correct for batch effects, and dimensional reduction was visualised by the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) method. The identification of signature genes for various cell populations was performed using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat. Cell populations were annotated using a combination of published marker genes and functional characterisation derived from signature genes. We further assessed the potential biological functions of cell subpopulations using R package clusterProfiler. Data visualisation was conducted with R packages ggplot2 and scRNAtoolVis.

Sample collection and ethical approval

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues from 112 cases of human cervical lesions were collected at Longgang District Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital of Shenzhen City from September 2021 to June 2022. The specimens were categorised based on the pathological diagnosis from biopsy tissues. Additionally, 35 serum samples were collected from the fresh peripheral blood of hospitalised gynaecological patients and healthy females undergoing routine health examinations from September to December 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, adhering to the Helsinki Declaration principles. The Ethics Committee of Longgang District Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital of Shenzhen City approved the usage of these samples (Approval No. LGFYYXLLQ-2021–006). Patients with a history of cancer, hysterectomy, clinical symptoms of pregnancy or recent pregnancy termination within eight weeks were excluded from the study. The collected serum was stored in -80℃ until analysis.

High-risk HPV E6/E7 mRNA detection

Approximately 7–10 slices (size 15 mm × 15 mm × 5 μm) of FFPE tissues were scraped off and transferred into centrifuge tubes. The copy numbers of high-risk HPV E6/E7 mRNA in the cervical tissues were identified by QuantiVirus system (Kodia Biotechnology Inc., China) via a DNA-mRNA capture hybridisation method. Samples and corresponding probes for 14 high-risk HPV types (HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66 and 68) were hybridised in microplates. After hybridisation, the amplified probes formed a branched-DNA complex with a solid-phase coated probe. The luminescent probe, labelled with alkaline phosphatase, facilitated the detection of luminescence using the QuantiVirus luminescent instrument. The luminescence values were analysed using Diacarta software, translating the relative light units into copy numbers. A positive result was defined as an HPV E6/E7 mRNA copy number of ≥ 1.

Cell culture and treatment

The HPV- human cervical squamous carcinoma (CESC) cell line C33A and the human monocytic leukaemia cell line THP-1 were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). C33A cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) containing 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone, USA), penicillin and streptomycin (100 U/ml, Gibco, USA). THP-1 cells were maintained in THP-1 specific medium (Procell, China). For exosome extraction, C33A cells were initially cultured in complete medium overnight. Upon reaching 70% confluence, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 10% exosome-free FBS (SBI, USA). After continuous culture for 24 h, the culture medium (CM) was collected, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min and processed for exosome extraction.

Macrophage differentiation in THP-1 cells was initiated with 50 ng/ml phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 24 h. After washing three times with PBS, the cells were cultured in complete medium for additional 24 h. The medium was then supplemented with either 20 ng/ml IFN-γ (for M1 macrophage) or a combination of 20 ng/ml IL-4 and 20 ng/ml IL-13 (for M2 macrophage) for another 24 h. A combination of 20 ng/ml IFNγ and 30 µM Ruxolitinib (JAK2 inhibitor, MCE, USA) was used for JAK2 inhibition of M1 macrophages. For exosome treatment, the THP-1 cell medium was replaced with RPMI-1640 (Hyclone, USA) containing 10% exosome-free FBS.

Cell transfection

Plasmids pcDNA-HPV16E6 and pcDNA-HPV16E7 were synthesised by Genepharm (Suzhou, China). For overexpression of HPV oncogenes, C33A cells were transfected with lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). MiRNA mimics were obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and transfected into THP-1 cells using the advanced DNA/RNA high-efficiency transfection reagent (Zeta life, USA), with 50 nM of mimics per well in cell culture plates.

Exosome extraction in cell culture medium and serum

Exosome were isolated from the cell culture medium using the exoEasy Maxi kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. To generate E6-C33A-derived exosomes lacking miR-204-5p expression, the chemically modified antisense oligonucleotides targeting miR-204-5p (Sangon Biotech, China) and HPV E6 were transfected into C33A cells for 24 h. Serum exosomes were isolated from 200 µl of serum per sample through the total exosome isolation reagent (Thermo Fisher, USA). A BCA detection kit (Beoytime, China) was employed for rapid quantification of exosomes.

Exosome identification and labelling

Exosomes were characterised by an electron microscope (HITACHI, Japan) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). The size and concentration of the exosomes were determined using the NanoFCM system (Flow NanoAnalyzer, China). For labelling, exosomes were resuspended in Dilutant C and mixed with PKH26 fluorescent dye (Solarbio, China). The staining reaction was stopped by adding an equal volume of PBS containing 5% FBS after a five-minute incubation. The labelled exosomes were then re-isolated using the exoEasy Maxi Kit. Post incubation of THP-1 cells with exosomes for 24 h, cells were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formaldehyde and analysed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAsimple total RNA kit (TIANGEN, China) and reverse-transcribed to cDNA using All-In-One 5X RT MasterMix (abm, Canada). The expression of target genes was quantified using PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen, China) with the following primer sequences: TNFA-F: 5′-GAG GCC AAG CCC TGG TAT G-3′, TNFA-R: 5′-CGG GCC GAT TGA TCT CAG C-3′, ARG1-F: 5′-TGG ACA GAC TAG GAA TTG GCA-3′, ARG1-R: 5′-CCA GTC CGT CAA CAT CAA AAC T-3′; JAK2-F: 5′-ATC CAC CCA ACC ATG TCT TCC-3′, JAK2-R: 5′-ATT CCA TGC CGA TAG GCT CTG-3′. TBP was detected as mRNA internal control: TBP-F: 5′-CAC GGC ACT GAT TTT CAG TTC T-3′, TBP-R: 5′-TTC TTG CTG CCA GTC TGG ACT-3′.

Exosomal miRNA was isolated using the miRNeasy Micro kit (Qiagen, Germany), reverse transcribed using the miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit by stem-loop (Vazyme, China) and quantified with the miRNA Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) using the following sequences: miR-204-5p-F: 5′-CAC TGC CTT TCC CTT TGT CAT CC-3′, miR-204-5p-RT: 5′-GTC GTA TCC AGT GCA GGG TCC GAG GTA TTC GCA CTG GAT ACG ACA GGC AT-3′; miRNA universal R: 5′-ATC CAG TGC AGG GTC CGA GG-3′; U6-F: 5′-GCT TCG GCA GCA CAT ATA CTA AAA T-3′, U6-R: 5′-CGC TTC ACG AAT TTG CGT GTC AT-3′, U6-RT: 5′-CGC TTC ACG AAT TTG CGT GTC AT-3′.

Cytokines detection

THP-1 cells were continuously cultured for 24 h after treatment. Subsequently, the CM was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to remove cell debris. The secretion levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-10 in the CM were assessed using immunoassay kits (BD, US). Additionally, the secretion of IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17, IFN-γ and TNF-α in serum cytokines was analysed using a multiple cytokine detection kit (Uni-Medica, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

The FFPE slices of cervical lesions and cell climbing films were preserved in 10% neutral formaldehyde and evaluated via IHC, as previously described7. The rabbit anti-CD68, anti-iNOS and anti-CD163 primary antibodies were purchased from Abcam (USA). Briefly, sections were blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min and antigen retrieval was performed by autoclaving in citrate repair buffer for 15 min. Then sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Secondary antibody incubation was conducted using two-step IHC reagents (Zhongshan Goldenbridge, China), followed by counterstaining with haematoxylin. Immunostaining was evaluated across ten randomly selected high-power fields (Zeiss, Germany). Two independent observers, blinded to patient details, reviewed and scored the slides. The scores were based on the proportion of positively stained cells and staining intensity as follows: 0 (no positive cells), 1 (less than 10% positive cells), 2 (10–50% positive cells) and 3 (more than 50% positive cells). Staining intensity was categorised as: 0 (no staining), 1 (weak staining, light yellow), 2 (moderate staining, yellow brown) and 3 (strong staining, brown). The staining index was calculated by multiplying the staining intensity score by the proportion score. This method facilitated the evaluation of IHC expression by calculating the staining index, which consisted of the scores 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 9.

Exosomal small RNA-seq and data analysis

Exosomal small RNA-seq was conducted for three replicates each of the E6-C33A and NC-C33A groups by BGI Tech, yielding an average of 25.33 million reads per sample on the BGI DNBSEQ platform. The generated sequencing data were deposited in the CNGB Sequence Archive of China National GeneBank DataBase (CNGBdb, https://db.cngb.org/) under the accession number CNP000560413. MiRNA targets were predicted using miRDB and TargetScan.

Western blotting

Protein extraction from cells was performed using RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beoytime, China). Then, the proteins were denatured by boiling them at 95 °C for 10 min before running SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. The proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes and blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-JAK2 antibodies (CST, USA). After they were incubated with peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) antibodies (Keygen, China) at room temperature for 2 h, visualisation was achieved using the Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio-rad, USA).

Luciferase analyses

Reporter plasmids including the wide-type and mutated 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of JAK2 was constructed by inserting the 3′ UTR downstream of the luciferase open reading frame (Sangon Biotech, China). These cloned plasmids were co-transfected with either miR-204-5p mimics or negative control (NC)-mimics into 293T cells using lipofectamine 3000 in 24-well plates. After 48 h of incubation, Firefly luciferase and Renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially using the dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Sangon, China) and the SpectraMax L chemiluminometer (Molecular Devices, USA).

Statistical analyses

For statistical analysis and visualisation, R version 4.1.0 and GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., LaJolla, CA) were used. A differential analysis of the three groups was conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with an adjusted p-value of less than 0.05. Additionally, a differential analysis between all sets of two groups was conducted using the students’ T-test, with a p-value less of than 0.05. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was employed for correlation analysis.

Results

HPV induced macrophage polarisation to M2 type in the cervical microenvironment

To elucidate the variation in macrophage infiltration among different grades of cervical lesions, we initially analysed immune-associated genes in CESC with different HPV statuses from the TCGA database. It was found that the expression of macrophage marker CD68 was notably higher in the HPV + CESC cohort. Similarly, the expression of anti-inflammatory IL10 was significantly elevated in the HPV + CESC cohort. In contrast, the expression of pro-inflammatory IL6 was higher in the surrounding non-cancerous tissues (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, immune cell scores for various cervical lesions in the GEO database were examined using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis method. Notably, the ratio of M1 to M2 macrophages increased significantly in LSIL tissues, whereas this ratio decreased progressively in HSIL, stage IA and stage IB/IIA CESC tissues, indicating an increased infiltration of M2 macrophages as cervical lesions worsen (Fig. 1B).

To validate the roles of infiltrating macrophage subpopulations across different grades of cervical lesions, we conducted further analysis using single-cell transcriptomic data from nine cases of cervical lesions with diverse HPV statuses. After rigorous data quality control and normalisation, CD14+/CD68 + macrophages were identified and subdivided into eight subpopulations (Fig. 1C). The distribution of these macrophage subpopulations varied significantly among the different lesion grades. In HPV− normal cervical tissues, clusters 0 and 2 macrophages predominated, while in HPV + normal cervical tissues, clusters 1 and 2 macrophages made up the primary subpopulations. HPV + HSIL tissues predominantly featured infiltration by clusters 2, 1 and 3 subpopulations. And HPV + CESC were mainly infiltrated by clusters 0 and 4 subpopulations. Functional exploration, guided by the top five differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and gene ontology analysis, highlighted distinct roles for each macrophage subpopulation (Fig. 1D, left). A comparison of the proportions of macrophage subpopulations across various cervical lesions revealed a reduced proportion of cluster 5 macrophages and an increased proportion of cluster 4 macrophages in HPV + tissues compared to HPV- normal cervical tissues (Fig. 1D, right). The cluster 4 macrophages predominantly expressed genes linked to the ERK1 and ERK2 pathways. Given that ERK/STAT3 signalling is closely associated with M2 macrophage polarisation, we speculate that cluster 4 macrophages are a subpopulation linked to tumour progression14. Cluster 5 macrophages exhibit high expression of genes that positively regulate type II interferon production, suggesting their role in antiviral defence. As HPV infection persists, the cervical microenvironment shows an increasing infiltration of anti-inflammatory macrophages and a decreasing presence of pro-inflammatory macrophages.

To verify the accuracy of these bioinformatic analyses, we retrospectively collected 112 cervical lesion specimens and clinical information from patients, including 30 cases of chronic cervicitis, 20 cases of LSIL, 38 cases of HSIL and 24 cases of CESC. The age of patients ranged from 25 to 64 years (mean 38.50 years, SD 9.53 years). High-risk HPV E6/E7 mRNA copy numbers were measured in these cervical tissue samples. The prevalence of HPV infection was 33.33% in chronic cervicitis, 80.00% in LSIL, 86.84% in HSIL and 91.66% in CESC (Table 1). The E6/E7 mRNA copy numbers were 415.15 ± 47.45 in chronic cervicitis, 3473.56 ± 382.85 in LSIL, 6374.05 ± 782.20 in HSIL and 9802.13 ± 1071.31 in CESC, showing a significant increase as cervical lesions worsened.

Subsequently, IHC analysis was employed to compare the differential expression of macrophage subpopulation markers, including CD68 and CD163 (an M2 macrophage marker). The results revealed a significant enrichment of M2 macrophages in HSIL tissues compared to chronic cervicitis and LSIL tissues (Fig. 1E).

HPV E6 overexpressed (E6+) CESC-derived exosomes induced M2 macrophage polarisation

To further investigate the influence of HPV E6 and E7 on macrophage differentiation, THP-1 cells were exposed to 20% CM from HPV- CESC cell line C33A, which overexpressed HPV E6, E7 and E6/E7, respectively. THP-1 cells treated with CM from HPV E6 + C33A cells exhibited increased expression of CD163, alongside decreased expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS, an M1 macrophage marker). Conversely, CM from HPV E7 overexpressed C33A cells showed minimal effects on the expression levels of CD163 or iNOS. CM from HPV E6/E7 overexpressed C33A cells showed effects comparable to those observed with HPV E6 alone (data not shown).

Given the potential confounding influence of cytokines in CM on macrophage polarisation, we also compared cytokine differences among HPV E6+, E7 + and E6/E7 + C33A cells. We observed an increased secretion of IL-10 and a decreased secretion of TNF-α in the CM from E6-C33A compared to E7-C33A. Additionally, higher level of TNF-α and lower levels of IL-6 and IL-10 were observed in the CM from E6/E7-C33A compared to E6-C33A (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, the relatively low concentration of cytokines in CM posed challenges in determining their specific roles in macrophage polarisation. Subsequently, E6-C33A cells were established, and exosomes were identified via TEM and NTA analyses (Fig. 2A, B). TEM analysis revealed exosomes with a double concave circular morphology, ranging from 50 to 150 nm in diameter. NTA analysis confirmed vesicle sizes of approximately 60–100 nm without contamination from other vesicle types. To assess the uptake of E6-C33A exosomes by macrophages, PKH26-labelled exosomes were co-cultured with macrophages for 24 h. The obvious red fluorescence was observed in the cytoplasm of THP-1 cells, indicating successful internalisation (Fig. 2C).

Subsequent macrophage polarisation assays demonstrated that macrophages treated with exosomes secreted by E6-C33A cells exhibited a significant decrease in TNFA mRNA expression (an M1 marker) and an increase in ARG1 mRNA (an M2 marker) (Fig. 2D). IHC revealed a significantly higher expression of CD163 and a lower expression of iNOS in macrophages treated with E6-C33A cell-derived exosomes compared to those treated with exosomes derived from C33A cells overexpressing negative plasmids (NC-C33A) (Fig. 2E, F). In addition, increased secretions of IL-10 and decreased secretions of TNF-α and IL-6 were observed in macrophages treated with E6-C33A derived exosomes (Fig. 2G). The expression levels of CD163 and iNOS in macrophages treated with exosomes from either E6-C33A or E6/E7-C33A cells were similar (Supplementary Fig. 2A). And a significantly higher expression of CD163 in macrophages treated with exosomes from E6/E7-C33A cells was observed. A decreased in IL-6 secretion was also observed in macrophages treated with exosomes from E6/E7-C33A compared to E7-C33A cells (Supplementary Fig. 2B), suggesting that these exosomes could induce M2 macrophage polarisation.

To ascertain the role of E6-C33A-derived exosomes in macrophage polarisation, the exosome generation inhibitor GW4869 was employed to treat E6-C33A and NC-C33A cells. IHC and cytokine assays revealed a significant impairment in the induction of M2 macrophages under E6-C33A-derived exosome inhibition conditions (Fig. 2H–J). These findings suggest that HPV E6 + CESC-derived exosomes induce macrophage polarisation towards the M2 type.

HPV E6 alters the exosomal miRNA expression profile of CESC

MiRNAs are the most abundant composition of exosomes and play a crucial role in modulating the immune microenvironment, as can be seen in numerous reports15,16. Therefore, we conducted a comparative analysis of miRNA expression profiles between E6-C33A- and NC-C33A-derived exosomes using small RNA-seq. PCA analysis revealed distinct differences between the miRNA profiles of E6-C33A and NC-C33A exosomes (Fig. 3A). In total, 379 miRNAs were identified, of which six were up-regulated and 15 were down-regulated in E6-C33A-derived exosomes compared to NC-C33A-derived exosomes (Fig. 3B, C). Then these differentially expressed miRNA sequences were validated selectively through real-time PCR. Exosomal miRNA sequences such as miR-92b-3p, miR-143-3p, miR-204-5p, miR-660-5p, and miR-3259-3p were found to be enriched in E6-C33A-derived exosomes, while miR-7-5p, miR-93-5p, miR-139-5p, miR-374c-3p and miR-3180-3p were enriched in NC-C33A-derived exosomes (Fig. 3D). Concurrently, we examined cellular miRNAs expression, which exhibited a similar trend to that observed in exosomes.

Next, we aimed to explore the exosomal mechanisms involved in macrophage polarisation. Utilising small RNA-seq data, we identified that miR-204-5p was significantly upregulated in THP-1 cells exposed to E6-C33A-derived exosomes (Fig. 3E). Given the involvement of miR-204-5p in macrophage polarisation, we further investigated its potential role in the induction of M2 macrophage polarisation by HPV E617,18. MiR-204-5p was significantly enriched in E6-C33A-derived exosomes and remained stable for up to 72 h. Additionally, miR-204-5p levels were substantially higher in exosomes from E6/E7-C33A cells compared to those from NC-C33A, whereas no significant difference was observed between E7-C33A and NC-C33A cells (Supplementary Fig. 3). To ascertain whether miR-204-5p could be transferred to macrophages, E6-C33A-derived exosomes were co-cultured with macrophages for 24 h. The data demonstrated higher levels of miR-204-5p in macrophages treated with E6-C33A-derived exosomes compared to those treated with NC-C33A-derived exosomes, highlighting the transferability of miR-204-5p from HPV E6 + CESC-derived exosomes to macrophages (Fig. 3F).

MiR-204-5p downregulates JAK2 to induce M2 macrophage polarisation

To elucidate the functional role of exosomal miR-204-5p in M2 macrophage polarisation, we utilised miR-204-5p mimics to modulate miRNA levels in macrophages (Fig. 4A). IHC assays revealed that miR-204-5p significantly upregulated the expression of CD163 while downregulating the expression of iNOS in macrophages (Fig. 4B). In contrast, the group treated with E6 + miR-204-5p-inhibitor exosomes showed elevated iNOS expression and reduced CD163 expression. Furthermore, immunoassays indicated that miR-204-5p markedly upregulated the secretion of IL-10 and downregulated TNF-α and IL-6 secretions in macrophages (Fig. 4C). The inhibition of miR-204-5p in E6-C33A-derived exosomes disrupted the enhanced secretion of IL-10 and the reduced secretion of TNF-α. These findings suggest that the blockade of miR-204-5p negates the M2 macrophage polarisation induced by E6-C33A-derived exosomes.

To explore the mechanism underlying miR-204-5p-induced macrophage polarisation, we identified JAK2 as a potential target gene for miR-204-5p using the TargetScan and miRDB databases (Fig. 4D). Luciferase assays confirmed the binding site of miR-204-5p to the JAK2 3′ UTR, leading to a significant inhibition of luciferase activity (Fig. 4E). Real-time PCR and Western blot analyses further revealed that miR-204-5p significantly downregulated the expression of JAK2 (Fig. 4F, G). HPV E6 + CESC derived exosomes inhibited the expression of JAK2 mRNA in a manner similar to M2 macrophages (Fig. 4H). In addition, the inhibition of JAK2 impaired the expression of TNFA mRNA in M1 macrophages (Fig. 4I). These results indicate that miR-204-5p can inhibit the polarisation of M2 macrophages through JAK2 inhibition.

Exosomal mir-204-5p expression correlates with cervical lesion severity

To delve into the clinical significance of miR-204-5p in cervical lesions, we analysed its expression in peripheral blood exosomes from patients with varying grades of cervical lesions. A total of 35 specimens from seven healthy females, eight LSIL and 20 HSIL patients were analysed. The age of patients ranged from 29 to 67 years (mean 40.40 years, SD 9.19 years). The prevalence of HPV infection was 14.29% in healthy individuals, 75.00% in LSIL and 100.00% in HSIL cases. Real-time PCR data indicated a significant increase in the levels of exosomal miR-204-5p in the serum of HSIL patients compared to healthy females (Fig. 5A). Additionally, we investigated the expression levels of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines in the serum. Our findings reveal the up-regulation of type I and type II cytokines, including IL-2, IL-4, IL-10, IL-17, TNF-α and IFN-γ, in HSIL patients compared to healthy females (Fig. 5B). However, it is noteworthy that the cytokine levels in the serum were relatively low, possibly due to the constraints of the cervical region not being fully reflected in peripheral blood. Subsequently, we explored the correlation between miR-204-5p and these cytokines. Our study did not find a significant correlation among miR-204-5p and cytokines in serum (Fig. 5C).

Discussions

With the widespread implementation of CESC, cervical lesions are frequently detected at the SIL stage. However, there remains a relatively high incidence of recurrence after treatment, particularly in patients with persistent HR-HPV infection19. The persistence of HPV is closely related with immune evasion20. HPV status, categorised into episomal and integrated types based on the presence of HPV DNA in host cells, plays a crucial role in immune modulation. Existing studies suggest that HPV integration into host genes can disrupt immune surveillance. For instance, HPV integration into the CD274 gene, encoding PD-L1, can inhibit the cytotoxic effects of T cells by inducing structural mutations in PD-L21. Moreover, HPV E6/E7 proteins can interfere with innate immunity by evading recognition by antigen-presenting cells through interactions with interferon regulatory factors22. However, due to the variability in integration length, region and locus of HPV genes within the host genome, defining the immune status solely based on HPV integration is challenging.

Since 2017, several countries, including the Netherlands, Sweden and the United Kingdom, have initiated primary HPV screening. Recently, the detection of HPV E6/E7 mRNA has emerged as a more specific method for estimating transcriptional activity, enabling the effective early prevention of cervical cancer23. The splicing patterns of HPV E6 transcripts are associated with severe cervical dysplasia, and higher HPV E6 viral loads correlate with the increased severity of cervical lesions24. Nonetheless, the impact of HPV E6 on the microenvironmental immune cells and its potential as a marker for evaluating the immune status of CESC require further investigation.

Combining bioinformatics analysis with clinical findings, we observed a significant decrease in the ratio of M1/M2 macrophages in the cervical microenvironment with increasing severity of cervical lesions. Based on previous studies, our hypothesis suggests that HPV E6 may induce macrophage polarisation towards M2 type via extracellular pathways. To eliminate potential confounders such as cytokines or soluble metabolites that could influence macrophage polarisation, we analyzed cytokine secretions from C33A cells overexpressing HPV oncogenes. The relatively low concentrations of cytokines suggest that these cytokines might not be the primary drivers of the macrophage polarisation induced by HPV E6. Additionally, we conducted targeted metabolomics to examine the differences in 540 soluble metabolites secreted by E6-C33A and NC-C33A cells. Only one differentially expressed metabolite was identified, and its validation is ongoing. Consequently, our study shifted focus towards exosomal mechanisms. Subsequent small RNA-seq and validation experiments demonstrated that miR-204-5p was enriched in exosomes derived from HPV E6 + CESC and induced macrophage polarisation towards M2 type through JAK2 inhibition. Besides, exosomal miR-204-5p was significantly up-regulated in the peripheral blood of HSIL patients compared to healthy individuals. These findings suggest that miR-204-5p holds promise as both a potential treatment strategy and a candidate biomarker for predicting the severity of cervical lesions.

Previous studies have indicated that E6/E7 overexpressed keratinocytes or CESC cell line-derived exosomes can facilitate tumour progression and angiogenesis25,26. Furthermore, exosomal miR-223 from SiHa cells has been found to induce the secretion of IL-6 from macrophages, establishing a positive feedback loop via STAT3 hyperactivation to promote the cell cycle progression of SiHa cells. Similarly, exosomal miR-1468-5p has been shown to suppress HMBOX1-SOCS1 expression and activate the JAK2 signalling pathway in lymphatic endothelial cells, leading to immune evasion by cervical cancer cells27. The activation of JAK2 signalling is pivotal in modulating macrophage polarisation. In our study, we present novel evidence elucidating the mechanism by which HPV E6 induces M2 macrophage polarisation through the miR-204-5p/JAK2 pathway. This finding enhances our understanding of the crosstalk between HPV E6 and macrophages and introduces a potential therapeutic target for immunotherapy in CESC.

Since HPV is an epitheliophobic virus, it is difficult to use conventional two-dimensional (2D) cell culture models are to simulate natural HPV infection. The utilisation of organotypic raft culture systems for HPV has enabled the faithful recapitulation of the complete viral life cycle within relevant host cell types28. However, investigating interactions between host cells and immune cells remains challenging. In our study, we obtained the exosomal miRNA profile from HPV E6 + C33A cells using a 2D culture model. To validate the reproducibility of miRNA sequencing, we compared our findings with differentially expressed exosomal miRNAs in various CESC cells, including HPV + SiHa, HeLa, Caski, C4I, SW756 and HPV- HT-3 and C33A. Consistent with our results, miR-92b-3p was enriched in HPV + CESC, while miR-3180 was enriched in HPV- CESC29. Furthermore, exosomal miR-92b-3p was significantly down-regulated in E6/E7-silenced SiHa cells. Recent studies have indicated that small RNA in exosomes from cells cultured in 3D closely resembles that in plasma from CESC patients30. We also observed the up-regulation of exosomal miR-204-5p (Fig. 5A) and miR-92b-3p (data not shown) in serum from HSIL patients. These results support the validity of our HPV E6 + CESC cell culture model. Additionally, Hu et al. recently developed a long-term 3D organoid culture model for HSIL and CESC, derived from human cervical lesions, which promises to facilitate the exploration of extracellular pathways associated with immune responses31.

Until now, immunotherapeutic approaches have been employed to treat precursor lesions in an effort to prevent CESC. Our study uncovers a novel mechanism underlying the increased infiltration of M2 macrophages in CESC, dependent on HPV E6. While this mechanism involves complex immune networks necessitating further detailed investigation, our findings suggest that HPV status and the level of M2 macrophage infiltration may inform the development of personalised treatment for CESC.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights the dependence of exosomal miR-204-5p expression on HPV E6. We have demonstrated that HPV E6 drives the polarisation of cervical microenvironment macrophages towards the M2 type through exosomal miR-204-5p by suppressing JAK2 expression. Importantly, we have identified a potential role for exosomal miR-204-5p in predicting the severity of cervical lesions, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for cervical lesions.
