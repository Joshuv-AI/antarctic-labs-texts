Development and challenge of coal-based nanocarbon materials and their application in water treatment: a review
Authors: MingShuai Xie, HongChao Luo, XinJuan Liu, ChaoChuang Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder the dual pressures of environmental protection and energy security, the development and application of coal-based nanocarbon materials, supported by the technical concepts of molecular chemical engineering and nanomaterial science, is of significant importance for achieving the high-value clean utilization of coal. Furthermore, it serves as an effective means to assist in the realization of dual carbon goals. Coal, with its abundant reserves, high carbon content, and aromatic and hydrogenated aromatic groups, exhibits great advantages and potential in the synthesis of nanocarbon materials. In addition to its applications in traditional power and chemical industries, coal-based nanocarbon materials also demonstrate significant value in the field of environmental pollution control. This article succinctly summarizes the preparation methods and properties of coal-based carbon nanotubes, coal-based carbon quantum dots, and coal-based graphene, elucidates their current applications in water pollution control and governance, and anticipates their development trends in water pollution control, aiming to provide support for the clean and efficient utilization of coal and water pollution control.Keywords: Coal, Coal-based nanocarbon materials, Design and preparation, Carbon quantum dots, Carbon nanotubes, Graphene, Water treatment

Introduction

Coal, as a widely distributed, abundantly available, and cost-effective natural resource, is a crucial asset for the advancement of human society. Presently, coal is primarily utilized in sectors such as power generation, coking, gasification, liquefaction, pyrolysis, and coal chemical industries. Beyond its efficient and clean use as fuel and non-energy applications in coal chemical industries, an innovative method of efficient and clean utilization of coal is through coal-based nano-carbon materials. Microscopically, coal can be divided into organic and inorganic components. The inorganic portion is mainly comprised of various minerals, including metal oxides, metal sulfides, and carbonates. Conversely, the organic constituents are rich in carbon elements, followed by hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and sulfur. These elements predominantly exist in coal as various functional groups, forming the macromolecular structure of coal, which includes aromatic cores with aromatic layered structures, surrounded by various functional groups and alkyl side chains [1–3]. Additionally, coal exhibits natural similarities to carbon materials, containing crystalline intermediates that lie between amorphous carbon and graphite states [4, 5]. Therefore, coal is an excellent precursor for the preparation of nanoscale carbon materials [6]. Common nano-carbon materials include fullerenes [7], carbon nanotubes [8], carbon quantum dots [9, 10], graphene [11], graphene oxide, and carbon fibers [12]. Due to its outstanding material performance parameters [13–15], coal-based nanocarbon materials have extensive applications not only in traditional fields such as electricity and chemical industry, but also in medical [16–18], energy [19–21], and water treatment sectors [22–24]. In the realm of water treatment, these materials are utilized for adsorbing pollutants like heavy metal ions [25–27], nitrobenzenes, antibiotics, bisphenol A, organic dyes, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and their oxygenated derivatives, owing to their significant specific surface area, abundant pore structure, high mechanical strength, and strong hydrophobic properties [28]. Moreover, their ease of functionalization renders coal-based nanocarbon materials suitable as catalyst supports for catalyzing the degradation of organic pollutants, demonstrating immense utility in the field of water treatment. Development of coal-based carbon nanomaterials in water treatment applications

At present, the preparation technology of coal-based nano-carbon materials has made certain progress, yet several challenges remain during the process, such as: (1) Environmental pollution issues during preparation. The pre-treatment of raw coal involves the use of strong acids, strong bases, high temperatures, high-pressure environments, and special protective atmospheres, resulting in waste liquid, waste gas, and solid waste that not only pose significant environmental hazards but also increase the difficulty of separating and purifying the products. (2) The technology for the large-scale preparation of coal-based nano-carbon materials remains to be refined, primarily due to the diverse types of coal, their complex compositions, and varying contents of impurities. The current preparation technologies are not universally applicable to all types of coal, greatly limiting the commercial application of coal-based nano-carbon materials. (3) The complex composition and structure of coal and its derivatives mean that the relationship between material performance and coal structure composition is still unclear. Therefore, further clarification of the structure-performance relationship between the properties of coal-based nano-carbon materials and their microstructures is required to achieve performance regulation of these materials at the molecular level. (4) The widespread application of carbon nano-materials necessitates modification, functionalization, and structural regulation. Transforming coal and its derivatives into various nano-carbon materials requires multiple operational steps. These four points currently constrain the large-scale preparation and application of coal-based nano-carbon materials in water treatment. Hence, there is a need to develop a process technology and route that couple various modifications, structural regulation, and green efficiency to lower synthesis costs, thereby highlighting the significant raw material and cost advantages in preparing nano-carbon materials based on coal and its derivatives. This paper primarily elucidates the preparation methods, properties, and research advancements of several typical coal-based nanocarbon materials derived from coal. It further explores their applications in water treatment and offers a prospective outlook on their future development, aiming to provide constructive theoretical and practical guidance for the high-value and clean utilization of coal in China.

Preparation method of coal based nanocarbon materials

Coal-based nanocarbon materials have indeed demonstrated the potential to transcend disciplinary boundaries in the fields of molecular chemical engineering and nanomaterial science, yielding significant economic and social benefits. As global attention increasingly shifts towards sustainable development and clean energy, the utilization of coal as a precursor for synthesizing complex compositions and structures of nanocarbon materials has garnered considerable interest. However, despite the proliferation of various preparation methods, substantial challenges remain in achieving scalability, efficiency, and environmental sustainability. For instance, the synthesis of coal-derived carbon quantum dots has attracted attention due to the abundance and low cost of its raw materials; however, comprehensive biotoxicity studies are imperative to ensure their safe application prior to widespread use. While the research prospects for coal-based nanocarbon materials are promising, the hurdles associated with large-scale production and sustainability must be addressed through further investigation and technological innovation.

Nanomaterials can be categorized into zero-dimensional, one-dimensional, and two-dimensional based on their dimensions. Zero-dimensional materials include carbon quantum dots and fullerenes, one-dimensional materials include carbon nanotubes and carbon fibers, and two-dimensional materials include graphene. Currently, the most researched nanomaterials are carbon quantum dots, carbon nanotubes, and graphene, primarily due to their stable chemical properties, low toxicity, and good biocompatibility.

Carbon Quantum Dots (CQDs) are a general term for zero-dimensional nano-sized particles, mainly composed of sp2, sp3 hybridized carbon, graphene fragments, or amorphous carbon. They have small particle sizes, typically less than 10 nm. Currently, the preparation methods of coal-based carbon quantum dots can be broadly classified into two categories: top–down [29–31] and bottom–up [32–35] approaches. The top–down approach involves using natural high-carbon materials such as biomass, coal, and their derivatives as precursors, and utilizing chemical oxidation, physical exfoliation, templating, and other techniques to oxidize, exfoliate, and shear the macromolecular precursors, resulting in carbon quantum dots with a graphite microcrystalline structure. The bottom-up approach uses polymer small molecules or organic small molecules as carbon sources (such as citric acid, urea, etc.), and under conditions such as hydrothermal, ultrasonic, or microwave-assisted heating in water or specific organic solvents, these molecules are thermally decomposed or dissolved to form nano-sized carbon quantum dots. The formation of carbon quantum dots involves processes such as polymerization, dehydration, assembly, and nucleation [36].

Carbon Nanotubes (CNTs), discovered by Japanese scientist Sumio Iijima in 1991 [37], have been widely studied for their excellent mechanical properties, electrical conductivity, and thermal conductivity. CNTs are one-dimensional nanostructures formed by the curling of carbon atoms, typically with sp2 hybridized carbon atoms, and can exist as single-walled, double-walled, or multi-walled structures, with diameters ranging from a few nanometers to several tens of nanometers [38]. Common methods for the preparation of carbon nanotubes include chemical vapor deposition (CVD), arc discharge, plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, and catalytic thermal decomposition.

Graphene (GN), as a novel two-dimensional nanomaterial, holds great promise in various fields such as materials science, biomedicine, and energy due to its outstanding mechanical, electrical, and optical properties [39]. The preparation of graphene from materials like anthracite, bituminous coal, and coal tar is primarily driven by the fact that the aromatic condensed rings in the molecular structure of coal undergo processes like condensation, dehydrogenation, and deoxygenation in the absence of air during pyrolysis, leading to the formation of a planar stacked structure resembling graphite crystals. Currently, common methods for producing coal-based graphene include high-temperature high-pressure methods, catalytic graphitization, chemical vapor deposition, and flash Joule heating. Chemical oxidation method

In the chemical oxidation method, commonly used oxidants include strong acids, hydrogen peroxide, ozone, N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), toluene, and ammonia. Ye et al. [49] used a mixture of strong acids (H2SO4 and HNO3) to oxidize bituminous coal, obtaining carbon quantum dots with an average size of 2.96 nm and an ID/IG intensity of 1.55 ± 0.19. Similarly, using a mixture of strong acids as the oxidant, Zhang et al. [50] prepared a large number of hydroxyl, carboxyl, and carbonyl-functionalized carbon dots from anthracite, with sizes ranging from 2.0 to 3.2 nm. Ye et al. [51] controlled the size of anthracite-based carbon dots by regulating the pore size of the cross-flow ultrafiltration membrane and the temperature of chemical oxidation (Fig. 3a). As the temperature of the mixed acid chemical oxidation increased from 50 to 150 °C, the average size of the carbon dots decreased from 54 ± 7.2 to 7.6 ± 1.8 nm. When the pore size of the ultrafiltration membrane was 76 ± 18 nm, the size of the carbon dots was 70 ± 15 nm. The study demonstrated that the size of the carbon dots can be controlled by regulating the oxidation temperature and the membrane pore size.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), as an inexpensive oxidant, finds widespread application in the industrial field. Hu et al. [52] used thermally generated ·OH from H2O2 to attack carbon atoms containing -OH and epoxy groups, resulting in the synthesis of carbon dots with diameters ranging from 1 to 3 nm (Fig. 3b). Shi et al. [53] used H2O2 as an oxidant to prepare 2.29 nm carbon dots from lignite in Shalun Lake. Yu et al. [54] used a mixed solution of H2O2 and HCOOH (formic acid) as the oxidant to prepare 1.72 nm carbon dots from brown coal in Inner Mongolia at a temperature of 80 °C.

Ozone, as a common oxidant, has been widely used for the preparation of layered nanomaterials such as graphene. Xue et al. [55] bubbled ozone into a coal powder water solution, producing carbon dots with a diameter of 4.2 nm. Liu et al. [56] used ozone as the oxidant to replace strong acid and used lignite as the carbon source to prepare blue-light emitting fluorescent carbon dots with sizes ranging from 2 to 4 nm. The ozone oxidation process is mild, the resulting products are non-toxic, and the preparation process is pollution-free and environmentally friendly. However, the use of ozone as an oxidant leads to a low yield of carbon dots, so it is currently used less frequently for carbon dot preparation.

Electrochemical method

Electrochemical methods can be used to prepare carbon dots from carbon-containing electrodes by controlling the electrolysis conditions. This method is simple and efficient, but natural coal has poor electrical conductivity and mechanical properties, necessitating pretreatment of the original coal to meet the electrode requirements. He et al. [57] used coke as a carbon source and prepared carbon dots through electrochemical action. By controlling the ratio of methanol to water added and regulating the change in current density with (NH4)2S2O8 as the electrolyte, they successfully prepared orange, yellow, and green multi-colored fluorescent carbon dots, as shown in Fig. 3c.

Physical exfoliation

Physical exfoliation for the preparation of carbon dots does not involve chemical reagents, which is beneficial for subsequent purification processes. The carbon dots produced through physical exfoliation tend to have lower oxygen groups and surface defects compared to those prepared using chemical methods. However, the energy consumption during the preparation process is higher. Zhang et al. [58] synthesized carbon dots using an ultrasonic disruptor. The principle is that in a DMF solution, DMF molecules form bridges with the oxygen groups of coal, and the cavitation generated by ultrasound can create localized high heat and pressure, leading to the cleavage of the bridges and the formation of new functional groups at the edges of coal molecules. Kang et al. [59] prepared carbon dots from a coal-ethanol solution using a laser source. The entire process does not require purification, and when the excitation wavelength is 365 nm, the carbon dots emit green fluorescence. Saikia et al. [60] employed a method combining H2O2 and ultrasound assistance to produce carbon dots from anthracite and bituminous coal.

Template method

Template synthesis involves depositing precursors onto the nano-sized pores and surfaces of a template using physical or chemical methods, and the removal of the template yields the desired nano-materials. Templates are inexpensive materials with nanostructures that are easily controllable. They can be designed and selected based on specific needs. Wang et al. [61] prepared carbon dots using dispersed carbon microcrystals in carbonized asphalt (Fig. 3d). When carbonized at 450 °C, the average particle size of the carbon dots was 4.50 ± 0.51 nm, with a quantum yield of 87%. At a carbonization temperature of 440 °C, the carbon dots emitted blue fluorescence in an ethanol solution under UV light at a wavelength of 365 nm.

Chemical vapor deposition

The CVD method is a process of catalytically decomposing carbon-containing raw materials to deposit and form carbon nanotubes (CNTs) under specific temperature and catalyst conditions. The choice of catalyst plays a crucial role in the synthesis of CNTs, with commonly used active components including transition metals such as Fe, Co, and Ni. Zhang et al. [62] used bituminous coal as the raw material and KOH as the catalyst for catalytic thermal decomposition to produce carbon nanotubes (CNTs). Research findings revealed that during the catalytic thermal decomposition of coal, small molecular volatiles such as (CH4) were cracked and converted into CNTs through the action of the catalyst. Simultaneously, the K element in the catalyst could etch the large molecular ordered structure in coal to generate a carbon source. Characterization of the carbon nanotubes revealed a diverse range of shapes, including linear and curved, with various opening shapes. The wall layers of the carbon nanotubes ranged from 18 to 65 layers, with an interlayer spacing of 0.34 nm. Mothi et al. [43] used gases generated from the thermal decomposition of coal as the carbon source to prepare carbon nanotubes, and found that CH4 and CO were the main carbon sources for the synthesis of carbon nanotubes, with the optimal temperature for coal pyrolysis determined to be between 400 and 500 °C. The CVD method for preparing carbon nanotubes is widely applied due to its easily controllable reaction process, relatively low reaction temperature, high yield, low cost, and strong applicability. However, the carbon nanotubes produced at low reaction temperatures tend to be non-straight and contain a higher number of defects and impurities.

In the preparation methods of coal-based graphene, chemical vapor deposition (CVD) has garnered attention for producing high-quality graphene with a large area [63]. Theoretically, any carbon-containing substance can serve as a carbon source for producing graphene, such as gaseous hydrocarbons and liquid alcohols [64–66]. However, considering the utilization of resources in China, using coal as a carbon source and employing pyrolysis as the carbon source for preparing graphene aligns with the resource utilization status in China. Zhang et al. [67] used low-grade coal of different metamorphic degrees as the carbon source and applied CVD after pyrolysis to prepare graphene, finding a linear relationship between the CH4 content in the pyrolysis gas and the quality of graphene, the schematic diagram of its preparation process is shown in Fig. 4a. Vijapur et al. [68] used coal as the carbon source, copper as the substrate, and employed CVD under an H2 reduction atmosphere to prepare graphene films, proposing the growth mechanism of graphene.

Arc method

The arc discharge method is based on the formation of temperatures above 3000 °C between electrodes through arc discharge, which causes the carbon atoms of the graphite anode to sublime and undergo molecular rearrangement to form carbon nanotubes (CNTs) on the catalyst surface. A vacuum arc discharge device, as shown in Fig. 4b, consists of inflow and outflow gas routes, an anode, and a cathode, and is equipped with a water-cooling system connected to the furnace wall [69]. Before operation, the arc furnace needs to be evacuated and a certain amount of arcing gas needs to be injected. After applying voltage to the anode and cathode, the arc is maintained at a distance of 0.01–10 mm to obtain a stable arc, generating temperatures of 4000–6000 K and ion bombardment that causes the graphite anode to evaporate [70]. The evaporated atoms re-crystallize to form carbon nanotubes or other carbon nanomaterials as they move toward the furnace wall as the temperature decreases. Liu et al. [71] designed a hydrogen-arc method that successfully realized the semi-continuous production of single-walled carbon nanotubes. Zhao et al. [72] customized a vacuum arc chamber with specifications of Φ300 nm × 400 nm and added a catalyst to the electrode material, successfully preparing carbon nanotubes with an average particle size of 1.3 nm. Qiu et al. [73] used Taixi anthracite from Ningxia as the carbon source, and after carbonization, obtained coal-based carbon, which was then mixed with a catalyst (Fe, Ni, etc.) in proportion. They successfully prepared highly pure and graphitized single-walled carbon nanotubes using plasma arc technology. The arc discharge method is simple, reliable, and easy to operate, and can produce perfectly structured carbon nanotubes, making it highly promising for future industrial applications, despite its high energy consumption.

Plasma jet method

Plasma jet etching has been introduced into the preparation process of coal-based carbon nanotubes to simplify the process, directly converting raw coal into coal-based carbon nanotubes through plasma arc without the need for high-temperature pretreatment of the raw coal [59, 60]. The principle involves using metal as the electrode for the reaction device. When the electrode is charged, gases such as Ar and H2 between the electrodes dissociate to form a high-temperature plasma arc. N2 carries coal powder or ethanol as the carbon source [75–77] into the arc region for dissociation reaction. Carbon nanotubes are collected around the arc reaction chamber and behind the arc using collection tubes. Unlike arc discharge methods, plasma jet etching allows for continuous production of carbon nanotubes. Tian et al. [74] used lignite as the carbon source, and after being subjected to Ar + H2 plasma gas, deposited carbon nanotubes were found on the inner wall of the reaction chamber, albeit with lower purity. Plasma jet etching, as a method for preparing coal-based carbon nanotubes, can lead to narrow reaction paths or blockages due to the deposited substances on the inner walls of the reaction chamber when coal is used as the carbon source [78]. The main components of these deposits are amorphous carbon, resulting in a low content of carbon nanotubes. Additionally, this method consumes a large amount of electricity and is not an ideal choice for large-scale production of carbon nanotubes. Currently, there is relatively limited research on the preparation of coal-based carbon nanotubes using this method.

Catalytic pyrolysis method

Catalytic thermal decomposition involves the preparation of carbon nanot ubes based on the thermal decomposition of a carbon source at high temperatures and its reaction with a catalyst, where carbon atoms are released from the carbon source molecules and form carbon nanotubes on the surface of a metal catalyst. Zhang et al. [79] used Shanxi air-dried bituminous coal as the carbon source and employed the catalytic thermal decomposition method to prepare carbon nanotubes. During the preparation process, it was observed that the iron content in the bituminous coal could affect the formation of carbon nanotubes. Studies indicated that the natural iron minerals in coal play a significant role in the formation of carbon nanotubes during coal pyrolysis. In the presence of KOH catalyzing coal pyrolysis, iron from the raw material migrates from the coal body to the surface of the coal particles, enriching the surface area of the coal particles. The presence of KOH significantly affects the micropores, specific surface area, and pore volume of carbon nanotubes. The catalytic growth of carbon nanotubes from natural siderite in coal follows the mechanism: FeCO3 → α-Fe → Fe3C + graphite → carbon nanotubes, The schematic diagram of its growth process is shown in Fig. 4c. This study provides a new approach for the utilization of low-grade coal resources rich in iron minerals.

Catalytic graphitization is an effective method for achieving the graphitization of coal-based carbon, involving a series of complex chemical reactions between the catalyst and non-graphitic carbon materials to enhance carbon crystallization. The catalysts used in this method can be classified into three categories: elemental, compound, and alloy. Elemental catalysts, particularly those from the iron group, such as Fe2O3, Fe3O4, and FeO, are commonly used, with Fe2O3 exhibiting the best performance in catalytic graphitization [80]. Alloy catalysts, represented by iron-silicon and phosphorus-nickel, have lower melting points than the individual metals, allowing them to reduce the temperature required for graphitization, thus achieving better catalytic effects [80]. The primary roles of the catalyst in the graphitization process are to accelerate the rate of graphitization and to lower the temperature required for the process [81]. Aside from reducing the graphitization temperature and enhancing production efficiency, catalysts can also alter the structure and morphology of the graphite.

Zhang et al. [82] used La2O3 as a catalyst for graphitization and found that the addition of the catalyst not only improved the development and growth of graphite microcrystals but also significantly enhanced the electrical and mechanical properties of the materials. Li et al. [83] utilized smokeless coal as the carbon source and silicon powder as the catalyst for catalytic graphitization, resulting in the preparation of coal-based graphite with different microstructures. They analyzed the catalytic reaction mechanism and found that when the catalyst addition was 5%, the prepared graphite microcrystals exhibited larger size, higher orderliness, a graphitization degree of 91.5%, and excellent electrochemical characteristics.

High temperature and high pressure method

Graphite is an amorphous form of carbon that undergoes high-temperature heat treatment, transforming into a crystalline form of carbon through “microcrystal” growth. At temperatures below 1600 °C, the structure of amorphous carbon does not exhibit significant changes. Between 1600 and 2100 °C, microcrystals undergo noticeable changes, with the bonds at the edges of microcrystals breaking and gasifying. This process can continue up to 2700 °C, transforming microcrystals from an amorphous arrangement in two-dimensional space to an ordered arrangement in three-dimensional space.

Lu Liangyou [84] used low ash anthracite coal from Taixi as a carbon source and explored the effect of different temperatures on coal graphitization. The study showed that the degree of graphitization was greatly affected by the calcination temperature and showed a linear relationship. At a calcination temperature of 2500 °C, the degree of graphitization was (79 ± 3)%. Wang et al. [85] used rich inertinite coal and rich vitrinite coal as carbon sources. After high-temperature graphitization at 3000 °C, the graphitized samples were oxidized using the Hummers' method and subsequently heated to 400 °C for 0.5 h in a nitrogen atmosphere furnace to obtain graphene nanosheets. Electrochemical analysis demonstrated that graphene nanomaterials prepared from coal as a precursor exhibited good stability, rate capability, and supercapacitor performance. Tang et al. [86] employed smokeless coal from Xiaofalu, Yunnan, as the carbon source and prepared coal-based graphene through oxidation–reduction after high-temperature graphitization at 2800 °C using the Hummers’ method. Xing et al. also studied the graphitization of coal at high temperatures [87–89]. This method requires high equipment demands, consumes significant energy during production, emits harmful smoke to the environment, and may lead to carbon framework collapse under high-temperature conditions. For some hard carbon materials that are difficult to graphitize, even at high temperatures, reducing d002 remains challenging. Flash Joule heating

Flash Joule heating (FJH) is a highly efficient, short-duration non-radiative heating technique developed based on Joule’s law. Compared to traditional furnace heating, FJH allows for the control of discharge voltage by changing the capacitance charging time, making it convenient to operate with controllable product structure and low energy consumption. Introduced by Luong et al. [90] in 2020, research has shown that flash Joule heating using coal and its derivatives as carbon sources can achieve flash vaporization samples in less than 1 s by adjusting the voltage and discharge time of the capacitor. Similarly, in a study by Liu and Luo [91] graphite was prepared using FJH with Guizhou anthracite as the carbon source, demonstrating that precise control of system parameters, particularly pulse voltage, can maximize the graphitization of coal, with the greatest degree achieved at 190 V.

Characteristics of coal based nanocarbon materials

Coal-based nanocarbon materials exhibit significant application potential across various fields due to their unique physicochemical properties. Firstly, these materials excel in energy storage, particularly as electrode materials in supercapacitors and lithium-ion batteries. Additionally, coal-based carbon materials demonstrate promising prospects in environmental remediation. By synthesizing coal-based magnetic carbon composites, it becomes possible to effectively remove heavy metal ions, such as arsenic and cadmium, from water, providing an innovative and eco-friendly solution for water treatment. Furthermore, the application of coal-based nanocarbon materials in the field of catalysis is increasingly gaining attention. Researchers have discovered that these materials show commendable catalytic activity in both photocatalytic and electrocatalytic reactions, significantly enhancing the efficiency of chemical reactions, especially in the degradation of environmental pollutants. With their diverse properties and broad application potential, coal-based nanocarbon materials are playing a crucial role across multiple domains. Consequently, there is a necessity for targeted property modulation based on specific applications during the preparation process, which imposes higher demands on fabrication technologies. Mechanical property

The C=C bonds in the graphene plane that constitutes carbon nanotubes are currently known as one of the strongest chemical bonds, with theoretical calculations revealing a strength approximately 100 times that of steel and an elastic modulus 50 times that of steel, while having only 1/6 of the density of steel. Experimental results indicate that the maximum Young’s modulus of CNTs is 1.4 TPa, and the tensile strength is around 100 GPa. Moreover, the outer walls of multi-walled carbon nanotubes not only protect their interiors from chemical influences, but also enhance their tensile strength compared to single-walled carbon nanotubes. Lee et al. [95] found that under the condition of a graphene layer thickness of 0.35 nm, the Young’s modulus of graphene is 130 ± 10 GPa, and the strength of the two-dimensional plane is 42 N m−2. Gόmez-Navarro et al. [96] and Poot et al. [97] obtained strength values of graphene with different thicknesses through various methods. Another remarkable characteristic of carbon nanotubes is their elasticity; when subjected to axial compressive forces, carbon nanotubes can bend and twist without damage. Compared to other materials, carbon nanotubes have significant advantages in terms of Young’s modulus and tensile strength, making them a very promising material for mechanical applications. Additionally, carbon nanotubes can significantly enhance the mechanical performance of composite materials as additives. However, the presence of structural defects reduces their mechanical properties in practical applications [98–100].

Optical properties

Coal-based carbon dots exhibit a strong absorption band in the ultraviolet region, ranging from 230 to 320 nm. The absorption peak at 230 nm corresponds to the π–π* transition of C–C bonds, while the peak at 320 nm arises from n–π* transitions of C=O or other surface groups. The fluorescent properties of carbon dots are a prominent feature, primarily attributed to sp2 or sp3 hybridization, surface functional groups, and defects. GENG et al. [101] synthesized carbon dots capable of emitting orange light, with an excitation wavelength at 535 nm leading to orange emission at 605 nm. CQDs do not exhibit significant changes in their fluorescence properties after being exposed to ultraviolet radiation for several hours. When dispersed in water, carbon quantum dots can be stored for long periods at room temperature without significant reduction in fluorescence intensity [102]. They also demonstrate stable ultraviolet absorption capabilities at different temperatures [103]. In studies of the optical properties of graphene, it has been found that a single layer of graphene can absorb approximately 2.3% of the incident light energy [104]. The light absorption intensity of graphene is independent of the frequency of light and is related to the number of layers of graphene [105]. For a single layer of graphene, the absorption and transmittance of visible light are 2.3% and 97.7% respectively.

Thermal properties

Prior to the emergence of carbon nanotubes, diamond was regarded as the premier thermal conductor; however, carbon nanotubes boast a thermal conductivity that is at least twice that of diamond. The exceptional thermal properties of carbon nanotubes are intricately linked to their unique structure and diminutive size. Due to their small dimensions, quantum effects become significant, rendering carbon nanotubes ideal candidates for investigating low-dimensional phonon band structures and for applications in both macro and micro thermal management [106]. Thermal properties such as specific heat and thermal conductivity in carbon nanotubes are predominantly governed by phonons. They are capable of withstanding high temperatures (750 °C under normal atmospheric pressure and up to 2800 °C in a vacuum) thanks to the strength of the atomic bonds within the nanotubes, which confer excellent thermal conductivity. The inter-tube coupling in single-walled carbon nanotube ropes and the inter-shell coupling in multi-walled carbon nanotubes result in low-temperature specific heat characteristics akin to those of three-dimensional graphite. Theoretical predictions suggest that individual nanotubes can achieve a thermal conductivity of 6600 W m−1 K−1 at room temperature. Measurements indicate that bulk samples of single-walled carbon nanotubes exhibit thermal conductivities exceeding 200 W m−1 K−1, while individual multi-walled nanotubes can surpass 3000 W m−1 K−1, compared to copper’s thermal conductivity of 385 W m−1 K−1. These remarkable attributes will facilitate solutions to the challenges posed by the miniaturization of future microprocessors, addressing critical issues of thermal management and heat dissipation that are essential for numerous electrical applications [99, 107–109].

Research has shown that the internal heat transfer in carbon nanotubes is usually attributed to the phonon conduction mechanism [110]. Some carbon nanotubes exhibit metallic behavior, but the heat transfer in metallic carbon nanotubes is mainly attributed to the collective vibrations of atoms [111]. Simulation studies have revealed that the thermal conductivity of carbon nanotubes exhibits a certain temperature dependence. In the temperature range of 100–500 K, the thermal conductivity of carbon nanotubes shows a trend of initially increasing and then decreasing with increasing temperature [112]. Che et al. [113] found that structural defects lead to a continuous decrease in the thermal conductivity of carbon nanotubes as the number of defects or vacancies increases. Due to its high elastic modulus and electron mean free path, graphene possesses a thermal conductivity ranging from 3000 to 6000 Wm−1 K−1. Research by Kim et al. [114] unveiled a thermal conductivity of graphene at 5300 Wm−1 K−1, which decreases with increasing temperature [111]. The carbon defects and disordered arrangement of edges in graphene can also reduce its thermal conductivity (Fig. 6) [115].

Electrical properties

The electrical properties of carbon nanotubes are directly influenced by their diameter and chirality. These remarkable structures exhibit exceptionally low resistance, which arises only when electrons collide with defects present in the crystalline lattice they traverse; such defects may manifest as atomic irregularities or impurities within the structure. The electrical characteristics of carbon nanotubes are also contingent upon their synthesis methods and aggregation states. It is reported that theoretically ideal carbon nanotubes behave as ballistic conductors over distances on the micron scale. The one-dimensional confinement of electrons, coupled with the principles of energy and momentum conservation, facilitates ballistic conduction, thereby enabling efficient and rapid electron transport while carrying current with zero resistance [99, 100, 107, 116, 117].

Utilizing the large specific surface area of coal based carbon nanotubes, electrolyte, and electrode graphene as an excellent semiconductor material, its conduction and valence bands are in the Brillouin zone Κ and Κ' The relationship between energy and momentum near the intersection of Dirac points [118] is Ε = hvFΚ [115], which makes the effective mass of electrons in graphene zero, meaning that electrons in graphene are a mass free Dirac fermion [119].

Structural regulation and modification

Due to the outstanding mechanical, thermal, optical, and electrical properties of coal-based carbon nanomaterials, they hold great potential for various applications in numerous fields. To better align their performance with practical applications, structural regulation and modification are required. The surface oxygen functional groups of carbon dots can determine their optical properties and catalytic active sites. Targeted changes in the composition and type of surface functional groups can enhance their performance. Hu et al. [120] prepared phosphorus-functionalized carbon dots using diammonium phosphate (DAP), and XPS analysis indicated that the resulting product contained N, P, and O. Phosphoric acid can be grafted onto the surface of carbon dots through double coordination bonds. The surface, pore size, and dimensions of graphene do not align with the ideal model, all of which can impact the properties of graphene. Structural regulation of graphene is essential to increase adsorption and active sites. Chemically oxidized and exfoliated graphene layers with rich oxygen functional groups are easy to process and assemble. Heteroatom doping can effectively enhance the catalytic activity of the material. The overlap of atomic orbitals between the heteroatoms and carbon atoms, along with the push–pull electronic effect of the heteroatoms, can change the electronic structure of the carbon dots, leading to band restructuring and improved material performance [121–124]. Commonly used doping elements include N, S, P, etc. [125]. Doping can generate unpaired electrons in the sp2-hybridized carbon framework of carbon dots, thus creating delocalized π-electron systems and promoting electronic transport in the material [126].

The application of coal based nanocarbon materials in environmental pollution control

As a new type of material, coal-based carbon nanomaterials demonstrate broad application prospects in the field of environmental pollution control. In water treatment technologies, coal-based carbon nanomaterials can be widely used in adsorption and catalytic oxidation. With their high specific surface area and abundant surface active sites, coal-based carbon nanomaterials can effectively adsorb organic substances and heavy metal ions in water, thereby achieving water quality removal and purification [127]. Additionally, coal-based carbon nanomaterials exhibit excellent catalytic oxidation performance, facilitating the degradation and oxidation of organic wastewater in water treatment processes, thereby further enhancing water treatment efficiency.

Adsorption technology, due to its rapidity, cost-effectiveness, and efficiency, is considered a versatile water treatment technique widely applied in the removal of environmental pollutants [128]. Activated carbon, as the most commonly used adsorbent due to its excellent adsorption properties, has been utilized for adsorbing various pollutants. However, the high production costs and challenges in regeneration limit its widespread use [129]. Coal-based carbon nanomaterials (such as graphene and carbon dots) have been developed as a new type of adsorbent material. Compared to graphene, carbon dots offer several advantages as an adsorbent material. For instance: (1) the small size and large specific surface area of carbon dots provide more adsorption sites, (2) the synthesis method of carbon dots is simpler, requiring no complex equipment and with inexpensive raw materials, and (3) carbon dots possess hydrophilic functional groups inherently, eliminating the need for modification treatment. These hydrophilic functional groups may be the primary reason for the adsorption of pollutants [130, 131]. The adsorption of heavy metal ions by carbon dots primarily depends on their chemical properties, pore volume, specific surface area, and the number of functional groups [132–137]. Electrostatic interactions are considered a significant adsorption mechanism between carbon dots and heavy metal ions [138]. Sabet and Mahdavi [132] used nitrogen-doped carbon dots to adsorb Cd2+ and Pb2+ from wastewater, showing that carbon dots with a large specific surface area and negatively charged nitrogen elements and oxygen functional groups on the surface can serve as active adsorption sites for heavy metal ions. Wang et al. [133] utilized organosilicate embedded carbon dots to adsorb Cu2+, Hg2+, and Pb2+, and compared to unmodified carbon dots, the modified silica-covered carbon dots displayed enhanced adsorption capacity for different metal ions. Rahmanian et al. [139] analyzed the adsorption kinetics and isotherms of metal ions by carbon dots, revealing that the adsorption kinetics and isotherms of Cd(II) on CDs/ZnAl-LDH followed a pseudo-second-order kinetic model and Freundlich adsorption isotherm model. Additionally, the functional groups on the CDs/ZnAl-LDH composite provided numerous active adsorption sites.

Doping coal-based carbon dots is beneficial for the separation and transfer of photogenerated carriers (e-h), significantly enhancing their photocatalytic performance [140]. These carbon dots can serve as photocatalysts themselves or be added to other composite materials to prepare composite photocatalysts with excellent catalytic activity. Sun et al. [141] utilized copper nanorod-supported nitrogen-doped coal-based carbon dots for the simultaneous photocatalytic degradation and hydrogen production of cotton pulp black liquor (CPBL) wastewater. Aldehydes, phenols, acids, ethers, alkanes, and other substances in the wastewater were degraded within 60 min, as depicted in the degradation mechanism of CPBL and H2 production in Fig. 7. Zhang et al. [142] combined carbon dots with Ag3PO4 for the degradation of wastewater containing methyl orange. As shown in Fig. 8b, it took 55 min for Ag3PO4 alone to completely degrade methyl orange, while the CQDs/Ag3PO4 composite system only required 25 min for complete degradation. Furthermore, as indicated in Fig. 8c, the CQDs/Ag3PO4 composite system exhibited sufficient stability in the recovery experiment, with no noticeable loss in photocatalytic activity. During the catalytic degradation process, carbon dots serve as both electron acceptors and donors. Electrons can easily transfer to the surface of Ag3PO4 and Ag/Ag3PO4, while the excess electrons on the surface of Ag3PO4 and Ag/Ag3PO4 can also transfer to the surface of carbon dots. Additionally, carbon dots can prevent the dissolution and corrosion of Ag3PO4, thereby enhancing its photostability. The use of carbon quantum dots to modify semiconductor photocatalysts for the visible light degradation of organic pollutants has attracted significant attention from researchers. Zhang and his research team employed coal tar pitch (CTP) as a natural source of carbon quantum dots, and utilized a solvothermal method to produce an effective CTP/TiO2 photocatalyst. When tested with Rhodamine B as the target pollutant, the prepared CTP/TiO2 photocatalyst demonstrated higher photocatalytic activity under visible light conditions compared to pure TiO2 [143].

Coal-based graphene with a large specific surface area, excellent light transmittance, and a unique mesoporous structure exhibits outstanding catalytic and adsorption capabilities for pollutants. Jacob et al. [144] employed a one-pot method to fabricate nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide-supported CuCo2O4 (NG/CuCo2O4) composite material under mild conditions, subsequently applying it in the reduction of toxic 4-nitrophenol (4NP). The results demonstrated that the NG/CuCo2O4 composite material exhibited stable catalytic activity in the reduction of 4NP, with an activity factor of 261.5 min−1 g−1. The catalytic conversion rate of 4NP by the NG/CuCo2O4 catalyst reached 99.5% within 11 min. Kinetic analysis confirmed that the nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide carrier altered the electronic energy levels on the surface of CuCo2O4 nanoparticles through electron transfer interactions, thereby enhancing the catalytic activity of CuCo2O4 in NG/CuCo2O4 by improving the adsorption of reactant ions and generating active hydrogen species. The NG/CuCo2O4 material exhibited good reusability, stability, and catalytic activity in the experiment. Zeng et al. [48] utilized lignite as the precursor for carbonaceous material and subjected it to high-temperature graphitization treatment. Subsequently, they employed an improved Hummers’ method to prepare graphene oxide. Using TiCl4 as the titanium source, they then utilized a hydrothermal method to fabricate coal-based graphene/TiO2 composite material with mesoporous characteristics. The specific surface area of the material could reach 88.53–169.64 m2 g−1, with pore sizes distributed between 2 and 12 nm. The study determined that the optimal photocatalytic performance was achieved when the mass ratio of coal-based graphene in the material was 8%. The degradation rate of Rhodamine B by CRGO/TiO2 composite material is significantly higher than that of CRGO and TiO, and its highest degradation rate can reach 98.9% within 2 h. Xu [145] prepared ZnO/GO/SiO2 composite photocatalytic material and established a Fenton-like photocatalytic system with H2O2 for the degradation of methylene blue. As shown in Fig. 9, the study investigated the effect of different catalytic systems and addition amounts (ZnO mass ratios in the ZnO/GO/SiO2 system were 100%, 80%, 70%, 60%, 50%, and 40%, and the prepared systems were labeled as SP0, SP1, SP2, SP3, SP4, and SP5, respectively) on the photocatalytic performance. The results indicated that when using the ZnO/GO/SiO2 catalytic system with a ZnO addition amount of 60%, methylene blue was completely degraded after 40 min of light exposure. The presence of GO/SiO2 greatly promoted the generation of active H2O2 radicals and facilitated oxidative degradation.

Membrane technology is one of the technical means for desalination and wastewater treatment [146]. As a barrier, the membrane allows specific substances to pass through while preventing the flow of other substances. In addition to numerous advantages, membrane technology also has some limitations, such as pore blockage due to contamination, leading to reduced water flux. However, the addition of nano carbon materials to polymers can significantly improve the anti-pollution performance of nanocomposite membranes, reduce membrane surface roughness, and enhance membrane retention efficiency and hydrophilicity [147]. Chang and colleagues [148] developed a membrane by immersing graphene oxide (GO) and PVP in a PVDF ultrafiltration membrane, resulting in a membrane with higher water flux and water retention performance, attributed to the hydrogen bonds formed between GO and PVP. The water flux of carbon nanotube-modified membranes is 3–4 times higher than that of unmodified membranes [149]. Nitrogen-doped carbon nanotube membranes can also be used in catalysis, molecular separation, and sensing applications [150]. Apart from nitrogen doping, carbon nanotube membranes can be doped with other elements to achieve different functionalities. Research has shown that silver-doped carbon nanotubes can effectively remove bacteria from wastewater, with the best removal efficiency achieved when the silver content is 10% [151]. Additionally, Wei et al. [152] prepared a sandwich-like carbon nanotube hollow fiber membrane (HFM) using a wet air method for the removal of microcystin-LR, a toxin produced by blue-green algae, from wastewater, achieving a separation efficiency of 99.8%. The carbon nanotube HFM also exhibited good electrochemical regeneration capability for microcystin-LR. In addition to the improvement of membrane composite materials by adding carbon nanotubes, the preparation of graphene oxide (GO) and reduced graphene oxide (rGO) membranes has also attracted significant attention from researchers, demonstrating high-resolution detection capabilities for various toxic pollutants, attributed to the special hydrophilic surface properties and excellent molecular transport in 2D nanofluidic channels of GO/rGO membranes [24, 153, 154].

Zhu et al. [155]. employed oxidized multi-walled carbon nanotubes (CNTs) in conjunction with ferrihydrite (Fh) to develop a novel and highly efficient multi-phase Fenton catalyst for the removal of bisphenol A. The research findings indicate that the incorporation of CNTs significantly enhances the redox cycling of Fe(III)/Fe(II) within the Fenton reaction system. Remarkably, the CNTs/Fh composite maintains excellent Fenton reaction activity even after four cycles of reuse. [156]. employed nitrogen-doped carbon dots (NCDs) to modify the surface of nitrogen-rich g-C3N4 (NCN), achieving the simultaneous removal of 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) and Cr(VI) under visible light irradiation. Utilizing the optimized NCD/NCN catalyst, the removal rates for Cr(VI) and 4-NP reached 97% and 95%, respectively, within 60 min, driven by photogenerated electrons and holes. The enhancement in the photocatalytic performance of the hybrid material is attributed to a reduction in carrier recombination rates, an increase in charge density, and the promotion of rapid charge transfer, which amplify the transient photocurrent response. Furthermore, in a mixed pollutant system, the improved photocatalytic efficiency is ascribed to the dual functionality of 4-NP oxidation and Cr(VI) reduction, leading to effective carrier separation and the generation of additional electrons and holes. The most significant innovation of this study lies in the simultaneous minimization of organic–inorganic waste and energy consumption. Coal-based nano-carbon materials not only effectively improve water quality and purify water resources, but also reduce energy consumption and the use of chemical reagents in water treatment processes. Additionally, these materials can be applied to the removal of atmospheric pollutants. Research has shown that coal-based nano-carbon materials exhibit high adsorption performance during the adsorption process, effectively removing harmful gases and particulate matter from the atmosphere. Furthermore, these materials demonstrate excellent catalytic effects in photocatalytic reactions.

The comprehensive research results indicate that the application of coal-based nano-carbon materials in the field of environmental pollution control is of significant importance, providing new ideas and methods for improving environmental quality and protecting the ecological environment. Therefore, future research and practice should focus on the preparation process of coal-based nano-carbon materials, performance optimization, and their application mechanisms in environmental pollution control, in order to promote their widespread application and dissemination in the field of environmental protection.

Stability of coal-based nanocarbon materials

The stability of coal-based nanocarbon materials directly determines their applicability in the realm of water treatment, which has sparked growing interest among researchers. Investigations into the stability of carbon quantum dots at both low and extremely high temperatures have been conducted. To assess their stability at low temperatures, carbon quantum dots were initially stored at 4 °C for a period, after which their fluorescence performance was evaluated, revealing that they maintained a commendable fluorescence intensity [157, 158]. Researchers have also explored their thermal stability at 1000 °C, as depicted in the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) curve shown in Fig. 12a [159]. Furthermore, carbon quantum dots exhibit remarkable stability even in solutions with high ion concentrations. Similarly, researchers synthesized yellow fluorescent carbon quantum dots through hydrothermal methods using concentrated saltwater, subsequently testing their stability. The results indicated that under high salt concentrations and at 100 °C, the carbon quantum dots remained considerably stable over several days. The influence of pH on fluorescence intensity is shown in Fig. 12b, and the influence of NaCl concentration on fluorescence intensity is shown in Fig. 12c [157, 160]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the exceptional stability of carbon quantum dots, thereby establishing a solid foundation for their application under various environmental conditions.

Prospects and challenges of coal based nanocarbon materials in the field of environmental pollution control

Coal-based nanocarbon materials offer significant potential for wastewater treatment due to their high specific surface area, porous structure, and selective adsorption of pollutants. For instance, coal-based carbon quantum dots (CQDs) exhibit remarkable stability, low toxicity, excellent biocompatibility, and outstanding photostability, rendering them promising candidates for detecting heavy metal ions and removing organic and inorganic pollutants, as well as photocatalytically degrading contaminants in wastewater. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) demonstrate excellent water permeability, selective adsorption, high adsorption capacity, substantial membrane strength, and favorable electrical properties, making them valuable in the electrochemical treatment of wastewater. Graphene (GN), with its exceptional mechanical strength and remarkable corrosion resistance, has also been extensively studied for its ability to adsorb pollutants from water.

Coal-based nanocarbon materials exhibit significant potential for wastewater treatment due to their unique properties; however, their extensive application also faces various challenges. (1) The preparation techniques for coal-based nanocarbon materials demand precise control to achieve specific structures and properties. Moreover, issues such as high production costs and limited batch sizes persist, making large-scale and efficient production a complex challenge. (2) Under certain conditions, coal-based nanocarbon materials may undergo degradation or transformation, adversely affecting their lifespan and processing rates in wastewater treatment. Consequently, future research should focus on enhancing the long-term stability and reusability of these materials under diverse environmental conditions. (3) A thorough assessment of the toxicity and biocompatibility of coal-based nanocarbon materials is essential to understand their potential impacts on human health and the environment. Additionally, corresponding risk assessment guidelines should be established to ensure the safe utilization of these materials in water treatment, thereby minimizing adverse effects on health and the ecosystem.

However, the fundamental challenge of utilizing coal as a precursor for coal-based nanocarbon materials is its non-renewability. This characteristic signifies that coal resources are finite; as extraction and consumption increase, the reserves will inevitably dwindle. This issue becomes especially salient in the context of the global focus on sustainable development and clean energy. In the study of coal-based nanocarbon materials, although coal offers an abundant source of raw materials at relatively low costs, its non-renewability constrains its long-term application potential. Researchers are actively exploring ways to enhance coal utilization efficiency through technological innovations while seeking alternative materials to reduce reliance on coal. In addition to employing coal as a precursor for nanocarbon materials, the consideration of biomass materials offers significant advantages. Biomass is widely available and renewable, exhibiting excellent environmental friendliness. It often possesses rich functionality and diverse chemical structures, which provide opportunities for the synthesis of nanocarbon materials with tailored properties. The extensive availability of biomass can also lower production costs, thereby facilitating the commercialization of related technologies. Furthermore, utilizing biomass alleviates some burdens on agricultural production.

In summary, while coal-based nanocarbon materials possess vast potential in the realm of environmental pollution control, challenges related to preparation techniques, stability, and safety must be addressed. As relevant research advances, it is anticipated that these challenges will gradually be overcome, enabling coal-based nanocarbon materials to play an increasingly vital role in environmental remediation.

Conclusions

Through the comparison of preparation methods of coal-based nano-carbon materials and their applications in the environmental field, the following conclusions can be drawn:

Compared to other fossil materials, coal’s high carbon content, loose structure, and ease of processing make it an ideal raw material for the preparation of carbon nano-materials.

Among these methods, the flash pyrolysis process has significant advantages when comprehensively compared.
