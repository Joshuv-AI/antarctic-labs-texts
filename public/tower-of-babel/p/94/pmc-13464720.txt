Bodily stimulation during rapid eye movement sleep: effects of muscle and vestibular cueing on lucid dreaming
Authors: Emma Peters, Xinlin Wang, Johanna Heitmann, Kathrin Fischer, Nicole Bühler, Nicolas Morath, Eva Nussbaumer, Susanne Zulauf, Ralf Kredel, Simon Maurer, Martin Dresler, Daniel Erlacher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesSensory stimulation during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep has been proposed as a method to induce lucidity, yet most approaches rely on distal sensory modalities such as light and sound. We explored whether bodily stimulation targeting the muscle and vestibular systems differentially influences dream incorporation and lucid awareness during REM sleep.Methods28 participants were assigned to electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) or galvanic vestibular stimulation (GVS) and completed a 2-week cognitive training protocol including dream journaling, reality checks, and association training, followed by two counterbalanced morning naps. One in the stimulation and one in the control condition, during which cues were delivered during REM sleep. Lucidity was assessed using external dream-report ratings and Dream Lucidity Questionnaire scores.ResultsNeither EMS nor GVS significantly increased direct or indirect dream incorporation relative to Control. EMS did not significantly increase lucidity. In contrast, GVS was associated with higher externally rated lucidity and higher Dream Lucidity Questionnaire-7 scores than Control. Lucidity rates were high across EMS conditions, suggesting possible contributions of cognitive training, expectancy, and baseline lucid dream frequency.ConclusionsTargeted stimulation of bodily systems during REM sleep may differentially influence dream experience. While somatosensory stimulation showed limited effects, vestibular stimulation was associated with increased lucid awareness despite minimal explicit incorporation into dream content. These findings suggest that modulation of bodily self-processing during REM sleep may influence reflective awareness independently of direct sensory incorporation. Non-invasive vestibular stimulation may therefore represent a promising approach for investigating mechanisms of lucid awareness during sleep. Statement of SignificanceExternal stimulation during sleep is often assumed to promote lucid dreaming by becoming incorporated into dream content. This study suggests that this assumption may be incomplete. While muscle stimulation showed some incorporation without clear lucidity benefits, vestibular stimulation was associated with greater lucidity despite little explicit incorporation. These findings support the idea that different sensory systems may influence dreaming through distinct mechanisms, including effects on bodily self-processing and lucid awareness. It highlights vestibular stimulation as a promising direction for mechanistic lucid dream research and raises the broader question of whether dream incorporation is necessary for externally induced lucidity. Keywords: lucid dreaming, REM sleep, dream incorporation, galvanic vestibular stimulation, electrical muscle stimulation, vestibular processing, sensory stimulation

Introduction

Lucid dreaming (LD) is a state of consciousness in which individuals become aware that they are dreaming while remaining immersed in the dream experience [1]. During LDs, dreamers may gain partial control over their actions and the dream environment [2]. Within sleep research, consciousness generally refers to the presence of subjective experience, whereas awareness refers more specifically to monitoring, reflection upon, or access to the contents of that experience [3]. Dreaming represents a conscious state as subjective experiences are present throughout sleep, but these experiences typically occur without reflective self-awareness or insight into the dream state [4]. LD differs from non-LD because dreamers gain metacognitive awareness that they are dreaming while remaining asleep, thereby reintroducing aspects of self-reflection and monitoring that are typically reduced during REM sleep [1, 5] Although spontaneous LD is relatively rare, it has attracted growing scientific interest due to its potential applications in the treatment of nightmares [6], insomnia and other sleep-related psychopathies [7], creativity and problem solving [8], and motor skill learning [9, 10].

Dream experiences are shaped not only by memory, emotion, and imagination, but also by signals originating from the sleeping body. External and internal sensory information can be incorporated into dreams, a phenomenon known as dream incorporation [11, 12]. Previous research has demonstrated that sensory cues presented during sleep, such as auditory tones, light flashes, or tactile stimulation can be integrated into dream content either directly or indirectly [11]. When paired with presleep cognitive training, such cues may facilitate LD induction through approaches such as Targeted Lucidity Reactivation (TLR) [13]. Most LD induction studies have relied on distal sensory cues, particularly visual and auditory stimulation [14]. However, bodily sensations are central to dream experience, especially in LDs, where dreamers often report heightened awareness of movement, balance, and bodily agency [15–17], raising the possibility that more direct stimulation of bodily systems may provide effective cues for influencing dream awareness.

One such approach is electrical muscle stimulation (EMS), which induces brief muscle contractions and somatosensory feedback. Despite REM-related muscle atonia, EMS-induced sensations can reach the dreaming brain and be incorporated into dream content [11, 18, 19]. Tactile and proprioceptive stimulation has previously been associated with isolated cases of signal-verified LDs [20], but systematic evaluations of EMS for LD induction under controlled conditions remain limited. Another bodily system closely linked to dream phenomenology is the vestibular system. Vestibular sensations such as flying, floating, or falling are frequently reported in LDs and have been associated with increased dream body awareness and control [21, 22]. The vestibular system contributes to bodily self-consciousness, including self-location, self-motion perception, and self-agency, processes that are also central to the experience of dreaming [23]. Importantly, REM sleep is characterized by internally generated sensorimotor activity, altered sensory input, and strong activation of cortical regions involved in multisensory bodily processing, creating conditions under which vestibular processing may contribute to dream experience [24, 25]. Unlike classical sensory systems with relatively localized cortical representations, vestibular signals are processed across distributed multisensory networks including temporo-parietal, insular, and sensorimotor regions involved in bodily self-processing [26]. Galvanic vestibular stimulation (GVS) offers a non-invasive method to activate vestibular afferents during sleep [18], yet its potential role in dreaming remains largely unexplored. Because vestibular processing contributes to bodily self-representation rather than merely conveying discrete sensory information, vestibular stimulation may influence lucid awareness even without explicit incorporation into dream narratives.

Although both somatosensory and vestibular stimulation have been shown to influence dream content, it remains unclear whether these modalities differ in their ability to facilitate LD, particularly when combined with structured cognitive training and compared against no-stimulation control conditions. Moreover, it is unknown whether direct incorporation of a bodily stimulus is necessary for enhancing lucidity, or whether more indirect modulation of dream phenomenology may be sufficient.

We explored whether EMS or GVS delivered during REM sleep, in combination with cognitive training and presleep cueing, would differentially affect dream incorporation and lucidity relative to sham stimulation. We further examined whether direct incorporation of externally delivered bodily cues is necessary for increased lucid awareness.

Materials and methods

Participants and ethics

Participants (n = 28) were recruited through advertisements in social media groups and on the campus of the University of Bern, Switzerland. Exclusion criteria included low dream recall (<3 dreams/week), late bedtimes (>01:00), prolonged sleep latency (>1 h), a personal or family history of epilepsy, sleep-related or current psychiatric disorders, or a LD frequency greater than once per month. The study was approved by the Faculty of Human Sciences Ethics Committee, University of Bern (Nr. 2022-01-00008). All participants provided written informed consent and received financial compensation. Data protection was ensured through pseudonymous coding.

Design and procedure

Participants were allocated to the EMS or GVS group using alternating assignment at enrollment to maintain balanced group sizes; allocation was therefore quasi-random rather than fully randomized.

The study followed a 2.5–3-week protocol comprising an adaptation nap, a training phase, and two counterbalanced test naps, one with stimulation, one with sham stimulation (Fig. 1). Each participant completed three morning nap sessions. An adaptation nap (day 1) was conducted without polysomnography (PSG) and sensory stimulation. Morning naps were selected because previous laboratory LD induction studies have demonstrated that early-morning REM sleep provides favorable conditions for LD induction due to increased REM density and duration while allowing controlled laboratory assessment within a standardized protocol [13].

Study protocol. On day 1, participants underwent an adaptation morning nap to familiarize themselves with the laboratory environment and assess dream recall. During days 2–14, participants completed a 2-week training phase consisting of daily dream journaling and reality checking at home and six 10-min laboratory-based association training sessions, during which sensory stimulation (electrical muscle stimulation or galvanic vestibular stimulation) was paired with lucidity-related instructions. Immediately before the experimental naps, a targeted lucidity reactivation (TLR) protocol was administered. On the last 2 days, participants completed two counterbalanced experimental morning naps under polysomnographic monitoring, receiving either stimulation (Stimulation) or sham stimulation (Control) during REM sleep. Following each REM awakening, participants provided a verbal dream report and completed the DLQ-7 lucidity questionnaire.

Participants arrived at 8:00 am and gave informed consent before the nap. They slept until 11:30 am and were excluded if they reported significant sleep difficulties or no dream recall. If they were excluded at this point, another participant was recruited to reach the required sample size. The training phase (week 2) occurred between the adaptation and test naps, with participants completing a cognitive training protocol consisting of daily dream journaling, reality checks, and six 10-min association training sessions, scheduled flexibly across afternoons. The test naps (week 3) were conducted on two consecutive mornings, with sessions starting at 7:00 am. Each participant received one stimulation (Stimulation) nap (EMS or GVS) and one sham stimulation (Control) nap, with the order counterbalanced across and within groups. The pre-sleep audio protocol, adapted from Carr et al. (2023), was administered before sleep. Participants were repeatedly reminded of their task to become lucid and perform a left–right–left–right (LRLR) eye-movement signal during wakefulness throughout the protocol. Instructions were provided verbally during PSG setup, incorporated into the daytime association training sessions, and repeated within the pre-sleep audio protocol while stimulation was administered before sleep onset. No verbal instructions or reminders were presented during REM sleep itself; during REM periods, only the stimulation protocol was delivered.

Association training protocol

Participants completed daily dream journaling and six guided association sessions distributed across the training period. During these sessions, participants visited the laboratory during daytime hours and completed a 10-min training protocol in which a dedicated audio track was presented while 2-s EMS or GVS stimulation was administered at 60-s intervals. The training aimed to establish an association between bodily stimulation and the mental experience of lucidification. Participants were guided to recall a previous dream and imagine becoming lucid whenever stimulation was presented. At regular intervals, verbal prompts instructed participants to mentally explore the recalled dream, identify unusual or bizarre elements, and imagine recognizing that they were dreaming as they experienced the stimulation. Example prompts included: “Notice how aspects of this experience are different from your normal waking experience” and “Think about taking control over the dream characters and the dreamscape. What would you like to explore?” This daytime association track was distinct from the separate pre-sleep audio protocol administered immediately before the morning nap sessions.

Polysomnography

PSG, including electroencephalography (EEG), electrooculography (EOG), and electromyography (EMG), was utilized to monitor sleep stages based on AASM guidelines [27] during the test naps. PSG setup adhered to the 10–20 system, utilizing 32 active gLADYBIRD electrodes with the gUSBamp EEG system [28], using two horizontal EOG, chin EMG, and right earlobe reference. The sampling rate was 256 Hz. Experiment management and streaming were done using the University of Bern’s custom experiment management system Streamix. The sleep room had speakers, a microphone, and a camera for further observation and communication. PSG was used to identify sleep stages online and to guide REM-targeted stimulation; PSG signals were not analyzed as study outcomes.

Stimulation methods

The study tested two different stimuli: GVS and EMS, with a stimulus duration of 2 s.

Galvanic vestibular stimulation

Binaural GVS was administered using the NeuroConn DC-Stimulator Plus [29], and two 4.5 × 3.5 cm carbon rubber electrode pads behind the ear over the mastoid bone. The electrodes were cut to a size that enabled precise application on the mastoid bone. Based on previous research, the stimulus parameters were set to a level at which participants felt a slight sway sensation without an uncomfortable skin sensation from the current behind the ear while sitting on the bed with their eyes open. Stimulus parameters were as follows: intensity varied from 1 to 3 mA peak-to-peak, frequency was 1 Hz, duration was 2 s, phase was 0°, fade-in/out time was 0 s, and the number of cycles was 2 × 2π. These parameters resulted in a quick LRLR head sway. The amplitude varied across participants from 1000 to 2500 μA and intensity was individually calibrated before sleep to elicit a noticeable vestibular sensation while minimizing discomfort; discomfort was not systematically quantified, but stimulation intensity was reduced if participants verbally reported discomfort during calibration or testing.

Electrical muscle stimulation

Two 50 × 100 mm self-adhesive TENS-EMS gel electrodes were placed on the volar side of the left forearm, targeting the flexor muscles responsible for ring-finger flexion. The distal electrode was positioned ~2–3 cm proximal to the wrist crease, aligned longitudinally with the forearm on the ulnar side. The proximal electrode was placed 5–6 cm above the distal electrode along the same axis. This placement corresponded to the anatomical course of the wrist flexor muscle group involved in flexion of the ring finger. The Rehamove 3 “ScienceMode” Device facilitated electrical stimulation, allowing external trigger control via connection to a computer [30]. EMS intensity was individually calibrated before sleep to elicit visible ring and pinky finger flexion while minimizing discomfort; discomfort was not systematically quantified, but stimulation intensity was reduced if participants verbally reported discomfort during calibration or testing. EMS was delivered with a pulse period of 20 ms (50 Hz), with no amplitude ramp, a waveform defined by a single stimulation point of 3 mA with a pulse duration of 500 μs. The stimulation was applied as a continuous pulse train lasting ~2 s.

TLR protocol

The presleep protocol was administered to reactivate the trained association between stimulation and lucidity. Participants were reminded of their task to become lucid and perform a LRLR eye-movement signal while attending to the stimulation. The protocol comprised 5 min of stimulation at 1-min intervals paired with verbal lucidity prompts, followed by 8 min of stimulation at 1-min intervals without verbal cues, and three final stimulations spaced 2, 4, and 7 min apart. After the final stimulation, participants were instructed to fall asleep, which marked the Lights Out time point.

Rapid eye movement stimulation protocol

Rapid eye movement (REM) periods were identified online from the PSG recordings using AASM criteria. REM sleep onset was defined as the first 30-s epoch showing low-amplitude mixed-frequency EEG activity, REMs in the EOG channels, and reduced chin EMG tone. Stimulation was applied only during clearly identifiable and stable REM periods. After REM onset, a 5-min timer was started to ensure stable REM sleep and allow sufficient time for dream development before stimulation delivery. Following this wait period, a series of 2-s stimulations at a 30-s interstimulus interval was presented continuously during REM sleep, as identified online via PSG monitoring. A minimum of 1 and a maximum of 30 stimulations was presented. In the control condition, no stimulation was delivered. Instead, a timing protocol identical to the Stimulation condition was followed, including the same waiting periods and REM-based timing structure, but without presenting EMS or GVS. Thus, participants experienced only the passage of time during sham periods rather than subthreshold or simulated stimulation. If a scheduled REM period did not occur or was interrupted by wakefulness, the corresponding stimulation or Control trial was postponed to the next available REM period rather than excluded. Consequently, participants did not always complete the same number of trials, and the assignment of conditions to specific REM periods varied across individuals. Nevertheless, the predefined counterbalancing ensured that, at the group level, stimulation and sham trials were distributed across REM periods with similar frequency.

REM awakenings

REM awakenings were initiated following AASM scoring criteria. Participants were awakened when REM sleep ended naturally or, if REM sleep persisted, after completion of the stimulation protocol. In the Stimulation condition, this occurred after all scheduled stimulations had been delivered (up to a maximum of 30). In the Control condition, participants were awakened after an equivalent waiting period had elapsed, determined by a timer matched to the duration required for 30 stimulation intervals. Once the participant confirmed being awake, they were asked: “What went through your head before you woke up?” After the participant completed their report, the follow-up question “Did you notice anything else?” was asked to gather additional details. Participants completed the Dream Lucidity Questionnaire (DLQ-7) [31]. Dream reports were audio-recorded. If time remained in the nap, participants were allowed to return to sleep, and the stimulation protocol was reinitiated during the next REM phase.

Data analysis

Audio dream reports were transcribed into written form and processed according to our Dream Report Conversion Manual, removing non-dream-related content. Because the dream reports were collected in several languages, all reports were translated into English using DeepL Translator to ensure high-quality and contextually appropriate translations. The translated reports were then randomized and evaluated by two external raters who were blind to the study conditions. Inter-rater agreement across all coding categories was 89.47%, and disagreements were resolved through consensus scoring by a third researcher.

Dream incorporation

Dream incorporation of the presented stimuli was analyzed following previously established methods [19]. Dream reports were scored by two independent researchers using a predefined dream content coding manual containing modality-specific incorporation categories. For both modalities, direct stimulus incorporation was scored using binary yes/no ratings, immediately following the open dream report, reflecting whether participants explicitly mentioned sensations or experiences corresponding to the stimulation modality. Additionally, for GVS, the category “Balance-Related Activities” was scored using the question: “Does the dreamer perform activities involving balance, such as flying or walking across narrow surfaces, or experience balance-related difficulties such as dizziness or fear of heights?”. For EMS, the categories “Movement of the Arms” and “Tactile or Somatosensory Sensations of the Arms” were scored using the questions: “Does the dreamer explicitly describe activities involving arm movements (e.g. waving, manual labor, boxing)?” and “Does the dreamer experience tactile or somatosensory sensations involving the arms?”

Lucidity measurements

Two complementary measures of lucidity were used to assess the prevalence of LD in this study:

External Rating of Lucidity (Ext_LD): Two independent researchers analyzed the open dream reports and if the dream report “showed any signs of explicit dream awareness or signs of metacognitive thinking or deliberate intention,” Ext_LD was scored as present.

External Rating of Lucidity (Ext_LD): Two independent researchers analyzed the open dream reports and if the dream report “showed any signs of explicit dream awareness or signs of metacognitive thinking or deliberate intention,” Ext_LD was scored as present.

Dream Lucidity Questionnaire (DLQ-7): This questionnaire is designed to evaluate subjective dream awareness (e.g. knowing one is dreaming, recognizing the unreality of the dream), control (e.g. ability to make choices, alter dream content, defy physical laws), and recall (e.g. remembering waking intentions or real-life facts). The DLQ includes 12 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“very much”). In our study, we excluded item 7, as this item is often interpreted as targeting dream recall rather than lucid awareness by participants.

Power analysis

An a priori power analysis was conducted based on effect sizes reported in previous LD induction studies [13, 32, 33], which have demonstrated medium to large effects for effective induction techniques. Assuming a medium-to-large effect size (w = 0.40), an alpha level of .05, and a desired power of 0.80, a power analysis for a design with three conditions (df = 2) indicated a required total sample size of N = 61. For a within-subject design, this corresponds to ~20 participants completing all conditions. This estimate is based on expected differences in lucidity rates comparable to prior findings (e.g. ~30% in Stimulation conditions vs. ~0% in control conditions). Under such assumptions, a sample size of ~20 participants in a within-subject design was considered sufficient to detect statistically significant differences in LD occurrence.

Statistics

All analyses were conducted in Jamovi [34]. The level of significance was set at p < .05. For binary outcomes (e.g. externally rated lucidity, incorporation), generalized linear mixed-effects models with a binomial distribution and logit link function were used. For continuous outcomes (DLQ-7 scores), linear mixed-effects models were applied. Participant was included as a random intercept in all models to account for the within-subject design. Analyses were conducted separately for the EMS and GVS groups. Descriptive statistics are reported as percentages for categorical variables and means ± standard deviations for continuous variables.

Dream incorporation

Dream incorporation analyses showed no robust differences between Stimulation and Control conditions in either group. In the EMS group, movement-related arm content occurred in 37.5% of stimulation dreams and 33.3% of Control dreams, while tactile or somatosensory arm content occurred in 25.0% of stimulation dreams and not in Control dreams. Direct EMS-related incorporation was observed in 37.5% of stimulation dreams and not in Control dreams. In the GVS group, balance-related dream content occurred in 33.3% of stimulation dreams and not in Control dreams, whereas direct vestibular incorporation was rare (11.1% in Stimulation, 0% in Control). Mixed-model analyses did not indicate reliable stimulation effects on movement-related dream content, while several incorporation outcomes produced unstable model estimates due to sparse observations and quasi-complete separation. Overall, although stimulation-related content occurred descriptively more often during Stimulation conditions, there was no consistent evidence that stimulation reliably increased explicit dream incorporation.

Lucid dreaming

Lucidity was evaluated using externally rated dream reports and DLQ-7 scores (see Table 3). There was no significant effect of EMS stimulation on externally rated lucidity (46.2% vs. 31.3%) or DLQ-7 scores. In contrast, GVS stimulation was associated with a higher probability of externally rated lucidity compared to Control (66.7% vs. 13.3%, χ2(1) = 6.91, p = .009). Similarly, DLQ-7 scores were significantly higher in the GVS-Stimulation condition compared to Control (F[1, 25] = 7.59, p = .011). Discussion

The present exploratory study investigated whether bodily stimulation targeting muscle or vestibular systems can influence LD induction when combined with cognitive training and targeted presleep cueing. Using a 2.5-week sham-controlled laboratory design, we compared EMS and GVS in terms of their effects on dream incorporation and lucidity. Overall, the findings suggest that vestibular stimulation may influence aspects of lucid awareness.

Dream incorporation and cue perception

Descriptively, EMS stimulation was more often incorporated into dream content than Control, with direct mentioning of EMS and somatosensory incorporations were observed in 37.5% and 25% of EMS Stimulation dreams, respectively, whereas no such incorporations occurred in the Control condition. However, these findings did not reach significance. It is possible that the EMS electrodes themselves served as a somatosensory cue, even in the Control condition. The physical presence of the electrodes on the arm, combined with repeated association training before sleep, may have increased bodily awareness or expectancy effects independently of active stimulation, thereby reducing differences between stimulation and Control conditions. This pattern is broadly consistent with previous findings on the incorporation of EMS into dreams [19]. GVS rarely led to explicit incorporation of the stimulation itself. However, balance-related dream content, such as sensations of instability or movement through space, was descriptively more commonly during stimulation than Control naps, although this difference was not significant. Similar indirect effects of vestibular stimulation on dream phenomenology have previously been reported using rocking paradigms or immersive virtual reality before sleep [22, 35].

Lucidity outcomes and measures

Lucidity was assessed using dream report analysis and questionnaire data, targeting different aspects of the LD experience. EMS stimulation did not significantly increase lucidity relative to Control. Importantly, in the EMS condition, lucidity rates were equally high in the Control conditions, particularly in the EMS group, compared to previous induction studies [14]. One possible explanation is that lucidity rates were already elevated due to the combination of cognitive training, presleep cueing, and the laboratory context, leaving little room for further increases from external stimulation. In addition, the EMS group contained fewer participants without prior LD experience than the GVS group. Because previous LD experience strongly predicts induction success [36], the EMS group may have been generally more responsive to the cognitive induction procedures, contributing to high lucidity rates in both stimulation and Control conditions.

In contrast, GVS stimulation was associated with higher lucidity ratings and DLQ-7 scores compared to the Control condition. Previous studies have similarly linked vestibular stimulation to LD, with increased lucidity reported during rocking in early-morning REM sleep and after immersive virtual reality flight simulations involving vestibular cues [22, 35]. Our use of GVS represents a more targeted approach to vestibular cueing during sleep. Although the stimulation was brief and subtle, the findings suggest that vestibular perturbations may influence lucid awareness without necessarily being explicitly incorporated into dream narratives. This dissociation between minimal incorporation and increased lucidity may reflect fundamental differences between vestibular and classical sensory processing. Unlike visual, auditory, or somatosensory systems, vestibular signals are processed within a distributed multisensory network involving regions such as the temporo-parietal junction, insula, and parietal operculum [24, 37]. Rather than appearing as discrete sensory events in dreams, vestibular input may influence higher-order aspects of bodily self-consciousness, including spatial orientation, self-location, and agency. Perturbations of these processes during REM sleep may destabilize the dream self-model, increasing the likelihood that dreamers detect inconsistencies in their experience and thereby enhancing reflective awareness.

From a predictive processing perspective, externally induced vestibular signals may also create a mismatch between internally generated bodily predictions and incoming sensory input during dreaming. Such sensory conflicts may disrupt ongoing bodily self-representation and alter the stability of the dream self-model without necessarily producing explicit incorporation of the stimulus into dream content. Similar multisensory and vestibular conflicts have been shown to influence bodily self-consciousness, self-location, and self-motion perception during wakefulness, including experimentally induced out-of-body illusions and altered embodiment experiences [38–40]. Recent work has further highlighted phenomenological overlaps between these bodily experiences and experiences reported during sleep and LD, including sensations of floating, flying, falling, and altered self-location [41]. Together, these findings support the possibility that vestibular stimulation may influence dreaming through modulation of bodily self-processing rather than through direct sensory incorporation alone. These effects could additionally involve modulation of frontoparietal networks implicated in metacognitive processing during LD.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was modest, limiting statistical power and generalizability. The stimulation modalities also differed in several procedural and sensory characteristics that may have contributed to their distinct effects. EMS produces a focal and salient somatosensory signal accompanied by visible finger movement, whereas GVS induces a more diffuse perturbation of balance and spatial orientation. EMS was generally more noticeable and occasionally resulted in brief arousals or awakenings, which were excluded for final analysis, potentially reducing its effectiveness as a cue during REM sleep. In contrast, GVS may have been less disruptive to sleep continuity while still influencing bodily self-processing. Furthermore, the stimulation sites, subjective perceptual qualities, and underlying neural pathways differed substantially between modalities, making direct comparisons challenging. In addition, the stimulation parameters, particularly for GVS, were brief and subtle (2-s, 1 Hz stimulation), and alternative timing, duration, or intensity parameters may produce different effects. Because the vestibular system typically responds to sustained or dynamic motion signals [37], future studies should investigate whether longer or continuous vestibular stimulation increases incorporation potential or lucidity effects. EMS occasionally caused premature awakenings, highlighting the need for adaptive stimulation protocols that dynamically adjust intensity to minimize sleep disruption [42].

An additional limitation is that tonic and phasic REM sleep were not differentiated during stimulation delivery or analysis. These REM substates differ substantially in their neurophysiological characteristics, sensory responsiveness, and cortical activation patterns, with phasic REM generally showing greater internally generated activity and altered processing of external sensory information compared to tonic REM [43]. Consequently, bodily stimulation may differentially affect dream incorporation and lucid awareness depending on the underlying REM substate. Because stimulation was delivered based on online REM identification rather than REM substage classification, potential differences between tonic and phasic REM could not be evaluated. Also, no formal offline sleep scoring was performed to retrospectively verify REM periods according to AASM criteria. Although stimulation was delivered only during clearly identifiable REM periods using established online PSG criteria, the absence of offline verification means that some stimulation periods may not have occurred during objectively confirmed REM sleep.

More broadly, bodily sensations in dreams and dream incorporation may reflect partially distinct REM sleep processes. Bodily representations are a fundamental feature of dreaming and may emerge from internally generated body models and bodily self-processing mechanisms independent of external sensory input. In contrast, dream incorporation requires the successful processing and integration of externally presented sensory information into ongoing dream content. It is therefore conceivable that different aspects of REM sleep support the generation of bodily dream experiences versus the incorporation of external sensory cues, a possibility that future studies should investigate. A further limitation is that some participants did not recall dreams during certain nap conditions. Although the absence of dream recall does not necessarily imply the absence of dreaming or LD, lucidity could not be assessed without reportable dream content. Consequently, the present study may underestimate the occurrence of lucid experiences that were subsequently forgotten after awakening.

Future research should replicate these findings in larger samples and further disentangle the contributions of cognitive training, expectancy, bodily stimulation, and REM sleep instability to LD induction. Combining vestibular and somatosensory stimulation, tailoring stimulation parameters to individual sensitivity, or applying stimulation adaptively based on ongoing sleep physiology may further clarify how bodily signals influence reflective awareness during dreaming.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study suggests that bodily stimulation may influence aspects of dream experience and subjective lucidity in different ways. While EMS occasionally appeared in dreams without increasing lucidity beyond Control conditions, GVS was associated with higher lucidity despite minimal explicit incorporation into dream reports. These findings suggest that external stimulation during sleep may influence lucid awareness not only through recognizable dream cues, but also through subtle perturbations of bodily and spatial experience. Targeting the dream body may therefore provide a promising avenue for future dream engineering approaches aimed at supporting conscious awareness during sleep.
