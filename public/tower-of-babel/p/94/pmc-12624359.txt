Clarifying Order and Carry‐Over Effects in Sequential MRgFUS Thalamotomy and Pallidothalamic Tract Ablation
Authors: Mickael Aubignat
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

I read with interest the study by Chen et al. reporting immediate motor improvements when adding pallidothalamic tract (PTT) ablation after ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) thalamotomy within a single magnetic resonance–guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) session for tremor‐dominant Parkinson's disease (PD) [1]. While the work is timely, two design features make it difficult to ascribe the observed immediate benefit specifically to PTT. Uncontrolled order effect: All participants underwent VIM first, then PTT; the sequence was never counter‐balanced [1]. In such stepwise intraoperative paradigms, the second lesion often yields additional on‐table gains even when the first lesion already helps, raising the possibility of sequence‐dependent augmentation rather than pure target specificity (e.g., anterior VIM/ventral oral posterior (VOP) followed by posterior subthalamic area (PSA)/zona incerta (ZI) in essential tremor) [2].Carry‐over and microlesional/edematous effects: I appreciate the authors' efforts to minimize carry‐over (scoring immediately before the first effective PTT sonication; latency < 10 min) and their acknowledgement that minor subthalamic nucleus (STN) involvement is possible given proximity [1]. Nonetheless, transient microlesional/edematous change and temperature‐dependent conduction block are inherent to within‐session MRgFUS workflows with repeated testing, making immediate pre/post at the second step vulnerable to residual effects from the first step. Notably, contemporary “staged bilateral” MRgFUS programs separate sides by ≥ 9 months specifically to reduce acute carry‐over and expectation confounds (an illustration of how timing safeguards causal attribution) [3].

To better isolate a PTT‐specific effect, future work might: (i) randomize or at least counter‐balance target order (PTT‐first vs. VIM‐first) in a subset; (ii) standardize and report dose/lesion metrics for each step (peak/average temperature, thermal dose, lesion volume) and provide tract/region overlap maps; (iii) use centralized, blinded video ratings and objective kinematics at pre‐specified timepoints immediately before and after each step; and (iv) include an intraprocedural VIM‐only control condition. Finally, since radiofrequency PTT tractotomy can improve rigidity and bradykinesia independently in akinetic‐rigid PD, a rigorous design is needed to quantify any incremental PTT contribution beyond VIM, rather than conflating plausible PTT efficacy with order‐ and dose‐related phenomena [4]. In summary, Chen et al. advance an important dual‐target concept in PD. Clarifying order and carry‐over effects using the above controls would strengthen the inference that the immediate add‐on effect is truly PTT‐specific.
