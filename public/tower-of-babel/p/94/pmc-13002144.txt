Successful Pregnancy and Delivery Following Autologous Abdominal Wall Reconstruction: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Ryo Kitai, Yoshichika Yasunaga, Aya Watarai, Shinya Suzuki, Jun Araki, Hirohisa Katagiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Abdominal wall reconstruction in women of childbearing age represents a complex and rarely reported clinical challenge. Various reconstructive techniques, including mesh repair and autologous tissue flaps, have been described, but no consensus has been established regarding optimal management. We present the fifth documented case of successful pregnancy and delivery following autologous abdominal wall reconstruction. A 35-year-old woman with a desmoid tumor underwent surgical removal, resulting in an 11 × 13 cm abdominal wall defect. Reconstruction was performed using a pedicled fascia lata flap based on perforators from the lateral circumflex femoral artery. The patient was advised to defer pregnancy for 1 year postoperatively. Twenty-eight months after reconstruction, she underwent cesarean delivery through a vertical incision to avoid the reconstructed area. No complications, such as abdominal wall hernia or bulging, were observed during the perinatal period or at 3 years and 2 months postoperatively. This case demonstrated that large abdominal wall defects can be safely reconstructed with vascularized tissue, yielding favorable perinatal outcomes during subsequent pregnancy. Our experience underscores the potential of vascularized fascial flaps as a durable and physiologically integrated option for abdominal wall reconstruction in women who desire future pregnancies. Further case accumulation is warranted to establish evidence-based guidelines for surgical and perinatal management.

Various reconstructive techniques, including mesh repair and thigh-based flaps, have been reported for managing abdominal wall defects in women of childbearing age.1–6 However, no standardized treatment has been established. Several classification systems and algorithms for abdominal wall reconstruction have been proposed,7 but most focus on defect size and location rather than considering future pregnancy.

Pregnancy and delivery are possible after mesh-based abdominal wall reconstruction, which is less invasive than autologous tissue reconstruction; however, reports have described pain during pregnancy and procedural challenges during delivery.1–3 One report noted persistent mesh-related pain during pregnancy, necessitating continuous analgesia from 34 weeks of gestation until delivery.1 Another case documented the need for vacuum-assisted delivery due to insufficient intra-abdominal pressure.2 In another report, epidural anesthesia was administered continuously throughout labor and delivery.3 Cesarean section after mesh repair can be technically challenging due to inflammation and adhesions.2

In contrast, only 4 cases of successful childbirth following autologous abdominal wall reconstruction have been reported to date, all involving defects measuring 8 cm in width or less.4 Herein, we present another case involving an 11-cm-wide defect reconstructed with a pedicled fascia lata flap, followed by cesarean delivery 28 months later. This case adds to the limited evidence regarding the safety of pregnancy and delivery following autologous reconstruction for large (>10 cm) abdominal wall defects.

CASE REPORT

A 35-year-old woman with a history of a transverse cesarean section 3 years earlier presented with a palpable mass in the right lower abdomen, first noticed 2 months prior. A biopsy at the referring hospital confirmed a desmoid tumor arising from the right external oblique muscle, and she was referred to our hospital for further management. The patient expressed her desire for future childbearing after tumor treatment.

She received 10 mg of oral meloxicam once daily for 6 months; however, the tumor enlarged from 5 × 5 cm at presentation to 8 × 9 cm during this period (Fig. 1). Consequently, the orthopedic oncology team performed surgical removal of the tumor.

Preoperative abdominal MRI (T2-weighted image) showing an 8 × 9 cm tumor in the external oblique muscle above the peritoneum. MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

The lesion was in the external oblique muscle above the peritoneum. After dissecting the skin and subcutaneous tissue overlying the external oblique fascia, the tumor was identified and excised with a marginal margin.

The abdominal wall defect measured 11 × 13 cm (Fig. 2) and was reconstructed with a pedicled fascia lata flap by the plastic surgery team. This flap was essentially a skin-paddle–sparing anterolateral thigh flap that was based on 2 perforators from the lateral circumflex femoral artery and included a 13 × 15 cm segment of fascia lata combined with the iliotibial tract. The flap was tunneled subcutaneously and inset into the abdominal wall defect (Fig. 3). It was anchored to the internal and external oblique muscles, pubic periosteum, and anterior rectus sheath with 2-0 Vicryl (polyglactin 910) sutures to ensure durable abdominal wall reconstruction. Postoperatively, the patient was instructed to wear an abdominal binder for at least 3 months to minimize intra-abdominal pressure and to defer pregnancy for 1 year after reconstruction.

After surgical removal of the tumor, the abdominal wall defect measured 11 × 13 cm.

After flap elevation and transfer, a pedicled fascia lata flap was harvested from the right thigh and tunneled subcutaneously to the defect.

Pregnancy was achieved 20 months following abdominal wall reconstruction. Throughout the pregnancy, the patient wore an abdominal binder and experienced no pain at the reconstructed site. At 28 months after reconstruction, she underwent cesarean delivery through a vertical incision. A repeat cesarean section through the previous transverse incision was not performed because it would have required incising the reconstructed abdominal wall, including the vascularized fascia lata flap, which might have compromised its integrity.

No abdominal wall hernia or bulging was observed during the perinatal period or at 3 years and 2 months after reconstruction. DISCUSSION

Here, we describe a 35-year-old woman who underwent autologous abdominal wall reconstruction for an abdominal wall defect larger than 10 cm following excision of a desmoid tumor. She subsequently underwent cesarean delivery 28 months later without complications.

Desmoid tumors of the abdominal wall are rare but occur more frequently in young women of reproductive age, and their growth may be influenced by pregnancy-related hormonal changes.8 Although nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs can be effective,9 surgical excision is recommended when medical therapy fails.10 Given the frequency of desmoid tumors in young women, reconstructive planning should account for future pregnancy.

To date, only 4 cases of pregnancy and delivery after abdominal wall reconstruction with vascularized tissue have been reported (Table 1), in which the defects were located laterally and measured less than 10 cm in width, thereby contributing to favorable outcomes, including the absence of hernia or bulging during pregnancy.5 In contrast, the present case involved a defect measuring more than 10 cm in both dimensions. Nevertheless, no perinatal complications occurred, suggesting that larger defects can be successfully managed with robust reconstructive techniques.

Evidence regarding pregnancy outcomes after autologous abdominal wall reconstruction remains limited. Further studies are required to clarify the following considerations and to allow comparisons with mesh-based reconstruction.

First, the optimal mode of delivery remains uncertain. Vaginal delivery may increase intra-abdominal pressure on the reconstructed area, whereas cesarean section may create additional scarring or stress on the reconstructed area. Second, the optimal timing for conception and delivery after abdominal wall reconstruction also remains undefined. Although 1 report advised avoiding vaginal delivery within 2 years after reconstruction,2 successful deliveries at shorter intervals have also been documented (Table 1).

Third, the comparative role of vascularized versus nonvascularized fascia grafts remains uncertain. To our knowledge, no cases of delivery after abdominal wall reconstruction using a nonvascularized fascia graft have been reported. Fourth, reconstruction using functional muscle may theoretically provide dynamic abdominal wall support through contraction, although its clinical advantage over fascial reconstruction has yet to be clarified.

CONCLUSIONS

This report represents the fifth documented case of successful pregnancy and delivery following autologous abdominal wall reconstruction. Despite the large abdominal wall defect, the patient experienced a favorable perinatal course and no abdominal wall herniation or bulging.
