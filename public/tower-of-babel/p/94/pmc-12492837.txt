CBP/p300, a promising therapeutic target for prostate cancer
Authors: Hongtao Xu, Yifan Hou, Zhenhua Zhao, Jianhua Zhang, Pan Li, Yujia Cao, Xiaobo Nie, Junqing Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer (PCa) remains the most prevalent malignancy among men, with the inevitable emergence of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) presenting the greatest challenge. Accumulating evidence has confirmed that the overexpression of cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB)-binding protein (CBP) and E1A-binding protein (p300), two highly homologous transcriptional coactivators, plays a crucial role in the development of PCa and its progression to CRPC, thereby making them prominent therapeutic targets for all types of PCa. In this review, we systematically discuss the structure and function of CBP/p300 and elucidate the detailed mechanisms by which CBP/p300 promote prostate carcinogenesis and development. Specifically, CBP/p300 facilitate prostate carcinogenesis by acetylating specific lysine residues on essential transcription factors involved in androgen receptor (AR) signaling, canonical Wnt signaling, p53 signaling, as well as other pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling. Additionally, they contribute to tumor immunosuppression and adaptive resistance to programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) blockade treatment by inducing the expression and secretion of the PD-L1 protein. Furthermore, we explore the latest advances in the use of various inhibitors targeting different domains of CBP/p300 and proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTAC) degraders in PCa. We propose that combing CBP/p300 inhibitors or degraders with current anti-PCa therapies, including androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, holds potential to overcome the challenges in treating advanced PCa and improve clinical outcomes for all PCa patients.Graphical abstract Keywords: CBP/p300, Prostate cancer, AR signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathways, Inhibitors, Therapeutic target

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most common malignant tumor in men, with an incidence rate ranking first among male malignancies and a mortality rate second only to lung cancer. The latest statistics indicate that since 2014, the incidence of PCa in North America has been increasing at a rate of 3% per year [1]. It is projected that by 2040, the number of new PCa cases worldwide will increase from 1.4 million in 2020 to 2.9 million. The pathogenesis of PCa is complex, with major risk factors including age, race, family history, genetic mutations, metabolic syndrome, obesity, and smoking. Additionally, environmental factors, lifestyle, and infections also contribute to the occurrence of PCa [2]. Currently, due to the popularization of PCa-related screening programs such as prostate specific antigen (PSA) detection and biopsy, and westernization of dietary habits, the incidence of PCa has shown an annual increasing trend in developing countries, particularly in China [3]. Most PCa sufferers lack typical early symptoms and are easily confused with conditions such as prostate hyperplasia, and existing early screening programs have limitations, leading some patients to miss the optimal treatment window [4, 5]. For early PCa, clinical practice relies primarily on androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT), including surgical or pharmacological castration, which function by inhibiting the activation of androgen-dependent androgen receptor (AR) signaling pathway. Second-generation endocrine therapeutic agents such as abiraterone acetate (AA), enzalutamide, and apalutamide are also widely used in clinical settings. However, although ADT can significantly improve prognosis and increase survival rates in early PCa, nearly all patients eventually progress to castration-resistant PCa (CRPC) and further to metastatic CRPC (mCRPC). At this stage, as patients become resistant to ADT, traditional chemotherapy remains the only treatment option, with the 5-year survival rate plummeting to 3%, leaving most patients in a state of despair. The primary molecular characteristics of CRPC include spontaneous activation of the AR signaling and restoration of serum PSA levels [6], driven by mechanisms such as AR gene mutations, enhancer amplification of AR gene, expression of incomplete AR splice variants, elevated co-regulatory proteins, and intratumoral androgen synthesis [7, 8]. Furthermore, due to the low infiltration of lymphocytes in PCa foci and low tumor mutation burden, immunotherapy is ineffective for most PCa patients, highlighting the clinical significance and the necessity of exploring new therapeutic targets and developing novel targeted drugs.

The cAMP response element binding protein (CREB)-binding protein (CBP) and E1A-binding protein (p300) are homologous and highly conserved proteins that act as transcriptional coactivators, participating in the activation of both the AR and Wnt signaling pathways. Aberrant activation of these pathways has been confirmed to contribute to the occurrence and progression of PCa to CRPC [9]. CBP/p300 also acetylate AR to prevent its degradation and maintain AR signaling activity [10]. Additionally, CBP/p300 is involved in inducing the expression and extracellular secretion of programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) protein in PCa, thereby inhibiting the effectiveness of tumor immunotherapy [11]. Consistently, CBP/p300 not only exhibits higher mRNA levels in primary PCa tissues but is also expressed in AR-null CRPC loci [12]. Transcriptome sequencing analysis of tissues obtained from advanced CRPC patients treated with ADT and developed resistance revealed that the mRNA levels of CBP/p300 in CRPC tissues were significantly higher than in primary PCa and positively correlated with AR mRNA levels [13]. This suggests that CBP/p300 is required for the development of PCa and its progression to CRPC. Strikingly, accumulating evidence indicates that inhibiting CBP/p300 expression or activity can suppress PCa and CRPC through multiple mechanisms, making it a promising target for treating all types of PCa. In this article, we provide a detailed review of the structure and function of CBP/p300, its mechanisms in the pathogenesis and progression of PCa, and the role and future application prospects of its targeted drugs in the treatment of PCa, aiming to provide more therapeutic options for all PCa patients.

The structure and function of CBP/p300

The structure of CBP/p300

CBP/p300 are histone acetyltransferases (HATs) with significant sequence and functional similarity [14]. CBP, also known as KAT3A, is encoded by the CREBBP gene, while p300 (KAT3B), is encoded by the EP300 gene. Both proteins comprise eleven conserved functional domains (Fig. 1A): the nuclear receptor interaction domain (NRID), transcriptional adapter zinc-binding 1 (TAZ1) domain, kinase-inducible domain interacting (KIX) domain, cell cycle regulatory domain 1 (CRD1), acetyl-lysine binding bromodomain (BRD), really interesting new gene (RING) domain, plant homeodomain (PHD), HAT domain, ZZ-type zinc finger domain (ZZ), TAZ2 domain, and interferon-binding domain (IBiD) [15]. The most studied domains include BRD, HAT, KIX, PHD, RING, TAZ2, and ZZ [16].

The HAT domain is essential for CBP/p300 stability and mainly acetylates lysine residues on histones, loosening chromatin structure and promoting gene transcription accessibility. CBP/p300 catalyzes the substrates acetyl-coenzyme A and lysine-containing proteins to generate coenzyme A (CoA) and acetylated lysine-containing proteins. Specifically, CBP/p300 transfers the acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to the ε-amino group of the lysine side chain, releasing free thiol-CoA. This process follows a Theorell-Chance mechanism, where no stable ternary complex is formed, and there is only a transient interaction between the peptide substrate and the enzyme, which complicates the resolution of the CBP/p300 crystal structure. Within the HAT domain, an unstructured, positively charged autoinhibitory loop (AL) acts as an intramolecular “pseudo-substrate”, preventing similarly positively charged lysine from accessing the active site. The inactive conformation of the RING domain can reduce the catalytic activity of p300 by restricting the accessibility of the active site to the AL. Activation of HAT involves the dimerization of certain transcription factor ligands, leading to p300 trans-autoacetylation in the AL, rearranging the autoinhibitory RING domain, and faciliating substrate entry into the HAT active site [17]. Deleting the AL impairs histone and p53 acetylation [18]. Acetylation of AL can bind to its own BRD, inhibiting CBP/p300 HAT activity by promoting their dissociation from chromatin [18].

The BRD domain, with a 98% sequence homology between CBP and p300, forms a hydrophobic pocket for acetyl-lysine binding [19]. Binding to acetylated lysine residues enhances chromatin accessibility, promoting transcription factors recruitment to gene promoters and enhancer. This facilitates the assembly and activation of the paused RNA polymerase II (RNAPol II) complex, and subsequent transcriptional activation of target genes [20]. The BRD also aids CBP/p300 binding to chromatin and enhances the acetylation of substrate histone by recognizing acetylated histone tails, increasing the accessibility of histone substrates, such as H3, to the HAT domain. Inhibiting BRD CBP/p300 in CRPC results in DNA repair de-enrichment and G2M checkpoint arrest, enhancing patients response to standard therapies [21].

The TAZ2 domain of CBP/p300 consists of four α-helices linked by three zinc-binding loops. The binding orientation of the amphipathic α-helix to TAZ2 possibly depend on specific interactions between polar residues and adjacent sequences with TAZ2 [22]. Transcription factors such as NF-κB and AR commonly bind to the TAZ2 domain. The TAZ2 domain inhibits the HAT domain, maintaining the HAT active site in a “closed” state. When transcription factors like p53 bind to TAZ2, they induce a conformational change that causes TAZ2 to dissociate from HAT, thereby converting the “closed” state to an “open” state for substrate acetylation. This switch enhances cellular sensitivity to histone deacetylase inhibitors [23]. The KIX domain of CBP/p300 is highly conversed and includes two distinct binding sites, designated as the mixed lineage leukemia (MLL)- and c-Myb/CREB-pKID-binding sites. Various transcription factors, including c-Jun, c-Myb, CREB, FOXO3a, MYB, and p53, bind to these allosterically regulated binding pockets [24, 25]. Biochemical analyses show that, compared to the CBP KIX domain, the p300 KIX domain exhibits a higher affinity for oncogenic transcription factor involved in proliferation and survival. This difference is possibly due to hydrophobic interactions between Ala627 of the p300 KIX domain and Met303 in MYB, while Asp647 in CBP KIX domain lacks this function [26].

The function of CBP/p300

CBP/p300 can acetylate specific lysine residues on basal transcription factors such as TATA-binding protein (TBP), TBP associated factors (TAFs), and RNA Pol II, as well as sequence-specific transcription factors like CREB, c-Jun, and c-Myb, thereby initiating RNAPol II transcription (Fig. 1B). Additionally, CBP/p300 serve as scaffolds for protein–protein interactions, recruiting other cofactors to regulate enhancer architecture, transcription factor interactions, nucleosome remodeling, enhancer-promoter communication, and transcriptional enhancer coordination (Fig. 1B) [27, 28]. Although CBP and p300 have similar functions and are often considered interchangeable, they exhibit subtle differences that enable unique roles. Cistrome mapping and transcriptome analyses of CBP and p300 in CRPC have revealed differences in their binding regions. CBP tends to binds to intergenic regions (42%), while p300 binds to introns (33%) and promoter (26%) regions. Additionally, motifs near CBP binding sites are enriched for Ets-factors Elk4 and Sp1, which are associated with oncogenic signaling. In contrast, motifs near p300 binding sites are enriched for GFY, ZNF143, and HOXB13, which are mainly related to mitochondrial signaling and zinc finger functions [21]. CBP/p300 may have opposing cellular processes in response to different stimuli, and mutations in the CBP/EP300 genes are even associated with the opposite progression of malignancies. Most described cancer-associated mutations in EP300 gene lead to a truncated p300 protein. Germline and point mutations in the CBP gene were initially identified in Rothmund-Thomson syndrome, an autosomal-dominant disease with a high malignancy risk, while such mutations have not been associated with EP300 gene [29]. Missense, nonsense, and frame-shift mutations or deletions in CBP gene are also found in a fraction of lung cancer [30], and 18.3% of relapsed acute lymphoblastic leukaemia cases have sequence or deletion mutations in CBP gene, impairing the function and transcriptional regulation of downstream targets [31]. EP300 loss-of-function enhances oncogenicity in bladder cancer by upregulating IL-1α signaling, which leads to the activation of the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway [32]. This suggests that CBP/p300 may act as tumor-suppressors in these cancer types. However, CBP/p300 overexpression contributes to uncontrollable cellular proliferation and aopotosis inhibition during the carcinogenesis and progression of lung and laryngeal cancers [33, 34]. In melanoma cells and clear cell renal cell carcinoma, knockdown of p300 inhibits their progression [35, 36]. In PCa, the induction of early growth response-1, which is upstream of CBP/p300, by growth stimuli such as serum, leads to increased transcription of CBP/p300, thereby promoting PCa cell survival and growth [37]. Conversely, the CBP/p300 inhibitor CCS1477 reduces AR and c-Myc signaling and sustainably decreases the expression of classical AR target genes, such as KLK3 and TMPRSS2 [13, 38]. This suggests that CBP/p300 are oncogenic regulators in PCa, and their inhibition could be a promising therapy for patients resistant to ADT.

CBP/p300 regulate gene transcription by modulating histone acetylation (Fig. 1B). Specifically, they can acetylate 20 distinct sites on the N-termini of core histones (H2A, H2B, H3, and H4) within nucleosomes. Notably, acetylation sites such as histone H3 lysine 18 and 27 (H3K18ac and H3K27ac) are predominantly located at promoters and enhancers, crucial for gene expression regulation [39]. Recent studies have revealed that CBP/p300 preferentially recognize histone H4 N-terminal tail acetylation (H4NTac) via its BRD pocket and interacts with the DNA minor groove outside this pocket. The N-terminus of histone H2B (H2BNT) is the main target for p300 acetylation through the recognition of H4NTac, facilitating H2A-H2B dissociation from nucleosomes. H2BNT can only be acetylated by CBP/p300, with RNA transcription detected in 79% of H2BNTac-positive regions [40]. Therefore, the acetyltransferase activity of CBP/p300, particularly towards H2BNTac, is essential for rapid activation of enhancers and RNAPol II recruitment at nearly all enhancers and enhancer-regulated genes [39, 41].

CBP/p300 also acetylates various non-histone proteins to regulate their activity. A recent mass spectrometry study identified over 200 non-histone transcription factors and coactivators acetylated by CBP/p300, many involved in key signaling pathways like Wnt, Notch, Hippo, TGF-β, and nuclear hormone receptors. It is now established that CBP/p300 acetylate transcription factors closely linked to PCa carcinogenesis, including p53, AR, ERα, and NF-kB. As an oncogenic ETS family member, ETS transcription factor ERG (ERG) frequently fuses with the AR target gene TMPRSS2 through chromosomal rearrangements, promoting PCa progression under androgen stimulation [42]. Importantly, p300 acetylates ERG, enabling its interaction with transcription cofactor bromodomain containing 4 (BRD4), enhancing the transcription of genes associated with invasive cancer phenotypes, such as Wnt5a. Consequently, p300 knockdown significantly reduces PCa aggressiveness [43, 44].

The mechanisms of CBP/p300 in promoting prostate carcinogenesis and development

The role of CBP/p300 in the AR signaling pathway

CBP/p300 are pivotal coregulators for numerous nuclear receptors (NRs), facilitating the acetylation of specific lysine residues in their substrate proteins. These substrates encompass acetylated histone H3, various transcription factors, and a broad range of NRs, such as the thyroid hormone receptor, estrogen receptor, retinoid X receptor, farnesoid X receptor, progesterone receptor, AR, and steroidogenic factor 1 [45–50]. The AR, one of the 48 human NRs, functions as a ligand-activated transcription factor that regulates the expression of multiple genes controlling muscle development and male reproductive organ maturation [51]. Extensive research indicates that CBP/p300 are integral to the AR signaling pathway. Aberrant activation of AR is a key driver in PCa development and progression, with CRPC occurrence and metastasis still partially dependent on spontaneous AR signaling activation [52, 53]. AR shares structural domains with other NRs, mainly consisting of the N-terminal DNA binding domain (DBD), the hinge region, and the C-terminal ligand-binding domain (LBD). In prostate cells, AR is typically sequestered in the cytoplasm by binding to heat shock proteins (HSP) under normal conditions [54]. When androgens or 5α-dihydrotestosterone (DHT) bind to the LBD of AR, HSP dissociates, revealing the nuclear localization signal domain on the AR hinge region. This leads to AR translocation to the nucleus and dimerization, where the dimerized receptors bind to half-sites in an inverted repeat DNA sequence [55]. Subsequently, AR binds specifically to the androgen response element (ARE) on ARE-driven genes, promoting the transcription of prostate proliferation-related genes such as KLK2, KLK3, and TMPRSS2 (Fig. 2). This transcriptional activation process also requires the participation of coactivators, among which the role of CBP/p300 is indispensable.

The binding of 5α-dihydrotestosterone (DHT), derived from testosterone (T), to AR induces the dissociation of HSP and a conformational change in AR, leading to dimerization and nuclear translocation. Within the nucleus, AR specifically binds to the androgen response element (ARE) on ARE-driven proliferation-promoting genes such as KLK3, and TMPRSS2, and subsequently inducing their transcription. Studies have demonstrated that AR is a direct substrate of CBP/p300, and transcriptional activation of DHT-activated AR requires further acetylation by CBP/p300 [10]. Approximately 47% of androgen-regulated genes depend on the acetylation of p300, whereas only 0.3% of them rely on CBP. Furthermore, p300 is essential for androgen-induced acetylation of histone H3, methylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4), and the recruitment of TBP and RNAPol II. Experiments based on p300 deficiency further confirmed that post-DHT activation, AR binding to the ARE necessitates p300 as a coregulator for chromatin remodeling and transcription complex assembly [56]. Additionally, the complex structure of the AR2/ARE/nuclear receptor coactivator 3 (SRC3)/p300, as elucidated by single-particle cryo-electron microscopy, reveals a unique head-to-head and tail-to-tail arrangement during AR activation. This configuration facilitates the recruitment of NR coactivators SRC3 and p300 within the N-terminal domain (NTD), rather than the LBD. This interaction pattern diverges from that of most NRs, which generally interact with their NTD domain and the LXXLL motif, a short sequence present in the receptor interaction domain of SRC3 [57]. AR mutants lacking LBD, such as incomplete AR splice variants, can still drive the transcription of downstream PCa-promoting genes [58].

The role of CBP/p300 in the canonical Wnt signaling pathway

The Wnt signaling pathways are highly conserved through evolution and play a crucial role in the pathogenesis, malignant progression, metastasis, and treatment resistance of various cancers, including PCa. Our recent publication has elaborated on the mechanisms by which the dysregulation of Wnt signaling pathways induce PCa pathogenesis and progression to CRPC through AR-dependent and AR-independent manners [59]. Based on the components and downstream target genes, the Wnt signaling pathways are primarily divided into canonical and noncanonical signaling pathways, with the activation of the canonical signaling mainly dependent on the accumulation and transcriptional activation of β-catenin. Under normal conditions, cytoplasmic β-catenin is phosphorylated by kinases within the destruction complex, which comprises adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β), Axin, and casein kinase 1α (CK1α), leading to its degradation by the proteasome following the ubiquitination. In contrast, during physiological processes such as embryonic development and tissue repair, as well as in pathological conditions such as carcinogenesis, elevated levels of Wnt ligands bind to frizzled class receptors (FZD) and LDL receptor related protein 5/6 (LRP5/6) coreceptors on the cell membrane to induce phosphorylation of the intracellular domain of LRP5/6 through the action of GSK3. The phosphorylated LRP5/6 binds Axin with high affinity and sequesters it away from the destruction complex. This binding also recruits Dishevelled proteins (DVLs), which are phosphorylated by CK1α and GSK3, forming a polymer that inactivate the destruction complex, thereby halting the β-catenin phosphorylation and degradation. Consequently, β-catenin accumulates and translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to transcription factors such as transcription factor (TCF)/lymphoid enhancer binding factor (LEF) to activate the transcription of downstream target genes determining the cell fate [60]. This transcriptional activation by TCF/LEF requires the participation of coactivators such as CBP/p300. In prostate tissues, most of these genes are oncogenic and capable of transforming the normal prostate cells or promoting the progression and metastasis of PCa cells, such as CCND1, c-Myc, and LGR5 (Fig. 3). Although the canonical Wnt signaling is not typically active in normal prostate cells and hormone-sensitive PCa, abnormal activation is detectable in approximately 18% of CRPC tissues. This abnormal activation is primarily due to the inactivation of negative regulators and the overexpression of activator in this cascade, including inactivating mutations of the APC gene and hypermethylation in its promoter region, activating mutations of β-catenin encoding gene, CTNNB1, and the upregulation of certain Wnt ligands or FZD expression levels. Therefore, the abnormal accumulation of β-catenin and the resultant transcriptional activation through its binding with LEF/TCF are core events in the overactivation of the canonical Wnt signaling pathway. Understanding its structure and regulatory mechanisms in this process is therefore crucial for developing novel targeted anti-cancer therapeutics.

CBP/p300 participate in the signaling transduction of the canonical Wnt pathway. Following palmitoylation by PORCN in the rough endoplasmic reticulum, Wnt ligands interact with Wnt ligand secretion mediator (WLS) and are released from the cell, where they bind to the FZDs and co-receptors LRP5/6. This interaction triggers GSK3 to phosphorylate the intracellular domain of LRP5/6. The phosphorylated LRP5/6 subsequently binds Axin, sequestering it from the destruction complex and recruiting DVLs. These DVLs are phosphorylated by CK1α and GSK3, forming a complex that inactivates the destruction complex and prevents β-catenin degradation. Consequently, β-catenin accumulates and translocates into the nucleus, where it binds to TCF/LEF transcription factors to activate target genes that influence cell fate such as CCND1, c-Myc, and LGR5. This transcriptional activation also requires the dissociation of transcriptional repressors, such as Groucho/transducin-like enhancer protein (TLE), and the recruitment of essential transcriptional coactivators, including CBP/p300, B-cell CLL/lymphoma 9 (BCL9), Brahma-related gene 1 (BRG1), and Pygo. The protein β-catenin, consisting of 781 amino acids, is comprised of a central structural core of 12 armadillo repeats (residues 138–665), an NTD (residues 1–137, abbreviated as βCATNTERM) and a C-terminal domain (CTD) (residues 666–781, abbreviated as βCATCTERM). The armadillo repeat domain sequences are more conserved than the terminal domains and form a positively charged groove that binds most β-catenin partners. In contrast, the terminal domains lack the ability to form a stably folded structure [61]. The βCATNTERM, upon phosphorylation, allows β-catenin to be recognized by β-TrCP1 ubiquitin ligase, leading to its proteasomal degradation [62]. TCF/LEF proteins bind to the 3–10 armadillo repeats of β-catenin, anchoring it to specific promoter regions of target genes for transcriptional activation. The first armadillo repeat of β-catenin is essential for binding the transcriptional coactivator B-cell lymphoma 9 (BCL9), which subsequently recruits Pygopus. The armadillo repeat 11 to βCATCTERM are responsible for binding other transcriptional coactivators, such as BRG1 and CBP/p300 [63]. Recent research indicates that the transactivation domains in βCATCTERM of β-catenin, particularly the C-terminal 31 amino acids, directly bind to the TAZ1 and TAZ2 of CBP/p300, thus facilitating the transcriptional activation of downstream target genes [64]. In the context of p53-dependent apoptosis, p53 competes with β-catenin to bind the TAZ2 domain of CBP/p300, thereby exerting its tumor suppressor function. Overaccumulation of β-catenin in cancer cells therefore inhibits the p53-dependent pathway by interfering with the association of p53 and CBP/p300 [65]. The acetylation of β-catenin by CBP/p300 at specific amino acid residues regulates its transcriptional activation ability, as acetylation of lysine 49 in β-catenin by CBP/p300 hinders the ability of β-catenin to activate the c-Myc gene [66]. Moreover, the phosphorylation of β-catenin at Ser675 in βCATCTERM by (cAMP)-dependent protein kinase (PKA) inhibits its ubiquitin-dependent degradation, resulting in the posttranslational stabilization of β-catenin and activation of canonical Wnt signaling [67].

The role of CBP/p300-mediated acetylation of p53 in promoting prostate carcinogenesis

P53 is a modular protein that binds to specific DNA sequences of target genes in a tetrameric form. Each subunit contains an N-terminal transactivation domain (TAD), a proline-rich domain (PRD), a core DBD, a tetramerization domain (TD), and a regulatory CTD (Fig. 4A) [68]. As a pivotal transcription factor, p53 plays an indispensable role in cellular stress response and tumor suppression by regulating the expression of genes involved in cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, metabolic adaptation, and apoptosis, thereby facilitating the repair or elimination of damaged cells through apoptosis or senescence pathways [69, 70]. Inactivation of p53 by missense mutation is identified in over half of all cancer types, and even in patients with wild-type p53, its function is frequently impaired due to genomic copy loss [71]. P53 alterations are also prevalent in men with treatment-naive metastatic PCa (36.1%) and CRPC but are less common in primary PCa [72, 73]. Radiation therapy combined with ADT treatment may worsen clinical outcomes for patients with localized PCa carrying P53 mutations [74], and complete loss or mutation of P53 also lead to the transformation of the PCa into a more aggressive neuroendocrine type [75].

The normal turnover of p53 is crucial for its function (Fig. 4B). CBP/p300, the first identified p53 acetyltransferase, acetylates p53 at multiple lysine residues in its CTD, thereby enhancing its stability and transcriptional activity. In unstressed cells, p53 polyubiquitination and degradation are mediated by a ternary complex comprising p53, the E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase mdm2 (MDM2) (also known as HDM2), and CBP/p300 [76]. Specifically, the activation domain 1/2 regions of the p53 TAD contain amphipathic binding motifs that can simultaneously bind to MDM2 and CBP/p300, forming the ternary complex [68]. Following DNA damage under stress conditions, p53 is phosphorylated at threonine 18, which inhibits the binding of p53 TAD to MDM2 but enhances its binding to the KIX domain of CBP/p300. Multisite phosphorylation of p53 TAD increases its affinity for the TAZ1 and KIX domains of CBP/p300 by tenfold, resulting in the stabilization and activation of p53 [68]. Recent structural analysis of the p300-p53 complex using cryo-electron microscopy reveals that the TAD of the p53 tetramer can bind to the NTD and CTD of p300 with varying affinities, triggering substantial rearrangement of the Bromo and HAT domains and exposing the HAT active site, thereby enhancing its acetyltransferase activity [77]. However, another study suggests that the NTD of CBP/p300 possesses both ubiquitin ligase (E3) and polyubiquitin ligase (E4) activities, which ubiquitinate and destabilize p53. This activity is restricted to the cytoplasm, while nuclear CBP/p300 still activates p53 [78]. In human lung adenocarcinoma cells, loss of CBP/p300 acetylation reduces the half-life and stability of p53, diminishes its transcriptional activation ability, and induces cell death [79].

The involvement of CBP/p300 in the extracellular secretion of PD-L1 to promote PCa

Protein PD-1, also known as CD279, is encoded by the programmed cell death 1 (PDCD1) gene located on chromosome 2q37. Its ligand, PD-L1, is encoded by CD274 gene on chromosome 9p24.1 [80]. Under normal physiological conditions, the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway inhibits cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) effector function, thus protecting body tissues from autoimmune attacks and maintaining peripheral immune tolerance [81]. However, within the tumor microenvironment (TME), the binding of PD-1 on T cells to PD-L1 expressed by cancer cells attenuates anti-tumor immunity, leading to immune tolerance of cancer cells and promoting their immune evasion [82]. Consequently, the immunosuppression allows tumor cells to escape recognition and destruction by the immune system, thereby facilitating their proliferation and metastasis [83]. Currently, blocking PD-1/PD-L1 recognition with therapeutic antibodies to overcome tumor immune evasion has emerged as an effective strategy for cancer treatment. However, its efficacy in PCa is limited, which some studies attribute to the paucity of PD-L1 expression in PCa cells [84]. Contradictorily, other studies report high expression of PD-L1 in 52.2% of aggressive primary PCa cases and approximately 19% of CRPC patients [85, 86]. Recent research has found that although PD-L1 translocation to the cell membrane is rare in primary PCa, cancer cells in the TME can secret PD-L1 in exosomes in an interferon gamma (IFN-γ) dependent manner, or as PD-L1 splicing variants lacking the transmembrane domain, contributing to tumor immunosuppression and resistance to PD-L1 blockade therapy [87, 88]. This suggests that merely blocking PD-L1 on the cell surface is insufficient to restore suppressed immune function in the prostatic TME. Strikingly, IFN-γ released from T cells activates JAK/STAT axis, leading to the upregulation of the transcription factor IFN regulatory factor 1 (IRF1) and its binding to the promoter region of CD274 gene [89]. IRF1 also recruits CBP/p300 to this region, resulting in the acetylation of histone H3 at this site, which promotes the transcription of CD274 gene and subsequent expression of PD-L1 protein or splicing variants lacking the transmembrane domain. PD-L1 is then secreted extracellularly in exosomes, directly inhibiting T cells function via PD-1, ultimately causing adaptive resistance to PD-L1 blockade treatment (Fig. 5). Strikingly, A-485, a CBP/p300 inhibitor, has been found to abrogate this process by silencing PD-L1 expression from the source, potentially enhancing the efficacy of PD-L1 blockade immunotherapy in PCa [11].

Interferon gamma (IFN-γ) released by T cells activates the JAK/STAT pathway, leading to an increase in the transcription factor IFN regulatory factor 1 (IRF1). IRF1 binds to the promoter of the CD274 gene and recruits CBP/p300, resulting in histone H3 acetylation and enhanced transcription and expression of PD-L1. PD-L1 can be secreted in exosomes, or as splicing variants lacking the transmembrane domain, thereby promoting tumor immunosuppression and resistance to PD-L1 blockade therapy

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) represent a heterogeneous population of immunosuppressive myeloid cells that are highly enriched in the TME. These cells significantly inhibit the functions of T cells and natural killer cells, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion [90]. Recent studies indicate that the CBP/p300 BRD inhibitor CCS1477 can effectively reduce exosomal PD-L1 transcription and markedly decrease the number of MDSCs within the TME by suppressing key cytokines such as IL-6, CSF-1, and CSF-2. This alteration creates a more conducive environment for tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and substantially enhances the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy in PCa and melanoma [91]. Additionally, resveratrol and its biotransformation product piceatannol have been demonstrated to significantly upregulate PD-L1 expression in certain breast cancer and colorectal cancer cells via p300-mediated NF-κB signaling, thereby augmenting the anticancer effects of ICB therapy [92]. Given that CBP/p300 regulates the expression of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) antigen processing and presentation (AgPP), which enables cancer cells to evade immune rejection and diminishes the efficacy of ICB, DNA-damaging anticancer drugs such as oxaliplatin and mitoxantrone upregulate MHC-I AgPP through CBP/p300-dependent transcriptional activation. Consequently, p300 ablation can prevent chemotherapy-induced MHC-I AgPP and restore the responsiveness of MHC-I low-expressing PCa cells to PD-L1 inhibitors [93]. Similarly, the CBP/p300 BRD inhibitor IACS-70654 has been shown to induce interferon responses and MHC-I expression in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells, increasing the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and enhancing the efficacy of docetaxel and ICB therapy. In summary, the silencing of CBP/p300 through appropriate methods appears to be a superior strategy for enhancing the therapeutic effect of ICB on PCa.

The involvement of CBP/p300 in other signaling pathways to promote PCa

The acetylation regulatory effects of CBP/p300 on various substrate proteins, especially NR proteins, inevitable impact other signaling pathways. This impact plays a role in the development and advancement of PCa. The overactivation of oncogenic pathways such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (PKB/AKT) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) plays a critical role in cancer formation, malignant progression, and the development of treatment resistance [94, 95]. Such overactivation further promotes the malignant progression and therapeutic resistance of PCa through intricate interactions with several key signaling pathways, including AR and Wnt. For more detailed information on this topic, please refer to the comprehensive review by Shorning and colleagues [96]. Krüppel-like factor 14 (KLF14), a key transcription factor regulating lipid metabolism, is significantly upregulated in CRPC cells due to the overactivation of PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways under oxidative stress conditions. This upregulation enhances the transcriptional activation of the HMOX1 gene in cooperation with CBP/p300 under androgen-depleted conditions. The HMOX1 gene encodes heme oxygenase-1, which represents one of the most crucial mechanisms for CRPC cells to adapt to oxidative stress [97]. Similarly, the pro-metastatic transmembrane protein CUB domain-containing protein 1 (CDCP1) is highly expressed in advanced PCa and CRPC, potentially associated with the transcriptional activation caused by elevated expression of transcription cofactors BRD4 and CBP/p300. Notably, PI3K/AKT and MAPK are downstream signaling pathways of CDCP1. Dual inhibition of BRD4 and CBP/p300 has been shown to downregulate the expression of CDCP1 as well as PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways, thereby inhibiting the proliferation and metastasis of CRPC cells [98]. Therefore, the combined inhibition of CBP/p300 and PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways may serve as a promising strategy for synergistic treatment of CRPC.

The application of CBP/p300 inhibitors in PCa treatment

As mentioned above, CBP/p300 are essential transcriptional coactivators involved in multiple signaling pathways that promote the occurrence and progression of PCa. Inhibiting their expression and activity can potentially reduce the current over-reliance on ADT and improve the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy [13]. Among the various domains of CBP/p300, the HAT and BRD domains are the most extensively studied as inhibitory targets [99]. Numerous inhibitors and degraders targeting CBP/p300 have been developed, some of which have demonstrated significant efficacy in inhibiting the occurrence and metastasis of various cancers, showing promising potential for applications in PCa treatment (Table 1) [7, 13].

Potential CBP/p300-based inhibitors and PROTAC degraders in the treatment of PCa

Inhibitors targeting CBP/p300 HAT

Compound 12 is a novel inhibitor of CBP/p300 HAT with half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 1.2 μM for CPB and 620 nM for p300 (Fig. 6A). It competes with histone substrates and non-competitive with the cofactor acetyl-CoA. This compound exhibits high selectivity for CBP/p300 compared to other HAT classes, such as Gcn5-related N-acetyltransferase and MYST. It inhibits the proliferation of PCa cells by targeting acetylation. Additionally, compound 12 shows significant antiproliferative effects on both parent MCF-7 cells (half maximal effective concentration (EC50) = 2.8 μM) and tamoxifen-resistant cells (EC50 = 3.4 μM). This suggests that inhibiting p300-mediated histone acetylation can suppress the growth of breast cancer by silencing several estrogen-induced, cancer-related gene signatures, regardless of their tamoxifen resistance status. Interestingly, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) indicates that the effects of compound 12 on p300 transcriptional signatures in breast cancer cells resemble the changes observed with p300 depletion in PCa cells, suggesting a potential clinical application of this p300 HAT inhibitor in PCa therapy [100].

B026 is another potent and selective CBP/p300 HAT inhibitor identified through an artificial-intelligence-assisted drug discovery approach and subsequent optimization, with an IC50 of 1.8 nM to p300 and 9.5 nM to CBP (Fig. 6A). It inhibits p300 enzyme activity approximately 30 times more effectively than the classical CBP/p300 inhibitor A-485. B026 demonstrates over 2000-fold selectivity within the HAT family and shows no inhibitory effect on the activities of other HAT family members. Moreover, B026 significantly inhibits tumor growth in mice bearing human leukemia cells in a dose-dependent manner, achieving IC50 values below 10 nM in two AR-positive (AR +) PCa cell lines, and is above 20-fold more potent than A-485, indicating its highly promising clinical application in PCa treatment [101].

Inhibitors targeting CBP/p300 BRD

GNE-049 is a novel small-molecule inhibitor of the CBP/p300 that targets its BRD. This compound exhibits high selectivity for CBP (IC50 = 1.1 nM) and p300 (IC50 = 2.3 nM), with a selectivity ratio of 3,850-fold over BRD4 (Fig. 6B). Although it has no effect on AR levels, GNE-049 inhibits the expression of AR target genes in a dose-dependent manner. Results from the in vitro study indicate that GNE-049 treatment can impair the growth of PC cell lines expressing full-length AR (AR-FL) or AR splice variants lacking LBD. However, it has no effect on the viability of AR-negative CRPC cell lines. Moreover, AR mutant (F877L) expressing PCa cells remain highly sensitive to GNE-049. Consistently, GNE-049 treatment significantly inhibits the growth of AR + PCa Patient Derived Xenograft (PDX) models and some well-documented ADT resistant models. Additionally, it is well tolerated and exhibits synergistic anti-tumor activity when combines with enzalutamide [102].

CCS-1477 (Inobrodib) is another selective and orally bioavailable inhibitor of the CBP/p300 BRD (Fig. 6B). It binds to p300 and CBP with dissociation constants of 1.3 and 1.7 nM, respectively, with a selectivity ratio of 170/130-fold over BRD4, given an IC50 of 19 nM for p300. This compound shows significantly higher efficacy in the growth of PCa cell line with AR-FL and CRPC cell lines AR-V7 compared to AR-null CRPC cell lines. Mechanistically, CCS-1477 inhibits AR and c-Myc signaling by reducing the recruitment of CBP/p300 and AR to known AR binding sites. Moreover, it inhibits the growth of a treatment-resistant PDX model that has AR-FL amplification, AR-V7 expression, and amplified AKT2 and c-Myc. The subsequent human phase Ⅰ trial in mCRPC, metastatic breast cancer, and advanced small cell lung cancer patients (NCT03568656) confirmed the inhibitory effect on AR activity, plasma KLK3 levels, and decreased c-Myc protein levels, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for advanced PCa [13].

CBP/p300 inhibitor 32 h is a novel 1-(1H-indol-1-yl) ethanone derivative that demonstrates high selectivity for CBP/p300 BRD, with an IC50 value of 37 nM (Fig. 6B). This compound significantly inhibits the growth of several AR + prostate cancer cell lines and markedly suppresses the mRNA expression of AR-FL, as well as AR-target genes including KLK2, KLK3, and TMPRSS2. Additionally, it also reduces the expression of c-Myc [103]. Similarly, Y08197 is another novel 1-(indolizin-3-yl) ethanone derivative and a selective inhibitor of the CBP/p300 BRD, and has an IC50 value of 100.67 ± 3.30 nM for CBP (Fig. 6B). It effectively inhibits the growth and induce apoptosis of several AR + PCa cell lines in a dose-dependent manner by downregulating the expression of classical AR target genes and oncogenes such as c-Myc and ERG [7]. Additionally, it markedly suppresses the invasive capacity of AR + PCa cells induced by ERG [42]. Therefore, inhibiting CBP/p300 activity with Y08197 presents a promising therapeutic strategy for PCa treatment.

Structure confidential but entering clinical trials CBP/p300 BRD inhibitors

HP537 is a novel, potent, and selective CBP/p300 BRD inhibitor currently exhibiting high activity against hematologic malignancies [104]. It significantly reduces protein expression levels of acetylated H3K27, c-Myc, and IRF4 in a dose- and time-dependent manner, while also inhibiting the growth rate of xenografts bearing in hematologic cancer cell lines. HP537 has been approved for Phase I clinical trials in both China and the United States for the treatment of hematologic malignancies and may have potential therapeutic value in PCa treatment.

FT-7051 is an another oral available and potent CBP/p300 BRD inhibitor [105]. In vitro studies on PCa cell lines have demonstrated that FT-7051 inhibits the expression of AR and AR-dependent genes, showing anti-proliferative activity in AR + PCa cell lines, including those with the hormone-resistant AR-V7 variant. Preliminary results from a Phase I, open-label clinical trial (NCT04575766) of FT-7051 in patients with metastatic CRPC indicate that some patients experienced more than a 50% reduction in PSA levels following treatment. However, the drug can cause mild side effects such as diarrhea and hyperglycemia, and its safety requires further evaluation.

Inhibitors targeting CBP/p300 BHC

A-485 is a selective and potent inhibitor of CBP/p300, targeting the BRD-HAT-C/H3 (BHC) domain of CBP (IC50 = 2.6 nM) and p300-BHC (IC50 = 9.8 nM) with minimal inhibitory effects on other HAT family members (Fig. 6C). High-content microscopy-based assay confirms that A-485 treatment results in a dose-dependent decrease in H3K27ac and H3K18ac, but not H3K9Ac. Compared with multiples solid tumors, most hematological malignancies show significantly greater sensitivity to A-485 treatment. It also demonstrates potent inhibition of the proliferation of AR + PCa cell lines compared to AR-null counterparts. However, A-485 inhibits AR transcriptional activity in both androgen-sensitive and CRPC more potently than enzalutamide, inducing 54% tumor growth inhibition in a castration-resistant xenograft model [106]. Recently, a series of new spirocyclic chroman derivatives of A-485, such as B16 and B16-P2, were designed based on molecular dynamics simulation. These compounds display favorable overall pharmacokinetic profiles and potent inhibition of the growth of enzalutamide-resistant AR + PCa cells (IC50 = 96 nM) both in vitro and in vivo [107].

Dual BRD and extra-terminal domain (BET) and CBP/p300 BRD inhibitor

EP31670 (NEO2734) is the first dual inhibitor of BET and CBP/p300 BRD, demonstrating potent activity in hematological malignancies and PCa (Fig. 6D). In primary PCa patients, the SPOP gene is the frequently mutated (15%), and cancer cells harboring this mutation are resistant to BET inhibitors. Notably, NEO2734 reduces the growth of BET inhibitor-resistant SPOP hotspot mutant PCa PDX model and organoids, as well as AR + and AR-null organoids, by inhibiting AKT and AR signaling and neuroendocrine markers such as ASCL1, INSM1 and SYP [12, 108]. NEO1132 is another dual inhibitor of BET and CBP/p300 BRD; however, it has a reduced affinity for both CBP/p300 and BET, resulting in lower efficacy in reducing the viability of a panel of acute myeloid leukemia cell lines and the growth of engraftment [109]. Currently, there is no research on the antitumor activity of NEO1132 in PCa cells.

Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTAC) degraders of CBP/p300

PROTACs are heterobifunctional molecules comprised of two ligands. One ligand is for binding to the target protein, and the other is for binding to the E3 ubiquitin ligase, linked by an appropriate linker. They are capable of effectively, specifically, and reversibly reducing target protein levels. The mechanism of PROTAC method relies on its rational design, which recruits the target protein to the E3 ubiquitin ligase, inducing its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation in a physiologically relevant manner [110]. The therapeutic benefits of PROTAC include low binding affinity requirements, complete degradation of target protein without the necessity of an active site, longer duration of action, and a broader therapeutic window [111]. Since traditional inhibitors targeting the HAT or BRD domains fail to completely block the activation of CBP/p300 in cancer cells, PROTACs has therefore emerged as an effective strategy for degrading the abnormal overexpression of CBP/p300 in PCa [112].

CBPD-268 is a PROTAC degrader of CBP/p300 based on the BRD inhibitor GNE-049 and the CRBN ligand (Fig. 6E). In vitro studies demonstrate that CBPD-268 effectively inhibits the growth of several AR + PCa cell lines by degrading CBP/p300 proteins, with a half-maximal degradation concentration (DC50) ≤ 0.03 nM and a Dmax > 95%. Subsequent in vivo studies show that oral administration of 0.3–3 mg/kg CBPD-268 leads to sustained depletion of CBP/p300 in tumor tissues and significant inhibition, even regression, of xenograft tumor growth. CBPD-268 exhibits a favorable half-life in rodents, moderate volume of distribution at steady state, low clearance rate, and excellent oral plasma exposure and bioavailability at low doses, with a therapeutic index of > 10. Additionally, it demonstrates reasonable metabolic stability in liver microsomes of mice, humans, monkeys, dogs, and rats, as well as good stability in plasma and favorable safety evaluations, making it a promising candidate for the clinical treatment of PCa [113].

CBPD-409 is an efficient, selective, and orally bioavailable CBP/p300 degrader, designed using thalidomide as the ligand of E3 ubiquitin ligase (Fig. 6E). In three AR + PCa cell lines, it effectively induces CBP/p300 degradation with a DC50 of 0.2–0.4 nM and inhibits cellular proliferation with an IC50 of 1.2–2.0 nM. It significantly reduces mRNA levels of AR, KLK3, and c-Myc genes compared to GNE-049, CCS1477, ARV-110, and enzalutamide. CBPD-409 demonstrates favorable pharmacokinetic properties, achieving 50% oral bioavailability in mice. A single oral dose of CBPD-409 (1 mg/kg) nearly depletes CBP/p300 protein in PCa tissues. Additionally, it exhibits stronger antitumor activity than enzalutamide and other CBP/p300 inhibitors such as CCS1477 and GNE-049. It is well-tolerated and shows no toxic side effects in mice at high doses, making it a promising candidate for the treatment of advanced PCa and other types of cancer [114].

MJP6412 is another novel and highly effective CBP/p300 degrader, utilizing CBP/p300-IN-4, an CBP/p300 BRD inhibitor for CBP/p300 binding and thalidomide as the ligand of E3 ubiquitin ligase (Fig. 6E). In three AR + PCa cell lines, it demonstrates high degradation efficiency for CBP/p300, with DC50 value below 4 nM for both CBP and p300, and Dmax exceeding 80% for CBP and p300. It also exhibits potent antiproliferative activity against hormone-sensitive AR + PCa cell lines (IC50 < 40 nM) and enzalutamide-resistant AR + cell lines (IC50 < 150 nM). However, it shows minimal impact on the proliferation of AR-null cell lines, indicating that the antiproliferative effect of MJP6412 on PCa cells is primarily mediated through the inhibition of CBP/p300 and subsequent blockade of AR signaling. The protein degradation effect of MJP6412 is rapid and sustained, with CBP/p300 degradation observed within 2 h, reaching a Dmax of 80% at 12 h, and persisting for 48 h, with protein levels beginning to recover at 72 h. Treatment of the AR + PCa cell line 22Rv1 with 100 nM MJP6412 for 6 h significantly reduces CBP/p300 protein levels and downregulates the expression of SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9), T-box transcription factor 3 (TBX3), and KLF transcription factor 5 (KLF5), which are positively regulated by p300-mediated H3K27Ac. Additionally, there is a dose-dependent decrease in AR-FL, AR-V7, and c-Myc, with the strongest inhibitory effect on c-Myc, far surpassing that of CCS1477 [115].

Recently, a novel p300-PROTAC utilizing the nanoselenium system as a drug carrier was developed to address the poor stability and limited cell membrane penetration of peptide drugs. Unlike other CBP/p300-targeting PROTACs, it specifically targets the cysteine-histidine rich 1 (CH1) domain of p300, rather than the HAT and BRD domains, thereby achieving specific degradation of p300 in cancer cells. Additionally, MDM2 was selected as the E3 ligase, which not only degrades p300 but also disrupts the interaction between MDM2 and p53, promoting the release of p53 and anticancer function of p53. Remarkably, it exhibits potent anti-proliferative, anti-invasive, anti-migrative, and pro-apoptotic effects across all PCa cell lines, including AR + , AR-null CRPC, and neuroendocrine PCa cell lines. In two xenograft mouse models bearing advanced PCa cell lines, this p300-PROTAC drug demonstrates a stronger inhibitory effect on tumor growth compared to p300 inhibitors. Furthermore, it primarily accumulates within the tumor sites and shows no toxicity to major organs even at higher concentrations, indicating its great safety and promising potential for clinical application [116].

Prospects of CBP/p300 inhibitors in combination with existing PCa therapies

Enzalutamide is currently a classical second-generation pharmacological castration drug for PCa treatment. However, castration resistance inevitably arises in the later stages of treatment, primarily due to a significant increase in AR splice variant expression. GSEA on a cohort of mCRPC patients reveals that the canonical Wnt signaling is the most modulated pathway [117]. Fortunately, CBP/p300 inhibitors, which can selectively inhibit both the canonical Wnt and the AR signaling pathways, hold potential for improving the prognosis of PCa patients who have progressed despite prior ADT therapy. Docetaxel, a microtubule inhibitor, is a commonly used chemotherapeutic agent for mCRPC and has been shown to extend overall survival. Nevertheless, its benefits are short-lived due to the rapid development of chemoresistance. Studies indicate that CBP/p300 expression is elevated in cancer tissues from patients treated with docetaxel compared to control groups. Furthermore, docetaxel-resistant PCa cells exhibit higher p300 protein expression levels than their docetaxel-sensitive counterparts. Therefore, p300 knockdown significantly reduces the clonal formation rate as well as the migration and invasion capabilities of docetaxel-resistant cells [118]. The phenomenon of multidrug resistance in cancer chemotherapy is largely attributed to the overexpression of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) membrane proteins, such as subfamily B member 1 (ABCB1) and ABC subfamily G member 2 (ABCG2), in resistant cells. These proteins enhance the efflux of chemotherapeutic agents from cancer cells, thereby altering the pharmacokinetics of anticancer drugs [119]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the CBP/p300 BRD inhibitor I-CBP112 can downregulate the transcription of ABC genes in drug-resistant cell lines of TNBC and non-small cell lung cancer, enhance intracellular accumulation of paclitaxel, and augment its cytotoxicity. Notably, I-CBP112 exhibits no toxicity toward primary cell lines, does not exacerbate the toxicity of anticancer drugs to immune cells in the blood, and polarizes human macrophages into a pro-inflammatory phenotype following treatment [120]. Furthermore, I-CBP112 and another CBP/p300 inhibitor, SGC-CBP30, have been shown to effectively reverse ABCB1-mediated resistance, specifically resensitizing taxane-resistant CRPC cells [121]. These findings suggest that CBP/p300 inhibitors can alleviate chemoresistance, providing additional treatment options for patients with advanced PCa. AA, a cytochrome P450 17α-hydroxylase/17,20-lyase (CYP17) inhibitor, is an FDA-approved drug for treating advanced PCa, specifically docetaxel-refractory locally advanced or mCRPC. Recent research indicates that AA treatment can activate PKA or other kinases, resulting in the phosphorylation of CREB1. This may enhance CBP/p300 activity in AA-resistant in PCa cells and increase their sensitivity to CBP/p300 inhibitors. Notably, AA and CBP/p300 inhibitors exhibit significant synergistic effects in inhibiting the growth of PCa cells, thereby significantly improving therapeutic efficacy in patients with this resistance [122]. Moreover, CBP/p300 inhibitors can potentiate the efficacy of immunotherapy for PCa by inhibiting PD-L1. As mentioned above, the CBP/p300 inhibitor A-485 can suppress CD274 gene transcription, thereby reducing exosomal PD-L1 secretion and reactivating T cell antitumor activity. Although a combination of anti-PD-L1 antibody and A-485 does not exhibit a combined effect in vitro due to the absence of an immune system, their combined use reduces intrinsic and endogenous PD-L1 levels in PCa cells xenograft mice stably expressing Ras and increases the number of infiltrating CD8+ T cells. Importantly, none of the mice in any treatment group experienced significant weight loss, indicating the safety of this treatment. Therefore, the combined use of CBP/p300 inhibitors represents a novel strategy for enhancing the efficacy of ICB in treating PCa patients [11].

Despite the remarkably high activity of CBP/p300 inhibitors observed in both in vitro and in vivo anticancer assays, significant challenges remain in their clinical application. The HAT and BRD domains are widely conserved across the histone acetyltransferase family members, not exclusively within CBP/p300. Consequently, achiving specificity for CBP/p300 among numerous family members while minimizing off-target effects remains a critical challenge for CBP/p300 inhibitors [123]. In this regard, PROTAC degraders of CBP/p300 may address these limitaions and represent a more promising alternative. Furthemore, CBP/p300 inhibitors may excessively suppress normal AR signaling and canonical Wnt signaling pathways, thereby impacting physiological functions such as skeletal metabolism, male reproduction, and gastrointestinal homeostasis. Therefore, it is essential to enhance their stability, solubility, bioavailability, and tissue-targeting specificity through appropriate drug delivery and release systems to minimize dosage and potential cytotoxicity. Potential incompatibilities between CBP/p300 inhibitors and conventional therapies have also been noted. For instance, studies have reported that A-485 reduces the transcriptional activity of STAT1 and interferes with the expression of several MHC molecules. If combined with ICB therapy, this interference could potentially compromise the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy [124]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying CBP/p300 involvement in prostatic carcinogenesis and advances in drug formulation for enhanced and more specific delivery, based on whole-genome and spatial genomics sequencing technologies, to accurately determine CBP/EP300 mutation types, alterations in their protein expression and fuction, and the cellular composition of the prostatic TEM at specific disease stage, represent top priorities [125]. Nevertheless, with the collaborative effort of scientists and clinicians, there is no doubt that CBP/p300-targeted drugs will show promising potnetial for precise PCa treatment.

Conclusion and perspective

CBP and p300 are highly similar in structure, both of which comprise several conserved domains such as the HAT domain and the BRD domain. The HAT domain is responsible for acetylating histone lysine residues, functioning as a “writer”, while the BRD domain acts as a “reader”. CBP/p300 plays a crucial role in numerous cellular activities. As an acetyltransferase, it is involved in the acetylation of transcription factors, thereby facilitating the recruitment of RNAPol II and the transcriptional activation of target genes. Additionally, it serves as a scaffold for the formation of various transcriptional complexes, influencing multiple crucial signaling pathways associated with PCa progression, such as the AR and canonical Wnt signaling pathways. In prostatic carcinogenesis, CBP/p300 is frequently elevated, acting as an essential coactivator that promotes the proliferation, metastasis, and drug resistance of cancer cells. Notably, it contributes to the resistance against all current anti-PCa therapeutic strategies, including ADT, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy. Therefore, CBP/p300 represents a promising target for all types of PCa, including primary PCa, CRPC, AR-null, and neuroendocrine PCa. Inhibiting its activity has shown highly encouraging results in treating these cancers. Most CBP/p300 inhibitors exhibit high selectivity, efficiency, and affinity, with dual-target inhibitors and PROTAC degraders demonstrating particularly promising therapeutic potential. Remarkably, some inhibitors have advanced to clinical trials, and combining CBP/p300 inhibitors or degraders with current therapies is expected to become a pivotal strategy for treating all PCa patients, significantly enhancing their prognosis.
