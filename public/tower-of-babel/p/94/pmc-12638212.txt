Regulation of Photosynthetic Electron Transport Is Affected Differently in the Arabidopsis thaliana Manganese Transporter Mutants pam71 and cmt1
Authors: Linnéa Strandberg, Anja Krieger‐Liszkay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTManganese (Mn) is an essential element in photosynthesis as a catalyst for water splitting in photosystem II (PSII). Previous studies have shown that Mn‐deficient plants are more susceptible to photoinhibition and have reduced PSII and PSI contents and activities. Here, we examined the impact of Mn deficiency on the regulation of photosynthetic electron transport, using the two previously characterized A. thaliana Mn transporter mutants pam71 and cmt1. PAM71 is localized in the thylakoid membrane, while CMT1 is found in the chloroplast inner envelope membrane. We quantified the metal content in thylakoids and leaves to clarify the roles of these proteins in Mn translocation. While the Mn content is decreased in thylakoids, the content of other cations (Ca, Cu, Mg, and Fe) is increased. Chlorophyll fluorescence and induction curves demonstrate that PSII is impaired in both mutants, while state transitions and NPQ are only reduced in pam71, indicating differences in regulation between the mutants. Moreover, quantification of the P700‐to‐plastocyanin ratio revealed that the relative amount of PSI compared to plastocyanin is reduced in pam71, but remains unaltered in cmt1. We hypothesize that the altered stoichiometry between the photosynthetic reaction centers and electron carriers restricts electron transport in pam71. Meanwhile, cmt1 might adjust to Mn deficiency, retaining pH and redox control through stoichiometric balance.Keywords: Arabidopsis thaliana, manganese, manganese transporter, photosynthetic electron transport, regulation

Introduction

Photosynthesis consists of a highly fine‐tuned network of electron transfer pathways fueled by light. Between the first photon harvest and the reduction of CO2 into energy‐rich sugars is a range of well‐balanced redox reactions. The first part of these redox reactions takes place in the photosynthetic electron transport chain, composed of the photosystem II (PSII), the plastoquinone (PQ) pool, the cytochrome b 6 f complex (Cyt b 6 f), plastocyanin (PC) and photosystem I (PSI) that reduces ferredoxin (Fd). Photosystem II catalyzes the water oxidation at the Mn4O5Ca cluster, also called the oxygen‐evolving complex (OEC), which provides the necessary electrons for oxygenic photosynthesis (Shen 2015; Lubitz et al. 2019). Mn deficiency strongly impacts PSII and thereby photosynthesis. Upon Mn deficiency, leaves become pale and oxygen evolution and the maximum quantum efficiency of PSII (F v/F m) are reduced (Anderson and Thorne 1968; Gong et al. 2010; Husted et al. 2009; Kriedemann et al. 1985; Lanquar et al. 2010; Schmidt et al. 2015). Moreover, this condition has also been linked to a lower ratio of Chl a/b, loss of PSI activity, higher susceptibility to photoinhibition and degradation of PSII and PSI proteins (Husted et al. 2009; Schmidt et al. 2016; Salomon and Keren 2011; Long et al. 2020).

Studies on Mn deficiency are often conducted by cultivating plants in Mn‐deficient conditions (Hebbern et al. 2009; Kriedemann et al. 1985; Lanquar et al. 2010). Since this experimental setup presumably affects all cellular processes requiring Mn, it is difficult to selectively study the impact of low Mn on photosynthesis. Instead, Mn transporter mutants can be used as a tool to specifically address the impact of Mn deficiency on photosynthetic electron transport. To date, two membrane proteins have been identified in Arabidopsis thaliana , which translocate Mn, Ca or both from the cytosol into the chloroplast and the thylakoid lumen, where the OEC is assembled. The first protein, named CHLOROPLAST MANGANESE TRANSPORTER1 (CMT1) or BIVALENT CATION TRANSPORTER2 (BICAT2), has been located to the inner chloroplast membrane (Eisenhut et al. 2018; Frank et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2018). The second protein, named PHOTOSYNTHESIS AFFECTED MUTANT (PAM71), CHLOROPLAST Ca2+/H+ ANTIPORTER1 (CCHA1) or BIVALENT CATION TRANSPORTER1 (BICAT1), is found in the thylakoid membrane (Frank et al. 2019; Schneider et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2016). Both proteins belong to the group Uncharacterized Protein Family 0016 (UPF0016), also called the Gdt1 family, of which members have been reported to transport Mn, and in some cases also Ca (Thines et al. 2020).

Knockout of PAM71 or CMT1 leads to suppressed growth, lower F v/F m, and reduced PSII and PSI activity. However, certain traits differ between the mutants. Studies on pam71 reported reduced activities and concentrations of PSII and PSI subunits, while their transcription rates were not affected (Frank et al. 2019; Schneider et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2016). pam71, unlike cmt1, also had a low capacity for dissipating excess energy [low non‐photochemical quenching (NPQ)] (Eisenhut et al. 2018; Frank et al. 2019; Schneider et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2018). In contrast, cmt1 showed downregulation of additional photosynthetic proteins, such as PC, subunits of Cyt b 6 f and ATP synthase, as well as changes in transcription levels of genes encoding subunits of the photosynthetic complexes (Eisenhut et al. 2018; Frank et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2018). Furthermore, insertion of PAM71 into the chloroplast membrane of cmt1 did not result in a wild‐type phenotype, while CMT1 expression on the pam71 background led to wild‐type levels (Hoecker et al. 2021). The differences between the mutants may indicate variations in metal composition and stress, affecting their respective capacity to regulate photosynthesis.

The aim of this study is (1) to clarify if the two Mn transporter mutants selectively lead to Mn deficiency in the thylakoid, and (2) to get a deeper insight into the relationship between Mn content and photosynthetic regulation, using pam71 and cmt1 as experimental tools.

Methods

Plant Material and Growth Conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana mutant seeds pam71 and cmt1‐1, with T‐DNA insertions in the genes At1g64150 (GK‐166A05) and At4g13590 (SALK_129037 N629037), respectively, were kindly given by Marion Eisenhut (University of Bielefeld, Germany). Plants were grown on soil at a light intensity of 100 μmol photons m−2 s−1 and a cycle of 16 h light, 21°C/8 h dark, 18°C; (light source: fluorescent light tubes; OSRAM fluora 58 W/77). Plant age is given from the day of sowing. All samples have been measured on a minimum of 2 plant batches grown at different times of the year, except for 77 K fluorescence, OJIP, photoinhibition and immunoblotting, where all biological replicates originate from plants grown at the same time point.

Chlorophyll Quantification

Chlorophyll concentration was determined in 80% acetone as described by Porra and Scheer (2019). For rosette samples, chlorophyll was extracted in 100% acetone and plants were dried for 2 days at 60°C to obtain the dry weight.

Protein Quantification

The protein concentration of thylakoids was measured with an amido black assay (Schulz et al. 1994).

Thylakoid Extraction

Thylakoids were isolated from rosettes. Leaves were homogenized with a mixer for 10 s in buffer (0.33 M sorbitol, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM MgCl2, 50 mM KCl, 25 mM MES pH 6.1). The slurry was filtered through 2 layers of cheesecloth and 1 layer of nylon cloth. Thylakoids were spun down at 5000 xg and washed twice (0.33 M sorbitol, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM MgCl2, 50 mM KCl, 25 mM HEPES pH 6.7). The final pellet was resuspended in buffer (0.33 M sorbitol, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM MgCl2, 50 mM KCl, 25 mM HEPES pH 7.6) and stored at −80°C.

Metal Determination

The concentrations of Mn, Ca, Fe, Cu, and Mg were measured in thylakoids (n = 3 batches of isolated thylakoids) and whole rosettes (n = 5) with microwave plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (MP‐AES). Whole rosettes were dried for 2 days at 60°C to determine dry weights. Nitric acid was added to a final concentration of 50% and 100% for thylakoid and rosette samples, respectively. The samples were heated to 96°C for 2–4 h and diluted to 10% nitric acid prior to measurement. Metals were quantified with an MP‐AES instrument.

Chlorophyll a Fluorescence and P700 Absorbance

Light curves were recorded with a DUAL‐PAM‐100 (Walz). Leaves were dark‐adapted for 2 min and exposed for 180 s at each light intensity (n = 6).

Induction curves, decay curves and state transitions were obtained with a MULTI‐COLOR‐PAM (Walz). For induction curves (OJIP), leaves were dark‐adapted for 30 min before giving a 300 ms multiple turnover flash using the Poly300ms fast kinetics program. For fluorescence decay curves (n = 4), leaves were untreated or infiltrated with 50 μM DCMU. After 10 min of dark incubation, a 50 μs single turnover flash was given and the relaxation in the dark was recorded. In untreated samples, five decay curves were averaged for each leaf, leaving out the first recording. For state transitions, leaves (n = 6) were dark‐adapted for 30 min and F V/F m was determined with a 300 ms saturating pulse (SP). Blue light of 440 nm, intensity 119 μmol photons m−2 s−1, was switched on. After 20 min, a second SP was applied and far‐red light (intensity setting 11) was added to the blue light. After 20 min, a third SP was applied and the far‐red light was switched off. After an additional 20 min of blue light, a final SP was applied and the blue light was switched off.

Photoinhibition experiments were executed with an IMAGING‐PAM (Walz). Leaves were either untreated (n = 12 for pam71 and wild type, n = 6 for cmt1) or infiltrated with 1 g/L lincomycin (n = 4). High‐light treatment was executed at a light intensity of 500 and 800 μmol photons m−2 s−1 in the presence and absence of lincomycin, respectively. A recovery phase for non‐infiltrated leaves was done at low light (8 μmol photons m−2 s−1) or in darkness (n = 6 per recovery condition). F m was measured with 800 ms saturating pulses after 15‐ and 10‐min dark adaptation for the high‐light treatment and the recovery phase, respectively.

Light‐induced absorption changes in the near‐infrared of P700 and PC were recorded with a DUAL‐KLAS‐NIR instrument (Schreiber and Klughammer 2016) (Walz). Leaves were dark‐adapted for 2 min for quantification of maximum P700+ and PC+ levels using the automated NIRmax program (n = 15). Re‐reduction of P700+ and PC+ was recorded in leaves (n = 11 for pam71 and wild type, n = 8 for cmt1) after 10 s illumination with far‐red light (intensity setting 10). Curves were averaged for each measurement, leaving out the first recording.

77K Fluorescence

PSII and PSI emission spectra at 77 K were recorded in leaves (n = 4) with a Carry Eclipse fluorimeter (Agilent), excitation wavelength 435 nm. Leaves were pre‐illuminated with blue light (440 nm, intensity 119 μmol photons m−2 s−1) for 15 min and immediately ground in liquid nitrogen. The powder was resuspended in a buffer (0.33 M sorbitol, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM MgCl2, 50 mM KCl, 25 mM HEPES pH 7.6) and refrozen in liquid nitrogen. Eight emission spectra were averaged for each replicate. The spectra were normalized to the intensity of the PSI emission.

Immunoblotting

Rosettes were ground in liquid nitrogen, resuspended in a buffer (5 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 10% w/w glycerol, 0.1 M Tris pH 7.5) and filtered through a nylon cloth. The extracted proteins were mixed with Laemmli buffer and denatured at 96°C for 5 min. Samples were loaded to pre‐casted 12% polyacrylamide gels with a total content of 1, 0.5 or 0.25 μg chlorophyll. Proteins were blotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane and stained with Ponceau. Membranes were labelled with polyclonal antibodies [PsaF (AS06 104), PC (AS06 141), PetB (AS18 4169); Agrisera]. After washing, bound antibodies were revealed with a peroxidase‐conjugated secondary anti‐rabbit antibody (AS09 602, Agrisera) and visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence. Quantification was performed using ImageJ (Schneider et al. 2012).

Data Analysis

Curves were fitted with Origin Software (v8.1.10.86, OriginLab Corporation 2009). Data were processed with R Statistical Software (v4.2.1, R Core Team 2022), with Welch's t‐tests performed with the rstatix package (v0.7.2, Kassambara 2023).

Results

Thylakoids in pam71 and cmt1 Have a Metal Imbalance

PAM71 and CMT1 have been reported to transport Mn, Ca, or both (Eisenhut et al. 2018; Frank et al. 2019; Schneider et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2018). To identify the specificity of the transporters, we measured the total content of Mn, Ca, Fe, Cu, and Mg in isolated thylakoids with MP‐AES. The Mn concentration was lower in pam71 thylakoids in comparison to the wild type (Table 1A). In contrast, the concentrations of Ca, Fe, Cu and Mg were increased. The same trend was seen in cmt1, which showed an even higher increase in Ca, Fe, Cu and Mg. It is important to note that the values are normalized to the total thylakoid protein content. It cannot be ruled out that this varies between the genotypes, making absolute quantification difficult. However, in relation to other metals, Mn is clearly reduced in both mutants. We also measured the metal content in whole rosettes to see if it was unaffected in the whole tissue. No Mn deficiency was seen in the leaves of pam71 or cmt1 (Table 1B). We thereby show that pam71 and cmt1 suffer from Mn deficiency in the thylakoids, while other cations are elevated.

The OEC of PSII Is Impaired in pam71 and cmt1

Since Mn is required for water oxidation and the relative metal content was lower in thylakoids of pam71 and cmt1, we investigated the performance of PSII by monitoring chlorophyll fluorescence parameters. Images of plants, F m, F 0, and F V/F m are shown in Figure S1. The light dependency of the quantum yield Y(II), the photochemical quenching qP, and the non‐photochemical quenching NPQ were determined in leaves (Figure 1). Y(II) was reduced in both pam71 and cmt1 in comparison to the wild type, indicating lower PSII efficiency. This was also demonstrated by a decrease in qP. For NPQ, pam71 showed a significant reduction when compared to cmt1 and the wild type. Taken together, while pam71 and cmt1 both have impaired PSII activity, the former is also impaired in dissipating excess energy through NPQ. To get a deeper insight into PSII performance, chlorophyll fluorescence induction curves (OJIP) were measured (Figures 2 and S2). Both pam71 and cmt1 showed a K‐phase (peak below 1 ms), indicative of a non‐functional water‐splitting complex at PSII (Strasser 1997; Tóth et al. 2007). Moreover, pam71 also demonstrated a D‐dip (drop below 100 ms) during the J‐P transition, which has been shown to correlate with the release of Mn from PSII (Pospíšil and Dau 2000; Schmidt et al. 2016).

To further characterize the reduced PSII efficiency, the electron transfer at the PSII acceptor side was investigated by monitoring fluorescence decay after a saturating single turnover flash. (Figure 3, Table 2). The curves were recorded in leaves with and without DCMU. Untreated leaves were fitted with two exponential decay functions to determine a fast and a slow phase. According to de Wijn and van Gorkom (2001), these phases correspond to the electron transfer QA − to a PQ bound to the QB site (fast phase, t 1), and electron transfer after PQ binding to an empty QB site (slow phase, t 2). As shown in Table 2, the time constants of both phases were slowed down by a factor of about 1.5 in pam71 and cmt1, indicating an alteration in the electron transfer between QA and QB. In the presence of DCMU, which binds to the QB‐pocket and blocks electron transfer from QA to QB, the fluorescence decay is instead attributed to the charge recombination reaction S2QA −, with S2 being an oxidation state of the Mn cluster of the OEC. The time constant of this decay was about 1.8‐fold slower in the mutants compared to the wild type, indicating a stabilization of the charge pair. The slower forward electron transport in the mutants presumably reflects an upshift in the midpoint potential of the redox couple QA/QA −, which is linked to inactivation of the OEC (Johnson et al. 1995; Krieger and Weis 1992). In summary, the slower re‐oxidation rates of QA − and QB − indicate that both mutants are impaired at the donor side and consequently at the acceptor side of PSII.

The Electron Flow Around PSI Is Altered in pam71 and cmt1

To investigate the performance of PSI, we recorded light curves in leaves (Figure 1). A strong decrease in Y(I) and a high donor‐side limitation Y(ND) were observed in pam71 and cmt1. As high donor‐side limitation may be caused by a relatively low amount of PC and downregulation of this protein has previously been reported in cmt1 (Eisenhut et al. 2018), we quantified PC and PSI. To investigate the ratio between P700 and PC, we monitored light‐induced absorption changes of both components in the near‐infrared with the KLAS‐NIR instrument (Figure 4A). Upon illumination with actinic light (570 μmol quanta m−2 s−1), P700 and PC were more oxidized in the mutants than in wild type, particularly in cmt1. Maximum levels of oxidized P700 and PC were obtained by a saturating flash at the end of a far‐red illumination period and used to quantify the ratio of P700 to PC (Figure 4B). A lower P700 to PC ratio was seen in pam71 in comparison to cmt1 and the wild type. To validate these results, we additionally quantified PSI and PC by immunoblotting (Figure 4C). This confirmed that the P700 content is lower in pam71.

To gain more insight into the importance of the altered P700/PC ratio in pam71 on electron flow, we followed the rate of re‐reduction of P700+ and PC+ after excitation with far‐red light (Figure 5), a condition known to favor cyclic electron flow (Heber et al. 1978). Both P700+ and PC+ were re‐reduced faster in pam71 and cmt1, possibly reflecting that, under this condition, a higher amount of electron donors (Fd, PC) is available in the mutants than in wild type (Table 3). This idea is also supported by the increase in PSI acceptor side limitation, Y(NA), at higher actinic light intensities (Figure 1), indicating a higher reduction state of downstream electron acceptors, such as FAFB, Fd and NADPH.

pam71 and cmt1 Are More Susceptible to High Light

Based on our findings that pam71 and cmt1 have an impaired OEC, we hypothesized that the mutants would be more susceptible to photoinhibition. To test this, we exposed leaves to high light and measured changes in F v/F m over time (Figure 6). As expected, the F v/F m dropped more drastically in the mutants, particularly in cmt1. After the high‐light treatment, we studied the repair process by measuring the recovery of F v/F m in leaves from pam71 and wild type incubated in low light or in darkness. The cmt1 mutant was too damaged to recover and was excluded from the recovery experiment. The light recovery was motivated by the requirement of photons to reassemble the OEC under the repair of PSII. The dark recovery was used as a control to exclude that the recovery in F v/F m was caused by the removal of a fluorescence quencher. Upon low light recovery, the wild type almost returned to its initial F v/F m value, while no differences were seen between dark and light recovery in pam71. This may be explained by the presence of a long‐term quencher, slow repair or by irreparable damage. Finally, we investigated differences in PSII repair rates upon photoinhibition between pam71 and wild type. To do so, we blocked the synthesis of the D1 protein by infiltrating leaves with lincomycin prior to the photoinhibition treatment. Under these conditions, pam71 was severely damaged, while the wild type was less affected. In conclusion, pam71 and cmt1 are highly susceptible to photoinhibition.

State Transitions Are Affected in pam71

Since the electron transport was affected differently in pam71 than in cmt1, we were interested in the distribution of antenna at PSII and PSI. State transitions balance the energy between the two photosystems, with a mobile part of the antenna system LHCII either docking to PSII (state 1) or PSI (state 2). State 1 can be triggered by far‐red light, which preferentially excites PSI, in turn triggering the LHCII to move to PSII. State 2, on the contrary, is favored in blue light, where LHCII moves to PSI to equilibrate the excess energy on PSII (Bellafiore et al. 2005; Bonaventura and Myers 1969). To test this, we alternated between state 1 and state 2 by illuminating leaves of pam71, cmt1 and wild type with blue light and temporarily adding far‐red light, as described by Husted et al. (2009). Saturating pulses were used to monitor the antenna size of PSII (Figure 7A). The percentage of state transitions was estimated from the difference in maximum fluorescence in the two light qualities (Figure 7B) (Jensen et al. 2000). Under the conditions tested, pam71 was not performing state transitions, while they were observed in cmt1 and the wild type (Figure 7A). What is also noteworthy is that far‐red light had no effect on the steady‐state fluorescence level in pam71. We also estimated the antenna distribution by measuring an emission spectrum at 77 K after 15 min illumination with blue light (Figure 7C,D). In comparison to the wild type, both pam71 and cmt1 had a relatively higher signal from PSII, indicating a larger PSII antenna or a higher proportion of damaged PSI to PSII. In conclusion, in pam71, the antenna is not relocated under varying light qualities and far‐red light does not impact the steady‐state fluorescence level.

Discussion

Mn is an essential element in photosynthesis as a catalyst for water splitting in PSII. In this study, we provide a deeper analysis of the effects of Mn deficiency by characterizing the Mn‐transporter mutants pam71 and cmt1 in A. thaliana . First, we quantified the ion content in thylakoids and leaves, clarifying the roles of PAM71 and CMT1 in Mn translocation (Table 1). Next, we provide details on OEC inactivation (Figures 1, 2, 3, Table 2) and show that the mutants are highly susceptible to PSII photoinhibition (Figure 6). We also confirm results from previous studies showing that both mutants have lower photosynthetic efficiency, while NPQ is reduced in pam71 and increased in cmt1 (Figure 1) (Eisenhut et al. 2018; Frank et al. 2019; Schneider et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2018). Then, we provide data on PSI kinetics (Figures 1 and 5, Table 3) and confirm earlier reports on PSI degradation in pam71 (Figure 4) (Frank et al. 2019; Schneider et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2016). Finally, we demonstrate that state transitions are absent in pam71, but not in cmt1.

Our data show that both mutants suffer from a Mn decrease in thylakoids in relation to other quantified metals, while the metal content is unaffected in whole rosettes (Table 1). This confirms that PAM71 and CMT1 are responsible for Mn transport across the thylakoid and the chloroplast membrane, respectively, as proposed by Schneider et al. (2016) and Eisenhut et al. (2018). We also observe an increase in Ca, Fe, Mg, and Cu. The elevated levels of cations might contribute to the increase in ion conductivity over the thylakoid membrane reported by Schneider et al. (2016). These authors also reported that thylakoids of pam71 accumulated more than twice the amount of Ca compared to wild type in light‐dependent Ca uptake experiments. The increase in Ca, Fe, Mg, and Cu in thylakoids of the mutants may be due to an upregulation of less selective ion transporters in the thylakoid membrane. High Fe and Cu concentrations in thylakoids may also induce high oxidative stress that contributes to the phenotype of both mutants in addition to the low Mn availability.

According to Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, electron transport in the photosystems is altered in both Mn transporter mutants and PSII is highly sensitive to photoinhibition. As shown in Figure S1, pam71 and cmt1 have pale leaves and elevated F0 levels, indicative of PSII with damaged OEC. Reduced incorporation of Mn into PSII has previously been reported in both pam71 and cmt1 (Eisenhut et al. 2018; Schneider et al. 2016). This is confirmed by our fluorescence induction and decay curves, showing that at least part of all PSII centers have impaired OECs and thereby a slower forward electron transport at the PSII acceptor side. Inactivation of OEC can have several consequences. On one hand, it is known that an impaired OEC leads to enhanced susceptibility of PSII to photodamage due to the accumulation of P680+/TyrZ + (e.g., Jegerschoeld et al. 1990; Hakala et al. 2005). On the other hand, a slowdown of forward electron transport at the PSII acceptor side indicates a more positive midpoint potential of QA/QA −, which also slows down backreaction and recombination, protecting against singlet oxygen‐induced photoinhibition (Rutherford and Krieger‐Liszkay 2001). Here, the negative effects on the electron transport in the mutants suggest that the donor‐side‐induced PSII photoinhibition overwhelms the protective effect at the PSII acceptor side. In addition, the PSII repair in low light that allows OEC assembly is not visible in pam71 (Figure 6), possibly due to low Mn availability.

Our data show that the ratio of PC to PSI is affected in pam71(Figure 4B). Immunoblotting confirms that this is due to a decrease in the PSI content, while PC remains at wild‐type levels (Figure 4C). This may be due to a downregulation of PSI to balance the stoichiometry between the photosystems. It is also possible that the high Fe, Cu content leads to concomitant oxidative stress and PSI photoinhibition (Table 1). In contrast, in cmt1, the stoichiometry of PC to PSI is retained (Figure 4B,C), which is also supported by previous studies (Eisenhut et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2018).

In a healthy system, PSII is protected from photoinhibition by NPQ, which is primarily activated by a high pH gradient. As seen in Figure 1, NPQ is lower in pam71, contributing to its susceptibility to PSII photoinhibition. In cmt1, on the contrary, NPQ is increased. The pam71 mutant was previously shown to have a small ΔpH across the thylakoid membrane (Schneider et al. 2016). This was also confirmed in another study, showing that the H+‐ATP synthase activity is reduced (Wang et al. 2016). As protonation of the lumen is a result of effective electron transport, this may be further limited in pam71 in comparison to cmt1, as discussed below.

A second pH‐regulated process is photosynthetic control at the Cyt b 6 f. High ΔpH reduces the electron flow to downstream components (Finazzi 2002; Tikhonov et al. 1981), protecting PSI against photoinhibition by the accumulation of P700+ (Sejima et al. 2014). Photosynthetic control is most likely weak in pam71, but not cmt1, as their different NPQ reflects a small and high ΔpH, respectively (Figure 1). Despite this, both mutants were equally limited at the donor side of PSI in actinic light (Figure 1). In cmt1, high photosynthetic control in combination with inactive PSII can explain this donor side limitation. In pam71, on the contrary, the limited electron transport must be attributed to a combination of inactive PSII and other factors, as discussed below.

We show that pam71 does not balance the energy distribution between PSII and PSI through state transitions, while this process is present in cmt1 (Figure 7). State transitions are redox‐regulated at Cyt b 6 f, where the kinase STN7 is activated upon reduction of the PQ pool. STN7 phosphorylates the mobile part of LHCII, triggering its partial relocation from PSII to PSI, while dephosphorylation of LHCII by PPH1 reverses the direction (Bellafiore et al. 2005; Shapiguzov et al. 2010). From our data, it is not clear if pam71 has the mobile antenna locked at PSII or PSI. The emission spectrum at 77 K shows a higher proportion of PSII to PSI fluorescence (Figure 7C,D), which may indicate higher antenna distribution at PSII. The fluorescence shift could also reflect PSI degradation or a larger PSII antenna system; the latter is supported by the low Chla/b ratio (Figure S3). Although questions remain on the precise antenna distribution, their static behavior suggests impaired redox regulation in pam71.

Beside linear electron flow, cyclic flow (CEF) contributes to the ΔpH (for review, see Nawrocki et al. 2019). The increase in re‐reduction rates of P700+ and PC+ after far‐red illumination in pam71 (Figure 5, Table 3) could reflect an activation of cyclic flow. The faster re‐reduction rates of PC+ and P700+ in pam71 may indicate that more reductants are available in the mutant than in the wild type. One must keep in mind that re‐reduction in the dark, as measured here, requires only a very low electron flow that seems to be not significant in the light. CEF under steady‐state conditions would result in a higher ΔpH and subsequent activation of NPQ, which is not seen in pam71 (Figure 1). The most likely scenario is that in both wild type and the mutants, the overall electron transport is dominated by the linear flow. Even in low actinic light (< 200 μmol quanta m−2 s−1), electron flow from PSII is low in pam71, explaining the high PSI donor‐side limitation. This would be the case if the ratio between PSI and PSII is unbalanced. The mutant may try to compensate for the imbalance by downregulating the number of PSI centers and by activating CEF. However, these two processes seem not to be sufficient to counteract the limitations at PSII.

pam71 has a seemingly reduced PQ‐pool in actinic light, as reflected by the high steady state fluorescence level (Figures 7 and S2), and possibly lower photosynthetic control than the wild type, as discussed above. However, this would presumably lead to a high electron flow reaching PSI, which is unlikely due to the high donor‐side limitation, Y(ND) (Figure 1). The coexistence of a reduced PQ‐pool, low photosynthetic control and high Y(ND) can be explained by inefficient electron transfer between the two photosystems. The bottleneck is likely not the amount of Cyt b 6 f, since PetB is at wild type levels (Figure S4), but rather in the diffusion of PQ, PC or both. This may be a result of stoichiometric imbalance of PSII and PSI to other components in the electron transport, as suggested in the model shown in Figure 8. This imbalance is further supported by previous studies, in which it was shown that the levels of PSII and PSI subunits were reduced, while Cyt b 6 f and ATP synthase levels were similar to wild type levels (Schneider et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2016).

Model of PQ and PC diffusion in pam71, cmt1 and wild type. A top view and a cross‐section of the thylakoid membrane are shown. Arrows mark diffusion pathways. The PQ is reduced to plastoquinol (PQH2) at PSII and oxidized back to PQ at Cyt b 6 f. This requires diffusion of PQ and PQH2 between the two protein complexes in the thylakoid membrane. PC is in turn reduced at Cyt b 6 f and oxidized at PSI, which requires its diffusion in the thylakoid lumen. In pam71, a low ratio of PSII to other proteins, such as light‐harvesting complexes (LHCII) and Cyt b 6 f, creates crowding of proteins in the thylakoid membrane which impairs diffusion of PQ and PQH2. As a result, locally reduced PQ molecules accumulate around PSII. The diffusion of PC in the lumen is also limited, as low stoichiometry of PSI to PC and Cyt b 6 f leads to competition for space between PC and PC+. In cmt1, stoichiometric balance facilitates diffusion of both PQ, PQH2 and PC.

Due to the relatively low amount of PSII, PQ and plastoquinol (PQH2) have to diffuse longer distances on average in the thylakoid membrane to access all Cyt b 6 f. It has been suggested that fast PQ/PQH2 diffusion is partly limited to smaller domains, containing a restricted number of PSII and Cyt b 6 f (Lavergne et al. 1992). In such a case, inactivation of PSII would restrain PQH2 oxidation at the Cyt b 6 f complexes within the same domain, lowering the overall number of complexes participating in electron transport (Kirchhoff et al. 2000). Moreover, low diffusion would generate a locally reduced PQH2 pool around PSII, increasing the steady‐state fluorescence as seen in Figure 7. A similar problem may be present in the lumen, where the low PSI content increases the average migration distance for PC between the photosystem and Cyt b 6 f. We propose that the stoichiometric imbalance in pam71 leads to reduced electron transport, impairing proton pumping, followed by lower NPQ. In addition, low diffusion of PQH2 to Cyt b 6 f would also impair the redox activation of state transitions (Bellafiore et al. 2005). In cmt1, on the contrary, stoichiometric balance is preserved by downregulation of additional proteins along with the photosystems. This is indicated by preserved ratios of PSI/PC (Figure 5) and Chl a/b (Figure S3), the latter reflecting a proportional number of antenna proteins to photosystems. Previous studies also showed that thylakoid protein complexes are equally affected in cmt1, preventing a stoichiometric imbalance (Eisenhut et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2018). In this manner, efficient activation of regulatory mechanisms is better maintained in cmt1, resulting in a different phenotype than pam71.

Unlike cmt1, no changes in transcription levels were seen in earlier studies of pam71 (Eisenhut et al. 2018; Schneider et al. 2016). We hypothesize that the differences in photosynthetic regulation between the two mutants might therefore reflect changes in expression levels, versus direct impairment of PSII through Mn deprivation. Variations between Mn‐deficient organisms were also reported in the literature. In accordance with our data of pam71, lower NPQ and loss of state transitions were shown in Mn‐deficient barley and maize (Husted et al. 2009; Long et al. 2020; Schmidt et al. 2016), while higher NPQ was seen in Marchantia (Hani and Krieger‐Liszkay 2024). In the latter, a decrease in PC content was also reported, which contrasts with the unaltered levels in pam71 (Figure 4). This could reflect a closer resemblance of Marchantia to cmt1. Finally, downregulation of transcription levels in cmt1 could speak for a combination of stresses (Eisenhut et al. 2018). This was shown in maize, where transcription of photosynthetic proteins was more affected when combining a latent Mn deficiency with high light, in comparison to low light conditions (Long et al. 2020). Based on this, we hypothesize that pam71 is a suitable model for effects directly related to Mn deprivation of photosynthetic components, while cmt1 may be a better representative of Mn deficiency combined with additional stress factors.

Conclusion

Both Mn transporter mutants show many common features, such as lower Y(I), Y(II), high Y(ND), higher susceptibility to PSII photoinhibition and alterations of electron transport at the PSII acceptor side. However, there are clear differences in their photosynthetic regulation. While cmt1 might adjust for Mn deficiency at the transcriptional level and thereby retain pH and redox signaling through stoichiometric balance, altered regulation in pam71 may be the direct cause of Mn starvation in PSII. The photosynthetic characteristics identified in this study can be used to gain more insight into other Mn‐deficient systems and the effects of impaired regulation.
