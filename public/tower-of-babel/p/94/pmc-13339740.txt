Decentralisation of the Healthcare Systems and the Functioning of Public Health Programmes in Single‐Party States: A Systematic Review
Authors: Phonevilay Viphonephom, Ho Van Hoa, Sengchanh Kounnavong, Daniel Reinharz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDecentralisation is the transfer of authority from central to local governments, involving shared responsibilities in planning, management, and decision‐making. In public health, decentralisation might help improve service delivery by allowing local authorities to tailor interventions to the specific needs of diverse, geographically dispersed communities. However, no systematic review has been done on the state of knowledge regarding the capacity of local governments to influence the emergence and operation of national public health programmes in Marxist‐Leninist single‐party states.MethodFollowing the PRISMA protocol, 859 articles from the PubMed, EMBASE and Web of Science databases were examined. To be selected, studies had to be conducted in a Marxist‐Leninist single‐party country with a centralised economy and focused on the question of the condition of power sharing between government levels in the design and implementation of specific public health programmes. Only articles in English published between 2013 and 2023 were considered, resulting in 18 studies. Their quality was assessed using the MMAT 2018 and SANRA tools.ResultsLimited resources and the Party's fundamental ideology may constrain the extent of power sharing influencing local government empowerment. However, factors such as the Party's local unit engagement, collaboration with external organisations, and use of digital communication platforms enhance the capacity of local government to participate in public health programmes design and execution.ConclusionDespite a politically centralising structure, one‐party countries can, under certain circumstances, share responsibilities across government levels in order to increase the adequacy of programmes to meet the health needs of the population.Keywords: decentralisation, one‐party state, public health programmes

Introduction

Decentralisation can be defined as the transfer of authority from the central government to local governments. It represents a sociopolitical process of sharing power and responsibilities between the central government and local authorities in the areas of planning, management and decision‐making [1]. In practice, the decentralisation form of the healthcare system varies widely across countries. This variation is influenced not only by the nature and extent of power transfer but also by the organizational patterns of the healthcare system in each specific country [2].

In the field of public administration, decentralisation is often categorised into three major groups: deconcentration, delegation, and devolution [1, 2, 3]. Deconcentration, which entails the handing over of some administrative authorities from the central ministry of health to ministry staff working at local levels. Since this type of decentralisation involves the transfer of administrative rather than political authority, the local staff may not be given the authority to decide how those functions are to be performed at their local levels. Delegation, which involves transferring management responsibilities to a semi‐autonomous entity, such as a health board representing various interest groups. Devolution, which encompasses transferring functions to sub‐national governments. This form of decentralisation has a clear legal status, recognized geographical boundaries, a number of functions to perform, and statutory authority to raise revenue and make expenditures. The local bodies of this form are largely independent of the national government in their area of responsibility [2, 3].

The justification underlying the decentralisation of state sectors is often based on arguments that put forward a desire to improve responsiveness to local needs, enhance accountability, increase efficiency and innovation, ensure a more equitable and efficient resource distribution, and to deploy efforts to build local capacity. In addition to this rationale, historical, political and social considerations further underscore the need to grant autonomy to local entities [1, 4, 5, 6]. From a public health perspective, decentralisation can serve as a tool to improve the quality and accessibility of healthcare for all populations, including vulnerable and marginalised groups. Effectively, decentralisation might increase the capacity of health services to be tailored to the specific needs of diverse and geographically dispersed communities thanks to the involvement of local authorities who are generally more attuned to the unique needs and sociocultural contexts of their communities than central government [2, 4].

Public health, as defined by the World Health Organisation (WHO), is an organised activity of society to promote, protect, improve, and restore the health of individuals, specified groups, or the entire population. As a social and political concept, public health aims to enhance quality of life by preventing disease and promoting health at the population level [7].

In a decentralised system, public health programmes tend to emphasise the participation of local governments as well as local communities. This active participation in national programmes is assumed to help ensure that the basic needs of vulnerable populations are met through more inclusive and balanced development, more so than if these programmes were conducted solely at the national level [3]. Decentralisation, by allowing greater representation for various local population groups, including ethnic communities, is often seen as a means of enabling communities to participate in decision‐making regarding their local health services. It promotes greater equity in the healthcare system among the subgroups of the population [2, 3].

The way in which community participation is exercised varies across different types of political systems. In pluralist countries for example, this participation is expressed through the free election of representatives or appointed local representatives who can be replaced depending on their public support. It therefore requires public participation of citizens feeling close to their elected representatives [8].

This concept of participation is quite different to what exist in the five countries that officially incorporate Marxist‐Leninist ideology into their governance (China, Cuba, Lao People's Democratic Republic (Lao PDR), North Korea, and Vietnam), even though their economic and political systems have evolved significantly, particularly in China and Vietnam [9]. These states are led by a single Party that formally considers legitimate to promote centralised economic planning and the suppression of political pluralism, in order to protect the ideal of the revolution and guiding the society to a communist‐based ideal organisation of the society. By ideal state, the Party means a stateless, classless society where all means of production are collectively owned, social and economic inequalities are abolished, and distribution is based on the principle from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs. Indeed, in these countries, political power remains concentrated in a single party of Marxist‐Leninist obedience, which controls the government apparatus and instills the collectivist ideology of Marxism‐Leninism among the population [9, 10]. Within these countries, public participation is expressed through party channels of communication, especially those of the mass organisations that are present at all levels of society. Mass organisations are large‐scale groups or associations controlled by the ruling party that serve as channels for the party to mobilise the population and to express community concerns. They channel grassroots opinions and demands upward, influencing policy decisions to some extent [11].

The specificity of single‐party Marxist‐Leninist countries encouraged us to consider the question of decentralisation of the healthcare system in a particular way. Except for North Korea, one‐party Marxist Leninist countries, that is, China, Cuba, the Lao PDR and Vietnam, have characteristics that justify decentralisation of services, such as the presence of diverse population, including ethnic minorities that are often concentrated in specific areas of the country [12, 13, 14, 15]. However, they are also led by a party that promotes the concept of centralised democracy. This concept proposes that although party members have the right to participate in the decision‐making process through open discussions, debates and elections of delegates to decision‐making bodies of the party, the actual decision‐making process takes place at the central level of party leadership. Decisions made at this level are binding to all party members. From an operational perspective, the concept of democratic centralism translates into a tendency towards centralisation of services offered to the population. This concept is formally characterised by three main fundamental principles: the establishment of party organisations' leadership through elections at different levels; the requirement for party organisations at all levels to report to the members or units who elected them; and the obligation for lower units to accept and unconditionally enforce decisions from higher units [16, 17].

In other words, forces advocating for more decentralisation, such as the diversity of the population, must contend with a political structure that maintains central control over the entire government apparatus. This review focuses on the outcome of this dialectic on power sharing and its effect on the emergence, implementation and functioning of public health programmes in Marxist‐Leninist single‐party countries. No systematic review of literature has been done to examine the state of knowledge on how decentralisation and local government empowerment intersect with national public health efforts in Marxist‐Leninist single‐party countries. A deeper understanding of the decentralisation dynamics in these countries would help stakeholders, the governments, external actors as NGOs and other international organisations, design public health interventions that align with the actual distribution of power and autonomy across different levels of government thanks to the knowledge about de jure decentralisation (as defined by legal frameworks) and de facto decentralisation (as observed in practice) that underlies the governance structures in the real life [1, 4, 6]. It would allow better identification of the factors influencing health policy development processes, including those driven by bottom‐up initiatives. Finally, it would support the design of effective accountability mechanisms that align with the flow of funding.

The objective of this review was to explore the state of knowledge on the capacity of local governments to influence the emergence and operation of national public health programmes in Marxist‐Leninist single‐party states.

Materials and Methods

This study protocol was published in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42023494078). This study was conducted according to the PRISMA guideline [18], a review of published scientific studies that included reviews, research articles, dissertations, and theses. Only studies written in English were included.

Eligibility Criteria

The eligibility criteria for the studies were as follows:

–Studies had to be conducted in a Marxist Leninist single‐party country.

Studies had to be conducted in a Marxist Leninist single‐party country.

–Studies had to focus on the functioning or implementation of a specific public health policy or programme within these countries that fall under the responsibility of the health sector or the Ministry of Health.

Studies had to focus on the functioning or implementation of a specific public health policy or programme within these countries that fall under the responsibility of the health sector or the Ministry of Health.

Literature Search

This review aimed to evaluate the knowledge about power sharing between levels of governance and the design and functioning of public health policies.

A librarian was consulted to identify sources of information, recommending the use of PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science (see S2 Search Strategy). These databases were chosen to capture the most relevant articles related to the research questions and topics of the study. Additionally, using at least three databases is a standard recommendation for a systematic review. Additional reference lists from articles included in the review were also reviewed. The literature search of these three databases was limited to the year range between 2013 and 2023. This time frame was selected for practical reasons (working with a manageable number of documents), while being able to capture recent and relevant research on the state of healthcare decentralisation in the countries of interest. In effect, the decade has witnessed socioeconomic events that have marked healthcare systems in the target countries. Two thousand and thirteen marked the launch of China's Belt and Road Initiative. This Initiative has brought about economic, social, and health‐related changes across the region, influencing patterns of population movement and local governance. Also, it has had a profound impact on the economic landscape and connectivity dynamics within various regions, including China local regions itself, as well as countries such as Lao PDR and Vietnam [19]. Marxist‐Leninist countries, except North Korea, experienced political changes, such as increased openness to international trade and public engagement for constitutions in Vietnam [20] and the emergence of nonstate forms of management in public organisations in Cuba [21]. Moreover, 2013 was described as the year of the internet of Things [22] because of marked advancements in technology that have transformed healthcare systems globally through digital communication tools and innovative management models related to health service provision and delivery. These advancements have reshaped relationship dynamics among health providers and patients, as well as within healthcare providers themselves [22, 23]. Additionally, in the wake of the COVID‐19 pandemic, there has been a notable focus on public health programmes by national governments, particularly in low‐resource settings. These changes are also expected to impact on the power‐sharing dynamics between governance levels in the health sectors of one‐party state Marxist Leninist countries.

The search strategy was based on the following three concepts: (1) Decentralisation (‘decentralisation’, ‘devolution’, ‘delegation’, ‘deconcentration’, ‘central level’, ‘central government’, ‘central authority’, ‘national level’, ‘local level’, ‘local government’, ‘local authority’); (2) Health programme (‘Health policy performance’, ‘Health policy application’, ‘Health policy implementation’, ‘Health policy’, ‘Health programme’); and (3) Lists of single‐party state Marxist Leninist countries (‘China’, ‘Laos’, ‘Lao PDR’, ‘Vietnam’, ‘Cuba’, and ‘North Korea’).

The selection of these five nations, China, Cuba, Lao PDR, North Korea, and Vietnam, is based on their status as Marxist Leninist single‐party states [9].

Data Management

A file containing the list of all articles identified through the search strategy was created and imported into EndNote. Any duplicate articles were manually identified and removed. The information and details of the journal articles were then exported to an Excel file.

Selection of Studies

Two reviewers independently selected the studies and extracted the data to ensure the validity and reliability of the extracted information. Any conflicts were resolved by a third senior reviewer in case of disagreement between the two reviewers. A list of publications from each database was screened in accordance with the study objectives. The article selection and screening process were based on the eligibility criteria. Qualitative, quantitative, and mixed‐method studies were all considered.

Data Extraction

From the articles included in the review, the following data were extracted: objective of the study, research question, study design (including the type of study, population, data year and source, and unit of analysis), main findings notably related to (1) the political, administrative, and financial characteristics of healthcare system decentralisation found from the selected countries; (2) the effects of decentralisation on public health programmes and health outcomes of the population; and (3) facilitators and constraints associated with local government involvement in national public health programmes.

Additionally, to provide information on the functioning of public health programmes and the capacity influencing local government involvement in managing national health programmes, associated factors were categorised into facilitators and constraints. This approach helped to structure the findings of this review.

Data extraction was independently conducted by two researchers (P.V and H.V.H), with any conflicts being resolved by a third reviewer (D.R). The interpretation of findings and synthesis of knowledge were achieved through consensus between the first two researchers.

Quality Assessment

The quality of the studies was assessed by two reviewers (P.V and H.V.H) using two tools: the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) version 2018 for systematic mixed studies reviews, for example qualitative, quantitative and mixed methods studies [24] and the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) for assessing the quality of review articles [25].

The MMAT is a five‐criteria instrument, with a total score ranging from 0% to 100%, where 100% indicates a high‐quality paper. The SANRA tool consists of six items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 12. A score of 4 or below indicates poor quality, a score between 5 and 8 indicates medium quality, and a score of 9 or higher indicates high quality [24, 25].

Results

A total of 1163 articles were identified. After removing duplicates, 859 articles underwent a review of titles and abstracts. Following the elimination of irrelevant articles based on the review objectives, 60 articles were selected for full‐text assessment. After full‐text assessment, 42 articles were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria. In total 18 studies were retained (Figure 1).

Out of the 18 studies, 11 were conducted in China, four in Vietnam, two in Lao PDR, and one in North Korea. Additionally, one of the studies, which were primarily conducted in Vietnam, discussed the implementation of health programmes in the context of decentralisation in Cuba. Among 18 articles, 13 were qualitative, two used mixed methods, and three were literature reviews. Fourteen studies focused on national policies/programmes, two focused on local/regional policies/programmes, and two focused on pilot programmes in specific cities (Table 1).

State of Decentralisation Underlying Health Policies/Programmes

The studies show diversity in the state of decentralisation of healthcare systems and the degree of political, administrative, and financial management within healthcare systems and public health programmes operating at the local level. Conversely, the sole article on North Korea indicates that the country operates a centralised healthcare system, wherein all power and responsibilities are fully controlled by the central government [39]. In other countries, decentralised healthcare systems typically exhibit a multilevel governance structure. The community, district, province, and central levels are all formally entitled to some responsibility for national public health programmes. However, regardless of the expected involvement of local governments, the central government still holds considerable power at the national level, largely through centrally designed, vertical‐oriented policies, plans, and monitoring mechanisms. National policies and programmes often operate according to a top‐down governance process. The central level disseminates administrative orders, documents, guidelines, regulations, and technical proposals to provinces, municipalities, and districts, thereby showcasing the vertical administrative structure [26, 27, 31, 34, 35, 37, 40, 42, 43]. In the real life, there is limited room for local input and autonomy in creating and sustaining new programmes [34, 35].

All the studies emphasise that healthcare funds for national programmes implemented across the country primarily come from the central government. However, the system can be flexible. The review shows that local authorities may sometimes formulate or develop local policies, plans, and actions to ensure that national programmes are adapted to the local context. Nevertheless, this local input requires proper funding, which tends to be scarce. This might be one of the reasons why only studies conducted in China present programmes developed and implemented by local governments [32, 33, 38, 40, 41]. Apart from China, the local governments included in the studies were found to have a limited ability to influence the design of national policies and programmes [31, 34]. This contrasts with other public health programme stakeholders, who have strong ties to local authorities and communities, notably external donors and NGOs. These stakeholders possess technical expertise that is both missing and valuable for the country. They work in specific regions and villages, legitimising the direct and indirect influence they might have on public health programmes. Moreover, to meet their financial and technical commitments, they often implement activities that empower local governments. This approach aims to include some bottom‐up strategies, from the community level to the policy‐making level, in public health programmes. Empowered local authorities, who are expected to be more familiar with the local context and the needs of the population in the community, can play a crucial role in transferring responsibilities from national health programmes to local health offices and then to local actors who have close contact with the target population in the village [33, 36, 42].

Effect of Decentralisation on Health Programmes/Policies

When national public health programmes lie on a certain degree of decentralisation, local authorities have the responsibility to help implement these programmes while adhering to centrally imposed guidelines, targets, and evaluation indicators. As expected, the involvement of local governments makes programmes more adaptable to local conditions. It also enhances the commitment of local governments to find additional local resources for the functioning of health programmes and policies [29, 32, 34, 38, 40]. However, local governments often struggle to generate sufficient local funds for public programmes [32, 33, 38, 40, 41].

National public health programmes also benefit from the active participation of local governments through an increased sense of community belonging at the local level. This leads to increased citizen participation in adapting these programmes to meet community needs [27, 33, 36, 37].

To summarise the findings, as showed on the Figure 2, of all the included studies show that giving power to local governments over public health programmes contributes to ensuring more equality in term of access to health provisions and service delivery [26, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 38, 41, 43], as well as in disease prevention and awareness for healthier behaviours [27, 29, 30, 31, 36, 38, 40], increasing the quality of health service delivery [28, 34, 36, 38, 43], and more cost‐saving within public health systems that are cost‐effectiveness in medical practices within healthcare systems [26, 34, 41, 43].

Facilitators

Facilitators consist of four main factors: (1) involvement of the Party's mass organisations at the local level; (2) collaboration between the local government and external actors; (3) use of technologies and internet platforms; and (4) sociocultural context underlying interpersonal relationships.

The first factor, which is specific to Marxist Leninist single‐party countries, consists of the involvement of the centrally‐lead Party's mass organisations in most social and health programmes across the country. These mass organisations, familiar with local healthcare issues and closely connected to health centres and communities, are almost essential collaborators in social and health programmes that are implemented and carried out not only by state organisations, but also by or in collaboration with foreign organisations. Their role aims to facilitate the implementation of health and social programmes by bridging the gap between healthcare workers and others, and the local community. This involves disseminating information related to the health programme and activities, mobilising community members to participate in planned activities, and connecting programme workers with relevant sectors or local organisations, particularly in health programmes aimed at promotion and behaviour change, such as tobacco cessation, dengue vector control, and the prevention and management of non‐communicable diseases like hypertension [30, 34, 37].

The second factor is the presence of collaboration between local governments and external donors and NGOs. Projects from external donors and NGOs can help local governments gain a better understanding of local health issues and community needs, which in turn facilitates their desire to assume more responsibility [33, 36, 42]. A deeper understanding of the local context and population needs of local leaders has been shown to incite local government involvement in centrally defined health programmes. This has a positive effect on the programmes' effectiveness by making health programmes more adaptable to the context of target populations. For example, several articles highlight health programmes that address sociocultural beliefs and hierarchical norms, including community behaviours related to malaria, mental health, and cervical cancer screening. Understanding beliefs and norms may help address low participation, limited acceptability, and lack of cooperation from patients and/or families in receiving services and interventions for health prevention and promotion. This, in turn, helps enhance the overall effectiveness of health programmes [29, 31, 38].

The third factor involves the use of new technologies, particularly mass media and internet communication platforms [40]. These technologies offer local communities to have a stronger voice in decision‐making processes at the central level regarding the design, planning and execution of national public health programmes. These technologies also allow communities and local governments to access information that supports their demands and disseminate information on local health issues that can be addressed by national public health programmes. They helped for example bring attention to sensitive issues rooted in societal norms and shared understanding, such as child sexual abuse or vaccine hesitancy, enabling more culturally informed and responsive interventions [27, 40]. Finally, new technologies empower communities and local governments in their involvement in public health programmes by providing them with tools that show their ability to be active participants in national public health programmes management teams [40].

Finally, one notes that the reported effect on the involvement of local governments, there is a cultural trait in some countries that puts significant influence on interpersonal relations, both formally and informally, among health actors across different authority levels. These interpersonal relations facilitate acknowledgement by those at the higher levels of the roles played at the local level. This allows fostering connections of the upper level with local communities and other key local bodies and actors in the field. These formal and informal interactions among these stakeholders form dynamic relationships that can be used to continuously adapt national programmes, policies, plans, and strategies to meet the specific needs of communities, especially for programmes that depend on strong multisectoral and multidisciplinary collaboration among diverse actors, such as those addressing non‐communicable diseases or involving public and private partnerships in drug policy initiatives [27, 34, 43]. It has been shown to empower local governments to manage and take more decisive actions in implementing national health programmes at the local level [43].

Constraints

Constraints mainly consist of four factors: (1) political will at the central level regarding the control of national health programmes; (2) availability of resources; (3) ambiguity in the distribution of responsibilities between levels of government; and (4) technological infrastructure in the health system.

The first constraint lies in the fundamental political principle of Marxist Leninist single‐party countries, the national political leadership over governments, which leaves little room for local levels to contribute to national policymaking and intervention process. A top‐down approach to the design, implementation and management of national public health programmes can lead to under‐exploiting the strengths of local health authorities in responding to the health needs of the population [34, 35, 39].

Another constraint is the reliance of public health programmes on external donors. When national health programmes are supported by external actors, governments may find themselves aligning more with donors' requirements than their own, constraining the capacity of local governments to adapt programmes to the local context [31, 42]. Additional factors include difficulty in raising local funds to support greater sharing of responsibilities between government levels, low financial incentives for local staff not under the central government payroll, as well as a lack of expertise and material resources for health interventions and activities at local levels [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 41, 42].

The third factor is the ambiguity regarding the distribution of power and responsibilities among government levels, leading to an unclear division of labour and role responsibilities within organizations [29, 31, 38]. This affects cooperation between teams at different authority levels and may result in low acceptance of services among the target populations of the programmes. Unclear distribution of responsibilities across health authorities has been shown to result in poor collaboration between healthcare providers, inadequate programme supervision and evaluation, and delays or inconsistencies in incentive payments for activities implemented for health at the community level [31, 38].

The fourth factor is the impact of the progressive digitalization of data in the health system, which affects the autonomy of local governments to assume more responsibility for public health programmes [29, 31, 33]. The central government might increasingly have the capacity to define the health needs of the population and control answers to these needs without requiring any involvement from local governments as before.

Finally, it can be highlighted that the factors mentioned above have mainly been reported in studies carried out in China, which among all the Marxist‐Leninist single‐party countries, has the most human, material and financial resources, enabling local governments to assume more responsibilities for ensuring the health and well‐being of all population groups.

In summary, there may be a power‐sharing across government levels in single‐party Marxist‐Leninist countries, regarding the development and implementation of national health programmes and policies. When this is the case, local government involvement can contribute to reaching positive health objectives, like a more equitable access to services, improved disease prevention and health awareness, enhanced quality of care, and greater cost‐effectiveness within the public health system. Although these countries are governed by the principle of democratic centralism, local governments can play a role in health governance. This involvement is more likely if mass organizations support requests to adapt programmes to local needs, adequate human and financial resources are available, the sociocultural context is actively promoting the specificity of local entities, and new digital health technologies are utilized at the local levels.

Discussion

This review highlights the presence of four critical dimensions for understanding how local governments in single‐party Marxist Leninist countries can actively participate in the development and execution of national policies and programmes. These four dimensions, as illustrated in Figure 3, encompass political, sociocultural, and technological development aspects, as well as resources.

Components of the four critical dimensions proposed for understanding the influence of local governments in single‐party Marxist Leninist countries in the development and execution of national health policies and programmes.

Political Aspects

In Marxist‐Leninist countries that operate under a single‐party state, the Party holds a leading influence over the country's national health and social policies and programmes. This influence stems from a particular governance model based on the concept of centralised democracy. The central level has the dominant role in overseeing decision‐making processes and governance across all administrative levels. Centralised democracy gives a voice to the local, the communities and the local governments, but the power to decide and act remains at the higher level of governance [16, 17]. This review provides evidence that the political importance of the concept of democratic centralism in single‐party Marxist‐Leninist countries has remained despite major political reforms experienced by nearly all these countries. Economic reforms have notably integrated market principles with the planned economy that predominated during the first decades of Marxist‐Leninist single‐party countries. These reforms have formally empowered local governments [44]. This political ideology and its concept make Marxist Leninist single‐party states unique in terms of their decentralised healthcare systems, which may differ from those in pluralist countries.

The findings of this review align with existing literature on pluralist countries, particularly liberal democracies in Western Europe and North America, such as the United Kingdom, Canada, and France, where political decentralisation occurs through the election of local assemblies whose power extends to fiscal and administrative decisions [8]. Above all, it can be shown that local governments and communities can influence national policies related to the population well‐being. As a result, health service delivery becomes more responsive to the specific needs of regions, communities, and individuals. In contrast, in Marxist‐Leninist single‐party states, most fiscal and administrative decisions are centralised, with political decisions made primarily by Party members from the central to the local levels [9, 11]. Whether the mechanisms that allow communities to express their health‐related needs, through Party's mass organisations, which differ from the way it is done in liberal democracies, are effective remains an important subject of study to be carried out.

Resources

Regarding financial resources, one has to take into account the fact that all these Marxist‐Leninist single‐party states differ in term of economic development. Two are upper‐middle‐income countries (China and Cuba), two are lower‐middle‐income countries (Lao PDR and Vietnam), and one is a low‐income country, North Korea [45]. Consequently, there is varying capacity at the central and local levels to raise financial resources. This diversity impacts the ability at the regional level to influence public health programmes and policies.

In addition, as highlighted in the Results section of this systematic review, one of the key factors shared among these countries is the lack of human resources for health at the local levels. This includes both an insufficient number of staff and unequal distribution of expertise across the various disciplines responsible for health interventions and activities at local and community levels [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 41, 42]. This issue is particularly evident in low‐resource countries, where highly skilled professionals are more likely to work for the central government rather than for subnational or local governments. As a result, the central government is often better positioned to plan, design, and implement interventions that are both cost‐effective and of high quality [46].

The literature also highlights the fact that while local governments may be more attuned to local needs in term of human and financial resources, they can also be more susceptible to influence from elites from central and local levels, leading to unequal distribution of public spending [4, 47]. However, this point was not addressed in the articles included in this review.

Finally, one notes that while the provision of social services such as education and healthcare is frequently carried out by local governments in pluralist countries [5], these services in Marxist‐Leninist single‐party states, tend to be centrally managed. In these countries, the state of decentralisation is not primary driven by a desire to enhance political accountability to the local population. Instead, it often serves to strengthen the state's capacity to promote economic growth. Decentralisation tends to primarily aim at facilitating the implementation of industrial, fiscal, or land policies to attract investment [46].

Sociocultural Aspects

The results from this review highlight the fact that, despite sharing a common political system based on a common ideology, single‐party Marxist Leninist countries have important differences that impact the involvement of local governments in national public health programmes.

First, these countries vary in terms of the percentage of the population belonging to officially recognized ethnic minorities, with the presence of minorities being a fundamental reason for the active involvement of local governments in public health programmes. Cuba does not have any formal ethnic minorities according to their national census [48]. Minorities constitute approximately 8% of the Chinese population [13], 15% of the Vietnamese population [15] and nearly half of the population in Lao PDR [14]. These demographic data may affect the perceived need to share responsibilities between government levels in establishing and managing national public health programmes.

Second, one notes that the countries, with one exception (Cuba), are located in the Asia‐Pacific region. Consequently, they are influenced by Asia‐Pacific sociocultural characteristics, with one of the major common denominators being a culture that gives importance to respect for hierarchies, where obligations flow from superior to inferior (elder to young, more educated to less educated, ruler to subject) and reciprocity is expected in return [49]. This constrains the ability of local governments to act autonomously not because of a lack of skills or capacity but rather because of a societal commitment to maintaining the hierarchical structure that underpins society.

However, this sociohistorical context has gradually transformed with the gradual adoption of more individualistic values from Western culture. There is an evolution of relationships in culture norms of the Marxist‐Leninist single‐party countries in Asia, where younger populations are increasingly questioning traditional hierarchies [50]. This societal shift could help empower local governments to contribute more effectively to ensuring that national public health programmes are adapted to better meet the specific needs of their populations.

Technology Development

As elsewhere around the world, the advent of social networks is reshaping relationships among healthcare professionals in Marxist Leninist single‐party states. They facilitate the rapid spread of knowledge across governance levels, bypassing traditional hierarchical channels. This provides local governments with a pathway for overcoming hierarchical constraints that limit their ability to advise higher levels and manage national public health programmes [50].

In summary, while single‐party Marxist‐Leninist countries formally maintain centralised political control over public health programmes, there is evidence that power‐sharing across levels of government is possible.

Limitations

This review has some limitations. First, there is a risk of selection bias since only English‐language articles were included. None of the retained articles were published in the country where the study took place. This exclusion potentially omits valuable information from non‐English sources, especially regarding the balance of centralisation and decentralisation in these countries.

Second, there is an unequal representation of studies among the countries examined. The majority of included studies were from China, followed by Vietnam, Lao PDR, North Korea, and none from Cuba. This disparity limits the generalisability of the findings and hinders comprehensive comparisons of programme outcomes across these countries. As a result, the conclusions of this review require confirmation by studies conducted in countries where few organizational studies have been carried out.

Third, it is noted that if only high‐quality articles were considered, the data would be essentially limited to China. Considering only high‐quality articles would also result in findings focused on facilitators such as the role of the Party's mass organisation, the empowerment of local governance by external donors and new communication technologies. Constraints related to external donor requirements, the capacity to raise funds at the local level and the unclear distribution of responsibility would not be presented.

Finally, this review highlights a diverse range of national health programmes and policies implemented across various Marxist‐Leninist single‐party states. However, its findings may not be readily applicable to other sectors. The health sector possesses unique characteristics, such as the high level of expertise required and the interdisciplinary nature of its workforce, that may shape the dynamics of decentralisation in ways that differ from other sectors.

Conclusion

Despite the centralising tendencies inherent in the Marxist‐Leninist political ideology, it is possible in Marxist‐Leninist single party states for local governments to be involved in the design and implementation of national public health programmes. Studies show that empowering local governments might help reaching the theoretical benefit pursued by decentralisation. It can promote more equitable access to health services, enhance disease prevention efforts, encourage healthier behaviours, and improve the quality and cost‐effectiveness of healthcare delivery. Yet, empowering local governments is not straightforward. Characteristics of the internal functioning of the party organs linking the central body to the communities, availability of resources, sociocultural factors, and technological adoption have to conjunct in order to create a favourable background for local governments' participation in public health governance and ultimately affect the impact and effectiveness of national health policies and programmes.
