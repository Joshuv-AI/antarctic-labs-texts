Implementing after action review in an Irish tertiary hospital: a mixed-methods evaluation of safety culture and second victim experience and support
Authors: Siobhán E. McCarthy, Mairéad Finn, Gintare Valentelyte, Fiona Boland, Catherine Hogan, Loretta Jenkins, Theresa Keane, Lisa Mellon, Lorraine Schwanberg, Aisling Walsh, David J. Williams, Natasha Rafter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence for After Action Reviews (AAR), a non-hierarchical debriefing approach, is limited. We explored AAR implementation and measured safety culture and second victim experience and support, before and after introduction of AAR at an Irish hospital.MethodsA mixed-methods study which coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic and a health system cyber-attack was conducted. Hospital selected staff were trained as AAR facilitators using a simulation-based programme (July-Sept 2021). The intervention was the hospital introduction of AAR over 12-months (Sept 2021–Aug 2022). Before training, the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety 2.0 (HSOPS 2.0) and Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST) were distributed to all staff (N = 586), and repeated at 12-months with additional questions on AAR awareness, frequency and duration. At 6-months, focus groups with trained facilitators explored implementation enablers and barriers. Costs were estimated. Quantitative analysis used chi-squared, Fisher’s exact and t-tests to compare pre-/post- responses. Qualitative data was analysed using the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation – Behaviour (COM-B) model. Findings were integrated via triangulation, and the level of implementation was categorised using Roger’s theory of the diffusion of innovations.ResultsNine per cent (n = 50) of staff were trained as AAR facilitators. Survey response rates were 33% pre- and 16% post- AAR implementation. After 12-months, 37% of staff were aware of AAR and 10% had participated in one, at €48 average cost. Approximately, 39 AARs were conducted. The ratio of informal to formal AARs was 2.6:1. No change in overall safety culture occurred despite a pandemic and cyber-attack. Response to error improved. Triangulation of results confirmed improvement in response to error was associated with staff awareness and participation in AAR. Second victim experience and support showed no change in psychological and physical distress, yet professional self-efficacy, perceived organisational support and turnover intentions worsened, with no observed association with AAR.ConclusionsSimulation-based training and national-level policy supported early adoption of AAR. Implementation of AAR was associated with improved response to error at the hospital, but not second victim experiences.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15134-5.

Introduction

Even though the majority of patient harm is preventable, consistent safe healthcare remains difficult to achieve [1, 2]. High reliability organisations demonstrate superior safety performance and culture despite complex and high-risk conditions [3, 4]. Foundational elements of high reliability include institutionalising social and relational learning habits which foster staff anticipation, containment and real-time learning from patient safety events [4, 5]. After Action Review (AAR) is a tool often employed in high reliability organisations [6, 7]. AAR is defined as a non-hierarchical facilitated discussion of an event which enables groups to come to a shared mental model about what happened, why it happened, and to identify learning and improvements [8]. An AAR is held as near as possible after an event and involves facilitating learning among groups about what was expected to happen, what actually happened, why there was a difference and what has been learnt [9].

AAR shares the learning focus of other debriefing approaches (e.g., clinical debriefing, critical incident stress debriefing) but is differentiated by its structured use of the multiple specific questions [10], and focus on incident management and process improvement [11]. Since 2018, the national health system in Ireland, the Health Service Executive (HSE), has incorporated AAR into its Incident Management Framework [9]. The HSE also provides accompanying guidance for AAR implementation [12]. All staff, clinical and non-clinical, at all levels of an organisation may be participants in an AAR [12]. Depending on the severity of the event, AAR may be used as stand-alone or complementary to another incident review [12, 13]. Formal AARs review moderate and minor incidents and debrief staff after major incidents, they must be carried out by a trained AAR facilitator and the production of an AAR report is recommended [12, 13]. In contrast, informal AARs are not a formal incident review methodology and can be led by any staff member to learn from everyday events and no documentation is expected [12, 13]. The HSE promote a ‘culture of AAR’, i.e. that AAR is incorporated into standard practice of incident management in the health sector [9, 12]. This is achieved through regular staff participation in AARs to learn from events, with trained AAR facilitators being supported by their organisations to engage in an ongoing AAR practice i.e. facilitation of formal and informal AARs [12].

Between 2018 and 2021, up to 500 healthcare staff across the public health service in Ireland were trained as AAR facilitators [14]. Except for formal AAR reports submitted to the National Incident Management System (NIMS), little is known about how frequently or how well formal and informal AARs are conducted [14]. The proportion of staff in organisations who require training as AAR facilitators, to generate a culture of AAR, is not known [14]. Meta-analyses have demonstrated that teams who debrief out-perform those who do not [15, 16]. In healthcare in particular, flexibility and suitability of the debrief approach to context is considered more important for effectiveness than the debriefing structure (e.g. low vs. high structure) [15]. Demonstrating evidence of effectiveness, a UK study attributed a 46% reduction in falls rate over a ten-year period to AAR [17]. No studies have examined the effects of healthcare AARs on safety culture and staff outcomes [15, 18].

Well-conducted AARs in the US fire-fighting industry have been positively related to incident learning, team and organisational safety culture [8, 19]. AARs in healthcare may have a similar relation to ‘safety culture’, i.e. the shared values and actions regarding safety which influence motivation to engage in safety behaviours [20]. Patients and their families are the first victims of errors, and there is some evidence that enhancing safety culture supports patient outcomes [21] and reduces adverse events [22]. However, healthcare workers are ‘second victims’ and are also negatively impacted [23]. Non-punitive and supportive elements of safety culture have been associated with improved second-victim experience [24, 25]. During AARs, participants agree ground rules such as respect, no-blame, non-hierarchy, allowing multiple perspectives, and a focus on learning and improvement (Table 1) [12–14]. Therefore, the use of AAR in incident learning may support staff recovery from the psychological, physical and professional consequences of unintended patient safety events and reduce second victim harm [26, 27].

To examine the implementation and effect of AAR in the Irish healthcare context, we mapped the AAR evidence base and national-level AAR implementation strategies to Proctor et al.’s Implementation Framework [28]. Our theory of change was if organisations, after a culture and readiness assessment, agree to adopt AAR, then site selected staff can be trained as AAR facilitators and raise organisational awareness about and conduct AARs, so that the facilitation of learning after patient safety events can be done well, so that AARs take place frequently and staff feel satisfied with the meeting and outcomes, so that safety culture and second victim experience improves [14]. The Irish Safety Culture and After Action Review Experience (iCAARE) study aimed to: [1] measure safety culture and second victim experience and support before and 12 months after the introduction of AAR practice into a hospital setting [2], train selected staff as AAR facilitators using a simulation-based programme [3], explore enablers and barriers to implementation of AAR, and [4] assess how AAR was implemented and the costs of implementation in the hospital [14].

Methods

Study design

We conducted an embedded mixed-methods effectiveness-implementation study [29, 30], using a pre- and post- design with a nested qualitative component, to examine AAR over 12-months (Sept 2021 – Aug 2022) [14]. The AAR intervention period coincided with a surge in COVID-19 cases in Ireland in late 2021 and early 2022 [31]. The strain on healthcare workers and patients was exacerbated by a major cyber-attack on the Irish health care system on 14th May 2021–80% of the HSE’s Information and Communication Technology (ICT) system was affected, taking several months to restore [32]. The study was conducted in the context of these events, rather than deliberately during them. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committees of the RCSI University of Medicine & Health Sciences and study site (REC202011024) [14]. The work is reported in line with Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies (STaRI) [33]. A study timeline is available in Supplementary File 1 and detailed methods are available in the study protocol paper [14].

Intervention

The intervention was the hospital introduction of AAR practice following training of hospital self-selected staff as AAR facilitators [14].

Implementation strategy

The implementation strategy included the (a) selection of the study site and organisational readiness assessment and (b) training of staff as AAR facilitators [14].

Study site selection and organisational readiness assessment.

Study site selection and organisational readiness assessment.

Study site selection and organisational readiness assessment.

The HSE issued an expression of interest call to acute hospitals in the Irish Health Service [14]. Eligibility included hospital willingness to adopt the AAR approach, support staff in its use, and have no staff previously trained as AAR facilitators [14]. The last requirement (for an AAR ‘greenfield’ site) led to only one suitable site volunteering. This was a tertiary specialist hospital with approximately 600 staff, the identity of which is anonymous as per the research ethical approval received. The site provides specialised therapeutic services to approximately 5000 patients annually, many of whom present with complex needs, including some with high acuity and potentially life-limiting conditions. A generic breakdown of staff included clinical care (68%), operational (24%) and clinical leader/manager (13%) roles. Most of the clinical roles were health and social care professionals rather than nursing or medical roles. Roles considered as health and social care professionals, under Irish legislation, i.e. the Health and Social Care Act 2005, include clinical biochemist, dietitian, medical scientist, occupational therapist, orthoptist, physiotherapist, podiatrist, psychologist, radiographer, social care worker, social worker, speech and language therapists [34, 35]. The HSE National Quality and Patient Safety Directorate (NQPSD) conducted their standard organisational readiness assessment (Supplementary File 2) with hospital management. The assessment covers implementation guidance; facilitator eligibility criteria (e.g. good communicators, experienced); management support required; and agreement of the site Quality and Safety Lead to co-ordinate AAR [12, 14].

b.Training of staff as AAR facilitators.

Training of staff as AAR facilitators.

Based on the estimation that one in every twelve staff requires AAR facilitator training to generate a culture of AAR practice [14], 64 training places (four cohorts of 16) were made available between July and September 2021. This was up to eight times the usual number [4–8] made available by the HSE to individual sites [12].

The training, delivered by the RCSI Graduate School of Healthcare Management (TK & SEM), comprised one full-day and one half-day six weeks later [14]. On the first day, participants learned how to set up, lead and conclude an AAR using ground rules, and practised facilitating AARs with simulated healthcare professionals, receiving peer, facilitator and video feedback [14]. During the six-week interval, participants agreed to raise organisational awareness of AAR and facilitate AARs on positive events or incidents, using the AAR promotional materials provided at the training (Supplementary File 3). After six weeks, participants shared perceptions of enablers and barriers to AAR and implementation ideas [14]. These facilitator reflections are part of the standard AAR training and were not treated analytically as research data [14].

Informed consent process

Prior to the training, all participants were provided with a consent form and information leaflet to acknowledge they were volunteering to be trained as AAR facilitators and were aware of the iCAARE study [14]. In a deviation from the study plan [14], the study gatekeeper could not distribute these materials due to a health system cyber-attack. A study researcher (MF), not involved in delivering the training, provided the information package, in the reception area, before the training began [36].

The leaflets informed participants they would be invited to provide separate consent on the final training day, to be contacted about AAR implementation at six and twelve months later [14]. Participants were assured, and reminded by facilitators at the start of training, that their decision would not affect their opportunity to undergo the training [14]. All research participants provided informed consent, prior to data collection [14].

Data collection

Pre- implementation measurement of safety culture and second victim experience and support

The Hospital Survey on Patient Safety, Version 2 (HSOPS 2.0) [37, 38] and Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST) [39] were administered to all staff in May/June 2021, before delivery of AAR training [14].

HSOPS 2.0 is a 32-item Likert scale which measures ten dimensions and two outcomes of safety culture in the work area where staff spend most of their time [14] (Supplementary File 4). SVEST is a 29-item tool measuring three dimensions of second victim distress, four of existing supports and two outcome variables [39].

A hospital gatekeeper distributed paper and electronic (SurveyMonkey) surveys with follow-up reminders [14]. Surveys were set up to be anonymous to encourage staff replies and avoid socially desirable responses [14]. The survey was deliberately designed to start with HSOPS 2.0, as answering SVEST first may bias HSOPS responses [24]. Two-hundred paper surveys were distributed in receptions across the hospital site. Five days later, a health system wide cyber-attack [32] curtailed staff ability to reply electronically and additional paper surveys (n = 100) were provided.

Mid-implementation identification of enablers and barriers to AAR implementation

Six months post completion of the training (March/April 2022), AAR facilitators were invited to focus groups [14]. A topic guide based on the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) focussing on AAR implementation was developed [40]. The TDF is a comprehensive tool which contains fourteen domains known to influence behaviour. To reduce social desirability of responses, focus groups were conducted by members of the research team (MF & AW) who were not involved with the training or implementation package [36]. The focus groups were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. Data was entered into NVivo Version 12 for management and analysis [14, 36].

Post- implementation measurements of safety culture, second victim experience and support, and AAR implementation outcomes

In September 2022, post-implementation surveys were similarly administered. In addition, the survey included questions on staff awareness of and participation in AARs, i.e. number, frequency, and duration [14]. Another survey was sent to AAR facilitators to determine the number and type of AARs conducted [14]. Surveys measured AAR as defined by HSE definitions (Supplementary File 5) and AAR question construction was informed by prior research [19, 41, 42]. The proportion of reported incidents with AAR reports over the 12-months was calculated using incident data obtained from the site [14].

AAR cost estimations

Two cost figures were produced [14]. First, the HSE NQPSD cost to fund the training was calculated as the number of staff trained as facilitators by the cost per staff trained [14]. Costs incorporated the simulation venue, training facilitators, actors, recording facilities and equipment, and catering. Second, the site average cost per AAR conducted was calculated by multiplying average hourly staff pay [43] by average AAR duration and total AARs conducted (i.e. as reported in the staff and facilitator surveys 12-months after AAR implementation) [14]. The average cost calculations per AAR conducted was based on three participants, which is the minimum requirement per AAR.

Qualitative and quantitative data analysis

Qualitative [36] and quantitative data were analysed separately prior to integration [14].

Focus group transcripts were analysed using NVivo and followed an inductive and deductive approach [36]. Applications of framework analysis [44] enabled identification of codes and themes, which were aligned to the TDF, and classified as enablers, barriers, or both (i.e. an enabler/barrier) to AAR implementaton [36]. Detailed TDF-aligned enablers and barriers to AAR are reported in a prior publication [36]. The current paper summarises these enablers and barriers according to the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation - Behaviour (COM-B) model [45], a concise higher level description of the TDF.

Returned surveys were imported into Stata version 17 [46]. Respondent characteristics were described using frequencies and percentages. Unweighted averages, percentages of agreement responses (e.g. ‘strongly agree/agree’ or ‘always/most of the time’) for each item and domain were calculated. Reverse scoring of negatively-worded items on HSOPS2.0 and of positively-worded items on SVEST [25, 47] was applied. On HSOPS2.0, overall safety culture (i.e. the ‘composite (domain) measure average’), was calculated for both assessment periods. Chi-squared, Fisher’s exact and t-tests were used to examine potential differences between pre- and post- AAR implementation survey responses. Furthermore, we estimated the effect size of these differences using Cohen’s d, with 0.2 representing a small effect, 0.5 a medium effect, and 0.8 a large effect [48]. Using post- implementation staff surveys only, ordinary least squares regression was used to explore if there was any evidence of a positive association between AAR awareness and participation (one or more) and positive perceptions of safety culture, and of negative associations between AAR awareness and participation (one or more) and second victim distress and inadequate support. Due to the low response to post-implementation surveys, associations are illustrative only and further research is required. Missing values were excluded from analyses. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Additionally, in line with Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) Guidelines [49], we had planned to make comparisons between male and female responders. However, this was not feasible due to limited samples sizes.

Data integration

Key findings from each data set were mapped to the study theory of change [14], which was based on Proctor et al.’s implementation framework [28]. Claims (i.e. inferences) about implementation and effects were deduced and integrated using a triangulation protocol (i.e. a matrix of claims structured around the study aims) [50]. The triangulation of data sources including researcher reflexivity enabled assessment of the influence of confounding variables (including the ongoing pandemic and cyber-attack) on the study outcome variables. Researcher reflexivity involved debriefing and bracketing of assumptions to lessen the impact of bias [51]. Due to the nature of the pre-post design of the surveys, no statistical controls were performed in regression analyses, as we were unable to link individual responses across both times points. Roger’s theory of the diffusion of innovations was used to categorise the level of adoption of AAR at the site [52]. To enhance validity, two researchers (SEM & GV) independently assessed the claims to identify where data sets agreed (convergence), complemented (complementarity) or disagreed (discrepancy or dissonance) [50].

Results

Uptake of AAR simulation-based facilitator training

Fifty staff completed the training, representing 78% uptake of training places (n = 64) and 9% of total hospital staff (n = 586). Training participants were a mixture of clinical and non-clinical staff at executive, managerial, senior, and junior levels. Of 50 staff trained, 34 (68%) consented to be contacted about participating in the research.

HSOPS 2.0 and SVEST respondent characteristics

Survey response rates were 33% (196/586) pre-implementation and 16% (92/566) post-implementation. Of those who responded, most completed both the SVEST and HSOPS surveys (pre: 85%, post: 76%). More HSOPS (pre: n = 196, post: n = 92) than SVEST (pre: n = 166, post: n = 70) surveys were completed at both time points. Of those who had returned post-implementation surveys, a third (29/92) also returned a pre-implementation survey.

Most respondents were female and worked in clinical roles as health and social care professionals (Table 2). Post-implementation, statistically significant differences in the sample compared to pre-implementation included: fewer respondents had direct interaction with patients (pre: 73%, post: 56%; p < 0.05), worked in medical, patient care or multiple units (pre: 29%, post: 8%; p < 0.05), or engaged in nursing roles (pre: 22%, post: 7%; p < 0.05).

Focus groups with AAR facilitators

Fourteen facilitators participated in a total of four focus groups at 6-months [36]. Only four informal AARs had taken place but facilitators had adapted AAR techniques and questions, implicitly or explicitly, as part of individual reflection or group meetings [36]. There was discomfort in labelling a conversation as AAR at this stage, for fear of ‘getting it wrong’ [36]. Facilitators described more barriers than enablers for implementing AAR: seven enablers, twelve barriers and eight enablers/barriers across all 14 TDF domains [36]. Capability

Facilitators had sufficient AAR knowledge and skills. However, there was an absence of organisational co-ordination and processes to request AARs, and identify and allocate ‘willing’ facilitators [36].

Opportunity

Contextual barriers were busyness, acute staff shortages, the COVID-19 pandemic and health system cyber-attack. Facilitators also perceived a lack of visibility and staff awareness of AARs as barriers to implementation. As a positive reflection of site safety culture, senior executive awareness of staff and service pressure was perceived as preventing ‘the push’ for AAR. It was generally perceived that hospital safety culture could evolve to make AAR a norm with recognition that it would take time for values like ‘non-hierarchy’ and ‘patient involvement’ to become espoused and lived norms [36].

Motivation

Facilitators were optimistic about the benefits of AAR, but a perceived lack of top-down support exacerbated hesitation about initiating AARs, should this cause offence or go wrong. Turnover in site quality and safety leadership prior to training contributed to a sense of ‘lost opportunity’ to support AAR implementation [36].

AAR implementation at 12- months

Staff survey

After 12-months, over one third (n = 30, 37%) of respondents (n = 81) to post-implementation surveys had heard of AAR. Most (n = 21) of these had not done the AAR training. Ten per cent (n = 9) of respondents had been a participant in one or more formal/informal AARs. Seven respondents had participated in formal AARs (min = 1, max = 3) and six in informal AARs (min = 1, max = 10). Overall, this amounted to 33 experiences of AARs (11 formal and 22 informal) among nine respondents. There was no way of knowing if these were discrete AARs, or multiple staff reports of the same AAR. The average duration of AAR was 40 min (formal: 36 min, informal: 42 min). Of 9 staff who participated in AARs, a majority were satisfied with the quality of learning from AARs (n = 5), the AAR process (n = 6) and with how AAR questions were applied (n = 7).

AAR facilitator survey

After 12-months, less than five of 11 respondents to the facilitator survey indicated that they had facilitated an AAR. These related to six informal AARs. The majority of respondents (n = 9) agreed that staff perceived AAR as a suitable approach for learning from incidents and events.

AAR costs

The HSE NQPSD cost for each staff member trained was €723. The cost borne by the site per AAR was €48 (range €23-€59) and was sensitive to the number of AAR participants, their duration, and the frequency of AAR usage (Supplementary File 6).

National incident reporting

No incidents recorded by the site and reported to National Incident Management System (NIMS) over the 12-months, had associated AAR reports.

Implementation-effectiveness findings post AAR implementation

Associations between AAR implementation and safety culture and second victim experience and support are presented in Table 5. Based on the post-implementation survey, staff awareness of AAR (30 of 81 respondents) was positively associated with positive perceptions of response to error (p = 0.04). Staff participation in AARs (9 of 81 respondents participated in one or more formal/informal AARs) was associated with positive perceptions of communication about error (p < 0.01), reporting patient safety events (p = 0.02), response to error (p = 0.03) and number of events reported (p = 0.04) and negatively associated with inadequate non-work support (p < 0.01).

Discussion

iCAARE is the first study to examine AAR implementation at a hospital site and its impact on safety culture and second victim experience and support. The study demonstrated AAR had a positive relation with safety culture as shown by positive associations between staff awareness and participation in AAR and positive staff perceptions of response to error. The study results showed no evidence of an association between AAR and second victim experience.

Post- implementation, there was no change in overall safety culture which was rated positively at the outset of the study. However, evidence of improved response to error reflects WHO Guidelines that AARs support organisational learning [56]. Effectively managing patient safety events is challenging and how organisations treat staff and learn from errors is often poorly rated in safety culture surveys worldwide [53–55]. Improved response to error in our study was associated with the early adoption of AAR. Our mixed-methods findings suggest that staff awareness and participation in AAR is a valuable mechanism for promoting responses to error which are fair and focussed on learning and supporting staff. Notably, staff had rated organisational learning positively at pre-implementation, and this did not significantly improve. Rather, the improvement in response to error suggests that individual and team learning takes place in AARs, but at the early adoption stage of AAR, staff may not perceive a change in learning at an organisational level. We also found an improvement in teamwork but this was not associated with AAR. Reasons for this were unclear and illustrate the study limitation that other unmeasured factors may have influenced changes in variables. For example, a large US study of team climate pre- and during the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated that settings with maintained or improved teamwork, better preserved safety culture and staff wellbeing [57]. The results demonstrate the protective factors of quality teamworking, which may have influenced our study safety culture findings more than the AAR intervention itself. At the same time, there were worse perceptions of overall unit/area patient safety and hand-offs and information exchange than at pre-implementation. This suggests perceptions of patient safety rating may be negatively impacted when staff are more critical or open about issues; ironically, improvements in response to error may lead to staff to seeing more vulnerabilities. Together, these findings illustrate AAR holds promise but is not a panacea to all safety culture challenges and complexities.

With the exception of low professional self-efficacy, reported second victim experience did not change over the 12 months. For second victim support however, there was a worse perception of organisational support and greater turnover intentions. These changes were not associated with AAR implementation and the reasons for this were unclear but may be related to the cumulative effects of healthcare worker burnout, widely reported during the COVID-19 pandemic [58, 59] which coincided with a major cyber-attack on the Irish health system [32]. For example, parallel trends included a peak in the HSE annual turnover rate in 2022 at 10.2%, an increase on 2021 (7.7%), 2020 (6.7%) and 2019 (5.9%), with 2024 data returning to pre-pandemic levels [60, 61]. Additionally, national and international studies identified peaks in hospital healthcare work burnout over the study timeframe [62, 63].

From an implementation perspective, there was a high site uptake of the AAR facilitator training, despite being delivered during the COVID-19 pandemic. After the training, facilitators raised AAR awareness and adapted AAR principles and techniques [36]. By the end of the 12-months, there was early adoption of AAR with ten per cent of respondents (n = 9) having participated in one or more AARs at relatively low cost. Mid- and post- intervention findings, together, illustrate the progression of AAR implementation from the innovation (i.e. novel) to early adoption (i.e. emergent) stage [52]. More than double the number of informal AARs were reported as being performed compared to formal AARs (29 vs. 11) over the 12-months. This finding is to be expected as informal learning occurs more frequently in healthcare settings [64, 65].

Theoretical contributions

The study implementation findings (i.e. more frequent informal AARs than formal) make theoretical contributions to how a culture of AAR is developed in organisations concerned with high reliability. They indicate the primary importance of allowing staff a period of experimentation with the AAR methodology and opportunity to participate in informal AARs, prior to formal AARs, which require a comfort with the approach. Therefore, implementation models should include measurement of informal as well as formal implementation outcomes in patient safety contexts. Informal and formal behaviour are interdependent and are necessary for staff to develop the psychological safety and shared etiquette for participating in safety culture initiatives [64].

The study’s mixed effectiveness findings (i.e. improved response to error but no overall safety culture change, and negative second victim experience trends) reinforce the complexity of capturing outcomes in applied safety culture intervention studies [36] and the impact of context [66]. Theoretically, the findings indicate that contexts with positive safety culture may simultaneously support and constrain safety interventions, though not for contradictory reasons. In our study, the level of the site management ‘push’ for AAR, was shaped by the need to support staff and patient safety without further compromising operational efficiency at a time of extreme external pressures. Theoretical questions which arise include: during health system shocks (e.g. pandemic, cyber-attack, serious staff shortages), what should be the organisational safety culture target? Should organisations emphasise maintaining overall safety culture or focus on improvements in domains which are considered ‘controllable’ or ‘contestable’ [67], such as response to error or teamwork? Based on our study findings, these domains, in contrast to staffing and work pace, and handoffs and information exchange, appear more resilient and adaptable during extreme times. Our findings imply that organisational response to new concerns, rather than the existence of the concern themselves, is of primary importance to the maintenance of safety standards and culture during times of health system shocks.

Strengths and limitations

iCAARE utilised a research–health service partnership, three theory-informed implementation frameworks [28, 40, 45] and a well-established co-designed training programme and implementation strategy to test the implementation and effects of AAR [14]. To reduce bias in our pre- and post- measurement, we selected a study site which had no prior exposure to AAR and no findings were shared with the site while the study was ongoing. Multi-level triangulation (researcher, data source, method) occurred across data analysis and interpretation. There was a large degree of complementariness among data sets, ensuring trustworthiness of final claims.

Our real-world approach to testing of the AAR implementation strategy was a considerable research strength. It addresses calls for patient safety researchers to test interventions, in local settings, for their effectiveness, implementation and affordability [68, 69]. Even though the study was at one hospital only, single-site mixed methods case study research is known to support refinement of interventions and implementation strategies, and future studies [70]. There were several challenges, however. We trained more staff and included a wider group of staff than was usual practice in the HSE. The multi-step approach to consent as well as the sensitive nature of the topic under study is likely to have reduced the numbers of trained facilitators willing to partake in the research. However, the consent approach reflected the real-world implementation strategy which does not require trained facilitators to partake in research. We measured AAR implementation by self-report of number, duration, and quality, but did not ask about what was learnt in the process. However, we felt that more direct observation or inquiry into the contents of AARs at the site could have had intrusive impacts on how AARs were performed and been a barrier to implementation [14].

The study had several limitations. To enable a pre- and post- comparison of AAR, it was necessary to select a greenfield hospital site. Yet, the site was one of the few remaining hospitals to undergo the AAR training, mandated by national policy. This may suggest a bias towards a less extensive culture than other hospitals already trained. However, the hospital willingness to volunteer during a pandemic and allow fifty staff to be trained suggests otherwise. Additionally, the pre- and post- HSOPS confirmed a positive safety culture and comparability with international benchmarks [55] and other studies in Ireland [71]. Furthermore, the study was a single tertiary-based hospital and this may limit the generalisability of these findings to other settings. However, there was strong participation by clinical staff with patient interaction, especially in the pre-implementation survey during the cyber-attack, when clinical staff had time to complete paper-based surveys. Additionally, the post-implementation survey response was small, so the regression associations between AAR and outcome variables are exploratory only. Our sample was predominantly female, but due to small sample sizes, we were unable to examine the effects of gender on study outcomes. This limitation prevented comparison with prior studies which have identified effects of gender on safety culture [72–74] and second victim experience [75]. For example, while studies assert females are more likely to experience distress and negative professional outcomes following patient safety events [75], we have no evidence to support this or the claim of superior coping [75].

The conduct of focus groups at 6-months was helpful in understanding the process of adaptation of AAR knowledge which enabled the adoption of AARs. At the same time, the focus groups may have reminded facilitators of the study and resulted in more AARs being performed after that point. However, not all of the trained AAR facilitators participated in the research (focus groups and surveys) and therefore would not have been affected by the focus group reminder of the study.

Our survey response rates were less than average for this type of research and the timing of the study during the COVID-19 pandemic is likely to have been an influential factor [76]. While the breakdown of the job role of our survey respondents matched the breakdown in the hospital population, there was no way of knowing if those who did respond had different views to those who did not, introducing the potential for bias. However, our survey findings were typical of other safety culture analyses [55] and this is reassuring: for example, in the pre- implementation survey, teamwork was rated most positively with the trio of response to error, hand-offs and information exchange, and staffing and work pace, being rated least positively. With the exception of response to error, this pattern repeated in the post-implementation survey. As evidenced by this pattern of results, there is no evidence of social desirability in survey responses. Respondents to our pre- and post- surveys were unmatched - this may be less limiting for the HSOPS 2.0, as safety culture reflects shared values [77], whereas SVEST is likely to be influenced by individual factors [26, 78]. Our post- implementation survey response rate was lower than for the pre- implementation survey and this limited our ability to estimate the true proportion of staff who participated in AARs and the actual number of AARs which had taken place. Additionally, we had no alternative site data sources to compare to, as AARs were not recorded through the site incident report system, or National Incident Management System, over the duration of the study. Interestingly, our pre-implementation survey uptake was higher and we believe this to be directly related to the cyber-attack at the time as curtailed digital working and postponement of non-urgent care likely increased capacity for paper-based survey returns. Another limitation, due to the lack of pre- and post- matched samples, was our inability to conduct a sensitivity analysis [79] to examine the impact of confounding variables on study outcomes. Even though we applied triangulation and researcher reflexivity in our data analysis, there is the possibility that observed changes (e.g. improved response to error and teamworking, worsened handoffs and information exchange, increased turnover intentions, decreased overall patient safety rating) or lack thereof might be partially attributable to internal (e.g. staff shortages) and external (e.g. pandemic, cyber-attack) stressors rather than the AAR intervention and implementation strategy. Yet, considering the successful resolution of the cyber-attack [32] and the hospital’s move to a more stable phase of the pandemic, it is important to note that the post-surveys may have been only partially influenced by these events in terms of the staff responses collected during that period. In addition, only nine (10%) post-implementation staff survey respondents had participated in AARs and therefore the associations of survey results with AAR participation should be interpreted with caution.

Implications

Our findings highlight the potential association between staff awareness of, and participation in, AARs and enhanced safety culture, in particular, response to error. The findings confirm safety culture as complex and context dependent [80] and have implications for planning AAR across health systems in Ireland and internationally. The implementation findings are of particular transferrable value as the main challenge in the field is not what an effective patient safety intervention is, but how to encourage organisations and staff to engage with the implementation [81]. We will publish a separate paper on the implications of our findings for the HSE AAR implementation strategy. As a first principle, consideration should be given to educational activities targeting staff, who may be AAR participants, as well as AAR facilitators. Additionally, there is a need to provide enabling and sustaining environments for AAR, while maintaining their core intent - real-time shared learning and improvements following patient safety events. For example, to overcome the identified barriers (e.g. lack of organisational co-ordination, leadership turnover impact, time pressures), organisations should consider making AARs part of existing processes, e.g. AAR of audit findings, induction training.

The iCAARE study findings imply that staff awareness and participation in AAR does not address the individual needs of staff who are experiencing a second victim impact and this is consistent with evidence that fair responses to error provide insufficient second victim support [82, 83]. Therefore, AARs alone are not a direct support for second victim experience and should not be positioned as such. Second-victim experiences typically need dedicated, individualised psychological support, while AAR may reduce blame, it is not a direct psychological intervention [82, 83].

Conclusions

iCAARE tested a national-level implementation strategy and the findings are a foundation for refining implementation strategies for AAR across the health system. Our study highlighted the potential of AAR to enhance multiple, but not all, safety culture domains. The positive association between staff awareness and participation in AAR with improved staff perceptions of response to error is encouraging. The finding that staff awareness and participation in AAR were not associated with second victim distress, suggests that more targeted supports for staff involved in incidents are likely to be required in addition to organisational AAR implementation. Future studies should focus on the subjective experiences of AAR participants and the lessons learned from AAR participation.
