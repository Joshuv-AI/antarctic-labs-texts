Extreme 2016 El Niño heatwave weakened carbon export and respiration in the Equatorial Pacific
Authors: Lionel A. Arteaga, Cecile S. Rousseaux, Ivona Cetinić, Seth Bushinsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The 2016 El Niño was the strongest warm-phase of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation recorded to date over the 21st century, leading to the development of severe ocean surface temperature anomalies - marine heatwaves - across the equatorial Pacific. Here we analyze biogeochemical model output and machine learning-based reconstructions of primarily Argo oxygen and backscattering measurements to demonstrate the impact of the 2016 El Niño in weakening oceanic carbon export - the transfer of biogenic carbon from the surface ocean to depth. We show that central equatorial Pacific anomalies in modeled carbon export flux and reconstructed optical particle backscatter and ecosystem respiration display interannual variations linked to the El Niño-Southern Oscillation, with an acute reduction of 50 % in carbon export during the 2016 El Niño. This plunge in export production is attributed to a large decrease in chlorophyll biomass observed from space, and modeled ecological changes in phytoplankton community composition.

Introduction

El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is the Earth’s strongest driver of interannual climate variability1, leaving pronounced impacts on the biogeochemical conditions of the Equatorial Pacific2,3 and the global carbon budget4–6. ENSO activity is expected to be impacted by the response of the tropical climate to changes in radiative forcing7, with ongoing background ocean warming enhancing the connection between ENSO and marine heatwaves (MHWs)8–10. One of the most extreme El Niño events on record took place in 2015/2016 (referred to here as 2016 El Niño), leading to sea surface temperatures (SSTs) rising by ~3 ∘C in some areas of the Equatorial Pacific11. In many locations within the Equatorial Pacific, this temperature increase represented a rise above the climatological 99.5th SST percentile, a threshold commonly used to define MHWs9,12. Thus, the 2016 El Niño can also be considered or categorized as a marine heatwave event9,13,14. The 2016 El Niño extended southward the oceanic area under heat stress previously initiated over the Northeast Pacific (Gulf of Alaska and the California Current System) by “the Blob” heatwave in 201315,16. Multiple Pacific regions reported major environmental impacts linked to marine heat stress from the Blob and the 2016 El Niño heatwaves, such as mass sea-bird die-offs17, increased whale mortality18, fisheries collapse19, unprecedented harmful algal blooms20,21, and coral bleaching events22,23.

While the environmental consequences of MHWs have become noticeable for organisms in high-trophic levels (top predators or species with high socioeconomic importance)14,24, biogeochemical alterations induced by changes in the open ocean planktonic ecosystem affected by extreme temperature events are still poorly understood. The immediate ecological and biogeochemical implications of extreme MHWs are virtually impossible to assess due to the lack of concurrent in situ sensors. A recent biogeochemical reanalysis informed by the assimilation of space-based ocean color observations into an ocean ecological model (NASA Ocean Biogeochemical Model-NOBM) suggested major changes in phytoplankton community composition during the 2016 MHW in the Niño 3.4 region25 (5∘S–5∘N, 170∘W–120∘W, Fig. 1). This area (Niño 3.4) represents a section of the Eastern to Central Equatorial Pacific where the atmosphere tends to respond strongly to underlying variations in SST, and is actively monitored to detect and forecast ENSO cycles26,27. Satellite and model-based analyses estimated a reduction of ~40% in surface chlorophyll that was linked to a near-total collapse in diatoms25. This phytoplankton group is characterized by high growth and sinking rates that facilitate the export of organic carbon to depth28,29. Here, we advance the assessment of the impact of the El Niño 2016 MHW and ENSO cycles on the carbon cycle of the central tropical Pacific Ocean. We compute export fluxes from the ocean biogeochemical reanalysis that estimate a stark weakening of about 50% in carbon export production in the Niño 3.4 region during the 2016 El Niño. Model-based carbon export production is defined here as the vertical sinking flux of carbon out of the surface mixed layer. In conjunction with output from the data-assimilating ocean biogeochemical model (NOBM- “Methods”), we examined two additional observationally-based data sets extended to the global ocean by leveraging machine learning (ML) algorithms. The first of these products is a neural network-based merging of satellite ocean color and autonomous depth-profiling Argo float data to provide a three-dimensional gap-filled product of particle backscattering (bbp) (SOCA-BBP for Satellite Ocean-Color merged with Argo data to infer the vertical distribution of the particulate backscattering coefficient)33,34 (“Methods”). Particle backscattering co-varies with the concentration of particles in the water, making it a good proxy for in situ detection of particulate organic carbon (POC)35,36. The second product renders ocean interior oxygen fields based on Argo and ship observations of dissolved oxygen via a combination of random forest regression (RFR) and feed-forward neural networks (GOBAI-O2 for Gridded Ocean Biogeochemistry from Artificial Intelligence-Oxygen)37, used here to infer changes in subsurface respiration (“Methods”). We show that these independent products exhibit similar interannual anomalies connecting the variability in atmospheric ENSO cycles and upper ocean export production with particle backscattering and associated respiration at depth (top 200 m), pointing to an acute reduction in the inferred vertical carbon flux and respiration during the extreme 2016 El Niño.

Results and discussion

Surface anomalies during the 2016 El Niño MHW

Anomalies in modeled export, particle backscattering and inferred respiration show broadly consistent surface patterns during the period of extreme SST in the Central Equatorial Pacific associated with the 2016 El Niño (November 2015–March 2016) (Fig. 1). Within the Niño 3.4 area, the modeled vertical flux of POC out of the surface mixed layer declines (i.e., negative anomaly) with respect to the 2002–2020 climatological mean, the period corresponding to the record of assimilated satellite ocean color observations (Fig. 1a). Comparable negative anomalies are observed in the ML-based backscattering coefficient (2002–2020 climatology) and apparent oxygen utilization (AOU) (2004–2020 climatology) surface fields (top 10 m) (Fig. 1b, c). The observed decline in modeled export and reconstructed backscattering coefficient is consistent with the reported reduction in phytoplankton biomass and productivity due to plummeting macronutrient concentrations and enhanced nitrogen limitation of phytoplankton growth during the 2016 El Niño MHW25. Across the larger tropical Pacific, modeled export flux and observationally-based bbp and AOU show comparable positive anomalies in the central and Western Northern Tropical Pacific (0∘–15∘N) and southeast of the Indonesian islands. The largest disagreement between model and ML-based products (bbp and AOU) is observed in the eastern tropical Pacific and Humboldt current off the coast of Chile and Peru, where modeled carbon export increases while AOU and particle backscattering decrease. This modeled increase in export production coincides with an ecological switch in modeled phytoplankton group dominance, where chlorophytes and (to a lesser degree) cyanobacteria overtake the ecological niche left by the decline in diatoms in this region during the 2016 El Niño MHW25. While the model correctly identifies diatoms as the group most sensitive to the interannual variability in nutrients due to ENSO cycles43, it is possible that the ecological simulation overestimates the growth of chlorophytes, which are a relatively fast-growing group, to the detriment of cyanobacteria, which have a much lower growth and sinking rate, and would contribute considerably less to downward particle sinking. A larger (lower) contribution of cyanobacteria (chlorophytes) could potentially result in a negative anomaly in modeled vertical carbon export in the Eastern Equatorial Pacific, in accordance to the pattern observed in bbp and AOU.

Interannual variability in export and respiration in the tropical Pacific

A sixteen year (2004–2020) time series of modeled carbon export out of the surface mixed layer averaged for the Niño 3.4 area shows an interannual range of about 120 mg C m−2 d−1 (from ~60 to 180 mg C m−2 d−1), with the lowest flux in late 2015/early 2016 (Fig. 2a). The corresponding time series of deseasonalized anomalies shows that the interannual variability in export production is highly (inversely) correlated with ENSO cycles (R = −0.8): Phases of La Niña, defined by negative (cooler) SST anomalies below −0.5 ∘C in the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), concur with high export flux, while warm SST anomalies (above +0.5 ∘C) during El Niño phases coincide with low (negative) export anomalies (Fig. 2b). The Central Equatorial Pacific exhibits exceptionally high interannual variability in particle export44, making it a unique region to assess the impact of strengthening ENSO cycles on marine biogeochemical fluxes.

Throughout the water column, the backscattering coefficient is highest in the upper 70 m, where sufficient light and enough nutrients enable high productivity of planktonic organic particles (phytoplankton, zooplankton, and detritus), one of the main drivers of variations in bbp (Fig. 2c) (see assessment of regional bbp estimates in Figs. S6–S8). The concentration of these organic particles decreases with depth, particularly below 100 m, as net primary productivity (NPP) declines with diminishing light, and particles are consumed and remineralized by heterotrophs as they sink. Interannual changes in bbp within the upper 300 m are difficult to detect, except for the low export period in 2016 (Fig. 2c). However, the depth-resolved time series of deseasonalized bbp anomalies in Niño 3.4 shows too a clear connection with ENSO and export cycles. Here, higher bbp within the upper euphotic depth (Zeu) (depth where light is 1% of the surface irradiance) suggests higher POC concentration35,36 and concurs with high export rates and La Niña events. Likewise, low bbp is observed during low export and El Niño periods, with the most negative and prolonged anomalies taking place in early 2016 (Fig. 2d). Below the mean Zeu (~86 m) and above roughly 120 m, bbp anomalies are anticorrelated with the surface (above Zeu), suggesting a subtle subsurface increase in biomass during El Niño, and vice versa during La Niña events. This subsurface pattern results from low productivity near the surface relaxing light attenuation and limitation at depth, and allowing increased subsurface productivity and particle-induced bbp during El Niño events relative to La Niña episodes (Fig. S2). Below 150 m, anomalies in bbp resemble those within the upper Zeu, likely reflecting the impact of the highest bulk productive region near the surface on the sinking of particles to depth. The correspondence in interannual anomalies between modeled export and water column bbp connects the impact and magnitude of MHWs in lowering upper ocean productivity (within Zeu) in the Equatorial Pacific with a decrease in the vertical distribution and concentration of particles at depth.

While the ocean biogeochemical model and SOCA-BBP are independent research products, they are both informed (in different ways) by satellite ocean color retrievals (model assimilation45 vs. ML input33,34, see “Methods”). However, our estimates of AOU are trained on dissolved oxygen measurements from Argo floats and ship surveys, and are agnostic to satellite or any other bio-optical information37. AOU approximates zero near the surface where the upper ocean is in direct contact with the atmosphere and increases with depth as organisms consume oxygen for the respiration of organic matter (Fig. 2e). A time series of depth-resolved AOU averaged within Niño 3.4 shows little discernible interannual variability within the upper 300 m (Fig. 2e), but deseasonalized anomalies unveil interannual fluctuations that coincide with the biogeochemical variables examined above (export flux and bbp) (Fig. 2f). Increased POC production and export during nutrient rich La Niña events lead to higher oxygen utilization throughout the upper 200 m of the water column as more organic matter is available for remineralization. Conversely, phases of El Niño where the upwelling and westward advection of nutrient-rich deep water from the Eastern Equatorial Pacific are suppressed46 result in lower POC production/export and reduced respiration. AOU anomalies are mostly constrained below the surface mixed layer47, where dissolved oxygen is prevented from immediate exchange with the atmosphere and biologically-induced changes are more discernable. The lowest (most negative) AOU anomaly period occurs between 2015 and 2016 as a consequence of the stark export decrease due to the El Niño MHW (Fig. 2f). The second strongest El Niño within this record was in 2010 (Fig. 2d, f), with a clear decrease in AOU and bbp, albeit less intense than in 2016. A third period of less acute but distinct negative anomalies is observed below 200 m between 2004 and 2007. During this period, the region between 100 and 200 m still shows an increase in AOU in 2006, also present in bbp, which is attributed to the increase in productivity and export from the 2006 La Niña11. The variability in AOU is ultimately driven by changes in dissolved oxygen, which is affected by both biological and physical processes. The strong agreement in bbp and AOU anomalies throughout the 16-year time series suggest that changes in AOU associated with particle flux occur predominantly in the 100–200 m range within Niño 3.4, while physical transport and mixing likely play a larger role relative to biology at greater depths, dimming the respiration signal.

It is important to note that the Equatorial Pacific is one of the regions with the highest uncertainty in the ML-based oxygen product37. This product is also likely to be less well constrained early in the record (near 2004), when there were fewer float oxygen measurements within this region and globally. While no float-based oxygen measurements were found for the earlier part of the time series (2004–2007) within Niño 3.4, we compared ML-based oxygen profiles against BGC-Argo data obtained between 2018 and 2024 (Figs. S9–S11). This comparison shows good agreement in the vertical distribution of dissolved oxygen for the upper 200 m of the water column between the neural network product and the floats. The ML-based product has also been used to assess the impact of the 2019 MHW on oxygen anomalies in the Northeast Pacific48.

The setting mechanism for El Niño can be summarized by an initial weakening in trade winds that prompts a reduction in upwelling in the eastern Pacific, reinforcing warm SST anomalies over the Equatorial Pacific through the Bjerknes feedback loop49. The matching interannual variability in water column bbp and AOU connect mesopelagic processes with changes in export production and atmospheric forcings. The interannual variation in export flux out of the mixed layer and bbp is most pronounced for backscattering anomalies near the surface, and both variables show high linear correlation in monthly anomalies (R = 0.82) (bbp averaged in the top 50 m) (Fig. 3). The variation in AOU is most noticeable in the 100–200 m range, where the respiration signal dominates over photosynthetic oxygen production and exchanges with the atmosphere occurring at the surface (Fig. 3). For this depth range, the linear correlation in monthly anomalies between AOU and mixed layer export is of (R = 0.53) (Fig. 3). The relative anomaly in AOU ranges approximately between ± 30%, with the highest point (~+35%) observed during the strong 2011 La Niña and a similar but opposite collapse of 30% during the 2016 El Niño. Export has similar maxima as AOU, but shows a larger decline of nearly 50% in 2016. Backscattering shows reduced magnitude but similar timing in its interannual maximum in 2011 (~+14%) and minimum in 2016 (~−20%).

The regional uncertainty in the ML-based products for the Niño 3.4 region was found to be relatively low, with 13.6% for bbp and 7.4% for oxygen (Supplementary discussion: Evaluation of ML Products Within the Equatorial Pacific). The modeled export flux shows a globally-integrated average (8.68 Pg C yr−1) close to the mean from observationally-based estimates (~8 Pg C yr−1), and a global pattern (Fig. S4) consistent with that shown in a recent recap of ocean biogeochemical models50 (Supplementary discussion: biogeochemical model evaluation). The correlation between modeled and individual matching flux observations is modest (R = 0.4) and somewhat dependent on the sampling method (Fig. S3), a common challenge for ocean biogeochemical models50,51. The output on phytoplankton groups (i.e., phytoplankton functional types (PFTs)) associated with this simulation has been recently assessed25, showing good skill in the representation of diatoms within Niño 3.4 and globally. Inferred changes in export, particle backscatter, and respiration are associated with variations in phytoplankton community composition, and the impact of large phytoplankton (diatoms) in driving export production in this region43,52.

Community composition impact on export

POC is considered to be the dominant contributor of biological carbon export and sequestration, which can also include other processes, such as zooplankton vertical migration and downward mixing of dissolved organic carbon 53,54. Living phytoplankton may sink to depth, but the estimated primary sinking pathway is the settling of detritus particles, which includes dead and fecal material from phytoplankton and zooplankton (Fig. 4a). The grazing diet of zooplankton is a major determinant of the detritus composition in the ecosystem model, where grazing is applied to the individual phytoplankton groups proportionately to their relative abundances55. ENSO phases corresponding to La Niña show marked differences in grazing and detritus composition with respect to El Niño events (Fig. 4b–e). The main model variations are observed in the relative contribution of diatoms and cyanobacteria to the total phytoplankton biomass grazed. In principle, the mineral composition of detritus is expected to affect the sinking speed of the associated organic carbon56,57. Our biogeochemical model does not account for this ballasting effect in detritus carbon, but it does reflect changes in community composition and diatom dominance associated with periods of high and low export production in the central tropical Pacific43,52. During the two strongest La Niña events of the 21st century to date, 2008 and 2011, the anomaly in export production was positive and had a maximum interannual deviation of >(+) 40 mg C m−2 d−1 in 2011 (Fig. 4a). For these two events, diatoms had a relatively high carbon contribution to the total phytoplankton biomass grazed of 52% in 2008 (Fig. 4b), and 32% in 2011 (Fig. 4d), while cyanobacteria represented a very small fraction of 1.6% (2008, Fig. 4b) and 2% (2011, Fig. 4d). In contrast, the two strongest El Niño to date, 2010 and 2016, triggered low export anomalies, with the largest interannual decline being lower than (−) 50 mg C m−2 d−1 in 2016 (Fig. 4a). In 2010, the grazed diatom fraction was 15%, considerably lower than during La Niña phase and closer to the grazed cyanobacteria fraction of 9% (Fig. 4c). During the 2016 MHW, grazing of diatoms declined drastically to only 3% of the total phytoplankton consumed by zooplankton, while cyanobacteria (and chlorophytes) showed a contribution of 13% (67%), relatively high with respect to the other acute ENSO events. While the mineral ballasting effect is not represented in the modeled detritus carbon flux, these swings in grazed composition may impact the quality of detritus and its transfer to depth. A higher contribution of diatoms signifies fast-growing and highly productive cells. In addition, their silica frustule has been proposed to enhance particle density and/or physically protect organic matter from degradation, facilitating the vertical transport of organic material56,57,62,63.

Despite the intensity and duration of recent MHWs in the Pacific Ocean, large-scale changes in phytoplankton community composition are not easily induced in ecological simulations25. The Equatorial Pacific is a notable except, where stark changes in bottom-up environmental conditions caused by the 2016 El Niño heatwave led to acute reductions in chlorophyll biomass and alterations in community composition25. These biological changes inferred from combined model and satellite analyses are here linked to declines in surface (mixed layer) export production and mesopelagic respiration, supported by evidence from observationally-based gap-filled products derived primarily from Argo floats33,34,37. Shifts in plankton regimes and carbon sequestration in the Equatorial Pacific have been suggested as amplifiers of the end-Permian mass extinction event triggered by intensified mega El Niño episodes64, partly based on the replacement of dominant eukaryotic plankton groups (e.g., diatoms) in favor of cyanobacteria and other groups of lower primary productivity65. Such mechanism is an amplified version of the interannual variability in equatorial export and productivity described here. Under transient mean global and equatorial warming, extreme El Niño events (such as in 2016) are expected to become more frequent in the near future66. This potential intensification in ENSO oscillations warrants close monitoring of variations in equatorial marine productivity, its planktonic composition, and resulting carbon and connected biogeochemical fluxes (e.g., nutrients and oxygen).

ENSO and global export

Many of the most extreme MHWs in recent decades (1980s–2010s) were associated with El Niño periods, both in the tropical Pacific and beyond10. ENSO appears to be a strong modulator of MHWs in both of it distinct phases, with El Niño leading to extreme warm anomalies in the Central-to-Eastern Equatorial Pacific and Indian Ocean, while La Niña is associated with strong MHWs in the subtropical South Pacific and extended regions of the temperate north and South Atlantic10. We investigate whether ENSO can be linked to the variability in export production outside the Central Equatorial Pacific, as for MHWs. In relation to export, ENSO cycles (summarized by Niño 3.4 SSTs in the ONI measure) are strongly negatively correlated (R < −0.5) with modeled local carbon export fluxes and reconstructed bbp in the Central Equatorial Pacific (Fig. 5a, b). Particle backscattering also shows a strong negative correlation with ONI in the eastern tropical Pacific, while modeled carbon export shows a positive relationship with ONI in the eastern part of the basin, which might be related to the overestimated growth of chlorophytes over cyanobacteria during strong El Niño episodes and its impact on particle sinking and carbon flux in the model. Local subsurface respiration (AOU) (100–200 m) has a marked shift from strong negative correlations (R < −0.5) with ONI in the Central-to-Eastern Equatorial Pacific to strong positive correlations (R > 0.5) in the Central-to-Western Equatorial Pacific (Fig. 5c). Overall, the inverse relationship between export variables and ONI in the Central-to-Eastern Equatorial Pacific is expected from the mechanistic connection between low export production and the warm ENSO phase, but might be accentuated by the observed concurrence of MHWs during strong El Niño events in this region. The positive relationship between export variables and ONI in the Central-to-Western Equatorial Pacific agrees with the increase in the vertical flux of biogenic material (carbonate, opal, and organic matter, including amino acids) obtained from sediment traps deployed across the Western Pacific warm pool during El Niño conditions between 1991 and 199967. Differences in the inferred response of carbon export to ENSO cycles between the eastern versus western basin of the Tropical Pacific agree with fluctuations in satellite-based NPP. Outside the tropical Pacific, the correlation between ENSO cycles and export variables (modeled particle flux, Argo-based bbp and respiration) weakens, but displays a few shared patterns (Fig. 5): the subtropical South Pacific emerges as a region of positive correlation between ONI SST and export/respiration (high export during El Niño and vice versa). This region also shows a positive correlation between ENSO and MHWs10, suggesting that cold tropical SST anomalies might trigger physical/biogeochemical mechanisms that lead to local high SST extremes and lower subtropical carbon export. MHWs highlight episodes of high and prolonged in situ SST but are not constrained to a single physical driving mechanism9,69. Moreover, ecosystem responses to extreme events can vary within the same geographical area70. Our results, in conjunction with the description of changes in PFTs25, outline a mechanistic framework driving changes in export production in connection with ENSO cycles and MHWs within Niño 3.4 in the Central Equatorial Pacific. However, further study is required to understand the connection between MHWs and carbon export across the global ocean.

Connection to the wider carbon cycle

The net impact of the 2016 El Niño on the global carbon budget was a record increase in the annual growth rate of atmospheric CO2 (~3 ppm year−1)71,72, surpassed only by the recent estimate for 2023 (~3.3 ppm year−1)73. The 2016 event had counteracting roles between the land and the oceanic carbon cycle. An initial reduction in oceanic outgassing due to suppressed equatorial upwelling of carbon-rich deep water was followed by a decrease in terrestrial biospheric uptake and enhanced emissions from biomass burning over Southeast Asia and Indonesia74. While changes in the physical transport of deep dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) to the surface dominate the response of the Equatorial Pacific’s carbon cycle to ENSO cycles75, our results confirm that the suppression of nutrient-rich deep-water upwelling also severely weakens biological carbon export during extreme El Niño episodes, such as in 2016. This decline in biological export further prevents this region from becoming a net carbon sink, even during periods of reduced upward carbon flux. Recent evidence suggests an unexpected decline in the nonpolar global ocean carbon sink in 2023, a strong El Niño year76. While the 2023 reduction in uptake was not driven by the Equatorial Pacific, it suggests a weakening in the role of nonthermal processes (including circulation, mixing, and biologically-mediated export) that compensate thermal outgassing by removing DIC from the surface ocean and prevent its eventual leak to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide.

Conclusions

We present evidence of the negative impact of the 2016 Niño marine heatwave on biological carbon export production in the Central Equatorial Pacific. Our evidence combines POC fluxes from a biogeochemical model guided by the assimilation of satellite ocean color and reconstructions of particle backscatter and oxygen utilization in the ocean interior. These reconstructions employ nonlinear ML algorithms to fill observational gaps in profiling data obtained from (primarily) BGC-Argo floats and high-quality ship measurements. Previous work has highlighted the relationship between ENSO cycles and surface ocean biology, primarily constrained to changes in surface chlorophyll and primary productivity2,68,77, with regionally restricted evidence from sediment traps67. Here, we extend beyond the surface and show the impact of ENSO cycles on optical particle backscatter (correlated with plankton particle concentration) in the upper 100 m, and on oxygen dynamics primarily controlled by respiration below the upper mixed layer, in the mesopelagic zone (100–200 m). The three main biogeochemical variables assessed here, POC flux, bbp, and AOU, show a consistent pattern of decline during El Niño events, with the largest decline in early 2016, concurrent with the highest SSTs in the record, driven by the extreme El Niño episode, categorized as a MHW9,13,14. These findings highlight the potential for even larger reductions in export production under yet more severe future anomalous warm conditions. The clear interannual agreement between these three variables (POC flux, bbp, and AOU), derived from largely independent information sources (biogeochemical model, SOCA-BBP, GOBAI-O2), suggests that the reported fluctuations in export production in the Central Equatorial Pacific, and their mechanistic connection to ENSO and MHWs, are robust (see Supplementary discussion on biogeochemical model evaluation and evaluation of ML products within the Equatorial Pacific).

The second main result of our study highlights the role of phytoplankton community composition in regulating carbon export, and the sensitivity of phytoplankton ecology to ENSO fluctuations and MHWs in the Equatorial Pacific. Our model-based results point to a strong role of diatoms as primary producers that enhance carbon export, evidenced by their large contribution to the detritus flux and dominance of the phytoplankton biomass composition25 during high export regimes characterized by La Niña. During El Niño events, the biomass share of diatoms is estimated to substantially decrease in favor of slower-growing groups, such as chlorophytes and cyanobacteria. The 2016 MHW represented a unique episode in which the model showed a distinctive, strong response, nearly eliminating diatoms from the Central Equatorial Pacific25. While the ecological output of the model has been validated against historical observations25, there is larger uncertainty in this result than in our export estimates, due to the lack of phytoplankton community measurements concurrent with extreme events, such as the 2016 El Niño. Other regions of the Northeastern Pacific Ocean also affected by extreme heat did not show clear changes in the modeled phytoplankton community, and even in the Equatorial Pacific, the community was rapidly restored after a few months, a feature also observed in other models evaluating ENSO ecosystem impacts78. Nonetheless, the pronounced ecological shifts simulated during the 2016 Equatorial Pacific heatwave coincide with a drastic decrease of 40% in surface chlorophyll, corroborated by remote sensing25, and the large decline in export-related variables discussed above. Our results corroborate the need for in situ measurements and/or alternative observations of oceanic plankton ecology to validate ecological modeling output and assess the use of these simulations in detecting ecological tipping points. In the absence of contemporary large-scale field observational programs, NASA’s plankton, aerosol, cloud, ocean ecosystem (PACE) mission (launched in 2024) is perhaps the most promising resource currently available to deliver phytoplankton community composition products that may allow a more direct evaluation of the ecological component in ocean biogeochemical models.

Methods

Modeled carbon export

Estimates of export production were obtained from the NOBM. The NOBM is a coupled ocean-biogeochemical model that provides a three-dimensional representation of marine biogeochemical processes, guided by the assimilation of satellite ocean color at the surface layer. This version of the NOBM has a near-global domain spanning from 84∘S to 72∘N at a spatial resolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\frac{1}{4}}^{\circ }$$\end{document}14∘ longitude and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\frac{2}{3}}^{\circ }$$\end{document}23∘ latitude, and restricted to waters deeper than 200 m. The coupled circulation model is Poseidon79, a reduced gravity model with 14 vertical layers in quasi-isopycnal coordinates. Meteorological forcing is provided by the Modern-Era Retrospective analysis for Research and Applications version 2 (MERRA-2)80,81, which includes wind speed and stress, sea surface temperature, shortwave radiation, relative humidity, sea level pressure, sea ice fraction, cloud fraction, and optical thickness. The model assimilates satellite ocean color retrievals of total surface chlorophyll, particulate inorganic carbon (PIC), and absorption by colored dissolved organic matter. The assimilation is conducted by minimizing the difference between the Laplacian of the modeled surface fields and that of the satellite observations using the conditional relaxation analysis method82,83. Optical water properties are derived by the ocean-atmosphere spectral irradiance model (OASIM)45. OASIM simulates the propagation of downward spectral irradiance in the atmosphere and ocean, and ultimately defines the level of photosynthetically available radiation (PAR) for phytoplankton growth. Access to NOBM software can be found at https://gmao.gsfc.nasa.gov/reanalysis/MERRA-NOBM/software, and access to the biogeochemical reanalysis data can be found at https://gmao.gsfc.nasa.gov/reanalysis/MERRA-NOBM/data.

Export production in this study is quantified by the sinking flux of POC. Gravitational particle sinking is not the only mechanism that transports biogenic carbon to depth, but it is considered to be the most dominant one, and it is virtually incorporated in all global ocean biogeochemical models50,54. Total POC in the model is composed by the phytoplankton carbon biomass of six PFTs (i) (described in the next section) in addition to detritus. Biogeochemical function of PFTs

The PFTs represented in the model were included to cover a spectrum of phytoplankton growth rates, nutrient requirements, and export rates observed in the global ocean (Table S2)90,91: Diatoms represent a group of fast-growing phytoplankton with demand for high nutrient concentrations and characterized by the formation of an outer silica shell that enhances their sinking towards greater depths. Cyanobacteria represent a functional opposite, with slow division and sinking rates, and low nutrient requirements. Coccolithophores depict an intermediate group with moderate nutrient demands and growth rates, relatively low light requirements, and relatively high sinking rates due to their calcium carbonate shell (coccoliths). Chlorophytes encompass a wide range of characteristics associated with nanoplankton, with intermediate growth and sinking rates, as well as nutrient requirements. Dinoflagellates are similar to diatoms in their fast growth and high nutrient demands, but are distinguished by very high light requirements and negligible sinking rates. Finally, Phaeocystis are also characterized by relatively high light requirements and sinking rates, but present slow division rates and low nutrient requirements. Changes in the overall assemblage of the planktonic community are expected to regulate the intensity and efficiency of biogenic carbon export (referred to as the biological carbon pump)92,93.

Biogeochemical output from the NOBM has been extensively assessed against observationally- and satellite-based products55,90,94,95. PFT output from the simulation analyzed in this study was recently evaluated25.

Model setup

The model run analyzed here was configured to simulate the same changes in phytoplankton community composition as in Arteaga et al. and Rousseaux et al.25, with the additional model output of the light-dependent chlorophyll-to-carbon (Chl:C) ratio, which facilitates the offline computation of carbon export fluxes. The model was initially spun up in free-run mode for 200 years using climatological forcing from MERRA80. The initial state for nitrate and silicate is taken from the World Ocean Atlas96, dissolved iron is set from an ocean budget analysis97, and ammonium is arbitrarily set to 0.5 μM. Phytoplankton concentrations are initialized to 0.5 mg Chl m−3. Starting in 1980, the model is forced with monthly MERRA-2 data. The model begins assimilating satellite ocean color data from SeaWiFS in January 1998. Output from this last step is used to initialize the assimilation of MODIS ocean color retrievals in July 2002. Daily output from the SeaWiFS-assimilated run between January and June 2002 and from the MODIS-assimilated run between July 2002 and December 2020 is averaged monthly and analyzed in this study. Anomalies in export production were computed with respect to the climatology of the entire NOBM run (2002–2020).

Neural network-based ocean particle backscattering

Particle backscatter (bbp) was obtained from the “satellite ocean-color merged with Argo data to infer the vertical distribution of the particulate backscattering coefficient” (SOCA-BBP) product33,34. The SOCA-BBP product provides depth-resolved bbp at 700 nm (bbp(700)) at a spatial resolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\frac{1}{4}}^{\circ }$$\end{document}14∘ x \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\frac{1}{4}}^{\circ }$$\end{document}14∘ for the near-global domain between 82∘S and 90∘N. Profiles of bbp(700) have a vertical resolution of 5 m between surface and 70 m, 10 m between 70 and 100 m, 25 m between 100 and 300 m, 50 m between 300 and 600 m, and 100 m between 600 and 1000 m (36 vertical levels in total). SOCA-BBP is archived as weekly gap-filled 3D data composites for the near-global domain, from which we analyzed the period between January 2002 and December 2020. The original SOCA-BBP product33 has been updated to improve accuracy in bbp (as well as POC and chlorophyll) estimates34. The initial neural network applied was a multi-layer perceptron (MLPs)98,99 with the final optimized configuration consisting of two hidden layers with eight and six neurons, respectively, in addition to the input and output layer. This architecture was updated to improve the performance of the neural network by using an ensemble of MLPs34. The updated algorithm infers depth-resolved profiles of bbp from matching PAR and remote sensing reflectance (Rrs) at five wavelengths (412, 443, 490, 555, and 670 nm) derived from the Copernicus-GlobColour product (developed by the E.U. Copernicus Marine Service Information; 10.48670/moi-00280), which is informed by multiple satellite sensors from NASA and the European Space Agency. Rrs replace previously used satellite-based observations of surface chlorophyll and bbp from MODIS-Aqua to avoid inherent errors in ocean color algorithms34. Altimeter satellite gridded sea level anomalies (E.U. Copernicus Marine Service Information; 10.48670/moi-00148) are now included to account for mesoscale processes that can impact the vertical distribution of phytoplankton biomass100. The vertical structure in bbp and mixed layer depth (MLD) is inferred from high-resolution 3-D temperature and salinity fields derived from in situ and satellite observations101 (E.U. Copernicus Marine Service Information; 10.48670/moi-00052). Location and the day of the year complete the list of input variables into the SOCA algorithm (Table S2). The algorithm was trained with BGC-Argo floats equipped with temperature, salinity, and bbp sensors at 700 and 532 nm. However, the initial float array assessed included 10 times more bbp(700) profiles than bbp(532), so all profiles were harmonized to bbp(700)33. We used the latest SOCA-BBP product34 (updated in 2024, 10.48670/moi-00046), which exhibited a global error of 11% in depth-resolved bbp estimates when compared against a 20% of the compiled BGC-Argo database not used for training the algorithm. This represents an improvement with respect to the 21% error estimated for the original bbp product33. A secondary validation against four independent floats from diverse environmental regions (Mediterranean Sea, North Atlantic Subpolar Gyre, North Atlantic Subtropical Gyre, and Southern Ocean) not included in neither the primary validation or training data sets showed a similar improvement and error reduction from 22% in the original product33 to 12% in the 2024 output34.

Neural network-based dissolved oxygen and derived AOU

Dissolved oxygen was obtained from the gridded ocean biogeochemistry from artificial intelligence-oxygen (GOBAI-O2) product37. Depth-resolved oxygen concentrations are provided in a 1∘ × 1∘ grid between 64∘S and 80∘N, with a vertical pressure resolution of 10 dbar between surface and 200 dbar, 20 dbar between 200 and 500 dbar, 50 dbar between 500 and 1500 dbar, and 100 dbar between 1500 and 1900 dbar. Three-dimensional oxygen fields are available monthly between 2004 and 2022. Anomalies computed in this study based on GOBAI-O2 data are for the period between 2004 and 2020. The gap-filled oxygen product represents the ensemble mean of three feed-forward neural networks (FNNs)102 and a RFR103. Algorithms were trained using a combined oxygen data set of float BGC-Argo and discrete Winkler titration data from the Global Ocean Data Analysis Project (GLODAP)104. A total of 11 algorithm predictors (Table S2) were derived from the latest update (as of January 2023) of Argo climatological temperature and salinity profiles105. Each FNN was comprised of two hidden layers with a combination of neurons in the first and second layer of (FNN1) 20 and 10, (FNN2) 15 and 15, and (FNN3) 10 and 20. The RFR consisted of 600 trees, a minimum leaf size of 10, and 6 of the 11 predictors used for each decision split. Each ML algorithm (FNNs and RFR) was trained on specific ocean basins chosen to represent distinct biogeographical features106. For the near-global domain, the mean difference between predicted and observed oxygen (based on data not used for training the ML algorithms) was −0.2 μmol kg−1, while the rooted-mean-square-difference (RMSD) was 8.8 μmol kg−1.

AOU (μmol kg−1) is calculated as the difference between saturated and observed oxygen using the MATLAB script made available by the Monterrey Bay Aquarium Research Institute at http://www3.mbari.org/Products/Matlab_shell_scripts/matlab/aou.m. Light propagation

PAR at the ocean surface (E m−2 d−1) (8-day average, period 2003–2020) based on MODIS-Aqua retrievals was obtained for the Niño 3.4 region (5∘S–5∘N, 170∘W–120∘W) from the NASA GSFC Ocean Color Web site (https://oceancolor.gsfc.nasa.gov). Depth-resolved PAR was computed based on the attenuation of surface PAR by chlorophyll. Mixed layer depth (MLD)

The thickness of the topmost layer in the quasi-isopycnal circulation model79 defines the depth the surface turbulent well-mixed layer, from which modeled carbon export is computed. A data-based MLD was also estimated from GOBAI-O2 temperature and salinity profiles47. Both monthly based MLD estimates agree well seasonally and interannually between 2004 and 2020 (coefficient of determination, R2 = 0.56), with GOBAI-O2 data yielding deeper MLD than the circulation model (bias of 11 m), and a root-mean-square-difference (RMSD) of 13 m.
