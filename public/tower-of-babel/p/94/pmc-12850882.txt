Endoscopic full-thickness resection as a cure for recurrent duodenal adenoma
Authors: Grace E Kim, Uzma D Siddiqui
Published: 2023

Duodenal polyps are rare, with a reported incidence rate of <5%; however, they carry high malignant potential up to 85%, and resection is advised, especially when the polyps have high-risk features such as high-grade dysplasia and size >20 mm.1,2 They are also at a higher risk for advanced colorectal adenomas and neoplasia (odds ratio of 7.8 and 3.6, respectively); thus, colonoscopy is also warranted.3 Duodenal polyps have high recurrence rates (as high as 37%); active surveillance is therefore advised,4 but subsequent polyp management may be hindered due to previous manipulations and resection attempts causing fibrosis.5 Here we describe a unique approach to managing recurrent, scarred duodenal adenomas via endoscopic full-thickness resection (EFTR).

Case description

A 43-year-old man with Hodgkin lymphoma in remission had incidental duodenal adenoma discovered on EGD performed for right upper quadrant pain. Results of colonoscopy, capsule endoscopy, and genetic testing were negative for any polyposis syndromes. Located just distal to the ampulla on the opposite wall in the second portion, the duodenal polyp was originally resected at an outside hospital but was referred to our center due to residual polyp found on surveillance examination (Fig. 1). Our initial examination revealed an approximately 15 mm residual polyp with significant scarring that could only be partially removed with EMR (Fig. 2). Pathology showed duodenal adenoma with high-grade dysplasia. Given significant scarring and concerning pathology, the decision was made to repeat the EGD with EFTR. Argon plasma coagulation was first used to demarcate the lesion borders (Fig. 3), and a successful EFTR was performed via therapeutic gastroscope using a Food and Drug Administration–approved EFTR device (full-thickness resection device; FTRD Colonic, Ovesco, Tuebingen, Germany) and FTRD graspers (Ovesco) to pull the tissue into the cap (Fig. 4). The electrocautery setting used was high cut mode, effect-4 at 200 W (ERBE USA, Marietta, Ga, USA). Final pathology showed a residual duodenal adenoma with focal areas of high-grade dysplasia and erosion, completely excised with negative margins (Fig. 5). Over the next 3 years, results of 2 subsequent surveillance EGDs were negative for recurrence (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Traditionally, EFTR was limited to colonic lesions with submucosal invasion and those with non-lifting sign, but with the advent of FTRD as a one-step all-inclusive method, EFTR has become an increasingly more versatile tool in the management of both upper and lower GI tract lesions ranging from superficial mucosal lesions to malignant lesions.6, 7, 8 At the time of this case, only the colonic FTRD was available, but now there are diagnostic FTRDs and gastroduodenal FTRDs. One recent systematic review and a meta-analysis evaluating 140 patients with 142 upper GI lesions (57 duodenal lesions) undergoing EFTR reported a success rate of 86.9% and an adverse event rate of 18.6%.6 These adverse events included bleeding and perforations as well as a mucosal damage from FTRD. Duodenal EMRs can be especially technically challenging due to the duodenum’s vascularity, thin wall, and mobility, increasing risks of bleeding and perforation.9 EMR anywhere in the GI tract can be especially difficult if there is underlying fibrosis from prior resections that prevents lifting of the submucosal layer. This case highlights that in such incidences, EFTR can be used to facilitate endoscopic removal of challenging scarred lesions and those that are potentially harboring malignancy by achieving deeper resection, including the muscularis propria.10 Limitations include difficulty navigating the device on the scope through the GI tract. This case shows the feasibility of EFTR and its success in curing a recurrent, scarred duodenal adenoma.
