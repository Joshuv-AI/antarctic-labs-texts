Patriarchal norms and reproductive vulnerability: association between son preference and high-risk fertility behaviour in India

Introduction

High-risk fertility behaviour (HRFB) defined as early childbearing (before age 18), late childbearing (age 35 or older), high parity (four or more live births), and short birth intervals (less than 24 months) remains a critical bio-demographic driver of maternal and child morbidity and mortality worldwide [1, 2]. These reproductive patterns substantially compromise global maternal health targets by elevating risks of obstetric complications, adverse perinatal outcomes, and infant mortality [1, 3]. Although India has achieved a remarkable decline in its total fertility rate (TFR), from 3.4 in 1992–93 to 2.0 in 2019–21, the persistence of HRFB continues to concentrate reproductive risk among vulnerable subpopulations [4]. Despite a national reduction in any HRFB from 51.3% in NFHS-1 (1992–93) to 29.4% in NFHS-5 (2019–21), and a decline in multiple concurrent risks from 12.7% to 5.3% over the same period, substantial geographic and socioeconomic disparities remain [4, 5]. This enduring burden highlights a critical disconnect between aggregate fertility decline and equitable reproductive health outcomes.

The health consequences of HRFB are well-documented across its constituent dimensions. Early and late maternal age at childbirth are strongly associated with elevated risks of hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes, placental complications, obstetric interventions, and adverse neonatal outcomes, including preterm birth and chromosomal abnormalities [6–8]. High parity exacerbates maternal physiological depletion, increasing susceptibility to severe anaemia, uterine atony, postpartum haemorrhage, and maternal mortality, while concurrently elevating risks of low birth weight, intrauterine growth restriction, and infant mortality among offspring [9–11]. Similarly, short birth intervals impair maternal nutritional recovery and heighten the likelihood of maternal depletion and obstetric complications, while compromising child survival, immune development, and cognitive outcomes [12–14]. Collectively, these overlapping risks underscore HRFB as a critical, yet modifiable, determinant of intergenerational health inequity.

Beyond demographic and clinical determinants, entrenched sociocultural norms—particularly son preference—play a pivotal role in sustaining HRFB. In India, son preference is deeply embedded within traditional kinship structures, where daughters are normatively expected to migrate to their husbands’ households upon marriage, while sons remain responsible for old-age support, lineage continuity, and ritual obligations [15–18]. This structural reality, compounded by inadequate formal social security systems, intensifies the instrumental valuation of sons and transforms gender bias into a reproductive imperative [19, 20]. Consequently, families frequently engage in compensatory fertility, continuing childbearing until a male heir is born [21, 22]. This behaviour directly manifests as shortened birth intervals, elevated parity, and delayed childbearing, thereby amplifying exposure to HRFB [17, 23].

As overall fertility declines in India, the reproductive window compresses, intensifying the pressure to achieve desired sex composition within fewer births and increasing the likelihood of high-risk reproductive practices among son-preferring women [24]. Regionally, this dynamic is most pronounced in North and Central India, where rigid patrilocal exogamy and strong economic dependency on sons sustain prolonged parity progression, whereas more flexible kinship arrangements and higher female autonomy in the Northeast and parts of South India correspond with lower HRFB prevalence [18, 25] Consequently, son preference operates not merely as an attitudinal bias but as a structural driver of prolonged and closely spaced fertility, exacerbating maternal and child health vulnerabilities in contexts with constrained reproductive autonomy and limited access to family planning services [2, 26].

While prior research has extensively documented the socioeconomic and demographic determinants of HRFB across low- and middle-income countries [26–31] and linked these patterns to adverse maternal anaemia and child nutritional outcomes [25, 32], the influence of deeply entrenched gender norms particularly how patrilocal kinship structures institutionalize son preference and subsequently shape high-risk fertility trajectories remains empirically underexplored in the Indian context. Son preference shapes reproductive behaviour through continued childbearing until the desired number of sons is achieved. These pressures can limit women’s reproductive autonomy, delay contraceptive use, and contribute to closely spaced, higher-order, and delayed births beyond 35 years of age, which are associated with both single and multiple HRFB [22, 23, 33]. Most existing studies treat son preference as a static cultural attitude, overlooking its structural roots in residence norms, old-age dependency, and the reproductive pressures created by declining fertility. To address this gap, the present study investigates how son preference, as a kinship-mediated sociocultural norm, independently shapes the risk of single and multiple high-risk fertility behaviours. Leveraging nationally representative data from the National Family Health Survey-5 (2019–21), we employ multivariable logistic and multinomial regression models to estimate the adjusted associations between son preference and HRFB, complemented by Fairlie decomposition analysis to isolate the relative contributions of observable socioeconomic and reproductive characteristics to the observed disparities. By moving beyond conventional demographic explanations, this study positions son preference as a structural, kinship-driven determinant of reproductive health inequity, offering critical evidence to inform gender-transformative family planning policies and accelerate progress towards Sustainable Development Goals 3 (good health and well-being) and 5 (gender equality).

Data methods

This study utilizes data from the child record files of the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5), conducted in India during (2019–2021). The NFHS is a nationally representative, cross-sectional household survey implemented by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS), Mumbai, under the aegis of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW), Government of India, with technical assistance from ICF, USA, through the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Program [4]. This cross-sectional survey collects the data from all states and union territories of India using standardized questionnaire administered in 18 Indian languages via computer-assisted personnel interview (CAPI), employing a two-stage stratified sampling design in rural and urban areas. In the first stage, primary sampling units (villages in rural areas and census enumeration blocks in urban areas) were selected using probability proportional to size, followed by systematic selection of households in the second stage. This survey. The child recode file, which forms the basis of this analysis, contains detailed information on all live births occurring in the five years preceding each survey, linked to maternal and household characteristics from the corresponding women’s and household files. NFHS-5 includes data on 232,920 children born to women aged 15–49. Data quality was rigorously maintained through uniform training protocols, field supervision, back-checks, and the use of standardized field-check tables.

Data cleaning and study variables

The identification and classification of study variables were guided by existing studies and experts’ views, along with reflections from the research team. The variables were then cleaned to facilitate analysis. Existing variables were recoded, and new variables were created. A detailed outline of the sample selection process and variables used in this study (outcome and predictor) is discussed below.

The analytical sample for this study was drawn from women aged 15–49 who participated in the National Family Health Survey-5 (NFHS-5; 2019–21) and reported at least one live birth in the five years preceding the survey (Fig. 1). The initial eligible sample comprised 232,920 mothers. Of these, 3,240 women who responded “not sure” to the question on ideal number of children were excluded, as their responses did not allow for clear classification of son preference. The remaining 229,680 women, who reported a specific ideal number of children, were categorized into two mutually exclusive groups based on their stated ideal sex composition of children: those with son preference (n = 45,577), defined as women whose ideal number of boys exceeded their ideal number of girls, and those without son preference (n = 187,343), which included women whose ideal number of boys was equal to or fewer than their ideal number of girls. Among women with son preference, 59.27% reported no high-risk fertility behaviour, while 31.32% and 9.41% experienced single and multiple high-risk fertility behaviours, respectively. In contrast, among women without son preference, 73.18% reported no high-risk fertility behaviour, whereas 22.47% and 4.35% experienced single and multiple high-risk fertility behaviours, respectively. This final analytical sample of 229,680 women was used to examine the association between son preference and high-risk fertility behaviour.

Outcome variable

The primary outcome of this study was high-risk fertility behaviour, defined based on three established criteria: (1) maternal age at birth younger than 18 years or 35 years and older; (2) birth interval less than 24 months; and (3) birth order of four or higher. Women who met at least one of these criteria were classified as exhibiting any high-risk fertility behaviour; those meeting exactly one criterion were categorized as having single high-risk fertility behaviour, while those meeting two or more criteria were classified as having multiple high-risk fertility behaviour. Since in this study our main objective is to see how the HRFB is different among women with and without son preference. If there is any difference, then what are the sociodemographic factors which contributes to this differential pattern. Therefore, we had categorized the HRFB as no risk “without having any of the above mentioned three risks” and any risk “one or more than one risk of HRFB”. This multi-level classification allowed for a more detailed assessment of how son preference is associated not only with the presence but also with the intensity of high-risk fertility practices among women in India.

Predictor variables

The main exposure variable was son preference, derived from women’s responses to the ideal number of boys and girls; women whose ideal number of sons exceeded their ideal number of daughters were classified as having son preference, while those whose ideal number of sons was equal to or fewer than their ideal number of daughters were classified as having no son preference. Maternal age at survey was categorized as 15–24, 25–34, and ≥ 35 years, and educational attainment was grouped as no education, primary, secondary, and higher. Age at first marriage was classified as < 18, 18–24, and ≥ 25 years, while age at first birth was categorized as < 20, 20–29, and 30–49 years. Maternal nutritional status was assessed using body mass index (BMI), categorized as underweight, normal, and overweight/obese, and maternal height was grouped as short, medium, and normal/tall. The number of living daughters was categorized as 0, 1, 2, and 3 or more. Desire for additional children was classified as wanted and undecided/no more/other, and pregnancy wantedness was grouped as wanted and unwanted. Exposure to family planning messages was categorized as exposed or not exposed, and current contraceptive use was classified as no method, modern method, and traditional method. Perceived distance to a health facility was grouped as not a problem and problem. Household wealth status was measured using the DHS wealth index and categorized into poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest quintiles, while household size was grouped as 1–4, 5–8, and more than 8 members. Sex of household head was classified as male or female. Social group (caste) was categorized as Scheduled Castes/Scheduled Tribes (SC/ST), Other Backward Classes (OBC), and Others, and religion was grouped as Hindu, Muslim, and Other religions. Place of residence was classified as urban or rural, and geographical region was categorized into North, Central, East, West, South, and Northeast regions following standard NFHS classification.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed and the results are presented as unweighted frequencies and weighted percentages. Bivariate analyses using cross-tabulations and Pearson’s Chi-square test were conducted to examine the distribution and association of sociodemographic and reproductive covariates with high-risk fertility behaviour (single and multiple HRFB). To further assess the association between son preference and high-risk fertility behaviour, both unadjusted and multivariable logistic regression models were fitted. Variables included in the multivariable model were selected based on theoretical relevance, previous literature, and their established association with high-risk fertility behaviour and son preference. Relevant sociodemographic, reproductive, and household-level variables were retained in the adjusted model to account for potential confounding. Results are reported as unadjusted odds ratios (UORs) and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Fairlie decomposition analysis

To quantify the extent to which differences in observed sociodemographic characteristics explain disparities in high-risk fertility behaviour (HRFB) between women with and without son preference, we employed the Fairlie decomposition analysis (FDA), a nonlinear decomposition technique suitable for binary outcomes estimated using logit or probit models [38, 39]. This method extends the classical Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition framework developed for linear regression models [40, 41] which has been widely criticized for its inappropriateness in nonlinear contexts such as logit and probit models due to issues related to model identification and scale dependence [42] to nonlinear probability models, thereby avoiding problems of identification and scale dependence inherent in applying linear decomposition to logit or probit models [42].

The variable-specific contributions to the explained component were obtained using the Fairlie nonlinear decomposition procedure, which estimates the change in average predicted probabilities when the distribution of each explanatory variable is sequentially replaced between groups while holding other variables constant. This procedure is implemented within the decomposition algorithm and does not rely on linear approximations of mean covariate differences (Fairlie, 2005). Differences in sample sizes between groups were accounted for within the decomposition routine through matching based on predicted probabilities, as implemented in the statistical software. The explained component reflects the proportion of the HRFB gap attributable to differences in observable sociodemographic characteristics, including maternal age, parity, education, household wealth, residence, region, and exposure to mass media, while the unexplained component represents differences due to differential effects of these characteristics and unobserved factors.

Results

The prevalence of high-risk fertility behaviour varies substantially across sociodemographic and household characteristics (Table 1). Notably, 40.73% of women with son preference experience HRFB, compared to 26.82% of those without son preference, highlighting a pronounced association between gender-biased fertility norms and reproductive risk. HRFB is most prevalent among women aged 35 or older (68.3%), those with no education (49.31%), and those who married before age 18 (41.19%). Women with two or more living daughters exhibit sharply elevated HRFB rates (74.79% for those with two daughters), underscoring the role of son preference in shaping continued childbearing patterns. Lack of exposure to family planning messages (38.05%) and no exposure to mass media (41.02%), along with belonging to Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (32.25%), are associated with an increased HRFB. Additional high-risk groups include women from the poorest households (42.04%), those with unwanted pregnancies (47.18%), and those with undecided or no further desire for children yet still experiencing births (34.89%). Regional disparities are also evident, with the highest HRFB observed in Central India (33.36%) and among Muslim women (36.53%). Conversely, higher education, later age at marriage and first birth, and greater media exposure are consistently associated with lower HRFB prevalence. In Table 2, the fully adjusted model (Model 2) shows that son preference remained significantly associated with higher odds of HRFB (AOR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.27–1.34). Women’s age showed a pronounced effect: those aged ≥ 35 years had over five times higher odds of HRFB (AOR = 5.03, 95% CI: 4.78–5.30) compared to women aged 15–24, while those aged 25–34 had slightly lower odds (AOR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.97). Higher educational attainment was strongly protective against HRFB. Women with higher education (AOR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.49–0.54) exhibited progressively lower odds of HRFB compared to women with no education. Later age at marriage was also protective: women who marry ≥ 25 years (AOR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.58–0.66) had significantly reduced odds of HRFB compared to those married before age 18. Similarly, age at first birth showed a protective effect for births occurring at 20–29 years (AOR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.62–0.67), though first births at 30–49 years were associated with elevated odds of HRFB (AOR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.08–1.26). Women with three or more living daughters exhibited substantially higher odds of HRFB (AOR = 7.59, 95% CI: 7.26–7.93) compared to women with no daughters. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously, as the number of living daughters is closely linked with parity progression and continued childbearing behaviour. Unwanted pregnancies were strongly associated with HRFB (AOR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.74–1.88). Women reporting modern contraceptive use also showed higher odds of HRFB (AOR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.11–1.17); however, this association should be interpreted cautiously, as contraceptive adoption may occur after experiencing high-risk fertility patterns, reflecting possible reverse causality. Women from larger households with 5–8 members (AOR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.42–1.51) or more than 8 members (AOR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.60–1.73) had significantly higher HRFB odds compared to smaller households (1–4 members). Wealth status was inversely associated with HRFB: women from the richest households had 41% lower odds (AOR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.55–0.62) than those in the poorest. Religious affiliation also mattered: Muslim (AOR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.33–1.43) and Other (AOR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.42–1.56) women faced higher HRFB odds than Hindus. Caste disparities persisted, with SC/ST women (reference) at higher risk than other caste women (AOR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.85–0.91). Regionally, women in Central, South and North India with son preference had elevated HRFB odds, while those in the Northeast had lower odds (AOR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.82–0.90).

Multivariable logistic regression results examining the association between son preference and high-risk fertility behaviour

The multinomial regression model presented in Appendix Table A2 reveals that son preference is a significant independent associated factor of both single and multiple high-risk fertility behaviours (HRFB) in India. Women expressing son preference had a 29% higher relative risk of experiencing single HRFB (ARR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.25–1.32) and 45% higher relative risk of multiple HRFB (ARR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.37–1.52) compared to women with no son preference. The number of living daughters also emerged as a strong associated factor. Women with three or more daughters had substantially higher relative risks of both, single (ARR = 6.24) and multiple, 23 times HRFB, compared to women with no daughters. Additional critical associated risk factors include unwanted pregnancy (ARR = 2.15 for multiple HRFB), no exposure to family planning messages, limited mass media access, and belonging to SC/ST communities, who serve as the reference group with the highest baseline HRFB burden. Conversely, higher education, later age at marriage and first birth, greater wealth, and exposure to media and family planning messages are consistently associated with lower likelihoods of both single and multiple HRFB.

Figure 2 shows the Fairlie decomposition estimates decomposing the HRFB gap by son preference status in India. Around 62.91% of the disparity is attributed to differences in observed individual and household-level characteristics, while the remaining 37.09% is attributable to unobserved factors or differences in the effects of these characteristics (i.e. coefficient effects). Among the observed characteristics, the number of living daughters is the largest contributor, accounting for 26.96% of the gap, followed by women’s education (20.49%) and household wealth index (14.47%). Additional notable contributors include age at first birth (10.12%), age at marriage (8.95%), and women’s current age (8.67%), collectively indicating the role of early fertility initiation and constrained reproductive autonomy. In contrast, a few variables exhibit negative contributions, slightly attenuating the HRFB gap: desire for more children (–3.59%), contraceptive use (–0.71%), and urban residence (–0.08%), suggesting that among women with son preference, clearer fertility intentions or adoption of contraception modestly reduce the observed HRFB gap.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that son preference, a deeply rooted sociocultural norm in India, remains a potent structural correlate of high-risk fertility behaviour (HRFB), defined as early (< 18 years) or late (≥ 35 years) childbearing, birth intervals of ≤ 24 months, and parity of ≥ 4. Using nationally representative data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–2021) comprising 229,680 women, we employed multivariable logistic regression, multinomial logistic regression, and Fairlie decomposition analysis. The results indicate that women with son preference have 30% higher odds of experiencing any HRFB (AOR = 1.30), with the risk intensifying to 45% for multiple concurrent risks. The strongest predictor was the number of living daughters: women with three or more daughters faced 7.6 times higher odds of any HRFB. Other significant associated factors included unwanted pregnancy, low educational attainment, early marriage, poverty, limited media exposure, and belonging to Muslim or SC/ST groups.

Son preference disproportionately shapes reproductive trajectories among socioeconomically and educationally disadvantaged women. Through sex-composition-driven fertility strategies, couples often engage in compensatory reproductive behaviour, continuing childbearing until the desired number of sons is achieved, thereby increasing the odds of HRFB [22, 23, 33]. This pattern is deeply embedded within India’s patrilineal and patrilocal kinship system, wherein sons are central to old-age support, inheritance, and lineage continuity, whereas daughters are frequently construed as economic liabilities owing to dowry obligations and post-marital migration [17, 19, 20]. In contexts of daughter-dominant birth sequences, intensified familial and social pressure constrains women’s reproductive autonomy, weakening intra-household bargaining power [43]. Consequently, contraceptive trajectories are disrupted through delayed initiation, post-daughter discontinuation, or reliance on less effective methods.

Son preference significantly increases the likelihood of multiple HRFBs, which reflect not merely the accumulation of reproductive risks, but the clustering of interconnected risks driven by persistent gender-biased fertility norms within structural and economic constraints. In such contexts, continued childbearing to achieve a desired son often results in closely spaced, higher-order births, advanced maternal age, and repeated pregnancies within a single reproductive trajectory [11, 45, 46]. These overlapping risks intensify maternal depletion, obstetric complications, and adverse child outcomes, while also limiting women’s access to timely maternal care and effective contraceptive use [24]. As fertility declines, a compressed reproductive window intensifies pressure to secure male offspring within fewer births, particularly among women with limited media exposure or health literacy, contributing to higher odds of HRFB [24, 29].

The observed association between son preference, the number of living daughters, and HRFB may reflect the influence of patrilocal and patriarchal kinship systems, where couples could continue childbearing in pursuit of a desired number of sons [24]. Son preference establishes a normative fertility target centred on male offspring; when early births yield daughters rather than sons, the gap between desired and achieved sex composition widens, triggering a compensatory reproductive response [33]. The number of living daughters thus functions as a direct demographic proxy for unmet son preference, as each additional daughter without a corresponding son intensifies familial pressure to continue childbearing. In India’s predominantly patrilocal structure, where sons are expected to provide old-age support, continue the family lineage, and fulfil ritual obligations while daughters are expected to marry out, couples with only daughters may continue childbearing to achieve a desired son, thereby increasing the likelihood of higher-order births and short birth intervals [17, 18]. This structural reality, compounded by inadequate formal social security systems, is associated with sustained fertility continuation until a male heir is secured [19, 20].

Consequently, the dramatic escalation of HRFB with each additional daughter reflects the cumulative pressure to close the son-preference gap through additional, closely spaced, and higher-order births. Within these kinship systems, daughters are often perceived as economic liabilities who offer limited long-term returns, whereas sons serve as instrumental assets for later-life care and social status [47, 48]. The persistence of son preference means that daughter-heavy fertility is frequently interpreted as reproductive incompleteness, subjecting women to psychological stress, social scrutiny, and diminished household bargaining power. Repeated daughter births further erode women’s agency through perceived reproductive failure [22, 44]. As overall fertility declines, the reproductive window compresses, intensifying the stakes of each birth and increasing the likelihood of high-risk reproductive practices among women whose living daughters signal unmet son preference [24]. In settings of weak social protection and labour precarity, the instrumental value of sons is reinforced, positioning son preference not merely as an attitudinal bias, but as a structural correlate of prolonged, high-parity, and closely spaced fertility patterns that are associated with elevated single and multiple HRFB risks [23, 49].

Women’s age and educational attainment are key associated factors of HRFB. Women aged ≥ 35 years faced five times higher odds of any HRFB, reflecting cumulative reproductive exposure that increases high parity, short birth intervals, and delayed childbearing. Advancing maternal age is associated with a compressed reproductive window, which may amplify overlapping HRFB. In contrast, women with higher education had lower odds of any HRFB as education enhances knowledge, reduces son preference, cognitive skills, economic opportunities, financial independence, and reproductive self-efficacy, enabling informed fertility choices and effective contraception use [28, 50]. Education is also associated with stronger bargaining power, autonomy, and decision-making confidence, allowing women to resist familial and societal pressures for high fertility, particularly in patriarchal, son-preferring households [2, 29]. Overall, education is linked to improved fertility regulation and lower likelihood of overlapping HRFB, thereby reducing cumulative reproductive risk.

Age at marriage and age at first birth are strong protective factors associated with lower HRFB in India, reflecting life-course timing and women’s reproductive agency. Women marrying at ≥ 25 years had lower odds of any HRFB compared with those married before 18, as delayed marriage is associated with a shorter reproductive window and reduced cumulative exposure to repeated, closely spaced pregnancies. Early marriage limits bargaining power and autonomy over contraception, birth spacing, and pregnancy timing, particularly in patriarchal, son-preferring households [51]. Initiating childbearing at 20–29 years, considered an optimal social and biological window, was associated reduction in any HRFB relative to first births before age 20, as socially mature women are better able to plan fertility and avoid overlapping risks [29]. Age at marriage and age at first birth are associated with enhanced agency, educational attainment, and effective use of family planning, thereby lowering cumulative HRFB [52].

Exposure to mass media is associated with reduction in the odds of any HRFB, likely by enhancing awareness of reproductive health, reshaping gender norms, and promoting contraceptive use. Media is also associated with informed fertility regulation through its influence on parity progression and birth spacing [53]. In contrast, women who reported no further desire for children but experienced additional births faced higher risk of any HRFB reflecting a pronounced intention–behaviour gap. This gap highlights limited reproductive autonomy, weak household bargaining power, and reduced self-efficacy in fertility control, particularly in patriarchal settings. Structural inequalities including unequal access to family-planning services, economic dependence, and insecure livelihoods further constrain women’s ability to realize their fertility intentions, resulting in unintended, closely spaced, and high-parity births that are associated with higher cumulative HRFB risk [30].

Women from the poorest households face substantially higher HRFB risks, with 41% higher odds of any HRFB reflecting economic deprivation that limits access to quality reproductive health services and effective contraception. Poverty is also associated with greater reliance on higher fertility as a form of perceived social and economic security, particularly under conditions of labour precarity [29, 54]. Household size further amplifies risk: women in households with 5–8 members had higher odds of HRFB, while those in households with more than eight members faced higher odds, a pattern consistent with patrilocal joint family norms that are associated with intensified reproductive pressure, psychological strain, and reduced bargaining power within extended kinship networks [45, 46].

Religious and regional disparities further illuminate how kinship geography shapes reproductive risk. Muslim women experienced 38% higher odds of any HRFB compared with Hindu women, reflecting a pattern consistent with delayed demographic transition associated with structural disadvantage, restricted female autonomy, and lower adoption of fertility-regulating behaviours [55, 56]. Regionally, women in Central and North India exhibited elevated HRFB odds, consistent with rigid patrilocal exogamy, stronger economic dependency on sons, and historically entrenched son preference that is associated with sustained parity progression [18, 29], In contrast, the Northeast exhibited lower HRFB risk, likely due to more flexible marriage arrangements, higher female autonomy, weaker patrilocal norms, and broader acceptance of fertility regulation.

The Fairlie decomposition analysis shows that 62.9% of the HRFB gap between women with and without son preference is explained by observable socioeconomic and reproductive factors. The number of living daughters is the largest contributor, reflecting pressure to achieve a male birth through continued childbearing, short birth intervals, and high parity [22, 44]. Early marriage and early first birth also increase the gap by extending reproductive exposure. Educational disadvantage and household poverty further widen the gap, as lower education and economic constraints among son-preferring women limit effective fertility regulation. In contrast, fertility intentions and contraceptive use reduce the gap, indicating that clearer reproductive goals and access to contraception partially mitigate HRFB risk [2]. Overall, son preference drives HRFB primarily through early life-course transitions, structural disadvantage, and constrained reproductive autonomy, highlighting the need to delay marriage and first birth, expand education and economic opportunities, and strengthen family-planning access to reduce HRFB disparities.

Conclusion

Son preference remains an important structural correlate of HRFB in India, of HRFB in India and is associated with early and late childbearing, short birth intervals, and high parity especially among women with multiple daughters and those aged ≥ 35 years driven by persistent pressure to bear sons. Crucially, 62.9% of this disparity is explained by measurable factors: number of living daughters, low education, poverty, early marriage, and early first birth. These findings call for targeted policy interventions beyond standard family-planning approaches. First, the Child Marriage Act must be strengthened to uplift and protect adolescent girls who are at high-risk fertility trajectories. Second, education-to-employment pathways for girls should be prioritized, given that higher education is associated with lower likelihood of multiple HRFB. Third, son preference must be explicitly addressed within national health programmes, such as the National Family Planning Programme and Mission Parivar Vikas, through gender-transformative messaging that challenges son-centric norms. Fourth, expand postpartum contraceptive access immediately after daughter births to prevent rapid, unwanted subsequent pregnancies associated with son preference. Monitoring systems should track gender equity indicators alongside fertility metrics. Frontline health workers (e.g., ASHAs) must be trained to counsel families on gender equality and reproductive autonomy at the household level. Improving reproductive health awareness among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations and strengthening healthcare infrastructure in high son preference and HRFB states are important for ensuring equitable access to maternal and reproductive healthcare services. Without addressing the cultural roots of son preference, national fertility declines may mask persistent and unequal reproductive risks for vulnerable women undermining progress towards SDG 3 (maternal and child health) and SDG 5 (gender equality).

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, it relies on cross-sectional data from NFHS-5, which limits causal inference due to the inability to establish temporal sequencing between son preference and HRFB. While son preference is measured via ideal desired sex composition, it may not fully capture deeply held gender norms or actual fertility decisions shaped by family or community pressures. Second, recall bias is possible, as birth history data depend on women’s self-reports over a five-year period, potentially affecting accuracy of birth intervals and parity. Third, the measure of son preference excludes women who responded “don’t know” to ideal number of children, possibly omitting a vulnerable subgroup with low autonomy or literacy. Fourth, unobserved factors such as intra-household power dynamics, regional cultural practices, or access to sex-selective technologies are not captured in the dataset but may influence both son preference and HRFB. One other important limitation of the study is that the NFHS-5 data collection period overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced certain sociodemographic, healthcare utilization, and reproductive health-related variables, potentially affecting the study estimates. Finally, while Fairlie decomposition explains majority of the HRFB gap through observed characteristics, the remaining (unexplained component) may reflect structural inequities or normative contexts not measurable in survey data. Despite these limitations, the study provides robust nationally representative evidence linking son preference to elevated reproductive risks, underscoring the need for gender-transformative policies alongside improved longitudinal and qualitative research to unpack causal pathways.
