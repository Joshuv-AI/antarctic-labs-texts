Sleep deprivation exhibits an age-dependent effect on infraslow global brain activity
Authors: Yibing Yang, Weiwei Zhao, Yutong Mao, Tianxin Mao, Yao Deng, Yifan Yang, Hengyi Rao, Xiao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceResting-state infraslow (<0.1 Hz) global brain activity has recently been linked to aging and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), possibly due to its potential role in sleep-dependent clearance of brain metabolic wastes. We show that total sleep deprivation increases the strength of this global brain activity and its coupling with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) inflow during wakefulness. Notably, this acute adaptive response is highly age dependent and largely diminished in adults aged 40 to 50 y. These findings highlight a potential mechanism underlying the complex interactions among aging, sleep disruption, and dementia risks.

Sleep is essential for maintaining homeostasis and restoring optimal brain functions (1, 2), potentially including the clearance of neurotoxic proteins (3). Recent studies have consistently suggested the important role of a glymphatic system in brain waste clearance, which exhibits enhanced activity during sleep to presumably facilitate the removal of toxic proteins (3–5). Consistent with this notion, sleep loss impairs the clearance function, reducing the brain’s capacity to eliminate toxic proteins and leading to their accumulation (6–9). For example, animal studies have demonstrated that sleep deprivation elevates levels of Aβ and tau, the key pathological markers of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), in both the interstitial fluid and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (8, 9). In parallel, human studies indicated that even a single night of total sleep deprivation (TSD) is associated with impaired clearance of toxic molecules, including increased Aβ burden in critical brain regions such as the hippocampus and thalamus (6, 7).

Recent work has revealed an infraslow (<0.1 Hz) global brain activity linked to sleep-dependent CSF dynamics (10, 11), which may play an important role in neurotoxic clearance (3, 4). This activity, manifested as pronounced peaks in the global mean blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (gBOLD) signal (12, 13), exhibits highly structured spatiotemporal patterns in both human and animal studies (13–16). In human fMRI and monkey electrocorticography recordings, it propagates as waves across cortical hierarchies, while in mouse neuronal recordings, it appears as cascades of sequential activation (14–16). Notably, these widespread cortical activations are accompanied by deactivations in subcortical arousal-regulating centers, consistent with associated fluctuations in arousal states (13, 14). Most importantly, this global activity is coupled to CSF flow, suggesting a potential role in brain waste clearance (10). Supporting this notion, the coupling strength between gBOLD signal and CSF inflow (hereafter referred to as gBOLD-CSF coupling) has been associated with various AD-related pathologies, including the accumulation of Aβ and tau (17, 18). Topological changes of gBOLD activity have even been linked to the spreading pattern of Aβ in the early stages of AD (19). Thus, while not directly quantifying metabolic waste clearance (20, 21), gBOLD activity and associated CSF inflow have been linked to the accumulation of metabolic waste products characteristic of AD (17–19).

While the global BOLD signal displays elevated amplitude during sleep (22, 23), the effect of sleep deprivation on gBOLD activity and its coupling with CSF dynamics remain poorly characterized. Emerging evidence implicates age as a critical modulator of sleep-dependent waste clearance (24), potentially influencing susceptibility to sleep deprivation–induced changes in gBOLD activity. A contrast-enhanced MRI study in clinical cohorts identified age-dependent declines in glymphatic clearance efficiency (24). Normative aging populations exhibit a nonlinear trajectory of gBOLD-CSF coupling marked by accelerated deterioration beginning around the mid-50s (25). These changes align with the well-documented age-related decline in sleep quality (26), characterized by reductions in slow-wave sleep (SWS), increased fragmentation, and diminished overall sleep efficiency (SE). Thus, the interaction between age and sleep deprivation on gBOLD activity may be critical for understanding the age-related decline in waste clearance, which may in turn underlie the heightened risk of dementia in midlife adults (27, 28).

In this study, we leverage resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) data from a rigorously controlled in-laboratory sleep deprivation experiment (29) to investigate changes in gBOLD activity and its coupling with CSF dynamics after one night of TSD and following two nights of recovery sleep across different age groups. Our results demonstrate that TSD induced a significant increase in gBOLD signal amplitude, which returned to baseline levels after two nights of recovery sleep. Interestingly, the TSD effect is highly age-dependent and almost absent in midlife subjects aged 40 to 50 y. Furthermore, the impact of TSD was more pronounced in higher-order brain regions, particularly within the default mode network (DMN), compared with lower-order sensory-motor regions. Given recent evidence implicating the key role of gBOLD activity and gBOLD-CSF coupling in brain waste clearance (19, 30), the increased global brain activity after sleep loss may represent a compensatory clearance mechanism. However, this response appears to be impaired after age 40, potentially contributing to the age-related decline in waste clearance efficiency and increased risk of dementia during aging.

Results

TSD Increases gBOLD Activity and Its Coupling with CSF Dynamics.

Resting-state fMRI data were collected from 67 subjects (34.1 ± 8.9 y, 29 females) who participated in a 5-d, 4-night TSD experiment, with scans conducted in the mornings of Days 2 (Scan1), 3 (Scan2), and 5 (Scan3) (Fig. 1A). gBOLD activity was quantified using two metrics: the standard deviation (SD) of gBOLD signal to assess its fluctuation amplitude and gBOLD-CSF coupling (see Materials and Methods for details) to measure its synchronization with CSF flow measured at the bottom of the cerebellum (Fig. 1 B–D). The TSD group (N = 51, 33.7 ± 8.8 y, 21 females) showed a substantial increase (i.e., more negative) in sex-adjusted (applied to all gBOLD metrics) gBOLD-CSF coupling during the TSD scan on Day 3 (P = 0.0071, paired t test) compared to the baseline scan (BS) on Day 1, and the coupling returned to baseline level during the recovery scan (RS) on Day 5 after two nights of recovery sleep (P = 0.00041, paired t test). In contrast, the control group (N = 16, 35.4 ± 9.5 y, eight females) showed no significant differences in gBOLD-CSF coupling between the TSD and BS scans (P = 0.78, paired t test) or between the RS and TSD scans (P = 0.88, paired t test) (Fig. 1E). Similar changes (TSD vs. BS, P = 0.00014, paired t test; RS vs. TSD, P = 0.0015, paired t test) were also observed for the gBOLD amplitude (Fig. 1F).

TSD Effects on gBOLD Activity Are Absent in Midlife Adults (40 to 50 y).

To investigate whether the TSD-induced gBOLD changes are age-dependent, we divided the TSD subjects into three age groups: Group I (N = 20, age: 20 to 30 y, eight females), Group II (N = 14, age: 30 to 40 y, seven females), and Group III (N = 17, age: 40 to 50 y, six females) and compared their gBOLD metrics. The gBOLD-CSF coupling increased significantly after TSD in both Group I (TSD vs. BS, P = 0.019, paired t test) and Group II (TSD vs. BS, P = 0.0085, paired t test) and then returned to baseline after recovery sleep (RS vs. TSD, P = 0.013 for Group I and P = 0.0037 for Group II, paired t test). In contrast, no significant TSD-dependent changes were observed in Group III, with no differences between the TSD and BS scans (P = 0.82, paired t test), or between the TSD and RS scans (P = 0.44, paired t test) (Fig. 2A). Consistent with these observations, there was a significant interaction effect between age and scan (P = 0.037, two-way ANOVA) on gBOLD-CSF coupling. The peak gBOLD-CSF correlations occur at the same time lag across conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), suggesting stable relative timing between the two.

The same analysis of gBOLD amplitude yielded similar results (Fig. 2B). In Group I, gBOLD amplitude increased significantly after TSD and then returned to baseline after recovery sleep (TSD vs. BS: P = 0.0036; RS vs. TSD: P = 0.00099, paired t test). Similar but smaller changes were observed in Group II (TSD vs. BS, P = 0.025; RS vs. TSD, P = 0.24, paired t test), whereas no significant changes were found in Group III (TSD vs. BS, P = 0.25; RS vs. TSD, P = 0.64, paired t test). The age–scan interaction was more significant (P = 0.0067, two-way ANOVA) than for gBOLD-CSF coupling. The head motion quantified by mean framewise displacement (FD) showed no significant correlations with either gBOLD-CSF coupling (rho = 0.13, P = 0.067, Spearman’s correlation) or gBOLD amplitude (rho = 0.0037, P = 0.96, Spearman’s correlation). The main findings remained robust and unchanged after controlling for head motion (SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3).

TSD Effects on gBOLD Activity Are Pronounced in Higher-Order Brain Regions.

Given that gBOLD activity shows more prominent reduction in higher-order DMN regions at the early stages of AD, which has been linked to early Aβ accumulation (19), we examined whether TSD-induced gBOLD changes varied across the cortex and were biased toward specific regions. We quantified the topology of gBOLD activity using gBOLD presence, defined as the correlation between regional BOLD signals and the gBOLD signal (19, 31). Similar to gBOLD amplitude, gBOLD presence exhibited age-specific alterations in response to TSD, with minimal changes observed in Group III (Fig. 3A). In Group I and Group II, TSD-induced increases in gBOLD presence were spatially heterogeneous, with larger effects observed in higher-order brain regions, including the bilateral posterior cingulate cortex, bilateral caudal anterior cingulate cortex, and bilateral superior frontal gyrus, which are all components of the DMN. These qualitative observations were supported by quantitative comparisons between the higher-order regions [DMN and frontoparietal network (FPN)] and lower-order regions of sensory-motor areas defined previously (19). The increase in gBOLD presence was significantly greater in higher-order brain regions than in lower-order brain regions for both Group I (P = 0.0014, two-sample t test) and Group II (P = 0.029, two-sample t test) (Fig. 3B). In addition, the age–scan interaction effect on gBOLD presence was more significant in higher-order regions (P = 0.0067, two-way ANOVA) than in lower-order regions (P = 0.045, two-way ANOVA). After two nights of recovery sleep, the gBOLD presence decreased back to baseline with a similar pattern of spatial heterogeneity in Group I (P = 0.0020, two-sample t test) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

TSD-Induced gBOLD Changes Are Correlated with Baseline Sleep Measures.

To assess whether and how TSD-induced gBOLD changes relate to baseline sleep quality, we examined their associations, among TSD subjects, with 14 polysomnography-derived sleep measures during the baseline night. TSD-induced gBOLD changes were quantified as the difference between the TSD scan and the average of the BS and RS scans, and the coupling changes were sign-inverted to index effect magnitude. After controlling for age, both gBOLD metrics showed a general pattern of positive associations with indices of good sleep quality and negative associations with measures reflecting poor or fragmented sleep. Specifically, smaller coupling changes were significantly associated with greater sleep fragmentation and impaired sleep depth, including higher percentage of Stage 1 sleep (N1/TST; r = −0.36, P = 0.0094), increased wake after sleep onset (WASO; r = −0.31, P = 0.027), and longer total wake time (TWT; r = −0.28, P = 0.047) (Fig. 4A). These same sleep measures exhibited similar negative correlations with gBOLD amplitude changes, although the associations did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4B). Sleep efficiency showed the strongest positive correlations with coupling changes, but the association was significant only before age adjustment. For changes in gBOLD amplitude, rapid eye movement (REM) sleep latency showed significant negative association (r = −0.28, P = 0.049), whereas total REM sleep duration (r = 0.33, P = 0.019) and REM sleep percentage (REM/TST; r = 0.30, P = 0.037) were positively associated with gBOLD amplitude change (Fig. 4B). Age-stratified analyses revealed largely consistent correlation patterns, particularly in Groups II and III (Fig. 4C). However, none of these associations survived Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, presumably due to the limited sample size.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrated the age-dependent effects of TSD on infraslow (<0.1 Hz) global brain activity, which has been linked to AD pathologies, particularly the accumulation of toxic proteins. Specifically, we observed that the global brain activity, measured via gBOLD using fMRI, showed increased amplitude and enhanced coupling with CSF inflow after TSD, returning to baseline following recovery sleep. Importantly, this effect, predominantly seen in higher-order brain regions, was present only in individuals aged 20 to 40 y but not in those aged 40 to 50 y. Given the growing evidence linking gBOLD activity to brain waste clearance, the TSD-induced increase in gBOLD activity may serve as a compensatory mechanism to counteract impaired clearance due to sleep loss. However, this mechanism appears to be less effective in middle-aged adults (ages 40 to 50). These findings suggest that age may interact with sleep disturbances to impact brain waste clearance, potentially contributing to the increased risk of dementia in old adults.

We demonstrated that TSD significantly increased the amplitude of gBOLD activity and its coupling with CSF dynamics. The gBOLD signal had been initially considered as non-neuronal noise and was often removed in early rsfMRI studies (32, 33), but its high sensitivity to brain arousal state was soon recognized. The fluctuation amplitude of gBOLD increased significantly from wakefulness to light sleep or following the administration of hypnotic drugs (34, 35), whereas decreased after caffeine administration (36). Recent studies further revealed the neurophysiological basis and spatiotemporal pattern of gBOLD activity. Widespread brain activations at gBOLD peaks are accompanied by specific deactivations in subcortical arousal-regulating nuclei, particularly the basal forebrain nucleus basalis (NB), and a stereotypical electrophysiological pattern indicative of arousal changes (13, 37). Pharmaceutically deactivating the basal forebrain NB on one side of the monkey brain effectively suppressed ipsilateral gBOLD signals (38). Interestingly, the cortical activations manifest as waves propagating across cortical hierarchies, typically from lower-order sensory-motor areas to higher-order DMN regions, with the early phase accompanied by the deactivation of brainstem nuclei, including the locus coeruleus (14). In large-scale neuronal recordings from mice, the infraslow global brain activity relevant to arousal takes the form of spiking cascades of sequential activations across ~70% of neuronal populations (15). Given these early findings, the effect of TSD on gBOLD activity is not entirely surprising and is likely mediated by subcortical neuro-modulatory systems. Our rigorous experimental design ensured that all rsfMRI data were collected during wakefulness with subjects’ eyes open (29), suggesting that large-amplitude gBOLD activity is not exclusive to light sleep (10, 22, 39) and that sleep deprivation promotes these low frequency (LF) fMRI oscillations beyond N1 sleep stage (40). The increased gBOLD amplitude after TSD aligns with the increased global BOLD variability observed previously after partial sleep deprivation (41). We further showed that TSD also promotes the coupling between gBOLD activity and CSF dynamics.

The TSD-induced increase in the gBOLD-CSF coupling may reflect a compensatory enhancement of clearance function, given recent evidence for its link to toxic protein accumulation in AD. The gBOLD activity has been associated with infraslow modulations of various physiological signals, such as heart rate and respiratory volume (42–44). Its coupling with CSF movement, initially found during light sleep, suggests a potential role in CSF-related perivascular clearance (10, 11). Subsequent studies indeed linked the gBOLD-CSF coupling to various AD pathologies, particularly the accumulation of Aβ and tau (17, 18). More recently, the preferential reduction of gBOLD activity in higher-order DMN regions has been shown to correlate with the early accumulation of Aβ in these regions, thereby linking topological changes in gBOLD with the spreading pattern of Aβ (19). Based on these findings, the increase in gBOLD activity and gBOLD-CSF coupling after TSD may represent an adaptive increase in clearance function. It has been hypothesized that the cholinergic and noradrenergic systems modulate at distinct phases of this gBOLD dynamic and act synergistically on cerebral arteries to generate vasomotion, thereby facilitating the clearance with the support of simultaneous neural/astrocyte activations (30). Recent animal studies confirmed the critical roles of the cholinergic (45) and noradrenergic (46) systems in driving and/or promoting the infraslow vasomotion and fMRI oscillations. Strong autonomic modulations associated with gBOLD activity may represent additional mechanisms through which gBOLD activity affects CSF dynamics and metabolic waste clearance. Future studies incorporating physiological measurements and other modalities, such as pupillometry and electroencephalography (EEG), are needed to elucidate the effects of TSD on other gBOLD-related processes, such as vascular and physiological modulations, in order to better understand the underlying mechanisms.

This hypothesis of compensatory clearance appears to be consistent with a previous TSD study combining rsfMRI and Aβ PET (positron emission tomography) measures. It examined the TSD effects on resting-state BOLD signals in an autonomic network and found a significant increase in the low-frequency (LF, <0.1 Hz) power, which is equivalent to the quantification of the fluctuation amplitude. In TSD subjects, a higher LF power was significantly associated with a lower Aβ burden (47). While the enhanced clearance function initially appears to contradict previous findings of TSD-induced clearance impairments, this discrepancy may reflect differences in the specific aspects of clearance assessed (6–9). Prior studies measured the level of Aβ and tau, representing a result of clearance processes (7–9), whereas the gBOLD-CSF coupling likely reflects the instantaneous function of clearance. Thus, the TSD-induced increases in gBOLD activity may represent a compensatory effect that does not fully offset the impaired clearance caused by sleep loss, leading to an overall reduction in waste clearance. However, it is important to note that neither gBOLD activity nor associated CSF inflow directly measures the clearance of metabolic waste. Future studies with direct quantification of cerebral clearance will be essential to validate this compensatory clearance hypothesis.

The interaction between age and TSD on gBOLD activity and its coupling with CSF inflow may suggest this compensatory mechanism is impaired in individuals over 40 y old. The previous study observed a similar interaction effect on gBOLD variability, which is however statistically nonsignificant presumably due to the use of partial sleep deprivation (41). The total TSD in our study enabled sufficient effect size and confirmed a significant age–TSD interaction effect on gBOLD amplitude, as well as gBOLD-CSF coupling of clearance relevance. Aging is one of the most important risk factors of dementia (28). Various theories have been proposed to explain the age-related increase in dementia risk (28, 48), and impaired clearance is one of them (49). Consistent with this hypothesis, the clearance function and gBOLD-CSF coupling have been found to reduce at old ages (25). The findings of the current study suggest that the age-related reduction in clearance may be partly attributed to the loss of the ability to compensate for clearance impairments due to sleep loss. Together with the well-documented sleep disturbances widely observed in older adults (26), this would highlight a possible pathway through which aging interacts with sleep disturbance to contribute to the age-related risks of dementia. The TSD-induced gBOLD increase may relate to enhanced infraslow norepinephrine oscillations, which drive infraslow vasomotion (46), observed in young (3 to 4 mo) wild-type mice following sleep deprivation (50). Intriguingly, this sleep deprivation effect is absent in age-matched amyloid precursor protein/presenilin-1 (APP/PS1) mice, an AD model characterized by amyloid pathology (50). These observations raise the question of whether the reduced gBOLD response to TSD in humans over 40 may be linked to early amyloid pathology, which can emerge during midlife (51) and predict future cognitive decline (52, 53). Future studies are needed to test this possibility. Finally, it is worth noting that the TSD-induced gBOLD changes are more pronounced in the higher-order DMN regions. This topological dominance is similar to those seen at the early stages of Aβ pathology, as well as to the pattern of early Aβ deposition in prodromal/preclinical AD (19). These DMN-dominant gBOLD reductions may also underlie age-related decreases in resting-state BOLD signal amplitude (54), which were in turn linked to decreased glucose metabolism (55). Meanwhile, age-related decrease in glucose metabolism in the DMN regions has been attributed to the loss of aerobic glycolysis (56), which correlates with tau accumulation in cognitively normal older adults (57). Taken together, these findings from metabolic studies are consistent with recent observations linking reduced gBOLD activity and tau accumulations at preclinical and prodromal AD stages (58, 59).

Correlations of the TSD-induced gBOLD change with baseline sleep measures support its hypothetical role as a compensatory mechanism related to dementia risk. The gBOLD modulation appears to be a beneficial trait, correlating positively with metrics of good sleep quality and negatively with measures of sleep fragmentation and poor quality. The sleep metrics most strongly correlated with gBOLD modulation, such as the REM proportion, N1 proportion, and WASO, are key indicators of age-related changes in sleep architecture (60–62). Nevertheless, their associations with gBOLD changes are significant after controlling for age. Very importantly, the less REM and more sleep fragmentation, including WASO, have been directly linked to a higher incidence of dementias (63–65). Furthermore, the observed correlations with REM metrics may relate to recent findings that gBOLD activity is markedly enhanced during REM sleep and in coordination with REM sleep events (66).

Materials and Methods

Participants and Study Data.

Seventy healthy adults were recruited for a controlled 5-d and 4-night in-laboratory sleep deprivation experiment (29). Three participants were excluded due to excessive head motion, technical issues, or missing data, leaving 67 participants (aged 34.1 ± 8.9 y, 29 females) for analysis. Among these, 51 participants underwent TSD on Night 2, followed by a 12-h recovery sleep on Night 3 and a subsequent 8-h recovery sleep on Night 4. The remaining 16 participants served as the control group, maintaining an 8-h time-in-bed (TIB) schedule across Nights 2 to 4. To acclimate to the laboratory environment, all participants were provided with a 9-h TIB sleep opportunity between 9:30 PM and 6:30 AM on Night 1. rsfMRI sessions and cognitive tasks were conducted on the mornings of Days 2 (Scan1), 3 (Scan2), and 5 (Scan3) (Fig. 1A).

All participants were nonsmokers, right-handed, and confirmed to have no acute or chronic medical or psychological conditions through a comprehensive screening process, which included interviews, medical history reviews, questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests (blood and urine). The sleep–wake patterns of participants were assessed using multiple methods, including at least 7 d of actigraphy, sleep questionnaires, diaries, and one night of laboratory-based polysomnography and oximetry. All participants maintained consistent sleep routines, with habitual bedtimes between 10:00 PM and 12:00 AM, wake times between 6:00 AM and 9:00 AM, and typical sleep durations of 6.5 to 8 h. None of the participants reported habitual napping, irregular sleep schedules, trans-meridian travel, or shift work in the 60 d preceding the study. During the week before and throughout the study, all participants abstained from caffeine, alcohol, tobacco, and medications (except oral contraceptives). Compliance with these requirements and adherence to sleep–wake schedules were verified using actigraphy, sleep diaries, and time-stamped call-ins. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pennsylvania, adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent. Participants were informed of study procedures, risks, benefits, data confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Other aspects of this experiment, which aimed to test various hypotheses, have been previously published (67–72).

MR Data Acquisition and Preprocessing.

All MR imaging data were acquired using a 3 T Tesla MR scanner (Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen, Germany) with magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence for high-resolution anatomical imaging. The BOLD fMRI data were collected using a multiband gradient-echo echo planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: TR = 2,000 ms, TE = 24 ms, FOV = 220*220 mm, matrix size = 64*64*36, and slices thickness = 4 mm. During the resting state scans, participants were instructed to remain still and look at a fixation cross displayed at the center of the screen. All three scans were monitored by an eye-tracker to ensure participants remained awake throughout the procedure.

We conducted preprocessing on the rsfMRI data by employing scripts derived from the 1,000 Functional Connectomes Project (73), slightly customized to suit our needs by using scripts in the Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Brain Software Library (74) and Analysis of Functional NeuroImages (75). The rsfMRI BOLD data were preprocessed, starting with motion correction using the average fMRI image—computed as the mean of all volumes in the time series—as the reference. Subsequent steps included skull stripping, spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel (full width at half maximum = 4 mm), temporal filtering to retain frequencies between 0.01 to 0.1 Hz, and the removal of linear and quadratic temporal trends. Following the registration of the rsfMRI BOLD data to the corresponding high-resolution anatomical images, the data were further aligned to the MNI-152 space. In line with the approach of a prior study (17), nuisance regression of gBOLD and CSF inflow signals was not performed, as these signals were key variables of interest. Similarly, nuisance regression of head motion parameters was omitted to avoid attenuating the global BOLD signal. While head motion was considered to affect the BOLD signal, recent research has shown that the impact of head motion on the BOLD signal is mediated by transient arousal change (76).

gBOLD Signal, gBOLD Amplitude, CSF Signal, and the Coupling between the gBOLD Signal and CSF Signal.

The gBOLD signal was calculated by averaging the rsfMRI signals across gray matter regions for each subject (Fig. 1 B and C). A gray matter mask, derived from the Harvard–Oxford cortical and subcortical structural atlas, was used to extract the BOLD signals (77). To minimize spatial blurring caused by registration, signal extraction was conducted in the original fMRI acquisition space for each subject, with the gray matter mask transformed accordingly. Following extraction, the data were temporally filtered to retain frequencies within the range of 0.01 to 0.1 Hz. The filtered signals were subsequently normalized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the SD to ensure comparability across subjects. To avoid edge effects from temporal filtering, the first and last five TRs were excluded from the analysis. The gBOLD amplitude was calculated as the SD of the gBOLD signal (Fig. 1C).

The CSF signal caused by the inflow effect was extracted from the bottom slices of the rsfMRI data, located near the base of the cerebellum (10, 17) (Fig. 1 B and C). Following the previous study (17), the rsfMRI data used for CSF inflow signal extraction only included motion correction, skull stripping, and temporal filtering for preprocessing. The CSF inflow signal for each session was extracted using a manually derived CSF mask from the T2*-weighted fMRI image, which is also validated by the corresponding high-resolution T1-weighted image.

The correlation between gBOLD and CSF inflow signals was computed using the Pearson correlation at different time lags. Similar to a previous study (17), we focused on the correlation at the negative peak (+4 s time lag) to represent the strength of the coupling (Fig. 1D). We then explored the changes in the strength of coupling after sleep deprivation and recovery sleep, as well as the association of coupling change with age. The paired t test was conducted to assess the strength of coupling before and after TSD within each group. A two-way ANOVA was utilized to explore the interaction between age and scan condition on changes in gBOLD-CSF coupling and gBOLD amplitude.

gBOLD Presence and Definition of Higher- and Lower-Order Region.

The gBOLD presence signal within specific brain regions was quantified by calculating the correlation between the gBOLD signal and the averaged BOLD signals from individual parcels defined by the DKT-68 parcellation (77). These correlation values were computed across all parcels, providing a detailed map of gBOLD presence throughout the brain (31).

To categorize brain regions into lower and higher order, we employed Yeo’s 7-network parcellation framework as used in one previous study (19, 78). Higher-order regions were defined as parcels belonging to the DMN and FPN, both of which are associated with complex cognitive processes (Fig. 3B). In contrast, lower-order regions were defined as those within the somatomotor and visual networks, which are primarily involved in sensory and motor functions (Fig. 3B). This classification facilitated a network-level analysis of gBOLD contributions.

Changes in gBOLD presence were calculated as T-values, representing the differences in gBOLD presence between scans for each parcel. T-values within higher-order and lower-order regions were compared to provide a summary of gBOLD dynamics across distinct functional networks.

FD.

To account for the influence of head movement on our results, we calculated the FD, a metric that quantifies the relative head movement between consecutive time points in the fMRI time series (79). FD was derived from the six rigid-body motion parameters obtained during motion correction, which included translations along the x, y, and z axes, as well as rotations about these axes. For each time point, the absolute differences in motion parameters relative to the preceding time point were calculated. The FD for each frame was then determined as the sum of these differences. The rotational parameters, initially expressed in degrees, were converted to their equivalent displacement in millimeters using a standard spherical head model with a radius of 50 mm.

We investigated whether head motion influenced the age-specific effects of sleep deprivation on gBOLD-CSF coupling by examining its correlations with both gBOLD-CSF coupling and gBOLD amplitude. To assess the robustness of our findings, we subsequently reanalyzed the primary results after controlling for FD (SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3).

Sleep Measures and Their Correlations with gBOLD Changes.

Fourteen sleep measures were derived from polysomnography collected during the baseline sleep night prior to the sleep deprivation procedure. These included total sleep time (TST), SE, sleep onset latency, Stage 1 sleep duration (N1 Time, min) and its proportion of TST (N1/TST), Stage 2 sleep duration (N2 Time, min) and its proportion of TST (N2/TST), slow-wave sleep duration (SWS Time, min) and its proportion of TST (SWS/TST), REM sleep duration (REM Time, min) and its proportion of TST (REM/TST), REM sleep latency (REM Latency, min), (TWT, min), and WASO. Latency variables exhibited substantial skewness and were thus Gaussianized to better satisfy normality assumptions for subsequent analyses (80). Age was regressed out of sleep metrics to control for age effects on sleep structure.

The TSD-induced gBOLD changes, for both gBOLD-CSF coupling and gBOLD amplitude, were calculated as their difference between the TSD scan and the average of the BS and RS scans. Because gBOLD–CSF coupling values are negative in general, the difference was sign-inverted to reflect the magnitude of its changes, facilitating direct comparisons with gBOLD amplitude changes. Pearson correlations were then computed between sleep measures and changes of two gBOLD metrics across all TSD subjects, as well as within each age group.
