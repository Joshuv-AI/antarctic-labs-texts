Phenotypic plasticity in wild Camellia japonica across climatic zones: responses to variations in soil moisture and light intensity
Authors: Xinyue Liu, Mingyan Li, Haijie Zhang, Jibo Jiao, Xianhu Guo, Jikai Yang, Cuiju Liu, Shaoxia Guo, Yingkun Sun, Weihua Guo, Xiao Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsPhenotypic plasticity is recognized as the important driver of adapting to the heterogeneous habitats and environment changes. The climate variability hypothesis suggests that plant populations in high latitudes experience greater environmental fluctuations and may exhibit higher phenotypic plasticity. Wild Camellia japonica is an important but small number of species, which is mainly distributed in warm-temperature and subtropical zones of China, yet it is not clear how its different populations have adapted to environmental changes.MethodsIn our study, wild C. japonica from both zones were subjected to three soil moisture levels and two light intensity levels in the common garden experiments. We focused on examining the morphological and physiological responses to determine how wild C. japonica populations adapt to heterogeneous habitats and environmental changes through phenotypic plasticity.Key resultsThe experimental results showed that severe drought stress reduced the activity of antioxidant enzymes and damaged the antioxidant enzyme system of subtropical wild C. japonica. In contrast, warm-temperate wild C. japonica performed better under variable soil moisture conditions and had higher phenotypic plasticity to soil moisture, which confirmed the climate variability hypothesis. Meanwhile, the warm-temperate and subtropical wild C. japonica showed similar phenotypic plasticity in response to light intensity, and both could reduce the effects of light deficit by improving its ability to capture sunlight energy. The similar phenotypic plasticity to light intensity might be due to the conservative strategy of subtropical wild C. japonica to avoid resource wastage.ConclusionsOur study suggests that conservation efforts for wild C. japonica should account for the response mechanisms of their different populations to environmental changes, providing differentiated conservation strategies tailored to each population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07070-z.Keywords: Climate variability hypothesis, Phenotypic plasticity, Soil moisture, Light intensity

Introduction

Research on the response mechanisms of species to environmental changes is crucial for predicting their future distribution and population dynamics, as well as for their conservation [1, 2]. Although some species often have limited distribution ranges and populations, they may be found across various climatic zones or have distribution areas that span particular latitudinal ranges [3, 4]. Plants have the capability to modify their morpho-physiological, bio-chemical, phenological and behavior traits in changing environment [5, 6] and also exhibit the plastic responses to environmental conditions with the ample flexibility [7, 8]. During these processes plants produce variable characteristics which benefit the plant performance with varying climatic conditions [9, 10]. However, prior studies on the responses of these species to environmental changes have typically focused on individual populations, with sampling primarily confined to specific climatic zones or latitudes [3, 11]. Given the common intraspecific variation in plant species [4, 12], the response mechanisms of geographically diverse populations to environmental changes may differ. Utilizing a single population of these species as a representative for studying its response mechanisms may not yield universally applicable conclusions.

Phenotypic plasticity is one of the mechanisms that often underpin plant populations’ adaptation to spatially heterogeneous environmental conditions [13]. Phenotypic plasticity can enhance plant performance and broaden ecological niche breadth across diverse environmental conditions [13, 14]. The phenotypic plasticity alters the interaction between organism and the biotic and abiotic factors of its environment [15]. Intraspecific variations in plant functional traits may help a species to adapt in diverse abiotic and biotic environments [16–18], affect species coexistence [19, 20] and alter ecosystem processes [21–23]. Observations of certain phenotypic traits suggest that phenotypic plasticity serves as a “stepping stone” for organisms, allowing them to rapidly acquire optimal phenotypes for survival before adaptive genetics and phenotypes evolve in the environment [24, 25]. Therefore, the ability of plant populations to colonize and persist in a wide range of environmental conditions may stem from phenotypic plasticity [26]. As some studies have found that invasive plants can adapt to the introduced environment through phenotypic plasticity and spread further [27, 28], whereas there are few studies on whether non-invasive plants can adapt to environmental changes through phenotypic plasticity. If local environmental conditions change and these plants are unable to adapt to this change through phenotypic plasticity, then this is likely to be one of the reasons for their limited numbers.

It is predicted that by the end of the 21 st century, average global precipitation will significantly decrease, exacerbating drought levels [29, 30]. Drought stress can severely impact plants, but they can adapt by altering their physiological and ecological traits [31, 32]. Drought increases the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which induce damage to DNA and proteins and disrupt plant cell membrane structure and function [31, 33]. However, plants have evolved complex strategies to cope with water shortages, including enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms, to mitigate the harmful effects of ROS [31, 32]. Nonetheless, the current increase in drought events continues to cause forest diebacks [34].

There is significant spatial heterogeneity in solar irradiation patterns at different latitudes due to the Earth’s axial tilt [35]. Human disturbance of natural ecosystems has increased habitat fragmentation and light heterogeneity for both plant and animal habitats [35, 36]. The light intensity in plant habitats can affect their reproduction, growth, and regeneration, while physioecological and morphological changes in plants drive their adaptation to varying light conditions [35, 37]. Typically, light-demanding species exhibit higher photosynthetic activity, thicker root systems, and longer branches, whereas shade-tolerant species enlarge their leaves, increase chlorophyll content, and lower their light compensation point to balance reduced photosynthesis [37]. However, it remains unclear how plant populations from different latitudes, through phenotypic plasticity, adapt to variations in precipitation and light intensity.

Precipitation variability and light heterogeneity are critical factors influencing global environmental changes [34, 38]. According to the climate variability hypothesis, plant populations in high latitudes experience greater environmental fluctuations and may exhibit higher phenotypic plasticity [39, 40]. As latitude increases, the range of climatic variability expands, suggesting that populations at higher latitudes possess a broader adaptive capacity to shifting environmental conditions [41].

Camellia japonica, an evergreen broad-leaved shrub, is recognized as one of China’s top ten traditional flowers, valued for its ornamental, medicinal, and economic benefits [11, 42]. Its distribution is mainly in China (restricted to Zhejiang and Shandong provinces), Japan (on Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu Islands), and along the southern coast of the Korean Peninsula [4, 11]. Zhoushan City in Zhejiang Province, characterized by a subtropical climate, serves as the natural population center of wild in China [11]. In Zhejiang Province, there are more than 300,000 wild C. japonica, with a distribution area of 220 hm2, but the number of natural populations is small [43]. In Shandong Province, featuring a warm-temperate climate, wild C. japonica is exclusively found on a few islands in Qingdao City, marking the northernmost point of its distribution in China [4, 44]. Due to geographic limitations and habitat modification, extensive natural populations of warm-temperate wild C. japonica have vanished, reducing their numbers to 506 individuals on the islands of Qingdao, China. With the intensifying global climate change, these extremely small natural populations of wild C. japonica face a high risk of decline and potential extinction. Therefore, exploring the response mechanisms of wild C. japonica populations from different latitudes to global environmental changes is crucial for conservation strategies.

Most previous studies on wild C. japonica have focused on populations at the same latitude [11], and there is a paucity of comparative research on the response mechanisms of wild C. japonica to variations in soil moisture and light intensity across different latitudes. In this study, we conducted two separate common garden experiments with varying soil moisture and light levels, presuming the phenotypic differences between wild C. japonica populations from warm-temperate and subtropical zones, the only two distribution climatic zones. Wild C. japonica from warm-temperate and subtropical zones may experience different precipitation and solar irradiation levels in the space of different original latitudes. It remains uncertain whether populations from different latitudes have developed phenotypic plasticity to variations in soil moisture and light intensity. Therefore, we measured physiological and morphological parameters associated with their acclimatization to different soil moisture and light intensity levels to test the following hypotheses: (i) severe drought and low light inhibit the physiological and morphological performance of warm-temperate and subtropical wild C. japonica; (ii) warm-temperate wild C. japonica exhibits higher phenotypic plasticity to soil moisture and light intensity. This is because, based on the climate variability hypothesis, plant population from the warm-temperate zone may experience greater environmental fluctuations and thus exhibit higher phenotypic plasticity.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and study sites

The experiment was conducted at Qingdao Agricultural University, located in Qingdao, China (36°31’N, 120°39’E). The region experiences a warm-temperate monsoon climate, with an annual average temperature of approximately 12.7 °C, mean annual precipitation of around 821.8 mm, and an average yearly sunshine duration of about 2,541.1 h. From July to October 2017, we carried out a 70-day experiment in a greenhouse maintained at a mean temperature of 23.14 ± 0.04 °C and a relative humidity of 82.26 ± 0.10%, as measured using a digital display humidity thermometer, model DL-WS20 (Hangzhou Gsome Technology Co., China).

In October 2016, mature seeds of wild C. japonica were collected in sufficient quantities from Changmenyan Island in Qingdao City, Shandong Province within the warm-temperate zone (W), and Daqing Mountain in Putuo District, Zhoushan City, Zhejiang Province within the subtropical zone (S) (Table S1). To capture within-population genetic variation, seeds were systematically collected from at least 20 spatially separated maternal plants spaced a minimum of 10 m apart within each population, ensuring representation of different genotypes within populations. The seeds were stored at approximately 4 °C. We obtained monthly precipitation data (Figure S1) from the Bureau of Statistics of Zhejiang and Shandong Provinces (https://tjj.zj.gov.cn/ and http://tjj.shandong.gov.cn/) and monthly solar irradiation data (Figure S2), specifically the monthly beam irradiation on a plane always normal to the sun rays from the Photovoltaic Geographical Information System (PVGIS) (https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/photovoltaic-geographical-information-system-pvgis_en) for Zhoushan and Qingdao from 2005 to 2020. Since the precise solar irradiation data from the Daqingshan sampling site in Zhoushan City are not available online, we used solar irradiation data from a nearby location (30°26′N; 122°32′E). Additionally, we calculated the coefficient of variation to assess the dispersion of precipitation and solar irradiation in Qingdao and Zhoushan using the formula: coefficient of variation = standard deviation/mean value.

In December 2016, we selected fully mature seeds and disinfected them with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 0.5 h. The seeds were then soaked in sterile deionized water for 24 h, and subsequently buried in wet sand. During their burial, we maintained seed germination by spraying the sand daily with sterile deionized water to retain moisture, stirring the sand to ensure ventilation, and controlling the temperature at approximately 20 °C. By March 2017, we selected seeds that had germinated with embryonic roots exceeding 4 cm in length and transplanted them into plastic pots. Each germinated seed of wild C. japonica was planted in the center of a plastic pot. The pots used in this study had a volume of approximately 0.8 L, measuring 11.0 cm in height and 14.0 cm in diameter, and were filled with about 1 kg of soil comprised of humus and loam mixed in a 1:1 volume ratio. We maintained adequate moisture by ensuring the soil was at approximately 80% of its field water capacity, facilitating proper seedling emergence. Standard practices for weeding and pest control were followed, and the greenhouse was sufficiently ventilated. We measured the plant heights of all seedlings (average height 6.4 ± 0.2 cm) to confirm there were no significant differences before initiating all treatments, ensuring that each seedling had developed 2 ~ 3 leaves.

Experiment 1: different soil moisture levels

On July 5th, 2017, we initiated the experiment. Prior to starting, watering was halted for seven days to reduce the field water capacity (FWC) to approximately 30%. We established three distinct soil moisture levels to simulate different plant water conditions: 75% FWC served as the wetting treatment (WT), suitable for plant cultivation; 55% FWC represented mild drought stress (MD), potentially causing mild water deficiency; and 35% FWC was used for severe drought stress (SD), which may lead to significant water deficiency in plants. Each of the two latitudinal zones (warm-temperate zone, W; subtropical zone, S) and three soil moisture levels (WT, MD, and SD) had seven replicates (n = 7), totaling 42 pots. The water treatment was maintained for 70 days, with water added to the plastic pots daily to compensate for evaporation losses. Throughout the experiment, pots containing soil were weighed every day at 17:00 and watered to keep their weight constant, thus ensuring that all seedlings maintained different constant soil moisture levels [45].

Experiment 2: different light intensity levels

Prior to the experiment, we measured natural sunlight (NS) in an open area from 7:00 am to 4:00 pm on a sunny, cloudless day, with intensity ranging from 321.5 ± 7.5 to 1244.5 ± 11.5 µmol·m−2·s−1. Meanwhile, we measured the light intensity in the forest gap or edge, and understory of Changmenyan Island in Qingdao City, Shandong Province within the warm-temperate zone (W), and Daqing Mountain in Putuo District, Zhoushan City, Zhejiang Province within the subtropical zone (S). We calculated the mean and set two light intensity levels: HL, 50% NS representing the forest gap or edge; and LL, 10% NS representing the understory. Based on previous studies, low light intensity was achieved by covering the saplings with black shade netting over and around the wild C. japonica seedlings [46]. In total, we established 28 experimental pots, with seedlings from two latitudinal zones (warm-temperate zone, W; subtropical zone, S) exposed to two light intensity levels (low light intensity, LL; high light intensity, HL). Each treatment was replicated seven times (n = 7). Water was added daily at around 6:00 pm, totaling 70 applications.

Throughout the experiment, standard measures for weeding and pest control were implemented, and the greenhouse was adequately ventilated. To prevent mutual shading, we maintained a specific distance between plants and randomly rotated the placement of pots weekly to minimize potential environmental variation effects during the experiment. These procedures were applied across all treatments.

Harvest and measurements

At the conclusion of the experiment, plant height (H) was measured. Crown area (CA) was calculated as CA = πab, where a and b are the lengths of the diagonals [47]. Following previous methodologies, chlorophyll concentration was determined using a UV-2100 spectrophotometer (Unico, Shanghai, China), and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were assessed with a Pocket PEA (Hansatech Instruments Ltd., UK) [48]. Two healthy, fully expanded leaves from the upper shoot of each plant per treatment were collected to measure total chlorophyll concentration (Chl), maximum fluorescence value (Fm), and variable fluorescence value (Fv). The maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm) of PSII was then calculated [48].

Gas exchange parameters were recorded using a portable leaf gas exchange system (GFS-3000; WalzGmbH, Effeltrich, Germany). Illumination was provided by a red-blue light-emitting diode set at an irradiance of 2000 µmol·m−2·s−1 (PAR) to ensure saturation of the seedlings [47]. Airflow through the leaf chamber was maintained at 400 µmol·s−1, chamber temperature at ambient level, and CO2 concentration at 400 µmol·mol−1 (Ca). Measurements included net photosynthetic rate (A) and transpiration rate (E), while water use efficiency (WUE) was calculated as A/E [47]. Three healthy, fully expanded leaves from the upper shoot of three seedlings were used for each treatment to measure these parameters. Superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) activity in seedling leaves were assessed using enzyme kits [49].

Seven pots per treatment were harvested and plants were thoroughly washed with tap water, then separated into stems, leaves, and roots. Leaf area (LA) for all plants was measured using a scanner (Epson Perfection V700; Seiko Epson, Japan) and calculated with an image analyzer (Image Pro Plus Version 4.5; Media Cybernetics, Inc., Silver Spring, MD, USA). Fresh leaf weight (LFW) was recorded, followed by soaking the leaves in deionized water for 8 h until full turgidity. Leaves were weighed again after surface moisture removal, then immersed in water for an additional hour until the weight stabilized, to determine the leaf turgid weight (LTW). Dry weight of plant parts was determined following drying in an oven at 100 °C for 48 h as leaf (LDW), stem (SDW), and root dry weight (RDW). Leaf relative water content (RWC) of each sample was calculated according to the referenced formula [34].

The specific leaf area (SLA; LA/LDW), shoot biomass (SB; SDW + LDW), total biomass (TB; SB + RDW), and root-to-shoot ratio (RSR; RDW/SB) were subsequently calculated [48]. However, due to the loss of root biomass materials under different light intensity levels, TB and RSR were assessed only at the soil moisture levels.

We categorized the functional traits of wild C. japonica into morphological and physiological parameters. The morphological parameters included plant height (H), leaf area (LA), and crown area (CA). The physiological parameters measured were specific leaf area (SLA), total chlorophyll concentration (Chl), maximum photochemical efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm), leaf relative water content (RWC), net photosynthetic rate (A), transpiration rate (E), water use efficiency (WUE), and enzymatic activities including peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT).

Statistical analyses

To assess differences in plant traits among various treatments, we conducted a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). To evaluate the impact of environmental context on the adaptability of plants from different latitudes to soil moisture and light intensity, we collected monthly precipitation and solar irradiation data from Qingdao and Zhoushan cities spanning 2005 to 2020. We calculated the coefficients of variation for precipitation and solar irradiation over these years and conducted a paired t-test to quantitatively assess the environmental heterogeneity across latitudes. Before performing the ANOVA and t-test, normality and variance homogeneity tests were conducted for each data group, and uneven distributions were transformed. Duncan’s test was applied with a significance level of α ≤ 0.05. All analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 software package (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

To compare the phenotypic plasticity of wild C. japonica from warm-temperate and subtropical zones, simplified relative distance phenotypic plasticity indices (RDPIs) were calculated only for traits that responded significantly to the interaction of latitude and environment as the relative distances among mean phenotypic values for each species-environment combination, divided by the total number of distances. Only parameters from WT and MD treatments were selected to calculate the RDPIs to avoid stress-based distortions in seedlings acclimated to precipitation at different latitudes. The RDPIs range from 0 (no plasticity) to 1 (maximum plasticity).

Redundancy analysis (RDA) was used to identify environmental variables correlated with changes in plant functional traits. We created Hellinger-transformed plot-by-plant functional trait matrices, including TB, RSR, H, LA, CA, SLA, Chl, Fv/Fm, RWC, A, E, WUE, POD, SOD, and CAT data (TB and RSR were included only for soil moisture level analyses). The full environmental matrix comprised variables such as soil moisture or light intensity and latitude. We quantified the contribution of environmental factors, latitude, and their interactions using the rdacca.hp function. All analyses were performed using the R software program (R Core Team 2023).

Results

Responses of wild C. japonica to different soil moisture levels

Results from the two-way ANOVA indicated that no significant differences in morphological parameters were observed among plants throughout the experiment (Table 1). Latitude did not significantly influence any functional traits of wild C. japonica (Table 1). However, soil moisture significantly affected WUE, with the highest value (6.10 ± 0.12 g kg −1) observed in the WT treatment within the subtropical zone (Table S2; Fig. 1D). Additionally, all other parameters were unaffected by varying soil moisture levels (Table 1). The interaction of latitude and soil moisture significantly affected on RSR, Chl, RWC, WUE, POD, and SOD (Table 1). For instance, RSR and RWC in the subtropical zone and Chl in the warm-temperate zone declined with increased drought (Fig. 1A-C). WUE and POD were notably highest under the WT treatment in the subtropical zone (Table 1, Fig. 1DE). The response of SOD to drought varied by latitude; it decreased in the subtropical zone with increased water, while it remained constant in the warm-temperate zone across different soil moisture levels (Table 1; Fig. 1F). Other physiological parameters, including TB, SLA, Fv/Fm, A, E, and CAT, were not significantly altered by this interaction (Table 1).

Responses of wild C. japonica to different light intensity levels

The two-way ANOVA revealed no significant differences in morphological parameters (Table 1). Compared to the warm-temperate zone, the Fv/Fm was significantly reduced in the subtropical zone (Table S2). Other physiological parameters were not significantly affected by latitude (Table 1). Variations in light intensity resulted in some physiological differences. Light intensity levels significantly influenced SLA, A, WUE, and SOD (Table 1). As light intensity increased, the SLA decreased in both the warm-temperate and subtropical zones (Fig. 4A). As light intensity increased, A in the warm-temperate zone and SOD in the subtropical zone increased (Fig. 4BD). WUE was higher under the HL treatment compared to the LL treatment (Fig. 4C). There were no significant differences in other parameters (Table 1). Additionally, the interaction between latitude and light intensity exerted negligible effects on all morphological and physiological parameters (Table 1).

Discussion

For the conservation of species, it is crucial to focus on their response mechanisms to environmental changes. Previous research has predominantly concentrated on singular populations, often neglecting that such species may be sporadically distributed across diverse geographical and climatic ranges [3, 4, 11]. This distribution increases the likelihood of phenotypic differentiation among populations due to varied environmental and climatic conditions [4, 12]. Therefore, conducting comprehensive studies that encompass all distribution areas of species is more effective for their conservation. In this study, we examined wild C. japonica across all climatic zones of its distribution. The phenotypic plasticity of warm-temperate populations in response to soil moisture was observed to be greater than that of the subtropical populations, and the phenotypic plasticity of light intensity was similar in both. This heightened plasticity in response to soil moisture supports the climate variability hypothesis, however, the response to light intensity did not align with this hypothesis. Furthermore, the warm-temperate populations exhibited greater drought tolerance compared to the subtropical populations. Both populations demonstrated an ability to acclimate to low-light environments by enhancing their photosynthetic capture capacity.

Phenotypic responses of wild C. japonica to different soil moisture levels

We observed that the acclimation strategies to water-scarce environments differed between warm-temperate and subtropical wild C. japonica. Drought is a pervasive natural phenomenon that impedes plant growth and development [50]. Contrary to previous studies [51], our study reported no significant effects on biomass accumulation and morphological parameters under varying soil moisture levels (Table 1). Additionally, as drought intensity increased, the RSR, RWC, and WUE of wild C. japonica from the subtropical zone decreased, while those from the warm-temperate zone remained unaffected (Fig. 1). Maintaining optimal water content is crucial for normal cell function and physiological activities in plants [52]. Thus, subtropical wild C. japonica invests more in root biomass to absorb soil moisture in response to drought stress. However, as drought intensifies, root water uptake becomes insufficient to maintain leaf tissue water content and normal physiological functions, leading to a decline in WUE. Similar findings were reported by Sapes et al., who found that RWC decreased with heightened drought stress in Pinus ponderosa populations, underscoring RWC as an accurate predictor of drought risk [53].

Drought disrupts the delicate balance of ROS and antioxidant defenses in plants [54]. Elevated cellular ROS levels, such as superoxide radicals (O2−), hydroxyl radicals (-OH), and H2O2, result in membrane lipid peroxidation [55]. To counteract the harmful effects of excessive ROS, plants have developed intricate enzymatic antioxidant defense systems to maintain intracellular redox homeostasis [31, 33]. SOD, POD, and CAT are key antioxidant enzymes that scavenge ROS, with SOD initially converting O2− into H2O2, which is subsequently decomposed by POD and CAT [55]. In our study, as water availability decreased, POD, SOD, and CAT activities in the warm-temperate zone were unaffected, while POD in the subtropical zone significantly decreased, and SOD activity initially increased before declining (Fig. 1). This suggests that mild drought stress can enhance the antioxidant system’s enzyme activity, effectively scavenging ROS. However, as drought stress intensifies beyond the subtropical wild C. japonica’s tolerance, ROS production surpasses cellular scavenging capacity, damaging the antioxidant system and reducing enzyme activities.

Overall, drought had a neutral effect on warm-temperate wild C. japonica, indicating its better adaptation to water-scarce conditions. In contrast, mild drought may stimulate subtropical wild C. japonica growth, but severe drought could severely impair its growth due to an inability to adequately remove ROS, potentially leading to growth collapse.

Phenotypic plasticity, the capacity of a genotype to generate multiple phenotypes in different environments, is a critical mechanism enabling plants to adapt to climate and other global changes [24, 56]. Our study found that annual precipitation fluctuations in Qingdao City, Shandong Province, were significantly higher than in Zhoushan City, Zhejiang Province, between 2005 and 2020 (Fig. 2B). Previous research suggests that climate variability selectively enhances phenotypic plasticity, with a potential positive correlation between plasticity and precipitation variability [41, 57], which is consistent with our findings. Our findings indicate that the interaction of latitude and soil moisture had a significant effect on six parameters such as RSR, Chl, RWC, WUE, POD, and SOD (Table 1), with subtropical wild C. japonica exhibiting greater phenotypic plasticity in RWC and SOD, compared to warm-temperate wild C. japonica, which demonstrated higher plasticity in RSR, WUE, and POD (Fig. 2A). This supports the climate variability hypothesis, which posits that variable climatic conditions enable broader morphological and physiological variation, resulting in increased phenotypic plasticity among plants [25, 58].

The warm-temperate wild C. japonica represents a distinct ecotype and includes the world’s northernmost populations of wild C. japonica [11]. However, the unique climatic conditions of the Yellow Sea in China limit this ecotype’s distribution to a few islands in the area [11]. A study on the genetic diversity of C. japonica (Theaceae) detected by inter-simple sequence repeat (ISSR) found that the percentage of polymorphic bands in the C. japonica population from Changmenyan Island in Qingdao City, Shandong Province was 71.09%, which was higher than that of 66.35% in Putuo District, Zhoushan City, Zhejiang Province District [4]. Therefore, we assume that the higher phenotypic plasticity to soil moisture observed in warm-temperate wild C. japonica may be attributed to its greater genetic diversity compared with other natural C. japonica populations [4]. Increased genetic diversity suggests a broader range of genotypes within the warm-temperate populations, enhancing its ability to adapt to environmental changes and various stresses, thus allowing superior performance across different soil moisture levels.

Phenotypic responses of wild C. japonica to different light intensity levels

Light is a vital source of energy for photosynthesis, but both excessive and insufficient light can negatively impact photosynthesis [46, 54]. In our study, the responses of warm-temperate and subtropical wild C. japonica to variations in light intensity were similar. We observed that excessively low light intensity reduced the A and WUE in wild C. japonica from both zones (Fig. 4). Consequently, in response to low-light environments, specific physiological parameters of wild C. japonica adjusted. SLA is closely associated with photosynthesis and play critical roles in light absorption, transmission, and conversion [59]. In our study, shading significantly increased SLA in leaves (Fig. 4), indicating that plants mitigate the adverse effects of light shortages on wild C. japonica growth by boosting their light-trapping capacities.

Under strong light conditions, plants can generate more ROS due to ultraviolet exposure [38]. Antioxidant enzyme activities, including SOD, POD, and CAT, can indicate the plant’s tolerance to intense light stress [54]. We observed that SOD activity in wild C. japonica increased with rising light intensity (Fig. 4), effectively scavenging ROS within cells and mitigating the detrimental effects of intense light. These results indicate that low light intensity (LL, 10%NS) hinders photosynthesis, adversely affecting the growth of wild C. japonica from both warm-temperate and subtropical zones. Therefore, high light intensity (HL, 50%NS) is more suitable for the growth of wild C. japonica in both zones.

Light is a crucial ecological factor influencing plant reproduction, growth, development, and renewal [37, 38]. We initially hypothesized that warm-temperate zones at higher latitudes would experience more significant fluctuations in solar irradiation, leading to higher phenotypic plasticity in warm-temperate wild C. japonica. However, our data revealed that annual solar irradiation fluctuations were actually greater in Zhoushan City, Zhejiang Province, at lower latitudes, compared to Qingdao City, Shandong Province, from 2005 to 2020 (Fig. S3). Nonetheless, in our experiments, the interaction of latitude and light intensity did not significantly affect any of the parameters (Table 1), and warm-temperate and subtropical C. japonica populations had similar phenotypic plasticity in response to light intensity, which does not align with the climate variability hypothesis.

Studies suggest that under stress, plants often adopt resource-conservative strategies associated with reduced phenotypic plasticity [40]. It is documented that low water availability or cold temperatures can limit plastic responses to light intensity [58, 60]. However, in our light intensity experiments, both soil moisture and temperature conditions were suitable for wild C. japonica growth, indicating that the low phenotypic plasticity observed in subtropical wild C. japonica regarding light intensity was not due to drought or cold stress. We propose another reason for the similar phenotypic plasticity of warm-temperate and subtropical C. japonica populations to light intensity fluctuations. Assessing the costs and benefits of adaptation to light environments, subtropical wild C. japonica might have employed a resource-conservative strategy. Research has shown that observed plasticity is often lower than anticipated, suggesting inherent costs and limitations to plasticity [40]. Although increased plasticity often enhances adaptation to varying environments, limited or absent plasticity may be advantageous when environmental changes are unpredictable, or when there is no beneficial phenotypic response to environmental challenges [40, 58]. In subtropical wild C. japonica, low phenotypic plasticity in response to light intensity might not enhance light capture capabilities but could help prevent or reduce resource depletion from unnecessary elongation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, unlike previous studies that focused solely on the response of a single population of wild C. japonica to environmental changes, our study encompassed populations from all climatic zones within the species’ distribution. We explored its phenotypic plasticity to environmental changes. Our findings indicate that examining only one population’s response is not adequately representative of the species’ overall response, as reactions to environmental changes vary significantly across different climatic zones. The warm-temperate wild C. japonica showed greater phenotypic plasticity to soil moisture, which further supports the climate variability hypothesis, whereas the warm-temperate and subtropical wild C. japonica showed similar phenotypic plasticity in response to light intensity, which is not consistent with the predictions of the climate variability hypothesis. Additionally, our study found light intensity explained only 16.75% of variation in the functional traits of wild C. japonica, while soil moisture levels explained just 7.02%. This suggests other environmental factors, besides soil moisture, might drive the natural selection of spatial heterogeneity in these populations. Future research should focus on identifying these additional environmental influences. Warm-temperate wild C. japonica populations displayed more tolerance to severe drought compared to their subtropical ones; however, both adapted to low-light environments by enhancing photosynthetic capture capacity. Despite the higher phenotypic plasticity of warm-temperate populations to soil moisture, they consist of only 506 trees [11], suggesting a specific adaptation to the environmental factor and emphasizing the need for prioritizing their conservation. We recommend establishing protected areas for in-situ conservation and collecting germplasm resources, particularly for warm-temperate populations. Our study, encompassing all climatic zones of wild C. japonica distribution, is crucial for the differentiated conservation of species across various climatic zones. However, intrapopulation variation in warm-temperate and subtropical populations was not considered in our study, so future studies should be rewarded for further exploring differences in phenotypic plasticity among individuals of different genotypes within populations.
