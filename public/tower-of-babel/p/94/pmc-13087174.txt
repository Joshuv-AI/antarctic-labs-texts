A school-based respect-for-life program integrating deceased organ donation awareness in South Korea: a post-class mixed-methods evaluation
Authors: Hee Jung Jeon, Yeong Hoon Kim, Hyun Joo Choi, Ji Yeon Lee, Mi Ae Kam, Jin Hyun Lee, Sumin Hyun, Chunhee Bok, Yoonkyung Lee, Curie Ahn, Jieun Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSouth Korea faces a critical organ shortage, with only 31.2% of families consenting to deceased organ donation following brain death. Traditional public awareness campaigns targeting adults have achieved limited success, suggesting that deeply rooted cultural concerns require more fundamental intervention through youth education. We developed and implemented a values-based education program that integrated deceased organ donation awareness within broader life-respect education. Between November 2023 and July 2024, the program reached 2333 middle and high school students across 8 schools in Seoul, Gyeonggi, and Busan regions. In the post-class evaluation, 1543 students (66% response rate) completed surveys, with 1174 providing open-ended responses (76%). Qualitative analysis employed dual large language models (ChatGPT and Google Gemini) for thematic analysis and contextual sentiment classification. The curriculum achieved high engagement, with 72.1% of students rating it highly helpful and 89.2% reporting increased appreciation for life’s value. Thematic analysis identified seven major themes including rediscovery of life’s intrinsic value, enhanced self-worth and empathy, suicide prevention awareness, and positive attitudes toward deceased organ donation. Sentiment analysis showed predominantly positive reception, with 86% (ChatGPT) and 87% (Gemini) of responses classified as positive (97.53% inter-model agreement). This implementation study suggests that values-based education cultivating adolescents’ appreciation for life’s interconnection may provide a promising foundation for deceased organ donation awareness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41727-5.Keywords: Respect for life, School education, Deceased organ donation awareness, Empathy, Interconnection, South KoreaSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Background and context

Organ transplantation is a life-saving treatment for patients with end-stage organ failure. According to the Korean Network for Organ Sharing (KONOS) 2023 Annual Statistical Report1, South Korea performed 4044 organ transplants, of which 2338 (57.8%) were from living donors and 1706 (42.2%) were from 483 deceased donors. South Korea’s organ transplantation system is heavily reliant on living donors. Changes in population structure and household composition have resulted in declining living donor numbers, with a 5.3% decrease from the previous year. According to the 2023 International Registry in Organ Donation and Transplantation (IRODaT) statistics2, South Korea’s actual deceased donation rate stands at 9.32 per million population (PMP), placing the country in the lower-to-middle tier globally. The registered organ donation intention rate among adults aged 20 and older remains low at 4.07%.

The disparity between organ availability and the number of patients in need is stark. As of 2023, a total of 43,421 patients were waiting for organ transplants, and approximately 2900 patients died annually while awaiting transplantation, averaging 8 deaths per day. While South Korea’s transplantation outcomes are internationally recognized, the system is fundamentally dependent on organ availability. Increasing deceased organ donation has therefore emerged as a critical priority.

The primary barrier to organ donation following brain death in South Korea is family refusal. When a family member receives a brain death diagnosis, the remaining family members must make a critical decision under difficult circumstances. Misunderstandings about brain death, concerns about the dignity of the deceased, and negative perceptions about organ donation contribute to refusal rates. According to the Korean Organ Donation Agency (KODA) 2023 Annual Report3, the family consent rate stands at only 31.2%, representing the largest drop-off point in the process from presumed brain death notification to actual deceased organ donation.

To address this critical public health challenge, a comprehensive educational approach to improve public awareness of brain death and organ donation is essential. This educational approach should cultivate a broader understanding of respect for life, interconnection, and compassion, within which accurate information about brain death and organ donation is meaningfully situated.

Vitallink’s educational initiative

In response to this need, Vitallink (a non-profit organization dedicated to promoting deceased organ donation awareness)4 collaborated with educators from elementary, middle, and high schools to develop a comprehensive youth education curriculum. Rather than focusing narrowly on brain death or organ donation, the program explores broader themes of respect for life, empathy, and interconnection with others and the natural world.

The curriculum employs diverse pedagogical methods including storytelling, group discussions, multimedia experiences, and reflective activities. Students are encouraged to contemplate the value of their own lives and the lives of others, recognize their interconnectedness, and understand organ donation as one expression of life-saving. This paper presents the development, implementation, and evaluation of this educational program, with particular emphasis on its observed outcomes in promoting youth awareness of respect for life and deceased organ donation.

Curriculum development process

To improve awareness of deceased organ donation, Vitallink established a network with elementary, middle, and high school teachers and completed an educational workbook titled “Seeds of Hope, Fruits of Sharing” in March 2021. The first half of this textbook addresses the concept of sharing, while the second half covers life-sharing related to organ donation after brain death. However, simply providing teachers with the developed workbook was insufficient to facilitate implementation in schools, and it was challenging to secure class time in actual school settings.

While grappling with this issue, Vitallink and school teachers developed a new curriculum that would allow implementation by allocating time from the legally mandated “Life Respect and Suicide Prevention Education.” The curriculum was designed to enable students to engage in deep reflection on the meaning of life through class participation.

Curriculum overview and pedagogical structure

Educational approach and pedagogical principles

The curriculum developers structured the course to progressively broaden students’ perspectives—from self → us → society and nature → practicing sharing—enabling students to learn to love themselves, empathize with others, and practice consideration in their lives.

The educational objectives are to enable students to: (1) realize the meaning of life and cultivate self-love, (2) understand the value of coexistence by recognizing the interconnection of all life—self, others, and nature, (3) appreciate the preciousness of life, and (4) comprehend the significance of deceased organ donation as one meaningful expression of life-sharing and continuity.

Implementation of the educational program

Specialized educator teams implement the curriculum. In Seoul, a dedicated team of respect for life education specialists delivers the program. In Busan, retired master teachers1 lead the instruction.

The target population for this educational program was middle and high school students in Seoul, Gyeonggi, and Busan regions. The program comprised two 45-minute sessions. Middle school students participated in small-group activities, forming teams of 5–6 students per class. This format facilitated peer discussion and collaborative task completion, emphasizing experiential learning of respect for life and sharing values. High schools adopted flexible delivery formats to accommodate college preparation schedules. Some schools conducted grade-level sessions in auditoriums, while others utilized school broadcasting systems to reach the entire student body. The program employed experience-centered and collaborative learning methods. Instructors asked questions, presented video materials and real-world cases, then facilitated student engagement through discussions and reflective writing.

Session structure and activities

Session 1: Exploring the Meaning of Life.

Learning objectives Students recognize the rarity and wonder of existence, explore life’s transformation and cycles, and understand their role in the larger web of life.

Opening activity Students explored the probability of life’s emergence through quiz activities on the lifespans of various organisms, understanding life’s diversity and finite nature.

Exploration of the meaning of life Through documentary footage of butterfly metamorphosis, students examined life’s natural progression: “Life blooms—grows—continues.” Students reflected on their own birth (miraculous), growth (supported by many), and the reality that while individual life is finite, it connects to and continues through other lives. Guided questions prompted consideration of “What does it mean to be alive?” and “What can we do with the life we have?” Through peer discussion, students recognized that life enables diverse contributions—including saving other lives through words, actions, and generosity.

Energy ping-pong activity Students drew self-portraits and listed three personal strengths. Taking turns, each student drew lines to share their strengths with peers who would benefit from them, ensuring all students received support. Students could also request specific strengths they needed from others. This activity showed how giving and receiving strengths creates collective wellbeing, broadening students’ concept of sharing to include intangible forms: communication, kindness, laughter, and emotional support.

Session 2: Connecting Lives through Empathy and Action.

Learning objectives Students understand how to protect and nurture others’ lives through empathic connection and action, exploring the scientific and social foundations of human interconnection.

Life’s interconnectedness Students examined documentary narratives and developmental psychology video footage demonstrating humans’ innate capacity for empathy. Real-world suicide intervention cases illustrated that individuals exist within networks of mutual support, emphasizing the importance of extending care to others.

Defining “The Touch of Life” Activity Through guided discussion, students explored various manifestations of life-preserving actions including verbal compassion, life-protecting behaviors, life-sustaining environments, life-saving professions, blood donation, and organ donation after brain death.

Brain death organ donation education Documentary footage depicting a young accident victim who saved lives through organ donation provided emotional engagement. Scientific education addressed the medical definition of brain death, distinctions from persistent vegetative state, and information about transplantable organs and tissues, combining affective and cognitive learning.

Reflective writing: learn-feel-act Students concluded through reflective writing addressing three dimensions: what they learned; what they felt; and what small actions they commit to undertaking. This activity facilitated integration of cognitive understanding with personal commitment to prosocial action.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study implemented education on respect for life among 2333 middle and high school students from Seoul, Gyeonggi, and Busan regions between November 2023 and July 2024. 1543 students (66% response rate) completed post-class surveys. Because the survey was conducted during inter-class breaks, students who needed to move to another classroom immediately after class or faced time constraints were unable to participate.

Post-education survey administration and data collection

A Google Forms survey was administered immediately after each education session, providing the primary data for this study. Given that most South Korean schools prohibit personal mobile phone use during instructional time, surveys were conducted during brief inter-class breaks. Students accessed the survey either through their personal phones (temporarily distributed) or school-issued Chromebooks via QR codes generated and managed by Vitallink, ensuring that response collection occurred independently of the instructors who delivered the program.

Survey instrument

The survey comprised five items. Items 1–4 employed multiple-choice formats, while Item 5 was open-ended to capture students’ post-class reflections.

Analytical approach

Quantitative analysis Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, frequency distributions, and cross-tabulation to examine response patterns across demographic groups and survey items.

Qualitative analysis Qualitative analysis of the 1174 open-ended responses employed a dual analytical framework utilizing advanced large language models (LLMs): ChatGPT (GPT-5.1)5 and Google Gemini (Gemini 3.0)6. These two models were selected because they represented the most widely adopted and publicly accessible generative AI tools for qualitative research at the time of analysis7,8, with no commercial interest influencing this selection. This AI-assisted approach was implemented to enhance analytical rigor and objectivity while managing the large volume of textual data. The qualitative analysis comprised two complementary components: thematic analysis and contextual sentiment analysis.

Thematic analysis An inductive thematic analysis was conducted to identify recurring patterns and dominant themes within students’ written reflections. The analysis proceeded iteratively: responses were examined to extract meaningful segments, which were then coded and grouped into thematic categories based on conceptual similarity. Given that student responses frequently contained multiple distinct ideas, a non-mutually exclusive coding framework was employed, allowing individual responses to be assigned to multiple themes when appropriate. Through this process, seven major themes emerged from the data. The frequency of each theme was quantified as the proportion of the 1,174 responses in which the theme appeared, providing insight into the relative prominence of different learning outcomes.

Contextual Sentiment Analysis To complement the thematic analysis, LLM-based contextual sentiment classification was performed using both ChatGPT-5.1 and Gemini 3.0. Unlike traditional sentiment analysis approaches that rely on keyword frequency, this method evaluated the semantic context and underlying intent of each response to classify them into positive, neutral, or negative categories. Positive responses were characterized by expressions of appreciation, emotional engagement, value internalization, or articulation of specific behavioral commitments. Neutral responses were identified as descriptive, ambiguous, or non-evaluative statements. Negative responses exhibited dissatisfaction or critical sentiment toward the curriculum. This structured framework enabled detection of subtle nuances in students’ subjective experiences, such as cognitive shifts in their understanding of life’s value. Both LLMs performed the classification independently, and inter-model agreement was assessed to evaluate the reliability and consistency of the sentiment classification results.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kangdong Sacred Heart Hospital (Protocol No. KANGDONG 2025-12-010). The study followed ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The requirement for informed consent (including parental/legal guardian consent for minors) was waived by the IRB. The waiver was granted because the research involved the analysis of anonymized survey data collected as part of a routine educational curriculum, and the study presented no more than minimal risk to the subjects.

Results

Participant demographics

The study sample comprised 1543 students from Seoul, Gyeonggi, and Busan regions. The distribution included 987 middle school students (64.0%) and 556 high school students (36.0%). Geographically, participants were evenly distributed between Seoul/Gyeonggi (772 students, 50.0%) and Busan (771 students, 50.0%) regions. Middle school participation was higher in Busan (544 students) compared to Seoul/Gyeonggi (443 students), while high school participation showed the reverse pattern, with 329 students from Seoul/Gyeonggi and 227 from Busan (Supplementary Table 1).

Quantitative findings

Question 1: prior Knowledge of Respect for Life Prior knowledge assessment revealed that 85.9% of students had at least some understanding of respect for life and organ donation before the curriculum, with 44.7% reporting good prior knowledge and 41.2% having some knowledge. Only 14.1% reported limited or no prior knowledge, providing context for evaluating curriculum impact across different baseline levels (Supplementary Table 2).

Question 2: perceived value and impact following education Following the curriculum, 89.2% of students reported heightened awareness of life’s value, with 64.1% strongly feeling the importance of respect for life and organ donation and 25.1% moderately feeling this importance. Only 3.2% reported no perceived impact, indicating the curriculum successfully cultivated meaningful awareness across the majority of participants (Supplementary Table 3).

Question 3: preferred future content topics Students’ preferences for future content reflected diverse interests: methods to increase confidence and self-esteem (47.2%), small practices for respecting life including environmental and animal care (28.7%), and effective communication methods with others (20.9%). These preferences reveal that students view respect for life education as encompassing personal growth, practical action, and relational skills (Supplementary Table 4).

Question 4: instructional effectiveness Instructional effectiveness ratings were highly positive, with 92.8% of students finding the curriculum highly or somewhat helpful in understanding respect for life and organ donation. Only 2.8% found it unhelpful, indicating that the experiential, discussion-based pedagogy successfully engaged students across diverse backgrounds (Supplementary Table 5).

Question 5: Qualitative Analysis of Student Reflections

Of the 1543 respondents, 1174 (76%) provided responses to the open-ended Question 5, suggesting a high level of engagement with the curriculum content.

Major themes identified from student reflections

We conducted a thematic analysis of 1174 open-ended responses. The qualitative analysis was performed using two large language models to identify recurring themes and patterns in student feedback, allowing multiple themes per response, as many students mentioned more than one idea. Both models independently identified seven key themes, which revealed substantial convergence and provided rich insights into students’ learning experiences and attitudinal shifts following the education (Fig. 1).

Thematic distribution of student open-ended responses (n = 1174). Bar chart illustrating the relative frequency (%) of the seven major themes identified through thematic analysis of open-ended post-class responses. Themes reflect the proportion of responses referencing each concept. Percentages represent the proportion of responses in which each theme appeared, with multiple themes coded per response where applicable.

Theme 1: rediscovery of life’s intrinsic value The most dominant theme is a deepened awareness that life is inherently precious and irreplaceable. Students articulated that all life forms possess unique value independent of external measures or comparisons. Critically, students recognized that their own lives are interconnected to and dependent upon other lives. Student reflections frequently emphasized the miraculous nature of existence and the responsibility accompanying the gift of life. This theme directly translated into an intended action: many students expressed commitment to cherishing their own lives more deeply, recognizing life as a precious gift and pledging to live more intentionally.

Theme 2: high engagement and emotional resonance Students consistently reported that the program was engaging, meaningful, and emotionally impactful. The word “fun” appeared frequently in responses, indicating that the educational approach successfully maintained student interest while addressing serious topics. Diverse pedagogical methods—including video content, small group discussions, and writing activities—successfully maintained student engagement. Many students noted that the curriculum provided memorable learning experiences and expressed strong willingness to attend again.

Theme 3: enhanced self-worth and self-compassion A significant proportion of students reported that the curriculum prompted self-reflection and contributed to growth in self-esteem and self-worth, indicating a positive psychological impact related to self-identity and emotional resilience. Students recognized their own unique strengths and contributions to the community and articulated commitment to valuing themselves. This theme connects to pedagogical design elements specifically targeting self-recognition and peer appreciation (the “Energy Ping-pong” activity), suggesting that structured reflection on personal worth effectively supports self-concept development.

Theme 4: expanded empathy and commitment to caring for others Students frequently expressed intentions to demonstrate increased kindness, empathy, and consideration toward others in their daily lives. The responses revealed a deepened understanding of interconnectedness—that individual lives are fundamentally linked to others and that personal well-being is connected to community well-being. Beyond general commitments to helping marginalized classmates and comforting those in distress, students articulated specific behavioral intentions: using warmer language, taking more considerate actions toward friends, actively listening to others’ emotional needs, and proactively supporting those facing difficulties. This integration of empathy and action reflects a meaningful translation of curricular values into concrete interpersonal practices.

Theme 5: suicide prevention awareness and peer support commitment A significant proportion of students reflected on suicide awareness and the importance of intervening for peers in distress, recognizing that small acts of kindness and attention can save lives. Students expressed intention to proactively reach out to friends in crisis. This finding validates integration of the curriculum into mandatory suicide prevention education—the connection between respect for life and suicide prevention awareness is direct and meaningful.

Theme 6: positive attitude toward deceased organ donation Organ donation emerged as a prominent and emotionally resonant topic, with numerous students expressing newly found understanding and positive attitudes toward deceased organ donation. Students demonstrated increased comprehension of brain death, donor shortages, and the life-saving potential of organ donation, frequently citing the fact that one donor can save multiple lives. Importantly, some students who initially expressed fear or hesitation reported a shift toward acceptance. This represents direct evidence that the curriculum successfully addressed a primary educational objective. Several students further expressed willingness to become organ donors after brain death, recognizing organ donation as a noble practice of sharing life.

Theme 7: Philosophical reflection on the meaning of life Some students engaged in deeper existential reflection. They contemplated the difference between “life and survival,” the cycle of nature, and the mystery of being alive. This suggests the program stimulated not only emotional growth but also cognitive and philosophical reflection.

Cross-cutting observations The remarkable consistency between themes identified independently by two language models strengthens confidence in these findings. The themes showed substantial alignment with the program’s educational objectives, and students’ overwhelmingly positive emotional tone indicated successful affective engagement critical for attitude change. Analysis of students’ open-ended responses revealed consistent evidence of intended behavioral changes following the education. Students demonstrated sophisticated understanding of complex concepts and made spontaneous connections between curriculum components—linking life respect to suicide prevention, self-worth to empathy for others, and personal value to willingness to help peers. This integration suggests the program’s holistic approach allowed students to construct coherent frameworks for understanding the value and interconnectedness of life. Education extends beyond mere knowledge transmission to cultivate both emotional connection and behavioral commitment.

Positive reception of the curriculum and key contributing factors

Analysis of students’ open-ended responses (n = 1174) revealed predominantly positive reactions to the curriculum. Independent sentiment classification using ChatGPT 5.1 and Gemini 3.0 yielded consistent results: 86.12% and 87.65% positive responses respectively, with neutral responses comprising 12.86% and 11.07%, and negative responses only 1.02% and 1.28% (Fig. 2). The high inter-model agreement (97.53%, n = 1145) strengthens confidence in these classifications. Most students demonstrated appreciation for the curriculum and reported positive shifts in their understanding of respect for life. Using both language models, we identified four key factors contributing to these positive responses.

Comparison of sentiment analysis results from ChatGPT-5.1 and Google Gemini 3.0 showing the percentage of responses classified as Positive, Neutral, or Negative. Factor 1. engagement and interest Many students described the curriculum as “fun” or “interesting,” suggesting that diverse teaching methods—including videos, quizzes, and group activities—effectively captured students’ attention and facilitated learning.

Factor 2. Emotional Resonance Numerous students reported that the curriculum evoked strong emotions, frequently using terms such as “moved” or “impressed.” This indicates that the content resonated deeply on a personal level and influenced their perspectives.

Factor 3. Expressions of gratitude Many students expressed appreciation for the curriculum and thanked instructors for the valuable lessons learned, supporting the finding that the curriculum positively impacted their understanding of life’s value.

Factor 4. Desire for further learning Some students expressed interest in learning more about the topics covered, with specific requests for additional sessions on respect for life and organ donation. This enthusiasm suggests that the curriculum successfully stimulated curiosity and sustained interest in the subject matter.

Discussion

This study suggests that values-based education may effectively promote youth awareness of respect for life and foster positive attitudes toward deceased organ donation. The curriculum achieved high levels of student engagement, with 72.1% finding it highly helpful and 89.2% reporting increased appreciation for life’s value. A significant finding was that a moderate number of students expressed positive attitudes toward deceased organ donation in their open-ended responses. These findings support the pedagogical principle underlying the curriculum: that effective education on deceased organ donation may emerge from cultivating deep appreciation for life’s inherent worth and interconnection, rather than presenting organ donation as an isolated medical procedure.

Rationale for targeting adolescents

Adolescence represents a critical developmental window during which values and attitudes are consolidated, making this period particularly receptive to values-based education. Although current participants lack legal decision-making authority over organ donation, they will become actual decision-makers as they progress through adulthood. Traditional public awareness campaigns targeting adults have achieved limited success in South Korea, suggesting that deeply rooted attitudes are resistant to change through informational approaches alone. We therefore view adolescent education as a long-term complementary strategy for cultural transformation, rather than a substitute for adult awareness campaigns. The understanding they developed—that life’s preciousness encompasses both protecting life and, when death is inevitable, enabling life’s continuation through others—may ultimately contribute to a cultural shift toward increased family consent.

Implications of qualitative analysis

The qualitative findings suggest that the program operated simultaneously on cognitive, affective, and behavioral levels, aligning with comprehensive models of attitude formation emphasizing that lasting shifts require engagement across major seven themes. The prominence of life’s intrinsic value (68%) as the dominant theme is consistent with our pedagogical sequence of establishing profound appreciation for life before introducing organ donation. That organ donation emerged spontaneously in 14% of responses—despite being embedded within broader curriculum content rather than emphasized as primary objective—suggests genuine conceptual integration rather than superficial compliance. The prominence of self-worth themes suggests an important pathway through which life-respect education may contribute to both suicide prevention and organ donation awareness—by first establishing that one’s own life has intrinsic value; the program creates a foundation for recognizing others’ value. The qualitative analysis highlights the potential importance of pedagogical approach. Students’ positive evaluations emphasizing engaging, accessible, and emotionally resonant delivery suggest that outcomes depend not only on content but also on presentation. The integration of multimedia elements, interactive activities, real-life stories, and skilled facilitation created a learning environment where students felt safe to engage with difficult topics and reflect honestly on their values and experiences.

Theoretical foundations and pedagogical rationale

The program’s design is grounded in converging scientific evidence supporting its developmental appropriateness for adolescent learners.

Adolescence as a critical period for values formation

The adolescent brain undergoes profound development in prefrontal cortex regions responsible for identity formation and moral reasoning9. Students’ identity-relevant insights such as “my life is precious, just as others’ lives are precious” suggest the curriculum engaged them at the level of self-concept development, integrating values of compassion and interconnection into their developing identities.

Empathy development and mirror neuron systems

Mirror neuron systems enable humans to share emotional states when observing others’ experiences10,11. The curriculum’s documentary footage, narratives, and guided discussion activated students’ empathic capacities through vicarious learning12,13, creating emotional resonance that factual instruction alone could not achieve.

Life’s interconnection: from cosmic to social scales

The curriculum was designed around the scientific understanding that we are made of stardust from exploded stars—these atoms reached Earth, became part of countless life forms over time, and now form us14,15. After death, we return to soil and become new life, an eternal cycle of interconnection. This cosmic perspective informed the curriculum’s approach to helping students recognize interconnection at multiple scales. At the social scale, activities like Energy Ping-pong visualized how individual strengths create collective flourishing through mutual aid. This successfully translated abstract interconnection into personally meaningful understanding, positioning deceased organ donation as one expression of the fundamental truth that individual lives sustain and enable other lives.

Respect for autonomy

The curriculum balanced information provision with respect for autonomy16. The “Learn-Feel-Act” reflective writing activity invited personal responses rather than prescribing attitudes, supporting autonomous reflection rather than coercive persuasion.

Memory consolidation through reflective writing

The “Learn-Feel-Act” writing engaged students in organizing learning and formulating commitments, transforming classroom experiences into consolidated memories17. The 76% response rate despite time constraints indicates students found the reflective process meaningful.

Strengths and limitations

Existing school-based organ donation education programs for adolescents have been predominantly conducted in high-income Western countries18–20 whereas formal intervention programs remain scarce in LMICs and non-Western regions21. Across both contexts, these programs have universally employed knowledge-based approaches focused on brain death and transplantation facts.

To our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated a school-based curriculum that integrates deceased organ donation awareness into a broader respect-for-life among thousands of middle- and high-school students.

This study has several limitations inherent to its design as a pilot implementation in a real-world educational setting. First, the evaluation relied entirely on immediate post-class surveys without baseline (pre-intervention) measures. Consequently, while high engagement was observed, we could not quantify the exact magnitude of attitudinal change attributable specifically to the intervention. Second, the study relied on self-reported data collected immediately following the educational sessions. These responses may be subject to social desirability bias and the “novelty effect” of a one-time special program, potentially capturing immediate emotional reactions rather than sustained attitude changes. Third, regarding the qualitative analysis, while the use of dual Large Language Models (LLMs) enhanced efficiency and pattern identification across a large dataset, the absence of traditional qualitative methods employing multiple independent human coders may limit the interpretive depth and confidence in the identified themes.

Despite these methodological constraints, this study provides valuable evidence of the feasibility, scalability, and high student engagement of a values-based organ donation curriculum implemented within the constraints of actual school schedules.

Future directions

Future research should address these limitations through more rigorous study designs. Studies employing pre-post assessments with control groups would better establish causal relationships between the intervention and attitudinal changes.

Our research plan is to conduct ongoing serial cross-sectional monitoring with new student cohorts using the post-class survey. We have continued data collection since August 2024 beyond the period reported here (November 2023-July 2024). This approach will assess whether the positive effects documented in this initial implementation remain consistent across different cohorts over time. While individual longitudinal tracking is not feasible due to the absence of personally identifiable data, our serial cross-sectional approach will provide evidence of program reliability and sustainability under real-world conditions.

The behavioral outcomes we ultimately anticipate are long-term in nature. As participants progress through adulthood, we expect the values cultivated during this program to manifest in two meaningful ways: donor card registration upon reaching legal adulthood, and informed, open decision-making regarding family consent for deceased organ donation. These outcomes directly address South Korea’s low family consent rate and would contribute to increasing the availability of deceased donor organs for transplantation.

Conclusion

This study offers a scalable, replicable model for organ donation education that respects adolescent autonomy while engaging them cognitively, emotionally, and behaviorally. Although this study was conducted exclusively with South Korean adolescents, the core values embedded in this curriculum—the preciousness of life, empathy, and the understanding that human existence is shaped through relationships with others—are not culture-specific. We therefore suggest that this educational framework may be adaptable to other national and cultural contexts, provided that the delivery methods, narratives, and illustrative examples are thoughtfully tailored to reflect the history and cultural traditions of each respective society.
