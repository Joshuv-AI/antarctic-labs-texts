MULTIPLE SCLEROSIS

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS) characterized by immune cell infiltration, demyelination, axonal damage, and progressive neurodegeneration (Fig. 1). Autoimmune responses to CNS antigens are considered key in the pathogenesis of MS. Disease initiation and progression result from a complex interplay between peripheral immunity, resident CNS cells, metabolic dysregulation, and tissue-intrinsic stress responses. Activated microglia, infiltrating macrophages, and astrocytes critically shape lesion development and evolution, while oligodendrocytes and neurons display differential vulnerability depending on disease stage and lesion activity [1–3,4–6▪].

Oxidative and nitrosative damage drives neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in multiple sclerosis. Reactive oxygen (H2O2, O2-•) and nitrogen species (NO•) secreted by peripheral or CNS immune cells induce blood–brain barrier disruption and MS lesions characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, iron accumulation, myelin loss, neurodegeneration, and ferroptosis. Redox proteins such as thioredoxin, glutaredoxin, and peroxiredoxin (Trx, Grx, Prx), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) protect against oxidative damage. Ferroptosis is based on formation of lipid hydroperoxides (ROOH) and lipid peroxyl radicals (ROO•) induced by increased Fenton reaction between redox active iron and hydrogen peroxide. Products of lipid peroxidation such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) are diagnostic tools. Antioxidative therapy trials mostly failed; therefore, novel therapeutic strategies inducing intracellular defense or affecting specific redox pathways are needed to combat MS.

Oxidative and nitrosative stress have emerged as central pathogenic mechanisms in MS. Excessive production of reactive oxygen (ROS) and nitrogen species (RNS) by immune and glial cells contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and loss of myelin integrity (Fig. 1) [7]. At the same time, low levels of reactive species are indispensable for physiological redox signaling [8], underscoring the importance of tightly controlled redox homeostasis in the CNS [9]. Disturbance of this balance is increasingly recognized as a key driver of chronic lesion expansion and neurodegeneration [9].

Recent years have highlighted ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death driven by lipid peroxidation (Fig. 1) [10,11], as a mechanistic link between oxidative damage, iron accumulation, and progressive tissue injury in MS [9,12]. Understanding how redox regulation and ferroptotic pathways contribute to distinct disease stages may open new avenues for targeted therapeutic and diagnostic strategies [12,13▪].

OXIDATIVE/NITROSATIVE DAMAGE AND REDOX SIGNALING

Cells produce several reactive species [8]. Here, we will focus on ROS and RNS. A key source for ROS are the mitochondria. Complexes of the electron transport chain and other mitochondrial proteins, but also enzymes located outside mitochondria, such as NADPH oxidases (NOXs), contribute to superoxide anion (O2−•) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) formation [8,14,15▪]. In the presence of redox active iron, these two ROS form the very reactive hydroxyl radical (•OH) via the Fenton reaction. Nitric oxide (NO•) is formed by nitric oxide synthases (NOS) and can react with O2−• to peroxynitrite (ONOO-) [16▪]. H2O2, •OH, NO•, and ONOO- induce a variety of modifications in various biomolecules. Several of these modifications and some of their products, for example, 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) [17▪], are important second messenger. In fact, NO• is the most important second messenger in the cardiovascular system [18]. Therefore, low amounts of ROS and RNS are essential for redox signaling. In the current definition of oxidative stress, this physiological situation is acknowledged as oxidative eustress [8]. Oxidoreductases of the thioredoxin family control redox signaling by regulating reversible thiol modifications induced by these reactive species, namely disulfide bridges, S-glutathionylation, sulfenic acid, and S-nitrosation. Both, thioredoxins (Trxs) and glutaredoxins (Grxs) reduce these posttranslational modifications by cysteines of the characteristic CxxC active site motif [19]. Peroxiredoxins (Prxs) act as peroxidases, oxidases allowing specific H2O2 signaling [19,20], and as selenium transporter to incorporate selenocysteine in active sites of several redox proteins [21,22]. Lipid hydroperoxides or lipid peroxyl radicals are reduced by glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) using an essential selenocysteine in the active site [23], ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) [24,25], and Prxs [26]. Several of these enzymes with repair and signaling functions are under transcriptional control of the transcription factor Nrf2 that is stabilized under oxidizing conditions [8]. In contrast to oxidative eustress, oxidative distress is a pathological situation leading to oxidative damage of cellular pathways by inducing irreversible modifications such as carbonylation, sulfonic acids, or nitrotyrosine. A prominent oxidation-driven cell death mechanism is ferroptosis. This cell death is based on peroxidation of poly-unsaturated fatty acids and subsequent membrane rupture mainly induced by increased Fenton reaction [10,11]. Two main pathways protect the cell against ferroptosis, the already mentioned GPX4-axis and the FSP1-axis but also Trxs, Grxs, and Prxs (Fig. 1) [11,27▪].

In summary, there is a smooth transition between signaling and damaging functions of ROS and RNS, and a variety of enzymes controls the redox states of biomolecules to guarantee both functional signaling and protection against oxidative damage.

IMPACT OF REDOX REGULATION ON THE IMMUNE SYSTEM

ROS and RNS play pivotal roles in activation and differentiation of macrophages, T, B, and dendritic cells via increased activities of NOS, NOX, and elevated levels of O2-•[28–30,31▪▪]. In addition, the redox state of low-density lipoprotein affects innate immune memory [32]. All the mentioned immune cells contribute to MS and their activation and differentiation is affected during autoimmunity [33]. Circulating immune cells in the blood of MS patients display mitochondrial dysfunction [34] with higher O2−• formation and lower mitochondrial function in patients showing oligoclonal bands [35]. Furthermore, activity of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation contributes to T-cell differentiation and autoreactive activation [36,37].

Redox homeostasis by the gut microbiome is an important driver of vertebrate immunity [30] and connected to MS progression [38]. Th17 cells are most likely activated in the gut before migration into the CNS. Dimethyl fumarate (DMF), an oral drug for relapsing MS with antioxidative function (see below), is metabolized in the gut. In the EAE (experimental autoimmune encephalomyelytis) model, DMF treatment abolished increased inflammatory gut conditions, attenuated disease promoting Th17 differentiation, and subsequently curtailed severity of EAE [39]. In macrophages, inhibition of Trx reductase (TrxR) by DMF and other established inhibitors induces an anti-inflammatory effect [40].

Early ferroptosis research showed that T-cell-specific loss of GPX4 impaired CD4+ and CD8+-mediated protection against infections in mice [41]. MS-derived CD4+ cells highly express GPX4 and CD4-specific loss of GPX4 attenuated EAE [42]. Within the CNS, T cells feature a low ferroptosis score similar to nonactivated microglia, whereas activated microglia and macrophages display high ferroptosis scores [43▪].

These findings demonstrate the eminent role of redox regulation on immune cell function. The impact of these functions might affect MS differently depending on whether the cells are located in or outside the CNS.

OXIDATIVE AND NITROSATIVE DAMAGE IN MULTIPLE SCLEROSIS

Oxidative stress signatures are connected to virtually all diseases either as source or consequence. In MS, oxidative/nitrosative stress initiates and/or amplifies disease as activated microglia secrete NO• that forms ONOO- inside oligodendrocytes and leads to damage and loss of both the myelin structure and the BBB [1,44,45,46▪]. Moreover, CNS invading macrophages produce high amounts of O2−• that may contribute to parenchymal damage in MS. Expression of mitochondrial-targeted H2O2-detoxifying catalase in these macrophages diminished EAE [47]. However, the knowledge about enzymatic activities regulating redox signaling in this context is surprisingly limited. Antioxidative enzymes detoxifying O2−• or H2O2 are upregulated in MS lesions [48]. Levels of proteins important for redox signaling, for example all Prxs and Trx2, are elevated in MS, especially in astrocytes in active demyelinating lesions, but also in serum [49–54]. Overexpression of Trx1, Prx1, or Prx6 protected against myelin damage via transcriptional regulation of genes encoding myelin proteins or inflammatory proteins [55], diminished demyelination [52], and stabilized BBB [50] in EAE. Grx2 uses its coordinated FeS cluster to detoxify NO• and to shield from myelin damage [56].

The emerging connection between frequency and severity of MS relapses and alterations of the gut microbiome has been highlighted in numerous recent publications [38]. Modulation of ROS formation and redox-dependent pathways, namely hypoxia-inducible factor 2a, makes the gut microbiome an important regulator of the host's immune system activity and iron metabolism [30,57,58]. Iron homeostasis is disturbed in MS brains as shown by iron accumulation in different cell types and the formation of paramagnetic iron containing lesion rims [59–61].

As described above, elevated iron levels increase the risk of ferroptosis. In recent years, ferroptosis was linked to inflammatory demyelination in animal models (EAE, cuprizone) and MS. Although few publications claim that ferroptosis is not involved in oligodendrocyte damage [62], others show that oligodendrocytes become increasingly vulnerable towards ferroptosis during maturation because of the high content of lipids and the high amount of iron needed for lipid biosynthesis. This is counteracted by parallel upregulation of the aldo-keto reductase 1C1-3, an enzyme with antiferroptotic function. Expression of this protein in MS is blocked by a specific miRNA [63▪▪]. The role of ferroptosis in stages and MS subtypes is also under debate. Ferroptosis has been suggested to be more relevant in chronic EAE and secondary progressive MS [64] but was also shown to be an early event in EAE, detectable even before CD4+ T-cell infiltration [65]. Highest levels of lipid peroxidation and further ferroptotic hallmarks were found in the rim of active lesions [43▪,66], whereas lowest levels were noted in remyelinating lesions [43▪]. Nonetheless, the majority of data indicate that ferroptosis is part of disease progression in EAE and MS. GPX4 and FSP1 as well as the respective transcripts are down-regulated in MS brain tissue, MOG35–55 peptide-induced EAE, and cuprizone-induced demyelination, whereas lipid peroxidation is elevated [64,67]. Oxidized lipids accumulate in MS lesions and promote neurotoxicity [68]. Lysolecithin-induced demyelination augments lipid peroxidation and diminishes glutathione levels [69]. Moreover, inhibition of ferroptosis protects the BBB [70]. The loss of neuronal GPX4 upon neuroinflammation is connected to stimulator of interferon genes (STING)-induced autophagy [71▪▪]. Moreover, activity of the histone methyltransferase G9a promotes demyelination upon neuroinflammation in EAE and MS patients via repression of antiferroptotic processes [72]. Next to lipid peroxidation or loss of antiferroptotic proteins, fibroblasts obtained from MS patients show mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammasome activation [73]. In line with these observations, neuronal protection against ferroptosis is impaired in EAE through diminished activity of the immunoproteasome [74▪▪].

The findings summarized above highlight that ROS/RNS/disturbed redox regulation affects many aspects of neuroinflammation. The regulation of these aspects is complex and differs within cell types. Although the importance of these regulatory mechanisms for disease severity is obvious, the knowledge of specific signaling pathways is scarce. This limited knowledge is reflected by the fact that currently diroximel fumarate (DRF) is the sole approved MS therapy based on redox processes (Box 1).

THERAPEUTIC AND DIAGNOSTIC OPTIONS

Past research and clinical trials have shown that treatment with antioxidants fail to protect against neuroinflammation [7,46▪,75]. More specific treatments may be needed to combat oxidative damage, for example, via promoting or restoring activity of antioxidative enzymes, improving compromised mitochondrial function to minimize O2−• formation or to protect against ferroptosis (Fig. 1). Moreover, in the CNS autoimmunity target site and accessibility by a therapeutic agent is crucial. The same drug might have opposing effects when applied before or behind the BBB. In the following, some recent potential redox-based therapeutic possibilities are summarized.

Although providing antioxidative protection to preserve myelin structure [76▪▪], DMF and DRF are oxidants [76▪▪,77] inducing a cellular alert state with Nrf2-activated antioxidative defense similar to vaccinations boosting specific defense mechanisms. In a similar way, auranofin is active in attenuating EAE. This gold compound inhibits TrxR and thereby the antioxidative Trx system, which activates the Nrf2 pathway [78].

Two other drugs in clinical use, teriflunomide and fingolimod, could provide protection against ferroptosis. Teriflunomide is a dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) inhibitor but evidence that DHODH is a mitochondrial antiferroptotic enzyme is controversial [79,80]. Fingolimod is a sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor modulator that traps lymphocyctes in lymph nodes and thereby prevents their invasion in the CNS. It can also protect mitochondrial function [81], decrease iron levels [82], and increase expression of antiferroptotic proteins [83]. In contrast, fingolimod induces ferroptosis in cancer cells [84], indicating that this drug works as an oxidant like the fumarates. Nevertheless, ferroptosis inhibitors protect against oligodendrocyte damage and demyelination. Ferrostatin-1, liproxstatin-1, and UAMC-3203 showed these protective effects in EAE and organotypic slice cultures [66,85,76▪▪]. In addition, deferiprone, an iron chelator, diminished lysolecithin-mediated demyelination and activation of microglia and astrocytes [69].

Abzymes or catalytic antibodies are antibodies with an enzymatic activity. Abzymes with O2-• or H2O2 detoxifying activities are increased in MS patients and EAE mice [86]. Treatment of patients with such abzymes may be assessed for therapeutic utility. Another therapeutic possibility has emerged with the usage of specific nanoparticles. ROS-scavenging cerium oxide nanoparticles targeting T cells promoted antigen-specific immune tolerance and recovery from EAE symptoms [87].

7-Nitroindazole, a specific inhibitor of neuronal NOS attenuated increased levels of H2O2, nitrite, nitrate, and myelin damage in the EAE model [88▪].

Concentrations of NO•, nitrite, nitrate, and nitrotyrosine are slightly elevated in CSF and blood of MS patients, especially in RRMS patients [89–91] compared to patients suffering from other neuroinflammatory diseases [92,93] and may therefore be promising diagnostic tools in MS. Next to markers based on NO•, many other markers of oxidative damage, such as for oxidative DNA damage (8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine), changes in the cellular redox state, for example, the GSH:GSSG ratio, or activity of antioxidative enzymes like catalase have been detected in blood and CSF of MS patients [93–95]. Because of the increasingly recognized role of ferroptosis in MS, ferroptotic markers have also been examined as potential diagnostic tools. Since the first finding that GPX4 is downregulated in MS patients and EAE mice [67], several projects established sets of ferroptotic markers to compare ferroptosis scores/diagnostic signatures in blood or CSF samples from MS patients and controls, or between different subtypes of MS [96▪,97▪]. A circulating marker of lipid peroxidation was reported to be elevated in patients with RRMS compared to PMS [98▪]. A study using iPSC-derived oligodendrocytes from MS patients or controls confirmed increased ferroptotic hallmarks in the disease [99].

In summary, several redox-based antineuroinflammatory compounds are available. Further research is needed to understand their impact on immune cells in and outside the CNS and neuro-glial cells and thereby potential differences in spatio-temporal disease promoting or inhibiting effects (Box 2).

CONCLUSION

There is accumulating evidence that oxidative and nitrosative damage may have a significant role in the initiation and progression of MS. However, the knowledge of specific pathways protecting against this damage is limited. The role of proteins controlling redox signaling based on ROS and RNS is more established in other (auto-)inflammatory diseases, for example, atherosclerosis, where it was shown that Grx1 overexpression inhibits oxidation of the macrophage proteome and thereby macrophage dysfunction [100].

However, the vast majority of clinical trials using extracellular treatment with unspecific nonenzymatic antioxidants have failed. The successful trials with fumarate-based drugs, can be explained by the fact that these compounds are rather oxidants inducing intracellular antioxidative capacity.

Moreover, the complex interactions of different cell types in the immune periphery and the CNS and their different vulnerability to oxidative/nitrosative damage has not been considered in a sufficient manner. Certain antioxidants may safeguard oligodendrocytes against damage but, at the same time, facilitate differentiation into pathogenic immune cells. Therefore, spatial treatment regimes discriminating between CNS and periphery are most likely more efficient [101]. Based on recent research, it became obvious that ferroptosis is of great importance for the initiation and progression of MS. Ferroptosis inhibitors are promising as a novel therapeutic approach in neurodegeneration; however, therapy against cancer is based on ferroptosis inducers [11,102], affecting not only survival of cancer cells but also the cancer microenvironment and antitumor immunity [103].

Evidence to support the crucial role of intracelluar or extracellular oxidative conditions for differentiation of immune cells has been mainly adduced in cancer research. Here, it was shown that tumor-associated macrophages supporting proliferation and metastasis of cancer cells are more resistant towards oxidative damage because of higher activation of the Nrf2 pathway compared to other forms of macrophages residing in the tumor [104]. Moreover, oxidative conditions including ferroptosis in the tumor environment affect immunosuppression provided by monocytes [105,106]. Several studies demonstrate that different immune cells and subpopulations display specific vulnerabilities towards ferroptosis [106]. These observations mandate tailoring therapies to specific cell types.

Future research in potential therapeutic approaches to prevent or mitigate oxidative and nitrosative damage in MS should aim to address the following questions:

(1)What are the detailed mechanisms underlying function, generation, and detoxification of ROS and RNS in cell types of the CNS and infiltrating immune cells?
