Thrombolysis and thrombectomy in a first trimester stroke: expanding the evidence for acute ischemic stroke management in pregnancy. Illustrative case
Authors: Esteban R Rivera-Rivera, Alexander I Acevedo-Jetter, Alejandro E Cedeño-Morán, Grecia Negron Torres, Rodolfo E Alcedo-Guardia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDStroke is the leading cause of disability and the fifth leading cause of death nationwide, with large-vessel occlusions (LVOs) contributing significantly to this burden. The risk of LVO increases in hypercoagulable states such as pregnancy; however, LVOs in pregnancy are rare. Management of this condition lacks well-established guidelines due to the exclusion of pregnant women from clinical trials. Case reports of alteplase and mechanical thrombectomy (MT) use during pregnancy suggest therapeutic potential; however, there is less evidence for agents such as tenecteplase (TNK).OBSERVATIONSThe authors present the case of a 27-year-old woman, G5P2A2, at 4 weeks’ gestation who presented with seizure-like activity, expressive aphasia, and right-sided hemiparesis, NIH Stroke Scale score of 21. The patient was diagnosed with an ischemic stroke. TNK was administered without symptom resolution, and the patient was transferred to the authors’ center. Imaging demonstrated left M2 occlusion, and MT was performed successfully. The patient carried the pregnancy to term and delivered a healthy infant at 37 weeks.LESSONSTo the authors’ knowledge, this represents the first formally reported case of LVO treated with TNK and MT in the first trimester of pregnancy. Ischemic strokes are acute neurological deficits caused by the obstruction of cerebral vasculature due to atherosclerosis, cardioembolism, or cerebral small-vessel diseaseand may cause permanent neurological disabilities and death if the emergency is not treated appropriately in the early stages.1 The risk of experiencing an ischemic stroke increases with age, with most ischemic strokes occurring in those who are 65 years or older and with other factors, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking, obesity, and dyslipidemia, among others.2 However, in young female patients, pregnancy increases the risk of maternal ischemic strokes, an uncommon event that occurs in approximately 12 of 100,000 pregnancies.3 The increased risk for maternal ischemic strokes occurs due to physiological adaptations in pregnancy, such as the increase in intravascular volume, a higher cardiac output, cardiovascular remodeling, an increase in the concentration of coagulation factors, a decrease of protein S, and acquired resistance to activated protein C.4–6

Treatment for ischemic strokes includes intravenous (IV) administration of a thrombolytic agent such as tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) or tenecteplase (TNK).7 Stroke care, particularly treatment of large-vessel occlusions (LVOs), has experienced significant advances in recent years, with the introduction of mechanical thrombectomy (MT) utilized as stand-alone therapy or in addition to thrombolytics. A gap still exists in care for populations such as pregnant patients, as there are no well-established guidelines for the management of stroke in pregnancy given the lack of inclusion of this population in clinical trials.8 TNK and tPA are considered FDA category C drugs with no prospective studies evaluating their use in pregnancy, and their use is only indicated when the benefits outweigh the risks to both the fetus and mother. Some case reports have shown the successful use of tPA in treating isolated cases of ischemic maternal stroke.9 As is the case with TNK and tPA, the use of MT in pregnant women has only been studied retrospectively, with limited information about long-term effects on the mother and fetus, and there has not been a comparison of MT outcomes at different trimesters of pregnancy.10 More data need to be published to address the knowledge gap regarding the safety and efficacy of these procedures in pregnant patients.

Illustrative Case

Initial Presentation

A 27-year-old female, G5P2A2, at 4 weeks’ gestation as per last menstrual period was transferred to our service from an affiliate hospital with a suspected diagnosis of ischemic stroke. Her past medical history was pertinent for two previous spontaneous abortions. The patient presented with a seizure-like episode witnessed by her mother, who reported the patient had a “blank stare” and fell to the ground speaking incomprehensibly and making irregular movements of her right upper extremity. On trying to stand, the patient reported significant right leg weakness. Physical examination at the affiliate hospital was positive for decreased strength of the right upper and lower extremities, right labial commissure deviation, and expressive aphasia. The patient was referred to our telestroke system and the initial evaluation yielded an NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 21. Noncontrast head CT imaging showed no evidence of hemorrhage; a diagnosis of ischemic stroke was made, and given that tPA was not available at the affiliate hospital, the recommendation of proceeding with TNK thrombolysis was made. The risks and benefits of thrombolytic administration, including its potential complications in pregnant and nonpregnant patients, were discussed with the patient and her mother, who agreed with the intervention. TNK was administered at 3.25 hours since last known well (LKW), and the patient was then transferred to our facility for possible endovascular intervention. Initial Workup

On transfer, reevaluation again yielded an NIHSS score of 21. Further imaging studies were pursued to better elucidate the underlying cause of the patient’s deficits. CT perfusion (CTP) demonstrated a small-volume ischemic penumbra of the left posterior middle cerebral artery (MCA) vascular territory with a time to maximum (Tmax) > 6.0 seconds, consistent with hypoperfused tissue with no matching cerebral blood flow (CBF) < 30%, indicating no core infarct and salvageable tissue with a favorable prognosis if reperfusion were achieved (Fig. 2). CT angiography (CTA) demonstrated a left inferior M2 segment LVO (Fig. 3). Given these findings, it was determined the patient was a candidate for MT. The case was discussed at length with the obstetrics and gynecology service, which recommended performing MT and scheduling for termination of pregnancy at a later date. The potential risks of the procedure, with particular emphasis on the potential risks of radiation exposure to the fetus, were discussed with the patient and her mother, both of whom agreed to proceed with MT.

Digital Subtraction Angiography and MT

The patient was placed supine on the table for angiography, and an 8-Fr short sheath was utilized to gain access to the right femoral artery. Both a BMX96 (Penumbra) catheter and a Berenstein catheter were utilized to navigate into the left internal carotid artery (ICA) under fluoroscopic guidance and roadmapping. Subsequently, digital biplane angiography of the left ICA was obtained. The BMX96 catheter was then positioned in the left common carotid artery and digital subtraction angiography of the left ICA was performed, confirming occlusion of the left inferior M2 segment (Fig. 4A). A Phenom 27 microcatheter (Medtronic) and Asahi 14 microwire were advanced to the left MCA and utilized to select the left inferior M2 segment. The microwire and microcatheter were advanced through the clot, and a Solitaire 4 × 40–mm revascularization device (Medtronic) was used for the MT (Fig. 4C and D). The distal stent was deployed beyond the clot, and distal injection confirmed reperfusion. Under constant pump-assisted suction connected to the RED 68 intermediate catheter (Penumbra), the Solitaire 4 × 40–mm stent retriever was withdrawn. Digital subtraction angiography of the left ICA was performed after completion of the MT and showed complete reperfusion of the previously occluded artery, yielding a posttreatment thrombolysis in cerebral infarction (TICI) score of 3 on the first pass at 7 hours since LKW (Fig. 4B).

Progression

Reevaluation the following day yielded an NIHSS score of 9 with some residual deficits; however, there was significant improvement and the patient reporting feeling well. MR angiography on postoperative day (POD) 1 demonstrated a patent left inferior M2 segment with no flow-limiting stenosis or residual occlusion (Fig. 5). Given the patient’s history of multiple miscarriages as well as this episode of LVO, a complete hematological workup for factor V Leiden mutation, lupus anticoagulant, protein C and S activity, antithrombin III, factor II activity, prothrombin time, international normalized ratio, and activated partial thromboplastin time was performed. The patient was noted to have a decreased protein S activity, with a value of 45% (normal range 60%–140%). All other hematological parameters were within normal limits. The patient underwent cardiological workup, including carotid ultrasound, transthoracic ultrasound, and electrocardiography, which yielded no definite cardioembolic source. The patient was placed on bi-daily subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin as a prophylactic measure. The patient remained stable with no acute neurological events and was discharged home on POD 5.

POD 1 MR angiogram demonstrating complete reperfusion of left MCA vasculature and resolution of previous cutoff.

The patient and fetus were closely followed at a high-risk pregnancy center throughout the pregnancy. Prenatal screening examinations including serial ultrasounds revealed no fetal abnormalities throughout the pregnancy. At the most recent clinical follow-up at 32 weeks’ gestation and 6 months poststroke, the patient had minimal residual deficits with a minor facial palsy, drift of the right upper extremity, and mild sensory loss of the right side of the face with an NIHSS score of 3. The pregnancy remained viable, and the patient delivered a healthy baby girl at 37 weeks’ gestation with no major complications.

Discussion

Observations

Strokes are the most common cause of long-term disability following pregnancy and the seventh leading cause of death among pregnant women.11 We report a case of ischemic stroke in a 27-year-old pregnant woman at 4 weeks’ gestation, presenting with seizure-like activity, right-sided hemiparesis, right-sided facial droop,1 and expressive aphasia. Pregnant women have a higher risk of stroke, as the pregnant state introduces a series of additional risks, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, postpartum cerebral angiopathy, peripartum cardiomyopathy, and amniotic fluid embolisms, which need to be considered in the evaluation of these patients.12

Management of patients with suspected ischemic stroke episodes focuses on identifying the type and location of the stroke using diagnostic imaging, restoring blood flow to salvageable tissue, and preventing future strokes. Guidelines have not addressed the unique situation of maternal stroke since pregnant women have been excluded from large ischemic stroke trials.4 Nevertheless, it has been suggested that pregnant women suspected of having an ischemic stroke should be evaluated for the same therapies as nonpregnant women.4

In the presented case, the patient presented with a history and physical examination highly suggestive of an ischemic stroke and no evidence of intracranial hemorrhage on noncontrast CT, leading to the administration of TNK for thrombolysis. To date, there have been no studies investigating fetal risk after the administration of thrombolytic agents such as alteplase or TNK, but it has been demonstrated in animal models that the large molecules that compose thrombolytics do not cross the placenta.13 Additionally, TNK has not been shown to have teratogenic effects; however, this has not been studied directly, and the risk cannot be excluded. The primary concern when utilizing TNK in pregnancy is therefore the increased chance of maternal bleeding, which could lead to life-threatening hemorrhage and loss of the fetus. More than two dozen cases have been reported using IV thrombolysis for maternal stroke treatment, and thrombolysis has been utilized for acute stroke management when benefits were felt to outweigh the possible risks to the fetus.12,14–16 Six cases of TNK use in pregnancy have been reported for varying pathologies, including myocardial infarctions, prosthetic heart valve thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism, but to the best of our knowledge, there has been only one formally reported case of the use of TNK for cerebral ischemic stroke in pregnancy, and that patient was in her third trimester of pregnancy.17–22 Our case therefore represents the first comprehensive report of TNK use for cerebral ischemic stroke in the first trimester of pregnancy.

Post-IV thrombolysis imaging evaluation of our patient demonstrated a small volume of hypoperfused tissue with no matching core infarct of the left posterior MCA on CTP, and CTA demonstrated a left inferior M2 segment LVO, for which she was determined to be a candidate for MT. The patient underwent successful thrombectomy at 7 hours since LKW, with a TICI score of 3 on the first pass. MT has not been extensively studied in pregnant women due to the exclusion criteria of relevant randomized controlled trials. However, a recent analysis of US national claims data identified 4590 pregnant and postpartum arterial ischemic stroke patients, with 180 (3.9%) treated using MT.23 This analysis, along with case reports, suggests that MT may be a safe and effective treatment for ischemic stroke during pregnancy.10 In our patient, POD 1 MR angiography demonstrated a patent inferior division of M2 with no flow-limiting stenosis or residual occlusion, confirming the success of the procedure. Reevaluation on POD 1 showed significant improvement in neurological deficits. The patient experienced no further complications, prenatal screening revealed no developmental anomalies or complications, and the pregnancy was successfully carried to term, with a healthy infant delivered at 37 weeks. These findings suggest that the use of TNK and MT did not result in adverse maternal or fetal outcomes in this case.

Given the patient’s history of two miscarriages, and the current LVO, a complete hematological workup was performed. Results were within normal parameters except for protein S, which had a value of 45%. Many of the changes in the pregnancy state alter normal coagulation, including an increase in intrinsic and extrinsic coagulation factors, total protein S reduction, resistance against protein C, and reduced endogenous tPA activity during the first trimester of pregnancy.24,25 Given this, it is difficult to establish with certainty whether this patient had a preexisting coagulopathy and further hematological evaluation should be pursued. However, in our case the patient opted for no further hematological evaluation.

Special considerations are merited in pregnant patients, primarily aimed at minimizing fetal radiation exposure as high doses of radiation exposure during pregnancy have been associated with a variety of fetal adverse effects, including stillbirth. Although ideally followed in all patients, pregnant patients particularly benefit from the as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA) principle endorsed by the Society of Interventional Radiology.26,27 When choosing the approach for thrombectomy, although largely operator dependent, preference should be given to radial approaches in pregnancy to avoid direct exposure of the gravid uterus and fetus to ionizing radiation, which occurs with femoral approaches. Additionally, the radial approach allows for abdominal lead shielding, which may provide psychological reassurance, although it does not add significant protection.28 One additional consideration is the use of available measures to aid in achieving first-pass reperfusion, such as the combined use of a stent retriever and aspiration, as significant increases in radiation exposure occur with subsequent thrombectomy attempts.29,30 In the presented case, given the urgent nature of intervention with MT and the initially established plan of termination of pregnancy at a later date, special measures were not taken. However, the fact that the pregnancy was successfully carried to term underscores the importance of implementing measures to minimize radiation exposure whenever possible, particularly during pregnancy, regardless of the established plan.

This case highlights the successful use of TNK and MT for first trimester stroke, contributing to the limited data on stroke management in pregnancy. Given the rarity of LVO in pregnancy and lack of standardized approach to these cases, as well as the urgent nature of stroke management, our ability to optimally manage this patient by implementing strategies for limiting fetal radiation exposure, such as attempting a radial approach and abdominal lead shielding, was limited. The incomplete hematological workup additionally prevents definitive identification of a hypercoagulable state. Additionally, long-term maternal and child outcomes have yet to be determined. Finally, as a single case, the generalizability of our case is limited. Despite these limitations, our patient benefited from initial presentation to a network-affiliated hospital, which enabled rapid transfer to our thrombectomy-capable center. The presence of multiple services on-site allowed for collaboration of an interdisciplinary team of anesthesiologists, neurologists, obstetrician-gynecologists, and neurosurgeons, optimizing management in this rare and complex scenario and achieving successful reperfusion on the first pass, limiting fetal radiation exposure. This case supports the consideration of TNK and MT in pregnant patients when the benefits outweigh risks. Further research is needed to guide management in this population.

Lessons

LVOs are a rare but potentially devastating complication in pregnancy. To our knowledge, this is the first formally reported case of cerebral ischemic stroke treated with administration of TNK in the first trimester of pregnancy. Additionally, this case represents the first reported case of successful treatment of cerebral ischemic stroke in pregnancy treated with combined TNK and MT in the first trimester of pregnancy. Despite the lack of randomized controlled trial data on these therapies in pregnancy, this case demonstrates TNK administration without reported complications to the patient or the pregnancy. Furthermore, MT was performed successfully with no major adverse outcomes. While caution is warranted given the absence of established guidelines, our findings support the consideration of these therapies in pregnant patients when the potential benefits outweigh the risks. This case adds to the growing body of evidence suggesting that acute stroke interventions should not be withheld solely based on pregnancy status. However, special measures, such as following the Society of Interventional Radiology ALARA principles, maximizing chances of first-pass reperfusion, abdominal lead shielding, and radial access, should be strongly considered in pregnant patients in order to minimize fetal radiation exposure. Further studies are needed to better understand the long-term maternal and fetal outcomes associated with these interventions.
