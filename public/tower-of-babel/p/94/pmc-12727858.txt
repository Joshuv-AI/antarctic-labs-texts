Mechanisms of Enterobacter and Bacillus in promoting aerobic composting and immobilization of Cd in livestock and poultry manure
Authors: Xinyu Mao, Wei Li, Daling Xu, Jianhong Ma, Rui Zhao, Junan Bao, Pengbin Tang, Jiaqi Liu, Huaizhi Yu, Xiaohou Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aerobic composting of livestock manure concentrates heavy metals like cadmium (Cd), elevating environmental risks after land application. This study screened composite microbial strains for simultaneous promotion of compost maturation and Cd passivation, exploring their mechanisms on the composting process, microbial community succession, and Cd speciation transformation. Three Cd-resistant strains—Enterobacter hormaechei (LB3), Enterobacter cloacae (LB4), and Bacillus velezensis (J-1–2)—were isolated from chicken manure, formulated into two composite inoculants (T1: LB3 + LB4; T2: LB3 + LB4 + J-1–2), and compared with an uninoculated control (CK) during composting. Maturity parameters, Cd species distribution, and microbial community dynamics were monitored. Results showed composite inoculants significantly improved composting: T1 extended the thermophilic phase and enhanced organic matter degradation; T2 achieved optimal nitrogen retention, with the highest NO3−-N (1504 mg/kg, representing a 13.51% increase compared to CK) and lowest NH4+-N (153 mg/kg, a 23.12% reduction compared to CK). Microbial community analysis revealed Ace/Chao1 indices in T1/T2 were 1.5–1.8 times higher than CK in the heating phase, while the Shannon index at maturity was 10.13% and 22.40% higher, respectively. T2 had the highest Cd passivation efficiency (66.7%), with exchangeable Cd decreasing from 27 to 9%. Inoculants promoted composting and Cd immobilization via microbial community modulation and adsorption–complexation mediated by key genera (e.g., Thauera), providing an effective strategy for safe reuse of livestock manure and heavy metal pollution mitigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10123-025-00730-y.

Introduction

With the expansion of the livestock and poultry industry, livestock manure output has risen sharply, challenging agricultural waste management. For instance, China produced 2× 1012 kg of livestock manure in 2017 (pig manure dominant) (Wang et al. 2021), while global annual livestock manure reaches 1.5–2.5 billion tonnes, much of which is used as fertilizer (Oyedun et al. 2025; Bolan et al. 2014; Nicholson et al. 2003). Due to trace metals (Cd, Cu, Zn) in feed additives, repeated manure application accumulates these metals in the soil. Improper treatment causes pollution and wastes nutrients. Aerobic composting, an eco-friendly technology, achieves manure harmlessness, reduction, and resource utilization (Zhi et al. 2020), but faces challenges: poor control of composting conditions or parameters leads to insufficient humification; immature compost harms soil and plants. Unreasonable field application may also cause soil compaction and salinization (Hernández et al. 2013), reducing fertilizer efficiency. Moreover, residual contaminants (heavy metals, antibiotics) in manure accumulate in soil-crop systems, threatening food safety, ecology, and human health. Thus, improving composting efficiency is critical. Prior studies show microbial agent inoculation promotes compost maturation (Shengnan et al. 2023), enhances organic matter conversion and nitrogen fixation (Asja et al. 2019), and shortens the cycle.

Recent global research on microbial agents for aerobic composting includes the following: Bemal et al. (Bernal et al. 2009) confirming improved manure decomposition; Abebe et al. (Abebe et al. 2021) noting accelerated organic matter degradation, shorter cycles, and nitrogen fixation; Zheng et al. (Zheng et al. 2021) isolating 5 Bacillus strains (from chicken manure) to boost decomposition; Ansari et al. (Ansari and Malik 2007) screening Cd2⁺-passivating Escherichia coli. However, gaps exist: a lack of composite agents that both promote maturation and immobilize heavy metals (especially Cd); most studies focus on single functions (organic degradation or metal passivation), ignoring synergistic consortia; microbial succession mechanisms and their correlation with Cd speciation remain unclear. This study addresses these gaps by in situ screening to isolate and combine dual-functional Enterobacter and Bacillus strains. Its novelty lies in a composite inoculum that optimizes composting, improves nitrogen retention, and enhances Cd passivation via microbial adsorption/complexation. In situ screening preserves microbial traits and community balance (Dawoon et al. 2020), while isolation-purification identifies functions, clarifies mechanisms, and eliminates interference—offering an innovative solution for compost efficiency and heavy metal issues.

Using chicken manure, this study obtained composite agents (promoting humification + Cd passivation) via in situ screening and isolation-purification, and applied them in aerobic composting. It is hypothesized that Enterobacteriaceae and Bacillus enhance compost humification and Cd passivation. After verifying Cd-resistant strain screening, target strains were inoculated; compost parameters, Cd speciation/distribution, and microbial succession were monitored to explore the agents’ impacts. By analyzing correlations among Cd passivation, microbial succession, and decomposition, the mechanism of functional strains on Cd bioavailability was clarified—providing theoretical/practical support for advancing aerobic composting and livestock manure recycling.

Preservation of strains

A 50% (v/v) glycerol solution was prepared by mixing 50 mL of glycerol with 50 mL of distilled water. Following autoclave sterilization, 1 mL of bacterial culture in logarithmic growth phase was aseptically mixed with 1 mL of the sterile 50% glycerol solution. The mixture was then aliquoted into cryovials and stored at − 70 °C for long-term preservation.

Physical and chemical properties of composting materials

Experimental design

A 59 × 39 × 35 cm foam box was used as the aerobic composting reactor. Chicken manure and rice husk (ratio 7:3) served as raw materials, with moisture content adjusted to 60% and C/N ratio set to 25:1; the pile was turned every 5 days. Experimental groups were set by varying composite microbial inoculant dosages, with a control group receiving equal sterile water (all treatments in triplicate). Samples were collected every 2 days via the five-point sampling method: mixed thoroughly to form a composite sample; then, 100 g was sterile-packaged into two portions—one for determining pH, moisture content, nitrate nitrogen, ammonium nitrogen, C/N ratio, and Cd speciation, the other for 16S rDNA analysis. Samples on days 0, 3, 8, and 33 represented the composting initial (0), mesophilic (S), thermophilic (G), and maturation (J) phases, respectively.

Determination of physical and chemical parameters of composting

A thermometer was inserted 1/3 of the way from the pile top; temperatures were recorded daily at 10:00 and 16:00, with the average taken as the measured value. For pH determination, 5 g of compost sample was mixed with 50 mL of distilled water, shaken at 25 °C and 180 rpm for 30 min, filtered, left to stand for 1 h, and then measured with a pH meter. Moisture content was determined by weighing 50 g of fresh compost, followed by drying in an oven at 105 °C for 4 h. Total organic carbon and total nitrogen were analyzed per national standard methods (Ministry of Ecology and Environment of the People’s Republic of China 2011), and the C/N ratio was calculated accordingly. NH4+-N was determined via KCl extraction (Jiang et al. 2025) and NO3−-N via colorimetry (Haihou et al. 2023). Absorbance values at 465 nm and 665 nm were measured with a cuvette to calculate the E4/E6 ratio. For the germination assay, the compost sample was mixed with distilled water at 1:10, shaken for 20 min, and left to stand for 24 h. After slow filtration to remove debris and precipitates, 5 mL of filtrate was evenly spread in a 9-cm petri dish. A control group with the same volume of distilled water was set in parallel. Twenty uniform-sized cabbage seeds were placed in each dish, with three replicates per treatment. Determination of heavy metal Cd forms

The chemical speciation of cadmium (Cd) in compost samples was determined via the modified three-step extraction method (Rauret et al. 1999), with procedures as follows:Exchangeable Cd (EX-Cd): Air-dried compost was ground and sieved through a 100-mesh sieve. A 1.0 g aliquot was placed in a 100-mL centrifuge tube, mixed with 40 mL of 0.10 mol/L glacial acetic acid, shaken at 25 °C for 16 h, and then centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 20 min. The supernatant was diluted to 50 mL for Cd2+ concentration measurement (designated Z1).Reducible Cd (OXI-Cd): The residual precipitate from step (1) was treated with 40 mL of 0.5 mol/L hydroxylamine hydrochloride, shaken at 25 °C for 16 h, and centrifuged (3000 r/min, 20 min). The supernatant was adjusted to 50 mL for Cd2+ analysis (designated Z2).Oxidizable Cd (RED-Cd): The precipitate from step (2) was first treated with 10 mL acidified H2O2 (standing for 1–2 h) and then heated to 85 °C with an additional 10 mL H2O2 (maintained for 1 h). After adding 50 mL of 1 mol/L ammonium acetate, the mixture was shaken (25 °C, 16 h) and centrifuged (3000 r/min, 20 min); the supernatant was diluted to 50 mL for Cd2⁺ measurement (designated Z3).Residual Cd (RES-Cd): The final precipitate was digested with 10 mL HNO3 and 4 mL HF in a sealed digestion tank at 140 °C. The digested solution was diluted to 50 mL for Cd2+ determination (designated Z4).

The concentration and quality of PCR amplification products were assessed, and only samples meeting the following criteria were selected for subsequent analysis: DNA concentration > 10 ng/µL and 260/280 nm absorbance ratio approximately 1.8. Qualified samples were then submitted to Tiangen Biochemical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd. for Illumina high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics analysis.

Data processing and analysis

Microsoft Excel 2020 was used for collating and preliminary processing of physicochemical and biological data. For sequence analysis, microbial sequences were processed and aligned via Notepad + + and MEGA 7.0, followed by phylogenetic tree construction based on sequence similarity and evolutionary relationships. Origin 2021 was employed for data visualization (line graphs for physicochemical indices, stacked bar charts for Cd speciation distribution). For microbial community analysis, sequencing data underwent OTU clustering via QIIME2’s DADA2 pipeline, with subsequent taxonomic annotation to determine species composition and relative abundance. Beta diversity matrices were calculated to assess sample relationships, rarefaction curves were analyzed based on OTU distribution, and Alpha diversity was evaluated using Chao1, ACE, Simpson, and Shannon indices.

All experiments had triplicates, with data presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS 27.0.1: one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test identified significant differences among groups at different composting stages (p < 0.05 = significant). Pearson correlation analysis and Mantel tests were used to assess relationships between microbial communities and environmental parameters.

Changes in decay indicators during composting

The pH of all treatment groups exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease during composting (Fig. 8A), consistent with the typical pH trend in aerobic composting (Mianshen et al. 2022). At the onset of composting, the pH values of the T1 and T2 treatment groups were higher than those of the CK group, likely due to the addition of composite microbial agents. Between days 3 and 16, the pH of each treatment group increased significantly, peaking on day 16, with the T2 group reaching the highest value (8.9). By the end of composting, the pH stabilized at a weakly alkaline level across all treatments, which is a key condition for compost maturation(Taha et al. 2022).Fig. 8Changes in decay indicators during composting

Changes in decay indicators during composting

The pre-composting phase was characterized by a rapid temperature increase in all treatment groups (Fig. 8B). By day 6, each treatment group had reached its peak composting temperature, with the T1 group recording the highest temperature (58 °C), which was then consistently maintained above 50 °C. Since maintaining temperatures above 50 °C for more than 3 days effectively eliminates pathogenic microorganisms and weeds, achieving the harmless treatment standard (Jain et al. 2018), all treatment groups in this study met this requirement. Notably, the T1 group reached the thermophilic phase first, suggesting that the microbial inoculant promoted compost decomposition. During the subsequent cooling phase, temperatures gradually decreased, with minor temporary increases observed after pile turning in all treatments, though none exceeded 50 °C.

The moisture content of all treatment groups exhibited a decreasing trend (Fig. 8C), declining from approximately 60% initially to 43.11% (CK), 41.56% (T1), and 41.89% (T2) during the maturation phase, representing an overall reduction of about 20%. The most rapid moisture loss occurred during the thermophilic phase, primarily because thermophilic bacteria dominated the microbial community during this stage. The intensive decomposition of organic matter and consequent heat generation significantly enhanced water evaporation.

In this experiment, all treatment groups exhibited a continuous decline in C/N ratio throughout the composting process (Fig. 8D). The most significant reduction occurred during the thermophilic phase, with C/N ratios decreasing to 19.05 (CK), 19.03 (T1), and 21.01 (T2), respectively. Subsequently, the rate of decrease gradually slowed until reaching the lowest values during the maturation phase: 15.82 (CK), 14.88 (T1), and 15.79 (T2).

Figure 8E illustrates the NO3−-N variation pattern during composting. During both the mesophilic and thermophilic phases, NO3−-N content remained relatively stable. However, a rapid increase was observed following the thermophilic phase. At composting completion, the NO3−-N content increased by 2.00-fold (CK), 2.06-fold (T1), and 2.18-fold (T2), with the T2 treatment group achieving the highest concentration (1504 mg/kg).

Figure 8F presents the NH4+-N variation pattern during composting. In contrast to NO3−-N, NH4+-N content increased rapidly during the mesophilic and thermophilic phases, reaching peak concentrations. Subsequently, NH4+-N levels declined sharply during the cooling phase, attaining minimum values by day 16. The T2 treatment group showed the lowest recorded concentration (153 mg/kg).

All treatment groups exhibited a characteristic rise and subsequent decline in E4/E6 ratios during composting (Fig. 8G). During the thermophilic phase, E4/E6 ratios increased concomitantly with temperature elevation, peaking on day 14 at 3.44 (CK), 3.20 (T1), and 3.34 (T2). Following the temperature maximum, the ratios decreased progressively as pile temperatures declined, ultimately reaching their lowest values during the maturation phase: 1.74 (CK), 1.52 (T1), and 1.41 (T2).

Seed GI demonstrated a consistent upward trend across all treatments during composting (Fig. 8H). Initial GI values were relatively low (about 25%) in all groups during the early composting stage. The GI progression exhibited phase-dependent characteristics: slower increases during mesophilic and thermophilic phases, followed by accelerated growth during the cooling phase, particularly in T1 and T2 treatments (significantly higher than CK). This upward trend gradually stabilized toward composting completion, with final GI values reaching 81.72% (CK), 86.22% (T1), and 87.35% (T2). Statistical analysis (e.g., one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test) revealed that the GI values of T1 and T2 were significantly higher than those of CK (p < 0.05), but there was no significant difference between T1 and T2.

Characterization of bacterial communities and their succession in composting processes

Differential analysis of bacterial species in compost samples

PCR-amplified sequence electrophoresis of compost samples showed clear bands (all treatments) meeting high-throughput sequencing standards (in the Supplementary Material Fig. S2). Sequencing 12 samples across four stages yielded 1,189,665 high-quality bacterial sequences (in the Supplementary Material Table S4) for subsequent analysis.

OTU analysis (97% similarity; in the Supplementary Material Fig. S3a) revealed inoculated groups (T1: 7785, T2: 7471) had more bacterial OTUs than CK (6691), indicating increased species richness from inoculation. OTU counts rose with composting, peaking at maturation. Rarefaction curves (in the Supplementary Material Fig. S3b) confirmed sufficient sequencing depth; the steepest T2S curve indicated the highest heating-phase richness.

Principal coordinate analysis (in the Supplementary Material Fig. S3c) showed that the initial-stage (0) microbial communities diverged sharply from other stages, reflecting composting-driven restructuring. Mesophilic phase (S) and maturation (J) samples clustered closely, suggesting similar community structures across treatments. Notably, CK separated from T1/T2 in the thermophilic phase (G), indicating active exogenous consortia significantly altered microbial composition.

Notably, thermophilic-phase samples exhibited distinct clustering patterns: CK treatment diverged significantly from T1 and T2 groups. This divergence presumably resulted from the active involvement of inoculated microbial consortia in T1/T2 treatments, which modified indigenous community structures and created differentiation from conventional composting (CK) microbiota.

Bacterial community succession during the composting process

To investigate the dynamics of the bacterial community during different composting stages, this study analyzed the composition of bacterial communities at the phylum level (groups with < 1% abundance were classified as “Others”) in compost samples from various treatments over time. The results are presented in Fig. 9.Fig. 9Composition (phylum) of bacterial communities in compost samples at different times

Composition (phylum) of bacterial communities in compost samples at different times

Overall, the dominant phyla at each composting stage included Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Acidobacteria, and Chloroflexi. Among these, Firmicutes and Actinobacteria exhibited significant fluctuations and higher abundance.

The relative abundance of Firmicutes varied significantly across composting phases. It was moderate in the initial phase (approximately 38.53–42%), peaked during the thermophilic phase (reaching 62.28% in CK, 77.24% in T1, and 89.51% in T2), and then plummeted to 15.06–20.60% in the maturation phase. Conversely, Actinobacteria exhibited an opposite trend, increasing sharply from a range of 5–38.61% in the first three phases to 46.01–58.90% during maturation. Notably, in the CK, T1, and T2 treatment groups, Firmicutes reached 62.28%, 77.24%, and 89.51%, respectively, suggesting their adaptability to high-temperature environments.

Additionally, during maturation, Actinobacteria abundance rose significantly to 46.01%, 58.90%, and 57.26% in the CK, T1, and T2 groups, respectively. In contrast, Chloroflexi, which had previously represented < 1% of the community, increased to 15.36%, 11.82%, and 14.65% during maturation.

In this study, bacterial communities with a relative abundance exceeding 1% were further classified statistically at the family level, and the results are presented in Fig. 10. A total of 27 major bacterial families were identified and analyzed for abundance variations across different compost treatments. The dominant families included Enterobacteriaceae (0.11–37.15%), Ruminococcaceae (0.93–76.79%), Lachnospiraceae (0.25–76.68%), Burkholderia (0.03–90.98%), Lactobacillaceae (0.25–33.13%), Wohlfahrtiimonadaceae (1.79–68.09%), Bacillaceae (0.01–51.55%), and Family XI (0.52–18.93%). The analysis showed that the abundance of both Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae increased in all treatments during the warming period but declined sharply in the maturation period. Specifically, Ruminococcaceae decreased from 2.79–21.51% in the initial phase to 0.93–1.67% in the maturation phase, while Lachnospiraceae dropped from 3.48–25.44% initially to 0.25–0.81% during maturation.Fig. 10Bacterial families with abundance greater than 1% during chicken manure composting

Bacterial families with abundance greater than 1% during chicken manure composting

The initial abundance of Lactobacillaceae was higher in the T1 and T2 treatment groups but decreased significantly during the thermophilic phase and reached its lowest level in the maturation phase. In contrast, the CK group exhibited a peak in Lactobacillaceae abundance (29.93%) during the warming phase, followed by a continuous decline to 0.33% by the maturation phase. Unlike these decreasing trends, Burkholderia abundance increased steadily throughout composting, rising from 0.03–0.59% in the initial phase to 22.36–23.56% in the maturation phase. Notably, Wohlfahrtiimonadaceae were detected only during the warming and high-temperature phases. Additionally, Anaerolineaceae and Chromobacteriaceae were absent in the first three composting phases but gradually increased in abundance, peaking during the maturation phase.

Composition of bacterial community at the genus level in different samples at different times

The analysis revealed that certain genera like Prevotella and Enterococcus showed minimal abundance during the maturation period, with their populations declining sharply in the late composting stage. This suggests these genera may have been primarily active in the pre-composting phase. Conversely, other genera including Tauerella, Aeromonas, and Comamonas exhibited low initial abundance but progressively increased throughout the composting process, peaking during maturation. This pattern indicates these genera likely adapted well to changing composting conditions and potentially played significant roles in mid-to-late stage transformation and metabolic processes.

In summary, the composting process drives a significant and stage-specific succession of the bacterial community. The addition of compound microbial agents (T1, T2) optimized the community structure by enhancing bacterial richness and diversity, particularly promoting the enrichment of key phyla (e.g., Proteobacteria) and genera (e.g., Thauera) during the maturation phase, which are potentially involved in pollutant degradation and passivation. This provides critical evidence for elucidating the microbiological mechanisms through which the inoculants enhance composting efficiency and cadmium (Cd) passivation.

Analysis of cadmium (Cd) fractionation patterns revealed distinct trends among treatment groups. For EX-Cd, while the CK group showed an initial increase followed by a decrease, both T1 and T2 groups exhibited continuous reductions during the final three composting phases. The T2 group demonstrated optimal performance by reducing the proportion of the more bioavailable EX-Cd fraction from 27 to 9% (see Fig. 13), concurrently with a significant concentration effect that increased the total Cd content (Fig. 12), which was slightly but significantly better than the 11% reduction achieved by T1, thus confirming the composite bacterial agent's effectiveness in EX-Cd reduction.

Oxidizable Cd (OXI-Cd) emerged as the dominant Cd storage form, consistently comprising > 45% of total Cd and showing progressive accumulation throughout composting. Reducible Cd (RED-Cd) exhibited minimal overall variation, with only T1 showing a slight 3% post-composting increase. Both CK and T2 groups showed reductions (7% and 5%, respectively). RES-Cd reached its lowest allocation during the thermophilic phase, suggesting unfavorable formation conditions. Final RES-Cd percentages decreased by 2% in CK, remained stable in T1, and increased 6% in T2.

Discussion

Analysis of in situ screening, compounding, and putrefactive effect of Cd-resistant bacterial strains

To tackle the dual challenges of promoting compost maturation and immobilizing Cd, we employed an in situ screening strategy to isolate three high-performance strains from fresh chicken manure—Enterobacter cloacae (LB3, LB4) and Bacillus velezensis (J-1–2)—which exhibited strong abilities in both Cd passivation and organic decomposition. Antagonism assays confirmed their compatibility, enabling the construction of synergistic consortia. The LB3 + LB4 and LB3 + LB4 + J-1–2 combinations outperformed single strains, achieving EX-Cd passivation efficiencies of 69.25% and 71.35% and enhancing decomposition rates by 12.70% and 12.79% over CK, respectively. These results underscore the value of a rationally designed multi-strain strategy, justifying the selection of these consortia for further composting trials.

Influence of microbial additives on the humification process of composts

In the initial composting phase, T1 and T2 had higher pH than CK. Inoculated Enterobacter cloacae and Bacillus velezensis accelerated organic matter decomposition (releasing ammonia) and nitrogen mineralization (generating ammonium to neutralize acids) (Chi et al. 2025), jointly raising pH. These microbes also metabolized labile substrates (e.g., sugars, proteins) to produce heat, speeding T1/T2 heating and extending thermophilic phases. Notably, though Bacillus velezensis in T2 is thermotolerant (Arya et al. 2025), its early substrate competition may have suppressed some bacteria, slowing decomposition and heating. In contrast, CK (amended with sterile water) heated slower due to limited microbial diversity and low metabolic activity.

By composting completion, all groups had C/N < 20 (mature compost threshold; C/N < 20 indicates full decomposition (Azis et al. 2023)). T1 had the lowest C/N, due to enhanced microbial organic matter degradation (Xiaorui et al. 2023). Notably, T2’s C/N (21.01) was slightly higher than CK’s (19.05) in the thermophilic phase, possibly from (1) Bacillus velezensis (J-1–2) partially suppressing native cellulose-degrading microbes (Chenchen et al. 2022), slowing T2’s carbon degradation, and (2) Bacillus velezensis preferring labile carbon (e.g., sugars) for proliferation over complex lignocellulose, temporarily slowing total organic carbon reduction. However, T2’s C/N matched CK’s in maturation, showing uncompromised organic matter decomposition. T2 also had better nitrogen retention (higher NO3−-N). This highlights the composite inoculum’s stage-specific effects: early nitrogen fixation/community establishment, later enhanced humification/passivation.

During the thermophilic phase, T1 and T2 groups demonstrated the most pronounced moisture reduction rates. This resulted from dual mechanisms: (1) water utilization for microbial growth and (2) activated water consumption by the inoculated microbial consortia to boost metabolic activity (Cao et al. 2024). These processes stimulated organic matter mineralization and heat generation, consequently accelerating moisture evaporation and loss.

All three groups ultimately reached the target E4/E6 ratio for compost maturity (Ozdemir et al. 2019), with T1 and T2 achieving the standard range by day 26. T2 showed the lowest E4/E6 ratio during maturation, indicating enhanced organic matter decomposition via microbial activity, confirming the composite consortium promotes thorough degradation. T2’s Bacillus velezensis secreted plant growth hormones like IAA (Kenfaoui et al. 2024), accelerating seed germination—explaining T2’s fastest GI increase, reaching 80% (phytotoxin-free (Kong et al. 2024)) by day 23. Inoculation neutralized germination inhibitors and accelerated maturation. Bacillus sphaericus modulated nitrogen metabolism via suppressing ammonification, immobilizing ammonium, enhancing nitrification, and facilitating nitrogen fixation (Kazutaka et al. 2017). Thus, T2 had optimal nitrogen speciation: minimal ammonium, maximal nitrate.

Influence of microbial additives on microbial community succession in composting

As the composting progressed, all treatment groups demonstrated increasing bacterial OTU numbers across all four phases. Notably, both bacterial inoculant groups exhibited more pronounced increases than the CK group. This enhancement primarily resulted from the added bacterial inoculants optimizing the composting environment through synergistic effects, directing microbial community dynamics, and accelerating the process—all of which collectively fostered microbial proliferation and diversity.

All treatments showed “high-low–high” fluctuations in microbial diversity during composting. In the warming phase, inoculated thermophiles dominated T1/T2, decomposing soluble organics (Ayodeji et al. 2022) to support their growth and stimulate overall microbial proliferation, raising diversity rapidly. Thermophilic phase temperature increases suppressed mesophiles, leaving a narrower range of thermotolerant taxa dominant, reducing diversity via drastic community succession (Ilaria et al. 2023), as observed in textile waste composting (Saloua et al. 2021). Maturation phase cooling reactivated mesophiles that utilized thermophilic metabolites, restoring diversity. This succession pattern underscores that the microbial inoculants did not merely increase diversity but actively orchestrated a more dynamic and functional succession process.

Four dominant phyla (Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria) accounted for > 85% of total microbial abundance throughout composting, consistent with prior research (Zhou et al. 2018). Firmicutes (primary carbohydrate degraders (Awasthi et al. 2017)) had the highest relative abundance except in maturation: peaking at 89.51% in thermophilic phases, then dropping sharply to 15.06% in maturation—reflecting heavy early carbohydrate utilization and near-depletion by maturation.

Proteobacteria, the second most abundant phylum, maintained a stable distribution across all phases (Takaku et al. 2006), reaching maximum abundance (46.01–58.90%) during maturation. Meanwhile, the abundance of Chloroflexi, an important group of bacteria for decomposing difficult-to-degrade macromolecules, also increased significantly (to 11.82–15.36%) during the humification period. The substantial presence of Proteobacteria and Chloroflexi during this phase confirms maturation as the critical period for lignocellulose decomposition, corroborating Qing’s findings (Qingxin et al. 2019). Bacteroidetes possess similar capabilities for decomposing both sugars and macromolecular organic matter (Pascal et al. 2019). The cellulose-rich rice husk and feather amendments in this experiment provided optimal substrates for Bacteroidetes growth. Notably, their abundance decreased significantly during the maturation phase compared to the thermophilic phases, suggesting near-complete decomposition of these macromolecules prior to maturation.

The microbial community composition exhibited distinct phase-dependent shifts during composting. In the initial phase, dominant genera included Prevotella, Phascolarctobacterium, Megamonas, and Lactobacillus, while the maturation phase was characterized by Vogesella, Aeromonas, Pseudomonas, and Thauera dominance. Notably, Bacteroides reached peak abundance during the thermophilic phase, consistent with its proteolytic function in degrading readily available substrates. Pseudomonas demonstrated a characteristic progression: lowest abundance during warming, moderate increase in the thermophilic phase, and maximal levels during maturation; this pattern correlated with post-thermophilic nitrate accumulation dynamics.

Effect of microbial additives on the passivation of heavy gold during the composting process

Analysis of Cd speciation under different treatments revealed that OXI-Cd consistently constituted the dominant fraction. Throughout composting, OXI-Cd content progressively increased, thereby enhancing Cd immobilization. This observed increase likely stems from microbial metabolites (e.g., oxidative enzymes) generated during composting (Changning et al. 2021), which facilitate Cd ion oxidation and subsequent conversion from reduced to oxidized states.

Although OXI-Cd was identified as the dominant and increasingly stabilized fraction during composting, its environmental stability under field conditions warrants further consideration. OXI-Cd, primarily comprising Cd bound to Fe/Mn oxides, is generally less bioavailable than EX-Cd, yet it may still be susceptible to re-mobilization under reducing or acidic soil conditions (Zhang et al. 2016). Therefore, while composting effectively reduces immediate Cd bioavailability, the long-term stability of passivated Cd in agricultural soils—particularly under fluctuating redox potential or pH—remains a critical factor for environmental safety. Future studies should assess the leaching behavior and plant uptake of compost-derived Cd in field trials to validate the sustainability of this passivation strategy.

RED-Cd represented the second largest fraction but remained relatively stable across all treatments. During the thermophilic phase, T1 and T2 groups exhibited significant EX-Cd reduction concurrent with RES-Cd increase, demonstrating favorable conditions for Cd immobilization. This stabilization likely derived from dual mechanisms: (1) organic matter decomposition-induced heat and moisture loss, as supported by microbial metabolic heat generation during composting ((Jain et al. 2018; Awasthi et al. 2017)), and (2) complexation between Cd and humic substance functional groups. Furthermore, the inoculated microbial consortia contributed through surface functional groups (carboxyl, amino, hydroxyl) that adsorbed EX-Cd, converting exchangeable fractions to cell-bound forms (Zhu et al. 2024). Notably, genera such as Thauera, Aeromonas, and Pseudomonas exhibited strong negative correlations with EX-Cd (p < 0.01), suggesting their active role in immobilizing Cd through mechanisms such as adsorption, complexation, or biomineralization. These microbial groups likely contributed to the transformation of EX-Cd into less bioavailable forms (e.g., OXI-Cd and RES-Cd), thereby reducing Cd mobility and ecotoxicity.

In this study, we observed a steady increase in the abundance of Burkholderia throughout the composting process. This genus has been previously reported to possess metal-resistant mechanisms, including the ability to bind or immobilize heavy metals such as cadmium through biosorption, bioaccumulation, or the production of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) (Honghui et al. 2012). For instance, Burkholderia spp. have been shown to enhance Cd immobilization in contaminated soils via surface complexation and redox transformations. Their increasing abundance in our composting system suggests a potential role in facilitating Cd passivation, possibly through similar mechanisms.

LSD analysis indicated significantly enhanced OXI-Cd immobilization in all treatment groups versus control (p < 0.05), despite comparable EX-Cd passivation efficiency. Therefore, the composite microbial inoculum (T2) not only enriched these functional taxa but also optimized the composting environment to favor Cd stabilization, ultimately reducing Cd bioavailability and potential ecological risk.

Conclusions

In this study, aerobic composting experiments of chicken manure were conducted by applying a composite microbial inoculant (screened and prepared to simultaneously enhance compost maturation and passivate Cd). The effects of the inoculant on compost maturity parameters, bacterial community structure, and the speciation distribution of heavy metal Cd were systematically investigated. The results demonstrated that inoculation with the composite microbial agent significantly accelerated the humification process and enhanced Cd passivation capacity during aerobic composting. Specifically, the inoculant prolonged the thermophilic phase, improved nitrogen retention, and promoted compost maturation, as evidenced by the lowest C/N ratio (14.88) and E4/E6 value (1.41) in the T2 treatment. Notably, a Cd passivation efficiency of 66.7% was achieved, effectively reducing the exchangeable Cd fraction from 27 to 9%. Microbial community analysis indicated that key genera such as Thauera (Proteobacteria) were strongly correlated with the formation of residual Cd. These key taxa may reduce Cd bioavailability and mobility through mechanisms such as ion exchange, adsorption-complexation, and metabolic secretions that alter compost physicochemical properties and heavy metal speciation, thereby mitigating contamination risks. Concurrently, they participate in nutrient cycling and enzymatic regulation, optimizing the composting environment and maturation process.

These findings provide important microbiological evidence and innovative technical pathways for the safe composting of livestock manure, soil ecological protection, and sustainable agricultural development. However, this study has certain limitations; for instance, further research is needed to determine the optimal conditions for enhanced Cd immobilization by the inoculated Enterobacter and Bacillus strains and to elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying their interactions with Cd immobilization in subsequent experiments.
