Health commitment’s associations with health outcomes are independent of health behaviours: insights from a cross-sectional population health survey in Singapore
Authors: Lixia Ge, Yun Hu, Annie Tan, Cherlyen Teo, Chun Wei Yap, Yeuk Fan Ng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn individual’s commitment to health is a crucial component of their healthcare engagement. However, research is scarce that has systematically examined the association between commitment and health behaviours as well as health outcomes. We conducted this study to examine their associations in a representative community-dwelling adult population in Singapore.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analysed data from the Khoo Teck Puat Hospital and Yishun Community Hospital’s Population Health Survey 2022, which interviewed a representative sample of community-dwelling residents aged 18 years and above in Singapore’s Northern Region between July and December 2022. Health commitment was assessed using the validated Commitment subscale of the Altarum Consumer Engagement (ACE) Measure™. Assessment of health behaviours encompassed smoking status, alcohol consumption, vegetable and fruit consumption, sleep duration and physical activity. Additionally, various health outcomes spanning physical, mental, and social health domains, as well as healthcare utilisation indicators, were collected. Social-demographic factors associated with commitment were identified via multiple linear regression. Multiple logistic or ordinal logistic regression models were employed to examine the associations between commitment and individual health behaviours and outcomes, adjusting for basic demographic variables.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 49.7 years, ranging from 18 to 94 years, with 51% being females, and 68.2% belonging to the Chinese ethnicity. The results identified age group, ethnicity, marital status, housing type, monthly household income, and number of medications as factors associated with commitment. Significant associations were found between health commitment and various health behaviours. Furthermore, higher health commitment was consistently associated with an increased likelihood of subjective health outcomes (odds ratios range:1.17–1.28) and a reduced likelihood of healthcare utilisation (odds ratios range: 0.91–0.93), even after adjusting for health behaviours.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the importance of assessing and addressing health commitment in healthcare practice and policy. Strategies to enhance health commitment hold potential for improving health behaviours and health outcomes, thereby contributing to overall well-being. However, it is important to note that the cross-sectional nature of this study does not allow for confirmation of causal pathways. Hence, longitudinal studies are needed to further clarify these relationships and their potential impact on overall well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24610-2.

Background

As the global burden of chronic diseases continues to escalate, there is growing recognition of the need to prioritise preventive health behaviours and advocate for healthy lifestyle choices to mitigate the impact of chronic conditions and improve population health outcomes [1]. Despite concerted efforts to promote health literacy and encourage adoption of healthy behaviours, many individuals encounter challenges in maintaining adherence to these behaviours over time due to multifaceted factors, including physiological factors, psychological barriers, socio-economic disparities, cultural influences, environmental constraints, and competing demands on individuals’ time and resources [2–4]. Additionally, an individual’s commitment to health, a critical dimension of health engagement [5, 6], is increasingly acknowledged as a crucial determinant influencing their adoption and engagement in health-promoting activities [7].

Health commitment is defined as an individual’s dedication, motivation, or a freely chosen internal resolve to maintain or improve one’s health. It involves a personal pledge to engage in behaviours that support health and well-being over the long term [5, 8]. The concept of health commitment is applicable to a broad spectrum of behaviours and practices, including regular physical activity, adherence to a balanced diet, avoidance of harmful substances such as tobacco and excessive alcohol consumption aiming at enhancing physical, mental, and emotional well-being. It also serves as the foundation for both activation and engagement as a committed individual is more likely to seek information and improve skills (activation) and take active steps (engagement) towards maintaining their health. The significance of health commitment in influencing long-term adoption of healthy behaviours and its pivotal role in shaping individuals’ clinical outcomes such as improved diabetes control and quality of life have garnered increased attention from researchers, healthcare professionals, and policymakers in recent years [9–11].

Despite the growing recognition of health commitment’s importance, there remains a need for a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing individuals’ health commitment and the associations between health commitment and health behaviours, as well as health outcomes, such as perceived physical and mental health status, healthcare utilisation, and quality of life. Understanding the factors associated with health commitment and its associations with healthy behaviours and health outcomes provides valuable insights into the pathways through which commitment to health influences different aspects of health and is paramount for the development of targeted interventions and public health strategies aimed at promoting healthier lifestyles and improving health states. Hence, we conducted this study to examine the associations between health commitment, health behaviours, and health outcomes in a community-dwelling adult population in Singapore, contributing to the growing body of evidence supporting the importance of fostering a culture of health commitment and empowerment to enhance individual and population health.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study employed representative data from the Population Health Survey (PHS) 2022 conducted by Khoo Teck Puat Hospital and Yishun Community Hospital (KTPH& YCH) in the Northern Region of Singapore, spanning from July to December 2022.

Setting and participants

The study targeted community-dwelling adult residents staying in the Northern Region of Singapore. To ensure adequate precision in measuring major disease prevalence and health risk factors, with a margin of error of 0.05 and a 95% confidence level, a minimum sample size of 1200 eligible participants was required. To account for individuals with higher needs and risks, the sample size was expanded to 2500 participants, with oversampling of individuals aged 60 years and above. Neyman’s allocation method [12] was employed to calculate sample sizes for four age categories: 18–59 years, 60–69 years, 70–79 years, and ≥ 80 years.

Two-stage sampling method involving household and individual selection was employed. Trained interviewers conducted eligibility screening and recruitment during their visits to the sampled households. The Kish grid, a traditional method for selecting a participant randomly from households with multiple eligible participants to avoid selection bias [13], was used to facilitate participant selection within individual households with more than one eligible resident. A total of 2,499 individuals participated in the survey. To ensure data completeness, accuracy, consistency, and validity, as well as to ensure that all responses reflect the personal experiences and perceptions of the participants in self-reported measures and subjective health outcomes, participants with proxy responses were excluded from the analysis. This resulted in a final sample size of 2,430 for this study.

Measures and data collection

The full questionnaire, as outlined in the Supplementary file, was designed to gather information to optimise care for residents in the northern region of Singapore and improve population health outcomes. It comprises 14 sections covering demographics, cognition, nutrition, sleep and rest patterns, physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, screening and vaccination, activation, health experience, healthcare utilisation and experience of care, volunteering, clinical/health conditions, and health-related quality of life.

Prior to deployment, the questionnaire underwent pre-testing with a sample of 30 participants, including members of the target population and staff, to assess clarity, relevance, and comprehensibility. Demographic information was not collected for the 30 participants. The process helped identify ambiguities, issues with question flow, structure, formatting, and response clarity. Feedback from the pre-testing informed refinements to question wording and overall structure of the questionnaire, ensuring the readability and effectiveness of the self-developed items. Notably, no modifications were made to the validated measures used in this study.

The survey questionnaire was administered via an electronic data collection platform on tablets during face-to-face interviews conducted by trained interviewers. The platform featured logic branching functions to minimise input errors and ensure response consistency. By enabling direct data capture, it eliminated the need for manual entry, reducing the risk of human error. Data quality was rigorously controlled: for each interviewer, 50% of their interviews were randomly selected for quality checks via telephone validation, and an additional 10% underwent verification by the research team. During these calls, respondents confirmed their residential address and answered verification questions related to approximately ten survey items, further enhancing data accuracy and quality.

This study utilised a subset of the PHS 2022 data, focusing on key variables such as demographics, health behaviours (smoking, alcohol consumption, vegetable consumption, physical activity, and sleep hours), health commitment, and subjective assessments of physical, mental, and social health. Additionally, self-reported healthcare utilisation data were included in the analysis. The details of these data were provided below.

Independent variables

Commitment

The 4-item Commitment subscale of the Altarum Consumer Engagement (ACE) Measure™ [5], was employed to assess individuals’ health commitment. It consists of the following four statements: Q1. Even when life is stressful, I know I can continue to do the things that keep me healthy. Q2. When I work to improve my health, I succeed. Q3. I can stick with plans to exercise and eat a healthy diet. Q4. I handle my health well. Each item offered five response options on a 5-point scale: “0 = Strongly disagree”, “1 = Disagree”, “2 = Neither agree nor disagree”, “3 = Agree”, and “4 = Strongly agree”. The Commitment score, ranging from 0 to 25, was derived by multiplying the mean score of the four items by 6.25 [5]. The score was used as a continuous variable in this study, with higher scores representing higher levels of health commitment. The 4-item Commitment demonstrated favourable readability, construct validity, and internal consistency reliability among the local adult population [14]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the 4 items in the study population was 0.82.

Health behaviours

The health behaviours measured in this study included current smoking status (currently smoking vs. not smoking), alcohol consumption in the past 12 months (yes vs. no), vegetable or fruit consumption (< 2 servings vs. ≥2 servings), time spent on moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) in the last 7 days or a typical week (< 150 min vs. ≥150 min), and the frequency of active recreation participation (categorised as never or occasionally vs. frequent). Additionally, the self-reported number of sleep hours was recorded. Based on the recommended hours of sleep per day outlined on the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention website [15], participants were categorised into two groups: “less optimum” for those not meeting recommendation or “optimum” for those meeting recommendation (7 or more hours per night for adults aged 18–60 years [16], 7–9 h for adults aged 61–64 years, and 7–8 h for those aged 65 years and older [17]).

Dependent variables – subjective physical, mental, and social health outcomes, and healthcare utilisation

Self-perceived physical, mental, and social health were evaluated using validated items and/or measures. Healthcare utilisation focused on those in emergency departments (ED) and inpatient wards (IP).

Subjective physical health

Self-perceived nutritional status was accessed using a single question: “What is your self-view of nutritional status?” with three response options: 0 for “Views self as being malnourished”, 1 for “Is uncertain of nutritional status”, and 2 for “Views self as having no nutritional problem”. The responses were then recoded into a binary variable, where 0 represented poor nutritional status and 1 represented good nutritional status.

Relative health status was assessed by asking participants: “In comparison with other people of the same age, how do you consider your health status?” Response options included “Not as good”, “Does not know”, “As good”, and “Better”. For the analysis, participants who responded “Not as good” and “Does not know” were grouped as “0 = poor health” and those who responded “As good” and “Better” were grouped as “1 = good health”.

Frailty status was assessed by trained interviewers using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), where participants were asked to select a score that best described their current status from 9-point scale with corresponding descriptors [18, 19]. Participants were categorised into two groups based on their responses: “0 = frail” (CFS scores ≥ 4) and “1 = not frail” (CFS scores ≤ 3).

Subjective mental and social health

Presence of depressive symptoms, as an indicator of mental health, was assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [20], a well-validated measure for depressive symptoms in the local general population [18, 21]. Each item was rated on a 4-point scale (0 = not at all, 1 = several days, 2 = more than half the days, 3 = nearly every day), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 27. The PHQ-9 demonstrated good internal consistency reliability in local population [22, 23]. In this study population, it exhibited a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87, indicating strong internal consistency. Participants were classified into two categories: 0 for “depressive” (PHQ-9 scores of 5–27) and 1 for “not depressive” (PHQ-9 scores of 0–4) for the analysis.

Social isolation and loneliness, reflecting individuals’ social well-being, were measured by the Lubben Social Network Scale-6 (LSNS-6) [24] and the three-item UCLA Loneliness Scale [25], respectively. For the LSNS-6, total score ranged from 0 to 30. Participants were categorised into two groups based on their total scores: 0 = socially isolated (score 0–12), and 1 = socially connected (score 13–30). The three-item UCLA Loneliness Scale yielded total scores ranged from 3 to 9, with higher scores indicating higher loneliness levels. Scores of 6–9 were classified as “0 = lonely”, while scores of 3–5 were classified as “1 = not lonely” [21]. Both measures have been extensively validated and widely used in research involving the general population in local contexts [21, 26]. In this study, they demonstrated strong internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.82 and 0.90, respectively.

Healthcare utilisation

Healthcare utilisation in ED and IP was obtained by asking individuals to report the number of visits to ED and the number of admissions to public hospitals (including community hospitals) in the past 12 months, respectively. Participants were classified into two groups based on their responses: 0 for those reporting no visits or admissions, and “1 for those reporting one or more ED visits or hospital admissions.

Confounding factors

Individuals’ socio-demographic variables used in the study included age (categorised into four age groups), gender (male and female), ethnicity (Chinese, Malay, Indian, and others), marital status (single, married, and divorced/separated/widowed), religion (free-thinker and with religion), work status (working, homemaker/housewife/unemployed, and inactive), and highest education level (no formal education/primary, secondary/high school/diploma, and bachelor’s degree or higher). Additionally, living arrangement, housing type, and monthly household income levels were also included.

The study also included health-related factors collected during the survey, such as individuals’ body mass index (BMI), medical history of 22 chronic conditions, and the number of types of long-term medications participants were taking. BMI was classified into four categories: <18.5 kg/m2, 18.5 to < 23.0 kg/m2, 23.0 to < 27.0 kg/m2, and ≥ 27.0 kg/m2. Multimorbidity (none, 1 or 2, and ≥ 3) was determined by the number of chronic conditions that individuals reported. The number of types of long-term medications was categorised into three groups: none, 1–4, and ≥ 5.

Those socio-demographic variables and health-related factors were treated as confounding factors due to their potential impact on both the independent and dependent variables, as established in literature. Adjusting for these factors helped minimise bias and control for variability, ensuring that the relationships being investigated in the study were more accurately reflected, thereby enhancing the validity and reliability of the findings.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of participants were described using frequency (n) and weighted percentage (%). Descriptive statistics including mean, standard deviation (SD), median, first quartile (Q1) and third quartile (Q3) of the health commitment scores were presented for individuals with different characteristics. Due to the skewness of the data, comparisons of commitment scores across characteristics groups were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis rank tests.

Multiple linear regression analysis was employed to identify the factors associated with health commitment (dependent variable), incorporating all observed confounding factors described above (independent variables). Regression diagnostics were performed to assess the model specification, normality of residuals, and leverage. To examine collinearity among the variables, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated. A VIF higher than 10 indicated problematic levels of multicollinearity [27], suggesting the need to either combine variables based on their conceptual similarity or remove the least important variable. All variables included in the regression model had VIF values below 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity issues. In this analysis, the commitment score served as the dependent variable and all the characteristic variables were treated as independent variables. The adjusted beta coefficients (Coeffs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

The distribution of health behaviours in terms of smoking status, alcohol consumption, sleep hours, vegetable or fruit consumption, time spent on MVPA, and frequency of active recreation participation were described using frequency and weighted percentage. Health commitment scores for individual behavioural groups were described and compared using the Mann–Whitney tests.

Since all the health outcome variables were binary, the difference in commitment scores across different health outcome states were compared using the Mann–Whitney tests. Additionally, three logistic regression models were performed to examine the associations between commitment (independent variable) and individual health outcomes (dependent variables). Model 1 examined the unadjusted associations between commitment and individual health outcomes. Model 2 extended Model 1 by adjusting for the variables listed in Table 1, including socio-demographic variables, BMI category, the number of medical conditions, and the number of medication types. Model 3 further adjusted for the health behaviours detailed in Table 2, in addition to the variables adjusted in model 2. Variables were selected based on their theoretical relevance to health commitment and health outcomes, as established in prior literature. Adjusting for these factors helped minimise bias and control for variability, ensuring that the relationships being investigated in the study were more accurately reflected, thereby enhancing the validity and reliability of the findings. Statistical techniques such as stepwise selection or backward elimination were not solely relied upon, as they potentially risked excluding theoretically important variables that may not have shown strong statistical associations in this dataset but were supported by prior evidence.

The model specification of the logistic regression models was evaluated using the linktest command to identify potential specification error. To assess goodness-of-fit, several measures were employed, including the Hosmer- Lemeshow test, pseudo R-square, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to assess how well the model fit the data [28]. Additionally, collinearity diagnostics were performed to detect multicollinearity among variables in Models 2 and 3. To identify influential observations or outliers, deviance residuals, standardised Pearson residuals, and leverage (hat values) were calculated and plotted against the predicted probabilities and index numbers [29].

For Models 2 and 3, the goodness-of fit results indicated a good model fit, no evidence of collinearity issues was found, and no significantly influential observations were identified. Hence, no variables or cases were excluded from the models. All the statistical analyses were conducted using Stata/SE 17.0. P < 0.05 was set as the level of significance.

The unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% CIs from Models 1, 2, and 3, which examined the associations between commitment and individual health outcomes, are presented in the Results section. Results

Participants’ profiles

The study encompassed 2,430 participants ranging in age from 18 to 94 years, with a mean age of 49.7 years and a SD of 16.9 years. The largest demographic segment fell between 40 and 59 years old (43.2%), followed by those aged 18 to 39 years (35.6%). Female participants constituted 51% of the population, and 68.2% were identified as Chinese. The majority of the participants were married (68.2%), working (71.0%), and living with their spouse (65.6%). Approximately a quarter (25.8%) of the population had a BMI ≥ 27 kg/m2 with 32.3% reporting at least one of the 22 listed medical conditions, and 28.5% taking long-term medications.

Factors associated with health commitment

Comparing commitment scores across the socio-demographic categories revealed significant differences in commitment scores across age groups, gender, ethnicity, marital status, housing type, and monthly household income (all p < 0.05). Individuals aged 40 to 79 years, male, of Indian or other ethnicities, married, and residing in public 3-room flats or above exhibited higher mean commitment scores. Individuals with an optimum BMI (23 - <27 kg/m2) exhibited the highest commitment scores (mean: 19.1, SD: 3.1), while those with three or more medical conditions demonstrated the lowest commitment scores (mean: 17.9, SD: 3.6) among their counterparts (Table 1).

The multiple linear regression model’s outcomes, delving into the links between these attributes and health commitment, unveiled compelling insights. Notably, in contrast to their counterparts aged 18 to 39 years, those aged 40 to 59 years and 60 to 79 years exhibited higher health commitment scores (Coeff: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.28, 1.02 and Coeff: 1.25, 95% CI: 0.76, 1.74, respectively). Moreover, individuals of Indian and other ethnicities exhibited higher health commitment scores (Coeff: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.40, 1.28 and Coeff: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.04, 1.67, respectively) in comparison to individuals of Chinese ethnicity. Additionally, individuals who were divorced, separated, or widowed displayed higher commitment scores (Coeff: 1.04, 95% CI: 0.30, 1.77) than their single counterparts. Furthermore, individuals who were inactive had higher commitment scores (Coeff: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.23, 1.06) than their employed counterparts. In addition, attainment of higher education and residing in larger housing types or private properties were generally associated with elevated commitment scores, while individuals with chronic conditions exhibited lower health commitment scores (Table 1).

Health commitment and health behaviours

Table 2 outlines the distribution of health behaviours along with their corresponding health commitment scores. Among the population, 13.8% were identified as current smokers, exhibiting lower commitment scores (mean score:18.1), compared to non-smokers (mean score: 18.9). Nearly 74% of the population abstained from alcohol consumption in the past 12 months, showcasing generally higher commitment scores (18.9), compared to those who consumed alcohol. Moreover, approximately 59% of participants achieved the recommended nightly hours of sleep, which correlated with elevated commitment scores. Similarly, individuals who consumed two or more servings of vegetable or fruit daily (86%) tended to score higher in commitment (mean score: 19.1), compared to those who consumed less than two servings, who had a mean score of 16.5. Regarding physical activity, participants who engaged in at least 150 min of MVPA or participated in active recreation more frequently demonstrated heightened commitment scores.

Association between commitment and subjective physical health outcomes

Health commitment demonstrated a positive correlation with self-perceived nutritional status, health status, and frailty status (all p < 0.001). Specifically, with each unit increase in commitment score, the likelihood of reporting good nutritional status increased by 1.29 times (Unadjusted OR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.24–1.34). Similarly, for each unit rise in commitment score, the odds of reporting a good health status increased by 1.33 times, while the odds of being not frail were 1.19 times (Table 3, Model 1). The pseudo R-square, AIC, and BIC for all the models are presented in the Supplementary file (Table S1).

Greater health commitment showed a significant inverse correlation with both ED visits and inpatient admissions (both p < 0.001). Discussion

This study identified factors associated with health commitment and examined its association with health behaviours and outcomes within an Asian setting. Consistent with existing literature highlighting the significance of health commitment, our findings revealed positive associations between health commitment and the adoption of healthy behaviours and positive health outcomes. Importantly, our study demonstrated that health commitment remained associated with subjective physical, mental, and social health outcomes, as well as healthcare utilisation at ED and inpatient wards, even after adjusting for characteristics and health behaviours. These results offer valuable insights into the potential mechanisms by which health commitment influences various aspects of health and underscore its importance in informing targeted interventions and public health strategies aimed at promoting healthier lifestyles and improving health states. By elucidating the intricate connections between health commitment, health behaviours, and health outcomes, this study contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the importance of fostering a culture of health commitment and empowerment to enhance individual and population health.

Despite the implementation of numerous health and well-being programmes aimed at shaping individuals’ health behaviours and lifestyles, a common trend presented is the declining programme participation rate and poor adherence rate over time [2]. This suggests that participants may lack full commitment to making meaningful improvements to their health. Merely participating in health-promoting programmes does not guarantee improved well-being because achieving better health outcomes necessitates active engagement and personal commitment. To enhance the effectiveness of such programmes and its sustainability, it is imperative to access and address individuals’ commitment to health. Identifying and addressing factors associated with health commitment offers potential solutions for promoting greater dedication. Our study, employing both univariate and multiple linear regression analyses, revealed associations between health commitment and various factors, including age group, ethnicity, marital status, housing type, monthly household income, and number of medications, while religion, highest education, living arrangement, and number of medications were not significant. These findings, which partially align with prior literature [14, 30, 31], offer insights into individual triage and help identify those who may benefit from coaching on commitment-focused coaching tailored to specific populations.

While there is substantial evidence in literature supporting the importance of patient activation, the impacts of patient engagement, which encompasses a broader scope than patient activation, have been less studied. This may be attributed to the inherent complexity in defining and measuring the impact of patient engagement as it is influenced by a multitude of context-based factors. Additionally, establishing a proven causal effect between engagement and desired outcomes remains challenging [32]. However, despite the limited literature on the subject, patient engagement has shown associations with several positive outcomes, including improved health outcomes, reduced healthcare costs, increased patient satisfaction, enhanced adherence to treatment plans, and improved self-management of chronic conditions [33]. Our study revealed that health commitment, as a crucial component of engagement, shares similarities with patient activation regarding its associations with health behaviours (such as cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, optimum sleep hours, healthy diet, and physical activity) and health outcomes (including self-perceived nutritional status, health and frail status, depressive symptoms, social isolation, loneliness, and healthcare utilisation). The consistent associations observed between commitment and health outcomes, even after adjusting for health behaviours, suggest that commitment plays both direct and indirect influences (via health behaviours) on these health outcomes.

These findings have significant implications for healthcare practice and policy. Firstly, they underscore the need to assess and strengthen health commitment across care settings. This encompasses individuals’ engagement practices, resources utilisation, and ability to effectively harness collaborative opportunities [6, 34]. Public health professionals should develop tailored interventions to enhance health commitment and behaviours to optimise the effectiveness of health programmes.

Moreover, our results suggest that interventions focusing solely on health behaviours may be insufficient for improving subjective health outcomes or reducing healthcare utilisation. A holistic approach that integrates commitment-building with behavioural interventions may be more effective in achieving positive health outcomes. Additionally, healthcare organisations and policymakers should recognise health commitment as a key determinant in health promotion efforts, embedding strategies that empower individuals to take ownership of their health.

To translate these insights into practice, we recommend the following actions: Integrate health commitment assessment into routine health check-ups to identify individuals at risk of poor health outcomes.Leverage commitment scores to tailor support, such as personalised counselling or structured programmes for low commitment individuals as part of their lifelong care plans.Develop personalised care plans and action plans based on commitment levels, encouraging ambitious health goals for highly committed individuals and smaller and achievable steps for those with lower commitment.Harness commitment-based risk stratification as part of population segmentation tools to allocate resources more effectively so that care plans for different population segments can more effectively drive health outcomes and equity.Develop and implement tiered interventions, such as one-on-one coaching for low commitment individuals and, group-based programmes for moderate commitment.Train healthcare providers in motivational interviewing to enhance health commitment and address ambivalence.

This study has several strengths, notably, the representativeness of its participants enhances the generalisability of the findings to the adult population in the Northern region of Singapore. Furthermore, the survey collected an extensive array of socio-demographic factors and health behaviours, facilitating better factor examination and thorough adjustment in analyses. However, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, the cross-sectional design constrains our ability to infer causality between health commitment, health behaviours, and outcomes. Longitudinal studies would offer a more robust understanding of the effects of health commitment on health behaviours and outcomes. Additionally, reliance on self-reported survey data introduces potential biases, particularly social desirability bias and recall bias, which may affect the reliability of the reported health behaviours and outcomes. Social desirability bias might cause participants to overreport behaviours perceived as favourable (e.g., healthy eating or physical activity) or underreport less favourable behaviours (e.g., smoking or alcohol consumption), potentially inflating associations between health commitment, health behaviours and outcomes, despite efforts to assure participants there were no right or wrong answers. Recall bias could lead participants to misreport past behaviours, particularly regarding the frequency or duration of specific physical activities. These biases necessitate cautious interpretation of the associations observed, as they could either overestimate or underestimate the strength of relationships between health commitment and health outcomes.

Moreover, although the study adjusted for a range of sociodemographic and health behaviour variables, residual confounding due to unmeasured variables may still impact our findings. Factors such as genetic predispositions, specific health conditions, or environmental factors could confound the associations observed. Future studies should incorporate objective measures of health behaviours and outcomes and control for a broader range of potential confounders to enhance the robustness of these associations and more accurately clarify the role of health commitment in promoting health outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study enhances our understanding of the associations between health commitment, health behaviours, and health outcomes. The findings underscore the potential role of health commitment in shaping healthy behaviours - such as adequate sleep, fruits and vegetable, and regular physical activity - alongside different aspects of health outcomes. Nevertheless, longitudinal studies are needed to examine the causal relationship between commitment and health outcomes and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of these associations. Such research will support the development of more effective intervention strategies to improve health commitment tailored to meet the specific needs of this population.
