Evaluation of subclinical atherosclerosis in women with polycystic ovary syndrome based on ultra-high-frequency ultrasound and vector flow imaging
Authors: Yinghui Dong, Shaofu Hong, Zimo Wang, Di Song, Mengmeng Liu, Jieying Zeng, Yigang Du, Jinfeng Xu, Liyun Shi, Wenjing Gao, Fajin Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe diagnosis of subclinical atherosclerosis is the key to the primary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD). Considering the increased risk of CVD in women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), the purpose of this study was to detect the early damage of the carotid artery in PCOS patients using carotid ultra-high frequency ultrasound (UHFUS) and vector flow imaging (V Flow).MethodsThis study prospectively recruited 40 PCOS patients and 30 age-matched control subjects (mean age 29±5 vs. 30±6 years, P=0.189). All subjects underwent comprehensive medical history collection, anthropometric and hematological examination. The maximum frequency 33 and 24 MHz probes were used to measure the intima/media thickness of common carotid artery (CCA) and the intima-media thickness (IMT) of carotid bulb (CB). The presence of atherosclerotic plaques was also assessed. V Flow was used to measure the wall shear stress (WSS), long-axis strain (LAS) and oscillatory shear index (OSI) of the anterior and posterior walls in CCA and CB.ResultsSubclinical atherosclerosis was detected in 20.0% of PCOS patients and 3.3% of the control group (P=0.039). Clinical/biochemical-hyperandrogenism and insulin resistance (IR) demonstrated significantly higher prevalence in the PCOS group (40% vs. 0%, P<0.001) (45% vs. 3.3%, P<0.001) compared to the control group. The PCOS group exhibited a higher mean body mass index (BMI) (23.7±4.1 vs. 21.1±2.62 kg/m2, P=0.002) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) (0.825±0.086 vs. 0.775±0.043, P<0.001) compared to the control group. Compared with the control group, the intima thickness (IT) (0.165±0.055 vs. 0.126±0.022, P=0.001) and IMT (0.767±0.281 vs. 0.681±0.086, P=0.002) of the PCOS group increased, while the WSS of the anterior wall (1.10±0.377 vs. 1.26±0.325 Pa, P=0.027) in CCA and the WSS of the anterior and posterior walls in CB decreased ( 0.450±0.175 vs. 0.572±0.126 Pa, P<0.001; 0.727±0.383 vs. 1.08±0.477 Pa, P=0.001). When the WSS was less than 0.985 Pa, the IT of CCA was thickened, especially for PCOS patients.ConclusionsThe incidence of early vascular injury in PCOS patients is higher than that in healthy individuals. In addition to systemic metabolic factors such as androgen levels and IR, local disturbed hemodynamic characteristics are more prone to induce the development of plaques, which could potentially be another contributing factor to the premature onset of carotid atherosclerosis. HRFUS and V Flow imaging would provide additional valuable information for characterizing the early development of atherosclerosis.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder affecting 8–13% women of childbearing age, and up to 70% of affected women remain undiagnosed (1,2). PCOS is not only closely associated with reproductive health disorders but has also been widely recognized as a risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD). Women with PCOS have an increased risk of subclinical cardiovascular disease (SCVD) (3-6). Obese adolescents aged 12 to 18 years with PCOS exhibit a higher arterial stiffness index compared to their obese peers, suggesting that SCVD in these PCOS patients may onset during pre-adulthood (7,8). Subclinical atherosclerosis is the initial stage of the pathological process of atherosclerosis. It indicates that the abnormal physiological remodeling of blood vessels has occurred, but has not yet reached the clinically recognizable disease state. Studies have found that the regression of atherosclerosis is possible in the early stages of disease, especially before young adulthood (<40 years) (9). The existence of this stage provides a crucial window for preventive intervention, which is of great significance for subclinical atherosclerosis assessment and identification in young PCOS women.

Intima-media thickness (IMT) is used as biomarker and an independent predictor of the incidence of CVD. However, recent studies have demonstrated a lack of significant correlation between carotid artery IMT and PCOS (10,11); early meta-analysis found heterogeneity in the continuous increase of IMT in PCOS women (12). Both histomorphological measurement and intravascular high-frequency ultrasound showed that the intima thickness (IT) gradually increased with the development of atherosclerosis (13,14). IT and high intima/media thickness ratio (IMTR) could indicate early arterial wall remodeling, which may provide better evidence for the evaluation of subclinical atherosclerosis than carotid artery IMT (15-18). Current non-invasive ultra-high frequency ultrasound (UHFUS) (>20 MHz) provides the possibility to evaluate the IT and media thickness (MT) of peripheral arteries (19). Due to its relatively superior resolution (axial resolution 50–75 vs. 150–200 μm, 10 MHz conventional ultrasound), UHFUS could achieve sub-millimeter visualization of a single intima or media layer of carotid artery that could not be reliably displayed and identified by standard diagnostic ultrasound, which is critical for early vascular injury assessment.

Blood flow plays an important role in the occurrence and development of atherosclerosis. The relationship between high and low wall shear stress (WSS) and lesions has been widely studied. On the one hand, the low WSS is a hemodynamic phenotype that is prone to atherosclerosis. The high oscillation of WSS usually corresponds to low WSS, which may cause the thickening of IT (20-24). Cibis et al. found that WSS value was inversely proportional to the wall thickness in the analysis of asymptomatic carotid plaques (25). However, the relationship between WSS and the changes of IT of arterial wall in PCOS patients has not been described.

Different measurements of arterial stiffness are another subclinical marker of CVD. Global pulse wave velocity, recognized as the gold standard for arterial stiffness assessment (26), is influenced by physiological factors such as age and obesity independent of PCOS status (7,27). Notably, healthy individuals exhibit natural variations in carotid WSS and pulse wave velocity (28). However, in metabolic disorders such as diabetes or obesity, WSS typically drops below 4 dyn/cm2 in atheroprone regions, coinciding with endothelial dysfunction. The threshold changes need to be explored for the preclinical vascular injury in PCOS population. While carotid artery strain has not been reported in PCOS, new ultrasound-based arterial mechanics measurements emerge. The reduction in carotid wall translation and strain observed in human immunodeficiency virus-infected patients suggests an augmentation in vascular wall stiffness (29). A previous study conducted in patients with end-stage renal disease revealed that this disease independently decreased both transverse radial strain and strain rate, as well as transverse circumferential strain and strain rate (30). These new imaging biomarkers could be used to characterize the premature development of atherosclerosis.

Recently, high frame rate ultrasound vector flow imaging (V Flow) has been developed (31,32), capable of measuring the vector velocities of blood flow. Consequently, WSS in complex flow can be directly measured based on the vector velocities (33,34). Furthermore, the oscillatory shear index (OSI), which indicates the extent of changes in the direction of WSS (35), can be calculated using WSS measurements (36).

The assessment of subclinical atherosclerosis could provide valuable information for primary prevention of CVD. This study utilized UHFUS and V Flow to assess early carotid artery injury in PCOS women, analyze differences in clinical risk factors and local hemodynamic characteristics between PCOS patients and healthy controls, and evaluate their correlation with subclinical atherosclerosis. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2024-2834/rc).

Methods

Study population

This study prospectively enrolled 40 patients with PCOS and 30 age-matched healthy control subjects, comprising a total of 70 females, from January 1, 2024 to August 30, 2024. Patients were recruited from the gynecological endocrine clinic of Shenzhen People’s Hospital consecutively. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Shenzhen People’s Hospital (No. LL-KY-2021685). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All participants received a complete medical history collection, anthropometric and hematological examination.

Inclusion criteria: (I) 20–40 years old; (II) the diagnosis of PCOS patients conforms to the European Society of Endocrinology 2023 guidelines; (III) giving informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: (I) history of clinical atherosclerosis (stable/unstable angina, myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, transient ischemic attack, atrial thromboembolism); (II) severe liver and kidney dysfunction; (III) history of carotid artery stenting or carotid endarterectomy; (IV) abnormal hormone levels for the control group.

Anthropometric and hematological examination

Height and weight were measured by electronic devices (SECA899, Seca GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany), accurate to 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg, respectively. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as: weight (kg)/[height (m)]2 (clinical normal thresholds: 18.5–23.9 kg/m2). Hip circumference and waist circumference were measured with a tape measure, accurate to 0.1 cm. Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was calculated as: waist circumference (cm)/hip circumference (cm). Central obesity was defined as WHR >0.85. After a 30-minute rest period, blood pressure was measured on the right arm in a sitting position using an electronic sphygmomanometer (HBP-1320, Omron European Healthcare B.V., Hoofddorp, Netherlands). Hypertension was defined as resting systolic blood pressure of 130 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure of 80 mmHg.

Fasting blood samples were taken to detect total cholesterol (CHO), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and triglyceride (TG), blood glucose, insulin. Hypercholesterolemia was defined as CHO >5.0 mmol/L and LDL >3.0 mmol/L. Homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as fasting blood glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (mIU/L)/22.5.

High-frequency ultrasound and V Flow

Ultrasound images were collected by an experienced sonographer using the Mindray Resona A20 pro-system (Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) and recorded in video. Thickness measurement position of arterial wall: the right common carotid artery (CCA) was measured at the posterior wall at 1 cm away from the carotid bifurcation on right side; the carotid bulb (CB) was the maximum thickness of carotid wall, by the maximum frequency 33 and 24 MHz transducers respectively. The presence of subclinical atherosclerosis, defined as an IMT greater than 0.9 mm and/or plaque formation at any examination site, was also evaluated (37,38). Plaques were defined as an increase in vascular wall thickness that exceeded 50% of the surrounding IMT or a wall thickness that exceeded 1.5 mm. On V Flow mode, videos were saved to measure the WSS, OSI in the anterior and posterior walls of CCA and CB, and long-axis strain (LAS) of CCA (measurement shown in Figure 1). All images were evaluated by two vascular ultrasound experts independently and blindly.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables and noncontinuous frequency variables were used for data evaluation, expressed as mean values with standard deviation (SD). Sample mean (x¯) is calculated by: x¯=(1/n)∑i=1i=nxi, where n is the number of samples. The sample SD is calculated by: s=1n−1∑i=1i=n(xi−x¯)2. The demographic data and clinical characteristics included age, BMI, WHR, smoking, hormone levels, etc. (see Table 1); the arterial wall characteristics of CCA and CB included IT, MT, OSI, etc. (see Table 2). Comparative analyses of the mean values of these parameters above between the PCOS and control group were conducted using an independent samples t-test, while a non-parametric test (Mann-Whitney U test) were employed for the non-normal distribution data. Pearson Chi-squared test was used to compare the incidence of subclinical atherosclerosis between the two groups. The Spearman correlation index was used to determine the degree of correlation between hydrodynamic characteristics and wall thickness of carotid artery. The repeated measurement results of the images by two experts were evaluated using interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to assess inter-observer reliability. P<0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. Statistical analysis was performed using the software program Matlab, SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) and Stata MP (version 15.1).

Results

Baseline

A total of 70 participants were recruited, with a median age of 30 years (mean age 29±5 vs. 30±6 years, P=0.189), including 40 PCOS patients and 30 controls. Subclinical atherosclerosis was significantly more prevalent in PCOS patients (20.0% vs. 3.3%, χ2=4.25, P=0.039) compared to the control group, with a higher mean IMT in the CB (0.767±0.281 vs. 0.681±0.086 mm, P=0.002); meanwhile, the IM of CCA was also higher in the PCOS group compared to the control group (0.165±0.055 vs. 0.126±0.022 mm, P=0.001). In the PCOS group, carotid plaque was observed in 1 case (2.5% vs. 0%, P=0.386), and small calcium of carotid wall was observed in 3 cases (7.5 % vs. 0%, P=0.128), which were all negative in the control group identified during ultrasound examinations, whereas they did not reach statistical significance. The clinical characteristics and cardiovascular risk factors of the participants are presented in Table 1. In the PCOS group, the mean values of BMI and WHR were higher than the control group. A higher proportion of patients in the PCOS group showed central obesity and insulin resistance (IR). The average lipid profile of PCOS was normal, but greater inter-individual variability in lipoprotein cholesterol were observed. Compared to the control group, the levels of TG (range, mean: 0.55–5.11, 4.56 vs. 0.69–1.79, 1.10), CHO (range, mean: 1.37–8.72, 7.35 vs. 1.78–6.22, 4.44) and LDL (range, mean: 1.67–4.58, 2.91 vs. 2.03–3.18, 1.15) in PCOS patients exhibited a wider range of distribution (maximum-minimum, range). Seven and two participants in the PCOS group and control group had dyslipidemia respectively. The mean FBG values of two groups were in the clinical ‘normal range’ (3.9–6.1 mmol/L), but the difference between the two groups was statistically significant (P=0.01). This was due to two individuals in the PCOS group meeting the diagnostic criteria for diabetes (FBG ≥7.0 mmol/L), compared to none in the control group. Additionally, the PCOS group showed a significantly higher prevalence of impaired fasting glucose (6.1–7.0 mmol/L) compared to controls [7/40 (17.5%) vs. 2/30 (6.7%)]. No difference was found in other clinical features. The inter-observer reliability was excellent. The ICC of IT measurement was 0.934; the ICC of MT measurement was 0.892 and that of IMT measurement was 0.964 (P<0.001).

Table 2 showed comparison of the arterial wall characteristics of CCA and CB between the PCOS and control group. In CCA, IT and IMTR in the PCOS group were significantly increased compared with the control group (P=0.002 and P=0.011). The mean WSS of anterior and posterior walls was significantly lower in the PCOS group (P=0.027, P=0.004). There was no significant difference in MT and LAS between the two groups. In CB, there were significant differences in IMT and WSS between the two groups (P<0.05), but there was no significant difference in OSI. Note that, 6 samples in the PCOS group were measured with an old version of V Flow, that could not provide measurements of OSI and LAS. Therefore, 34 results of OSI and LAS in the PCOS group were used in the statistical analysis.

A negative correlation was observed between WSS and OSI of CB in the PCOS group, with a correlation coefficient of ρ=−0.504. In the PCOS group, a weak-to-moderate positive correlation was found between LAS and OSI of the posterior wall in CB, while a negative correlation was observed between IT and WSS in CCA. In control group, there was no significant correlation between LAS and OSI/WSS. For LAS and IMT of CB, no significant correlation was found in the two groups. Discussion

Our results showed that PCOS women had a higher incidence of subclinical atherosclerosis with increased IT in CCA and IMT in CB compared to age-matched controls; meanwhile, WSS of the posterior wall of CCA and the anterior and posterior wall of CB decreased (P<0.05); LAS and OSI were similar in the two groups. When WSS was less than 0.985 Pa, IT was significantly thickened, especially for PCOS, and even if WSS ≥0.985 Pa, IT of PCOS patients was higher than that of normal people. These findings indicated that local hemodynamic factor of PCOS were more likely to lead to development of carotid atherosclerosis in the patients.

In this study, significant differences in androgen levels and IR were observed between PCOS patients and controls. Previous studies have demonstrated that exposure to androgen or insulin plays a crucial role in the early stages of atherosclerosis development (39,40). This study showed that women of childbearing age with PCOS had a higher proportion of subclinical atherosclerosis than the control group. This study provided further evidence to support the aforementioned research findings. Furthermore, despite the normal average lipid profiles observed in both groups, clinical levels of cholesterol or apolipoprotein were abnormal in seven individuals in the PCOS group and two individuals in the control group, respectively. Notably, the distribution range of CHO and LDL was broader in the PCOS patients. These changes in lipid metabolism might be related to the greater CCA IMT in PCOS. In addition, BMI, waist circumference and WHR of PCOS patients were significantly different from those of the control group, reflecting the potential role of obesity as a risk factor in the early arterial damage in PCOS (41,42).

We found that the increase of IT and IMTR associated with early vascular injury in PCOS women was higher than that in controls. Previous studies have found that IT increases with the progression of atherosclerosis, and our findings are in agreement with those observed using histomorphometry and intravascular high-frequency ultrasound (43-45). Previous studies using conventional ultrasound data showed that changes in IMT of CCA in PCOS women showed a certain degree of heterogeneity (10,11). The potential explanation for the discrepant results is that early pathophysiological changes in atherosclerosis primarily affect the intima layer of the aorta and its branches. The utilization of UHFUS for measuring arterial IT may offer greater sensitivity in detecting this pathological alteration (16). In addition, with the gradual progress of atherosclerosis, increase of IT can be accompanied by the decrease of MT, and the deviation of intima and media may offset the assessment intensity of IMT (43). The absence of a significant difference in MT between the two groups may be attributed to the inclusion of young female participants without pre-existing CVD. This finding was consistent with prior observations of subclinical atherosclerosis in adolescent patients with mental illness using UHFUS for the carotid artery (46).

The local hemodynamic factor WSS is involved in the physiology of the pathological atherosclerosis process. Low levels of WSS have been shown to be associated with increased arterial IMT and plaque formation (47,48). There is no data reported on WSS in PCOS patients to the present. This study revealed that when the threshold of WSS was set at 0.985 Pa, the statistical difference in IT was maximal, and the IT of PCOS patients was significantly elevated compared to that of healthy individuals. Studies had also observed differences in WSS of CCA between diabetic subjects and control groups (49,50). Although WSS =0.985 Pa is not a threshold of subclinical atherosclerosis that clearly identifies PCOS patients and controls. However, when below this threshold, even in the absence of other risk factors for SCVD, such as hypertension and smoking, alterations in the hemodynamics of the CCA may contribute to the development of atherosclerosis. With the decrease of WSS, the arterial wall is more vulnerable to damage.

This study showed that there was no difference in LAS of CCA between PCOS and control group. Utilizing the same strain measurement methodology employed in this study, a reduction in both transverse radial strain and transverse circumferential strain of the carotid artery was observed in patients with end-stage renal disease. Similar to our observation, there was no significant difference between LAS groups (30). First, the lower strain indicates that the artery is harder, because the deformation caused by pulse pressure is smaller. However, the strain in the long axis direction may be more susceptible to external factors such as swallowing, respiratory interference and longitudinal stretching of the carotid artery wall caused by cardiac pulsation, giving rise to bias in the results. Nevertheless, in terms of local hemodynamics within the group, WSS and OSI were negatively correlated, as in previous studies (51). Since the existence of high and low WSS is usually related to unstable flow conditions (33), where low WSS can be found along with a low momentum of fluid, such as vortex accompanied by frequently changes of flow direction during one cardiac cycle resulting in a high OSI (52,53). On the contrary, the laminar flow could have high velocity without direction change, exhibiting relatively high WSS and almost zero OSI. For LAS and OSI, PCOS group showed a weak positive correlation, and the control group showed no significant correlation, both of which are unfavorable factors of vascular function, and the hemodynamic state of PCOS patients was more complex, which might lead to the change of this correlation. There was a weak positive correlation between IT and WSS in PCOS, while LAS and OSI had a tendency to increase or decrease at the same time, reflecting the interaction between the physical rigidity of the wall and fluid mechanics to some extent. Due to the limitations of the machine version, axial and circumferential strain data are not available, and we will consider further evaluation in future research.

Limitations

There are several limitations in this study. First of all, the sample size is relatively small, which hinders the analysis of the difference of subclinical atherosclerosis in different phenotypes of PCOS. Secondly, because it is a cross-sectional study, the observed causal relationship is limited. These limitations require a large sample follow-up study in the future.

Conclusions

The incidence of early artery injury in PCOS patients is higher. Compared with the healthy people, in addition to the effects of systemic metabolic factors such as androgen levels and IR, local carotid hemodynamic characteristics are more likely to induce the development of plaques, which could be another factor leading to the premature development of atherosclerosis. The evaluation of the carotid artery using high-resolution ultrasound and V Flow imaging techniques can offer supplementary valuable insights into the progression of early atherosclerosis.
