NMN/NAD+/SIRT1 axis activates NAMPT to ameliorate H2O2-triggered the impairment of progesterone synthesis in ovarian granulosa cells
Authors: Yunduan Wang, Qiao Li, Yueyue Li, Zifeng Ma, Hongmei Xu, Kechao Chen, Jian Li, Daoliang Lan, Wei Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oxidative stress is a major driver of granulosa cell (GC) dysfunction and disrupted steroidogenesis. However, the mechanisms underlying oxidative stress–induced progesterone synthesis impairment and potential intervention strategies remain inadequately defined. In this study, RNA-seq re-analysis revealed that hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) markedly altered gene expression in mouse granulosa cells (mGCs), particularly affecting pathways related to progesterone biosynthesis. In vitro experiments confirmed that H₂O₂ suppresses progesterone production via downregulation of Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1). Conversely, β-nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) supplementation increased intracellular NAD⁺ levels, and upregulated SIRT1 and Nicotinamide phosphoribosyl transferase (NAMPT) expression. This regulation enhanced cell proliferation, stimulated the expression of progesterone biosynthetic enzymes, and promoted NAMPT transcriptional activity. Notably, the beneficial effects of NMN were diminished upon SIRT1 inhibition. Similarly, inhibition of NAMPT attenuated the positive regulatory effect of NMN, leading to a marked reduction in SIRT1 levels and a consequent suppression of progesterone secretion. Thus, SIRT1 activation induces NAMPT expression, which in turn feeds back to enhance SIRT1 activity and support progesterone biosynthesis. Together, these findings suggest that NMN alleviates H₂O₂-induced progesterone synthesis disruption in mGCs through activation of the NAD⁺/SIRT1/NAMPT axis, providing a potential therapeutic strategy for oxidative stress–related reproductive dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02025-w.

Introduction

The health and functionality of the female reproductive system are influenced by a range of factors, with hormonal regulation being the most critical and direct determinant of female reproductive capacity [1]. Granulosa cells (GCs) are essential for ovarian function, supplying vital nutrients and regulatory signals to developing oocytes. GCs are pivotal in follicular development, the maturation of oocytes, post-ovulatory corpus luteum formation, and the synthesis and secretion of steroid hormones [2]. Specifically, GCs synthesize two primary classes of steroid hormones: estrogens (e.g., E2) and progestogens (e.g., P4) [3]. During folliculogenesis, the primary function of GCs is to convert the androgens produced by theca cells into estrogens via the enzyme aromatase (e.g., CYP19A1) [4]. Following ovulation, the remaining parietal GCs and other cells, including basement membrane cells and internal and external theca cells, differentiate into the corpus luteum (CL) under the stimulation of luteinizing hormone (LH) [5].

Progesterone is a crucial hormone essential for the maintenance of pregnancy. It prepares the endometrium for embryo implantation by modulating the uterine environment. Furthermore, progesterone prevents early miscarriage by suppressing contractions of the uterine smooth muscle [6, 7]. Signaling pathways, such as LH and cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) [8], facilitate the conversion of cholesterol into progesterone via steroidogenesis in GCs. Progesterone synthesis begins with cholesterol as its precursor. Firstly, upon stimulation by LH, cholesterol is transported to the inner mitochondrial membrane by steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR) [9]. Within the mitochondria, cholesterol is converted into pregnenolone by the cholesterol side-chain cleavage enzyme (CYP11A1) [10]. Subsequently, pregnenolone is transported to the endoplasmic reticulum, where it is converted into progesterone by 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3β-HSD) [11].

GCs are essential throughout the entire follicular lifecycle, encompassing folliculogenesis, oocyte maturation, and follicular atresia. Specifically, abnormal apoptosis of GCs leads to reduced levels of critical hormones such as estrogen and progesterone [12], resulting in follicular atresia and impaired capacity to maintain pregnancy [13]. Extensive studies now indicate that oxidative stress inhibits the proliferation and differentiation of GCs [14], while excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) impair its functions [15].

Moderate ROS levels play a regulatory role in steroidogenesis [15], whereas excessive ROS cause oxidative damage to steroidogenic enzymes, including 3β-HSD and CYP11A1, leading to reduced progesterone production [16, 17]. Nonetheless, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is a potent oxidizing agent commonly used to induce oxidative stress in experimental models. Exposure to H₂O₂ induces apoptosis [18] and autophagy [19], thereby contributing to the inhibition of steroidogenesis in GCs [20]. Research has also demonstrated that exposure to H₂O₂ disrupts mitochondrial function, reduces oocyte quality, impairs steroidogenic enzyme activity, and weakens luteal function [21]. These changes result in reproductive system dysfunction, leading to disorders such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and premature ovarian failure (POF) [22]. Luteal cells are the primary source of progesterone secretion. Exposure to H₂O₂ reduces progesterone production, impairs luteal function, and accelerates premature luteal regression [23].

β-Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) is a key precursor of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺). NAD⁺ is a fundamental coenzyme involved in various metabolic pathways, including redox reactions, energy metabolism, and cellular repair [24, 25]. Recent research has highlighted the significant potential of NMN in disease prevention and even in reversing certain aspects of aging [26]. Mechanistically, NMN serves as an efficient substrate for NAD⁺ replenishment, participating in the full metabolic pathway and enhancing NAD⁺ production via multiple mechanisms [27]. Furthermore, NAD⁺ functions as a coenzyme for multiple antioxidant enzymes, including catalase and superoxide dismutase, and plays a crucial role in cellular protection against oxidative damage [28]. By elevating NAD⁺ levels, NMN strengthens cellular antioxidant defenses and contribute to lifespan extension. Additionally, NAD⁺ is a vital cofactor in redox reactions [29], and is involved in activating sirtuins, especially Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), which plays a pivotal role in regulating cellular metabolism and responses to oxidative stress [30]. SIRT1 alleviates oxidative stress by deacetylating and activating transcription factors such as FOXO1, which are central regulators of cellular stress responses [31]. The activation of the NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis has also been shown to improve cellular resistance to oxidative damage and enhance metabolic function [32]. In addition to oxidative stress, the NMN/NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis also plays a critical role in regulating steroidogenesis. SIRT1 regulates the expression of genes involved in steroid hormone biosynthesis, such as Cyp11a1, Star, and Hsd3b [33]. A key downstream target of SIRT1 is Nicotinamide phosphoribosyl transferase (NAMPT), which catalyzes the conversion of NMN to NAD⁺ and thereby promotes NAD⁺ biosynthesis [34]. Research has further shown that NAMPT stimulates progesterone synthesis in bovine GCs [35]. Therefore, we hypothesized that NMN may activate NAMPT via the NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis to mitigate oxidative stress in GCs.

Our earlier research demonstrated that NMN could mitigate H₂O₂-induced apoptosis in ovarian GCs and partially restore estradiol secretion [36]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether NMN can alleviate the inhibitory effects of H₂O₂ on progesterone synthesis and secretion in GCs. Herein, we reanalyzed RNA-seq data from mouse granulosa cells (mGCs) to investigate the impact of oxidative stress on steroid hormone synthesis. Subsequently, using an in vitro cell model, we investigated the mechanism by which H₂O₂ disrupts progesterone synthesis and evaluated the capacity of NMN to alleviate oxidative stress and restore progesterone production in mGCs. This study deepens our understanding of how oxidative stress impairs steroid hormone biosynthesis and supports the potential application of NMN as a therapeutic candidate for oxidative stress–related reproductive disorders.

Materials and methods

Mouse granulosa cell collection

Three-week-old ICR mice (Dashuo Laboratory Animal, Chengdu, China) were intraperitoneally injected 5 IU PMSG (Ningbo Second Hormone Factory, Ningbo, China). After 48 h, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The mice were then dissected, and their ovaries were collected in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Hyclone, Utah, USA). Under a stereoscopic microscope, the bursa and surrounding adipose tissues were carefully removed. To obtain mouse granulosa cells (mGCs), 1 mL syringes equipped with 27-gauge (27G, 0.4 mm) needles were used to puncture the follicles, fully releasing the cells [37]. The suspension was transferred to a 1.5 mL tube, centrifuged at 200 × g for 3 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were resuspended in PBS and centrifuged again to remove the supernatant. A 0.25% trypsin solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was then added to digest the cells for 4 min. The digestion was stopped by adding cell culture medium. After centrifugation and removal of the supernatant, the cells were resuspended in the cell culture medium, placed in 6 cm petri dishes, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The cell culture medium consisted of DMEM-F12 (Gibco, New york, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Newzerum, Christchurch, New Zealand) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Hyclone). All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the Regulations for the Administration of Experimental Animals, promulgated by the Ministry of Science and Technology of China (Beijing) in 1988 and last revised in 2017. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Southwest Minzu University (Approval Code: SMU-202309001). Clinical trial number: not applicable.

mGCs culture

mGCs were incubated in a CO₂ incubator for 6–12 h. Then, the culture medium was discarded. The cells were rinsed with PBS containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin and then removed. Next, 1 mL of 0.25% trypsin (Biosharp) was added and incubated for 3 min to digest the cells. Cell morphology was observed under a stereomicroscope to monitor the digestion. To stop the reaction, 0.5 mL of cell culture medium was first added, followed by an additional 0.5 mL, ensuring a 1:1 ratio between trypsin and neutralizing medium. The cells were transferred to a 1.5 mL tube, centrifuged at 200 × g for 3 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were resuspended in 1 mL of fresh culture medium and transferred to new petri dishes or wells.

The culture medium was replaced every 24 h. Once the cells adhered and reached approximately 70% confluence, the medium was replaced with treatment-specific basal medium to initiate experimental interventions. Cells were randomly allocated to different wells in the culture plate for treatment assignment to minimize potential spatial bias. The treatment groups were defined as follows: Group C (control): standard culture medium only; Group H: 150 µM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, Beyotime); Group N: 500 µM β-nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) ; Group HN: co-treatment with 150 µM H₂O₂ and 500 µM NMN. Following the 24-hour treatment, cells were harvested for subsequent analyses according to experimental requirements.

High-throughput RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

The RNA-seq data used in this study were reanalyzed from our previous work [36]. Four groups were established: group C (control), group H (150 µM H₂O₂), group N (500 µM NMN), and group HN (150 µM H₂O₂ + 500 µM NMN). Total RNA was extracted from mGCs using the TRIzol method, and 1 µg of RNA per sample was used for library preparation. After poly(A)⁺ mRNA enrichment with Oligo(dT) magnetic beads, strand-specific libraries were constructed using the Hieff NGS® Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit (YEASEN, USA). Library quality was assessed via Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp [38], aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 [39], and assembled with StringTie [40]. Gene expression was quantified using RSEM [41] and expressed as raw counts and FPKM. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2 using FC ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67 and P < 0.05 [42]. Functional annotation was performed using DAVID v6.7 for GO terms, and pathway enrichment was conducted using KEGG and Reactome databases. The RNA-seq dataset is publicly available in the NCBI BioProject database (PRJNA930590).

Real-Time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was performed as described in our previous study [43]. Firstly, Total RNA was extracted from mGCs using the TRIzol method. For each sample, 1 µg of total RNA from was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) according to the instructions of the reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The reaction mixture totaled 10 µL, comprising 5 µL of SYBR Prime Ex Taq TM II (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), 1 µL of cDNA template, 0.5 µL of each 10 µM forward and reverse primers, and 3 µL of RNase-free ddH₂O. The thermal cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s; followed by 39 cycles of 94 °C for 10 s (denaturation) and 60 °C for 15 s (annealing); with a final step at 65 °C for 5 s, and a melting curve analysis from 65 °C to 95 °C, increasing by 0.5 °C every 5 s. Each gene was tested in triplicate, with β-action as the internal reference gene. All primers used in this study are listed in Table 1.

BrdU cell proliferation determination

mGCs were seeded at 5 × 10⁵ cells/well in 12-well plates. After 24 h of treatment, 40 µM BrdU (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added and incubated in the dark for 40 min. Cells were washed three times with PBS (5 min each), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and washed again. DNA was denatured with permeabilization solution for 10 min, followed by PBS washes and DAPI staining for 10 min. Cells were re-fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed, treated with 1 M HCl at 37 °C for 10 min, and fixed again. After washing, cells were blocked at room temperature for 30 min, incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-BrdU primary antibody, then with secondary antibody for 1 h. Following final PBS washes, images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Detection of the progesterone level

The analytical sensitivity of the progesterone ELISA kit (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd.) was 0.1 nmol/L, with a detection range of 0.1–16 nmol/L. Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were both < 15%, ensuring reliable precision and reproducibility. mGCs were seeded at 5 × 10⁵ cells/well in 12-well plates. After 24 h of treatment, the culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 900 × g for 10 min. The kit was equilibrated to room temperature for 20 min before use. Blank, standard, and sample wells were prepared. Except for the blank, 100 µL of HRP-labeled detection antibody was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 60 min. Wells were washed five times using wash buffer (1 min each), with drying between steps. Subsequently, 50 µL each of substrate solutions A and B were added and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 15 min. The reaction was terminated with 50 µL stop solution, and optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm within 15 min.

Total NAD determination

Total NAD levels were measured using a NAD⁺/NADH Detection Kit (Beyotime) with a seeding density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well. After removing the culture medium, 200 µL of extraction buffer was added, and cells were gently pipetted to facilitate lysis. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. NADH standard was prepared by dissolving 5 mg NADH in 655 µL of preparation solution, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C. Working concentrations were obtained by diluting the stock in extraction buffer. Ethanol dehydrogenase was diluted 1:45 in reaction buffer. Then, 20 µL of sample was added to each well of a 96-well plate (in duplicate), incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 10 min, followed by addition of 10 µL developer solution. After mixing, the plate was further incubated for 30 min to generate orange formazan. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm. A standard curve was plotted using NADH concentration (x-axis) versus absorbance (y-axis).

Western blots

In our experiment, mGCs were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in a 6-well plate. Following a 24-h treatment period, mGCs were harvested for total protein extraction. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Each sample, containing 15 µg of protein, was subjected to electrophoresis at a constant voltage of 120 V for 1 h. The proteins were then transferred from a 12% SDS-PAGE gel to a PVDF membrane and blocked with 1% BSA solution for 1 h. The membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies [HSD3B (1:1000; 15516-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China); STAR (1:1000; 12225-1-AP, Proteintech); SIRT1 (1:1000; DF6033, Affinity, Nanjing, China); NAMPT (1:1000; 11776-1-AP, Proteintech), CYP11A1 (1:1000; 13363-1-AP, Proteintech)] and β-ACTIN (1:2000; # 4970, Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, USA) as a loading control. After three washes with TBST, the membrane was immersed in a dilution of the secondary antibody IgG(H + L) (1:4000; A0208, Beyotime) at room temperature for 1 h. Following three washes with TBST (Biosharp, Hefei, China), enhanced chemiluminescence (Beyotime) was applied, and images were captured using an imaging system (iBrightCL1000, Thermo). Data were subsequently analyzed using ImageJ (NIH).

Cell viability determination

The CCK-8 Cell Proliferation and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Biosharp) was employed to measure cell viability. The cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. After 24 h, cells were treated according to the experimental groups. Subsequently, CCK-8 solution was added to each well. After incubating for 1 h, the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured. Cell viability was calculated as follows: (A experimental group − A blank well) / (A normal control group − A blank well) × 100%.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate. The results were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism software 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Transcriptomic reanalysis reveals that H2O2 disrupts the expression of genes related to progesterone synthesis in mGCs

To further explore the effects of oxidative stress on progesterone synthesis and secretion in GCs, we reanalyzed our previously published RNA-seq data [36]. The threshold for identifying new differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was set at Fold Change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67 with P < 0.05. Initially, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) based on gene expression levels. The analysis revealed that the first principal component (PC1) accounted for 78.2% of the total variance, while the second principal component (PC2) explained 15.6%. Furthermore, group H (150 µM H₂O₂) and group C (control) showed clear separation in the PCA plot, indicating significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, correlation analysis revealed strong within-group consistency, with all correlation coefficients exceeding 0.9 (Fig. 1B). Analysis of genes with FPKM > 1 revealed that 11,140 genes were shared between the two groups, with 209 genes specifically expressed in group C and 183 in group H (Fig. 1C). Differential expression analysis showed that H₂O₂ exposure significantly altered the gene expression profile in mGCs, identifying 1,288 DEGs, including 568 upregulated and 720 downregulated genes in the group H compared to the group C (Fig. 1D and Table S1). We further conducted Gene Ontology (GO) classification and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses on these DEGs. GO analysis indicated that the enriched DEGs were categorized into biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC) domains (Fig. 1E and Table S2). Analysis of the top twenty GO-enriched items showed that these items were primarily associated with metabolic processes and biological regulation, including cell cycle progression and DNA replication (Fig. 1F).

Further analysis of the KEGG pathways revealed that the DEGs between the groups C and H were predominantly enriched in lipid metabolism and signal transduction pathways (Figure S1A and Table S3). Notably, steroid hormone biosynthesis relies on lipid metabolism, particularly cholesterol metabolism. Cholesterol serves as the primary precursor for steroid hormone biosynthesis. Consequently, any signaling pathway that modulates lipid metabolism may indirectly influence steroid hormone production. Similarly, steroid hormone biosynthesis is regulated by several critical signaling pathways, including the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways. Reactome pathway analysis revealed that the most remarkably enriched pathways were associated with mitotic progression and cell cycle regulation, including spindle checkpoint signaling, chromatid cohesion resolution, and kinetochore–microtubule attachment (Figure S1B and Table S4). These results suggest that H₂O₂ induces mitotic arrest in granulosa cells, potentially impairing their steroidogenic capacity. KEGG enrichment analysis further highlighted the involvement of the cell cycle pathway (Fig. 2A). Moreover, pathways such as progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation, oocyte meiosis and ovarian steroidogenesis were found to be closely associated with steroid hormone function and oocyte maturation (Fig. 2A). In the progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation pathway, only Cped4 and Pde3b were significantly upregulated in the group H compared to group C (Fig. 2B and Table S5). In both the progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation and oocyte meiosis pathways, the expression levels of Adcy1, Cdk1, Plk1, Mad2l1, Cdc25c, Ccnb2, and Ccnb1 were obviously downregulated in group H relative to group C (Fig. 2B-C and Table S6-7). In the ovarian steroidogenesis pathway, Cyp17a1 and Cyp19a1 were upregulated, while Cyp1a1, Hsd3b2, and Hsd17b7 were downregulated in group H compared to group C (Fig. 2D). Using the STRING protein interaction database, we analyzed protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks associated with the DEGs in these three pathways, and the top 30 interaction pairs were selected for further investigation. Cdk1, Plk1, Cdc20, Hsd3b2 and Cyp19a1 were identified as hub nodes within the PPI network (Fig. 2E-G). Cdk1 and Plk1 were identified as key nodes in the PPI network of the progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation pathway (Fig. 2E), while Cdc20 and Hsd3b served as critical nodes in the PPI networks of the oocyte meiosis and ovarian steroidogenesis pathways, respectively (Fig. 2F and G). In summary, these pathways and biological processes are intricately linked to progesterone biosynthesis, reinforcing the need to explore the effects of H₂O₂ exposure on progesterone production.

H₂O₂ inhibits progesterone synthesis in mGCs by reducing NAD⁺ and SIRT1

Based on the RNA-seq results above, we first examined the effect of H₂O₂ on mGC proliferation. In our previous study, 150 µM H₂O₂ was determined to be the optimal concentration for inducing cellular senescence in mGCs [36]. A significant decrease in BrdU incorporation was observed in the group H (150 µM H₂O₂) compared to the group C (P < 0.01), indicating that H₂O₂ markedly impaired mGC proliferation (Fig. 4A-B). Next, we quantified progesterone (PROG) levels in the culture medium using ELISA, revealing that H₂O₂ markedly suppressed PROG synthesis and secretion (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). Concurrently, total NAD⁺ assays showed that H₂O₂ exposure remarkably reduced NAD⁺ levels (P < 0.01) in mGCs (Fig. 4D).

Additionally, mRNA expression of GCs marker genes (e.g., Fshr and Lhcgr) and key genes involved in steroidogenesis (e.g., Star, Hsd3b, and Cyp11a1) was remarkably decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4E and F). Notably, the mRNA expression of Nampt and Nmnpt, two pivotal genes in the NAD⁺ biosynthesis pathway, was also notably decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4G). Previous studies have identified NAMPT as a critical regulator of steroid hormone biosynthesis [35]. Moreover, we verified these findings at the protein level through Western blotting, where STAR, HSD3B, CYP19A1, and NAMPT protein levels mirrored the RT-qPCR results (Fig. 4H-J). Given that Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) is a critical upstream regulator of NAMPT, the rate-limiting enzyme in NAD⁺ biosynthesis, SIRT1 protein levels were measured following H₂O₂ treatment. A significant reduction in SIRT1 protein levels was observed in group H compared to group C (P < 0.05; Fig. 4J). Collectively, these findings extend previous observations by demonstrating that exposure to 150 µM H₂O₂ reduces NAD⁺ content and SIRT1 activity, thereby hindering progesterone biosynthesis in mGCs.

NMN enhances progesterone synthesis and secretion in mGCs

The results above suggest that H₂O₂ reduces intracellular NAD⁺ levels in mGCs, thereby impairing progesterone synthesis and secretion. To further assess this, we examined the effect of NMN supplementation, an NAD⁺ precursor, on progesterone production in mGCs. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay in mGCs treated with varying concentrations of NMN (e.g., 300 and 500 µM). The results indicated a significant, dose-dependent increase in mGC viability (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, transcriptomic reanalysis revealed that NMN addition (500 µM) had no substantial impact on the global gene expression profile of mGCs. Only 75 DEGs were identified in group N compared to group C, including 31 upregulated and 44 downregulated genes (Figures S2A-C and Table S9). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the enriched pathways included glycerophospholipid metabolism, cardiac muscle contraction, and the IL-17 signaling pathway (Figure S2D and Table S10). ELISA results further demonstrated a significant increase in progesterone levels (P < 0.01) in mGCs treated with NMN (Fig. 5B). Additionally, NAD⁺ levels in the group N were significantly higher than those in the group C (Fig. 5C).

Moreover, RT-qPCR results demonstrated a significant upregulation (P < 0.05) of GCs marker genes (e.g., Fshr and Lhcgr), as well as a marked increase (P < 0.05) in the mRNA expression of progesterone synthesis–related genes, including Star, Hsd3b, and Cyp11a1 (Figs. 5D-E). Importantly, mRNA expression levels of Nampt and Nmnpt, two key genes in the NAD⁺ biosynthesis pathway, were also markedly increased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5F). Western blot analysis further confirmed elevated protein levels of STAR, HSD3B, and CYP11A1, consistent with the RT-qPCR findings (P < 0.05) (Figs. 5G-H). Since SIRT1 is a key regulator of NAD⁺ synthesis that can upregulate NAMPT and promote NAD⁺ production, we analyzed the protein levels of SIRT1 and NAMPT in the group N, which were significantly higher than those in the group C (P < 0.05) (Figs. 5I). Overall, our results indicate that NMN notably promotes NAD⁺ biosynthesis, enhances reproductive function of mGCs, and increases progesterone production.

NMN activates the NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis to mitigate H₂O₂-induced impairment of progesterone synthesis in mGCs

The above results suggest that NMN can enhance the ability of mGCs to synthesize and secrete progesterone. It has been reported that NMN, a key intermediate in NAD⁺ biosynthesis, inhibits ROS production and counteracts the reduction in cell viability caused by NAD⁺ depletion under oxidative stress [44]. Therefore, we sought to determine whether NMN could mitigate H₂O₂-induced impairment of progesterone synthesis in mGCs and to explore the underlying mechanisms. Based on high-throughput transcriptome sequencing, we performed enrichment analysis on gene expression profiles from groups C, H and HN (150 µM H₂O₂ + 500 µM NMN) (Figure S3 and Table S11). Profiles 2 (543 genes) and 5 (1,147 genes) were of particular interest and selected for further analysis based on their representative expression trends (Figure S3). GO analysis of the top 10 enriched terms from Profile 2 and Profile 5 revealed that these were mainly associated with intracellular metabolic signaling pathways (Figure S4A-B and Table S12-13). Among the top 10 KEGG-enriched pathways, notable hormone-related pathways included GnRH secretion and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Figure S4C-D and Table S14-15).

To further assess the ability of NMN to restore progesterone synthesis in H₂O₂-treated mGCs, we co-treated mGCs with NMN and H₂O₂. Specifically, H₂O₂ exposure markedly reduced progesterone levels (P < 0.05), which were restored by 500 µM NMN supplementation (Fig. 6A). Similarly, NMN supplementation notably restored NAD⁺ levels in H₂O₂-treated mGCs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6B). RT-qPCR results showed that mRNA expression of GCs marker genes (e.g., Fshr and Lhcgr), key NAD⁺ biosynthesis genes (e.g., Nampt and Nmnpt), and progesterone biosynthesis–related genes (e.g., Star, Hsd3b, and Cyp11a1) were signally decreased in mGCs exposed to H₂O₂ (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6C-E). Notably, NMN remarkably restored the expression of these genes in H₂O₂-treated mGCs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6C-E), indicating that NMN can partially reverse H₂O₂-induced damage and disruption of progesterone biosynthesis. Furthermore, Western blot analysis validated the restorative effect of NMN on the H₂O₂-induced reduction in protein levels of STAR, HSD3B, CYP11A1, and NAMPT in mGCs (P < 0.05) (Figs. 6F-H). Notably, NMN also significantly restored SIRT1 protein levels in mGCs cultured with H₂O₂ (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6H). In conclusion, these results demonstrate that NMN can enhance NAD⁺ production and mitigate H₂O₂-induced impairment of progesterone synthesis in mGCs. NMN restores the progesterone synthesis and secretion of H₂O₂- suffered mGCs via the NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis

SIRT1 enhances progesterone production through two possible mechanisms: by reducing intracellular ROS levels via deacetylation and activation of antioxidant enzyme genes [15], and by promoting the expression of key genes involved in progesterone biosynthesis. However, SIRT1 activity is strictly dependent on intracellular NAD⁺ availability [45]. We found that H₂O₂ exposure reduced intracellular NAD⁺ levels and suppressed SIRT1 activity, whereas NMN supplementation alone effectively increased both NAD⁺ and SIRT1 levels. Thus, we hypothesized that the NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis plays a crucial role in the ability of NMN to alleviate H₂O₂-induced damage in GCs. Hence, we treated H₂O₂-exposed mGCs with SRT1720 (SRT), a specific activator of SIRT1.

Our results showed that in group H + SRT (150 µM H₂O₂ + 10 µM SRT), SIRT1 protein levels were significantly increased compared to group H (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7A and B), accompanied by a significant upregulation in NAMPT mRNA expression, a key enzyme in the NAD⁺ biosynthesis pathway (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7C). These findings indicate that SRT effectively activates SIRT1 under oxidative stress conditions. Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis showed that the mRNA expression of progesterone biosynthesis–related genes, including Star, Hsd3b, and Cyp11a1, was signally upregulated in group H + SRT compared to group H (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7D). Consistently, progesterone levels in group H + SRT were notably higher than in group H (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7E). Collectively, these results suggest that H₂O₂ disrupts progesterone synthesis by impairing the NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis, while SRT restores progesterone production by activating SIRT1, which in turn upregulates NAMPT expression and alleviates the inhibitory effects.

NMN/NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis activates NAMPT to enhance progesterone synthesis and secretion in H₂O₂-exposed mGCs

Previous studies have shown that NAMPT overexpression alone increases progesterone production, whereas NAMPT knockdown suppresses STAR protein expression and reduces HSD3B enzymatic activity [46]. These findings suggest that NAMPT functions not only as a rate-limiting enzyme in NMN biosynthesis but also as a potential regulator of steroidogenic gene expression, including genes involved in progesterone biosynthesis. To investigate whether the NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis regulates progesterone synthesis and secretion in GCs through NAMPT, we added the NAMPT-specific inhibitor STF-118804 (STF, 50 nM) to group HN. NAMPT mRNA and protein levels were assessed via RT-qPCR and Western blotting, respectively, and results showed that both NAMPT and SIRT1 expression levels were significantly lower in the HN + STF group compared to group HN (P < 0.05) (Fig. 9A-C). Furthermore, RT-qPCR and ELISA analyses revealed that the expression of progesterone synthesis-related genes (e.g., Star, Hsd3b, and Cyp11a1) and progesterone levels were markedly decreased in group HN + STF compared to the group HN (P < 0.05) (Fig. 9D-E). In summary, NMN activates NAMPT via the NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis, which subsequently promotes the expression of key steroidogenic enzymes, thereby alleviating H₂O₂-induced inhibition of progesterone synthesis in GCs and restoring progesterone levels.

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the in vitro role of NMN in counteracting H₂O₂-induced disruption of progesterone synthesis. The main findings are as follows: (1) H₂O₂ exposure significantly reduced SIRT1 expression, thereby disrupting progesterone synthesis in mGCs; (2) NMN treatment enhanced SIRT1 activity and mitigated the impairment of progesterone synthesis caused by H₂O₂; (3) Inhibition of NAMPT activity markedly diminished the protective effects of NMN; (4) NMN restored H₂O₂-induced progesterone synthesis dysfunction by upregulating NAMPT through activation of the NAD⁺/SIRT1 signaling pathway.

Following ovulation, follicular granulosa cells differentiate into luteal cells, which serve as the primary source of progesterone production [47]. Progesterone is essential for maintaining follicular fluid homeostasis, supporting oocyte metabolism, and promoting oocyte maturation and ovulation [48]. Its synthesis is regulated by key steroidogenic genes, including Star, Cyp11a1, and Hsd3b [49]. A variety of physiological (e.g., abnormal follicular development), pathological (e.g., reproductive endocrine disorders), and environmental (e.g., heavy metal or chemical exposure) factors can impair progesterone biosynthesis. Among these, oxidative stress has been recognized as a major pathological contributor [12, 50–53]. Excessive accumulation of ROS induces apoptosis in GCs, leading to impaired follicular development [54]. H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress impairs the steroidogenic capacity of GCs by reducing 3β-HSD activity and disrupting mitochondrial function, which subsequently inhibits CYP11A1 enzymatic activity [55, 56]. Transcriptomic re-analysis showed that H₂O₂ exposure markedly altered gene expression profiles in mGCs, with DEGs enriched in lipid metabolism, cell cycle regulation, and steroid biosynthesis pathways. H₂O₂ treatment led to a marked reduction in cell proliferation and viability, and downregulated gonadotropin receptor genes (e.g., Fshr and Lhcgr). This was accompanied by reduced progesterone synthesis and secretion, further supporting that oxidative stress directly impairs steroidogenic capacity. These results are consistent with previous studies conducted in humans [57] and pigs [58]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic strategies remain to be elucidated.

Although oxidative stress has been widely shown to suppress steroidogenesis, research on strategies to effectively reverse this inhibition remains limited. Through reanalysis of RNA-seq data, this study found that H₂O₂ exposure led to the upregulation of Pde3b and the downregulation of Adcy1. Both PDE3B and ADCY1 are core regulators of the cAMP signaling pathway. PDE3B hydrolyzes cAMP and acts as a negative regulator, whereas ADCY catalyzes the conversion of ATP to cAMP, thereby promoting intracellular signal transduction [59, 60]. Previous studies indicate that cAMP can elevate intracellular NAD⁺ levels, which in turn enhances the activity of the NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase SIRT1 [32]. SIRT1 plays essential roles in regulating cellular stress responses, metabolic homeostasis, and senescence [61]. According to Yang et al., SIRT1 deficiency leads to excessive accumulation of ROS [62]. Moreover, SIRT1 activation has been shown to enhance steroidogenesis and promote GCs survival under oxidative stress conditions [33, 63]. Our results further demonstrated that H₂O₂ exposure markedly suppressed progesterone synthesis, whereas treatment with a SIRT1 agonist effectively restored progesterone levels following H₂O₂ exposure. These findings suggest that enhancing SIRT1 activity or expression may represent a promising strategy to counteract oxidative stress–induced impairment of progesterone synthesis.

Shin-ichiro et al. reported that NMN enhances energy metabolism by promoting NAD⁺ synthesis and modulating sirtuin activity [32]. Although NMN is widely recognized for its anti-aging properties, its role in oxidative stress resistance has received increasing attention. Accumulating evidence indicates that NAD⁺ functions as both a central metabolic coenzyme and a key antioxidant, facilitating the generation of NADPH, a critical cofactor for glutathione metabolism [64]. Several studies have demonstrated that NMN enhances cellular resistance to oxidative stress by promoting NAD⁺ biosynthesis [65]. In our previous study, we confirmed that NMN alleviates H₂O₂-induced cell cycle arrest and disruption of estradiol (E₂) biosynthesis [36]. However, the molecular mechanisms by which NMN modulates steroidogenesis under oxidative stress remain unclear. In this study, we found that NMN supplementation alone significantly increased progesterone synthesis and secretion. Further analysis revealed that NMN treatment under H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress restored steroidogenic function in GCs. This effect was dependent on intracellular NAD⁺ elevation and SIRT1 activation. Inhibition of SIRT1 abolished or markedly attenuated the protective effects of NMN. Collectively, these findings indicate that NMN reverses oxidative stress–induced impairment of progesterone biosynthesis via activation of the NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis, providing a potential strategy for restoring steroidogenic function.

NAMPT is the rate-limiting enzyme in the NAD⁺ biosynthetic pathway. It catalyzes the conversion of nicotinamide to NMN, which is subsequently converted into NAD⁺ [34]. In this study, SIRT1 expression levels were significantly reduced when NAMPT activity was inhibited in H₂O₂-exposed mGCs supplemented with NMN. These findings suggest that NAMPT acts as a critical downstream effector and regulate SIRT1 expression through a feedback mechanism. Recent studies have also demonstrated that NAMPT plays a regulatory role in reproduction. Diot et al. reported that NAMPT modulates steroidogenesis in chicken granulosa cells [66].

Equally important, NAMPT also functions extracellularly as an adipokine (visfatin) secreted by adipose tissue and other cells. Visfatin was originally identified in fat but is highly expressed in ovarian compartments, having been detected in granulosa and theca cells, the corpus luteum, and even oocytes [35, 67]. Indeed, visfatin has been shown to enhance granulosa cell steroidogenesis in vitro: treatment of cultured granulosa cells with recombinant visfatin increases progesterone (and estradiol) secretion and upregulates steroidogenic factors, effects which are abrogated by NAMPT knockdown or SIRT1 inhibitio [35]. Moreover, NAMPT/visfatin plays a role in oocyte maturation and quality. In vivo, administration of visfatin during superovulation significantly improved oocyte developmental competence and yielded more offspring in aged female, highlighting a positive influence of NAMPT on oocyte quality and overall fertility [68]. Consistent with the above findings, inhibition of NAMPT activity in H₂O₂-exposed mGCs supplemented with NMN significantly suppressed progesterone synthesis and secretion. These results indicate that the absence of NAMPT diminishes the protective effects of NMN on progesterone biosynthesis, even in the presence of NMN. Our findings further support the role of NAMPT in oxidative stress regulation and demonstrate that NAMPT activation via the NAD⁺/SIRT1 signaling axis is a key mechanism by which NMN alleviates H₂O₂-induced impairment of progesterone biosynthesis in mGCs. Additionally, NAMPT may act as a molecular bridge linking metabolic signaling to steroid hormone biosynthesis, and its roles in transcriptional and post-translational regulation warrants further investigation.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were conducted using primary mGCs in vitro. While this model provides valuable insights into cellular mechanisms, it may not fully capture the complexity of ovarian physiology in vivo. Second, although we identified the involvement of the NAD⁺/SIRT1/NAMPT pathway in modulating progesterone synthesis under oxidative stress, the downstream transcriptional regulatory networks and precise mechanisms remain to be further investigated. Future studies involving in vivo models and extended treatment durations are needed to validate and expand upon these observations.

Conclusions

The main findings of this study demonstrate that NMN alleviates H₂O₂-induced disruption of progesterone biosynthesis in ovarian GCs through the NAD⁺/SIRT1/NAMPT signaling axis. Mechanistically, NMN supplementation elevates intracellular NAD⁺ levels, thereby enhancing SIRT1 activity, which subsequently activates NAMPT. NAMPT activation not only accelerates NAD⁺ biosynthesis but also upregulates the expression of key progesterone biosynthesis–related genes, thereby effectively counteracting H₂O₂-induced suppression of progesterone production in mGCs.
