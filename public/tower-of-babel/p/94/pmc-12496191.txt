Spatiotemporal planning of electric vehicle charging infrastructure: Demand estimation and grid-aware optimization under uncertainty
Authors: Jingbo Wang, Harshal D Kaushik, Roshni Anna Jacob, Jie Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryDeploying strategically planned charging infrastructure is essential to support the rapid electrification of transportation. This study presents a three-phase framework for planning and deploying electric vehicle (EV) chargers. First, charging demand is forecasted using real-time spatial and temporal data, including traffic and point-of-interest activity. Second, a two-stage stochastic maximum coverage model identifies optimal charging locations and capacities while accounting for demand uncertainty and installation costs. Third, power flow simulations assess the impact of added chargers on grid performance to ensure safe integration within hosting capacity limits. The framework is demonstrated using data from Austin, Texas, by integrating regional transportation activity with a real power grid model. Simulation results compare deployment strategies and assess trade-offs in grid load and service coverage. This integrated approach supports infrastructure planning that balances cost, accessibility, and grid reliability in growing urban EV networks.Subject areas: engineering, energy management, energy modeling

Introduction

Electrification in transportation is becoming increasingly prevalent due to advancements in battery technology that offers a longer lifetime, lower costs, and higher energy density.1,2,3 Another significant factor contributing to this expansion is government subsidies and supporting policies. With the rising EV adoption, the simultaneous expansion of infrastructure at home and in public spaces is crucial. While home charging satisfies most drivers’ needs, public infrastructure enhances the appeal of EV for potential buyers. It plays a crucial role in reducing range anxiety and mitigate grid overloading, both of which are critical for developing a scalable and sustainable transportation network. To accommodate projected EV market growth, the announced deployments of public and workplace level 2 chargers cover 62%–84% of the anticipated requirements for 2030 in the United States. By 2032, the number of EVs on the road could reach 55 million, requiring approximately 3.7 million level 2 chargers at workplace and public locations, 136,000 public DC fast chargers, and 40.1 million chargers at single-family and multifamily homes.4 This estimate indicates that around 3.7 million publicly accessible non-home chargers will be needed by 2032, which would require a 27% annual increase in the deployment of public and workplace chargers from 2024.

Considering the potential impact of a significant number of EVs connected to the grid, it is crucial to manage the power of charging facilities. Thus, a key question is how to optimize the use of existing nationwide infrastructure while accommodating more EVs within the existing power grid limitations. From both the charging facilities’ and EVs’ perspectives, the solution lies in the distributed planning of chargers at the workplace and public locations. Building a charging station infrastructure involves three key phases: (1) predicting the future EV charging demands, (2) identifying optimal locations and determining appropriate capacities for new chargers while accounting for demand uncertainty, and (3) ensuring that the charger capacities do not violate the operating limits of the power grid.

Related work

Accurate forecasting of the EV charging demand is essential for effective charging infrastructure planning. A common approach in the literature for estimating the EV charging demand involves modeling the EV battery usage and simulating the energy consumption under various traffic scenarios.5 Studies in the literature have integrated vehicle ownership, travel behavior patterns, household travel surveys into transportation, and simulation frameworks to estimate regional charging demand.6,7 For instance, Kontou et al.8 explored the relationship between daily activities and charging demands for private vehicles, emphasizing the role of behavioral factors in demand prediction. Similarly, Vazifeh et al.9 employed cellular data to map trajectories in Boston for spatial-temporal demand forecasting. Building on these approaches, more advanced frameworks have been developed to address the complexities of EV demand prediction. Jiang et al.10 leveraged activity-based modeling and dynamic simulations to forecast spatial-temporal charging needs across large transportation networks, integrating variations in travel demand and network conditions. Despite these advancements in the simulation-based approaches, significant challenges persist in acquiring realistic, high-resolution data on EV charging demands for specific regions. Additionally, computational requirements continue to increase as more areas are taken into consideration. Moreover, regional differences in EV adoption rates and socio-economic factors greatly affect the accuracy and scalability of predictive models.11

Given the limitations of simulation-based approaches, data-driven methods leveraging urban mobility data and point-of-interest (POI) features have recently emerged as a popular alternative for EV demand prediction. Dong et al.12 proposed a two-step approach to optimize EV charging point placement using spatial statistics and a maximal coverage location model. Yi et al.13 employed a modified geographical page-rank model to integrate the trip origin-destination and social dimension features. Koohfar et al.14 proposed a transformer-based approach to address long-term EV demand prediction by capturing complex temporal patterns in charging behavior. A recent data-driven approach15 employs physics-informed networks and temporal pattern attention in EV demand prediction using large-scale real-world datasets. These methods offer promising approaches for demand prediction by effectively identifying relationships between charging behavior and spatial characteristics across larger areas, such as a city or state, in order to make a significant impact.

While these methods offer large-scale applicability, maintaining accuracy and scalability present a major challenge, demanding careful consideration of spatial and temporal factors in data-driven approaches.16,17,18 Moreover, transportation network models often neglect the interaction between EV charging demand and grid capacity, whereas power grid-based approaches improve resilience but often overlook practical EV demand prediction data.19,20 These limitations highlight the need for data-driven solutions that integrate spatiotemporal demand, user behavior, and grid constraints to enable scalable and reliable EV infrastructure planning on a large scale. A major limitation in current EV planning research is that interdisciplinary studies combining transportation and grid perspectives are still in early stages. Many approaches focus solely on spatial or demand-side factors, often assuming that grid upgrades are easily achievable.21 However, the placement of EV charging stations places substantial power demands on the grid, which, if not properly managed, can lead to voltage instability and network overloads. While emerging studies are beginning to address these challenges by incorporating hosting capacity analysis and voltage regulation constraints,22,23 there is still a need for greater focus on incorporating temporal demand variability.

We next provide a review of existing literature for optimization-based techniques on EV infrastructure planning. Optimization based studies focus on various objectives for infrastructure planning: minimizing installation and life cycle costs,24 reducing the waiting times,25,26 maximizing charger utilization,25,27 and achieving optimal geographical coverage to meet demand.28,29,30,31 Additionally, some studies consider complexities such as land acquisition, maintenance, and network reinforcement costs.32,33,34 Apart from location, charger sizing is also important, requiring enough charging ports to meet demand at appropriate speeds without exceeding grid capacity. There are studies that use queuing theory to account for demand variability35 or capacity limits.36,37 Further, sizing also includes the charger power levels, emphasizing the need to assess grid capacity, as high charger density in weak-grid areas can lead to instability.35,38 With increasing the size of the area under consideration, computational complexity increases.39,40,41,42 In this study, while deciding the location and capacity, we consider fixed establishment costs and variable costs based on the number of ports added, utilizing level 2 chargers with capacity constraints.

The key missing elements in the current literature are the lack of consideration for spatiotemporal real-time data and the disconnect between spatial and grid-focused approaches. Existing spatially focused studies often neglect the practical limitations of the electrical grid, while grid-focused research tends to rely on hypothetical, statistically modeled EV charging demands. Multidisciplinary approaches integrating transportation and electrical networks remain in their infancy. To address these gaps, this study presents a hybrid framework that bridges these critical shortcomings in EV infrastructure planning. This approach integrates spatiotemporal network infrastructure planning with electric grid considerations. Our contributions are listed as follows.

EV charging demand prediction over a spatiotemporal field

We develop a pipeline for accurate demand prediction over a real continuous spatiotemporal field. A comprehensive sensitivity analysis is conducted to optimize grid resolution, strengthening the correlation between points of interest (POIs) and charging EV demand for more stable and accurate forecasting. To create a lasting impact and account for uncertainty, the demand forecast is leveraged to generate multiple time-dependent scenarios.

Stochastic two-stage nonlinear mixed-integer programming problem

After integrating time-varying EV charging demand, we solve a nonlinearly constrained stochastic two-stage optimization problem for location allocation and sizing planning, incorporating electrical grid considerations across a citywide area network.

Grid hosting capacity analysis and validation through a real-world case study

Power flow simulations and a hosting capacity study are performed to assess the impact of the proposed charger deployments on the existing grid infrastructure, ensuring they do not lead to overloads or undervoltage conditions. This framework is applied and tested in Austin, Texas, within Travis County, integrating spatial and grid data to demonstrate its practical utility and scalability.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We first present the demand estimation process. Next, we introduce the proposed stochastic two-stage maximum coverage optimization problem for EV charger planning, followed by simulation results and a series of experiments evaluating deployment strategies under different budget constraints, and to make sure that integration of new chargers does not lead to power grid overloading. Finally, we conclude with a summary of key findings and future research directions.

Methods

In this section we present a comprehensive methodology that integrates multiple data sources to enhance EV charging infrastructure planning. The methodology is structured into three interconnected phases. Phase I develops a prediction model to estimate EV charging demand using spatiotemporal EV charging data and POI features. Phase II introduces a co-design framework that combines an optimization model with electric grid power flow analysis to determine optimal charger locations, and capacities where hosting capacities align with power demands. Phase III evaluates the feasibility of new charger installations by analyzing their integration into the power grid. A summarized representation of this process is depicted with the flowchart in Figure 1. The study region includes Travis County, Texas, bounded by 30.6376 N to 30.02547 S and −97.3755 E to −98.1821 W (see Figure 2).

Flowchart of the proposed three-phase framework

The process begins with data collection, utilizing spatiotemporal data from Google Maps to identify points of interest (POIs) and divide the area into an appropriate grid.

Phase I employs an XGBoost-based EV charging demand prediction model to estimate demand distribution. Subsequently, an integrated power grid network is constructed to analyze different demand scenarios.

Phase II involves the maximum coverage model, which identifies the optimal locations and capacities for new chargers. This is achieved by solving a two-stage stochastic maximum coverage model, formulated as a mixed-integer programming (MIP) problem and solved using Gurobi.

Finally, in phase III, power flow calculations assess the hosting capacity of the distribution grid, ensuring charger deployment does not lead to grid overloading. This comprehensive framework balances EV charging demand with power grid constraints for efficient infrastructure planning.

Phase I: EV charging demand estimation

It is challenging to accurately estimate the EV charging demands at a city level due to the complex interplay of numerous uncertain factors. These include unpredictable human behavior patterns, rapidly evolving EV technology, shifting government policies, changing urban infrastructure, and fluctuating economic conditions. Moreover, the lack of comprehensive historical data and the potential for unforeseen policy changes add layers of complexity. Without precise knowledge of individual charging habits and future adoption rates, predictions remain inherently speculative. Charging demand varies both spatially, depending on the surrounding environment, and temporally, based on daily activity patterns. Our primary goal is to determine actual EV charging demand in a specific area using a data-driven approach.

Description of POI data

The POI activity data were collected via the Google Maps API in November 2024, covering 2,719 establishments across multiple categories relevant to public charging demand, including retail, service, restaurants, entertainment, as well as traffic-related locations obtained from Texas Transportation Data. Each POI record includes geolocation coordinates and an hourly Popular Times vector, which provides normalized percentages (0%–100%) representing relative site busyness throughout the week.

Public charging station data were collected via the Google Maps API (November 2024), yielding 416 charging sites with 2,580 ports. The dataset includes geolocation, connector types, port counts, and hourly Popular Times data capturing usage patterns. This dataset reflects a mix of charger types—including Level 2, DC fast chargers, and Tesla Superchargers—thus capturing the full spectrum of public charging activity. We leverage this dataset to estimate spatiotemporal public charging demand. Since popular times data reflect site activity rather than energy consumption, including all charger types helps capture real-world utilization patterns. However, residential and restricted-access chargers were excluded from the analysis. Residential charging is typically private, occurring overnight or during long dwelling periods, and is less dependent on external traffic or POI-based demand. Multi-family housing units, such as apartments and condominiums, generally operate closed-access charging managed by property owners, limiting public availability. Since our study focuses on optimizing publicly accessible EV infrastructure that serves a broad user base, only public charging stations are included, aligning with the goals of equitable access, scalable urban deployment, and integrated transportation-power planning.

Traffic data were obtained from the Texas Department of Transportation’s 2022 Average Annual Daily Traffic (AADT) dataset, providing daily average traffic volumes for roadway segments. These static values were incorporated as additional explanatory features representing transportation flow and spatial charging demand; however, finer-grained hourly traffic counts were not available at the countywide scale.

Grid hosting capacity was assessed using the publicly available 2018 SMART-DS Austin synthetic distribution network, simulated using OpenDSS. The grid model includes detailed feeder topology, spatial bus coordinates, line impedance parameters, and baseline node voltage levels across six distribution feeders. Since the goal is to evaluate the feasibility of adding new chargers, grid impact analysis was performed under peak-load conditions, as is conventional in power grid planning studies. The resulting minimum bus voltages were compared to baseline voltage thresholds to evaluate grid hosting limits. This conservative approach ensures that the deployment of new chargers remains grid-safe even under highly stressed system conditions.

Grid cell formation and sensitivity analysis

The segmentation of the study area into grid cells is a critical step analyzing the spacial relationship between EV charging demand and POIs. Previous studies segmented the geographic map based on operational functions, or trip segments.9,45 Selecting an appropriate grid size is essential to ensure a meaningful balance between spatial resolution and predictive accuracy. A finer grid captures localized demand but may result in sparse data, limiting the robustness of predictive modeling. Conversely, a coarser grid may aggregate diverse demand characteristics, potentially obscuring local relationships and misrepresenting the spatial distribution of demand (see Figure 3A). Thus, selecting an optimal grid size requires balancing practical considerations, such as EV users’ willingness to travel for charging, and analytical needs, such as ensuring sufficient data density within each grid.

Prediction model development

The features extracted from the realistic Google map data for Austin area now are normalized for consistency across scales. Further, to address imbalances in charger distribution, we use the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE), ensuring robust model performance across underrepresented regions, explained in detail later.

For the training and prediction datasets, we established four classifications, as shown in Figure 4. C1 captures the relationship between existing charger capacities and the characteristics of their surrounding areas within each grid cell, serving as a basis for understanding how current infrastructure meets localized demand. C2 represents regions without both EV chargers and POIs. C3 highlights grid cells containing POIs but no existing EV chargers, identifying areas with potential charging demand. These C3 cells are treated as candidate locations for new chargers, with their centers representing prospective placement points where the model predicts the required charging capacity. Finally, C4 comprises cells with existing EV chargers but no nearby POIs, which are excluded from the analysis.

Classification of grid cells based on EV charger and POI presence.

For each grid cell, there is also a temporal dataset for EV demand and the previously estimated features. The training data for the C1 and C2 category values is imbalanced, which could potentially limit the accuracy of training and prediction. To address this, we used the SMOTE algorithm, which generates synthetic samples to balance the dataset. SMOTE creates data points by interpolating between existing samples of minority class, which helps to prevent over-fitting and improve model performance on imbalanced data. In our study, we applied the SMOTE to balance the C1 and C2 categories. The training target is the charging capacity of existing stations, represented by the total number of ports, while the training features consist of the number of existing POIs as binary indicators.

We used the XGBoost46 model to predict EV charger capacity at each potential location. Model performance was evaluated using two key metrics: Mean squared error (MSE), which quantifies overall prediction error, and R2, which assesses goodness of fit. We conducted a comprehensive comparison against several baseline and advanced models, including linear regression, artificial neural networks (ANN), deep neural networks (DNN), decision trees, and random forest. As shown in Table 4, XGBoost achieved the lowest test MSE and the highest R2, demonstrating superior accuracy and generalization.

Our POI data includes hourly activity information and is used as structured, tabular features, such as hourly averages and peak usage values, rather than as sequential time series. In this format, XGBoost is particularly well-suited due to its ability to handle high-dimensional, non-sequential data and to capture complex nonlinear relationships without overfitting. Moreover, the limited dataset size posed challenges for effectively training deeper neural network models such as ANN and DNN. As a result, XGBoost was selected for the demand prediction task in Phase I (as defined in the STAR Methods section). Its gradient boosting mechanism further enhances performance by iteratively correcting prediction errors, resulting in efficient and robust forecasting. Key hyperparameters were configured as follows: 800 boosting rounds, a maximum tree depth of 8, a learning rate of 0.01, a training instance sampling rate of 0.7, and a feature sampling rate of 0.8.

This study incorporates methodological advances to improve the accuracy and spatial resolution of EV charging demand estimation. In addition to POI features, we integrate road traffic volume and transportation infrastructure variables, which strongly correlate with charging demand and capture dynamic travel behavior. An adaptive grid sizing approach is applied to balance spatial correlation, driver mobility patterns, and data density, allowing the model to capture localized demand variation while maintaining stability. Combined with XGBoost’s nonlinear learning capabilities, this framework enables effective and scalable forecasting of public EV charging demand at high spatial and temporal resolution.

Phase II: Two-stage stochastic maximum coverage model for optimal placement and sizing of EV charging infrastructure

In this section, we present our stochastic problem formulation for the maximum coverage problem, along with an algorithm to solve it. We model this as a two-stage stochastic MIP problem. The primary goal is to determine the optimal locations for expanding charging facilities, focusing on both establishing new charging stations and increasing the capacity of existing ones by adding additional ports. The uncertainty comes into the picture from hourly varying EV charging demands throughout the day. This problem is formulated as a two-stage stochastic programming model, where in the first stage, the decision focuses on determining the location and capacity of newly established charging stations and added ports at the existing chargers. The second stage focuses on the assignment of vehicle demand to nearby charging stations.

For any given demand point, there are two possible actions: either route the demand to a nearby existing charging station with potential for port expansion, or convert the demand point itself into a charging station to cater to its own demand as well as that of nearby points, shown in Figure 5. The stochasticity in the model arises from the hourly variation in charging station demand. Here, we restrict the analysis to level 2 charging stations to ensure feasibility and standardization.

Phase II: Two-stage stochastic optimization framework

The EV infrastructure planning problem is modeled as a two-stage stochastic maximum coverage optimization where: The first stage determines the optimal location and capacity of charging ports, while the second stage focuses on the efficient assignment of EV charging demands to the available nearby stations, accounting for spatial and temporal demand variability.

Establishing a new charging station involves both fixed and variable costs. The fixed costs primarily include the construction of the station itself, which is assumed to accommodate 12–15 charging ports. This requires approximately 4,000 square feet of land. Based on Austin commercial listings, the cost of such land is around $35,000, while the construction cost for a 15-car parking lot is an additional $15,000. Thus, the total cost to construct the parking lot amounts to approximately $50,000. Variable costs, on the other hand, are incurred for each new charging port added. Taking into account labor, materials, permits, and taxes, the cost per port for a level 2 charging station is approximately $3,000 4.

Decision variables

For clarity, we define J as the set of all potential locations for establishing a charger and existing chargers, where J:=J1∪J2. Here, J1 denotes the set of all candidate locations for establishing new chargers, while J2 refers to the set of all existing chargers. P represents the set of all buses within the Austin power grid. A binary variable xj equals 1 when a charging station is established at a new site for j∈J. Superscript “1” (xj1) indicates a newly opened station, while “2” (xj2) represents the expansion of an existing one. The capacity of newly added ports is represented by two sets of integer nonnegative decision variables: zj1 for a newly opened charging station and zj2 for an existing one j∈J.

Next, the second-stage decisions are captured by a continuous nonnegative decision variable, yi,j,t, which represents the amount of EV demand traveling from demand point i to charging station j∈J within scenario t∈T. For this decision and infrastructure planning, we have selected a 24-h timeline, making T=24. While this timeline could be extended for a yearly planning horizon, obtaining year-long data from Google maps poses significant challenges. Therefore, in our model, we limit the timeline to 24 h, with decisions being made on an hourly basis. Furthermore, superscripts “1” in yi,j,t1 indicate the amount of demand traveling to a newly established charging station j∈J1, while yi,j,t2 specifies the demand directed to an existing charging station j∈J2.

Establishing a completely new charging station is more expensive than expanding an existing one. Specifically, for term−1 in the objective function equation 2, E1 is greater than E2. For the Austin area, the establishment cost is assumed to be $50,000. Values E1 and E2 are adjusted and normalized for the multiobjective objective function in equation 2. Next, term−2 represents the additional capacity required at a charging station, quantified by the number of charging ports. In this context, C1 is the per-port installation cost for level 2 charging stations. As discussed earlier, this cost is approximately $3,000, considering labor, materials, permits, and taxes. Another critical factor is the distance matrix, represented by Ci,j2 and Ci,j3. The distance matrices are calculated using the Euclidean distance between demand points and charger locations. It is assumed that all scenarios are equally likely, and consequently, term−3 in the formulation reflects the average travel cost across all scenarios. Lastly, term−4 emphasizes the distributed placement of charging stations across the 8,500 power grid buses to avoid overloading any single bus, where Cp,j4 denotes the minimum voltage value at bus p∈P in the power grid and its nearest charging station j∈J. We have determined the placement of chargers to ensure that voltage limits in the power grid are upheld, prioritizing buses with higher minimum voltage levels. The matrix Cp,j4 encapsulates the priority list of preferring bus p∈P for any charger j∈J.

Phase III: Distribution grid EV hosting capacity

In this phase, the primary objective is to evaluate the technical feasibility of integrating newly planned charging stations and expanding existing ones, while accounting for the operational constraints of the distribution network. Phase III ensures that such deployments do not degrade grid performance, particularly with respect to maintaining voltage stability under increased loading conditions.

Hosting capacity analysis is a well-established concept in power system planning, particularly in the context of integrating distributed energy resources (DERs). While traditional hosting capacity assessments primarily address overvoltage concerns from power injection by DERs, the analysis for EV chargers shifts the focus to network overloading and voltage drop, since EV chargers act as large, variable loads. Thus, the objective is to assess the minimum voltage levels across feeder buses under peak load conditions to ensure the network can accommodate the additional demand introduced by the EV charging infrastructure.

The power network model for the Austin area network in OpenDSS is modified by the addition of existing chargers for simulation. Since the baseline network is modeled to incorporate all existing EV loads and their associated impacts, the analysis in phase III is focused on evaluating the additional charging load from both newly planned chargers and the expansion of existing ones. The hosting capacity analysis involves a stochastic scenario generation method, which is detailed in Algorithm 1.

The resulting EV hosting capacity (EVHC) from the hosting capacity analysis represents the maximum additional load that can be safely introduced by the EV chargers at locations identified in the planning model. The charger capacities determined in phase II can be compared against this value to assess whether the deployment plan yields a technically feasible configuration.

Results and discussions

In this section, we present the simulation results obtained from our proposed three-phase methodology. We first showcase the optimal placement and sizing outcomes from the two-stage stochastic optimization, followed by the analysis of minimum voltage levels across six different regions. We later provide three different experiments that include multiple case studies to identify effective strategies for expanding EV charging station infrastructure.

Optimal placement and sizing of new EV charging infrastructure

From phase I, determining the EV charging demand requirements, we solve the two-stage stochastic optimization problem to select the best location of the charging station in phase II. Objective here is to reduce the establishment costs for charging station, reduce travel cost, and try not to overload the grid. In Figure 6, the EV charging demand points within the Austin network are represented by red triangles. To meet these demand points, new charging ports are added at existing charger locations, as well as entirely new charging locations are established. This problem is addressed by utilizing a network that integrates both power and transportation.

In addition to cost minimization, the objective function incorporates travel distance reduction and seamless grid integration without causing overload. To prevent localized overloading of power grid nodes, the model favors a spatially distributed placement of charging stations, avoiding excessive clustering around a single power bus. Overall, the resulting deployment balances accessibility, cost-efficiency, and grid reliability, ensuring that all EV charging demands are met with minimal system-wide travel effort and no adverse impact on the grid infrastructure. Details of this optimization are mentioned in phase II of our methodology. Next, we will explain the final phase III process for hosting capacity.

Distribution grid power hosting capacity

The final step in evaluating the proposed charging network is to verify that the additional load introduced by EV chargers do not violate power grid operating limits. Specifically, we examine hosting capacity and steady-state voltage constraints to ensure that integrating the newly planned chargers is feasible under existing distribution infrastructure. Our analysis leverages a synthetic feeder model of the Austin-area network, where P1R represents a suburban feeder and P1U through P5U represent urban regions (see Figure 7A).

To establish a baseline, the current EV charger network in Austin was mapped onto the Austin area synthetic test feeder model, and a power flow simulation in OpenDSS was performed to capture baseline voltage profiles under existing load conditions. Notably, the minimum per-unit (pu) voltages at the baseload conditions are 0.718 pu for the region P1R, 0.851 pu for P1U, 0.814 pu for P2U, 0.783 pu for P3U, 0.819 pu for P4U, and 0.860 pu for P5U. These values serve as the baseline against which the additional charging load is evaluated for each feeder.

A hosting capacity analysis methodology has been utilized that incorporates scenario generation and power flow simulations to evaluate network operational limits. Each scenario involves a new placement of level 2 chargers while incrementally increasing capacity. The hosting capacity for each feeder is then defined as the maximum additional charger capacity that does not overload the network beyond that of its baseline, i.e., the voltage does not fall below the baseline minimum voltage. Importantly, the hosting capacity is determined independent of the power ratings of the planned chargers; it simply represents the maximum power that can be drawn without adverse voltage effects. The total requested power, on the other hand, is calculated by multiplying the number of planned charging ports by candidate power ratings, i.e., 7.2 kW and 14 kW. The deployment scenarios are assessed for feasibility by ensuring that the total power ratings of the added charging ports do not exceed the feeder’s hosting capacity.

In Figure 7B, the hosting capacity of each feeder (from Figure 7C) is compared with the power demand resulting from the addition of new chargers. The total number of ports to be added, determined using the maximum coverage model, corresponds to one of the two types of level 2 chargers (i.e., 7.2 kW, 14 kW). The power demand from these newly added ports, across different charger ratings, is then compared to the hosting capacity and is found to remain within this limit in all cases. Our results reveal distinct differences among the feeders. Urban feeders (P2U, P3U, and P5U) exhibit relatively higher hosting capacities, largely due to the presence of an already well-established network of EV chargers that minimizes the need for extensive new installations, resulting in fewer additional charging ports being assigned during the optimization process. In contrast, feeder P4U, which currently has fewer chargers in its vicinity, is allocated a larger number of new ports. Although P4U’s hosting capacity is somewhat more constrained compared to the urban feeders, the higher allocation reflects the anticipated regional demand and indicates that special considerations such as the deployment of smart charging algorithms or potential grid reinforcement may be necessary to prevent exceeding the available capacity in the future. Lastly, the suburban feeder (P1R) is characterized by longer radial lines and a less robust baseline infrastructure, meaning that it may face more stringent hosting constraints and require additional attention in future planning.

In summary, the discussion of Figure 7B emphasizes that while the entire network can accommodate level 2 chargers, the optimal deployment strategy must consider both the existing infrastructure and the specific hosting capacity of each feeder. Urban areas with high hosting capacity may require only modest expansions, focusing on upgrading or optimizing existing installations, whereas regions such as P4U with lower pre-existing charger density may need a more aggressive expansion plan and potentially additional grid support measures. This grid-aware approach ensures that new EV charging sites are both technically viable and aligned with local demand, ultimately contributing to a more resilient and efficient distribution system.

The proposed multi-stage framework effectively addresses the critical challenge of integrating the EV charging infrastructure with real-world demand and power grid constraints. By leveraging spatial and temporal data, in phase I, the framework predicts EV charging demand, ensuring that new charging stations are placed in locations with the highest potential utilization. Unlike conventional approaches that either focuses on transportation demand modeling or power grid constraints in isolation, this framework seamlessly combines both elements. In phase II, the framework optimizes charger placement by solving a two-stage stochastic maximum coverage problem, where the first stage identifies optimal locations for new charging stations and determines the required capacity expansion at existing stations, while the second stage dynamically assigns EV users to available chargers. This dual-stage approach ensures that the infrastructure remains adaptable to fluctuating demand throughout the day. By integrating transportation and power networks, the model ensures that the distribution of chargers not only improves accessibility for EV users but also prevents excessive strain on specific grid locations, maintaining overall system stability. Finally, phase III validates the feasibility of the proposed charging network by evaluating power grid hosting capacity and voltage stability. The power flow analysis provides insights into the ability of different feeders to accommodate additional EV charging loads without exceeding operational limits. Results indicate that urban feeders generally have higher hosting capacities due to pre-existing infrastructure, whereas suburban and underdeveloped feeders may require additional reinforcements. By comparing each feeder’s hosting capacity with the aggregated power demand from newly deployed chargers, the study highlights potential constraints and suggests adaptive strategies, such as smart charging algorithms or grid reinforcements, to mitigate overload risks. This grid-aware planning approach ensures that new EV charging infrastructure is both strategically located and technically viable, ultimately fostering a more reliable and scalable charging ecosystem. Conclusions

This study develops a comprehensive three-phase framework that combines spatiotemporal demand estimation, stochastic optimization, and power grid hosting capacity analysis to address key challenges in EV charger planning. The framework is validated using a real-world case study in Austin, Texas. Phase I develops a high-resolution EV charging demand model using Google Popular Times, POI data, road traffic, and network features, generating 24-h temporal scenarios. Phase II formulates a two-stage stochastic MIP to optimize charger siting and sizing by minimizing installation cost, travel distance, and grid stress under power and budget constraints. Phase III performs power flow analysis in OpenDSS to verify compliance with grid hosting capacity limits. We perform experiments under varying budget levels and charger sizing configurations. Results demonstrate that budget flexibility supports more cost-effective and grid-compliant deployments, whereas tighter budgets lead to spatially concentrated loads, increased EV travel costs, and unmet demand. These findings underscore the importance of integrating transportation behavior, real-time spatial data, and power grid constraints in EV infrastructure planning.

Future directions

In future work, economic factors, projected EV growth, policy interventions, and reliability metrics can be incorporated into the planning framework to improve the estimation of future demand. Efforts will also be directed toward enhancing the accuracy of forecasting models. This study assumes that Google Popular Times data serves as a reliable proxy for human activity patterns and, by extension, typical EV charging demand. While this approach offers valuable insights, validating the model with real-world charging session data would further strengthen the robustness of the analysis. Potential enhancements include incorporating actual charging usage data, extending the optimization to capture year-long temporal dynamics, and integrating smart charging technologies to improve grid resilience. Key challenges remain in balancing scalability, model tractability, and data availability.

Limitations of the study

The effectiveness of the proposed EV charging infrastructure planning framework is influenced by several data- and model-related limitations:

First, the traffic data used for demand prediction are based on annual 24-h average volumes, which may not fully capture fine-grained temporal fluctuations in mobility patterns. This limits the resolution and responsiveness of demand forecasting in dynamic urban contexts.

Second, while Google Popular Times data effectively represent footfall intensity and general site activity, they do not directly measure EV charging session duration or energy usage. As a result, our estimation of charging demand based on location busyness and charger availability remains an approximation. Availability of real charging session data (e.g., energy dispensed, dwell time) would improve model accuracy.

Third, the analysis is based on a static snapshot and does not incorporate seasonal or long-term temporal variation. Incorporating seasonal patterns could enhance the robustness of demand predictions and infrastructure planning strategies.

Fourth, the grid hosting capacity assessment is based on peak load thresholds. Future extensions could incorporate dynamic power flow simulations across hourly or daily cycles to better reflect grid stress and operational constraints.

Method details

This study implements a three-phase framework for EV charging infrastructure planning, incorporating demand estimation, optimization, and grid impact analysis.

Charging demand estimation

Charging demand is estimated using two primary data sources: (1) traffic flow data from the Texas Department of Transportation, and (2) point-of-interest (POI) activity levels from Google Popular Times. Road networks are extracted using OSMnx and spatially joined with POI locations and traffic data to define charging demand regions. Temporal demand variation was modeled by aggregating traffic volume and footfall data across hourly intervals.

Two-stage stochastic optimization

A two-stage stochastic maximum coverage model is formulated to identify optimal charger locations and sizes under demand uncertainty. The model minimizes installation cost while maximizing regional service coverage. Demand scenarios were generated by sampling fluctuations in POI and traffic intensity. The problem is solved using Gurobi Optimizer within a custom Python implementation.

Grid impact evaluation

Installed chargers were evaluated against hosting capacity constraints using power flow simulations. A real power grid model of Austin, Texas, is used to simulate feeder loading under the proposed charger configurations. Simulations are conducted using OpenDSS via the “opendssdirect.py” Python package. Chargers exceeding grid limits are flagged or adjusted to ensure compliance.

Data integration and processing

Spatial datasets are unified via shapefile overlays and region labeling. All modeling steps are implemented in Python using Jupyter Notebooks. Full source data, software packages, and links are listed in the key resources table.

Quantification and statistical analysis

This study does not involve statistical hypothesis testing, confidence intervals, or p-values. Instead, to evaluate the grid EV hosting capacity under uncertain EV charging demand, we employed a Monte Carlo simulation process.

For each grid region, 1,000 scenarios were generated by randomly assigning power demand values (ranging from 1 kW to an adjustable upper bound) to planned charger sites. Each scenario was simulated using three-phase power flow analysis in OpenDSS. We recorded the total added demand and the minimum voltage observed across all buses in the region. The goal was to assess whether grid voltage constraints remained within allowable limits (e.g., not falling below 0.98 p.u. of baseline voltage).

Simulation results are shown in Figure 7 as scatterplots of total installed capacity versus minimum voltage. These results are deterministic for a given input set but rely on random sampling to evaluate robustness under uncertainty. No classical statistical test was applied.
