Spectrophotometric determination of trace-amount tungsten in environmental and biological samples via ultrasound-assisted ionic liquid microextraction
Authors: Fahad M. Alminderej, Yasmeen G. Abou El-Reash, Alaa M. Younis, Alaa S. Amin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A new, accurate, and selective process has been established for the extraction, preconcentration, and detection of tungsten (W6+) ions in water, alloys, and biological matrices. This technique employs ionic liquid-based ultrasound-assisted dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction, followed by the spectrophotometric measurement of a characteristic deep-red color. For the first time, the compound 4-(2-hydroxy-4-nitroazobenzene)-2-methyl quinolone (HNABMQ) was utilized as a tungsten-selective chelating agent. Extraction was performed in an ultrasonic bath at 25 °C ± 2.0 °C using HNABMQ, together with the ionic liquid 1-octyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([Omim][TF2N]). After centrifugation, the W6+ complex was concentrated within the ionic liquid droplets, and 200 µL of acetonitrile was subsequently added to the ionic liquid-enriched layer before conducting spectrophotometric measurements. The main factors determining the recovery of the W6+ ions were thoroughly evaluated and optimized to attain the best performance. Following the adjustment of both the experimental and instrumental parameters, the approach showed a detection limit of 2.9 ng mL−1 and a quantification limit of 9.7 ng mL−1. A preconcentration factor of 1200 was attained, with a linear calibration range extending from 10 to 280 ng mL−1. At a W6+ concentration of 150 ng mL−1 (n = 10), the relative standard deviation (RSD) was determined to be 2.3%. Validation studies confirm that this technique provides a rapid, straightforward, and effective means of quantifying W6+ ions across a range of sample types, including water, alloys, and biological specimens, with the added benefit of requiring only minimal solvent volumes.

Introduction

Being a transition element from Group VIB of the periodic table, tungsten exhibits remarkable chemical stability. This is demonstrated by its resistance to corrosion and its inertness towards alkalis and acids at ambient temperatures.1,2 Moreover, the material demonstrates superior retention of strength and hardness at elevated temperatures.3–6 Advancements in science, technology, and the global economy have greatly intensified the demand for tungsten. As a critical strategic metal, tungsten holds a vital position in national defense, specialty chemicals, advanced material production, and various other sectors.7,8 The past decades have witnessed substantial advancements in both the theoretical research and industrial practice of tungsten smelting,9–11 resulting in a series of unique technologies.

Tungsten was historically considered harmless; however, recent studies have revealed that increased levels of tungsten in groundwater are significantly associated with higher rates of childhood leukemia and community diabetes.12,13 The analysis revealed significant positive associations between tungsten concentrations (in both blood and urine) and the prevalence of cardiovascular diseases, specifically spasms and stroke.14–16 Consequently, tungsten is categorized as an emerging contaminant of concern.17 In addition to its toxic effects, tungsten has been reported to exhibit greater solubility and mobility than previously assumed.18–20 Because of its exceptional physical characteristics, including high melting point, density, and hardness, it has been extensively employed in technological and industrial applications.21 The release of tungsten and its compounds into the environment, leading to their enrichment, is a consequence of industrial processes, including ore mining and smelting, product utilization, and waste disposal.22,23 At locations affected by tungsten-based munitions, concentrations of dissolved tungsten in soil pore water may rise to several hundred mg L−1, and contamination of nearby groundwater has likewise been reported.18,24 The ability to obtain precise data on tungsten concentrations in soil and groundwater is a prerequisite for modeling its fate and transport and for developing effective pollution mitigation strategies. Nevertheless, a validated, robust methodology for preserving and quantifying tungsten in these environmental matrices has not yet been established.

A range of analytical methods for tungsten determination has been documented in previous studies, such as atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS),25–27 inductively coupled plasma techniques, including atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES)28 and mass spectrometry (ICP-MS),29 X-ray-based approaches, like the microprobe18 and absorption spectrometry, as well as electrochemical methods like voltammetry30,31 and spectrofluorimetric analysis.32–34 sequential-injection spectrophotometry,35 and spectrophotometry.36–40 Many methods used to measure tungstate concentrations generally involve preliminary preconcentration and advanced analytical instruments,28,29 leading to substantial expenses and necessitating skilled operators.

Analysis of metals typically requires extraction techniques to concentrate the target metals from the bulk matrix. Several approaches have been described for this purpose, including liquid–liquid solvent extraction (LLSE),41 co-precipitation,42 and solid-phase extraction (SPE).43,44 The cloud point extraction (CPE) method has also been employed.45,46 A significant limitation common to one or more of these techniques, however, is their low separation efficiency, reliance on cumbersome processes, and the requirement for large volumes of solvent.47 SPE is considered a solvent-free and effective approach; however, it requires considerable volumes of eluents and involves a prolonged extraction process. In contrast, the traditional liquid–liquid solvent extraction (LLSE) technique depends on large quantities of hazardous solvents.48

Nevertheless, most of these techniques are hindered by several drawbacks, including poor metal recovery, elevated blank readings, limited sensitivity, significant interferences, and strict reaction requirements. In response to these shortcomings, numerous advancements have been introduced in extraction procedures. The advent of miniaturized extraction processes—namely, solid-phase microextraction (SPME), single-drop microextraction (SDME), and liquid-phase microextraction—has ushered in a new paradigm characterized by reduced consumption of toxic solvents and enhanced analytical sensitivity.49 Within the SDME technique, target compounds are typically transferred from water-based solutions into a small droplet of organic solvent. Recently, this droplet-based extraction method has gained attention as a more effective procedure for quantitatively isolating metals from complex matrices. Several well-established analytical approaches have been documented for assessing tungstate. These techniques encompass colorimetric50 and spectrometric assays,51 high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (HPLC-ICP-MS),26,53 inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS),52 flame atomic absorption spectrometry (FAAS),53 catalytic current polarography,54 electrothermal atomic absorption spectrometry (ETAAS),55 inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES),56 as well as X-ray fluorescence (XRF).57 Despite their utility, these analytical techniques present significant limitations. However, these techniques are often costly, labor-intensive, require extensive sample handling, and typically result in the destruction of the analyte, limiting their practicality in routine laboratories. Consequently, a simple and cost-effective technique needs to be established for the analysis of W6+ ions when dealing with a large number of environmental samples. Over recent years, there has been growing interest in IL-USA-DLLME coupled with spectrophotometry. The exclusive advantages of the proposed method-based approach, including reduced solvent use, green chemistry features, and analytical simplicity, are illustrated.

Colorimetric and spectrometric methods, for instance, are prone to interference from concomitant ions, compromising their sensitivity. Similarly, the accuracy of tungstate analysis by flame atomic absorption spectroscopy (FAAS) is highly dependent on flame conditions. These limitations become particularly challenging in complex matrices, like biological samples, where the quantitative analysis of trace-level analytes remains a persistent difficulty for analytical chemists.

Assadi and colleagues pioneered the development of dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (DLLME), a method recognized for its remarkable efficiency in both preconcentration and measurement of organic and inorganic ultra-trace analytes in aqueous matrices. DLLME offers numerous benefits, including minimal consumption of organic solvents, straightforward operation, small sample requirements, high recovery rates, and significant enrichment factors. This method has been employed for the detection of W6+ ions at trace amounts in a variety of environmental specimens.58

Ionic liquids (ILs) represent a new category of environmentally friendly compounds that have attracted considerable interest in both scientific research studies and industrial utilization. Because of their unique characteristics, they provide a promising alternative to conventional toxic organic solvents. A major advantage lies in their extremely low volatility, which underlines their role as sustainable and eco-compatible solvents.59–61 Ultrasound-assisted extraction, employing both ultrasonic bath and probe systems, has proven to be a powerful approach for the preparation of samples for ultra-trace analysis, enabling the efficient extraction of trace metals from a broad spectrum of matrices.62,63 Conventional extraction techniques often necessitate large quantities of highly purified solvents, which present safety and environmental hazards owing to their volatility.64 To mitigate these issues, several microextraction strategies, including cloud point extraction, have been introduced and documented.65,66

The novelty of this study lies in isolating and enriching W6+ ions using ultrasound-assisted dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (USA-DLLME) coupled with spectrophotometric detection, with a full assessment of its practical utility. In this method, W6+ ions are first chelated with 4-(2-hydroxy-4-nitroazobenzene)-2-methylquinolone (HNABMQ), enabling both their preconcentration and extraction. The procedure employs an ionic liquid-based ultrasound-assisted dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (IL-USA-DLLME) technique, followed by spectrophotometric measurement. In IL-USA-DLLME, ultrasonic waves accelerate and enhance the transfer of analytes across different phases.67 The aim of the present work was to test the green detection of W6+ in water, biological, and environmental samples by HNABMQ using IL-USA-DLLME for a sensitive and selective spectrophotometric approach.

Experimental

Instrumentation

In a Sonorex ultrasonic bath (Bandelin, Germany) operating at 35 kHz, the ionic liquid 1-octyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl) imide ([Omim]) was homogenized for 2.0 minutes. Phase separation was accelerated using a thermostatically controlled water bath for temperature stability and a centrifuge equipped with 25 mL graduated tubes (Superior, Germany). Elemental analysis was carried out with an ICP-AES instrument (PerkinElmer, model 5300 DV, Waltham, MA, USA). UV-vis absorbance spectra were recorded on a JASCO 530 V spectrophotometer with a 1.0 mm quartz cell, while the solution pH was determined using an Orion Research digital ion analyzer (model 601 A).

Sample solutions and reagents

All chemicals and reagents employed were of analytical grade, and deionized water was used in the preparation of sample solutions. To prepare a 1000 mL stock solution containing tungsten at a concentration of 100 µg mL−1, a measured quantity of sodium tungstate dihydrate was first solubilized in water and then diluted to the required volume. Standardization of this solution was performed gravimetrically using the tannic acid–phenazone procedure.68 Working solutions were freshly made each day by suitable dilution of the stock, with lower concentrations produced through successive dilutions in double-distilled water. The ionic liquid ([Omim][TF2N]) was synthesized according to reported protocols.69–71 From Fluka (Busch, Switzerland), the surfactants Triton X-100 and Triton X-114 were purchased. Before use, 25 mL conical centrifuge tubes with rounded bottoms were immersed in 10% nitric acid for at least 24 hours and subsequently rinsed extensively with double-distilled water.

The ligand 4-(2-hydroxy-4-nitroazobenzene)-2-methylquinolone (HNABMQ), employed in this study, was prepared according to an earlier published process.72 Comprehensive structural characterization and purity were performed using elemental analysis (C, H, N), IR and 1H-NMR spectroscopy, and mass spectrometry.72 The analytical data confirmed the structure of HNABMQ (Scheme 1). A measured quantity of the compound was solubilized in 100 mL of acetone to obtain a 5 × 10−3 M solution, which remained stable over a two-week period. Buffer systems covering the pH range 2.75–11.63, including phosphate, thiol, acetate, borate, and universal buffers, were prepared following standard procedures.73 Acetate buffer solutions were prepared using variable volumes of 0.05 M acetic acid and sodium acetate to reach the required pH value.

Extraction protocol

A 15 mL portion of both the sample and reference solutions, containing tungsten ions (W6+) in the range of 0.22–7.0 µg, was mixed with 2.0 mL of HNABMQ (5 × 10−3 M). The pH was adjusted to 4.75 by adding 5.0 mL of a buffer solution inside a 25 mL glass conical centrifuge tube. The prepared mixture was heated at 90 °C for 5.0 minutes, promoting the formation of the W–HNABMQ complex. This complexation step is essential in liquid-phase microextraction (LPME), as it enhances the movement of W6+ into the organic solvent.

The reagent HNABMQ had a dual role: first, it coordinated with W6+ ions to facilitate extraction efficiency during LPME; second, it ensured selective separation of W6+ by forming stable complexes. After this stage, 2.5 mL of Triton X-100 (0.1% v/v), 0.5 mL of sodium nitrate (2.0% w/v), and 100 mg of [Omim][TF2N] were introduced into the solution. The tube was positioned in an ultrasonic bath for about two minutes until turbidity appeared, indicating dispersion of [Omim][TF2N] microdroplets within the aqueous phase. These droplets served as carriers for the extracted W6+ complexes.

The turbid mixture was subjected to centrifugation at 4000 rpm for three minutes, allowing the [Omim][TF2N] droplets to accumulate at the bottom of the tube. Most of the aqueous supernatant was prudently removed by decantation. The remaining ionic liquid (IL) phase was solubilized in 200 µL of acetonitrile. Finally, the solution was moved to a quartz cuvette (1.0 mm path length), and its absorbance was noted at λmax = 587 nm. A blank solution, prepared under identical circumstances but without W6+ ions, served as the control.

Determination of stoichiometry

The stoichiometric ratio of the W6+–HNABMQ complex was examined using both the continuous variation (Job's method) and the molar ratio methods. These experiments were carried out at a controlled pH of 4.75, with the concentrations of W6+ and HNABMQ fixed at 5 × 10−3 M.

Sample preparation

Water samples

A total of five different water sources were examined: tap water from Benha (Egypt), dam water from Aswan, wastewater from Shobra El-Khema, groundwater, and rainwater from Zagazig. The suggested process was utilized to quantify tungsten levels. Each one-liter sample was first acidified to pH 2.5 using nitric acid to inhibit metal ion adsorption onto the container walls. The solutions were then filtered to eliminate suspended particles. For the preconcentration step, the pH was readjusted to 3.5 with an acetate buffer before analysis according to the outlined process.

Human hair samples

Human hair samples (both female and male) were initially rinsed with deionized water, followed by three successive washes using chloroform and acetone. They were then washed again three times with deionized water and subsequently dried at 60 °C ± 2.0 °C. A precise portion of the dried hair (0.50 g) was transferred into 250 mL beakers, to which 12 mL of concentrated HNO3 and 2.0 mL of concentrated HClO4 were introduced. The mixture was digested on a hot plate, first at 100 °C for 45 minutes and subsequently at 150 °C for an additional 45 minutes. After complete dissolution, the solution was cooled to 70 °C, and 5.0 mL of 30% H2O2 was introduced. The contents were further heated to dryness at 200 °C. The obtained white residue was treated with about 20 mL of 1.0 M HNO3 and kept at 100 °C for 1.0 hour. The final solution was diluted with water and standardized to a final volume of 250 mL in a volumetric flask. Aliquots of 20 mL from this clear solution were subjected to analysis using the specified procedure.

Blood and urine samples

Blood (5.0 mL) and urine (25 mL) samples obtained from a human male (specify the approval number: FSC No: 062-26) were placed in a 100 mL beaker and digested with 25 mL of a concentrated acid mixture composed of HNO3 (63%) and HClO4 (70%) in a 2 : 1 ratio. Initially, the beaker was heated at 100 °C for 15 minutes, followed by heating at 150 °C for 10 minutes on a hot plate. Following the removal of the watch glass, the solution was evaporated to dryness at 150 °C. The white residue formed was treated with 3.0 mL of HClO4 and reheated at 160 °C until dry. Subsequently, 5.0 mL of 1.0 M HNO3 was added and then subjected to heating at 150 °C for 1.0 minute. The solution was afterward diluted to volume in a 50 mL volumetric flask. Finally, 20 mL aliquots of the clear digest were subjected to analysis using the specified method.

Tea leaves

The tea leaves (Lebton brand, black and green varieties, Benha, Egypt) were first dried and finely ground. The powdered material, 500 mg, was precisely weighed and positioned into a PTFE digestion vessel. To every sample, 8.0 mL of nitric acid (HNO3) was added, followed 10 minutes later by 5.0 mL of hydrogen peroxide (30%). The closed vessel was then heated in an oven at 125 °C for 5 hours. Following cooling, the contents were transferred to a hot plate and concentrated to a final volume of 2.0 mL. Afterward, 4.0 mL of 50% hydrochloric acid (HCl) was introduced, and the mixture was reheated for 30 minutes. Once cooled and the pH was adjusted, the solution was filtered and diluted to 50 mL using deionized water. The level of W6+ ions was then detected using the specified method.

Soil analysis

Soil specimens were obtained from different locations in Egypt. The first sample (Soil 1) was sourced from Zagazig, while the second (Soil 2) was taken from roadside soil within the industrial zone of Shobra city. For analytical preparation, finely powdered fractions (0.1 ± 0.03 g) were treated with 25 mL of 0.5 M K2SO4 solution, hydrogen peroxide, and concentrated nitric acid. The blend was subjected to oxidation, converting W5+ to W6+ over 20 hours at 25 °C. Following oxidation, centrifugation was carried out at 4500 rpm for 20 minutes to achieve phase separation. The aqueous layer was then passed through a Millipore filtration unit fitted with a 0.45 µm membrane to remove residual solids. The clear filtrate was subsequently examined using the designated sensing system in accordance with the outlined procedure.

Results and discussions

To maximize recovery of W6+ ions, the optimal experimental parameters for the ionic liquid-assisted ultrasound-dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (IL-USA-DLLME) process were investigated. The study evaluated the influence of key variables, including sodium nitrate (NaNO3) concentration, HNABMQ concentration in the ionic liquid, ionic liquid volume, solution pH, centrifugation and sonication times. Ionic liquid screening

The unique features of ionic liquids (ILs) largely stem from the capability of their constituent salts to act simultaneously as hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, as well as from the extent to which electrical charges are concentrated on the anionic portion.61,74 Key factors shaping the observable properties of ILs include their intrinsic hydrogen-bonding capacity, the charge distribution within anions, overall polarity, and the magnitude of dispersive interactions.75 ILs containing the imidazolium cation display a highly organized hydrogen-bonding network, significantly impacting chemical reactions and various processes. The presence of the [TF2N] anion often promotes the formation of biphasic systems, primarily due to its limited solubility in water. The environmentally favorable reputation of ILs is frequently linked to their extremely low volatility.61,76 While their low tendency to vaporize minimizes direct atmospheric pollution, it does not automatically eliminate potential environmental risks. Additionally, the [TF2N] anion provides enhanced stability in aqueous conditions and offers further advantages because of its hydrophobic characteristics.61,76

Influence of ionic liquid amount

Stock solutions of [Omim][TF2N] at a concentration of 1.0 mg L−1 were prepared using propanone. The amount of [Omim][TF2N] ionic liquid plays a critical role in achieving efficient analyte extraction. Nonetheless, using an excessive quantity can result in an unfavorable phase volume ratio. Consequently, the experiments were intended to establish the minimum IL amount required to maximize the preconcentration factor. The effect of different IL dosages, spanning from 15 to 150 mg added to a 25 mL sample, on extraction efficiency was examined. Findings demonstrated a considerable dependency of extraction performance on the IL amount. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the best extraction occurred with 100 mg of the ionic liquid, with no significant enhancement noted at larger amounts. Given the necessity of low flow resistance, efficient enrichment, and high hydrophobicity, 100 mg of IL was selected as the most appropriate quantity for the extraction procedure.

Influence of acidity and alkalinity

The acidity or alkalinity (pH) of the sample solution is a critical factor in the IL-USA-DLLME procedure, as it directly affects both the formation of the hydrophobic complex and the efficiency of its extraction. The influence of pH on complexation was evaluated within a span of 2.75 to 11.66, while maintaining all other experimental parameters constant. When the pH is below 3.5, the nitrogen atoms in HNABMQ are protonated, limiting the binding sites available for W6+ ions. In contrast, at a pH between 4.5 and 5.0, the hydroxyl group of HNABMQ undergoes deprotonation, facilitating coordination of W6+ to the azo nitrogen and enabling effective complex formation. In basic conditions, the transformation of W6+ into its hydroxylated form diminishes the efficiency of its separation and concentration. Experimental results (Fig. 2) show that reliable extraction occurs within the pH range of 4.5–5.0, leading to the selection of pH 4.75 as the optimal value for all subsequent experiments.

Impact of HNABMQ concentration

The influence of HNABMQ concentration (0.4–6.0 × 10−4 M) on the extraction of W6+ ions was evaluated using the IL-USADLLME technique. As shown in Fig. 3, the absorbance of the resulting complex increased linearly with HNABMQ concentration until a plateau was reached at 4 × 10−4 M. This concentration was deemed optimal and was adopted for subsequent studies.

Influence of salinity

Increasing the concentration of the salt has previously been proposed as a strategy for enhancing extraction in single-drop microextraction (SDME),77,78 hollow-fiber liquid-phase microextraction (HF-LPME), and dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (DLLME).79,80 Salts can influence the volume of the extraction phase through either “salting-in” or “salting-out” mechanisms.81 In contrast, a distinctive feature of ionic liquids (ILs) is that the solubility of [Omim][TF2N] is mostly unaffected even at elevated salt levels. Moreover, their high density enables the small extraction droplets to separate easily in saturated salt solutions,82,83 minimizing the adhesion of target analytes to container surfaces. The negligible solubility of hydrophobic ILs in water also ensures that residual salts from the sample matrix exert minimal impact. Therefore, altering the salt concentration is not expected to substantially modify the extraction-phase volume. Sodium nitrate (NaNO3) was used to investigate the effect of salinity over a 0.0–0.8% (w/v) range. Recovery of W6+ ions increased slightly up to 0.4% (w/v) NaNO3, probably because of a salting-out effect, but remained essentially unchanged between 0.5% and 0.8% (w/v) (Fig. 4). Consequently, the IL-USA-DLLME method is effective for isolating and concentrating W6+ ions in samples comprising up to 0.4% (v/v) salt. To optimize recovery, 0.4% (v/v) NaNO3 was employed in all subsequent experiments.

Selection and quantity of anti-sticking agent

After centrifugation, a part of the ionic liquid (IL) phase was found to adhere to the walls of the centrifuge tube. To address this problem, non-ionic surfactants were added to the samples.84 These surfactant molecules enveloped the tiny IL droplets during phase separation, forming a protective layer that minimized contact between the IL and the tube surface, thereby avoiding adhesion. Two non-ionic surfactants, Triton X-100 and Triton X-114, were tested and compared. Triton X-114 caused a decrease in recovery, whereas Triton X-100 enhanced it. Consequently, Triton X-100 was chosen as the favored anti-adhesion agent. Furthermore, a concentration of 0.01% (v/v) was identified as optimal for integration into the IL-USA-DLLME procedure.

Influence of ultrasonic treatment time

The viscosity of the extraction solvent restricts the movement of analytes from the aqueous phase. In the IL-USADLLME approach, forming a turbid mixture substantially increases the interface between the extraction solvent and the primary aqueous sample.70 Thus, achieving efficient dispersion is crucial for successful extraction. One key factor in this process is the duration of ultrasonic treatment. Prolonging sonication enhances the spread of the extracting solvent throughout the aqueous solution, resulting in greater turbidity. The effect of sonication time was evaluated over a range of 1.0 to 5.0 minutes, revealing that at least 2.0 minutes were required to attain full turbidity. Therefore, 2.0 minutes was established as the ideal duration for the dispersion step.

Influence of centrifugal separation time

The time allocated for centrifugal separation plays a crucial role in effectively isolating the ionic liquid from the aqueous phase. Experiments were carried out using centrifugation intervals between 1.0 and 7.0 minutes at a speed of 4000 rpm. Prolonged centrifugation may lead to re-dispersion of the ionic liquid back into the aqueous layer, whereas too short a duration prevents adequate phase separation. Therefore, a centrifugation time of 3.0 minutes was found to provide optimal results.

Nature of the fixed complex

The stable W6+–HNABMQ complex was illustrated at pH 4.75, using the molar ratio and Job's continuous variation procedures. By altering the concentration of HNABMQ, the graph of absorbance against the [HNABMQ]/[W6+] molar ratio displayed a distinct inflection point at a 3 : 1 ratio, indicating the generation of a stable compound including three HNABMQ molecules. Additionally, the Job's process shows a ratio [HNABMQ]/[W6+] of 3.0. Consequently, the results verified a stoichiometric proportion of 3 : 1 between [HNABMQ] and [W6+]. HNABMQ acts as a bidentate ligand, possessing two donor atoms arranged within the same plane, as displayed in reaction Scheme 2. The metal center establishes coordination bonds with the nitrogen atom of the azo group and the hydroxyl moiety located next to the aromatic ring in the HNABMQ framework.

Effect of coexisting ions

The selectivity of the IL-USADLLME technique for isolating W6+ ions was assessed by investigating the effect of common ions found in water and ore matrices in optimized experimental conditions. The tolerance limit was defined as the maximum concentration of interfering ions that produced a deviation of not exceeding 5.0% in the sample's absorbance. Results indicated that even high levels of typical cations and anions in water samples had minimal impact on the performance of IL-USADLLME for tungsten analysis. In addition, most transition and heavy metals, such as molybdenum, vanadium, and chromium, caused negligible interference, demonstrating that the method provides satisfactory selectivity for W6+ ion extraction (Table 1).

Average of six determinations.

The calibration curve was established by processing a series of standard solutions through the IL-USA-DLLME method and then analyzed spectrophotometrically. A linear response was observed for W6+ concentrations between 10 and 280 ng mL−1. The correlation between absorbance (A) and W6+ concentration (C, expressed as µg L−1 in the final solution) was represented by the equation A = 7.43C + 0.009, with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.9982. The enrichment factor, computed according to eqn (2), was determined to be 1120. Detection and quantification limits (LOD and LOQ) were estimated using the 3Sb/m and 10Sb/m criteria, respectively, where Sb is the standard deviation of the blank (2.05 × 10−4) and m denotes the slope of the post-extraction calibration curve (1.67 ng mL−1). The calculated LOD and LOQ were 2.9 ng mL−1 and 9.7 ng mL−1, respectively. Precision, expressed as RSD for ten replicate determinations of a 150 ng mL−1 W6+ solution, was ±2.3%.

Method validation and deployment for multiple matrices

To evaluate the reliability of the IL-USA-DLLME method, it was used to quantify W6+ in diverse samples—including water, human hair, urine, tea leaves, soil, vegetation, stream sediments, and rocks—with varying native concentrations of the ion.

Analysis of water samples

The improved procedure was applied to determine W6+ ions in tap and wastewater samples obtained from various locations in Egypt and Saudi Arabia (including Zamzam Well). The reliability of the process was confirmed through spiking experiments and parallel sample analyses. Results from these five different water sources are summarized in Table 3. Conclusion

Ultrasonic irradiation rapidly promoted the formation of a turbid dispersive extraction mixture, completing the process in just two minutes. The IL-USA-DLLME approach notably shortens sample preparation time while minimizing the use of volatile organic solvents. Compared with traditional DLLME, this process requires no separate dispersing solvent, relying on a solvent-minimized design. Employing [Omim][TF2N] as the extraction solvent removes the need for ice-bath cooling to generate turbidity. Within the IL-USA-DLLME system, the W6+ complex exhibits enhanced extraction efficiency. This technique is distinguished by its simplicity, rapid execution, and reproducibility, making it suitable for both qualitative and quantitative determination of W6+ ions, using only small sample volumes and micro-scale amounts of [Omim][TF2N]. A significant enrichment factor, coupled with ease of operation, constitutes a further benefit. The method achieves low detection and quantification limits, comparable to or exceeding those of alternative tungsten analysis approaches, while offering an extended calibration range with decreased sample consumption. Cooling during centrifugation aids the ongoing formation of fine extractant droplets, concentrating the analytes at the bottom of the tube and supporting continuous processing with potential for automation.
