CCL20 mediates the effect of cathepsin S on hepatocellular carcinoma development insights from Mendelian randomization and bioinformatics analysis
Authors: Chunling Yuan, Qian Li, Min luo, Chaoyong Liang, Lu Huang, Lifen Wei, Qinglin He, Zhihui Liu, Liming Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDysregulation of cathepsins is associated with cancer development and progression. However, their specific role in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains unclear.MethodsWe employed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses to investigate potential causal relationships between cathepsins, 91 circulating inflammatory cytokines (CICs), and HCC. Subsequently, we explored causal associations between identified cathepsins and carcinogenic CICs. Finally, using the GSE14520 dataset, we conducted comprehensive bioinformatics analyses on the encoding genes of the screened cathepsins and CICs.ResultsInverse-variance weighted (IVW) MR revealed that the genetically predicted Cathepsin S (CTSS) and seven CICs, including C-C motif chemokine 20 (CCL20), Fibroblast growth factor 19, Interleukin-1-alpha, Interleukin-20 receptor subunit alpha, Interleukin-24, Monocyte chemoattractant protein-3 and Stem cell factor levels, were intrinsically associated with increased HCC risk. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of primary liver cancer, accounting for approximately 75–85% of cases worldwide [1, 2]. HCC occurrence is often associated with underlying liver diseases such as chronic hepatitis B or C infection, cirrhosis, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcohol abuse, Aflatoxins and so on [2]. Given its high incidence rate, HCC imposes a substantial burden on human society [3]. Despite significant advances in immunotherapy and targeted therapy for HCC, the prognosis was still dismal due to the limited objective response rate (ORR), adverse drug reaction and drug resistance [4–6]. Hence, further researches into possible molecular targets for managements and prognostic assessment of HCC is highly warranted.

Cathepsins are a family of proteases that have been shown to promote tumor progression by facilitating invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis [7–9]. Besides, cathepsins may be involved in the progression of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis [8], an important pathogenesis of HCC. Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) has been demonstrated to enhance the invasiveness of Hepa1-6 and H22 cell lines, with cathepsins potentially playing a role in this process [10, 11]. Apoptosis is critical for HCC development and progression; it is widely accepted that cathepsins can enter the cytosol and trigger apoptotic cascades by cleaving key proteins in these pathways [12, 13]. In fact, Cathepsin S and L may hinder apoptosis, which would facilitate cancer growth [8]. Intracellular cathepsins have also been implicated in dysregulated cancer cell proliferation, specifically through disruption of the pro-survival and proliferative PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [14–16]. Studies suggest that a positive feedback loop exists between cathepsin C and the TNF-α/p38 MAPK signaling pathway, while cathepsin K induces ubiquitination and degradation of SIAH1 in the absence of its homolog 1—both mechanisms that promote HCC development [17, 18]. Moreover, Cathepsin A functioned as an oncogene and was a potential biomarker for HCC prognosis [19, 20]. Chronic liver inflammation is a crucial pathogenic process in HCC. Interleukin-6, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, accelerates the transformation of hepatic progenitor cells into a more malignant phenotype [21]. Beyond their role in promoting or inhibiting liver cancer, the release of inflammatory cytokines is also a key mechanism underlying drug resistance in HCC, including in chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [22, 23]. Nevertheless, the causal associations of cathepsins and HCC and its regulation of inflammatory mediators are still elusive.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a statistical method that leverages inherent properties of common genetic variants to clarify observational associations [24]. In our study, two-sample MR analysis was conducted to investigate the potential causal relationships from cathepsins and 91 circulating inflammatory cytokines (CICs) to HCC [25–27]. Furthermore, the casual associations between the identified cathepsins and the carcinogenic CICs were explored. Lastly, based on the GSE14520 dataset, we performed comprehensive bioinformatics analyses for the encoding gene of screened cathepsins and CICs.

Materials and methods

Study design and fundamental assumptions of Mendelian randomization analysis

Our study integrated Mendelian randomization (MR) and bioinformatics analyses. Firstly, the two-sample MR analyses was conducted to investigate the potential causal relationships from cathepsins and 91 circulating inflammatory cytokines (CICs) to HCC. Nextly, the casual associations between the identified cathepsins and the carcinogenic CICs were explored. Lastly, using the GSE14520 dataset, we performed comprehensive bioinformatics analyseson the encoding genes of the screened cathepsins and CICs. These included differential expression, overall survival, function enrichment, gene correlation, immune infiltration correlation and drug sensitivity analyses. The overall study design is illustrated in Fig. 1.

MR analysis leverages single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) for causal inference in epidemiological genetics. This approach estimates the causal effect of an exposure on an outcome by utilizing the random distribution of genetic variants. Valid IVs must satisfy three core assumptions: (1) Strong association with the exposure, (2) No association with confounders affecting the outcome, and (3) No direct effect on the outcome except through the exposure. Our methodology adhered to the STROBE-MR guidelines [25, 28].

The genome-wide association studies data of cathepsins and circulating inflammatory cytokines

GWAS summary statistics for nine cathepsins (Cathepsin S, F, G, H, B, O, E, Z, and L2) were obtained from the IEU GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.uk/). These data originated from the INTERVAL study [27], a large-scale plasma proteome genetics investigation that analyzed associations between 27 million sequence variants and plasma levels of 4,719 proteins in 35,559 Icelandic individuals.

In addition, the summary statistics data for 91 circulating inflammatory cytokines (CICs) were sourced from the GWAS Catalog (accession numbers: GCST90274758 to GCST90274848; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/), and the genetic instruments of CICs were derived from a genome-wide protein quantitative trait locus study. This extensive study included 14,824 European-ancestry participants, which applied the Olink Target Inflammation panel to measure proteins [26]. Detailed information for all 91 CICs is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

GWAS summary data of hepatocellular carcinoma

As the outcome in MR analysis, SNPs data for HCC were extracted from the large-scale GWAS summary statistics, the IEU open GWAS project (GWAS ID: bbj-a-158, Trait name: Hepatocellular Carcinoma; Published by Ishigaki K et al., available at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas, accessed on 18 October 2022). This HCC outcome dataset comprises 1,866 cases and 195,745 controls of East Asian ancestry, encompassing 8,885,115 SNPs.

The instrumental genetic factors selection of exposure

For the screening of IVs related to exposures, the cathepsin-related IVs for MR analyses were chosen using a predefined threshold of P < 5 × 10− 6, [29]. For CICs, we adopted a more permissive cutoff, a P-value of 1 × 10− 5, to identify the SNPs correlated with every CIC for further MR analysis, potentially improving the viability [30–32]. The screened IVs for each cathepsin or CIC were then clumped and determined further using linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis (parameters: LD r² < 0.001 and distance >10,000 kb), with the 1000 Genomes Project European samples being taken as the LD reference panel [33]. Additionally, the SNPs that were absent from the panel were also removed [34]. These procedures managed to keep the causalities from being biased. The F-statistic is employed in MR analysis to appraise the IVs’ validity by gauging their potency and susceptibility to bias in causal inference [35]. F-statistics for SNPs corresponding to each CIC were calculated, and only IVs with an F-statistic ≥ 10 were included in the subsequent two-sample MR analysis (Supplementary Table S2).

MR estimates

The effect variants, that is the eventually screened IVs, of cathepsins or CICs were harmonized with the SNPs information in GWAS data of outcome (HCC), wherein palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies were removed. Subsequently, the two-sample MR analysis was further performed using the harmonized data, with only exposures having more than three IVs included in the analyses. The statistical methods employed contained inverse variance weighted (IVW), Weighted median, Weighted mode, Simple mode, and MR-Egger regression approaches. IVW method was taken as the dominating one [36]. Consequently, if the PIVW < 0.05, statistically significant causality was established.

As a supplementary MR method, MR-Egger method expands the possibilities of traditional MR by allowing for potential biases due to pleiotropic effects, and provides adjusted estimates of causal effects. The weighted median approach requires that at least half of the SNPs be valid IVs to achieve appropriate assumptions of causal effects. Additionally, weighted mode and simple mode analyses were performed to enhance the accuracy and consistency of the results.

Multivariable MR is a statistical technique that integrates associations of SNPs with multiple phenotypes into the analytical framework. Using “MendelianRandomization” and “MVMR” packages, we further employed multivariable MR to calculate the each phenotype’s direct impact on the outcome, in which the genetic instruments for each of the significant cathepsins and ICIs were integrated and clumped.

Genetic instrument validation and sensitivity analyses

The presence of genetic instrument heterogeneity will weaken the inferred causal assumptions. Therefore, we assessed heterogeneity using Cochran’s Q test for both IVW and MR-Egger results. Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated via the MR-Egger intercept test. Significant heterogeneity or pleiotropy was indicated by a Cochran’s Q test P-value < 0.05 or a significant MR-Egger intercept (P < 0.05). Finally, leave-one-out analysis evaluated the influence of individual instrumental variables (IVs) on each causal estimate to ensure robustness.

The data source of bioinformatics analyses

Our MR analyses implicated Cathepsin S (CTSS) and its potential downstream mediator CCL20 (C-C motif chemokine 20) in HCC development. Bioinformatics analyses focused on their encoding genes, CTSS and CCL20. Transcriptome data and survival information for HCC patients were obtained from the GSE14520 dataset (Gene Expression Omnibus [GEO]; accession: GSE14520, available at: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE14520). To validate prognostic findings, data from the GSE76427 (accession: GSE76427, available at: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE76427) and International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC; https://dcc.icgc.org; LIRI-JP project) datasets were additionally retrieved.

Differential expression, survival and function enrichment analysis of CTSS and CCL20 genes

Using GSE14520, CTSS and CCL20 expression levels were compared between HCC tumors and adjacent normal liver tissues. Their expression correlation was assessed via Spearman analysis. Based on the MR-derived causal association, HCC patients in GSE14520 were stratified into four clusters using median CTSS/CCL20 expression, that is CTSShiCCL20hi (C1), CTSSlowCCL20hi (C2), CTSShiCCL20low (C3), CTSSlowCCL20low (C4). Overall survival (OS) differences between clusters were evaluated by Kaplan-Meier analysis (log-rank test) and Cox proportional hazards models (reporting Hazard Ratios [HRs] with 95% Confidence Intervals [CIs]). This stratification and survival analysis were replicated in GSE76427 and ICGC datasets.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed on GSE14520 using the “clusterProfiler” R package (v4.0) with gene sets from Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (“c5.all.v7.0.symbols.gmt”, “c2.all.v7.0.symbols.gmt”; “org.Hs.eg.db” for annotation) [37]. Enriched pathways were defined by nominal P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25. The top 6 most significant pathways (P-value) were visualized.

Immune cell infiltration analysis and drug sensitivity prediction analysis

Stromal, Immune, and ESTIMATE scores for GSE14520 HCC samples were computed using the “estimate” R package (reference: “commonGenes.gct”) [38]. Differences between clusters were assessed. Immune cell composition was quantified via CIBERSORT (v1.03) using gene expression data [39]. Infiltration levels of 22 immune cell types were compared across clusters. Correlations between CTSS/CCL20 expression and immune cell infiltration were examined.

Drug sensitivity for chemotherapeutic and targeted agents was predicted using the “oncoPredict” R package (ridge regression) within GSE14520 [40]. IC50 values were compared across clusters to identify differential drug susceptibility patterns.

Statistical analysis

All analyses used R (v4.3.1). Continuous variables across groups were compared via Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Spearman correlation assessed associations between continuous variables. MR analyses employed the “TwoSampleMR”, “VariantAnnotation”, and “gwasglue” packages. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 unless otherwise specified.

Results

MR analysis results

MR analyses examining causal relationships between nine cathepsins and HCC revealed a significant association between genetically predicted elevated cathepsin S (CTSS) levels and increased HCC risk (IVW method: OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.01–1.39, P = 0.04; Supplementary Table S3). This finding was supported by weighted median analysis (P < 0.05) and visualized in scatter/forest plots (Fig. 2A, B).

Among circulating inflammatory cytokines (CICs), elevated levels of CCL20 (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.00–1.67, PIVW < 0.05) and IL-20RA (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.08–1.94, PIVW = 0.01) were associated with increased HCC risk, while FGF-19 (OR = 0.69), IL-18R1 (OR = 0.81), IL-24 (OR = 0.68), CCL7 (OR = 0.73), and SCF (OR = 0.84) showed protective effects (all PIVW < 0.05; Fig. 2E; Supplementary Table S4). Scatter plots for these associations are in Supplementary Fig. 1A-G.

The body’s synthesis and metabolism can have an impact on the inflammatory mediators in specific organs or throughout the body [41]. To identify CTSS-regulated CICs influencing HCC, we performed MR from CTSS to HCC-associated CICs. Elevated CTSS levels causally increased CCL20 levels (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.00–1.075, PIVW = 0.02; Supplementary Table S5), with supporting scatter/forest plots (Fig. 2F, G).

Sensitivity analyses revealed no significant heterogeneity (Cochran’s Q P > 0.05) or horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept P > 0.05) for these associations (Supplementary Tables S3-S5; Fig. 2C, H). Leave-one-out analyses confirmed robustness, with consistent effect directions upon sequential SNP exclusion (Fig. 2D, I; Supplementary Fig. 1H-N).

Multivariable MR (34 instruments) showed no independent effects of CTSS (β = 0.143, P = 0.069) or CCL20 (β = -0.089, P = 0.489) on HCC risk using IVW or MR-Egger methods. Multivariable median analysis suggested nominal significance for CTSS (β = 0.233, P = 0.038) but not CCL20 (Supplementary Table S6).

The upregulated expression level and prognosis value of CTSS and CCL20 genes in HCC

In the GSE14520 dataset, CTSS and CCL20 expression was significantly upregulated in HCC versus normal liver tissue (Fig. 3A, B), with positive intergene correlation (R = 0.17; Fig. 3C). Survival analysis revealed the CTSShiCCL20low subgroup (Cluster 3) had the shortest OS and lowest 5-year survival rate in GSE14520 (Fig. 3D), validated in GSE76427 and ICGC cohorts (Fig. 3E, F).

The CTSShiCCL20low subgroup exhibited the lowest stromal scores in GSE14520, with decreasing immune/ESTIMATE scores from Cluster 1 to 4 (Fig. 4A). CIBERSORT analysis revealed significant immune infiltration differences: Cluster 3 showed reduced infiltration of CD8+ T cells, activated CD4+ memory T cells, γδ T cells, M1 macrophages, mast cells, and neutrophils, but elevated activated NK cells, monocytes, resting dendritic cells, and activated mast cells (all P < 0.05; Fig. 4B).

CTSS expression correlated positively with M1 macrophages, CD8+ T cells, γδ T cells, activated CD4+ memory T cells, neutrophils, and M2 macrophages, but negatively with resting CD4+ memory T cells, activated NK cells, monocytes, and resting dendritic cells (Fig. 4C). CCL20 correlated positively with M0 macrophages, activated mast cells, and resting dendritic cells, but negatively with monocytes and resting mast cells (Fig. 4D).

Enrichment pathways of CTSS and CCL20 genes

GO enrichment for elevated CTSS expression revealed involvement in immune regulation pathways (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Additionally, KEGG analysis identified enrichment in: “KEGG_ANTIGEN_PROCESSING AND_PRESENTATION, KEGG_COMPLEMENT_AND_COAGULATION_CASCADES, KEGG_CYTOKINE_CYTOKINE_RECEPTOR_INTERACTION, KEGG GRAFT_VERSUS_HOST_DISEASE, KEGG_LEISHMANIA_INFECTION, KEGG SYSTEMIC_LUPUS_ERYTHEMATOSUS” pathways were enriched in the up-regulation CTSS expression subgroup (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Downregulated CCL20 expression was enriched in catabolic/metabolic processes for amino acids and proteins (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D).

Drug sensitivity analysis

The CTSShiCCL20low subgroup showed significantly lower predicted IC < sub > 50</sub > values for multiple HCC-relevant agents (Fig. 5), suggesting enhanced susceptibility to the kinase inhibitors (Afatinib, Dasatinib, Saracatinib, Sunitinib, Tivozanib, Alectinib, Axitinib, Cabozantinib, Pelitinib, Foretinib) and chemotherapeutics (Paclitaxel, Gemcitabine, Docetaxel).

Discussion

Chronic liver inflammation, primarily triggered by HBV/HCV infection, metabolic abnormalities (alcoholic fatty liver disease and NASH) and long-term exposure to toxic substances, represents a crucial underlying risk factor of HCC development [42]. Cathepsin S (CTSS) has been identified as a key mediator in the inflammatory microenvironment of tumors and other inflammatory conditions, facilitating intercellular communication across various cell types and ultimately driving disease progression [43]. It is expressed not only in tumor cells but also in T cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, and other components of the tumor microenvironment [43]. Unlike CTSD and CTSB, which stimulate apoptosis, CTSS actually CTSS inhibits this process—an effect that may accelerate tumor growth and contribute to angiogenesis in HCC [8]. Additionally, CTSS is preferentially secreted by CD47+ HCC cells and regulates liver tumor-initiating cells through a CTSS/protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2) feedback loop [44]. CTSS is highly expressed in HCC hepatocytes but barely detectable in normal hepatocytes; notably, higher CTSS expression in HCC patients correlates positively with extrahepatic metastasis [45], which were consistent with our findings. In our study, the CTSShiCCL20low subgroup exhibited the lowest or relatively lower infiltration level of CD8+ T cells, activated CD4+ memory T cells, T cell gamma delta, Macrophages M1, Mast cells and Neutrophils, but the highest or higher infiltration levels in NK cells activated, Monocytes, Dendritic cells resting and Mast cells activated. And the CTSShighCCL20low HCC patients could have a more dismal prognosis, which were validated by multiple datasets, but be more susceptible to some chemotherapy and targeted drugs. Cancer cells often evade host immune surveillance by inducing immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment. T cell exhaustion, in particular, is a primary driver of HCC progression, poor prognosis, and suboptimal response to immunotherapy [46]. Additionally, as part of immune surveillance, macrophage M1 function is to present antigens, release pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and engage in an active immunological response [47]. Collectively, our results may indicate that the CTSShiCCL20low subgroup may be characterized by more immunosuppressive tumor environment.

Moreover, CCL20, alternatively named macrophage inflammatory protein-3 alpha (MIP-3 alpha), is pivotal in orchestrating inflammatory and immune responses by attracting immune cells to sites of inflammation and infection via its receptor, CCR6 [48, 49]. In our study, the CCL20 expression was associated with the catabolic and metabolic processes of multiple amino acids and fatty acids. Wang et al. demonstrated that lipid droplet-laden macrophages may secrete CCL20, which recruited CCR6+ Tregs into HCC and contributes to immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [50]. Elevated level of CCL20 was also served as a potential prognosis biomarker for HCC patients [51], and targeting CCL20/CCR6 interactions could be a promising therapeutic strategy for modulating liver immune responses in HCC. When cathepsin D was lost, tumor metastasis and development were inhibited, which resulted from the skimped polarization of tumor-associated macrophages from immune-inhibiting M2 to immune-stimulating M1 subtype caused by secreting transforming growth factor beta-induced (TGFBI) and CCL20 [52]. Actually, the relationship between CTSS and CCL20 remains unclear. Based on two-step MR analysis, our study revealed that CCL20, an inflammatory mediator, may serve as a mediating role in the effect of Cathepsin S on HCC development. However, our study identified a critical discrepancy: while univariable two-sample MR analyses supported significant causal effects of both Cathepsin S and CCL20 on HCC, these associations were attenuated to null in multivariable MR (MVMR) models after mutual adjustment. This inconsistency is most parsimoniously explained by high genetic correlation between the exposures, suggesting shared biological pathways or environmental confounding. The positive univariable MR signals likely reflect inter-exposure confounding rather than independent causal effects, as MVMR explicitly accounts for covariance between traits. Future studies should: (i) validate these interactions in larger cohorts to mitigate weak instrument bias, and (ii) utilize tissue-specific instruments (e.g., cell-type-specific eQTLs) to improve exposure specificity.

Some potential limitations exist in our study. Firstly, a relatively loose screening P-value was adopted, which may lead to the SNPs of exposures that was not so relevant being included. Secondly, MR analysis provided a clue for further bioinformatics analysis in our study. Nevertheless, the significant casual MR estimates were based on the cutoff value (PIVW < 0.05), and the MR causalities thus drawn were less reliable than those determined by applying FDR values. Lastly, the findings still need more experiments in vivo and in vitro to confirm and explore the underlying mechanism.

Conclusions

This exploratory, hypothesis-generating study utilized Mendelian randomization and bioinformatics analyses to demonstrate that CCL20 may mediate the effect of Cathepsin S (CTSS) on hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) development. Furthermore, HCC patients in the CTSShighCCL20low subgroup could have a poorer prognosis and a more immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, yet exhibit greater susceptibility to certain chemotherapeutic and targeted agents. However, further in vivo and in vitro studies are required to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
