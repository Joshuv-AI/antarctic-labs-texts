Association Between Relapse and Long-Term Kidney Outcomes in IgA Nephropathy
Authors: Xue Shen, Pei Chen, Miao Hui, Jingyi Li, Wanyin Hou, Hongyu Yang, Yang Li, Lijun Liu, Sufang Shi, Xujie Zhou, Jicheng Lv, Hong Zhang

Immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common form of glomerulonephritis worldwide and a leading causes of kidney failure.1 It often follows a chronic course, with approximately 20% to 40% of patients progressing to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) within 20 years after kidney biopsy and experiencing multiple relapses during their disease trajectory.2, 3, 4, 5

Histopathological features of IgAN are highly variable, with common findings including increased mesangial matrix and hypercellularity.2 Clinically, IgAN manifests in a broad spectrum, from asymptomatic microscopic hematuria to rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis.6 Proteinuria, a frequent clinical manifestation, is an established adverse prognostic factor in IgAN.7,8 Multiple studies demonstrated the association between time-averaged proteinuria and poor kidney outcomes,9,10 but relapse rates after remission and the impact of relapse are less reported. IgAN may relapse following upper respiratory tract infections or without a clear trigger, and some reports suggest that tonsillectomy combined with steroid therapy may help reduce relapse rates.11, 12, 13 Although proteinuria remission can be achieved with various treatment strategies, multiple long-term follow-up clinical trials have reported proteinuria rebound after withdrawal.14,15

In kidney diseases such as antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated glomerulonephritis and lupus nephritis, relapse is strongly associated with poor prognosis.16,17 Current studies on IgAN relapse mainly focus on transplanted kidneys, whereas primary IgAN relapse has been less studied.18 A Japanese study on adult patients with IgAN highlighted a significant association between relapse and the progression to ESKD. This study also identified predictors of relapse at 5 years after kidney biopsy, including proteinuria, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), mesangial hypercellularity, endocapillary hypercellularity, segmental glomerulosclerosis, and crescents.19 A study of recurrent IgAN in pediatric patients suggests that age at the onset of IgAN, and the appearance of hematuria after the remission of proteinuria, are critical factors for the relapse of proteinuria.20 However, the significance of relapse in IgAN and the necessity for intensive treatment require further validation. More data are needed to fully understand the risk factors for relapse, and there is currently limited research on the impact of treatment strategies on IgAN relapse.

In our study, we analyzed relapse following IgAN remission among 1,069 individuals in a prospective cohort. We reported relapse rates, elucidated the strong association between relapse and poor prognosis, investigated factors influencing relapse, and examined the effects of various medications on relapse rates and clinical outcomes. This research represents the first comprehensive investigation into IgAN relapse, integrating these aspects to provide new insights into the condition.

Methods

Study Participants

An observational, prospective cohort study was conducted at Peking University First Hospital from January 2003 to June 2023. Participants were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) aged 18 years or older at diagnosis; (2) had IgAN confirmed by kidney biopsy, defined as IgA deposits in the mesangial area according to immunofluorescence; (3) had a follow-up period of at least 1 year.21

Exclusion criteria included individuals with the following: (1) systemic diseases causing secondary IgAN, such as IgA vasculitis, lupus, cirrhosis, or inflammatory bowel disease; (2) IgAN in a transplanted kidney, or pathologically confirmed combined membranous nephropathy, microscopic lesions, or interstitial nephritis; (3) conditions affecting treatment, such as malignancy, pregnancy, or severe malnutrition; (4) ESKD (eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2, dialysis, or kidney transplantation) at the time of biopsy; (5) a follow-up period of less than 3 months postremission. Ultimately, 1,069 individuals were included in this study for analysis (Fig 1). The study received ethical approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: 2013 [548]) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients gave fully informed written consent. The ethnicity of all participants was Chinese.

Data Collection and Measurements

Patients in the cohort were followed up every 1-6 months as part of routine clinical practice (http://ckd.edc-china.com.cn). Baseline demographic and pathological data collected included sex, age at biopsy, and Oxford classification MEST-C scores assessed by 2 experienced pathologists. Laboratory data comprised mean arterial pressure (MAP), serum creatine levels, and 24-hour urinary protein (UP) excretion. eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula.22 Medication details were meticulously recorded, including renin–angiotensin system inhibitors (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin II receptor blockers), corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide, calcineurin inhibitors), sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, and hydroxychloroquine. Records noted the name of the medication, initiation and cessation dates, and reasons for any exceptional discontinuation.

All laboratory tests were conducted by the Department of Clinical Laboratory at Peking University First Hospital, adhering to standard procedures.

Definitions and Outcomes

In this study, remission and relapse are key definitions. Remission is defined as a previous 24-h UP >1.0 g/d, followed by 24-hour UP <0.5 g/d at 2 or more consecutive follow-up visits (with intervals ≥1 month). Relapse is defined as having at least 2 consecutive follow-up 24-hour UP ≥1.0 g/d (with intervals ≥1 month) following remission. Patients who did not experience a relapse after remission were categorized as the nonrelapse group, whereas those with at least one relapse after remission were categorized as the relapse group.

The kidney endpoint event was a composite outcome defined as the first occurrence of either ESKD (eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2, dialysis, or kidney transplantation) or a 50% decline in eGFR.

Statistics Analysis

For numerical variables, those with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using the t test. Variables not normally distributed were reported as median and interquartile range, and compared using the Mann–Whitney test. Categorical variables were analyzed with χ2 tests for between-group comparisons. To explore the relationship between relapse and prognosis, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated. To calculate eGFR slope, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model using “lme4” package in R adjusting for age, sex, MAP, 24-hour UP, and MEST-C scores.

To address missing data for numerical variables, we used the "MICE" package in R for multiple imputation. The missing rate for all numerical variables was less than 20%, and the imputation did not alter the distribution of these variables. Patients with missing medication information or MEST-C scores were excluded from the multivariate Cox analysis.

To identify significant predictors of relapse, we applied LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) Cox regression using “glmnet” package in R. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined to be 0.02 using 10-fold cross-validation, which minimized the mean squared error of the model. To illustrate the varying effects across different time periods, we conducted a landmark analysis using Kaplan–Meier survival curves with the "jskm" package in R.

Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05, with two-tailed tests. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0.1 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; http://www.R-project.org/).

Results

Incidence of Relapse in IgAN

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, our study ultimately included 1,069 patients who achieved remission. All patients were followed for at least 3 months postremission, and were subsequently categorized into the relapse group (n = 348) and the nonrelapse group (n = 721). A total of 348 patients (33%) experienced a total of 411 IgAN relapses, for a relapse rate of 0.104 relapses/patient-year. The median time to the first relapse was 2.3 years after remission. Within the relapse group, 55 patients (16%) experienced more than 1 relapse. Specifically, 293 patients had a single relapse, 48 patients had 2 relapses, 6 patients had 3relapses, and 1 patient had 4 relapses. The relapse group was younger at biopsy compared with the nonrelapse group (34.76 vs 36.99 years) and exhibited higher baseline 24-hour UP (1.32 vs 0.96 g/d) and lower baseline eGFR (79 vs 84 mL/min/1.73 m2). There were no significant differences in the incidence of hematuria or the level of MAP between the 2 groups. At remission, the relapse group continued to show higher 24-hour UP and lower eGFR compared with the nonrelapse group (0.34 vs 0.29 g/d, 63 vs 72 mL/min/1.73 m2, all P values < 0.001). Regarding histopathological features, the relapse group exhibited more severe mesangial hypercellularity (M), segmental glomerulosclerosis (S), tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis (T), and crescents (C), though there was no significant difference in endocapillary hypercellularity (E). Moreover, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors were the primary treatment for IgAN at our center, with a 92% usage rate. The use of additional medications, including corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and hydroxychloroquine throughout the follow-up period, did not differ significantly between the relapse and nonrelapse groups. We noted that the relapse group had a longer follow-up but a shorter duration of remission, implying that differences in follow-up time had little effect on the observation of relapse, which depended on the characteristics of the patients.

Relationship Between Relapse and IgAN Prognosis

The kidney outcomes for patients in the relapse and nonrelapse groups are summarized in Table 2. At last follow-up, patients in the relapse group had significantly higher 24-h UP (1.03 vs 0.26 g/d) and lower eGFR (50 vs 75 mL/min/1.73 m2) compared with those in the nonrelapse group. The relapse group also had a higher incidence of kidney impairment, with significantly more patients progressing to chronic kidney disease stages 3-5 (P < 0.001). This group experienced a greater frequency of eGFR declines greater than 30%, 40%, and 50%, as well as a higher rate of kidney replacement therapy (12% vs 1%, P < 0.001) and kidney endpoint event (42% vs 8%, P < 0.001). Multiple Clinicopathologic Predictors of Relapse

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified several significant predictors of relapse (Table 3): 24-hour UP at remission; eGFR at remission; time to remission; medication used to induce remission; and M, S, and T lesions. As for medication used to induce remission, basic treatment referred to renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitor and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors treatment, not involving corticosteroids, immunosuppressants and hydroxychloroquine. We applied LASSO Cox regression to identify significant predictors of IgAN relapse (Fig 4A, B). The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined to be 0.02 using 10-fold cross-validation. The Association Between Treatment and Relapse

Relapse following remission significantly affects kidney outcomes. Our analysis provides the following recommendations. We examined the influence of induction with corticosteroids or not on relapse rates using landmark analysis. Among the 957 patients studied, 364 (38%) received corticosteroids to induce remission, whereas 593 (62%) did not. Our results indicate that the induction with corticosteroids was associated with a higher likelihood of relapse during the 3-year follow-up period (Fig 5A). Therefore, clinicians should be vigilant about relapse risks in patients treated with corticosteroids. We further divided the patients into basic treatment group (N = 510, 53%) and intensive treatment group (N = 447, 47%) according to the drugs used to induce remission, the basic treatment group exhibited a higher relapse-free rate (Fig 5B), potentially because of less severe disease at baseline. As shown in Table S2, these patients exhibited lower baseline 24-hour UP (0.83 vs 1.50 g/d), higher eGFR (87 vs 76 mL/min/1.73m2), and generally lower MEST-C scores compared with the intensive treatment group. Additionally, proteinuria levels at remission emerged as a significant risk factor for relapse, controlling the 24-hour UP to ≤0.3 g/d at remission was associated with a markedly improved relapse-free rate (Fig 5C, D). Discussion

Relapse in IgAN has received less attention compared with other nephropathies like lupus nephritis, yet it remains a common clinical issue. In this study, the cumulative relapse rate was 33% with a median follow-up of 6.3 years, and the median time to first relapse was 2.3 years after remission. Comprehensive and systematic studies on IgAN relapse are lacking. For instance, Yuan et al23 reported a 15.5% postremission relapse rate with a median follow-up of 66 months, whereas Tsushima et al19 observed a 25.1% cumulative relapse rate over 20 years, based on 5-year landmark analyses with follow-up exceeding 5 years. Variability in relapse rates across studies may be attributed to differing definitions of relapse and remission, as well as inherent selection biases. Although the rate of relapse varies across different studies, it remains a prevalent and significant challenge in the management of IgAN.

We investigated the association between relapse and prognosis in IgAN. Our findings indicate that relapse is closely linked to poorer kidney outcomes, aligning with results from 2 previous studies.19,23 Currently well-accepted predictors of IgAN prognosis do not include relapse, but studies have confirmed that longer duration of proteinuria remission is associated with a reduced risk of adverse kidney outcomes.24 Universally used predictive tools consider factors, such as 24-hour UP, eGFR, blood pressure, other clinical indicators, medications and histopathological scores, and do not take into account the level of clinical indicators during long-term follow-up and at remission.25, 26, 27, 28 Further multicenter studies are needed to clarify the relationship between relapse and prognosis and to assess the prognostic value of relapse.

Our analysis identified 24-hour UP levels at the time of remission as a significant predictor of relapse. Setting different thresholds, we found that controlling 24-hour UP below 0.3 g/d while under remission significantly reduced the relapse rate, whereas a threshold of 0.4 g/d did not. Proteinuria is an important biomarker for prognosis in IgAN and a key surrogate endpoint in clinical trials.29,30 Recent studies suggest that patients with a time-averaged proteinuria <0.5 g/d have significantly better outcomes than those with ≥0.5 g/d,9,29,31, 32, 33 a finding also supported by our center's long-term follow-up cohort.34 Additionally, Tang et al10 calculated hazard ratios for proteinuria levels of 0.3 to <0.5 g/d, 0.5 to <1.0 g/d, 1.0 to <2.0 g/d, and ≥2.0 g/d as 2.22, 4.04, 8.46, and 38.00, respectively, compared with the <0.3 g/d group, suggesting that patients with <0.3 g/d proteinuria have better prognosis. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes 2024 Clinical Practice Guidelines (draft) recommend maintaining proteinuria ≤0.5 g/d, preferably ≤0.3 g/d.35 These findings support the notion that lower therapeutic targets for 24-hour UP may be necessary, which is also supported by our study. Notably, previous studies on IgAN relapse have not specifically addressed clinical data at remission.19,23 Tsushima et al.19 suggested that baseline proteinuria level was a predictor of relapse, whereas Yuan et al.23 only analyzed the relationship between 24-hour UP at 3 and 6 months of treatment and long-term prognosis. Our study revealed that individuals with lower 24-hour UP levels at the time of remission had a reduced risk of relapse, suggesting that a more stringent proteinuria reduction target should be considered in the management of IgAN.

We suggested that medications used to induce remission was a predictor of relapse. In the TESTING trial,36,37 and several meta-analyses,38, 39, 40 corticosteroids were found to significantly improve kidney survival in patients with IgAN. Our study revealed that patients who achieved remission with corticosteroids were more likely to experience a relapse within 3 years compared with those who did not receive corticosteroids before remission. This is supported by the long-term follow-up results of the TESTING study, in which the reduction in UP excretion was not evident at 36 months after randomization.14,37,41 In addition, an increase in proteinuria after discontinuation of Nefecon (budesonide enteric-coated capsules) and mycophenolate mofetil was also observed.15,42,43 Our finding underscores the need for heightened vigilance regarding relapse in patients who achieve remission through corticosteroid therapy. Beyond the critical factors of 24-hour UP and medication use, our study developed a relapse prediction model using variables identified using LASSO regression, which performed well in predicting relapse at both 5 and 10 years.

Our study has several notable strengths. First, it includes a large sample size and a long follow-up period with a significant number of relapse cases. Second, it provides a comprehensive and rare analysis of IgAN relapse, clarifying predictive factors and building a prediction model. However, there are also some limitations. First, there is no standardized definition for IgAN remission and relapse, and we defined remission as 2 consecutive 24-hour UP <0.5 g/d, as multiple studies supporting a lower threshold of proteinuria (0.5 g/d).9,31,33 Second, given the racial and geographic variability in IgAN,44 our findings may not be generalizable beyond the Chinese population studied at a single center. Third, our cohort did not systematically collect information on vascular lesions, such as arteriolar hyalinosis, which are important factors to consider as potential adjustments. Fourth, some novel medications such as sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors were not analyzed because the limited number of users and their limited duration of use.

In conclusion, our study highlights that relapse is a critical risk factor affecting kidney outcomes in IgAN. It identifies proteinuria level at remission, eGFR at remission, and the medications used to induce remission as significant predictors of relapse. These findings underscore the need for increased clinical awareness of IgAN relapse and suggest that more stringent treatment goals may be necessary.
