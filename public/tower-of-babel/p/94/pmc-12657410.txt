Multifunctional DSF-loaded ICG-chelated nanozyme for four-in-one enhanced synergistic tumor therapy
Authors: Kaiqi Hu, Chunlei Ou, Junyou Li, Wai-Wing Cheng, Ying-Yi Lee, Dinggeng He, Hung-Wing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Conventional chemotherapy remains a mainstay of cancer treatment but suffers from poor tumor selectivity and severe side effects. Addressing these limitations, we developed an innovative "4-in-1" strategy utilizing Disulfiram (DSF)-loaded Fe-based near-infrared-triggered DMTICH nanozymes integrating synergistic chemotherapy, chemodynamic therapy, photothermal therapy, and photodynamic therapy. DMTICH nanozymes can specifically release DSF and Cu2+ within the tumor microenvironment, enabling in situ formation of a highly toxic bis (N, N-diethyldithiocarbamate) copper (II) complex (CuET) to reduce the off-target effects and side effects of Chemotherapy. Notably, DMTICH nanozymes demonstrated triple enzyme-mimicking functions in vitro, including catalase-like, peroxidase-like, and glutathione peroxidase-like activities to efficiently mediate Chemodynamic therapy. These enzymatic functions were significantly reinforced by hyperthermia (Photothermal therapy) under NIR irradiation, thereby boosting total therapeutic efficacy within TME. Furthermore, by chelating Cu2+ with the hydrophilic domains of ICG to form ICG/Cu (II), DMTICH maintained the enhanced effect on 1O2 quantum yield, enabling effective Photodynamic therapy. The potent anticancer capability of DMTICH was also validated in animal studies, where it elevated the tumor site temperature to ∼58.3 °C and reduced tumor size to 12.6 % of that in the control group after a complete treatment period. This innovative approach highlights a promising synergistic "4-in-1" strategy, offering a powerful enzymatic platform for multimodal cancer therapies.

Introduction

Cancer remains a pressing global health challenge, with a significant increase in cancer-related mortality observed worldwide over the past five decades [1,2]. This alarming trend has imposed substantial social and economic burdens on healthcare systems [3]. Traditional chemotherapy as a cornerstone of clinical cancer treatment [4,5] offers significant antitumor efficacy but is often limited by severe side effects and poor tumor selectivity [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

To address these limitations, drug repurpose has emerged as a promising strategy for maintaining the efficacy of chemotherapy while reducing its adverse effects [10]. Disulfiram (DSF) monotherapy has been recognized for its low clinical toxicity for many years [11,12]. Interestingly, DSF can be reduced by glutathione (GSH) to form diethyldithiocarbamate (DTC), which rapidly chelates with Cu2+ to produce a bis (N, N-diethyldithiocarbamate) copper (II) complex (CuET) [13]. This complex exhibits significantly enhanced antitumor activity compared to free Cu2+, DTC, or DSF alone, promoting cancer cell apoptosis and acting as a sustained cytotoxic metabolite in cancer therapy [14].

Beyond chemotherapy, the development of synergistic anticancer strategies that integrate multiple therapeutic modalities is crucial for enhancing efficacy while minimizing chemotherapy-induced toxicity [15]. Emerging therapeutic approaches such as chemodynamic therapy (CDT) [16,17]. photothermal therapy (PTT) [18,19], and photodynamic therapy (PDT) [20] have shown great promise. As an emerging innovation in nanotechnology, nanozymes are nanomaterials that mimic the activity of natural enzymes [21,22], which have been found widespread applications in the biomedical field [23,24]. Compared to natural enzymes, nanozymes offer several advantages, such as lower cost, simpler preparation and storage, and greater stability. Additionally, their structures can be tailored to modulate molecular activity, enhancing their therapeutic potential [25,26]. For instance, peroxidase-like (POD-like) nanozymes catalyze the conversion of endogenous hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into hydroxyl radical (·OH) via Fenton or Fenton-like reactions, which are central to CDT [27]. Incorporating high-valence transition metal ions (e.g., Fe3+, Cu2+) imparts glutathione peroxidase-like (GPx-like) activity, depleting excess GSH-a key antioxidant in the TME that reduces reactive oxygen species (ROS) and diminishes antitumor efficacy [[28], [29], [30]].

By designing sophisticated nanozymes that integrate single CDT, PTT, or PDT, researchers have advanced novel anticancer strategies that go beyond traditional chemotherapy [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. These combinatorial therapies often result in synergistic enhancements rather than a mere summation of individual effects. For example, PTT-triggered hyperthermia can facilitate drug release, improve tumor penetration, and increase cancer cell sensitivity to chemotherapeutics [35]. Similarly, CDT-generated ROS can sensitize tumor cells to thermal injury, enhancing PTT efficacy [36]. PDT-induced singlet oxygen (1O2), effectively targets cancer stem cells and suppresses the recurrence of radioresistant tumors [37]. Therefore, integrating chemotherapy, PTT, PDT, and CDT within a single nanozyme platform holds immense potential for multimodal cancer treatment.

Indocyanine green (ICG) is the only FDA-approved fluorescent dye for clinical use in humans [38,39], with efficient absorption in the near-infrared (NIR) region. It has been demonstrated to generate 1O2 for PDT and convert NIR light into thermal energy for PTT, making it an ideal candidate for combined phototherapeutic modalities [40,41]. However, ICG's clinical utility is hindered by limitations such as photobleaching, low photothermal conversion efficiency, poor photostability, and rapid systemic clearance due to binding with plasma proteins [43]. Additionally, ICG lacks tumor specificity, restricting its accumulation in tumor tissues and reducing its therapeutic efficacy [[44], [45], [46]]. To overcome these challenges, advanced nanocarrier platforms are needed to enhance ICG's photostability, improve its photothermal performance, and increase its tumor-targeting ability. Recent study has shown that metal ion coordination can significantly improve the quantum yield of 1O2 generation, thereby augmenting PDT efficacy [42].

In this study, we developed a novel DSF-loaded, ICG-chelated, Fe-based metal-phenolic network multifunctional nanoenzymes (DMTICH), in which the incorporation of ICG/Cu (II) enhances 1O2 generation and deep tumor penetration, reducing chemotherapy-related side-effects and boosting the synergistic effects of CT, PTT, CDT and PDT (see Scheme 1). A Fe-based metal-organic framework (MOF), MIL-100 (Fe), was employed as a nanocarrier to load the hydrophobic chemotherapeutic agent DSF. The incorporation of the metal-phenolic network transformed the hydrophobic DSF/MOF complex into a hydrophilic construct, facilitating its biological application. Cu2+ ions were subsequently coordinated to the hydrophilic domain of ICG, yielding an active nanozyme termed DMTIC. This design not only induced quenched fluorescence of ICG, which can facilitate non radiative thermal decay, potentially achieving higher photothermal efficiency, but also significantly enhanced its photostability and 1O2 quantum yield. The designed DMTICH integrated “4-in-1" strategy: (i) The liberated Cu2+ reacted with DSF to generate the cytotoxic metabolite CuET in situ, which could exert sustained chemotherapeutic effects; (ii) the engineered nanozyme exhibited CAT-like, POD-like, and GPx-like activities, enabling the conversion of H2O2 into O2 or ·OH and depleting intracellular GSH, thus disrupting the redox balance within tumor cells; (iii) the intense local hyperthermia by PTT ensured most tumor tissue ablation which also accelerated catalytic processes; and (iv) ICG/Cu (II) facilitated the conversion of O2 into 1O2 for PDT more efficiently to mediate PDT process. In short, the DMTICH nanozyme, which integrated in situ generation of active chemotherapeutic agents, excellent photothermal performance, and robust ROS production, represented a promising platform for synergistic combinatorial CT/PTT/PDT/CDT, providing new insights into multimodal cancer treatment strategies with high therapeutic potential.Scheme 1Schematic diagram of the preparation of DMTICH nanozymes and therapeutic mechanism.Scheme 1

Synthesis of DMTICH (DSF/MIL-100 (Fe)@TA-ICG/Cu@HA)

FeCl3·6H2O (1.68 g) and BTC (0.54 g) were dissolved in 10 mL of N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and stirred for 10 min. The resulting mixture was subjected to microwave heating at 130 °C for 5 min. After natural cooling, the products were centrifuged at 11000 rpm for 30 min. The precipitate was then washed three times with DMF, and the MIL-100 (Fe) nanoparticles were collected and dispersed into MeOH for subsequent use. 2 mL of a 3.75 mg/mL DSF methanol solution was added to 5 mg MIL-100 (Fe) nanoparticles MeOH solution. The mixture was stirred overnight. The products were collected by centrifugation at 11000 rpm for 15 min and washed with MeOH three times to give DM NPs (DSF/MIL-100 (Fe)). 2 mL of a 2.5 mg/mL TA solution (pH = 7) was added to a 5 mL of 1 mg/mL DM methanol solution. After stirring for 30 min, the products were separated by centrifugation at 11000 rpm for 15 min and washed with DI water three times and redispersed in DI water to give DMT NPs (DSF/MIL-100 (Fe)@TA). Then, 20 mg of ICG was dissolved in 2 mL of DI water to give a 10 mg/mL solution, and 60 mg of CuCl2·2H2O was dissolved in 10 mL of DI water to afford a 6 mg/mL solution. Next, 1.4 mL of the ICG solution and 1.4 mL of the CuCl2·2H2O solution were combined and diluted to 10 mL with DI water and stirred for 3 h at room temperature to form an ICG/Cu (II) dispersion. After sonication for 30 min, 5 mL of 4 mg/mL DMT NPs aqueous dispersion was added to the former solution. The mixture was stirred overnight in the dark at room temperature. Lastly, 5 mL of 8 mg/mL HA was introduced into DMTIC NPs dispersion and stirred for 24h in the dark at room temperature. The resulting products were then centrifuged at 11000 rpm for 30 min and washed three times with DI water to produce DMTICH (DSF/MIL-100 (Fe)@TA-ICG/Cu@HA). The DMTICH NPs were stored in water at 4 °C.

Evaluation of POD-like activity of DMTICH

3,3′,5,5′-tetramethyl-benzidine (TMB) was employed as an indicator to detect the generation of ·OH. Briefly, 10 μL of a 5 mg/mL TMB solution was added to 200 μL of solution containing different concentrations of aqueous dispersion (10, 25, 50, 100, 200 μg/mL) of DMTICH nanoparticles. Afterwards, 10 μL H2O2 (200 mM) was added into the former solution, and all were incubated for 2 h. The mixture was then centrifuged and 150 μL supernatant was subjected to UV–vis spectra analysis.

The medium was then aspirated, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Fresh culture medium containing DCFH-DA was added to each well and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. Green fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Evaluation of GPx-like activity of DMTICH

5,5′-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) was employed as an indicator to detect the decline of GSH level. Aqueous suspensions of DMTICH nanoparticles (25, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200 μg/mL) were incubated with 10 mM GSH in PBS containing 200 mM H2O2 for 1 h. Afterwards, 1 mM DTNB was added to measure the remaining GSH. The absorbance at 412 nm was recorded using a microplate reader.

4T1 cells were seeded onto 8-well plates and cultured for 24 h to reach a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. The cells were then treated with MIL-100 (Fe), DMTH, DMTIH, DMTICH NPs (20 μg/mL). The selected light groups were irradiated with 808 nm irradiation (1.50 W cm−2) for 5 min. After incubation for an additional 12 h, the medium was removed, and the intracellular GSH level was measured using a GSH assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd.).

Evaluation of CAT-like activity of DMTICH

4T1 cells were seeded onto 8-well plates and cultured for 24 h to reach a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. Fresh culture medium containing Ru(dpp)3Cl2 was added to each well and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. Red fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Evaluation of Michaelis-Menten curves

Evaluation of Michaelis-Menten curves and relative conditions referred to previous literature [50]. Briefly, 10 μg of DMTICH nanozyme was dissolved in 2 mL of PBS buffer (pH 5.5). Various volumes (from 0 to 100 μL) of the TMB stock solution (10 mg/mL in DMSO) were added to the reaction mixture in various tubes. Then, H2O2 was added to the reaction mixture to a final concentration of 1 M. The sample was placed in a 37 °C water bath and the initial rate of change of absorbance was recorded at 652 nm. The absorbance increase versus substrate concentration could be obtained and expressed in micromoles per minute (i.e., the catalytic reaction velocity, ν) and this was plotted against the substrate concentration to produce a Michaelis-Menten curve.ν=(νmax∗[S])/(Km+[S])where ν was the initial reaction velocity and νmax was the maximal reaction rate which was observed at saturating substrate concentrations. [S] was the concentration of the substrate and Km was the Michaelis constant. Km reflected the affinity of the nanozyme for its substrate and was defined as the substrate concentration at half the maximum rate.

Evaluation of 1O2 quantum yield of free ICG, ICG/Cu (II) and DMTICH

Evaluation of 1O2 quantum yield and relative conditions referred to previous literature [34]. The ability of ICG, ICG/Cu (II) and DMTICH to generate 1O2 under NIR light was evaluated using DPBF as a probe. The samples were irradiated with an 808 nm NIR laser at a power density of 1.50 W cm−2. Freshly prepared DPBF was dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL prior to use. 3 mL of ultrapure water containing either free ICG, ICG/Cu (II), or DMTICH (each at an ICG concentration of 10 μg/mL) was mixed with 100 μL of the DPBF solution in transparent quartz cuvettes. After laser irradiation, the absorbance at 420 nm was measured over time using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV-3600, Shimadzu, Japan) to monitor the degradation of DPBF, which indicates 1O2 production. The 1O2 QY (Φsample) for ICG/Cu (II) and DMTICH were calculated relative to that of free ICG (ΦICG = 0.2 %) using the following equation:Φsample=ΦICG(KsampleKICG)(AICGAsample)where, Φsample is the 1O2 QY of the test sample (ICG/Cu (II) or DMTICH). ΦICG is the known quantum yield of free ICG (0.2 %). Ksample and KICG are the rate constants for DPBF degradation by the sample and free ICG, respectively. Asample and AICG represent the integrated absorbance areas of the samples between 750 and 850 nm, reflecting the effective light absorption.

In vitro cellular uptake

4T1 cells were seeded onto 8-well plates and incubated for 24 h. The culture medium was then aspirated, and fresh culture medium containing Rhodamine B-labeled DMTIC, DMTICH NPs at a concentration of 20 μg/mL was added. The cells were incubated for 4 h and 24 h, respectively, followed by being washed with PBS solution three times. The cells were stained with hoechst 33342 and fluorescence images were recorded using a fluorescence microscope.

Cell viability assay

To assess the cytotoxicity of DMTH, DMTIH, DMTICH nanoparticles in vitro, 4T1 and fibroblast (L929) cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. The cells (3 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in a 96-well microplate and incubate for 24 h. Subsequently, culture medium was replaced by fresh culture medium with various concentrations (0, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 μg/mL) of DMTH, DMTIH, DMTICH nanoparticles. After 4 h incubation, the cells in the designated groups were irradiated with 808 nm lasers (1.50 W cm−2) for 5 min to induce PTT. After further incubation of 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, cell viability was measured using the MTT assay kit.

Detection of the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

4T1 cells were seeded onto 8-well plates and cultured for 24 h to reach a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. After incubation with culture medium containing MIL-100 (Fe), DMTH, DMTIH, DMTICH (20 μg/mL) for 4 h, the cells in the selected light groups were irradiated with 808 nm irradiation (1.50 W cm−2) for 5 min. The cells were then incubated with 0.5 mL of JC-1 staining solution for 20 min, and then fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

In vitro live/dead staining experiment

4T1 cells were seeded onto 8-well plates and cultured for 24 h to reach a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. After 12 h of incubation, the cells were treated with DMTH, DMTIH, DMTICH (500 μg/mL). A control group was treated with an equivalent volume of PBS. After 4 h incubation, the cells in the designated groups were irradiated with 808 nm laser (1.50 W cm−2) for 5 min to induce photothermal therapy. After 12 h, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. Fresh FBS-free medium containing PI and calcein was added and incubated for another 30 min. Afterwards, the cells were washed again with PBS, and live/dead staining images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

In vitro annexin V-FITC/PI-based apoptosis analysis

4T1 cells were seeded onto 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h to reach a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. After incubation with culture medium containing MIL-100 (Fe), DMTH, DMTIH, DMTICH (20 μg/mL) for 4 h, the cells in the selected light groups were irradiated with 808 nm irradiation (1.50 W cm−2) for 5 min. After incubation for 12 h, cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS. They were then resuspended in binding buffer, and 100 μL of the cell suspension (containing 1 × 105 cells) was mixed with 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC, followed by a 10-min incubation at room temperature. Subsequently, 5 μL of PI was added, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 5 min. The total volume was adjusted to 500 μL using 400 μL of PBS. Apoptotic cell populations in each treatment group were then quantified using flow cytometry.

Bio-TEM imaging

4T1 cells were seeded onto 8-well plates and cultured for 24 h to reach a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. After incubation with culture medium with or without DMTICH (20 μg/mL) for 24 h. After 4 h-injection, the cells were irradiated with 808 nm irradiation (1.50 W cm−2) for 5 min. After washing with PBS, the cells were collected and then added to 2.5 % glutaraldehyde fixative at 4 °C for 24 h. Samples were dehydrated, embedded, sliced and measured by bio-TEM.

Mice tumor model

All animal research protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hunan Normal University (2023−101) and conducted according to the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hunan Normal University. The mice were acclimatized for one week prior to experimentation under pathogen-free conditions. Tumors were established by injecting 1 × 106 4T1 cells in PBS buffer into the right flanks of the mice. Mice with tumors approximately 120 mm3 in size were used for subsequent experiments.

In vivo fluorescence imaging of DMTICH biodistribution

The mice were acclimatized for one week prior to experimentation under pathogen-free conditions. Tumors were established by injecting 1 × 106 4T1 cells in PBS buffer into the right flanks of the mice. When the tumor volume reached around 50 mm3, the mice were intravenously injected with DID-labeled DMTICH (8 mg/kg). The major organs were imaged using an IVIS spectrum imaging system at desired time points (0, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48 h). The organs and cancer tissues were collected from the sacrificed mice at 48 h and observed using the IVIS.

Time-dependent biodistribution of Cu/Fe

Organs of mice and tumor tissues were collected at different time points (0.5, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48 h) after intravenous injection of DMTICH and were dispersed in 4.0 mL concentrated HNO3 overnight, then added with 3.0 mL 30 % H2O2 and heated to about 200 °C until the solution volume decreased to 0.5 mL. At last, the remaining solution was cooled and diluted to 3.0 g with a 2 % diluted HNO3. The contents of Cu/Fe element in the organs were detected by ICP-MS, normalized to the tissue weight per gram.

In vivo anti-tumor therapy efficiency of DMTICH

When the tumor volume reached around 120 mm3, the tumor-bearing mice were treated with PBS, PBS + L, DMTICH (8 mg/kg), DMTICH (8 mg/kg) + L. The mice were treated once every three days and continued treatment for 11 days. During the treatment process, the body weight and tumor volume of the mice were continuously recorded every two days. After 11 days of treatment, mice were executed, and tumors and major organs were obtained from each group. The tumors were weighed and photographed. The collected tumor tissues and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney) were analyzed by H&E staining. In addition, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nickend labeling (TUNEL) staining, and Ki-67 immumohistochemical staining were used to observe cellular apoptosis after different treatments.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of MIL-100 (Fe), DMT, DMTIC and DMTICH

First, MIL-100 (Fe) was synthesized using a microwave-assisted reaction. The preparation process of DMTICH is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Subsequently, DSF was loaded into the pores of hydrophobic MIL-100 (Fe) to give DSF/MIL-100 (Fe) NPs. To confirm the successful encapsulation, nitrogen adsorption measurements were performed on both MIL-100 (Fe) and DSF/MIL-100 (Fe) (Fig. S1). The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of pristine MIL-100 (Fe) was found to be 1048.9 m2/g, which slightly dropped to 927.6 m2/g after DSF loading, indicating that the pores of MIL-100 (Fe) were occupied by DSF molecules in the DSF/MIL-100 (Fe) composite. The EE (Encapsulation efficiency) and LE (Loading efficiency) were tested to be 17.16 % and 20.51 %. Tannic acid (TA) was directly coated onto the surface via coordination with Fe, which completely transformed the hydrophobic nature of the nanoparticles into hydrophilic ones. This intrinsic property was also presented when we attempted to directly incorporate ICG along with DSF into MIL-100 (Fe), the dispersion aggregated immediately (Fig. S3). Subsequently, ICG/Cu (II) dispersions were introduced through π-π stacking interactions with TA. The EE and LE of ICG were tested to be 75.04 % and 34.44 %. The MIL-100 (Fe) NPs exhibited good dispersibility in ethanol (Fig. 1B). TEM imaging showed that neither the incorporation of DSF nor the TA coating affected the morphology of the nanoparticles (Fig. 1C), but significantly enhanced the dispersibility in water, which facilitated their potential application under physiological conditions. Similarly, modifications with ICG/Cu (II) (Fig. 1D) and HA (Fig. 1E) preserved the nanoparticle morphology.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed that the diffraction pattern of DMTICH NPs closely resembled that of MIL-100 (Fe) NPs (Fig. 1F), indicating that the structural integrity of the MOF was not disrupted by the modifications. The loading amount of DSF was then determined using liquid chromatography. Based on the calibration curve of free DSF (Fig. S2), the drug EE and drug LC of DSF in DM NPs were calculated and optimized by varying the added amount of DSF (Table S1). Initially, attempts were made to directly attach Cu2+ ions onto the surface via chelation with TA (Table S2). However, ICP-OES analysis revealed that the copper ion loading was extremely low, which is insufficient to meet the requirement of CuET generation. Interestingly, upon the introduction of ICG, a greater amount of copper ions could be readily loaded. The π-π stacking interactions between ICG and TA left more chelation sites available for subsequent Cu2+ binding, avoiding competition between Cu2+ and Fe ions for coordination with TA. This hypothesis was verified by ICP-OES analysis, which showed that, under identical copper addition, the ICG group achieved a 2.3-fold higher copper loading capacity compared to the TA group (Table S2), which could significantly promote CuET generation and boost the Cu2+-mediated Fenton reaction. The UV–vis spectrum of DMTICH NPs (200 μg/mL) (Fig. 1G) displayed a characteristic absorption peak of ICG at 780 nm, confirming its successful incorporation. Compared to DMTIC NPs, DMTICH NPs exhibited a more negative zeta potential (Fig. 1H), indicating successful HA coating on the nanoparticle surface. This highly negative zeta potential also improved colloidal stability by preventing long-term aggregation. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed the average diameters of MIL-100 (Fe), DM, DMTIC, and DMTICH NPs as 133.5 nm, 147.2 nm, 160.1 nm, and 171.3 nm, respectively (Fig. 1I and S6). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 1J) was employed to confirm the successful integration of each component. MIL-100 (Fe) NPs exhibited characteristic absorption bands, including ν(C-H) at 760 cm−1 and bands at 711 cm−1, 1446 cm−1 (δ(O-H) and ν(O-C-O)), and 1380 cm−1. The characteristic absorption peaks of DSF were observed at 543 cm−1, 1315 cm−1, and 1498 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of S-S, C=S, and C-N-C, respectively. Additionally, absorption peaks at 1693 cm−1 (C=O stretching) and 3160 cm−1 (O-H stretching) were attributed to TA, ICG, and HA, confirming their successful incorporation.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis further validated the composition of DMTICH NPs, revealing the presence of Fe, Cu, C, N, O, and S (Fig. 1K). The valence states of Fe and Cu in DMTICH NPs were examined using XPS (Fig. S4). Peaks at 727.5 eV and 712.8 eV confirmed the presence of Fe3+, while peaks at 710.1 eV and 723.8 eV confirmed Fe2+. Similarly, the Cu 2p XPS spectrum exhibited a binding energy peak at 932.5 eV (Cu2+ 2p3/2) and another at 952.4 eV (Cu2+ 2p1/2). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Fig. S5) and elemental mapping (Fig. 1L) directly visualized the homogeneous distribution of Fe and S elements, with Cu overlapping with S, confirming successful nanoparticle synthesis.

Degradable and photothermal performance of DMTICH

DMTICH nanozymes exhibited excellent stability at pH 7.4, with negligible changes in size observed over 1, 3, 7, and 14 days, as evaluated by DLS analysis (Fig. S7). MIL-100 (Fe) NPs are well-known to be unstable under acidic condition. Besides, the presence of GSH further accelerated the degradation of their framework, as GSH reduced Fe3+ to Fe2+, disrupting the coordination bonds within the MIL-100 (Fe) structure [43]. The acid-responsive behavior of DMTICH nanozymes was further investigated. TEM revealed some structural cavitation after 8 h of incubation at pH 5.5. In the presence of GSH, this phenomenon was more pronounced, indicating augmented framework degradation (Fig. 2A). After 24 h of incubation at pH 5.5, the framework underwent further erosion, leaving only fragmented remnants of the structure. In GSH-treated groups, residual nanoparticles were barely detectable, indicating near-complete degradation. The leakage of copper and DSF would facilitate the formation of high toxic complex CuET, which demonstrated significant UV–vis absorption at 268 nm and 433 nm [51,52]. Therefore, the in-situ generation of CuET at different time point was subjected to UV–vis spectra analysis (Fig. 2D). The formation of CuET progressively increased over time, indicating that the released DSF effectively reacted with co-released Cu2+. Notably, this process was significantly accelerated at elevated temperatures. After the same incubation period (4 h), a significantly larger amount of CuET was generated at 43 °C. These findings demonstrated that the DMTICH nanozyme undergoes pH-triggered disassembly and hyperthermia-enhanced drug generation in acidic tumor environment.

TA has been reported to coordinate with Fe ions in the MIL-100 (Fe) framework, enhancing ultraviolet absorption and enabling effective PTT [43]. Based on our findings, ICG/Cu (II) exhibited quenched fluorescence (Fig. S12), indicating that, after photon absorption, the excited energy was predominantly converted into heat through molecular vibrations and intermolecular collisions, which was subsequently transferred to the surrounding medium [53]. Therefore, the persistent quenched state of ICG/Cu (II) can facilitate non-radiative thermal decay, potentially achieving higher photothermal efficiency than ICG alone. The photothermal performance of DMTICH nanozymes was evaluated at varying concentrations (Fig. 2E). Remarkably, upon 808 nm laser irradiation (1.50 W cm−2) for 5 min, the temperature of the DMTICH nanozyme dispersion (200 μg/mL) rapidly increased, reaching 64 °C within 3 min and 70 °C within 5 min, significantly outperforming TA or ICG alone (Fig. 2F). Additionally, the temperature increase of the DMTICH dispersion (200 μg/mL) was strongly correlated with laser power density (Fig. 2G).

ICG, despite its photothermal capabilities, exhibits several limitations, including a short half-life (2–4 min), photobleaching, and poor stability [32]. Upon coordination with Cu ions, the photostability of ICG was also partially enhanced. After five heating-cooling cycles, the maximum temperature dropped to 63.8 °C, which is 91 % of the initial maximum temperature (70 °C). Even after ten cycles, the maximum temperature could still reach 57.7 °C, maintaining 82 % of the initial maximum temperature (Fig. 2H). The photothermal conversion efficiency (η) of DMTICH was calculated to be approximately 47.58 %, surpassing many well-known photothermal agents such as graphene oxide (25 %), Cu2-xSe (22 %), and gold nanorods (21 %) (Fig. 2I) [44]. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that DMTICH nanozymes efficiently and rapidly converted light energy into thermal energy, making them promising candidates for PTT applications.

GSH, a prominent antioxidant, plays a crucial role in maintaining redox balance. Excessive endogenous GSH can scavenge ROS, thereby alleviate oxidative stress and limit the efficacy of ROS-mediated cancer therapies [37]. The GPx-like activity to deplete GSH was evaluated using 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), which detected thiol groups in GSH and produced a yellow-colored compound with an absorbance peak at 412 nm (Fig. 3E). DMTICH nanozymes demonstrated outstanding GSH depletion efficiency, achieving near-complete eradication of GSH from the solution within 2 h (Fig. 3F), with a concentration-dependent effect (Fig. S13). As anticipated, the GSH consumption process was further accelerated at 43 °C (Fig. 3G), resulting in a 1.82-fold increase in the depletion rate compared to that at 25 °C, confirming the thermally enhanced GPx-like activity of DMTICH nanozymes. The catalytic mechanism of these processes was schematically illustrated in Fig. 3H. Under acidic TME conditions, the pH-responsive DMTICH nanozymes, composed of mixed-valence metal ions, underwent biodegradation to release iron (Fe2+/Fe3+) and copper (Cu+/Cu2+) ions. Fe2+ and Cu + participated in generating POD-like ·OH activity, while Cu2+ and Fe3+ were responsible for GPx-like activity to deplete endogenous GSH. Furthermore, both POD-like and GPx-like activities were enhanced under hyperthermic conditions. Importantly, due to the redox cycle of Cu2+ to Cu+ and Fe2+ to Fe3+, DMTICH nanozymes enabled the regeneration of catalytic substrates, promoting sustained catalytic activity and amplifying oxidative stress for effective tumor therapy.

Copper also catalyzes the disproportionation of H2O2 into water and O2, exhibiting CAT-like activity [34]. To validate this, H2O2 was repeatedly added while the increasing concentration of dissolved O2 was continuously monitored (Fig. 3I). ICG has been reported to induce the transformation of O2 in the TME into 1O2 under light stimulation, which can locally and instantly damage cancer cells [47]. The singlet oxygen quantum yield (1O2 QY) of ICG/Cu (II) was assessed to evaluate the boosting effect on 1O2 generation efficiency of chelation between ICG and copper. Under 808 nm laser irradiation, 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) was employed as a chemical probe to detect 1O2 production and to examine the photosensitizing performance. Absorbance spectra of DPBF were recorded at various time intervals (0, 2, 4, 8, 10, and 15 min) under irradiation in the presence of ultrapure water, pure ICG, ICG/Cu (II), and DMTICH (CuCl2·2H2O: 6 μg/mL; ICG: 10 μg/mL) (Fig. 3L–O). Among the tested groups, the ICG/Cu (II) dispersion induced the most pronounced decrease in DPBF absorbance, indicating the chelation of ICG with copper could significantly enhance 1O2 QY. Normalized absorbance values at 420 nm were plotted over time (Fig. 3P), showing that after 15 min of irradiation, DPBF absorbance in the presence of free ICG remained at 68 % of the initial value, whereas it declined to 21 % and 26 % in the ICG/Cu (II) and DMTICH group, respectively, demonstrating superior 1O2 generation capacity. Taking free ICG (1O2 QY = 0.2 %) as a reference, the 1O2 QY values were calculated to be 2.19 % for ICG/Cu (II) and 1.09 % for DMTICH (Fig. 3Q–S). Notably, ICG/Cu (II) exhibited a nearly 11-fold increase in 1O2 QY relative to free ICG, while DMTICH presented 5-fold increase in 1O2 QY, also demonstrating 1.85-fold higher 1O2 QY compared to recently reported ICG/Fe (III) systems [42]. These results highlighted the chelation of ICG with copper could significantly boost 1O2 QY, even though the π-π stacking interaction of ICG/Cu (II) on the MOF surface decreased photosensitizing performance. As the reaction proceeded, the nanozyme demonstrated significant CAT-like activity, facilitating the sustained production of 1O2.

The generation of ·OH and 1O2 were further investigated by electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy, employing 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) and 2,2,6,6-Tetramethyl-1-piperinedinyloxy (TEMPO) as the spin-trapping agent for ·OH and 1O2, respectively (Fig. 3K). In a pH 5.5 solution containing 5 mM GSH, DMTICH could generate hydroxyl radicals regardless of light irradiation. However, under the same conditions in the absence of light, no 1O2 was produced, while 1O2 signals could be detected under light exposure. This observation was consistent with the fact that ICG required light activation for its photosensitizing effect.

In vitro cellular uptake and anticancer activity

The modification of DMTICH nanozymes with HA was anticipated to enhance their active targeting capability [48]. To validate this property, the uptake of various formulations by 4T1 cancer cells was assessed using fluorescence imaging. After incubation for equal durations (4 or 8 h), the uptake of HA-unmodified DMTIC NPs by 4T1 cells was significantly lower compared to HA-modified DMTIC NPs. The fluorescence signal of Rhodamine B (RB) in DMTICH-treated cells was stronger than that observed in the DMTIC group (Fig. S15, and 4A, B). This bolstered internalization was likely due to the specific interaction between HA and CD44 receptors that were overexpressed on the surface of cancer cells. This finding prompted our decision to modify all nanoparticles with HA for subsequent experiments, ensuring active targeting ability across all formulations and enabling a more consistent comparison of their therapeutic efficacy.

Mitochondria play a crucial role in various cellular functions, like metabolism, energy production, and signal transduction. The overload and accumulation of Fe3+/Cu2+-induced ROS can lead to mitochondrial membrane depolarization [49]. Biological transmission electron microscopy (bio-TEM) was employed to visualize specific morphological changes under different treatments. It could be clearly seen that in untreated cancer cell; the mitochondria are elongated (Fig. 4E). In contrast, damage to the outer mitochondrial membrane, reduction in size, and loss or disappearance of cristae were noted in 4T1 cells treated with DMTICH nanozymes, with some cells exhibiting mitochondrial swelling and cavitation (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, JC-1 was used to assess the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) of 4T1 cells after various treatments (Fig. 4G). JC-1 emits red fluorescence in healthy mitochondria with high MMP but shifts to green fluorescence in dysfunctional mitochondria with low MMP. The strongest green fluorescence was observed in 4T1 cells treated with DMTICH + L, attributed to mitochondrial damage caused by Fe3+/Cu2+-catalyzed Fenton reactions and ICG-induced hyperthermia, which enhanced the effects of PTT and CDT.

To further explore the biosafety of DMTICH nanoparticles, H&E staining of major organs, a blood routine test, and biochemical analysis were performed (Fig. S29–34). No significant organ damage or severe inflammatory responses were observed across all treatment groups, indicating favorable biocompatibility of the therapeutic agents. Key hematological and biochemical parameters, including albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were measured in each group. These results collectively supported the excellent biosafety of DMTICH nanoparticles in vivo.

Conclusion

Considering the inherent toxicity associated with conventional chemotherapy and the poor stability of ICG, we have developed a multifunctional DSF-loaded, ICG-chelated, NIR-responsive nanozyme platform (DMTICH NPs). This rationally designed 4-in-1 nanoplatform was capable of simultaneously performing PTT, CDT, PDT and CT and demonstrated significantly enhanced antitumor efficacy through the synergistic integration of these multiple therapeutic modalities. Notably, the DMTICH NPs exhibited triple enzyme-mimicking activities: CAT-like, POD-like, and GPx-like with enhanced 1O2 quantum yield. The enzymatic activities were markedly accelerated upon NIR irradiation, amplifying therapeutic effects within the TME. In vivo experiments revealed that DMTICH NPs selectively accumulated in tumor tissues after intravenous administration. Upon NIR exposure, local tumor temperatures rapidly increased to average 58.3 °C, confirming the strong photothermal ablation capacity of the platform, which resulted in robust tumor growth inhibition in the 4T1 murine model, while maintaining excellent biocompatibility and minimal systemic toxicity. In short, this work presented a highly promising strategy for the design of multifunctional nanozyme platforms that enable the synergistic application of CT, CDT, PTT, and PDT, representing a significant advancement in the development of multifunctional therapeutic agents, providing a versatile and potent tool for combating cancer.
