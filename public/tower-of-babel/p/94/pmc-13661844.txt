SOX10-mediated regulation of the MiR-106a/363 cluster drives RAF inhibitor resistance in BRAF-mutant melanoma
Authors: Ying Li, Yu-le Yong, Zhe Zhi, ZhenZhen Lu, Zongguan Huang, Lanlan Cui, Mo Zhang, Zihao Wu, Yiming Hu, Guanfei Zhang, Xiaofei Zhao, Shujun Han, Yan Zheng, Yongping Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdaptive resistance to MAPK inhibitors remains a major clinical obstacle in BRAF-mutant melanoma, driven by reversible transcriptional and epigenetic reprogramming. Previous studies have implicated loss of SRY-box transcription factor 10 (SOX10) and concurrent activation of TGF-β signaling in this process, but the molecular link between them has remained unresolved. Here, we identify a SOX10-miR-106a/363-TGFBR2 regulatory axis that governs adaptive resistance. SOX10 directly activates transcription of the miR-106a/363 cluster through a distal cis-regulatory element. In turn, the miR-106a/363 cluster members miR-106a-5p and miR-20b-5p suppress TGFBR2 post-transcriptionally, while miR-363-3p indirectly reduces TGFBR2 expression by targeting SOX4, a transcriptional activator of TGFBR2. This dual-layered repression restrains TGF-β signaling and maintains drug sensitivity. Loss of SOX10 diminishes miR-106a/363 expression, derepresses TGFBR2, and promotes a mesenchymal-like, drug-tolerant state. Restoring miR-106a/363 expression partially re-sensitizes melanomas to RAF inhibitors in vivo. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized regulatory circuit linking SOX10 to TGF-β signaling and highlight the miR-106a/363 cluster as a potential therapeutic target to overcome adaptive resistance in melanoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06354-3.Keywords: Drug tolerance, Phenotype switching, MAPK inhibitor resistance, TGF-β signaling, Non-coding RNA

Introduction

Melanoma represents the most aggressive and life-threatening form of skin cancer, with a steadily increasing global incidence in recent decades [1]. Approximately 40–60% of cutaneous melanomas harbor BRAF V600 mutations, which constitutively activate the MAPK pathway [2]. While targeted therapies such as BRAF inhibitors (BRAFi) and MEK inhibitors (MEKi) initially elicit therapeutic responses, tumors frequently develop resistance, leading to disease progression [3]. Melanoma drug resistance is broadly classified into genetic and non-genetic mechanisms [4]. While genetic mechanisms are well characterized, adaptive non-genetic resistance, driven by reversible transcriptional and epigenetic reprogramming, has emerged as a major clinical challenge [5].

Emerging evidence suggests that the transcription factor SOX10, essential for melanocyte development and melanoma maintenance, has been implicated as a key regulator of such adaptive non-genetic resistance [6, 7]. SOX10-deficient clones have been identified in minimal residual disease following BRAFi/MEKi treatment in both BRAF-mutant patient-derived xenograft models and clinical samples, and these SOX10-deficient cells may preexist in treatment-naïve tumors at low frequencies and expand under therapeutic pressure [8]. Importantly, SOX10 loss is associated with increased TGFBR2 expression and subsequently upregulates the target genes of TGF-β, which promotes a mesenchymal-like, drug-tolerant phenotype [6]. Elevated TGF-β signaling is one of the well-established drivers of drug resistance in multiple cancers, including melanoma, where it induces a mesenchymal-like switch characterized by the upregulation of extracellular matrix components and drug-tolerant gene expression programs [6, 9–11]. However, the mechanistic basis of the inverse relationship between SOX10 expression and TGF-β signaling remains unresolved.

Studies have highlighted microRNAs (miRNAs) as critical post-transcriptional regulators of therapy resistance [12]. miRNA clusters are composed of neighboring miRNA genes transcribed as a single primary miRNA (pri-miRNA) transcript and subsequently processed into multiple mature miRNAs [13]. Since it contains multiple miRNA encoding genes, miRNA clusters exhibit more complex and integrated regulatory functions in cancer. The miR-106a/363 cluster, which comprises six miRNAs (miR-106a, -18b, -19b-2, -20b, -92a-2, and − 363), has been linked to tumor progression in several cancers [14–16]. However, its role in melanoma and therapy resistance remains largely unexplored. Although its paralogous clusters, miR-17/92 and miR-106b/25, have been implicated in drug resistance [17, 18], the contribution of the miR-106a/363 cluster to melanoma adaptive responses remains undefined, highlighting an important gap in knowledge.

Here, we reveal a SOX10-miR-106a/363-TGFBR2 circuit that modulates TGF-β signaling and drives adaptive resistance in BRAF-mutant melanoma. We show that SOX10 activates the miR-106a/363 cluster via a distal cis-regulatory element, leading to both transcriptional and post-transcriptional suppression of TGFBR2. Loss of SOX10 therefore downregulates the cluster, relieves TGFBR2 repression, and activates TGF-β signaling, promoting a drug-tolerant state. This study resolves a critical gap in the understanding of SOX10-TGF-β crosstalk and identifies the miR-106a/363 cluster as a potential therapeutic entry point for overcoming adaptive resistance in BRAF-mutant melanoma.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Reagents used in this study: Vemurafenib was from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA). Doxycycline was from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Antibodies used in this study: anti-Fibronectin (FN), anti-Smad2, anti-Smad3, anti-phospho-Smad3(Ser423/425), anti-phospho-Smad2 (Ser465/Ser467), anti-phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Thr202/Tyr204), and anti-phospho-MEK1/2 (Ser217/221) antibodies were from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Anti-GAPDH, anti-SOX4, and anti-TGFBR2 antibodies were from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA). Anti-SOX10 was from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). Anti-Actin antibody was from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Anti-Smad7 and anti-DUSP6 were from abcam (Cambridge, England, UK).

Cell culture, conditions and treatments

The 1205Lu cell line was kindly provided by Dr. Meenhard Herlyn (The Wistar Institute). The M238 cell line was generously donated by Dr. Antoni Ribas (University of California, Los Angeles). The A375, RPMI-7951 and HEK293T cell lines were commercially obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). To generate BRAFi-resistant cells, cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of vemurafenib (up to 2 µM), and resistance was confirmed by measuring cell viability under treatment. All drug-resistant cell lines were permanently cultured under 2 µM of vemurafenib. 1205Lu and M238 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Macgene, Beijing, China) with 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin/streptomycin. A375, RPMI-7951, and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Macgene, Beijing, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% streptomycin/penicillin. All cells were cultured in 95% air and 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere at 37 °C.

miRNA microarray analysis

Total RNA containing small RNA was extracted from A375 and M238 melanoma cells stably expressing shRNA targeting SOX10 (SOX10 shRNA) or non‑targeting control shRNA using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and was further purified with the mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit (Ambion) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and concentration were determined by OD260/280 measurement using a NanoDrop ND‑1000 spectrophotometer. RNA integrity was assessed by capillary electrophoresis with the RNA 6000 Nano Lab‑on‑a‑Chip kit and the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer; only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) > 6 were subjected to further analysis.

miRNA profiling was performed using the Agilent human miRNA microarray (8 × 60 K format), which contains probes interrogating 2,549 human mature miRNAs (miRBase R21.0), with each miRNA represented by 30 replicate probes, along with 2,164 Agilent control probes. Labeling and hybridization were carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 200 ng of total RNA was dephosphorylated and ligated with pCp‑Cy3, and the labeled RNA was purified and hybridized to the miRNA arrays. Images were acquired with an Agilent microarray scanner, gridded, and analyzed using Agilent Feature Extraction software (version 10.10).

Data summarization, normalization and quality control were performed with GeneSpring GX software v13 (Agilent). Signal intensities were normalized using the default 90th percentile method. Differentially expressed miRNAs were identified using a fold‑change threshold of ≥ 2 or ≤ − 2 and a Benjamini‑Hochberg corrected P value < 0.05. The filtered data were Log2 transformed and median‑centered by genes using the Adjust Data function of CLUSTER 3.0 software, and then subjected to hierarchical clustering with average linkage. Cluster results were visualized with Java TreeView.

Western blots

The protein lysates from melanoma cells were separated on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Following 1 h blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Biodee, Beijing, China) at room temperature, the membranes were probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were visualized using Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA interference

For the RNA interference experiment, melanoma cells were transfected with RNA oligonucleotides using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as directed by the manufacturer. Non-targeting siRNA control (5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3), siRNAs for SOX10 (#1 5’-CCGUAUGCAGCACAAGAAA-3; #2 5’-GUAUGCAGCACAAGAAAGA-3’), siRNAs for SOX4 (#1 5’-GGACAGACGAAGAGUUUAATT-3’; #2 5’-AAGACGACCTGCTCGACCTGA-3’) were purchased from Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Total RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Gene expression analysis was conducted by quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Total RNA was isolated with AG RNAex Pro Reagent (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA (1 µg) was reverse transcribed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Mountain View, CA, USA). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Reverse transcription of miRNAs was carried out using miRcute Plus miRNA First-Strand cDNA Kit and quantitative PCR using miRcute Plus miRNA qPCR Kit (both from Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The following primers were used: GAPDH (forward, 5’-GTCAAGGCTGAGAACGGGAA − 3’; reverse, 5’-AAATGAGCCCCAGCCTTCTC-3’), SOX10 (forward, 5′- GACCAGTACCCGCACCTG-3′; reverse, 5′-CGCTTGTCACTTTCGTTCAG-3′), pri-miR-106a/363 (forward, 5′-CGGCCTGTGGAAGAAAGGAG-3′; reverse, 5′-CATCGTGATCCACCCGACAA-3′). The qPCR primers of miR-106a-5p, miR-20b-5p, and miR-363-3p purchased from Tiangen Biotech (Beijing, China).

Dual-luciferase assay

HEK293T cells (3 × 10⁵ cells/well) were co‑transfected with the pmirGLO‑TGFBR2 wild‑type or mutant TGFBR2 3′ UTR reporter constructs and the miR‑106a/363 cluster overexpression vector or empty pcDNA3.1 control using jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus, Illkirch, France). The wild‑type construct contained the TGFBR2 3′ UTR sequence 5′‑AATAACATTTCACTTTATTAATGCCTG‑3′, which harbors the common target site of miR‑106a‑5p and miR‑20b‑5p; the mutant construct carried the nucleotide substitutions 5′‑AATAACATTTCACGGTATTAATGCCTG‑3′ (mutated bases underlined) to disrupt this site. After 48 h of incubation, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual‑Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Luminescence signals (firefly and Renilla luciferase) were quantified with a GloMax 20/20 Luminometer (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase for data analysis.

Annexin V/PI apoptosis assay

Following treatment, cells were harvested, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in Annexin V Binding Buffer. Apoptosis was assessed using the APC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with PI (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were stained with APC-conjugated Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. The stained cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Data were analyzed using CytExpert software.

Colony formation assay

Melanoma cells were seeded at low density (1000 cells/well in 6-well plates) and exposed to varying concentrations of vemurafenib (0.5, 1, and 2 µM). Following drug treatment, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (10 min), stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 1 h at room temperature, washed with distilled water, and air-dried. Visible colonies were manually counted. The average optical density was analyzed by ImageJ.

CRISPR interference

The sgRNAs targeted to transcription start site (TSS) of miR-106a/363 cluster were designed according to Drobna-Śledzińska, M et al. [19]. The sgRNA coding sequences (sgRNA#1: sense 5’- TAAGAACAGTACCTTTGAGC-3’, antisense 5’-GCTCAAAGGTACTGTTCTTA-3’; sgRNA#2: sense 5’- ATGCAACTTTCGACCTTTAA-3’, antisense 5’-TTAAAGGTCGAAAGTTGCAT-3’; non-targeting control: sense 5’-GACGGAGGCTAAGCGTCGCAA-3’, antisense 5’-TTGCGACGCTTAGCCTCCGTC-3’) were purchased in the form of DNA oligonucleotides from Tsingke (Beijing, China) and cloned into Lenti-CRISPR V2-dCas9-KRAB vector. For lentiviral particle assembly, psPAX2 and pMD2.G packing vectors were used.

Distal cis-regulatory element knockout using CRISPR/Cas9

A dual sgRNA-directed CRISPR/Cas9 system was employed to achieve distal cis-regulatory element knockout via lentiviral transduction. Two sgRNAs (sgRNA#1 5′-GCCTTTCGGAATGAGCAATG-3′, sgRNA#2 5′-TGTTTAGGAATGTCATGAAT-3′) targeting the flanking regions of the distal cis-regulatory element were cloned into the lentiCRISPR v2 vector, which co-expresses SpCas9. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293FT cells using standard packaging plasmids (psPAX2 and pMD2.G) and transfected via jetPRIME (Polyplus, Illkirch, France). Viral supernatants were harvested at 48 h, filtered through a 0.45 μm filter, and used to transfect A375 and 1205Lu cells. After 48 h, the medium was replaced, and cells were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL) for a week. Single-cell clones were isolated by limiting dilution into 96-well plates. Genomic DNA from expanded clones was extracted and screened for successful deletion by PCR across the targeted region. Positive clones were further validated by Sanger sequencing.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

A375 HA-SOX10 cells and RPMI-7951 cells were cultured in 15 cm dishes until reaching ~ 90% confluence before being lysed for ChIP analysis. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using the SimpleChIP Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads) (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and quenched with 125 mM glycine. Nuclei were isolated, and chromatin was digested with micrococcal nuclease, followed by sonication to shear the chromatin to an average size of 150–900 bp. Immunoprecipitation using magnetic beads conjugated to the indicated antibodies, including anti-HA (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). DNA was purified and analyzed by quantitative PCR using primers as follows: NC_forward, 5′-GCTTCTTCCAGAATCAAGACTTTAGAG − 3′; NC_reverse, 5′-AGAGTGTTCTTAAACCTTAGCATC-3′; DCRE_forward, 5′-GAAACAGTGTCCCCATTGTTGTT-3′; DCRE_reverse, 5′-TTTCCCTGTAAAAGGCTGGGGT-3′. Normal rabbit IgG was used as a negative control, and input DNA was used for normalization.

Micro-Capture-C

Micro-Capture-C assay was performed by Ningan Life Sciences Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) as previously described [20, 21] with slight modification. Briefly, approximately 1 × 10⁷ A375 cells were cultured and then resuspended to a concentration of ~ 1 × 10⁶ cells/ml. The cells are then fixed with formaldehyde at a final concentration of 2% for 10 min at room temperature. The fixation reaction is quenched by adding glycine, followed by centrifugation to remove the supernatant, and then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells are resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 1–2 × 107 cells/ml and permeabilized with 0.005% digitonin. Chromatin was digested with Micrococcal nuclease (MNase), and careful titration of MNase concentration is required to achieve high-quality library preparation. After digestion, DNA fragments were treated with Polynucleotide Kinase, Klenow, and T4 ligase to construct spatially proximal linking. The DNA was purified and sonicated to generate DNA fragments ranging from 200 to 500 bp. Target enrichment was achieved using biotinylated oligonucleotide probes, followed by adaptor ligation and PCR amplification before Illumina platform sequencing.

The raw sequencing data were processed through a standard pipeline. Briefly, adaptor trimming was performed by Trim_galore, and then reads were split to isolate viewpoint-associated sequences. Bowtie2 [22] was used to align to the hg38 reference genome, and then the alignment file was analyzed to form a bigwig file for visualization using the UCSC Genome Browser.

Wound healing assay

Melanoma cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until reaching ~ 90% confluence. A sterile pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch in the cell monolayer. Detached cells were removed by washing twice with PBS. The culture medium was replaced with a low-serum medium containing 1% fetal bovine serum to suppress cell proliferation. Images of the wound were captured at 0 h and at 24 h using an inverted microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan). The wound area was measured using ImageJ software, and the percentage of wound closure was calculated.

Soft agar colony formation assay

A base layer of 2% agar in complete growth medium was prepared in 6-well plates and allowed to solidify at room temperature. A second layer of 0.3% agar in the same medium was overlaid. After this layer solidified, cells (2,000 per well) suspended in 0.3% agar were added as the third layer. A final overlay of 0.3% agar was added on top, followed by 500 µL of complete growth medium. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, and the top medium layer was refreshed every 3–4 days. Colony formation was imaged under an inverted microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan) and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Transwell migration assay

Cell migration was evaluated using Transwell chambers with 8-µm pore polycarbonate membranes (Corning, Lowell, MA, USA). Cells were resuspended in a low-serum medium containing 1% fetal bovine serum, and 2 × 10⁴ cells in 200 µL were added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 500 µL of complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum. After incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min, and rinsed with PBS. Stained cells were imaged under a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan). Migrated cells were counted by Image J software.

Animal studies

Five-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (Charles River Laboratories, Beijing, China) were randomly divided into four treatment groups. A375 cells carrying an empty vector or a miR-106a/363 overexpression vector (4 × 10⁶ cells per mouse) were intradermally injected into the flanks of mice. Tumors were allowed to establish for 7–10 days until they reached a palpable size (approximately 40–100 mm³). Mice were then treated intraperitoneally with either Vemurafenib (30 mg/kg) or DMSO every other day. Tumor sizes were measured every 2 days, and volumes were calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width²) × 0.52. Mice were euthanized on day 14 (DMSO-treated groups) or day 16 (Vemurafenib-treated groups). Tumors were harvested for downstream analysis, including qPCR quantification of pri-miR-106a/363 and mature miRNAs, and western blotting to assess TGF‑β and MAPK signaling pathway proteins. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Investigators were not blinded to group allocation during the experiment.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, while one-way ANOVA was used for multiple-group comparisons. Significance levels are denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001; N.S. indicates non-significant differences.

Results

SOX10 regulates the expression of miR-106a/363 cluster

Previous studies have demonstrated that in a subset of melanoma patients, SOX10 downregulation is closely associated with TGF-β signaling activation, which drives adaptive drug resistance [6]. However, the precise regulatory mechanism linking SOX10 and TGF-β remains unclear. Since SOX10 functions as a general transcriptional activator [23], we hypothesized that a negative regulatory mediator might bridge SOX10 suppression and TGF-β activation. Considering the repressive role of miRNAs [24], we performed microarray screening to identify SOX10-regulated miRNAs in BRAF-mutant melanoma cells (A375 and M238) (Fig. S1A, B). It was found that miR-20b-5p and miR-363-3p, both derived from the miR-106a/363 cluster, were significantly downregulated upon SOX10 knockdown. Notably, miR-106a-5p and miR-20b-5p in the miR-106a/363 cluster harbor identical seed sequences and are computationally predicted to target the 3’UTR of TGFBR2, a key component of the TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig. S1C). We speculated that the miR-106a/363 cluster may mediate TGF-β pathway activation following SOX10 downregulation. Thus, we initially examined whether SOX10 downregulation would reduce the expression of the miR-106a/363 cluster. We found that the depletion of SOX10 using siRNA decreased the expression of the primary miR-106a/363 transcript (pri-miR-106a/363) and significantly decreased the expression of its members (miR-106a-5p, miR-20b-5p, and miR-363-3p). Similar results were obtained in three BRAF-mutant melanoma cell lines (A375, 1205Lu, and M238) (Fig. 1A-C). Moreover, in BRAFi-resistant melanoma cell lines (A375Res and 1205LuRes) established by chronic exposure to escalating dosage of vemurafenib, we observed that SOX10 expression was decreased, accompanied by reduced expression of pri-miR-106a/363 and its member miRNAs (Fig. 1D-F). Conversely, overexpression of SOX10 in A375 and 1205Lu cells upregulated both pri-miR-106a/363 and three constituent miRNAs (Fig. 1G, H). These results suggested that SOX10 positively regulated the expression of the miR-106a/363 cluster.

The miR-106a/363-TGFBR2 axis mediates BRAFi resistance in BRAF-mutant melanoma

Given that SOX10 knockdown activates the TGF-β signaling via TGFBR2 upregulation to drive drug resistance in melanoma [6], we examined whether the miR-106a/363 cluster directly targets TGFBR2. Using dual-luciferase reporter assays with constructs containing either wild-type or mutated TGFBR2 3’UTR sequences, we demonstrated that miR-106a/363 cluster overexpression significantly reduced luciferase activity for the wild-type 3’UTR but not the mutant version (Fig. 2A). The mutation disrupted the common target site of miR-106a-5p and miR-20b-5p in the TGFBR2 3′UTR. These results indicated that miR-106a/363 cluster can negatively regulate TGFBR2 expression by targeting its 3’UTR sequence.

To assess the regulatory effect of miR-106a/363 cluster on TGF-β signaling, we overexpressed the cluster in both parental (A375 and 1205Lu) and their corresponding BRAFi-resistant cell lines (A375Res and 1205LuRes). A375Res and 1205LuRes cells exhibited significantly reduced SOX10 levels (Fig. 1D) but higher expression of fibronectin (FN), phospho-Smad2 (p-Smad2), phospho-Smad3 (p-Smad3), the TGF-β negative regulator Smad7, and TGFBR2 in comparison to their parental counterparts, corroborating that SOX10 depletion is correlated with the activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig. 2B, C). However, ectopic expression of miR-106a/363 using a doxycycline (Dox) inducible system reduced the expression of these TGF-β pathway components in both parental and resistant cells (Fig. 2B, C, Fig. S2A, B). Furthermore, CRISPR interference (CRISPRi)-mediated knockdown of miR-106a/363 cluster stimulated the TGF-β signaling pathway in both A375 and RPMI-7951 cells (Fig. 2D, E). These results demonstrated that miR-106a/363 negatively regulated the TGF-β pathway.

To determine whether the miR-106a/363 cluster regulates BRAFi resistance-associated phenotypes, we modulated miR-106a/363 cluster expression and measured vemurafenib sensitivity through apoptosis assays and colony formation assays. Overexpression of the miR-106a/363 cluster significantly enhanced vemurafenib-induced apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2F, G, Fig. S2C-G). Furthermore, the colony formation assay confirmed that overexpression of the miR-106a/363 cluster increased the sensitivity of melanoma cells to vemurafenib treatment (Fig. 2H, I, Fig. S2H, I). We further tested the translational relevance of the miR‑106a/363 cluster using the clinically used BRAF inhibitor dabrafenib and the BRAFi/MEKi combination dabrafenib plus trametinib. Based on the prior study, the IC50 values of dabrafenib and trametinib in A375 melanoma cells were determined to be 0.7 µM and 5.0 µM at 72 h, respectively, and their combination exhibited strong synergistic cytotoxicity [25]. Therefore, we treated A375 cells with either 0.7 µM dabrafenib alone or 0.7 µM dabrafenib plus 5.0 µM trametinib for 72 h. Consistent with our vemurafenib results, overexpression of miR‑106a/363 significantly increased apoptosis and reduced colony formation in melanoma cells treated with either dabrafenib alone or dabrafenib plus trametinib (Fig. S2J-M). In contrast, knockdown of the miR-106a/363 cluster using CRISPRi markedly reduced the sensitivity of melanoma cells to vemurafenib, as evidenced by fewer apoptotic cells and an increased number of clones at various concentrations of vemurafenib (Fig. 2J, K, Fig. S3A, B). Similarly, CRISPRi-mediated knockdown of the miR‑106a/363 cluster in A375 cells reduced sensitivity to dabrafenib alone and to dabrafenib plus trametinib, as evidenced by fewer apoptotic cells and increased colony formation (Fig. S3C, D). We next examined whether the miR‑106a/363 cluster is functionally involved in acquired BRAF inhibitor resistance. Consistent with a role in drug tolerance, the expression of miR‑106a/363 cluster was markedly reduced in vemurafenib‑resistant melanoma cells compared with their parental counterparts (Fig. 1E). To determine whether restoring this cluster could overcome resistance, we overexpressed the miR‑106a/363 cluster in two resistant lines (Fig. S3E, F). Ectopic expression of the cluster significantly increased apoptosis upon vemurafenib treatment (Fig. 2L, M, Fig. S4A, B) and profoundly suppressed colony formation in the presence of 2 µM vemurafenib (Fig. 2N, O, Fig. S4C, D). These findings indicate that re‑expression of miR‑106a/363 re‑sensitizes resistant melanoma cells to BRAF inhibition. To test whether TGF-β signaling is the major downstream effector of miR‑106a/363‑mediated drug sensitivity, we stably re‑expressed TGFBR2 in miR‑106a/363‑overexpressing melanoma cells. In the presence of vemurafenib, TGFBR2 reconstitution significantly restored colony formation compared with cells overexpressing miR‑106a/363 alone (Fig. 2P, Q, Fig. S4E, F) and reactivated TGF‑β pathway without affecting p‑ERK1/2 and p-MEK1/2 levels (Fig. S4G, H), indicating that the miR‑106a/363 cluster re‑sensitizes melanoma cells to BRAF inhibition primarily by suppressing TGF‑β signaling rather than reactivating the MAPK pathway. These findings suggest that the miR-106a/363 cluster inhibits TGF-β signaling and increases the sensitivity of BRAF-mutant melanoma to BRAFi.

SOX10 regulates miR-106a/363 cluster expression via a distal cis-regulatory element

To understand how SOX10 regulates the expression of the miR-106a/363 cluster, we explored the DNA sequence upstream of the miR-106a/363 cluster transcription start site (TSS) for potential SOX10 binding sites. Analysis of DNase-seq data from RPMI-7951 and SK-MEL5 melanoma cells [26] revealed a prominent DNase I hypersensitive site (DHS) located approximately 6 kb upstream of TSS of MIR106AHG, the host gene for the miR-106a/363 cluster (Fig. 3A). Further analysis of this distal DHS identified a pair of DNA sites matching the SOX10 binding consensus motif (Fig. 3A). We hypothesized that these SOX10-binding sequences may act as a distal cis-regulatory element (DCRE) to regulate the miR-106a/363 cluster expression. ChIP-qPCR assays confirmed SOX10 occupancy at DCRE in both A375 and RPMI-7951 cells (Fig. 3B). To further explore whether this element regulates the miR-106a/363 cluster, we employed the dual sgRNA/Cas9 strategy to generate DCRE-knockout monoclonal A375 and 1205Lu cell lines (Fig. 3C, D). Expression analysis revealed that DCRE knockout significantly reduced the level of pri-miR-106a/363, as well as the levels of several mature miRNAs within the cluster (Fig. 3E, F). In addition, Micro-Capture-C assays demonstrated that DCRE physically interacted with the promoter region of the MIR106AHG gene over long genomic distances (Fig. 3G).

DCRE contributes to miR-106a/363 cluster-regulated BRAF inhibitor sensitivity in melanoma

Upon establishing that SOX10 regulated the expression of the miR-106a/363 cluster through its binding to DCRE, we subsequently aimed to determine whether DCRE functionally contributed to miR-106a/363 cluster-regulated BRAFi resistance in BRAF-mutant melanoma. We assessed TGF‑β and MAPK signaling in vector, DCRE knockout (DCRE KO), and DCRE knockout with miR‑106a/363 overexpression A375 and 1205Lu cells treated with 0–2 µM vemurafenib. DCRE knockout upregulated TGFBR2, p‑Smad2, p-Smad3, Smad7, and fibronectin (FN) regardless of vemurafenib dose, and these changes were reversed by miR‑106a/363 re‑expression. In contrast, p‑MEK1/2, p‑ERK1/2, and DUSP6 were suppressed similarly by vemurafenib in all three groups, with no intergroup differences (Fig. 4A, B, Fig. S5A, B). Thus, miR‑106a/363 modulates vemurafenib sensitivity through TGF‑β signaling without affecting MAPK pathway inhibition. Apoptosis assays further demonstrated that deletion of the DCRE markedly suppressed vemurafenib-induced apoptosis in A375 and 1205Lu melanoma cells (Fig. 4C-F). However, overexpressing the miR-106a/363 cluster in DCRE-deficient cells partially restored apoptotic sensitivity (Fig. 4G-J). Additionally, we employed a colony formation assay to further validate that the miR-106a/363 cluster can partially rescue the reduced sensitivity to vemurafenib caused by DCRE deletion in melanoma cells, as evidenced in Fig. 4K, L, and Fig. S5C-F.

Moreover, activation of TGF-β signaling has been associated with enhanced migration of melanoma cells. Consistent with activated TGF-β signaling, DCRE deletion promoted melanoma cell migration both in the presence and absence of vemurafenib treatment, while the reintroduction of the miR-106a/363 cluster attenuated this enhanced migratory ability (Fig. 4M, N, Fig. S5G-L). To determine whether the DCRE also modulates sensitivity to clinically relevant BRAFi and BRAFi/MEKi regimens, we treated vector, DCRE knockout, and DCRE knockout with miR‑106a/363 overexpression A375 and 1205Lu cells with dabrafenib or dabrafenib plus trametinib. DCRE knockout reduced apoptosis and increased colony formation under both dabrafenib monotherapy and combined treatment. Conversely, re‑expression of miR‑106a/363 in DCRE knockout cells restored apoptosis and suppressed colony formation. (Fig. S6A-E) These data confirmed that the DCRE modulated the expression of the miR-106a/363 cluster, thereby altering TGF-β signaling and ultimately influencing phenotypes associated with melanoma BRAFi resistance.

miR-363-3p targeted SOX4 to regulate TGF-β signaling

SOX10 knockdown led to a significant reduction in the expression of members of the miR-106a/363 cluster, including miR-106a-5p, miR-20b-5p, and miR-363-3p (Fig. 1A-C). Unlike miR-106a-5p and miR-20b-5p, miR-363-3p lacks a predicted binding site in the 3′UTR of TGFBR2, and therefore its functional relevance in melanoma BRAFi resistance remains uninvestigated. It had been reported that miR-363-3p could negatively regulate the expression of SOX4 in colorectal cancer [27]. Furthermore, sequence alignment analysis revealed that miR-363-3p potentially targets the 3’UTR of SOX4 (Fig. 5A). Notably, SOX4 also acts as a direct transcriptional activator of TGFBR2 [28]. To confirm the regulatory role of the miR-106a/363 cluster on SOX4 in melanoma cells, we performed western blot analysis and observed significantly elevated SOX4 protein expression in DCRE-deleted melanoma cells compared to control cells (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, silencing SOX4 using siRNA in DCRE knockout cells resulted in a simultaneous decrease in the levels of FN, p-Smad2, p-Smad3, Smad7, and TGFBR2 (Fig. 5C). We further demonstrated that SOX4 knockdown led to reduced TGFBR2 transcription in DCRE-deleted melanoma cells (Fig. 5D). In contrast, SOX4 overexpression increased the levels of FN, p-Smad2, p-Smad3, Smad7, and TGFBR2 in BRAF-mutated melanoma cells (Fig. 5E). These results indicated that SOX4 could enhance the TGF-β signaling by activating TGFBR2 transcription. We also examined SOX4 protein levels in miR‑106a/363‑overexpressing cells that had been engineered to stably re‑express TGFBR2 and were subsequently treated with vemurafenib. Under these conditions, SOX4 showed no significant changes in expression compared to cells overexpressing miR‑106a/363 alone, consistent with TGFBR2 acting downstream of SOX4 (Figs. S4G, H). In addition, compared to DCRE knockout alone, silencing of SOX4 in DCRE-deficient melanoma cells reduced cell migratory capacity (Fig. 5F-I). These results demonstrated that miR-363-3p could also modulate the TGF-β signaling pathway by targeting SOX4, thereby contributing to TGF-β signaling-mediated BRAFi resistance in melanoma.

The miR-106a/363 cluster enhances melanoma sensitivity to BRAF inhibitor in vivo

To validate the role of the miR-106a/363 cluster in regulating BRAFi resistance in vivo, A375-vector or A375-miR-106a/363-overexpressing cells were subcutaneously injected into nude mice. Long-term exposure to vemurafenib significantly reduced SOX10 expression in both A375-vector and A375-miR-106a/363-overexpressing tumors, when compared to the untreated groups (Fig. 6A). Notably, both pri-miR-106a/363 and its three mature miRNA members were also markedly reduced in A375-vector tumors upon vemurafenib treatment (Fig. 6B-E), indicating that both SOX10 and the miR-106a/363 cluster are suppressed by prolonged vemurafenib treatment.

Furthermore, in A375-vector tumors, vemurafenib treatment significantly elevated the expression of TGF-β signaling components, including FN, p-Smad2, p-Smad3, Smad7 and TGFBR2. However, miR‑106a/363 overexpression did not alter MAPK signaling. (Fig. 6A). In contrast, these increases were not observed in A375-miR-106a/363-overexpressing tumors, indicating that miR-106a/363 overexpression suppresses TGF-β pathway activation (Fig. 6A). Tumor growth monitoring showed that intraperitoneal injections of vemurafenib every other day resulted in significantly smaller tumors in mice inoculated with A375-miR-106a/363-overexpressing cells compared to those inoculated with A375-vector cells (Fig. 6F).

Collectively, these in vivo findings demonstrated that overexpression of the miR-106a/363 cluster enhanced melanoma sensitivity to vemurafenib, potentially through inhibition of the TGF-β signaling pathway.

Discussion

SOX10 expression is heterogeneous in melanoma, and its downregulation promotes a mesenchymal-like, therapy-resistant phenotype [6, 8, 29, 30]. In some BRAFi-resistant patients, reduced SOX10 is accompanied by upregulation of TGF-β signaling, suggesting a potential role in driving resistance, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear [6]. Our study identified the miR-106a/363 cluster as a downstream regulator of SOX10 that negatively modulated the TGF-β signaling pathway. We propose that reduced SOX10 expression diminishes its binding to the DCRE of the miR-106a/363 cluster, leading to downregulation of the cluster and its key members, including miR-106a-5p, miR-20b-5p, and miR-363-3p. Downregulation of miR-106a-5p and miR-20b-5p relieved post-transcriptional repression of TGFBR2, while decreased miR-363-3p expression elevated SOX4 levels, resulting in transcriptional activation of TGFBR2. The convergent upregulation of TGFBR2 ultimately enhanced TGF-β signaling, contributing to BRAFi resistance in melanoma (Fig. 6G).

SOX proteins constitute a large family of transcriptional regulators defined by the presence of a high-mobility group (HMG) DNA-binding domain. All SOX proteins recognize similar DNA-binding motifs characterized by a 7-base pair consensus sequence: (A/T)(A/T)CAA(A/T)G [31]. SOX10, SOX8, and SOX9 belong to the SOXE subgroup, sharing 97–99% sequence identity/similarity in their HMG DNA-binding domains [32]. These findings raise an intriguing possibility that other SOX family members may similarly bind to the miR-106a/363 cluster DCRE and modulate TGF-β signaling-mediated drug resistance in cancers where SOX10 is not the primary driver. Since numerous SOX proteins have been implicated in cancer therapy resistance [33–35], it will be of great interest to investigate whether this SOX10-mediated regulatory mechanism represents a conserved paradigm across other SOX family members. SOX10 is a key transcription factor that exerts strong regulatory effects on melanoma progression [36]. Previous studies have shown that the majority of SOX10-binding events occur at distal genomic regions [37]. Consistent with this, our study identified that SOX10 binds to distal enhancer regions of the miR-106a/363 cluster and facilitates the formation of chromatin loops to the promoter of MIR106AHG, which promoted the expression of the miR-106a/363 cluster (Fig. 3). This observation not only supported SOX10’s role as a transcriptional activator but also suggested its involvement in shaping the three-dimensional chromatin architecture required for long-range gene regulation. Given that chromatin looping is a highly regulated process involving multiple essential cofactors [38], it is reasonable to hypothesize that SOX10-mediated regulation of the miR-106a/363 cluster may require the cooperation of additional chromatin architectural components. Whether SOX10 directly recruits these mediators of chromatin loops or instead functions through interactions with pioneer or scaffold transcription factors to establish chromatin loops remains to be determined.

RAFi and MEKi treatments induce miRNA expression reprogramming in melanoma, which contributes to developing drug resistance in BRAF-mutant melanoma [39–41]. For instance, prior studies reported that RAFi treatment induced miR-204-5p and miR-211-5p upregulation to promote resistance in BRAF-mutant melanoma cells [42, 43]. In contrast, increased miR-199-5p and miR-204-5p levels suppressed tumor growth and prevented resistance development in the same context [44]. Our study demonstrated that the role of the miR-106a/363 cluster in conferring BRAFi resistance in BRAF-mutant melanoma was likely driven by three of its key members: miR-106a-5p, miR-20b-5p, and miR-363-3p. These findings highlighted that the functional effects of individual miRNAs were highly context-dependent, varying across tissue types, disease states, and temporal stages. In addition, it revealed a synergistic regulatory mechanism by which multiple miRNAs within the same cluster act in concert to modulate a common signaling axis. This observation proposed that targeting the entire miR-106a/363 cluster rather than focusing on individual miRNAs may be a more effective strategy to overcome compensatory effects among cluster members and achieve a more robust therapeutic outcome.

Of note, we observed a context‑dependent expression pattern of miR‑20b‑5p. In vitro, stable DCRE knockout reduced the levels of miR‑106a‑5p and miR‑363‑3p but did not significantly affect miR‑20b‑5p; in contrast, in vivo treatment with vemurafenib decreased all three cluster members. A possible explanation is that mature miRNAs derived from the same primary transcript may differ in processing efficiency or intrinsic stability, and miR‑20b‑5p might be processed more efficiently or be more stable, thus resisting the transcriptional decline in DCRE‑KO cells. In vivo, vemurafenib may induce additional post‑transcriptional effects, such as inhibition of the miRNA processing machinery or enhanced degradation, which could override these intrinsic differences and lead to a coordinated downregulation. Importantly, miR‑20b‑5p shares an identical seed sequence with miR‑106a‑5p and is functionally redundant; however, our experimental data showed that miR‑106a‑5p is expressed at much higher levels than miR‑20b‑5p, making miR‑106a‑5p the dominant functional regulator. Therefore, the context‑dependent changes in miR‑20b‑5p levels do not alter the mechanistic conclusions of this study.

To conclude, our study elucidates a novel SOX10-mediated adaptive resistance mechanism in BRAF-mutant melanoma, revealing that SOX10 orchestrates a multi-level regulatory network through distal enhancer-controlled miR-106a/363 expression to dynamically modulate TGFBR2 signaling. This finding not only provides crucial insights into the molecular basis of BRAFi resistance but also opens new therapeutic avenues for melanoma treatment by targeting the SOX10-miR-106a/363-TGFBR2 axis, offering promising strategies to overcome clinical drug resistance and improve patient outcomes.
