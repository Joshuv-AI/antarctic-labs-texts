Mechanisms and therapeutic implications of metabolic heterogeneity and histone lactylation in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Jinhu Ma, Ziyang Qiang, Wenhao Xie, Liang Jiao, Yanxiong Wang, Lin Mi, Zhangjie Yang, Guojun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is characterized by metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic dysregulation, primarily attributable to the loss of the Von Hippel-Lindau(VHL) gene and the resulting stabilization of HIF.Its metabolism is predominantly driven by the Warburg effect. However, metabolic patterns vary both across different tumors and within individual tumors.Histone lactylation is a recently discovered epigenetic mark linking glycolysis-derived lactate to gene expression.Lactate accumulation drives histone lactylation, thereby regulating the expression of genes involved in tumor progression and other malignant phenotypes.The interplay between metabolic diversity and histone modifications shapes distinct tumor phenotypes and their microenvironments.Finally, emerging therapies—including HIF-2α inhibitors, metabolic enzyme inhibitors, and epigenetic regulators—are introduced along with their potential use in combination with immunotherapy.

Introduction

ccRCC is the most common kidney cancer subtype, accounting for approximately 75% of cases. Over 80% of ccRCC tumors have an inactivated VHL gene [1]. Loss of VHL function stabilizes HIF-1α and HIF-2α. These transcription factors reprogram cellular metabolism even at normal oxygen levels. This phenomenon is known as “pseudohypoxia” [2]. Thus, metabolic dysregulation plays a central role in ccRCC pathogenesis [3]. Otto Warburg’s century-old observation of aerobic glycolysis in cancer, known as the “Warburg effect,” remains highly relevant to ccRCC [4]. VHL-deficient ccRCC cells show reduced mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. These cells convert glucose to lactate via glycolysis at a markedly increased rate [4]. HIF-2α upregulates the expression of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK). PDK inhibits pyruvate entry into the Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) cycle, thereby redirecting pyruvate toward lactate. Meanwhile, HIF induces expression of GLUT1 and glutamine transporters. This promotes alternative metabolic pathways, such as glutamine-dependent reductive carboxylation, that sustain lipid biosynthesis under hypoxic conditions [5]. ccRCC cells accumulate abundant glycogen and neutral lipids. This accumulation leads to the characteristically “clear” cytoplasm observed histologically [5]. Importantly, metabolic reprogramming in ccRCC is not uniform. Significant metabolic heterogeneity exists among patients and within individual tumors. Multi-omics analyses have revealed several metabolically distinct ccRCC subtypes with prognostic significance [6, 7]. For example, a recently defined “de-clear cell differentiation” (DCCD) subtype is characterized by sparse lipid droplets, reduced mitochondrial content, and low metabolic activity. However, this subtype shows elevated nutrient uptake and high proliferative capacity. This metabolic phenotype correlates with more aggressive behavior and poorer survival [8, 9]. Even early stage I tumors can contain regions that have undergone such a metabolic shift. The presence of these regions indicates a higher risk of recurrence. In contrast, other ccRCC subtypes maintain predominantly oxidative or lipid-rich metabolic profiles. These metabolic profiles are associated with less aggressive phenotypes [3].It is reasonable to hypothesize that some aggressive metabolic subtypes may be more permissive to lactate-driven chromatin remodeling. however, direct measurements of intratumoral lactate and histone lactylation across ccRCC subtypes remain limited. We therefore avoid assigning higher lactate or lactylation levels to DCCD in the absence of direct experimental evidence.Spatial analyses confirm the presence of metabolic zonation within individual tumors. Well-vascularized areas often rely on oxidative metabolism or fatty acid utilization. Conversely, poorly perfused, hypoxic regions rely on glycolysis. They deliver metabolic products to adjacent cells through lactate shuttling [5]. This metabolic partitioning creates microdomains characterized by acidic and nutrient gradients. These microdomains influence local angiogenesis and immune cell function [6, 8]. The impact of metabolic reprogramming in ccRCC is not limited to energy metabolism. Lactate, once considered a waste product of glycolysis, is now recognized as a central mediator of cancer biology and intercellular signaling [9, 10]. High intratumoral lactate concentrations, often in the millimolar range (~ 10–20 mM and sometimes higher in localized regions), contribute to acidification of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Tumor extracellular pH is typically acidic (approximately 6.5–6.9), although some regions may be more acidic [2]. This highly acidic environment inhibits T-cell and NK-cell(Natural Killer) activity. It also polarizes macrophages toward an immunosuppressive phenotype [11]. Lactate can also serve as a metabolic fuel for both oxidative tumor cells and stromal cells. Lactate shuttles between hypoxic and normoxic regions of the tumor via monocarboxylate transporters (MCT1/4). This shuttling helps integrate metabolism at the tissue level [12]. In ccRCC, elevated levels of lactate and other metabolites (e.g., succinate, kynurenine, adenosine) collectively shape an immunosuppressive TME. These metabolites promote angiogenesis and impair dendritic cells’ antigen-presenting function. They also induce regulatory T-cell generation and drive tumor-associated macrophages toward an M2 phenotype [13, 14]. These mechanisms illustrate how metabolic remodeling can influence tumor biology and the tumor’s response to therapy [15]. Emerging evidence indicates that metabolites can directly regulate gene expression via epigenetic modifications. In 2019, the discovery of histone lactylation established a direct molecular link between aberrant metabolism and epigenetic reprogramming in cancer [16]. Histone lactylation is the covalent addition of lactate-derived lactyl groups to lysine residues on histone tails. This modification is analogous to other histone acylations, such as acetylation. This modification was initially found to activate gene transcription. For example, in macrophages exposed to bacteria, histone lactylation activated genes involved in maintaining homeostasis [17]. The implications are far-reaching. A cell’s metabolic state (e.g., high glycolytic flux) can leave an “imprint” on histones. This imprint alters gene expression programs, helping reinforce or adapt them to the prevailing metabolic and microenvironmental context [18]. In cancer, this metabolic-epigenetic interplay may create a feed-forward loop that drives tumor progression and contributes to therapeutic resistance. For tumors with metabolic dysregulation and common epigenetic mutations (e.g., in PBRM1, SETD2, or BAP1), an in-depth investigation of histone lactylation is especially important.This article is a narrative review. Literature was identified through searches of major biomedical databases using combinations of terms related to ccRCC, metabolic reprogramming, lactate, histone lactylation, tumor immunity, and therapy, with emphasis on mechanistically relevant and recent studies; seminal earlier reports were included where necessary to provide conceptual context.

This review will examine the following aspects: (1) Overview of ccRCC metabolic reprogramming and its heterogeneity across patients and tumor subclones. (2) Mechanisms and functional impacts of histone lactylation in cancer, with an emphasis on recent evidence that highlights lactylation as a key epigenetic regulator in ccRCC. (3) Combined influence of metabolic alterations and histone lactylation on tumor behavior, immune evasion, and therapy resistance. (4) Therapeutic implications of these findings, including potential strategies to target metabolic and epigenetic vulnerabilities in ccRCC. To this end, we will integrate recent advances in genomics, metabolomics, and epigenetics to elucidate how metabolic heterogeneity and histone modifications interact in ccRCC. We will also discuss how best to leverage this knowledge to improve patient outcomes.

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) accounts for approximately 2% of all adult malignancies [19], ranking second only to bladder cancer among urinary system tumors. Although its incidence in China is lower than in developed countries, it has shown a steady upward trend in recent years [20]. Globally, nearly half of RCC patients are diagnosed before 65 years of age, with a 5-year survival rate exceeding 90% for localized disease but dropping below 10% for metastatic cases. As the most prevalent subtype (70%–80% of RCC cases), ccRCC originates from proximal tubular epithelial cells and is characterized by distinct molecular drivers and therapeutic challenges.

Clinically, ccRCC often remains asymptomatic in its early stages and may be detected incidentally, whereas advanced disease is associated with a substantially worse prognosis. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for localized tumors; however, recurrence and metastatic progression still occur in a considerable proportion of patients. Although VEGF-targeted therapy, HIF-2α-directed therapy, and immune checkpoint blockade have significantly improved outcomes in advanced disease, primary and acquired resistance remain major challenges. These clinical limitations provide the rationale for examining metabolic and epigenetic mechanisms that may create actionable therapeutic vulnerabilities in ccRCC.The core oncogenic driver of ccRCC lies in the inactivation of the Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) gene, which occurs in over 80% of tumors. Loss of VHL function stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF-1α and HIF-2α), triggering “pseudohypoxia”—a state where cellular metabolism is reprogrammed even under normoxic conditions [2]. This VHL-HIF axis is the linchpin of ccRCC metabolic dysregulation, directly linking to the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis), altered mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, and alternative metabolic pathways (e.g., glutamine-dependent reductive carboxylation) [3, 4]. These metabolic adaptations support tumor proliferation, invasion, and treatment resistance, with metabolic heterogeneity emerging as a critical feature: distinct subtypes (e.g.,DCCD) exhibit variable metabolic profiles that correlate with aggressiveness and prognostic outcomes [6].

In-depth investigation of ccRCC’s metabolic reprogramming—driven by VHL inactivation, HIF stabilization, and pseudohypoxia—is therefore central to addressing its clinical challenges. This review focuses on how such metabolic remodeling interacts with histone lactylation, shapes the tumor microenvironment, and mediates therapeutic resistance, with the goal of identifying actionable metabolic-epigenetic vulnerabilities.

Mechanisms of metabolic reprogramming in ccRCC

Aerobic glycolysis and lactate production

Even under normoxic conditions, ccRCC cells still rely primarily on glycolysis rather than mitochondrial OXPHOS for ATP production [21]. They generate large amounts of lactate and exhibit the Warburg effect. Loss of VHL is critical in this process. The resulting stabilization of HIF-1α and HIF-2α activates transcriptional programs that upregulate glycolytic genes such as GLUT1/4, HK, PFK, and lactate dehydrogenase A(LDH-A), while simultaneously suppressing glucose oxidation. At the same time, HIF-induced PDK1 inactivates the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. This inactivation diverts pyruvate from the TCA cycle toward lactate production [22]. Consequently, ccRCC cells have fewer mitochondria and decreased OXPHOS capacity. This profile is consistent with a Warburg metabolic state [23]. Excess lactate, co-exported with protons, acidifies the tumor microenvironment. This acidification leads to necrotic regions in the tumor core that further stabilize HIF and maintain a pseudohypoxic state [24].

Glutamine metabolism and reductive carboxylation

ccRCC cells depend on glutamine as a fuel. In most ccRCC, glutamine is broken down by GLS in the mitochondria into glutamate. This glutamate replenishes the TCA cycle. Under pseudohypoxic conditions with high glycolytic activity, ccRCC cells generate citrate and other TCA cycle intermediates in reverse via the reductive carboxylation of α-ketoglutarate(α-KG). This IDH enzyme-driven pathway provides precursors such as acetyl-CoA needed for lipid synthesis [25]. In doing so, it sustains anabolic metabolism even when OXPHOS activity is limited. Furthermore, HIF-2α and c-Myc together upregulate glutamine transporters (e.g., SLC1A5) and GLS expression. Meanwhile, they suppress OXPHOS enzymes such as SCO2 [26]. some tumors produce even more TCA cycle intermediates from glutamine than from glucose [27]. As a result, many ccRCC tumors become glutamine-addicted. Glutamine serves as the primary carbon source for processes such as fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis. Some transcriptomic analyses indicate that ccRCC subtypes with high expression of glutamine-metabolizing enzymes are more aggressive. This suggests that glutamine metabolism not only provides building blocks but also plays a key role in tumor growth [25].

Lipid accumulation and fatty acid reprogramming

ccRCC typically exhibits dysregulated lipid metabolism. Normal proximal tubule cells rely on fatty acid oxidation for energy, whereas ccRCC cells shift toward lipid synthesis and storage. Loss of VHL leads to activation of HIF and mTORC1 pathways. This activation promotes SREBP1-mediated increases in exogenous lipid uptake and de novo fatty acid synthesis. As a result, ccRCC cells accumulate large quantities of cholesterol esters and neutral fats in cytosolic lipid droplets. Meanwhile, lipid synthesis enzymes such as FASN are upregulated. At the same time, the PPARα pathway is inhibited, resulting in reduced fatty acid β-oxidation. This lipid accumulation protects cancer cells from lipotoxicity and provides an energy reserve. Conversely, Research has found that a ccRCC subtype with low lipid content (the DCCD subtype) exhibits higher proliferative and invasive capabilities [28]. These tumors express high levels of lipid transport proteins (e.g., CD36), indicating a reliance on exogenous lipid uptake rather than internal storage. These differences underscore the heterogeneity of lipid metabolism in ccRCC. Some tumors behave as inert “fat depots,” while others actively exploit lipids [29]. Additionally, lipidomic analyses show that elevated cholesterol ester content in ccRCC is associated with worse prognosis [30]. This may be due to alterations in cell membrane composition and signaling.

Tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and mitochondrial function

Although mitochondrial function in ccRCC is diminished, it is not completely lost. Approximately 10–20% of ccRCC cases carry mutations in the FH or SDH genes, especially in hereditary syndromes. These mutations lead to accumulation of fumarate or succinate and the consequent stabilization of HIF. Such tumors exhibit an even more pronounced Warburg effect. Their OXPHOS function is nearly absent. In contrast, sporadic ccRCC cases (without these mutations) show diverse mitochondrial functionality. Some tumors still retain partial OXPHOS activity. Single-cell RNA sequencing has identified ccRCC subgroups with high expression of electron transport chain genes. Overall, mitochondrial DNA content in ccRCC is lower than that in normal kidney tissue. Electron microscopy reveals significantly reduced numbers of mitochondria in ccRCC cells. Metabolic heterogeneity means that different regions within the same tumor can undergo a “metabolic switch.” Some regions prefer glycolysis, whereas others maintain oxidative metabolic characteristics [31]. This metabolic plasticity may lead to therapeutic resistance. Different metabolic subclones respond variably to treatment. For example, clones with a more oxidative metabolism are insensitive to glycolysis inhibitors. In contrast, highly glycolytic clones create an acidic, immunosuppressive microenvironment that confers resistance to VEGF inhibitors.

Metabolic effects on the microenvironment

The metabolic state of ccRCC cells profoundly affects the tumor microenvironment. For instance, highly glycolytic tumor cells secrete large amounts of lactate, which acidifies the local environment. This acidification inhibits the cytotoxic functions of T cells and NK cells. It also promotes macrophages to polarize into an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype [24]. Meanwhile, lactate binds to GPR81 receptors on endothelial cells and adipocytes. This binding triggers angiogenesis in endothelial cells and induces lipolysis in adipocytes, thereby fueling the tumor [32]. In addition, HIF-induced overexpression of CA9 and CA12 in ccRCC catalyzes the hydration of CO₂ to H+ and HCO₃−. This reaction further acidifies the TME to maintain intracellular pH balance. This is consistent with the tumor’s highly glycolytic state. At the same time, depletion of glucose and oxygen in the tumor core creates metabolic “cold zones.” This results in limited immune cell infiltration and activity. High lactate concentrations can even stabilize HIF-1α under normoxic conditions. This establishes a self-reinforcing pseudohypoxic state [24]. Metabolic crosstalk also occurs between tumor cells and the surrounding stroma. Some cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) supply lactate to tumor cells through aerobic glycolysis, a phenomenon termed the “reverse Warburg effect” [33]. Other lipid-rich CAFs provide fatty acids to tumor cells and suppress ferroptosis by sequestering polyunsaturated fatty acids [34]. This protects the tumor cells from ferroptosis.Emerging evidence suggests that redox-regulatory pathways, including Nrf2-associated programs, may shape the oxidative stress–glycolysis balance in the ccRCC microenvironment. This may indirectly influence lactate accumulation and the permissiveness of lactylation-dependent transcriptional responses, although direct mechanistic validation in ccRCC remains limited.

In summary, metabolic reprogramming in ccRCC involves a shift toward glycolysis and lactate production along with changes in glutamine and fatty acid utilization. Together, these metabolic shifts create a heterogeneous metabolic landscape within the tumor. These changes not only promote cancer cell growth but also reshape the microenvironment to favor tumor survival. As we will discuss next, one of the most striking consequences of this metabolic state is the emergence of histone lactylation. This modification is a key epigenetic link between metabolism and gene regulation.

Histone lactylation in cancer epigenetics

In the tumor microenvironment, lactate can bind to extracellular matrix components and promote cancer cell migration by regulating integrins on tumor cell surfaces [35]. Lactate production lowers extracellular pH, thereby further enhancing the migratory capacity and invasiveness of cancer cells. Elevated lactate levels are associated with greater metastatic potential in a variety of primary tumors [36]. Furthermore, lactate is an essential metabolite for histone lactylation. It can activate gene expression, causing macrophages to shift from an M1 to an M2 phenotype and thereby promoting tumorigenesis [37]. Four independent methods have confirmed that histone lysine lactylation is a lactate-mediated post-translational modification occurring in vivo. This finding offers a new perspective on the role of lactate in cancer and other physiological and pathological conditions [38].

Histone lactylation is a recently discovered post-translational modification (PTM) that links cellular metabolism to epigenetic regulation. Zhang et al. first reported this modification in 2019 [39]. Mechanistically, histone lactylation involves the addition of a lactyl group (derived from lactate) to the ε-amino group of lysine residues on histone tails. Researchers initially identified histone lactylation using high-sensitivity mass spectrometry. They found at least 28 distinct lactylation sites on core histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4 in human and mouse cells. Conceptually, histone lactylation is analogous to histone acetylation: it neutralizes the positive charge of lysine, weakens histone–DNA interactions, and is generally associated with a more open chromatin state and active gene transcription. In fact, Zhang et al. showed that lactate-derived histone lactylation can directly stimulate gene transcription on chromatin, establishing it as a bona fide epigenetic mark [39].

Biochemically, histone lactylation is linked to cellular metabolism via the intermediate metabolite lactyl-CoA (also known as lactoyl-CoA). In cells with high lactate levels, lactate can be converted into lactyl-CoA; however, this process is less understood than acetyl-CoA production. Lactyl-CoA then serves as the donor substrate for lysine lactylation. Several histone acetyltransferases with broad acyltransferase activity serve as “writers” of histone lactylation. These include p300/CBP, GCN5 (KAT2A), and possibly KAT8, which has been shown to catalyze histone lactylation in the presence of lactyl-CoA [40]. Notably, p300 (the same enzyme that acetylates H3K27 and many other sites) can also lactylate histones on certain lysine residues when lactyl-CoA is available [12]. This dual functionality suggests some interplay or competition between acetylation and lactylation marks, as both modifications target lysine residues and utilize similar enzymatic mechanisms.Supporting this, cancer cells undergoing Warburg metabolism accumulate acetyl-CoA and lactyl-CoA, resulting in simultaneously elevated histone acetylation and lactylation levels. Each of these modifications influences gene expression in distinct ways [41].

Lactyl group removal (i.e., the ‘eraser’ function) is carried out by deacetylases. Class I histone deacetylases (HDAC1–3) and sirtuin family deacetylases have been implicated as histone “delactylases.” These enzymes can hydrolyze lysine lactylation, thereby erasing the lactyl mark [42]. For example, HDAC1–3 can remove acyl modifications (including lactyl groups) in vitro. Additionally, SIRT3 has been shown to influence lactate levels and lactylation in a kidney injury model [43]. It appears that many of the enzymes that remove acetylation can also remove lactylation, although the efficiency and regulation of delactylation may differ from those of deacetylation. Interestingly, the use of HDAC inhibitors (explored in cancer therapy) is expected to increase histone lactylation levels, as these inhibitors block lactylation erasers. This increase could lead to gene expression changes associated with lactylation. Overall, the “writers” and “erasers” of histone lactylation have only begun to be characterized. p300/CBP act as writers, whereas HDAC1–3/SIRT family delactylases may serve as erasers (some certain lysine demethylases possess broad lysine deacylase activity) [12] (Fig. 1). To date, no dedicated “reader” protein that specifically recognizes lactyl-lysine has been fully validated in a universal biological context. Emerging evidence suggests that lactyl-lysine reader functions may not be entirely undefined: recent studies have implicated DPF2 as a reader of H3K14la, whereas YEATS-domain proteins—known to recognize broader acyl-lysine marks—provide a plausible framework for lactyl-lysine interpretation in chromatin remodeling complexes. However, whether these candidate reader mechanisms operate in ccRCC and which chromatin programs they control remain to be determined. Bromodomain-containing proteins (which read acetyl-lysine marks) are also being investigated for potential cross-reactivity with lactyl-lysine, an area of active research [44].

Building on the aforementioned interplay between lactylation and other chromatin modifications, as well as the role of lactate in tumorigenesis, direct evidence currently supports a VHL-loss/lactate/H3K18 lactylation(H3K18la)/PDGFRβ regulatory axis in ccRCC. Nevertheless, the broader crosstalk between lactylation and other chromatin marks (e.g., methylation) remains incompletely defined. We therefore propose, as a working hypothesis, that lactylation may compete with acetylation at shared lysine residues (consistent with the dual functionality of enzymes like p300) and may indirectly influence methylation states through metabolic effects on α-ketoglutarate-dependent enzymes.

Physiological and pathological roles of lactylation

The functional significance of histone lactylation is still under active investigation. Initial reports demonstrated a role for histone lactylation in immune cell polarization. For instance, in bacterially stimulated M1 macrophages, lactate accumulation over time increases histone lactylation, which in turn activates genes associated with wound healing and M2 polarization in the later stages of the immune response [39]. This “lactate clock” concept suggests that histone lactylation acts as a metabolic timer in macrophages, switching cellular programs from an inflammatory state to a reparative state [39]. Beyond macrophages, histone lactylation has been observed in a variety of other contexts.

Normal physiology

Histone lactylation marks have been detected in normal tissues, suggesting a role in development and differentiation. For example, H3K18la has been detected in mouse and human zygotes. This mark is associated with gene activation during early embryonic development and somatic cell reprogramming [45]. In osteoblasts, lactylation of specific histones can downregulate JunB expression, thereby affecting osteogenic differentiation [46].

Inflammation and sepsis

H3K18la has been identified as a potential biomarker in septic shock, as its levels correlate with disease severity [47]. In a sepsis-associated acute kidney injury model, elevated H3K18la at promoters of genes such as RhoA promotes inflammatory gene expression and exacerbates tissue damage [48]. These findings suggest that under acute stress conditions, lactylation can promote aberrant gene expression.

Fibrosis and chronic disease

Studies indicate that lactylation is involved in the development of multiple fibrotic diseases. In chronic kidney disease, sustained glycolysis in damaged renal tubular cells leads to elevated histone lactylation levels (particularly at H4K12la). This increase in lactylation activates an NF-κB–mediated pro-fibrotic gene program [49]. In liver fibrosis, elevated histone lactylation levels in hepatic stellate cells are similarly associated with activation of pro-fibrotic genes [50].

Non-histone lactylation

Notably, lactylation is not limited to histones. Proteomic studies have identified over 1,000 lactylated proteins in cells [51]. Lactylation of non-histone proteins can modulate their functions. For example, lactylation of p53 at specific lysine residues has been reported to impair its DNA-binding ability and tumor suppressor function [52]. Similarly, some metabolic enzymes (such as those in the glycolytic pathway) are lactylated, suggesting self-regulating feedback loops in metabolism [53]. Even METTL3, a co-regulator of RNA methyltransferase activity, can undergo lactylation, thereby affecting patterns of mRNA modification [54]. Although this review focuses on histone lactylation, the broader “lactylome” (the full spectrum of lactylated proteins) is an emerging field linked to numerous cellular processes.

Histone lactylation in cancer

Given lactate’s abundance in tumors and the Warburg-type metabolism of many cancer cells, it is not surprising that high histone lactylation levels contribute to cancer progression. Several recent studies have linked increased histone lactylation levels to more aggressive cancer phenotypes [51]. To improve interpretability, we distinguish below between findings directly demonstrated in ccRCC and evidence extrapolated from other tumor types or experimental systems. The latter is discussed to generate mechanistic hypotheses but should not be interpreted as ccRCC-specific proof.

Tumor proliferation and metastasis

Studies in multiple cancers have found that high H3K18la levels can activate pro-tumor signaling pathways [51]. In ccRCC, Yang et al. found that loss of the VHL gene leads to increased H3K18la levels. This increase upregulates PDGFRβ (platelet-derived growth factor receptor-β) expression, thereby driving tumor cell proliferation and metastasis. Notably, they uncovered a positive feedback loop: PDGFRβ signaling, in turn, further elevates histone lactylation levels, forming a self-reinforcing cycle that accelerates ccRCC progression [55]. Clinical data indicate that ccRCC patients with VHL inactivation exhibit higher global histone lactylation levels and poorer prognoses, underscoring the clinical relevance of this epigenetic mark. In colorectal cancer (CRC), high H3K18la levels reportedly activate pro-autophagy genes (e.g., RUBCNL/Pacer), thereby promoting tumorigenesis [56]. In uveal melanoma, elevated histone lactylation levels correlate with poorer patient survival. This increase in lactylation was reported to upregulate the m6A reader protein YTHDF2. YTHDF2, in turn, reduces the mRNA stability of certain tumor suppressor genes (such as PER1 and TP53), thereby promoting tumor growth [57].

Metabolic gene regulation

Histone lactylation may directly influence tumor cell metabolism by regulating metabolic enzyme gene expression. In a proteomic “lactylome” analysis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) samples, lactylation was found to occur mainly on metabolic enzymes involved in glycolysis and the TCA cycle. Moreover, higher lactylation of these enzymes was significantly associated with greater disease aggressiveness [51]. In lung cancer, elevated histone lactylation levels were linked to a shift in metabolic gene expression patterns from glycolysis towards the TCA cycle pathway. This shift might represent a compensatory adjustment [58]. These findings reveal a complex feedback mechanism. Lactylation driven by metabolic changes can in turn reshape the expression of metabolism-related genes — for example, enhancing glucose uptake and TCA cycle activity under certain conditions [58].

Immune evasion and TME

Histone lactylation levels in tumor cells are also linked to mechanisms of immune evasion. Research has shown that genetic or pharmacological reduction of histone lactylation levels can increase colorectal cancer cell sensitivity to anti-VEGF therapy (bevacizumab) [56]. This finding suggests that lactylation helps regulate pro-angiogenic factors and influences the response to anti-angiogenic therapy. Accumulation of lactate in the tumor microenvironment can also affect immune cells via lactylation. For example, elevated histone lactylation in tumor-infiltrating macrophages induces polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, as evidenced by high Arginase-1 (Arg1) expression in these cells [39]. Evidence suggests that regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in tumors may utilize lactate-driven lactylation to maintain high expression of suppressive molecules (e.g., IL-10 and inhibitory checkpoint proteins), although this hypothesis remains under investigation. Lactate itself, through its acidifying action and signaling effects, can inactivate effector immune cells [59]. Histone lactylation likely acts as a transcriptional amplifier of these suppressive programs in various TME cells, thereby further promoting immune evasion.

Therapeutic resistance

Recent studies have revealed links between lactylation and resistance to anticancer therapies. As mentioned, high histone lactylation levels may confer resistance to anti-angiogenic therapy in colorectal cancer by sustaining alternative pro-angiogenic signaling pathways [56]. In tumors such as gliomas, lactate-driven upregulation of DNA damage response genes (e.g., XRCC1), together with increased histone lactylation, enhances tumor cells’ DNA repair capabilities, rendering them more resistant to radiotherapy and alkylating chemotherapy [60]. Additionally, lactylation of critical non-histone proteins can mediate therapy resistance. For instance, lactylation of the DNA repair enzyme MRE11 increases the efficiency of homologous recombination repair in colorectal cancer cells, raising their tolerance to chemotherapy [61]. Collectively, these findings suggest that in high-lactate environments, cancer cells leverage lactylation-mediated gene expression changes to survive therapeutic stress.

In ccRCC specifically, aside from the aforementioned VHL/PDGFRβ pathway study, bioinformatic analyses indicate that lactylation-related gene expression signatures can stratify patients. Wan et al. performed an integrated proteomic study and identified 441 lactylation sites with significantly different abundances between ccRCC and normal tissues. These sites were enriched on proteins involved in multiple metabolic pathways (purine metabolism, glycolysis, fatty acid breakdown), indicating that lactylation is extensively involved in metabolic reprogramming in ccRCC [62]. Similarly, Liu et al. analyzed 328 lactylation-related genes in the TCGA ccRCC cohort and found 16 whose expression was significantly associated with overall survival. Unsupervised clustering of these 16 genes’ expression separated ccRCC tumors into two molecular subtypes. Interestingly, one subtype was enriched in metabolic pathways and corresponded to a relatively better prognosis, whereas the other subtype exhibited inflammatory and immune exhaustion traits with worse outcomes.In this inflammation associated subtype, lactylation “writer” enzymes (e.g., p300) were expressed at higher levels and lactate metabolic turnover was increased. This suggests that high lactylation may drive an immunosuppressive, aggressive tumor state. Furthermore, a machine learning risk model based on lactylation-related gene expression distinguished high-risk from low-risk ccRCC patients, further emphasizing the clinical relevance of the lactylation axis [63].

In summary, the discovery of histone lactylation marks a paradigm shift in our understanding of cancer epigenetics. As a modification derived directly from metabolic flux, lactylation serves as a conduit for metabolic signals (e.g., lactate accumulation) to influence chromatin structure and gene activity. In cancers like ccRCC with prominent metabolic abnormalities, histone lactylation plays a key role in regulating tumor cell behavior and tumor–immune interactions. The next section will explore how metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming converge in ccRCC, as well as how metabolic reprogramming and lactylation synergize in disease progression.

Interplay between metabolic reprogramming and histone lactylation in ccRCC

Metabolic reprogramming and histone lactylation in ccRCC are interdependent processes. They form a positive feedback loop that amplifies tumor-promoting events. In ccRCC tumor cells, the Warburg effect produces an excess of lactate. This lactate is not only a metabolic byproduct but also a signaling molecule. Lactate drives histone lactylation, reshaping gene expression to meet the needs of cancer cells [64]. This section explores the key aspects of this interaction and its implications for tumor progression, the immune microenvironment, and therapeutic resistance.

Lactate-driven lactylation feedback loop

One of the clearest examples of metabolic–epigenetic crosstalk is the VHL–HIF–lactate–lactylation–PDGFRβ positive feedback loop identified in ccRCC [55]. In ccRCC cells lacking the VHL gene, HIF-1 and HIF-2 are constitutively active. This leads to a high glycolytic flux and the production of large amounts of lactate. Yang et al. demonstrated that lactate accumulation triggers increased levels of histone lactylation (particularly H3K18la) [55]. This epigenetic change upregulates PDGFRβ expression by increasing chromatin accessibility at the PDGFRβ locus. At the PDGFRβ locus, H3K18la is consistent with a more permissive chromatin state and increased transcription. We further propose that, under therapeutic stress, competition between H3K18la and H3K18lac may influence transcriptional choice, with the dominant mark likely determined by local metabolite availability, writer/eraser activity, and chromatin context. The resulting PDGFRβ signaling is a powerful growth and survival pathway. It further promotes glycolysis and lactate production, thereby sustaining histone lactylation and creating a self-reinforcing positive feedback cycle [55]. This feedback mechanism helps explain how the initial loss of VHL leads to aggressive tumor behavior. A metabolic alteration (lactate accumulation) is converted into an epigenetic change (histone lactylation), which locks in the expression of pro-tumor genes. In preclinical models, disrupting this loop significantly slows ccRCC growth. This can be achieved either by blocking the histone lactylation “writer” enzyme or by inhibiting the PDGFRβ signaling pathway. Thus, a weakness at the metabolic level (excess lactate) is converted into a vulnerability at the epigenetic level, rendering the tumor dependent on lactylation-driven gene expression.

Another feedback interaction is evident in lactylation’s regulation of metabolic enzyme expression. For example, in non-small cell lung cancer, high levels of histone lactylation have been shown to downregulate glycolytic enzymes and upregulate TCA cycle enzymes [58]. This effectively shifts metabolism toward a more oxidative state. This may represent a compensatory response. As lactate accumulates and lactylation rises, the cell may attempt to curb glycolysis and reroute lactate into the TCA cycle. Notably, some tumors can metabolize lactate as a fuel [65]. Indeed, isotope tracing studies reveal that lactate can be taken up by well-oxygenated tumor regions and fed into the TCA cycle, generating citrate and other intermediates [65]. The tumor can dynamically adjust its metabolic phenotype by lactylating histones at the gene loci of metabolic regulators. For example, this modification can occur at genes encoding regulators such as PGC-1α or metabolic enzymes like LDH-A and GLS. Although direct evidence in ccRCC is still emerging, this mechanism likely contributes to intratumoral metabolic heterogeneity.

Impact on tumor progression and invasion

The interplay between metabolism and lactylation often forms a self-reinforcing pro-tumorigenic cycle. High lactate levels in ccRCC provide energy and building blocks for biosynthesis, thereby accelerating cell proliferation. At the same time, through histone lactylation, lactate activates the expression of numerous genes that promote cell cycle progression, angiogenesis, and metastasis. For example, in addition to PDGFRβ, histone lactylation may activate EMT(epithelial–mesenchymal transition) regulators and other metastasis-related genes. A study in gastrointestinal cancer found that lactylation of the heterochromatin protein CBX3 enhances its binding to H3K9me3 marks [66]. This epigenetic modification silences differentiation-related genes and drives an invasive phenotype in cancer cells. Similarly, in some cancers, elevated histone lactylation is associated with increased expression of YAP/TAZ target genes [67]. This change correlates with a more invasive tumor phenotype.Notably, many chromatin-modifying factors are frequently mutated in ccRCC. Histone lactylation may further exacerbate the genomic instability and differentiation dysregulation caused by these mutations. ccRCC tumors often exhibit genome-wide DNA hypomethylation and a globally open chromatin state. In a lactate-rich tumor microenvironment, histone lactylation could further relax the chromatin structure, facilitating the expression of latent oncogenes. PET imaging and other methods have shown that ccRCC tumors with higher lactate levels tend to have higher grades and more advanced stages [55]. This observation is consistent with elevated histone lactylation in those tumors and supports its correlation with disease progression. In summary, the coupling of metabolic and epigenetic changes provides cancer cells with a mechanism to convert metabolic stress into pro-survival genetic programs. During tumor growth, when nutrient and oxygen supplies fluctuate, this mechanism confers a significant survival advantage to cancer cells.

Immune microenvironment modulation

One of the most far-reaching consequences of the interplay between metabolism and histone lactylation is its impact on immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. Lactate is a well-known immunosuppressive metabolite. It imposes acidic stress on effector T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, limiting their proliferation and cytotoxic function [24]. Furthermore, when tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) take up lactate, they adopt an M2-like immunosuppressive phenotype. One mechanism behind this phenomenon is histone lactylation. In TAMs, lactate-driven histone lactylation at H3K18 upregulates Arg1 and other M2-associated genes. This epigenetic change skews macrophages toward a wound-healing, tumor-promoting phenotype. M2-type TAMs secrete VEGF and IL-10, which promote angiogenesis and suppress T cell function [39].ccRCC tumors often exhibit abundant TAM infiltration, especially in hypoxic regions. Therefore, the lactate-driven, lactylation-mediated immunomodulatory effects described above are likely widespread in ccRCC. Indeed, TAMs in ccRCC commonly express high levels of ARG1, VEGFA, PD-L1, and other immunosuppressive factors. Lactate likely contributes to the upregulation of these genes through histone lactylation. In addition, lactate can suppress dendritic cell (DC) function. Specifically, lactate impairs DC maturation and antigen presentation. Research suggests that this occurs because lactate, via lactylation, inhibits the activation of NF-κB signaling in DCs [68]. If high lactate levels in the tumor microenvironment induce histone lactylation in DCs, the expression of co-stimulatory molecule genes will be downregulated. This will weaken the ability of DCs to activate anti-tumor T cell responses (Fig. 2).

Therapeutic resistance resulting from metabolic–epigenetic coupling

The convergence of metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic alterations may endow ccRCC with therapy resistance. A standard treatment strategy for ccRCC is to inhibit VEGF signaling (for example, with sunitinib or pazopanib). Tumors with high lactate and histone lactylation levels often evade anti-angiogenic therapy by engaging alternative angiogenic pathways or via TAM-mediated protection of the tumor vasculature. In a colorectal cancer study, reducing histone lactylation improved tumor sensitivity to VEGF blockade [56]. This suggests that histone lactylation helps maintain neovascularization, possibly via PDGFRβ or other VEGF-independent pro-angiogenic factors. Similarly, in ccRCC, tumors with high histone lactylation may be less dependent on VEGF signaling. It has been speculated that inhibiting histone lactylation could increase the sensitivity of such tumors to PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). However, elevated lactate levels are generally associated with a poorer response to immunotherapy because lactate suppresses immune effector function. Clinical observations indicate that ccRCC patients with high glycolysis gene signatures (implying high lactate production) exhibit slightly lower response rates to PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint blockade. This is likely attributable to the lactate-induced immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Thus, combining lactate metabolism inhibitors with PD-1/PD-L1 ICIs has emerged as a promising strategy. Indeed, ongoing clinical trials are investigating combined metabolic therapy and immunotherapy approaches.At the molecular level, histone lactylation can upregulate the expression of DNA damage repair (DDR) genes. ccRCC typically exhibits a moderate mutational burden and a tendency toward intrinsic treatment resistance. If lactylation enhances the expression of DDR genes or anti-apoptotic factors, cancer cells become better equipped to withstand damage from targeted therapies or radiation. Studies show that in macrophages, lactylation of the glycolytic enzyme PKM2 creates a feedback loop that drives persistent glycolysis and triggers immunosuppression [56]. Similarly, in tumor cells, lactylation of PKM2 might help maintain metabolic stability under therapeutic stress [69].

Metabolic heterogeneity within tumors can itself lead to differential therapy responses. For example, subpopulations of ccRCC cells with lower glycolytic activity (and thus lower lactate production and lactylation levels) might survive glycolysis-inhibiting treatments. By contrast, subpopulations that are highly glycolysis-dependent might be more tolerant of mitochondrial inhibitors. Histone lactylation levels could serve as a marker to distinguish these subgroups. For instance, an aggressive DCCD subtype of ccRCC exhibits a unique treatment response pattern. Clinical trial data show that this metabolically dysregulated subtype derived significantly more benefit from combined immunotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy than from tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) monotherapy [28]. Moreover, if such tumors do not receive prompt and effective treatment, they have the poorest prognosis among all ccRCC subtypes [28]. In this aggressive metabolic state characterized by high nutrient uptake and rapid proliferation, lactylation may function as an epigenetic amplifier that converts persistent glycolytic stress into pro-survival transcriptional programs. This model helps mechanistically link the DCCD subtype’s metabolic features (enhanced nutrient acquisition, glycolytic dependency) to its aggressive behavior and poor prognosis, as lactylation could potentially rewire transcriptional outputs to favor tumor survival, proliferation, and immune adaptation. Although the DCCD subtype is characterized by sparse lipid droplets, high nutrient uptake, and aggressive clinical behavior, direct quantitative evidence linking DCCD to higher lactate content or histone lactylation in ccRCC is currently lacking. We therefore present lactate-driven chromatin remodeling in DCCD-like tumors as a testable hypothesis rather than an established subtype-defining feature.Therefore, treating this subtype requires targeting both its metabolic dependencies and its immunosuppressive microenvironment. For example, co-administering PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint inhibitors with VEGF pathway blockers could help normalize tumor vasculature and improve immune cell infiltration.

In summary, metabolic reprogramming and histone lactylation in ccRCC form a vicious cycle that promotes tumor growth and immune evasion. Lactate serves as both an energy source and a signaling molecule. It not only fuels surrounding cells but also acts as a substrate for an epigenetic modification (histone lactylation) that alters gene expression to reinforce the tumor’s metabolic state. This metabolic–epigenetic crosstalk influences ccRCC progression and affects its response to therapy. Understanding these connections offers opportunities for new interventions. Current research is exploring strategies to disrupt this metabolic–epigenetic positive feedback loop—for example, by targeting lactate production, lactate transport, or the histone lactylation process—to develop more effective treatments for ccRCC.

Therapeutic significance and emerging strategies

ccRCC is a metabolism-driven disease. Histone lactylation entrenches the tumor’s malignant characteristics via epigenetic mechanisms. Currently, standard therapies for advanced ccRCC—vascular endothelial growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)—do not directly target the tumor’s metabolic or epigenetic features. This lack of direct targeting may explain why durable complete remissions remain rare. An emerging therapeutic strategy is to exploit ccRCC’s metabolic and epigenetic dependencies, either by developing new drugs or by repurposing existing ones. Among metabolism-linked interventions, HIF-2α inhibition has the strongest current clinical relevance, exemplified by belzutifan in VHL-associated renal cancer. By contrast, p300/CBP inhibitors such as A485 remain hypothesis-generating tools in this context, but they are mechanistically attractive because they may reduce lactylation-writer activity and dampen transcriptional programs linked to angiogenesis and tumor survival.Potential synergy with ICIs, TKIs, or epigenetic drugs is mechanistically plausible but should be interpreted in light of limited clinical validation and possible toxicity related to systemic metabolic or chromatin perturbation. In the future, integrating multi-omics data is expected to enable treatment regimens to be tailored to the tumor’s metabolic-epigenetic subtype. In future biomarker-driven studies, DCCD-like tumors with independently confirmed high histone lactylation could be evaluated for rational combinations integrating HIF-2α inhibition, metabolic targeting, and immunotherapy; this remains a hypothesis requiring prospective validation. In contrast, patients with metabolically quiescent tumors could avoid the toxicity of metabolic drugs and be treated with only the standard VEGFR-TKI plus ICI.

At present, some clinical trials have begun stratifying patients based on biomarkers. For example, patients with high expression of HIF target genes are selected to receive HIF-2 inhibitor therapy. On the epigenetic side, Standardized H3K18la immunohistochemistry, ideally supported by orthogonal validation, may help enrich for tumors with active lactylation-associated transcriptional programs and guide future trials of p300/CBP-directed strategies.Therapeutic strategies discussed in this review differ in clinical maturity. HIF-2α inhibition has already demonstrated clinical utility in VHL-associated renal cancer, whereas most lactate/lactylation-directed interventions, including p300/CBP inhibition and LDH/MCT targeting, remain preclinical or early translational approaches (Fig. 3). Accordingly, we distinguish established therapies from hypothesis-generating combination strategies and emphasize the need for biomarker-guided patient selection.

Future directions

Defining lactylation “reader” proteins and functional targets

“Writer” enzymes (e.g., p300) and “eraser” enzymes (e.g., HDAC) involved in histone lactylation have been identified.However, the “reader” proteins that specifically recognize lactylated histones and mediate downstream transcriptional effects in ccRCC have not been fully established. Emerging evidence suggests that lactyl-lysine reader functions may not be entirely undefined: recent studies have implicated DPF2 as a reader of H3K14la, whereas YEATS-domain proteins—well-characterized as readers of broader acyl-lysine marks—provide a reasonable framework for understanding how lactyl-lysine could be interpreted within chromatin remodeling complexes. Whether these candidate reader mechanisms are functionally relevant to ccRCC and which specific chromatin programs they regulate remain to be fully determined.Identifying these reader proteins is crucial for understanding how histone lactylation triggers transcriptional changes. Several approaches could be used to identify candidate reader proteins. For instance, researchers could use lactyl-lysine analogs for affinity pull-down or screen libraries of protein domains to find those that bind lactyl-lysine (such as bromodomains or YEATS domains). In addition, a genome-wide mapping of histone lactylation (for example, by ChIP-seq) in ccRCC tumors versus normal tissue could reveal which genes are directly regulated by this modification. Is histone lactylation predominantly enriched at the promoters of metabolism-related, immune-related, or cell-cycle genes, or does it mark only a specific subset of genes? Answering this question will elucidate the functional role of lactylation in ccRCC.

Interplay of lactylation and other epigenetic modifications

Histone lactylation does not occur in isolation. It may have synergistic or competitive relationships with other histone marks, such as acetylation and methylation. Lactylation and acetylation likely have overlapping functions. Both neutralize the positive charge of lysine residues and activate gene expression. Lactylation may preferentially occur at sites that are already acetylated, or at sites that lack acetylation. Additionally, there may be crosstalk between epigenetic modifications. For example, could high lactate levels or an acidic microenvironment inhibit demethylase enzymes by limiting the availability of α-KG, thereby indirectly increasing histone methylation levels? To address this, researchers could perform multi-omics time-course experiments after perturbing cellular lactate levels. For instance, combining metabolomics, ChIP-seq for different histone marks, and RNA-seq could dynamically reveal these complex interactions.

Mapping intertumoral and intratumoral metabolic zonation

With advances in single-cell RNA sequencing, as well as spatial transcriptomics and metabolomics, we can now dissect ccRCC tumors with unprecedented precision. An important future direction is to map the spatial distribution of metabolism and lactylation in ccRCC. This involves identifying which regions of a tumor have high lactate content and high lactylation levels, and which regions have relatively low levels. This spatial information could link lactylation levels to the tumor’s oxygenation status, immune cell distribution, and clonal architecture. In one scenario, lactylation levels could be extremely high at the tumor–stroma interface or in hypoxic niche regions, but much lower in oxygen-rich areas near the capsule. Such gradients could be therapeutically exploited. For example, delivering monocarboxylate transporter (MCT) inhibitors locally to high-lactate regions could selectively disrupt the microenvironment on which the tumor depends. Heterogeneity in lactylation levels within a tumor might also explain why certain treatments yield only partial responses. Only some subclonal regions may be heavily reliant on the metabolic–epigenetic pathway, while other regions are not.

Biomarkers for patient stratification

As new metabolic (HIF-2, glutaminase, etc.) and epigenetic (HDAC, p300) targeted therapies are developed, biomarkers will play a critical role in selecting the right patients.Because many metabolism- and lactylation-directed interventions remain investigational, patient selection will likely require integrated biomarkers, including HIF activity, metabolic subtype, and tissue lactylation status. Potential biomarkers include several categories. First, lactate levels in patient plasma or metabolic imaging with PET could indicate tumor metabolic activity; for example, FDG-PET serves as a proxy for Warburg metabolic activity, and new lactate-specific tracers are in development. Second, tumor gene expression signatures may be useful; for instance, Liu et al. identified a 16-gene lactylation-related signature that could be refined into a clinical assay. Third, direct measurement of histone lactylation in tumor biopsies is another approach. Some studies have used IHC staining for H3K18la, and standardizing this method could enable patient stratification. One could also use a “metabolic score” of the tumor to guide treatment decisions. For example, the metabolic–tumor–stroma score proposed to predict immunotherapy response could help determine whether a patient should receive a metabolic combination regimen or the standard treatment. Future trials may prospectively incorporate such scoring systems.

Drug development targeting the lactate/lactylation axis

New drugs directly targeting lactate production and histone lactylation are expected to emerge. For lactate metabolism, a primary goal is to develop LDH-A inhibitors with good tumor penetration and high specificity. The aim is to replicate the effects of LDH-A knockdown observed in kidney cancer models—namely, significantly suppressed tumor growth and restored T cell function. For the lactylation modification, if the corresponding reader proteins are identified, “lactylation reader domain inhibitors” could be developed. This approach would be analogous to bromodomain inhibitors for acetylation readers. For example, if a YEATS domain protein were found to be a lactylation reader, a small molecule could be designed to block its binding to lactyl-lysine. This would inhibit lactylation-mediated epigenetic effects without altering cellular metabolism. Another idea is to develop activators of delactylase enzymes, such as allosteric activators of SIRT3 or HDAC3, to reduce histone lactylation levels. It should be noted that this approach might simultaneously affect histone acetylation levels. Meanwhile, nanotechnology might also play a role in targeting this pathway. For instance, nanoparticles could deliver lactate oxidase or bicarbonate buffers to the tumor site to degrade lactate in situ [64]. Alternatively, nanoparticles could be used to co-deliver metabolic inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors directly into the tumor microenvironment [55]. These innovative approaches aim to minimize systemic toxicity and maximize on-target effects against the tumor.Therapeutic targeting of the lactate/lactylation axis also has limitations. Potential challenges include intratumoral heterogeneity, uncertain biomarker thresholds, adaptive resistance, and systemic toxicity resulting from interference with broadly shared metabolic or chromatin-regulatory pathways. These issues should be addressed in future biomarker-guided clinical studies.Additionally, the toxicity of metabolic and chromatin-directed therapies, as well as their effects at the targeted treatment site and outside the tumor, remain important concerns.

Critical evaluation of mechanistic uncertainties in lactylation research

While the aforementioned future directions (Sect. 6.3–6.5) outline promising avenues for translating lactylation research into clinical applications, several critical uncertainties persist that demand careful consideration to avoid overemphasizing the current mechanistic certainty—aligning with broader caveats in the field. Several uncertainties warrant caution. First, the intracellular origin and abundance of lactyl-CoA remain incompletely resolved: although lactate is generally accepted as the primary precursor, the enzymatic cascade driving lactyl-CoA synthesis is less defined than that of acetyl-CoA, and potential contributions from non-enzymatic or alternative metabolic pathways to lactyl-CoA pools have not been fully explored, raising questions about the robustness of this metabolite in diverse tumor contexts. Second, interpretation of pan-lactyllysine and site-specific lactylation antibodies requires substantial caution, particularly in tissue staining or chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-based studies. As highlighted, antibody-based detection of lactylation may have specificity limitations depending on context, with potential cross-reactivity with structurally similar lysine acylations (e.g., acetylation, malonylation) that could confound results without orthogonal validation (e.g., high-resolution mass spectrometry). Third, histone lactylation exhibits strong environment dependence across different tumor cell subsets, immune cells, and stromal components of the ccRCC microenvironment. This contextuality is further complicated by its interplay with acetylation: both modifications target shared lysine residues, leading to potential competition or cooperation that varies with metabolic flux, microenvironmental conditions, and cell type, and their functional consequences are often intertwined rather than mutually exclusive. These uncertainties underscore that while lactylation holds great promise as a therapeutic target and biomarker, its mechanistic complexity is not yet fully delineated, necessitating rigorous validation in future preclinical and clinical studies.

Clinical trials and combination therapy

In the next 5–10 years, clinical trials may combine metabolic therapies (such as HIF-2 and glutaminase inhibitors) with epigenetic therapy and immunotherapy. A key direction is to design rational combination regimens. For instance, for highly aggressive ccRCC, one could combine an HIF-2 inhibitor with a PD-1 inhibitor and a CTLA-4 inhibitor. In tumors with strong co-dependencies in metabolism and epigenetics, a glutaminase inhibitor could be paired with an HDAC inhibitor. Each combination should be guided by solid preclinical evidence and biomarker-driven patient selection. Given the heterogeneity of ccRCC, an adaptive trial design is advisable. Patients would first receive a backbone regimen (e.g., ICI + TKI). Then, based on early metabolic imaging or biopsy results, those with elevated lactate or insufficient immune activity could have a metabolic drug added. In summary, the convergence of metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic modification via histone lactylation represents a new frontier in cancer research—particularly in ccRCC. By embracing this integrated perspective, we can identify new biomarkers, therapeutic targets, and combination strategies. Hopefully, in the near future we will be able to better stratify ccRCC patients by their tumor’s metabolic-epigenetic profile and deploy precision therapies accordingly. This two-pronged approach could yield more durable remissions or even cures for a disease that remains lethal in its metastatic form. The road ahead will require close collaboration between basic scientists and clinicians. Basic scientists must map these complex pathways, and clinicians must conduct innovative trials. However, the potential rewards for patients are enormous.

Conclusion

Metabolic heterogeneity and histone lactylation are redefining our understanding of ccRCC biology. We can no longer view tumor metabolism and epigenetics in isolation—together they form a coordinated network that enables the tumor to thrive under adverse conditions and evade therapy. ccRCC typically exhibits Warburg-like metabolism along with a high frequency of mutations in epigenetic regulator genes. Research over the past decade has illuminated how lactate, once considered a “metabolic waste” byproduct, becomes a key regulator of gene expression through histone lactylation, and through feed-forward loops drives oncogenic pathways and immune evasion [65]. It is now recognized that ccRCC displays metabolic diversity: certain regions and subclones are highly glycolysis-dependent and have high lactylation levels, whereas other regions prefer oxidative metabolism, and these differences influence treatment response and prognosis [66]. Notably, a balanced appraisal of this field must acknowledge existing uncertainties and validation gaps. First, the number of ccRCC-specific lactylation studies remains limited, with many mechanistic interpretations extrapolated from other tumor types or non-tumor models. Second, critical questions surrounding lactylation biogenesis and the identity of specific reader proteins that mediate its downstream effects remain unresolved. Third, technical limitations of current assays may constrain the accuracy of lactylation detection and functional characterization. These caveats highlight the need to distinguish clearly between established data (e.g., metabolic heterogeneity in ccRCC, clinical success of HIF-2α targeting), emerging evidence (e.g., lactylation’s role in oncogenic signaling), and hypothesis-generating concepts (e.g., therapeutic targeting of metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk).In terms of therapy, targeting HIF-2α has achieved clinical success. However, more forward-thinking strategies aim to intervene in the epigenetic changes induced by metabolic reprogramming—essentially, disrupting the “information” that tumor metabolism conveys to the genome via lactylation. Although these approaches are still in early stages, they promise to add powerful new tools to our therapeutic arsenal, with potential both as monotherapies and in combination therapies to preempt resistance.Finally, integrating high-dimensional data from genomics, epigenomics, and metabolomics can drive personalization of ccRCC treatment. Patients whose tumors have high lactate flux and lactylation levels can be identified before treatment and given tailored combination therapies that include metabolic disruptors or epigenetic modulators, whereas patients with metabolically quiescent tumors can be spared unnecessary toxicity from metabolic drugs and treated with immunotherapy or targeted agents alone. Precision therapies directed at metabolic and epigenetic features will complement and strengthen existing treatments, ultimately improving patient survival and quality of life. Realizing this vision will require close collaboration between basic scientists and clinicians to address current limitations, and the rewards in terms of patient outcomes could be tremendous.
