Integrative Bioinformatics Analyses Reveal X Chromosome Inactivation Escape Genes Dosage Dysregulation Is the Primary Contributor to Turner Syndrome
Authors: Huisheng Liang, Zhongyang Li, Fei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Turner syndrome is a rare disorder resulting from abnormalities in the number or structure of a single X chromosome. It stands as the sole survivable monosomy observed in humans. Individuals affected by Turner syndrome commonly face significant complications, including impaired gonadal development, short stature, and intellectual disabilities. Despite extensive research, the complete understanding of the pathogenesis behind Turner syndrome remains elusive.Objectives: This study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of Turner syndrome by identifying key X chromosome inactivation escape genes and exploring their potential contributions to genotype–phenotype correlations and therapeutic targets through integrative bioinformatics analyses.Materials and Methods: The study included three RNA-seq datasets, consisting of 72 patients with Turner syndrome and 58 individuals with normal cytogenetics. Differentially expressed gene analysis, protein-protein interaction network analysis, and functional enrichment analysis were conducted to ascertain hub genes. Furthermore, pivotal lncRNAs and transcription factors were identified through competitive endogenous RNA network analysis and transcription factor enrichment analyses.Results: This study made a significant contribution by exploring the relationship between Turner syndrome genotypes and various phenotypes at the transcriptomic level. We found hub genes associated with hormone regulation, including growth hormone. Moreover, we highlighted 10 crucial X chromosome inactivation escape genes (ASMTL, CD99, DHRSX, EIF1AX, JPX, KDM5C, KDM6A, SLC25A6, XIST, and ZFX), which may serve as core pathogenic factors in Turner Syndrome. Additionally, based on our findings, we proposed certain drugs as potential new therapeutic strategies for Turner Syndrome.Conclusions: Our study provides substantial evidence supporting the correlation between genotype and various phenotypes in Turner Syndrome. Furthermore, we shed light on the potential pathogenic significance of X chromosome inactivation escape genes. 1. Background

Turner syndrome (TS) is a rare disease caused by the presence of an abnormal number or structure of a single X chromosome. It is the only survivable chromosomal monosomy syndrome in humans, with an incidence of about 1/2500 in newborns ( 1 ). The most common karyotype is 45, X in the patients ( 2 ). Complications such as gonadal dysplasia, short stature, intellectual disability, and autoimmune diseases are common in patients with TS ( 3 , 4 ). Some patients with the same karyotype show typical symptoms, whereas others have few features ( 5 ). In regard to the disease, our understanding of the relationship between genotype and phenotype was severely limited, and only a few specific candidate genes related to clinical features were identified ( 6 ). Therefore, a more in-depth study on the pathogenesis of TS is needed to be conducted.

X chromosome inactivation (XCI) means that one of the X chromosomes in female mammalian cells is silenced; as a result, the dosage of X-linked gene expression in women is the same as that in men ( 7 , 8 ). Although XCI is a chromosome-wide process, some X-linked genes escape silencing, called X chromosome inactivation escape genes ( 9 ). These include more than 15% of human X-linked genes and may be essential in female development and disease susceptibility ( 10 ). However, the underlying pathogenic mechanism responsible for the change in expression dosage of XCI escape genes remains largely unexplained.

Despite the research conducted on TS by some scholars, such as Wang ( 11 ), Cheng ( 12 ) and Zhang ( 6 ), the interpretation of TS by these studies was incomplete. Samantha’s review on how genes that escape XCI contribute to TS specifically pointed to the importance of RPS4X ( 13 ).

2. Objectives

No study has so far investigated whether the genome-wide analysis of the changes in RNA expression could explain the phenotypic characteristics of TS. This study identified the hub genes involved in the pathogenesis of TS by integrating transcriptomic analysis. Our study not only revealed the TS gene–phenotype association but also provided new insight into the pathogenic mechanism of XCI escape genes. Additionally, we proposed certain drugs as potential new therapeutic strategies for TS. The brief process of the study is shown in Figure 1.

3. Materials and Methods

3.1. Microarray Data Acquired and Processing

The gene expression datasets GSE46687 and GSE126712 were acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/) of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Dataset GSE46687 contains gene expression profiles of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 10 cases of 46, XX normal women and 26 cases of 45, X TS women. GSE126712 contains gene expression profiles of primary lymphocytes from 14 cases of 46, XX normal females and 14 cases of 45, X TS females. Another one NCT00624949 was available in ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov/). Details of the three datasets are shown in Table 1. In the GSE46687 dataset, only the karyotype of the sample was indicated, and detailed clinical information was not provided. The GSE126712 dataset included 14 females with Turner syndrome (mean age ± SD: 8.27 ± 1.83 years) and 14 age-matched typically developing female controls (mean age ± SD: 7.71 ± 1.77 years). In the NCT00624949 dataset, 36 women with 45, X (median age 46.1 years [95% CI: 42.8–49.4]) and 34 healthy, age-matched women (median age 43.9 years [95% CI: 40.2–47.5]) were recruited as controls. We performed the Robust Multi-Array Average (RMA) background correction and log2 transformation to normalize the raw gene expression data (GSE46687) using R packages “affy” and “affyPLM”. Default parameters in the algorithm are used for background correction during Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) processing. The default offset value is typically “0”, according to the Affymetrix microarray platform. Prior to bioinformatics analysis, the gene symbol annotation data (platform: GPL570) was used to match probes with analogous gene symbols. The genes with multiple deleted expression values (expression level = 0, and 20% more) were excluded. If multiple probes corresponded to the same gene, their expression levels were averaged to obtain the gene’s expression level. These data were obtained from open-access resources, and thus ethical approval was not required. In addition, we also included the results of the completed differential expression analysis of the two datasets, GSE126712 ( 6 ) and NCT00624949 ( 14 ).

3.2. Differentially Expressed Genes Analysis

Based on the processed data, we used the R package limma to further analyze and obtain differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The critical standard of DEGs for subsequent analysis was as follows: fold change (FC) ≥1.5 and adjusted P value <0.05, corrected by the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

3.3. Protein-Protein Interaction Network Analysis

The STRING ( 15 ) database shows a system-wide understanding of cellular function by consolidating known and predicted protein–protein interaction (PPI) data for a large number of organisms. DEGs were uploaded to the STRING website, and the minimum interaction score >0.4 was considered meaningful. Then, the results of PPI were downloaded. The PPI network was constructed through Cytoscape software (version 3.7.2).

3.4. Gene-Disease Correlation Analysis

DAVID ( 16 ) (https://david-bioinformatics.freeforums.net/) was used for gene–disease correlation analysis, which was mainly based on the genetic association database (GAD). The genetic association database contains genetic association data from complex diseases and disorders. Using this database, we could obtain the degree of correlation between DEGs and human diseases. The false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 indicated statistical significance.

3.5. Disease Ontology Enrichment Analysis

Disease Ontology (DO) analysis enriched related diseases based on gene sets. We could identify the diseases caused by some genes using DO analysis to clarify the correlation between genes and diseases. R package “DOSE” ( 17 ) and “enrichplot” were used for DO analysis and showing the result. FDR < 0.05 indicated statistically significance.

3.6. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes Pathway Enrichment Analysis

The Gene Ontology (GO) analysis is a frequently used method for annotating gene products and characteristics of functional features, which includes three independent ontologies: biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a knowledge base for systematically analyzing gene functions, linking genomic information with higher-order functional information. The study used R package “clusterProfiler” ( 18 ) for GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. The R package “GOplot” ( 19 ) was used to display the result. The FDR <0.05 indicated statistical significance.

3.7. Competitive Endogenous RNA Network Construction and Analysis

Competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network is a regulation model in which different RNA types regulate each other through miRNA response elements that are competitively shared. A ceRNA network was constructed stepwise as follows to explore the regulatory mechanism of lncRNAs in the X chromosome on mRNAs in DEGs. First, we used the miRcode repository (http://www.mircode.org/) to predict the interaction between lncRNAs and miRNAs. Second, we predicted the target mRNAs for miRNAs, using TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org/), miRDB (http://www.targetscan.org/), miRDB (http://mirdb.org/), and miRTarBase (http://mirtarbase.mbc.nctu.edu.tw/) databases. Subsequently, the intersections of the target mRNAs and the mRNAs in DEGs were selected for subsequent analysis. Finally, Cytoscape software (version 3.7.2) (https://cytoscape.org/) was used to construct and visualize the lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA ceRNA network, based on the aforementioned results.

3.8. Transcription Factor Enrichment Analysis

Transcription factors (TFs) are proteins that control gene expression by binding and unbinding near coding regions to regulate the transcriptional machinery. TF enrichment analysis (TFEA) prioritizes transcription factors based on the overlap between given lists of differentially expressed genes, and previously annotated TF targets assembled from multiple resources. ChIP-X Enrichment Analysis 3 (ChEA3) (https://maayanlab.cloud/chea3/) ( 20 ) is a transcription factor enrichment analysis tool that ranks TFs associated with user-submitted gene sets. In the study, online tool ChEA3 was performed for transcription factor enrichment analysis.

3.9. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) can help us identify the biological functions of candidate genes in a given dataset. The typical features in the GO database were selected as the reference genome. The results were considered significant when the P value adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg method was <0.05. R package “clusterProfiler” were used for GSEA and visualization.

3.10. Chemical-Gene Interaction Analysis

We utilized the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) ( 21 ) to explore the connection between chemicals/drugs and genes. CTD is a comprehensive database that compiles curated information on chemical-gene interactions. By utilizing this integrated data, we were able to examine the underlying mechanisms by which chemicals can either induce or mitigate diseases.

3.11. Statistics Analysis

The statistical methods involved in this study are all based on algorithms built into the R package. The moderate t-test was used to identify DEGs; Fisher’s Exact test was used to analyzed GO and KEGG annotation enrichments. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.0.1.

4. Results

4.1. Identification of X chromosome Related Differentially Expressed Genes

By comparing the PBMC transcriptome data of 10 cases of 46, XX normal women and 26 cases of 45, X TS women, 1396 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified. Among them, there were 694 up-regulated genes and 702 down-regulated genes. We identified X chromosome related DEGs (XDEGs, including 57 X chromosome genes and 289 autosomal genes) by integration analysis (PPI, ceRNA, TF network, etc.) of DEGs. Figure 2A represents the chromosomal localization of XDEGs and the regulatory network between them, and Figure 2B indicates the localization of DEGs on the X chromosome. X chromosome-related differentially expressed genes (XDEGs) were identified by integrating PPI, ceRNA, and TF network analyses. Additionally, the chromosome most affected by a single X chromosome deletion is identified through chromosomal mapping of all XDEGs and determining which chromosome contains most of these genes. This finding enhances our understanding of the possibility that a single X chromosome deletion causes other chromosome-associated disorders. The statistical results showed that, excluding the X chromosome, the top five chromosomes most closely related to XDEGs were chr19, chr1, chr2, chr17, and chr11.

4.2. DO Analysis and Gene-Disease Correlation Analysis

We could understand the potential diseases caused by X chromosome deletion through DO enrichment analysis and gene–disease correlation analysis, thus providing theoretical support for preventing and treating related symptoms. DO analysis mined 166 diseases with significant correlations (q-value < 0.05) with XDEGs. The 30 most significantly correlated diseases are shown in Figure 3. These included most of the common TS comorbidities including type 1 diabetes mellitus, hypersensitivity reaction type IV disease, Sjogren’s syndrome, coronary artery disease, thyroid gland disease, and autoimmune disease of the urogenital tract, endocrine system disease, integumentary system disease, autoimmune disease of the nervous system, kidney disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, and hematopoietic system disease.

Disease Ontology (DO) enrichment analysis of XDEGs.

We also performed a gene–disease correlation analysis based on GAD to further confirm the diseases associated with XDEGs. The results showed 5 types of diseases and 33 specific diseases associated with XDEGs. These results were in general agreement with the findings of the DO analysis. This suggested that XDEGs were important causative factors for the common phenotype of TS.

4.3. Gene Ontology and Pathway Enrichment Analysis of Xdegs

We performed functional enrichment analysis on the X chromosome–related PPI network to reflect the dysfunction caused by X chromosome deletion. To reflect the richness of gene ontology and signal pathways, R packages clusterProfiler was implemented to visualize functional annotations. In our research, 17 rich GO terms and 27 KEGG pathways were identified. The top 10 projects in GO terms and KEGG pathways enrichment are shown in Figure 4. As we have seen, in GO terms, it was mainly involved in extracellular matrix structural constituent, cytokine activity, glycosaminoglycan binding, growth factor activity, collagen binding, receptor ligand activity, signaling receptor activator activity, hydrolase activity, acting on acid phosphorus-nitrogen bonds, serine hydrolase activity and so on (Fig. 4A). In addition, we noticed enrichment of multiple signal pathways, such as staphylococcus aureus infection, pertussis, complement and coagulation cascades, chagas disease, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, hematopoietic cell lineage, malaria, primary immunodeficiency, graft-versus-host disease (Fig. 4B).

4.4. X Chromosome Related Protein-Protein Interaction Network Analysis

We performed PPI analysis on XDEGs to discover the most direct effect of a single X chromosome deletion on TS. As a result, we obtained a PPI network with 298 nodes and 3814 edges (Fig. 5A), which included 46 X chromosome proteins with 243 autosomal proteins. It suggested that the deletion of a single X chromosome led to TS caused by an insufficient dosage of related genes. The cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape could help us identify the key modules from the PPI network. Each module is given a rating to assess the importance of that module. The four modules of interest are shown in Figure 5B-5E. Modules 1-4 were rated 23.8, 14.5, 8.1, and 3.7 respectively. Our functional enrichment analysis of module 1 (Fig. 5B) revealed pathways associated with neuroactivity/neuropeptide signaling. Genes in module 2 (Fig. 5C) are mainly involved in immune response. Modules 3 (Fig. 5D) and 4 (Fig. 5E) are mainly associated with functions such as cell cycle and chromosome organization. Specifically, we identified the presence of genes closely related to growth hormone regulation, such as SST and PLCB3, in Module 1 and differential expression of GHRL and SSTR5. Gonadal development-regulated genes (TAC3, LPAR2, PROKR2, and GPRC6A) and estrogen metabolism-related genes (AREG, EREG, and GNB2) were also enriched in Module 1. The aberrant expression of these genes may be a primary cause of the main manifestations of TS, characterized by abnormalities in growth hormone and sex hormone secretion. Subsequently, we conducted a degree analysis on the PPI network to identify genes with the highest connectivity. As it is well-known, the loss of the X chromosome is pivotal in TS. Accordingly, we selected the top 10 X chromosome genes with the highest connectivity as Hub genes, namely TLR8, EIF1AX, KDM6A, KDM5C, SLC25A6, GDI1, ZFX, CD99, ASMTL, and DHRSX.

4.5. X chromosome Related ceRNA Network Construction

lncRNA is indispensable in XCI. We obtained 2 lncRNAs, 21 miRNAs, and 52 mRNAs by predicting the potential targets of lncRNAs on the X chromosome, of which 2 lncRNAs were located on the X chromosome and 52 mRNAs were DEGs of TS. We constructed a ceRNA network to explore the potential functions of the hub lncRNAs. In the network (Fig. 6), we found that 2 lncRNAs (XIST, JPX) on the X chromosome might regulate 52 mRNAs by competing with 21 miRNAs. XIST and JPX are essential long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) for X chromosome inactivation. Additionally, these two lncRNAs are closely associated with other functions. Therefore, we also conducted functional enrichment analysis on the 52 mRNAs in the network. The results indicated that ceRNA network regulated by the two lncRNAs might be related to the following functions: RNA interference, human breast cancer, myeloid leukemia, host cell, estrogen receptor, map kinase, and cell activation.

Competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network of XDEGs.

4.6. Transcription Factor Enrichment Analysis

A total of 17 TFs were found to be enriched in XDEGs, of which 2 TFs (ZFX and SHOX) were on the X chromosome and 15 TFs were on the autosomes. A transcription factor regulatory network was constructed to clarify the effects of transcription factor dysregulation on the X chromosome. In the network, we found that the transcription factor ZFX on the X chromosome could be involved in the transcription of 71 genes, whereas SHOX was involved in the transcriptional regulation of 15 genes (Fig. 7). It is noteworthy that both XIST and JPX, key lncRNAs involved in chromosome inactivation, are directly regulated by ZFX.

4.7. Identification of Genes of Interest

Based on the aforementioned analysis, the top 10 genes in the PPI network, lncRNAs in the ceRNA network, and TFs on the X chromosome were recognized as hub genes, indicating their significant involvement in the pathogenesis of TS. We verified the expression of these genes in the validation set (GSE126712 and NCT00624949). The results showed that the expression of 10 of them (ASMTL, CD99, DHRSX, EIF1AX, JPX, KDM5C, KDM6A, SLC25A6, XIST, and ZFX) was consistent with our findings (Table 2). The results of GSEA analysis suggested that these 10 genes might be involved in the phenotype of TS by performing different functions (Fig. 8). For example, the high expression of ZFX might be associated with polysomial ribosome, TBP class protein binding, RNA capping, proteasome accessory complex, and methylosome. However, the specific associations between these GO items and various TS phenotypes need further elucidation.

5. Discussion

The most common karyotype in TS is 45, X, which is a single X chromosome deletion ( 22 ). In normal females, one of the two X chromosomes undergoes random inactivation, wherein the gene expression on the inactivated X chromosome is silenced. This phenomenon is referred to as X chromosome inactivation ( 23 ). Interestingly, some genes can evade this inactivation, allowing them to continue expression. These genes are referred to as XCI escape genes, constituting approximately 20% of all X chromosome genes ( 10 ). Strictly speaking, the dosage imbalance of XCI escape genes is the key pathogenic factor in TS. Our understanding of the genotype-phenotype relationship in TS is particularly limited. Through integrated analysis of the whole genomes of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from females with normal karyotypes (46, XX) and TS females (45, X), our study aims to elucidate potential key pathogenic genes in TS and their associations with common phenotypes, revealing the critical pathogenic role of XCI escape genes in TS.

We identified 346 XDEGs, including 57 X chromosome genes and 289 autosomal genes, by integrating multiple interaction networks. This suggested a genome-wide ripple effect of X chromosome ploidy differences, which was similar to the findings of Zhang ( 6 ). The results of DO analysis based on XDEGs align with the clinical phenotype observed in TS patients. Consistent with common comorbidities in TS, such as short stature, incomplete gonadal development, autoimmune disorders ( 24 ), type 1 diabetes ( 25 - 28 ), and congenital heart diseases ( 29 ), our analysis confirms the association between these clinical manifestations and XDEGs. This correlation was further validated through subsequent analysis using the GAD. Overall, it is evident that the dysregulation of XDEGs plays a crucial role in the development of TS phenotype. In addition, we found that some autosomes exhibit strong genetic interactions with a single X chromosome, with the X-linked differentially expressed genes (XDEGs) related to chr19 being the most numerous. The XDEGs on chr19—including TICAM1 ( 30 ), FPR1 and FPR3 ( 31 ), IRF3 ( 32 ), GZMM ( 33 ), SIGLEC7, SIGLEC10, LILRA4, CD33 ( 34 ), and S1PR4 ( 35 )—interact with X-linked immune regulators such as TLR8. These genes play key roles in the fine regulation of the immune system, including recognition, activation, inhibition, and cell migration. Such interactions may contribute to the development of autoimmune diseases in patients with TS. The results of GO and KEGG enrichment analysis suggested that different phenotypes of TS might be achieved through multiple functions and pathways. Autoimmune diseases were common in patients with TS ( 24 , 36 ). This might be related to immune receptor activity. Cytokines could be involved in autoimmune and inflammatory disease pathogenesis by transducing intracellular signals via JAK and STAT ( 37 ). Recent studies showed that the immune response was suppressed in TS, drawing attention to the JAK–STAT signaling pathway ( 38 ). Luis and Souyris found that the copy number of the X-linked gene Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) influenced the innate and adaptive immune responses ( 39 , 40 ). The actual relationship between TS phenotypes and various pathways needs to be confirmed through further detailed investigation.

We identified several key modules in the PPI network. Module 1 appears to be associated with neuroendocrine functions, involving functions such as growth hormone regulation, gonadal development, and estrogen metabolism. The disorder of these functions are major symptoms in TS patients. High expression of SST inhibits the release of growth hormone. Mutations in TAC3 can cause hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and its elevated levels are associated with central precocious puberty. Cases of TS with concomitant central precocious puberty are not uncommon. Downregulation of AREG reduces the release of estrogen and progesterone. This is the first instance of discovering a correspondence between the genotype and endocrine dysfunction in TS at the transcriptional level. Aberrant expression of immune-related genes in Module 2 also explains why TS patients often experience comorbidities such as autoimmune diseases. The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis successfully identified 10 hub genes on the X chromosome. Importantly, the validity of these hub genes was further confirmed in two independent datasets, with eight of them consistently observed across all datasets. This strengthens the significance and reliability of the identified hub genes in the context of the study. KDM6A affected ovarian development in female mice by regulating Rhox6 and Rhox9 ( 41 ). Decreased dosages of KDM6A might cause congenital hyperinsulinemia in infants with TS ( 42 ). Sun’s study suggested that decreased KDM5C in women with TS led to hypogonadism ( 43 ). Studies suggested that CD99 might be involved in the development of autoimmune diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease ( 44 ), multiple sclerosis ( 45 ), and systemic lupus erythematosus ( 46 , 47 ).

The ceRNA network analysis identified two non-negligible XCI- related lncRNAs: XIST and JPX. XIST initiates the switch of XCI ( 48 ), and JPX partners with XIST to complete this inactivation process ( 49 , 50 ). Our study suggested that XIST and JPX not only played important roles in the XCI process but also might indirectly regulate other functions through the ceRNA network. Different studies suggested that the miRNA sponge activity of XIST was associated with oncogenic effects in different types of cancer ( 51 - 54 ). LncRNA-XIST protects cardiomyocytes from hypoxia-induced injury by regulating the miR-125b-Hexokinase 2 axis ( 55 ). Additionally, miR-125a has been shown to protect the myocardium in rats from injury ( 56 ). It is reasonable to speculate that the common cardiovascular diseases in TS patients result from the down-regulation of XIST, which influences cardiomyocyte damage through miR-125a and miR-125b. Furthermore, XIST could suppress diabetic peripheral neuropathy by inducing autophagy through the miR-30d-5p/SIRT1 axis ( 57 ). Hsa-miR-363 may contribute to the pathogenesis of ankylosing spondylitis, an autoimmune disease, by regulating the immune response ( 58 ). The ceRNA network analysis revealed that the JPX-hsa-miR-363 axis might be involved in the development of autoimmune diseases in patients with TS. JPX could bind to EZH2 and protect the heart from acute myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury by reducing H27K3me2 expression ( 59 ). However, the biological functions of these two XCI center lncRNAs remain to be explored more deeply and extensively.

We identified two key X chromosome TFs based on the X chromosome gene transcription factor regulatory network. Among these, ZFX showed a strong transcriptional ability to directly regulate the transcription of X chromosome genes, including the four hub genes we identified earlier: EIF1AX, KDM5C, KDM6A, and XIST. It was evident that ZFX, a key transcription factor, might exert its diverse biological functions by regulating the transcription of other key genes in the networks such as PPI and ceRNA. ZFX deficiency has long been found to cause symptoms similar to those of premature ovarian failure ( 60 ). ZFX was involved in the development of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma ( 61 ), endometrial cancer ( 62 ), leukemia ( 63 ), skin healing ( 64 ), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy ( 65 ), and other diseases. The specific biological functions of ZFX in TS need to be further investigated. Although SHOX has a remarkable transcriptional capacity, it mainly transcribes autosomal genes, not X chromosomal genes. The effect of SHOX on height in TS was well established ( 66 - 68 ). However, differential expression of SHOX was not found in the validation dataset. The confirmation of other tissue samples, such as skeletal muscle, may be needed.

The results of two independent datasets confirmed the expression levels of the hub genes we identified for TS. The GSEA analysis revealed that these genes might play various biological roles in the TS phenotype. Although several studies were conducted to initially explore these genes, the relationship between these genes and various phenotypes of TS was still unclear. The relationship between most Hub genes and the clinical characteristics of TS has been described in detail. In addition, an epigenomic study confirmed that multiple loci, including the PAR1 region (such as ASMTL), are in a hypomethylated state, which is significantly associated with the growth disorders and bone phenotype of TS ( 69 ). The core regulatory role of DHRSX in retinoic acid metabolism and embryonic development provides a molecular basis for its involvement in congenital heart malformations—especially left heart dysplasia, such as the bicuspid aortic valve and the coarctation of the aorta. It also contributes to characteristic facial morphology, including features such as epicanthus and micrognathia, in patients with TS ( 70 ). Studies have confirmed that EIF1AX gene mutations cause ovarian dysfunction by damaging the mechanism of translation initiation, strongly supporting its pathogenic role in the premature ovarian failure phenotype of TS patients ( 71 ). Haploinsufficiency of SLC25A6 is associated with metabolic disorders and defects in energy metabolism, which may partially explain common metabolic problems such as insulin resistance, obesity, and easy fatigue in patients with TS ( 72 ).

ZFX exhibited excellent criticality in both PPI and transcriptional regulatory networks. Moreover, ZFX might regulate the transcription of XIST while achieving the sponge effect of ceRNA. ZFX might play an important role in female gonadal development, immune system disorders, cardiac disorders, and sex difference–related diseases. These disorders are closely related to the multiple clinical symptoms in patients with TS. ZFX may even have the opportunity to be a target for the treatment of TS in the future due to the dosage effect of X chromosome genes. We hypothesized that TS with X chromosome chimerism or other specific karyotypes might be due to dosage incompleteness of these hub genes. The key to TS might not be the karyotype of the X chromosome, but the missing genes in it.

The early diagnosis and timely treatment of TS are crucial for improving patients’ quality of life and preserving fertility. However, both the karyotype and phenotype of TS vary, making early diagnosis challenging. The Hub genes we have identified hold promise as indicators for serum screening of TS, rather than karyotype screening. Moreover, current treatment options for TS are limited. Early use of growth hormone and timely administration of sex hormones are generally considered the main strategies ( 73 - 76 ). Our findings offer a molecular-level explanation for the efficacy of estradiol/ethinyl estradiol and progesterone in alleviating symptoms of TS. ZFX is a sex-determining factor gene ( 77 ) that is also associated with intellectual development ( 78 ). Ethinylestradiol, a synthetic estrogen, upregulates ZFX, which is hypothesized to potentially alleviate developmental and gonadal symptoms in TS patients by promoting gonadal differentiation and intellectual development. Furthermore, we anticipate that additional drugs/chemicals may serve as novel therapeutic agents for patients with TS. Of course, these possibilities need to be explored further through larger-scale clinical studies.

The study was based on the integrated analysis of the GSE46687 dataset and the validation of 2 datasets involving 72 patients with TS. It was the largest integrated study with the largest sample size included so far. But the limitations of this study warrant careful consideration. The absence of experimental validation, such as CRISPR knockouts to confirm the functional relevance of identified hub genes, restricts the robustness of the findings. As TS is a rare disease, we did not collect enough samples for experimental validation. The samples we had analyzed were derived from PBMC. Since TS is a systemic disease, other tissue samples are worth exploring. Future research could verify whether these hub genes show similar levels of downregulation or less pronounced downregulation in patients with chimeric TS, as chimerism typically alleviates TS symptoms.

6. Conclusion

This study aims to explore the association between the global gene effects of X chromosome dosage imbalance and various phenotypes in TS. For the first time, we have identified key genes at the transcriptional level that are associated with endocrine dysfunction in TS. The 10 most critical X chromosome escape genes we identified might be central to the X chromosome haploidy-related disorders. We should conduct more pioneering studies on these genes to reveal the associations between genotypes and various phenotypes of TS and the biological functions they perform. In addition, our study has identified specific molecular targets for estradiol/ethinyl estradiol and progesterone therapy in TS. Furthermore, we propose certain chemicals as potential candidates for adjuvant therapy in TS. These findings provide valuable insights into the development of therapeutic interventions for TS and offer promising avenues for further research and clinical exploration.
