Resistin alleviates lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammation in bovine alveolar macrophages by activating the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway and autophagy
Authors: Xiaoping Ma, Aining Yang, Xiaoben Fan, Hong Liu, Yu Gu, Zhisheng Wang, Hongrui Guo, Jing Fang, Hengmin Cui, Liping Gou, Junliang Deng, Dongjie Cai, Zhicai Zuo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveResistin (RETN) is an adipocyte-specific hormone that participates in metabolism and modulates cellular inflammation. Our study aimed to assess the effects of RETN treatment on autophagy and the underlying molecular and biological mechanisms in bovine alveolar macrophages (BAMs).MethodsThe optimal concentration of RETN + lipopolysaccharide (LPS) on macrophages was screened and then used to co-culture with alveolar macrophages. Autophagosomes in BAMs were examined using a transmission electron microscope (TEM). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was used to detect the mRNA expression of microtubule-associated protein light chain 3 (LC3) and p62. Western blot (WB) was used to detect the protein expressions of LC3 and p62. The distribution of LC3 and p62 proteins in the cells was observed by immunofluorescence (IF). The concentrations of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The protein expression of adenosine-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK), p-AMPK, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), and p-mTOR was detected using WB.ResultsThe treatment of BAMs with RETN or LPS increased the number of autophagosomes and the ratio of LC3II/LC3I and decreased the expression level of p62 protein. RETN treatment significantly triggered autophagy compared to LPS treatment. Moreover, the ratios of p-AMPK/AMPK and p-mTOR/mTOR were upregulated and downregulated, respectively, after RETN treatment, suggesting that AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway activation is required for RETN-mediated autophagy in BAMs. Additionally, the ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I was lower, and the concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α significantly decreased in the LPS and RETN co-treatment groups compared to the single LPS treatment group. However, both autophagy- and LPS-induced inflammation were partially alleviated by RETN treatment.ConclusionRETN can promote autophagy in BAMs by activating the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway, it may help prevent LPS-induced inflammation.

Introduction

Resistin (RETN) is a low molecular weight cysteine-rich protein initially discovered in a mouse adipose tissue by Steppan that was originally proposed as an adipocytokine involved in insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes[1,2]. However, further studies have confirmed it to be a pro-inflammatory cytokine that plays a regulatory role in many chronic inflammatory [3] and infectious diseases Mantula et al., 2018 [4]. RETN can induce cellular inflammation by activating multiple signaling pathways, such as toll-like receptor-4 (TLR4)[5], mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)[6], and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)[7], which promotes the synthesis and release of inflammatory cytokines and recruit and activate neutrophils and macrophages[8,9].

Interestingly, RETN exhibits anti-inflammatory effects under certain conditions, including lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced cellular inflammation. LPS is one of the primary factors that result in lung inflammation. However, when RETN was added to the LPS-induced inflammation model, the inflammatory response was unexpectedly inhibited by RETN (Han et al., 2018 [10]). Additionally, this phenomenon could be attributed to RETN, which competes with LPS for binding to TLR4 in the inflammatory signaling pathway, to reduce the LPS-generated cellular inflammatory response through the TLR4/NF-kB signaling pathway Tarkowski et al., 2010 [11]; [12]. Cellular inflammation is usually accompanied by autophagy, which has a dual-directional regulatory function and is vital in pro- and anti-inflammatory responses [[13], [14], [15]]. Therefore, we hypothesized that autophagy plays an important role in the ability of RETN to relieve LPS-induced inflammation and reduce the release of inflammatory cytokines; however, the specific mechanism remains unclear.

RETN treatment was recently reported to increase the autophagic flux in human breast cancer cells[16]. In contrast, autophagy was inhibited by RETN in human neuroblastoma cells[17]. Although these studies show that RETN can affect autophagy, different responses are observed when treated under different conditions. Therefore, the effects of RETN on autophagy and the underlying molecular mechanisms of RETN still require further study. The adenosine-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway is one of the classic pathways that affect autophagy, and studies have shown that RETN can inhibit or activate the activity of AMPK [16,[18], [19], [20]]. Therefore, the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway is a potential pathway through which RETN regulates autophagy.

This study aimed to assess the effects of RETN treatment on autophagy and the underlying molecular and biological mechanisms in bovine alveolar macrophages (BAMs). Furthermore, Compound C (Com C), which is a specific AMPK inhibitor, was used to explore the role of RETN in autophagy Zhou et al., 2001 [21], and an LPS-induced inflammation BAM model was established to investigate whether RETN affects inflammation by regulating autophagy.

Materials and methods

Isolation and culture of BAMs

Prokaryotic expression and purification of bovine recombinant RETN[22] were performed at the Laboratory of the College of Veterinary Medicine, Sichuan Agricultural University.

Bovine lungs were obtained through aseptic surgery, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used to flush the lung surface to remove contaminants. BAMs were obtained from intact bovine lungs, and the preparation protocol was performed as proposed by Xu et al.Xu et al., 2009 [23]. Briefly, lungs were lavaged three times with approximately 2 L of Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and filtered using a 200-mesh sterile gauze to remove tissues. Next, cells were washed three times with PBS and subsequently cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sangon, Shanghai, China), penicillin 200 U mL−1, and streptomycin 200 μg·mL−1 (Gibco, Naperville, IL, USA). The cells were incubated with 5 % carbon dioxide at 37 °C. Finally, the non-adherent cells were removed after 6 h, and the remaining cells were digested, resuspended, and adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/well.

After 6 h, the cells were washed three times with the culture medium to remove non-adherent cells and cultured in serum-free DMEM. In the first group, BAMs were treated with different concentrations of RETN (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. SRP4560, Louis, MO, USA) (0 ng mL−1, 25 ng mL−1, 50 ng mL−1, and 100 ng mL−1) for 12 h. BAMs were treated with 100 ng mL−1 RETN for 0, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h in the second group. In the third group, BAMs were treated with 100 ng mL−1 RETN for 12 h after pretreatment with 10 μM Compound C (Com C) (MedChemExpress, MCE) for 1 h. The AMPK inhibitor, Com C, was initially dissolved in PBS and further diluted with DMEM to a final concentration of 10 μM. Finally, in the fourth group, BAMs were treated with 1 μg mL−1 LPS (Solarbio, cat. L8880, Beijing, China) and 100 ng mL−1 RETN for 12 h.

BAMs survival and phagocytosis rates

BAM suspension was mixed with an equal volume of 0.4 % trypan blue (Solarbio, Beijing, China). After 2–3 min staining, the stained and unstained cells were counted to calculate the survival rate. BAM suspension was pipetted and seeded in 24-well plates at a cell density of 1 × 105 mL−1. In total, 100 μL of ink (Solarbio, Beijing, China), autoclaved and diluted 10 times in PBS), was added to each well after culturing for 2 h in a cell culture incubator, and an equal volume of PBS was added to the control group. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. In each test group, BAMs from 10 different visual fields were randomly selected under a transmission electron microscope (TEM). Finally, the number of autophagosomes in BAMs was counted by three laboratory personnel, and the average value was calculated.

Transmission electron microscopy

BAMs were incubated as described above. Briefly, BAMs were washed three times with PBS and collected using a cell scraper. Next, the cells were centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min. Precipitated BAMs were fixed in ice-cold 3 % glutaraldehyde (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and post-fixed in 1 % osmium tetroxide (Leica). After dehydration in an ascending series of ethanol (30, 50, 70, 80, 90, and 100 %) for 10 min each, the samples were subsequently embedded in Epon epoxy resin (Beijing Zhongjingkeyi Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Sections were cut to a thickness of 50–60 nm and stained with 0.1 % lead citrate (Beijing Zhongjingkeyi Technology Co., Ltd) and 10 % uranyl acetate (Beijing Zhongjingkeyi Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Finally, they were examined under a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400PLUS, JEOL), and images were recorded on a film at 15000 × magnification.

Western blot

BAMs were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) (Servicebio, cat. G2002-100, Wuhan, China) lysis buffer with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (MedChemExpress, MCE). The protein concentrations were measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (ServiceBio, cat. G2026-200T. Wuhan, China). Next, the proteins were separated using sodium dodecyl-sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked in a non-fat dry milk solution for 30 min. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-AMPK (CST, cat. #2532, 1:1000, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-phosphorylated-AMPK (p-AMPK) (Affinity, cat. AF3423, 1:1000, Cincinnati, OH, USA), anti-mTOR (Bioss, cat. bs-1992R, 1:1000, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-phosphorylated-mTOR (p-mTOR) (Affinity, cat. AF3308, 1:1000 dilution, Cincinnati, OH, USA), anti-microtubule-associated protein light chain 3 (LC3) A (Novus Biologicals, cat. NB100-2331, 1:1000, Cambridge, UK), and anti-p62 (GeneTex Inc, cat. GTX100685, 1:1000, Irvine, California, USA) primary antibodies in a dilution buffer. After washing with Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween, the membranes were cultured with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody (Servicebio, cat. GB23303; 1:3000, Wuhan, China) at room temperature for 30 min. Finally, the membranes were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence Western blot system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Immunofluorescence

BAMs were seeded in 20 mm glass-bottomed microwell dishes. After culturing as described above, BAMs were washed three times with PBS and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, cat. #G1101, Wuhan, China) for 15 min. Next, 0.1 % TritonX-100 (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was used for cell permeabilization for 20 min. The cells were blocked with 3 % BSA (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) in PBS at room temperature for 30 min and subsequently cultured with anti-LC3A (Novus Biologicals, cat, NB100-2331, 1:100, Cambridge, UK) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, cells were cultured with a secondary antibody (Servicebio, cat, GB21303, 1:300, Wuhan, China) at room temperature for 1 h. After washing with PBS, the procedures were repeated using anti-p62 (GeneTex Inc., cat. GTX100685, 1:500, CA, USA) and secondary antibodies (Servicebio, cat, GB25303, 1:400, Wuhan, China). The 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Servicebio, cat. #G1012, Wuhan, China) counterstain was used to visualize the BAM nuclei. Finally, the stained BAMs were observed under an immunofluorescence microscope (NIKON ECLIPSE C1).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Release of interleukin (IL)-1β (cat. SMLB00C), IL-6 (cat. S6050B), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (cat. SMTA00B) into the culture medium was measured using ELISA kits obtained from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA). Protocols and data processing were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. For each target protein, a standard curve of the concentration versus optical density was established to generate a linear regression equation, which was used to quantify the concentration of each cytokine released. The OD value was obtained at 450 nm on the enzyme marker (Roche, Switzerland). Each experiment was independently repeated in triplicate to measure cytokine expression.

Statistical analyses

All experiments were performed at least three times. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). Differences in the means were determined using a one-way analysis of variance for multiple comparisons, followed by the least significant difference test for two-group comparisons among the multiple comparisons. P-values of less than 0.05 and 0.01 were considered statistically significant (∗P < 0.05) and highly statistically significant (∗∗P < 0.01), respectively.

The survival rate of cells was 98 % after trypan blue staining. As BAMs have a phagocytic function, we added ink to the BAM culture system, and the culture was continued. Analyzing the autophagosomal structures of BAMs and comparing the changes in their number using TEM showed that autophagosome formation increased with increased RETN concentration, implying that RETN induced autophagosome formation (Fig. 2E). Effect of RETN treatment time on autophagy in BAMs

We directly observed the changes in autophagosomes in BAMs under a TEM to explore the effect of different RETN treatment times on autophagy in BAMs. WB results showed that compared to the blank control (0 h), the ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I in the 6- and 12-h treatment groups was significantly upregulated (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively) but was downregulated in the 18- and 24-h treatment groups (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3B), with an increase in RETN treatment time. Meanwhile, the expression level of p62 protein significantly decreased in all experimental groups compared to that in the blank control, particularly in the 12-h treatment group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3C). Moreover, as the treatment time increased, the intensity of the red fluorescence-labeled LC3 in the experimental groups increased, while the green fluorescence-labeled p62 decreased macroscopically (Fig. 2D). RETN treatment recruited LC3-II, which appeared as red-fluorescent LC3-II puncta during autophagosome formation (Fig. 3D). In each test group, the number of autophagosomes in BAMs was counted, and the TEM result illustrated that the autophagosomes formed in the 12-, 18-, and 24-h treatment groups were significantly higher than those formed in the 0- and 6-h treatment groups (Fig. 3E). Additionally, LC3B mRNA expression level increased with increasing RETN treatment duration (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, p62 mRNA levels increased in the 6- and 12-h treatment groups, with 12 h being the most significant (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3G). RETN induces autophagy in BAMs by activating the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway

BAMs were pretreated with Com C, which is a specific AMPK inhibitor, to further determine whether RETN-induced autophagy resulted from AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway activation. Additionally, the ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I decreased in the single Com C pretreatment group compared to that in the single RETN treatment group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B). The expression level of p62 protein also decreased in the single Com C pretreatment group compared to that in the blank control (P < 0.01); however, it increased compared to those in the single RETN and Com C + RETN treatment groups (P < 0.01 and P < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 4C). It appears that Com C induced autophagy in BAMs. Moreover, the ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I in the Com C + RETN treatment group decreased compared to that in the single RETN treatment group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4B), while the expression level of p62 protein increased (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4C). These results indicated that Com C inhibited RETN-induced autophagy. As shown in Fig. 4D, the p-AMPK level was inhibited by the pretreatment with Com C, while the p-mTOR level was increased. Simultaneously, almost no changes were observed in the total AMPK and mTOR protein expression levels in the different groups. Therefore, RETN-induced upregulation of the p-AMPK/AMPK ratio and downregulation of the p-mTOR/mTOR ratio were reversed by Com C (Fig. 4D and E). Under an IF microscope, the intensity of the red fluorescence-labeled LC3 in the experimental groups increased and that of the green fluorescence-labeled p62 macroscopically decreased compared to those in the blank control (Fig. 4F). As shown in Fig. 4G, analysis of the autophagosomal structures of BAMs using TEM revealed that autophagosome formation increased in the experimental groups compared to that in the blank control; however, autophagosome formation decreased in the Com C + RETN treatment group compared to that in the single RETN treatment group, implying that Com C inhibited the RETN-induced increase in autophagosome formation. The mRNA expression levels of LC3B and p62 in the single Com C pretreatment group increased compared to those in the blank control (P < 0.01 and P < 0.01, respectively) and decreased compared to those in the single RETN treatment group and Com C + RETN treatment group (P < 0.01 and P < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 4H and I). The mRNA expression level of p62 decreased compared to that of the single RETN treatment group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4H and I). RETN reduces the LPS-induced inflammation in BAMs by regulating autophagy

Next, we explored the role of RETN in LPS-induced inflammation. WB showed that the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio in the LPS and/or RETN treatment groups increased (P < 0.01) compared to that in the control, which was more significant in the single LPS treatment group (Fig. 5A and B). The expression level of the p62 protein decreased (P < 0.01), and this was more significant in the LPS and RETN co-treatment group (Fig. 5A–C). RETN attenuated LPS-induced autophagy in BAMs. Moreover, the intensity of red fluorescence-labeled LC3 in the experimental groups macroscopically increased, whereas that of green fluorescence-labeled p62 reduced (Fig. 5D). LPS and RETN treatments prominently triggered the formation of the characteristic red fluorescent LC3-II puncta, suggesting the recruitment of LC3-II during autophagosome formation (Fig. 5D). Changes in autophagosomes in BAMs were directly observed using TEM. As shown in Fig. 5E, after treatment with LPS and RETN, the average number of autophagosomes in BAMs formed in the LPS and/or RETN treatment groups was significantly higher than that in the blank control. Compared to the blank control, the mRNA expression level of LC3B and p62 in the experimental groups increased (P < 0.01 and P < 0.01, respectively), and this was significant in the LPS and RETN co-treatment group (Fig. 5F and G). Furthermore, we examined the inflammatory status after co-treatment with LPS and RETN. Notably, LPS and RETN treatment prominently promoted the serum secretion of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, and this was significant in a single LPS treatment group (P < 0.01, P < 0.01, and P < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 5H–J). However, compared to the single LPS treatment group, the concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the LPS and RETN co-treatment group showed a downward trend (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 5H–J). Discussion

RETN dose-dependently promotes autophagy in LPS-induced BAMs

Autophagy is an essential lysosome-mediated bulk degradation pathway in cellular survival, development, and homeostasis Mizushima and Komatsu, 2011 [24]. Although recent investigations have revealed that RETN can stimulate or inhibit autophagy in different cell types, this mechanism remains unclear in unproven cell types [16,17]. Notably, this study's results showed that RETN treatment promoted autophagy in BAMs, consistent with those of Liu's study, which showed that RETN could sufficiently trigger autophagy[16]. LC3 is a mammalian homolog of yeast Atg 8 that contains water-soluble LC3-I and fat-soluble LC3-II. During autophagy, LC3 is cleaved by Atg 4 to yield LC3-I, which is subsequently conjugated to PE by Atg 7 and Atg 3 to form LC3-II, which binds to the autophagosome membrane's surface [25]. Changes in autophagy are typically detected using the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio. Meanwhile, p62 is a selective autophagy receptor that degrades the ubiquitinated substrates. It can promote the assembly and removal of ubiquitylated proteins by forming protein aggregates, mediate their encapsulation in autophagosomes, and finally degrade with autophagosomes Harper and Schulman, 2006 [26]. Therefore, a decrease in the expression level of p62 protein is considered a sign of enhanced autophagy. Our data showed that an increase in the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and a decrease in the expression level of p62 protein could directly indicate autophagy occurrence. However, in this study, the qRT-PCR and western blotting results of p62 did not show the same trend as those of LC3 gene expression, which also supported the assessment of autophagy. Although the mRNA expression level of p62 increased, the degradation rate of its protein also increased with autophagy occurrence. This phenomenon resulted in an increase in the mRNA expression level of p62 after RETN treatment, whereas its protein level decreased. Therefore, this study mainly assessed autophagy based on the western blotting results, whereas those of the qRT-PCR were used for auxiliary verification. RETN treatment prominently triggered autophagosome formation, confirming that RETN induces autophagy. Similar to our results, in a study using rat glaucoma models, allopregnanolone was found to promote autophagy by upregulating the mRNA expression levels of LC3B and p62, consequently increasing the ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I and reducing the expression level of p62 protein Ishikawa et al., 2020 [27]. Furthermore, the results showed that the ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I was upregulated, and the expression level of p62 protein decreased with an increase in RETN concentration. During the initial 12 h of RETN treatment of BAMs, the ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I was upregulated, and the expression level of p62 protein decreased with increasing RETN treatment time. Although many autophagosomes in BAMs were observed under the TEM at 18 and 24 h, the ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I was significantly downregulated, and the expression level of p62 protein increased, indicating the suppression of autophagy, which was most likely initiated because the long-term treatment with RETN and serum-free culture may injure the normal physiological functions of BAMs. Summarily, RETN dose-dependently promoted autophagy in BAMs, and the best treatment time was 12 h [16]. found that RETN treatment significantly increased autophagic flux dose- and time-dependently in adriamycin-treated breast cancer cells, which slightly differs from our study results and might be the outcome of RETN treatment in different cells.

RETN promotes autophagy in BAMs by activating the AMPK signaling pathway

Next, we explored the molecular mechanisms underlying RETN-mediated promotion of autophagy in BAMs. The AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway is a classic signaling pathway of autophagy that plays an important role in increasing skeletal muscle glucose uptake and enhancing insulin sensitivity O'Neill, 2013 [28]; [29]. RETN, as a cytokine related to insulin resistance, reportedly can affect the activity of AMPK Liu et al., 2016 [30]; [20]. Under normal circumstances, mTOR is highly phosphorylated and autophagy is inhibited. After RETN treatment, the protein expression levels of p-AMPK and p-mTOR were significantly upregulated and downregulated, respectively. Therefore, we used Com C, which is an AMPK inhibitor, to further determine the role of the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway in RETN-induced autophagy. Although Com C can reduce cells' sensitivity to adenosine triphosphate and promote autophagy to a certain extent, it is extremely weak compared to the degree of RETN-induced autophagy [31]. After Com C pretreatment, AMPK and mTOR phosphorylation were inhibited and enhanced, respectively, indicating that the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway was suppressed. Consistent with our results, Kyung-Min et al.Kim et al., 2017 [32] found that Com C suppressed AMPK phosphorylation and its downstream targets in mouse alveolar macrophages. Although Com C pretreatment suppressed RETN-induced autophagy, the red fluorescence-labeled LC3 showed no obvious change, whereas the green fluorescence-labeled p62 was weakened. Additionally, the average number of autophagosomes in BAMs decreased significantly after Com C treatment. Therefore, these results suggest that RETN-promoted autophagy in BAMs is due to the activation of the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway (Fig. 6).Fig. 6RETN promotes autophagy in BAMs by activating the AMPK signaling pathway. RETN-induced autophagy relieved LPS-induced inflammation in BAMs

Furthermore, we explored whether RETN affects LPS-induced inflammation. Notably, single LPS or RETN treatment both promoted autophagy and inflammation in BAMs, and the inflammatory cytokine levels in the LPS and RETN co-treatment group were lower than those in the single LPS treatment group, indicating that RETN and LPS simultaneously acted on BAMs and that RETN inhibited LPS-induced cellular inflammation to a certain degree, consistent with the results of Han et al.(Han et al., 2018). Autophagy, as a mechanism of cell self-protection, is significantly activated in inflammatory diseases, relieves inflammation, and regulates cell damage Panigrahi et al., 2019 [33]. However, excessive activation of autophagy in inflammatory diseases can induce cellular disorders [34,35]. Therefore, only moderate autophagy can benefit organisms [36,37]. In this study, the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and expression level of p62 protein in the LPS and RETN co-treatment group were lower than those in the single LPS treatment group. Additionally, the mRNA expression levels of LC3B and p62 in the single LPS treatment group were lower than those in the LPS and RETN co-treatment groups. Consequently, it is speculated that LPS-induced inflammation leads to excessive autophagy and that the basic functions of BAMs are disturbed, blocking the synthesis of the autophagy-related mRNA gene and the p62 protein degradation. The presence of RETN inhibited excessive LPS-induced autophagy and promoted positive autophagy, which played a role in alleviating and protecting BAMs. Similarly, Ziang et al.[34] found that saturated hydrogen saline ameliorates LPS-induced acute lung injury by reducing excessive autophagy. Although RETN-induced autophagy relieved LPS-induced inflammation in BAMs, further investigation is required to elucidate the detailed mechanism of autophagy in this process.

Limitations and strengths of this study

This study showed that RETN induced autophagy in BAMs by activating the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway. Therefore, we proposed the possibility that RETN alleviates the excessive autophagy in BAMs induced by LPS to relieve LPS-induced inflammation. Thus, this study may have a positive impact that revealed that RETN may be an important target for the treatment of bovine pneumonia. However, this study had some limitations. Firstly, although we demonstrated the functional role of RETN in autophagy in BAMs through the experiments mentioned above, further studies are still required. Furthermore, whether RETN-induced autophagy can protect BAMs is unclear. Finally, the study was performed in an in vitro setting; therefore, future studies are needed to investigate the effects of RETN using in vivo animal models.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that RETN promoted autophagy in BAMs by activating the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway. Therefore, based on these findings, it can be inferred that RETN can relieve LPS-induced inflammation by regulating autophagy, which provides new insights into the amelioration of LPS-induced inflammation in BAMs and indicates that RETN may be a novel strategy to treat LPS-induced lung inflammation.
