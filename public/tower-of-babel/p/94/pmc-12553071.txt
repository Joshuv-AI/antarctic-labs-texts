The ABCs of PEMs: Using Artificial Intelligence to Enhance the Readability of Patient Educational Materials in Pediatric Orthopaedics
Authors: Oceane Mauffrey, Shaian Lashani, Jessica H. Heyer, Stephanie Logterman, Christine M. Goodbody, Stuart L. Mitchell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile the American Medical Association and National Institute of Health recommend patient educational materials (PEMs) be written at a 6th-grade reading level, studies consistently show that PEMs in orthopaedics are written at the 10th-grade level or higher. This mismatch disproportionately affects patients with limited health literacy, who are at increased risk for poor clinical outcomes. This study investigates the potential of artificial intelligence (AI) platforms, including ChatGPT and OpenEvidence, to generate PEMs in pediatric orthopaedics that reach readability standards without sacrificing clinical accuracy.MethodsFifty-one of the most common pediatric orthopaedic conditions were selected using the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons OrthoInfo PEM database. For each condition, PEMs were generated using two AI platforms: ChatGPT 4 and Open Evidence utilizing a standardized prompt requesting a sixth-grade level explanation that included relevant anatomy, symptoms, physical exam findings, and treatment options. Readability was assessed using eight validated readability metrics via the Python Textstat library. PEMs were scored for accuracy and completeness by four blinded, pediatric orthopaedic surgeons. Interrater reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), and statistical comparisons were performed using paired t-tests.ResultsChatGPT-generated PEMs had the lowest average reading grade level (8.7) compared to OrthoInfo (10.8) and Open Evidence (10.1). OrthoInfo PEMs were rated highest for accuracy and completeness (total accuracy: 6.95; total completeness: 6.98), compared to Chat GPT (total accuracy: 6.15; total completeness: 5.90) and Open Evidence (total accuracy: 3.25; total completeness: 3.05), but ChatGPT approached OrthoInfo in several subdomains, including treatment descriptions, timeline, and follow-up recommendations.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the promise of AI platforms in generating readable, patient-friendly educational materials in pediatric orthopaedics. While OrthoInfo remains the gold standard in content accuracy and completeness, it falls short of national readability guidelines. AI tools like ChatGPT and OpenEvidence produced significantly more readable PEMs and, in some categories, approached the quality of expert-validated materials. These findings suggest a potential role for AI-assisted content creation in bridging the health literacy gap. However, concerns surrounding accuracy, hallucinations, and source transparency must be addressed before AI-generated PEMs can be safely integrated into clinical practice.Key Concepts(1)Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems capable of performing tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as language processing; in this study, AI was used to revise and assess the readability of patient education materials.(2)Patient education materials (PEMs) are written or visual tools designed to inform patients and families about medical conditions, treatments, and procedures; they play a critical role in supporting shared decision-making in pediatric orthopaedics.(3)Readbility refers to how easily a written text can be understood by a target audience; improving the readability of PEMs ensures that patients and caregivers can comprehend essential health information.(4)Health literacy is the ability of individuals to obtain, process, and understand basic health information needed to make informed decisions; enhancing the readability of PEMs supports improved health literacy in pediatric populations.(5)Natural language processing (NLP) is a branch of AI that enables computers to understand and generate human language; in this study, NLP was used to revise PEMs and improve their readability and accessibility.Level of EvidenceIV

Introduction

The average reading level in the United States is eighth grade [1]. The American Medical Association (AMA) and the National Institute of Health (NIH) recommend that patient educational materials (PEMs) are written to a sixth-grade reading level [2]. Despite this, data show that PEMs across medical specialties are written to much higher reading levels, rendering them incomprehensible to patients with lower health literacy [[3], [4], [5]]. In surgical specialties in particular, studies have demonstrated that patients with lower health literacy have worse clinical outcomes with more postoperative complications, higher readmission rates, and greater risk of mortality [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

In orthopaedics, organizations such as the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) have tried to make patient educational resources more accessible by creating online materials with detailed explanations and corresponding images and diagrams. Nevertheless, the readability of these texts remains far above the recommended average [1,[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. A study of 176 PEMs in pediatric orthopaedics published by the AAOS, the Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America (POSNA), and the American Academy of Pediatrics found that none were at the appropriate sixth-grade level and only seven (4%) of the articles were written below the eighth-grade level [15]. Creating PEMs that are accessible and accurate remains a challenge as it can be time-consuming and is costly to adjust each informational material to the specific needs of each patient [16]. When the gap between physician-validated patient information and patient comprehension is so high, patients have historically turned to online search engines for simpler answers. Over 70% of patients with a new medical diagnosis have reported using search engines such as Google for health-related queries [17]. In recent years, many of these search engines have partnered with artificial intelligence (AI) companies to streamline simplified and tailored responses to complex questions [18]. As more patients are turning their health-related queries toward AI, it becomes essential to evaluate whether these platforms are better than current physician organizations at creating readable materials and whether the information they produce is accurate and complete. Though several studies have assessed the ability of AI to simplify PEMs, few have explored these trends specifically in pediatric orthopaedics and none have systematically assessed the quality of the content produced. The present study assessed the reading level of 51 pediatric orthopaedic PEMs from the AAOS OrthoInfo website and compared them to AI-generated PEMs. PEMs for 10 of the most common pediatric orthopaedic conditions were selected and assessed for accuracy and completeness by pediatric fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons.

Methods

Institutional review board approval was not required for this study. Fifty-one of the most common pediatric orthopaedic conditions were selected. The list of conditions was generated using the AAOS PEM webpages. ChatGPT 4 and Open Evidence were used as the sample AI platforms. Open Evidence is a new AI platform designed specifically for medical practitioners (physicians, advanced practice providers, etc.) which provides responses using peer-reviewed references and is therefore more transparent than ChatGPT.

A specific prompt was used to generate uniform responses from all AI platforms: “Create a detailed patient information sheet for the general patient explaining [CONDITION], and include points such as anatomy, symptoms, physical examination, and treatment (both surgical and nonsurgical). Please provide this information at a sixth-grade reading level.”

Readability scores were calculated through the Python library Textstat. The readability scores utilized are listed in Table 1 below. The scores yielded correspond to the reading grade with any score greater than or equal to 0.5 rounding up to the next grade level. The Flesch Reading Ease score is a different metric than the others as it operates on a 100-point scale with a greater score indicating greater ease of readability. Paired t-tests were used to compare the mean readability of two groups. P-values <.05 were considered significant. A three-point scoring scale was modified from a prior study to assess the accuracy and completeness of PEMs (Appendix 1). This scale was first validated by a pediatric orthopaedic surgeon comparing the AI generated and OrthoInfo PEMs for a single condition. Storino et al. conducted a study assessing the readability of online materials for patients with pancreatic cancer and developed a 5-point scoring system evaluating the percent of accurate statements in each text; a score of 1 corresponded to 25% accurate statements ranging up to a score of 5 indicating 100% accurate statements [19]. In this study, the scoring system was modified with the addition of a completeness variable to capture errors of omission and was converted to a three-point scale.

From the list of 51 conditions, the surgeon selected the 10 most common pediatric orthopaedic conditions. The scoring system and PEMs were then distributed to four pediatric board certified orthopaedic surgeons who were blinded and rated the PEMs from each source for the 10 chosen conditions. The scoring scale was assessed for interrater reliability using the intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC (2,1)] based on a two-way random effects model for absolute agreement. The ICC was 0.71 (95% CI: 0.60-0.76), indicating good agreement across four raters scoring 300 items on a 3-point scale (0-2). Most variance was attributable to differences between PEMs (mean square rows = 1.43), with lower variance due to rater effects (mean square columns = 0.17) and residual error (mean square error = 0.17).

When comparing individual reading metrics, the two AI models largely outperformed the existing PEMs on OrthoInfo. Open Evidence received more favorable reading scores compared to OrthoInfo. No statistical difference was found between Open Evidence and OrthoInfo on Coleman–Liau, Dale–Chall readability score, and Gunning Fog. In contrast, ChatGPT outperformed OrthoInfo on every readability metric. ChatGPT also outperformed Open Evidence across multiple readability metrics. There was no statistical significance between ChatGPT and Open Evidence in Linsear Write Formula scores.

Though ChatGPT scored most favorably on readability metrics, it was also the resource with the greatest average number of difficult words with significantly more words than OrthoInfo and Open Evidence. Open Evidence had the lowest number of difficult words with significantly less words than OrthoInfo as well.

OrthoInfo was more highly rated than both AI platforms in accuracy and completeness (Table 3). OrthoInfo outperformed ChatGPT overall in accuracy and completeness, although subgroup-analysis of each category revealed that ChatGPT did not differ from OrthoInfo significantly in descriptions of treatment, nor in timeline of disease course, and follow-up. Discussion

The present study demonstrates that AI models were able to generate PEMs closer to the recommended sixth-grade level when compared to the AAOS OrthoInfo PEMs, although the AI models were varied in their ability to create patient information that was accurate and complete.

Orthopaedic health literacy

Though several organizations such as the NIH and the AMA have recommended PEMs be written between the fifth and sixth-grade level, simplifying complex medical material without sacrificing necessary information remains a challenge. It may be costly and inefficient for physicians to create different educational materials tailored to the needs of individual patients but patient care suffers when the gap between their understanding and the level of available resources is too wide. A survey study in orthopaedic trauma assessed patient understanding at their first postoperative visit. Only 47.9% of patients knew which bone they fractured and 18.5% knew their healing time [5]. A subsequent study at the same institution found survey scores improved significantly in those who were provided with an informational handout in addition to regular discharge instructions. Patients with the handout were 1.3 times more likely to know which bone was fractured than those without [6]. Creating patient information that patients can easily understand, continuously reference, and reliably access is an essential element of providing high quality medical care. This issue is particularly salient for pediatric orthopaedic patients whose care, in addition to surgical intervention, may require complex brace wearing regimens, physical therapy protocols, or serial casting. Studies have shown that literacy on musculoskeletal conditions is even more limited than general health literacy. Of 248 patients presenting to the emergency department for an orthopaedic injury, 69% of the patients were identified as having low musculoskeletal literacy and 48% had low general health literacy [20]. A questionnaire provided to 133 orthopaedic patients found that only 33% of patients were able to correctly define “fractured bone,” even when given multiple choices, only 51% were able to select the correct definition [21]. This underscores the urgent need for national orthopaedic associations to produce PEMs that are readily accessible and understandable for patients from diverse educational backgrounds.

Concerns associated with artificial intelligence

The present study found that OrthoInfo, one of the mainstream sources for PEMs, provides PEMs at an 11th-grade reading level overall. Not only is this far above the level recommended but also two grade levels above the national reading average. Both AI platforms outperformed OrthoInfo in producing readable PEMs. Open Evidence produced PEMs at an average 10th-grade level and ChatGPT at the 9th-grade level. Though both AI platforms came closer to the sixth-grade standard, several concerns over using AI remain. These platforms are known for their ability to synthesize and simplify complex information, but they may also hallucinate, a term coined to describe the confabulation of AI when presented with a gap in information. This is particularly concerning when the erroneous information produced is being used to inform medical decision-making [22,23]. Moreover, ChatGPT, unlike Open Evidence, does not provide transparency in its sources; for any given response the platform may equally draw from a reputable study and an online blog. There are also ethical discussions regarding the potential for plagiarism when text is analyzed and potentially replicated by a large language model such as ChatGPT with no credit to the original author [24,25]. In these regards, Open Evidence, though not producing PEMs at the same readability as ChatGPT, was still improved from the existing resources, and provides peer-reviewed references, thereby reducing some of the transparency concerns surrounding AI.

Strengths of artificial intelligence

As expected, our study found that pediatric orthopaedic surgeons rated OrthoInfo as the highest in accuracy and in completeness across every category. Interestingly, our study found that ChatGPT scored more favorably in accuracy and completeness when compared to Open Evidence. In fact, ChatGPT scored similarly to OrthoInfo in description of treatment options, timeline of disease course, and follow-up guidelines. Both AI platforms were not able to reach the level of detail provided by the OrthoInfo PEMs in describing the condition and weighing the risks and benefits of various interventions. This difference is an important consideration as these two categories require a great amount of knowledge and clinical experience, and are likely better left addressed in a visit with a clinical expert than on even the most detailed PEM. Other studies have supported these findings, indicating that ChatGPT has adequate orthopaedic knowledge to provide grossly accurate responses to patient questions across multiple subspecialties in orthopaedics [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. One assessment compared ChatGPT’s ability to answer more complex clinical questions from the Orthopaedics In-Training Exam. The model performed at a level comparable to that of a first-year orthopaedic resident even without the supplementary materials such as images and x-rays provided in several of the question stems [30]. If AI platforms can adequately educate patients on the basics of their condition and the associated treatment options at a level they can understand, in addition to informing them of when it is appropriate to seek further clinical care, this already represents a significant advantage over the existing, largely inaccessible resources.

Limitations

Though our study created a scoring instrument to assess the quality of PEMs, several limitations remain. Firstly, the reading scores used have been validated as tools for reading grade levels but may not correspond to health literacy or patient comprehension. Further studies should involve survey analysis of pediatric orthopaedic patients to more accurately assess their understanding. In the future, a panel of patients and their family members could be created to assess not only their understanding of materials but how useful they find specific content in making decisions regarding their child’s condition. Moreover, though the pediatric orthopaedic surgeons were blinded while grading the materials, those generated by AI were noticeably more succinct than those produced by OrthoInfo, introducing a potential bias in the assessment of their quality. In addition, the surgeons selected to review the PEMs were trained in the same pediatric orthopaedic fellowship, and although they are in various practice settings throughout the country, their common fellowship may introduce a bias in their assessment of PEM content. Finally, both ChatGPT and Open Evidence were only provided with one prompt per condition; this study did not assess the consistency of AI in creating PEMs. Though each AI platform assessed over 50 conditions, it is important to consider that responses may vary in quality for each condition depending on how prompts are phrased, how many times they are asked, and the generation of the AI model being utilized (eg, ChatGPT 3.5 vs 4).

Despite these concerns, this study shows the power of AI in potentially reducing large discrepancies in patient education. Existing PEMs in pediatric orthopaedics are validated by experts in the field and provide a detailed overview of complex orthopaedic diagnoses. Many patients, unfortunately, are not equipped with the health literacy required to read, understand, and extrapolate information from these materials to apply to important decisions regarding the healthcare of their child. It is imperative for pediatric orthopaedic surgeons and large orthopaedic associations such as POSNA and the AAOS to recognize and address the significant gap in understandable and accurate information for patients and their families in pediatric orthopaedics. Though ChatGPT and Open Evidence varied in the accuracy and completeness of the materials they generated, ChatGPT, in particular, proved competent at explaining both surgical and nonsurgical treatment options, the timeline of each pathology, and the recommendations for follow-up care. Both patients and providers should utilize these tools with caution ensuring that content is appropriately vetted and verified whenever possible to prevent misinformed medical decision-making. AI cannot replace the clinical expertise and nuance of a trained pediatric orthopaedic surgeon but these models may help to provide patients with the background understanding they need to optimize their time in clinic and know when to seek out an orthopaedic provider, and which questions to ask, helping them make more informed decisions for their child.
