Amine-Functionalized Lignin for CO2 CapturePart 2: A Double Amine Grafting Strategy
Authors: Hadi Shayesteh, Abdelhamid Sayari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Global warming has been exacerbated by the escalating emissions of greenhouse gases from fossil fuel power plants and other industrial and transportation sources. Postcombustion CO2 capture by amine-containing materials has evolved into a burgeoning industry for mitigating point-source emissions. Nevertheless, the deployment of sustainable and biodegradable solid-supported amine adsorbents remains underexplored despite their tunable surface chemistry that can be leveraged for CO2 adsorption. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to valorize lignin for CO2 capture using a double amine functionalization method. Lignin was subjected to two separate grafting procedures: one with diethylenetriamine (DETA) to produce DETA-aminated lignin (DAL) and another procedure involving 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane (APTMS) and triaminosilane (TRI) to make APTMS- and TRI-grafted lignin. Subsequently, DAL was further grafted by TRI (TRI/DAL) to increase amine content and improve CO2 capture. TRI/DAL was found to achieve a considerably higher CO2 uptake than that of individually modified materials. In the presence of 15% dry CO2/N2 at 25 °C, CO2 uptake reached 1.31 mmol/g, representing a 72% increase compared to DAL (0.76 mmol/g) and more than 250% of TRI/Lignin-1 (0.37 mmol/g). Humidity further promoted CO2 capture, with a CO2 uptake of 1.84 mmol/g and an amine efficiency of 0.43 mol CO2/mol N at 55% relative humidity (RH). In addition to enhancing CO2 uptake, the presence of moisture improved the cyclic stability of the material, with TRI/DAL retaining 97.5 and 95% of its initial capacity after ten cycles at 25 and 50 °C in the presence of 15% CO2/N2 with 55% RH.

Introduction

The anthropogenic CO2 emissions and their deleterious impact on climate change have been among the most serious environmental concerns, particularly over the past two decades. , CO2 emissions from the combustion of fossil fuels, which represent a significant contribution to global warming, are predicted to increase substantially over the next years. Nevertheless, the complete elimination of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions seems unfeasible. Therefore, carbon capture technology provides a short-term solution for mitigating GHG emissions, while we transition toward more sustainable sources of energy. − Postcombustion CO2 capture includes techniques such as adsorption, absorption, membrane, cryogenic, and hydrate-based separation methods, which can be implemented by installing CO2 capture units within existing power plant facilities and other industrial sites. − More importantly, the recovered pure CO2 may be transformed into value-added chemicals or used in different industrial processes.

While CO2 scrubbing (absorption) using amine-based solvents has been practiced commercially owing to the high chemical affinity of amines toward CO2, , this technology suffers from inherent shortcomings, including high energy consumption during regeneration, severe equipment corrosion, solvent loss, and emanation of harmful emissions. − To overcome these limitations, amines can be immobilized onto a variety of supports to combine the effectiveness of amine-containing solvents with a facilitated pathway for improved CO2 diffusion while decreasing the energy penalty associated with CO2 capture. For instance, the required energy for CO2 capture in solid-supported amines was found to be ca. 50–80% of the energy demand associated with their liquid counterparts.

As for amine-functionalized CO2 adsorbents, although materials such as silica, alumina, metal–organic frameworks, and polymers have been routinely used as supports, there remains a significant gap in utilizing and exploring biodegradable, sustainable, and low-cost materials. While such eco-friendly materials do not inherently guarantee improved CO2 capture performance, it is nonetheless desirable that the sources or precursors for adsorbents be economically viable and environmentally harmless.

Recently, amine-functionalized lignocellulosic biomass for CO2 capture has attracted significant attention. The rich surface chemistry of these materials makes them promising candidates for CO2 adsorbents. Lignin, an amorphous aromatic polymer, is a major byproduct of the pulp and paper, biofuel, and bioenergy industries. It possesses a variety of functional groups, particularly hydroxyl groups (−OH). The free C3 and C5 positions (Figure S1) in the phenolic ring make it amenable to direct grafting of amine-containing species. Although attachment of amine groups to the phenolic rings via the Mannich reaction has been extensively studied, − the potential of lignin’s abundant hydroxyl functionalities to enable covalent bonding with aminosilanes remains largely unexplored.

Several authoritative reviews have addressed the intrinsic properties of lignin and the diverse strategies for its functionalization. , Also, Rojas and co-workers have documented the potential of lignin-based porous carbon materials for CO2 capture, with most studies focused on the synthesis of biochars via pyrolysis. Nevertheless, very few investigations were devoted to the utilization of amine functionalization of lignin for CO2 capture. Wu et al. prepared amine-functionalized lignin nanoparticles from bamboo by employing an in situ amination strategy using aniline and formic acid during biomass fractionation. The maximum dry CO2 uptake reached only ca. 0.30 mmol/g in the presence of pure CO2 at 25 °C. In another study, Carrier et al. investigated a covalent functionalization of kraft lignin with a series of linear polyamines through the Mannich process. Elemental analysis revealed a progressive increase in nitrogen content with longer polyamine chains, reaching a maximum of 5.46 wt % nitrogen for lignin functionalized with 60 wt % tetraethylenepentamine. This material achieved a CO2 uptake of 0.80 mmol/g at 25 °C under pure CO2, which corresponds to 17.6 and 9.6% higher CO2 uptake compared to the diethylenetriamine (DETA)- and triethylenetetramine (TETA)-functionalized counterparts, respectively. Also, in an earlier contribution, we developed DETA-functionalized phenolated lignins and tested for CO2 adsorption under various operational conditions. Phenolated materials demonstrated higher CO2 uptake compared to nonphenolated DETA-functionalized lignin, achieving a maximum CO2 uptake of 1.45 mmol/g at 35% RH at 25 °C in the presence of 15% CO2/N2.

To the best of our knowledge, the aforementioned studies represent the only reports to date that have employed the amination of pristine lignin for CO2 adsorption. Although the grafting of aminosilane species onto lignin is expected to be feasible, this strategy has not been explored. Therefore, in the current study, a two-step amine and aminosilane grafting strategy is proposed for enhancing surface amine density and the total nitrogen content over pristine lignin for CO2 capture. We first synthesized DETA-functionalized lignin based on our best-performing Mannich-aminated formulation, using a mass ratio of lignin:DETA:formaldehyde of 1:1:1. As shown in Figure , the resulting DETA-modified samples were subsequently grafted with a triaminosilane (TRI), namely, 2-[2-(3-trimethoxysilylpropylamino)­ethylamino]­ethylamine. This work aims to assess the performance and applicability of amine-functionalized lignin in advancing the design of a sustainable CO2 adsorbent. All materials were evaluated based on their CO2 adsorption performance. Moreover, their stability was examined using CO2 adsorption–desorption cycling in the presence of 15% CO2/N2 feed gas under both dry and wet conditions at 25 and 50 °C.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Kraft lignin (alkali, Sigma-Aldrich), DETA (99%, Sigma-Aldrich), formaldehyde (37% w/w aqueous solution, stabilized with 10–15% methanol, Fisher), and isopropyl alcohol (70% v/v aqueous solution, Fisher) were used for the synthesis of DETA-functionalized lignin. For subsequent grafting of aminosilanes onto the DETA-functionalized lignin, 3-[2-(2-aminoethylamino)­ethylamino]­propyl trimethoxysilane (TRI; Gelest Inc.), 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane (APTMS; Gelest Inc.), and anhydrous toluene (99.9%, Fisher) were employed. Deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d 6, 99.9%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All reagents were used as received. Gas cylinders, including ultrahigh-purity nitrogen (99.999%), high-purity CO2 (99.99%), and a 15% CO2/N2 gas mixture, were supplied by Messer Canada.

Preparation of Amine-Functionalized Lignin

Mannich-Aminated Lignin

Mannich-aminated lignin samples were prepared based on the best-performing formulation in our earlier contribution. In brief, 1 g of lignin was dispersed in 15 mL of 0.2 M NaOH in a 50 mL two-neck flask and stirred at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, 1 g of DETA was added, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 30 min. The mixture was heated to 80 °C under reflux before adding 1 g of formaldehyde, with the reaction proceeding for 4 h. After addition of isopropyl alcohol, the DETA-aminated materials were precipitated and separated by filtration, followed by washing with copious amounts of isopropyl alcohol and deionized water. Finally, the Mannich-aminated lignin was vacuum-dried at 80 °C for 6 h. It was labeled as DETA-aminated lignin (DAL) (Figure , route 1).

TRI/APTMS-Grafted Lignin

In a typical procedure, 1 g of pristine lignin was added to a 250 mL three-neck flask containing 50 mL of toluene and stirred at room temperature for 2 h. Next, the flask was connected to a reflux condenser, followed by the dropwise addition of a specific amount of TRI or APTMS (0.25–3 mL per g of support), while the temperature was increased to 90 °C. After 24 h of mixing under nitrogen flow, the grafted materials were filtered, washed with toluene, and vacuum-dried at 40 °C for 24 h. Samples were referred to as TRI/Lignin-x or APTMS/Lignin-x, where x represents the volume of aminosilane used in the grafting procedure. A schematic representation of TRI grafting onto lignin is shown in Figure , route 2.

Double Amine Functionalization Procedure

Double amine-functionalized lignin was obtained by performing route 1 (Figure ), followed by route 2, in which 1 g of DETA-aminated lignin (DAL) was further grafted with 1 mL of TRI. The resulting material was denoted as TRI/DAL.

Material Characterization

Elemental analysis (nitrogen content) was performed using a Vario EL Cube elemental analyzer (Elementar, Germany) configured for the NC mode. Approximately 2–5 mg of sample was weighed into tin capsules and combusted at 925 °C under oxygen flow, and the resulting gases were analyzed by thermal conductivity. To confirm amine functionalization and evaluate structural changes in lignin, 1H and 2D-HSQC NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker AVIII 600 MHz spectrometer. 25 mg of sample was dissolved in 0.7 mL of DMSO-d 6 and stirred for 12 h. The 1H NMR spectra were obtained using the zg30 pulse sequence with a 10 s delay, a 2 s acquisition time, and 256 scans, while the 2D-HSQC spectra were acquired using the hsqcetgp sequence with a 2 s delay, a 2 s acquisition time, and 8 scans. ATR-IR spectra were recorded on a Nicolet 6700 spectrometer over the range of 4000–500 cm–1, with a resolution of 2 cm–1 and 64 accumulated scans. In addition, images of the supports were acquired by using a JEOL JSM-7500F field-emission scanning electron microscope.

CO2 Adsorption–Desorption Measurements

To evaluate the CO2 uptake performance of amine-containing materials, we used both dry and humid conditions. Specifically, adsorption under dry CO2 was investigated by using a thermogravimetric analyzer (Q500 TGA, TA Instruments, USA) with a 15% CO2/N2 gas stream. Measurements under humid CO2 (1–85% RH) in the presence of different CO2 concentrations (0.25–100% CO2, balance N2) were performed using a dual vapor gravimetric sorption analyzer (DVS Resolution, Surface Measurement Systems, UK), which enabled precise control of humidity and gas composition. The pretreatment protocol was identical for all of the experiments. Approximately 15–20 mg of sample was heated under nitrogen flow (100 mL/min) with a temperature ramp of 10 °C/min to 110 °C and held at that temperature for 2 h. The sample was then cooled to the target adsorption temperature. Under dry conditions, the purge gas was switched to a 15% CO2/N2 gas mixture, and the weight change was monitored over time until stabilization. For adsorption measurements under humid conditions, an additional water-saturation step was included; the sample was first exposed to humidified nitrogen at 25 °C to reach equilibrium, after which a CO2/N2 stream (15% CO2) with the same RH was introduced. The resulting weight gain was calculated as the CO2 uptake. As shown in Figure S2, no weight change was detected under pure N2, confirming that the observed mass increase originates exclusively from the CO2 adsorption. For desorption, the flowing gas was switched back to N2, while the sample was heated to 110 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min and kept at that temperature for 90 min. To assess long-term regeneration stability, 10 consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles were performed under both dry and humid conditions at 25 and 50 °C using a 15% CO2/N2 feed gas. Unless stated otherwise, all adsorption–desorption cycles were carried out under the same conditions as those described above.

Results and Discussion

Material Characterization

To evaluate the success of the two-step Mannich amination and TRI grafting, ATR-IR analysis was performed for lignin, DAL, and TRI/DAL samples (Figure ). Lignin, as a macromolecule, contains a wide range of functional groups in its side chain and phenolic units, with particularly noticeable IR bands at 1000–1600 cm–1 (Figure S3). In the 3100–3500 cm–1 region, a broad absorption band is attributed to the aliphatic and aromatic hydroxyl groups of lignin. After Mannich amination and TRI grafting, a new peak centered around 3194 cm–1 appeared, corresponding to N–H stretching in −NH2 groups of DETA and TRI. In the case of DAL, partial deprotonation of phenolic hydroxyl groups due to their substitution at ortho positions (C3 and/or C5) resulted in a lower intensity and slightly shifted −OH stretching bond, while the −OH groups are consumed during TRI grafting. Amine immobilization is further confirmed by focusing on the 1400–1700 cm–1 region, where new broadening at 1650 cm–1 is caused by vibrations of N–H stretching. , Moreover, a slight broadening at 1040 cm–1 band stems from C–N stretching of amine incorporation. The increased intensity in the 2800–3000 cm–1 region, corresponding to C–H and −CH2– stretching vibrations, arises from methylene and methyl groups. In the TRI/DAL spectrum, small and sharp peaks appearing at 694 and 734 cm–1 are assigned to Si–O–Si and Si–C bending from silane grafting, respectively, confirming that TRI has been successfully grafted onto DAL.

ATR-IR spectra of pristine lignin, DAL, and TRI/DAL.

To further confirm the grafting of amine groups, lignin, DAL, and TRI/DAL were analyzed by NMR. It was reported that both primary and secondary amines present in DETA can participate in the Mannich reaction (Figure ). As shown in Figure , the intensity of DAL signals in the 6–8 ppm region, corresponding to aromatic protons, is reduced compared to pristine lignin. Simultaneously, new peaks attributed to aliphatic protons appeared in the 2–3 ppm region of DAL. These findings confirm the incorporation of DETA at the H3,5 or G5 positions of ρ-hydroxyphenyl (H) and guaiacyl (G) units in the phenolic ring of lignin (Figure S1). DETA grafting over pristine lignin was further characterized by 2D HSQC NMR, particularly in the aromatic (δC/δH 100–140/6–8) and aliphatic (δC/δH 30–80/2.5–4.5) regions (Figure S4). A pronounced alteration in the aromatic region, the disappearance of cross-peaks related to G and H units, suggests substantial structural modifications caused by the Mannich functionalization. In the aliphatic region, particularly δC/δH 50–70/2–4 region, a significant number of new signals can be attributed to the occurrence of the methylene bridge of DETA and lignin, forming Ar–CH2–N. ,, These observations are consistent with the results reported by Pan et al. and Jiao et al., who studied Mannich amination of lignin and phenolated lignin, respectively. Compared with DAL, the 1H NMR spectra of TRI/DAL exhibit slight but noticeable changes in the aliphatic region. Although the inherently broad and overlapping signals of lignin may partially obscure fine details, new resonances at ca. 1.2 and 2.6 ppm are discernible, which can be attributed to the extended −CH2–N– linkages introduced by the triamine units. While these changes are relatively modest in the 1H NMR spectra, they align well with ATR-IR results, which confirm more clearly the successful triamine incorporation through distinct N–H and C–N stretching vibrations.

1H NMR spectra of lignin, DAL, and TRI/DAL.

Scanning electron microscopy images of DAL and TRI/DAL (Figure S5) show that both materials exhibited spherical particles, mostly with diameters within 0.1 to 0.5 μm, interconnected into irregular shapes. Comparison of Figure S5a,b indicates that no discernible morphological changes occur upon amine grafting.

Dry CO2 Adsorption

The performance of Mannich-aminated and TRI- and APTMS-grafted materials was investigated independently under dry conditions. DETA-functionalized lignin (DAL), achieved via the Mannich reaction, exhibited a maximum dry CO2 uptake of 0.76 mmol/g in the presence of 15% CO2/N2 at 25 °C. Elemental analysis revealed that the nitrogen content of DAL was 4.15 mmol N/g, corresponding to an amine efficiency of 0.18 mol CO2/mol N. Further details regarding the synthesis and performance of Mannich-aminated lignin were provided in our earlier study.

Aminosilane grafting onto hydroxyl groups in the lignin side chains and phenolic units was proposed to assess the feasibility of accommodating amine groups. Accordingly, TRI and APTMS were grafted in varying amounts (0.25–3 mL per g of support) to evaluate their influence on CO2 uptake (Figure S6). The maximum CO2 uptakes of 0.37 and 0.27 mmol/g were achieved by TRI/Lignin-1 and APTMS/Lignin-2 at 25 °C, respectively. Compared with APTMS, TRI exhibits higher nitrogen content, which could potentially improve CO2 uptake. Nevertheless, at high amine grafting levels, the amine efficiency may decrease due to pore clogging and the limited surface accessibility, which hinders CO2 diffusion. For instance, as the TRI grafting amount increased to 3 mL per g of support, the CO2 uptake dropped below that of its APTMS-grafted counterpart.

In the next step, TRI/DAL was synthesized by TRI grafting over DAL. It should be mentioned that when the order of functionalization is reversed, i.e., the Mannich amination is performed over TRI/Lignin, a slurry-like phase is formed, and no solid phase is obtained. Therefore, DAL was subjected to TRI grafting (1 mL per g of DAL) for further CO2 adsorption measurements. Here, the double amine modification of lignin, through the Mannich amination followed by amine tethering, is a novel strategy compared to the combination of amine grafting and impregnation reported in the literature. ,,

Three different amine-functionalized lignin materials, DAL, TRI/Lignin-1, and TRI/DAL, were exposed to 15% CO2/N2 at different temperatures (Figure and Table S1). The corresponding nitrogen contents were 4.15, 1.02, and 4.32 mmol/g for DAL, TRI/Lignin-1, and TRI/DAL, respectively. TRI/DAL exhibited the highest CO2 uptake of 1.31 mmol/g at 25 °C, followed by a gradual decrease with increasing temperature, reaching 0.70 mmol/g at 85 °C. The higher CO2 uptake of TRI/DAL cannot be attributed only to the additive effect of the two individual grafting steps. Instead, it likely results from the structural and chemical activation of the lignin matrix by DETA and is mainly due to the remaining significant fraction of hydroxyl groups after Mannich amination, which improves accessibility of the subsequently grafted TRI groups. Although the Mannich reaction occurs only at aromatic sites, it may increase the surface polarity and slightly disrupt the compact lignin structure, improving accessibility for bulky aminosilanes like TRI. Moreover, the presence of preattached amine groups is likely to facilitate additional interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, during TRI grafting. These combined effects could result in more effective amine immobilization and higher CO2 uptake than the sum of each step independently. While DAL showed a nitrogen content of 4.15 mmol/g and a low amine efficiency (0.18 mol CO2/mol N at 25 °C), TRI/Lignin-1 had significantly higher amine efficiency (0.36 mol CO2/mol N) despite its low nitrogen loading (1.02 mmol/g). Notably, TRI/DAL demonstrated a balanced performance, achieving a relatively improved amine efficiency (0.30 mol CO2/mol N) than DAL, resulting in an approximately 70 and 250% increase in CO2 uptake compared to DAL and TRI/Lignin-1, respectively. Therefore, TRI grafting not only did not hinder CO2 diffusion but also led to higher CO2 uptake. It should be noted that APTMS grafting (1–3 mL per g of adsorbent) into DAL did not improve CO2 uptake compared to TRI/DAL. The temperature-dependent behavior also varied among the materials. As for DAL, temperature had an adverse effect on CO2 uptake, with lower amine efficiency at increased temperature. This behavior is likely due to the relatively low amine content in DAL (4.15 mmol/g), which minimizes diffusion limitations, leading to thermodynamically controlled adsorption process. In contrast, for TRI/Lignin-1, CO2 uptake versus temperature displayed a volcano shape, initially increasing, to a maximum CO2 uptake of 0.48 mmol/g at 55 °C, before slowly declining at higher temperatures. At low temperatures, the adsorption process is predominantly kinetically controlled, whereas elevated temperature enhances CO2 diffusivity. Beyond 55 °C, the adsorption becomes thermodynamically controlled. Since the interaction between CO2 and amines is exothermic in nature, further temperature increases reduce the driving force. As for TRI/DAL, adsorption was thermodynamically controlled, indicating that diffusion was negligible.

(a) CO2 uptake and (b) amine efficiency for DAL, TRI/Lignin-1, and TRI/DAL at different temperatures in the presence of 15% CO2/N2.

TRI/DAL was also exposed to different CO2 concentrations, from 0.25 to 100% CO2, balanced with N2 at 25 °C. The corresponding CO2 uptake and amine efficiencies are summarized in Table S2. As shown in Figure , CO2 uptake increased sharply with an increased CO2 concentration up to ca. 10% and slowed to a more gradual rise at higher concentrations, ultimately reaching a maximum capacity of 1.71 mmol/g at 100 vol % CO2. This is typical of chemisorption behavior. At low CO2 concentrations, accessible adsorption sites, particularly those on the exterior surface of TRI-grafted DAL, readily interact with CO2 molecules, while diffusion resistance may limit access to internal sites. As the CO2 partial pressure increases, enhanced diffusion into internal regions provides a greater driving force for CO2 transfer, thereby improving amine efficiency. Some of the CO2 uptake at high partial pressure may also be attributable to physisorption. This behavior is consistent with the findings of Zhao et al., confirming that higher CO2 concentrations promote carbamic acid formation, thereby enhancing the CO2 uptake.

CO2 adsorption isotherm of TRI/DAL at 25 °C.

CO2 adsorption isotherm data were fitted to the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin models, and the corresponding fitting parameters and correlation coefficients are summarized in Table S3. As shown in Figure , the Temkin model provided the best fit to the experimental data, suggesting a gradual decrease in adsorption energy with increasing CO2 coverage, consistent with a heterogeneous surface profile.

Humid CO2 Adsorption

From the perspective of CO2–amine chemistry, the enhanced CO2 uptake under humid conditions, often reported in the literature, arises from a fundamental difference in the reaction mechanism. In the absence of moisture, CO2 reacts with two amine groups to form an ammonium carbamate, whereas in the presence of moisture, it requires only a single amine group to form ammonium bicarbonate. This shift may result in up to twice the CO2 uptake compared to dry conditions. Nevertheless, depending on the supports and relative humidity, amine-containing materials have exhibited both enhanced and diminished CO2 uptake under humid conditions. Hence, adsorption measurements over TRI/DAL were carried out in the presence of 15% CO2/N2 with different levels of RH. The results are shown in Figure and are listed in Table S4. As for water uptake versus RH, it showed a continuous increase without any particular event, while a trade-off was observed in CO2 uptake. As RH increased up to 55%, the CO2 uptake increased, reaching a maximum capacity of 1.84 mmol/g, while further increase of RH up to 85% led to a ca. 23% decrease (1.42 mmol/g) in CO2 uptake. The corresponding amine efficiency at 55% RH was 0.43, representing an increase of ca. 43% compared with that under dry conditions. The gradual promotion of CO2 uptake up to 55% RH is mainly attributed to a shift in the adsorption mechanism, where bicarbonate formation becomes dominant over carbamate formation. In contrast, at high RH, the affinity of amine groups for water may lead to competitive adsorption between H2O and CO2, which can inhibit CO2 uptake. Additionally, it was found that the formation of a liquid-like layer on the surface can hinder CO2 access to active sites, thereby suppressing the overall CO2 uptake performance. The optimum RH associated with maximum CO2 uptake varies widely depending on the nature of the supports and the CO2 uptake conditions. For instance, the optimum RH was found to be 70 and 80% for TRI-grafted SBA-15 with pore sizes of 6.6 and 9.2 nm, respectively. It was 10% for PEI-impregnated HP2MGL resin in the presence of 400 ppm CO2/N2 and 40% in the presence of 5000 ppm CO2/N2, 25% for PEI-impregnated single-walled zeolite, and 74% for propylamine-grafted pore-expanded silica.

CO2 and water uptake of TRI/DAL versus RH in the presence of 15% CO2/N2 at 25 °C.

Cyclic CO2 Adsorption–Desorption

To evaluate the long-term stability of TRI/DAL, a sample was subjected to 10 cyclic CO2 adsorption–desorption under dry and humid 15% CO2/N2 at 25 and 50 °C (Figure ). Under dry conditions, each cycle consisted of 1 h adsorption, followed by 1 h desorption in flowing N2 at 110 °C. Under humid conditions, the sample was first water-saturated with humid nitrogen for 6 h at 55% RH, followed by exposure to 15% CO2/N2 with the same RH, while the desorption step remained unchanged. As illustrated in Figure , compared to the fresh material, CO2 uptake in the absence of humidity decreased by ca. 8 and 5% in the first cycle at 25 and 50 °C, respectively. After ten cycles, the residual CO2 uptake was ca. 84 and 78% at 25 and 50 °C, respectively. The CO2 uptake loss can be attributed to the formation of stable urea linkages, a process that is accelerated under dry CO2 conditions. In contrast, during repeated humid CO2 cycling, only a slight decrease in CO2 uptake was observed, ca. 2.5% at 25 °C and 5% at 50 °C over ten cycles. This minor loss was consistent with earlier observations, suggesting that the presence of moisture during the CO2–amine reaction inhibits the irreversible formation of urea linkages, favoring the reversible formation of bicarbonates. ATR-IR analysis of TRI/DAL further confirmed this result, as no urea-characteristic bands were observed in the 1450–1750 cm–1 region after humid CO2 cycling (Figure S7). As shown in an earlier work, urea formation of amine-supported materials would be associated with IR bands at around 1500, 1560, 1660, and 1700 cm–1. Their absence in the present case is consistent with the suppression of urea formation under humid conditions. Therefore, humidity not only contributes to higher CO2 uptake, as discussed earlier, but also prevents the formation of stable urea species.

Dry and humid (55% RH) CO2 adsorption–desorption cycles in the presence of 15% CO2/N2 at (a) 25 and (b) 50 °C. Cycle “0” represents the fresh material.

Conclusions

In this study, the feasibility of double amine functionalization of lignin was investigated for CO2 capture. Although the Mannich reaction is one of the well-established methods for lignin amination, it was found that through its hydroxyl groups, lignin could be functionalized by aminosilane grafting, beyond what is achievable through the Mannich reaction alone. Hence, DAL and APTMS- and TRI-grafted lignin were synthesized through Mannich and grafting processes, respectively. To accommodate higher amine-containing species, DAL was further subjected to TRI grafting. The results showed that although the second grafting step led to a marginal increase in DAL nitrogen content, from 4.15 to 4.32 mmol/g, the amine efficiency for TRI/DAL increased by about 67%, capturing up to 1.31 mmol/g CO2 at 25 °C in the presence of 15% CO2/N2 feed gas, which was a significant improvement compared to both single-functionalized adsorbents. Humidity promoted CO2 uptake, with a maximum of 1.84 mmol/g in the presence of 15% CO2/N2 at 55% RH versus 1.31 mmol/g under dry conditions. Cyclic CO2 adsorption–desorption tests were conducted with and without humidity at 25 and 50 °C. Over ten consecutive cycles, TRI/DAL exhibited capacity losses of 16 and 22% under dry conditions, whereas under humid conditions (55% RH), the decrease in CO2 uptake was limited to 2.5% at 25 °C and 5% at 50 °C. This enhanced stability was attributed to the suppression of irreversible urea formation under humid environments, favoring the reversible formation of ammonium bicarbonate instead. Although lignin, as a biodegradable and sustainable biopolymer material, still requires further improvement to compete with more traditional supports, the use of low-cost materials for CO2 capture offers a new perspective for future research.
