Comparison of periglottic oxygen fraction, tracheal oxygen fraction and safe apnea time under different apneic oxygenation techniques: a randomised non-inferiority controlled study
Authors: Weilian Geng, Changxing Chen, Yaobing Chen, Xiaojuan Yu, Xinhua Yu, Shaoqiang Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundApneic oxygenation prolongs safe apnea time and reduces hypoxemia risk during airway management. The primary objective of this study was to compare the safe apnea time among various techniques, while the secondary objective was to evaluate intergroup differences in periglottic and tracheal oxygen fractions (FgO₂ and FtO₂).MethodsThis randomized, non-inferiority trial enrolled 125 participants, assigned to five groups: modified nasopharyngeal airway with 10 L/min oxygen (Naso group), nasal cannula oxygenation at 2 L/min (L2 group), 5 L/min (L5 group), and 10 L/min (L10 group), and a control group without supplemental oxygen (L0 group).ResultsThe success rate in the L10 group was lower than that in the Naso group (82.6% vs. 95.7%; risk difference, -13.0%; 95%CI: -27.8% to 1.7%), and non-inferiority was not established, but had similar safe apnea times (15 [15 to 15] min vs 15 [15 to 15] min, P = 0.138).The L10 group demonstrated superior performance compared to the L0, L2, and L5 groups terms of achieving a safe apnea time of 15 min (82.6% vs 0、8.7% and 43.5%,respectively; P < 0.001). At all measured time points,FgO2 and FtO2 in the L10 group were lower than those in the Naso group, but higher than those in the L0, L2 and L5 groups (P < 0.001). FgO2 at the end of the apnea was positively correlated with safe apnea time.ConclusionThe nasal cannula at 10 L/min, along with the modified nasopharyngeal airway, was associated with longer safe apnea times and relatively higher FgO₂ and FtO₂ levels. Higher oxygen flow rates were associated with increased FgO₂, FtO₂, and longer safe apnea duration.Trial registrationThe study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Obstetrics and Gynaecology Hospital of Fudan University (2022–197) on January 9, 2023. The study was registered on ChiCTR (ChiCTR2300067642) on January 16, 2023.Keywords: Apneic oxygenation, Safe apnea time, Periglottic oxygen fraction, Tracheal oxygen fraction, Nasal cannula oxygenation, Nasopharyngeal airway

Introduction

From anesthetic induction to successful tracheal intubation during general anesthesia, a period of apnea is inevitable, and a difficult airway may result in a prolonged apnea time and increased risk of severe hypoxemia. During apnea,alveolar negative pressure due to the imbalance between CO2 and O2 exchange drives passive gas flow from the pharynx to the alveoli—this is known as apneic oxygenation [1, 2].

Common apneic oxygenation techniques include oxygen delivery through a nasal cannula, face mask, and nasopharyngeal airway. Nasal cannula oxygenation can be categorized into low-flow nasal oxygenation (LFNO, oxygen flow rate 0.5–2 L/min), standard-flow (oxygen flow rate 4–15 L/min), and high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC, oxygen flow rate 30–70 L/min) [3–5]. Even if the oxygen flow rate is as low as 0.5 L/min, it can still effectively extend the safe apnea time [6–8].

A computational model has suggested that the safe apnea time may be related to the periglottic oxygen fraction (FgO2) [9]; but this lacks clinical validation. The oxygen concentration in the trachea (FtO2) closely approximates to the intra-alveolar oxygen concentration, fluctuates with low-flow face mask oxygenation, whereas high flow (≥ 15 L/min) stabilizes it [10]. Whether different apneic oxygenation techniques affect FgO₂ or FtO₂? Is the change in the FtO2 related to safe apnea time? However, these questions remain unanswered.

The objective of this study was to compare how different apneic oxygenation methods affect safe apnea time by measuring FgO₂ and FtO₂ to assess whether higher oxygen flow rates enhance oxygenation efficiency.

Methods

Study design

This single-center, randomized controlled trial was conducted at the Obstetrics and Gynaecology Hospital of Fudan University between January and August 2023. Ethical approval (No. 2022–197) was obtained on January 9, 2023, and all participants gave written informed consent. The study was registered on ChiCTR (ChiCTR2300067642) on January 16, 2023, and followed CONSORT guidelines.

Participants

Female Patients aged 18–60 years, ASA physical status I-II,without difficult airway, who were scheduled to undergo elective laparoscopic gynaecological surgery under general anesthesia were eligible to participate in this study. The exclusion criteria included anatomical abnormalities or a surgical history in the face, nose, or upper airway; nasal obstruction; a previous history of difficult intubation; ASA class > II; New York Heart Association class > 2; smoking; chronic respiratory diseases; uncontrolled hypertension; increased intracranial pressure; SpO2 still less than 97% after 3 min of pure oxygen inhalation; difficulty in face mask ventilation; Body Mass Index (BMI) ≥ 28 kg/m2; pregnancy; severe gastroesophageal reflux; neuromuscular diseases; or refusal to participate in this study.

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1:1:1 ratio to five groups: modified nasopharyngeal airway (Naso Group), nasal cannula oxygenation at 2 L/min (L2 Group), 5 L/min (L5 Group), 10 L/min (L10 Group), and a no-oxygen control (L0 Group). Randomization used a computer-generated permuted block design (block size = 5). A researcher not involved in the study prepared sealed, coded envelopes, which were opened by the investigator on the day of surgery. Both patients and the outcome assessor were blinded to group allocation.

Interventions and anesthesia management

Patients routinely fasted before surgery, and they did not take any preoperative medications. After entering the operating room, the patients were placed in the supine position and monitored with electrocardiogram (ECG), non-invasive blood pressure, and SpO2 and venous access was obtained. The bispectral index (BIS) (BIS™; Medtronic Limited, Boulder, GO, USA), transcutaneous CO2 monitor (TCM400, Radiometer Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Denmark) and quantitative neuromuscular transmission monitor (HXD-1, CO28; Heilongjiang Huaxiang Technology, Ltd, Harbin, China) were also monitored throughout the study period. The patient started to breathe 100% oxygen through the face mask at 12 L/min until the end-tidal oxygen fraction (FE′O2) was ≥ 90%. Anesthesia was induced with 5 μg/kg glycopyrronium bromide, propofol 2 mg/kg, and sufentanil 0.5 μg/kg.

Once the patient completely lost consciousness, rocuronium bromide was administered intravenously at 0.9 mg/kg, and the treatment commenced. Depending on the group assignment, a modified nasopharyngeal airway [8] or nasal cannula was placed, and oxygen flow rates were adjusted accordingly. Sixty seconds after the muscle relaxant injection, airway assessment was performed via video laryngoscopy, and insert a sterile gas sampling tube (Vital Signs™ 8004383VS, CareFusion, Finland) connected to a monitor (CARESCAPE™ Monitor B450, GE Healthcare, USA) for oxygen concentration measurement. The distal tip of the sampling tube was trimmed, marked 5 cm from the end (Fig. 1A), and positioned near the supraglottis (Fig. 1B) to record FgO₂. It was then repositioned 5 cm below the glottis (Fig. 1C) to record FtO₂ at baseline (T0) and every minute until apnea ended. FgO₂ was measured again upon tube withdrawal. Throughout the procedure, SpO₂ was continuously monitored, and the video laryngoscope remained in place to ensure proper tube positioning.

C The sampling tube was placed 5 cm below the glottis to measure FtO2. FgO2: periglottic oxygen fraction; FtO2: tracheal oxygen fraction

Apnea was defined as starting at the time of rocuronium injection [8]. Apnea was terminated if any of the following occurred: SpO₂ dropped to 95%, apnea lasted 15 min without desaturation, or transcutaneous carbon dioxide pressure (TcPCO₂) reached ≥ 100 mmHg [5]. After sampling tube removal, tracheal intubation was performed using a video laryngoscope. Mechanical ventilation was initiated with 100% oxygen, tidal volume 6 mL/kg, and respiratory rate 15 breaths/min to eliminate accumulated CO₂. Patients were excluded if glottic exposure was inadequate or the sampling tube became blocked.

During the study, propofol was continuously infused at a rate of 6 mg/kg/h to maintain anesthesia, and the infusion rate of propofol was adjusted based on the target BIS value of 40–60. Hypertension or hypotension was defined as a ≥ 30% change in mean arterial pressure from baseline, and a heart rate of < 50 beats/min or > 100 beats/min was defined as bradycardia or tachycardia, for which the corresponding cardiovascular activity drugs were administered. At the end of the study and the beginning of the surgery, gastrointestinal distension was assessed under direct laparoscopic visualization. Before the patients left the post-anesthesia recovery room (PACU), they were assessed for discomfort, such as epistaxis or nasal mucosal injury.

Endpoints

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with a safe apnea duration of 15 min. The non-inferiority test was performed for the primary endpoint, with a one-sided type I error rate of 0.025 and 95% two-sided CI.

Secondary outcomes included FgO2 at 1 min of apnea and the end of the apnea (FgO2s and FgO2e), FtO2 from T0 to 5minitues, and FtO2 at the end of apnea (FtO2e). Safe apnea time was defined as the time from cessation of breathing to when SpO2 decreased to 95%. The apnea time started immediately after the bolus injection of rocuronium bromide. For the patients who had an apnea time of 15 min but whose SpO2 did not drop to 95%, the safe apnea time was recorded as 15 min.

Additional data collected included age, height, weight, BMI, baseline SpO₂, FE′O2 before the injection of anesthetics (Pre FE’O2), SpO2 at the start of induction of anesthesia (SpO2s) and at the end of the apnea (SpO2e), TcPCO2 at the end of the apnea (TcPCO2e), the number of patients with incidence of hemodynamic events (hypertension, hypotension, bradycardia, tachycardia), and discomfort before leaving the PACU, were recorded.

Sample size and statistical analysis

PASS 15.0.5 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, USA) was used for the sample size calculation. Based on our preliminary study and given the purpose of this study, we first conducted a non-inferiority test between the Naso groups and L10 group to determine the proportion of patients with a safe apnea time of 15 min and then compared the two groups under different oxygen flow conditions. Our previous study revealed that 97% of the patients in the Naso group could maintain a safe apnea time of 15 min [8]. Under such circumstances, rather than the average safe apnea time, the percentage of patients with a safe apnea time of 15 min was more appropriate as the main observable indicator. We considered that a reduction of up to 15% would still preserve acceptable clinical efficacy without compromising patient safety. Based on this assumption, the L10 group would be considered non-inferior to the Naso group if the decrease did not exceed this predefined margin. At the one-sided α = 0.025 and β = 0.2 levels, 21 patients were required for each group. In our pilot experiment, the proportions of patients in the L0, L2, L5, and L10 groups who still had an SpO2 ≥ 95% at 15 min were 5%, 15%, 35%, and 80%, respectively. When we compared each group with the L10 group with α = 0.05 and β = 0.8, 22 patients in each group were required. On the basis of these results, 22 patients were included in each group. Considering a dropout rate of approximately 10%, we recruited 25 patients for each group.

The measurement data were first tested for normality. The normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and the non-normally distributed data are presented as median [interquartile range]. The one-sided Wald test was used to examine the non-inferiority of the L10 group compared to the Naso group in terms of on the proportion of patients with a safe apnea time of 15 min, while the percentages of the L0, L2, L5, and L10 groups that reached a safe apnea time of 15 min were compared using the chi-squared test. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were drawn to analyze the safe apnea time. Analysis of variance was used to compare differences in normally distributed indicators between multiple groups, and the rank-sum test was used to compare differences in non-normally distributed indicators. Because the FgO2 and FtO2 data were repeated measurements, a mixed linear model was used for analysis and comparison. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. SAS 9.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) software was used for data collation and statistical analysis. Prism 8.3.0 (GraphPad software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used for plotting.

Primary outcome

Despite the lack of confirmed non-inferiority, the L10 and Naso groups showed comparable safe apnea times

Nineteen Patients (82.6%) in the L10 group and 22 patients (95.7%) in the Naso group reached a safe apnea time of 15 min. The difference between the Naso and L10 groups was −13.0% (95% CI, −27.8% to 1.7%) (P = 0.417), exceeding the non-inferiority margin of −15%; thus, non-inferiority could not be established.

There was no significant difference in the safe apnea time between the L10 and Naso groups (15 [15 to 15] min vs. 15 [15 to 15] min, P = 0.138) (Fig. 3A). The Kaplan–Meier survival curves for safe apnea time in the two groups are shown in Fig. 3B.

By linear regression analysis, we found that FgO2e was positively correlated with safe apnea time (R2 = 0.919, P < 0.001).

Discussion

Despite the lack of confirmed non-inferiority, the L10 and Naso groups showed comparable safe apnea times. The L10 group performed better than the L0, L2, and L5 groups. Its FgO2s, FgO2e, and FtO2e values were lower than those in the Naso group but higher than in the L0, L2, and L5 groups. FgO2e increased with higher oxygen flow rates and longer safe apnea durations.

Specifically, we clarified that although both the L10 and Naso groups achieved comparable safe apnea times and adequate oxygenation levels (FgO₂ and FtO₂), the higher success rate observed in the Naso group suggests it may offer more consistent oxygenation performance, particularly in clinical situations requiring high reliability. However, the nasopharyngeal airway is invasive, more costly, and carries a risk of nasal injury. Our earlier study also demonstrated that nasopharyngeal airway oxygenation was as effective as high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) in extending safe apnea time, despite the significantly higher cost of HFNC due to disposable components (~ $100). Taken together, these findings support the clinical use of simpler, non-invasive, and cost-effective strategies. A nasal cannula at 10 L/min appears to be a preferable option for routine patients.

Recent studies have increasingly confirmed the ability of LFNO to prolong safe apnea time [4, 11]. However, comparative data between LFNO and standard nasal cannulas are limited. The Society of Obstetric Anesthesiologists and the Difficult Airway Society recommended standard nasal cannula oxygenation to prolong the safe apnea time, but no consensus exists regarding optimal flow rates [12]. Therefore, our study investigated nasal cannula oxygenation at varying flow rates and found that higher flow was associated with prolonged apnea tolerance, accompanied by corresponding changes in SpO2e and TcPCO2e.

In the Naso group, the FgO2S, FgO2e, and FtO2e were all better than those in the L10 group, which may be related to the different oxygenation locations [13]. The oxygenation location of the nasal cannula is only the nostrils, whereas the nasopharyngeal airway provides oxygen closer to the proximal trachea, possibly creating a more effective oxygen reservoir [13]. In the Naso group, FgO2e was slightly greater than FgO2s, further supporting this explanation.

Previous computational and experimental studies emphasize the importance of maintaining high subglottic oxygen concentration to optimize apneic oxygenation [9, 14, 15]. However, direct measurement is challenging [14, 15]. Previous studies used invasive cricothyroid puncture to monitor FtO₂,which is difficult to use in clinical practice [10, 16]. Toner et al. [17] introduced a less invasive approach by placing a catheter just below the glottis, showing that higher FtO2 correlates with longer apnea times—consistent with our findings. Notably, this is the first clinical study comparing FgO2 across multiple apneic oxygenation techniques.

During apnea, alveolar negative pressure during apnea drives oxygen from the upper airway to the alveoli, resulting in a pattern similar to that of “alveolar ventilation”. The establishment of a pharyngeal oxygen bank through apneic oxygenation technology can strengthen this negative pressure gradient and prolong safe apnea time [6, 18]. In this study, higher nasal cannula flow rates increased FgO2, sustained pharyngeal oxygen reserves, and effectively prolonged apnea time. Conversely, low-flow groups failed to sustain FgO2, resulting in earlier desaturation. These findings were corroborated by the observed increase in FgO2e with escalating flow rates.

Nonetheless, the effectiveness of apneic oxygenation is not determined solely by pressure gradients. Progressive accumulation of alveolar CO2 during apnea diminishes the driving gradient, ultimately impairing oxygen transport even in the presence of adequate pharyngeal oxygen stores [1, 19]. Additional factors such as absorptive atelectasis and intrapulmonary shunting may further compromise oxygenation, particularly under high FiO₂ conditions [20, 21]. Moreover, increasing flow raises the risk of nasal mucosal injury, although insufficient oxygen during preoxygenation may heighten the risk of hypoxemia, especially in difficult airways.

Time trend of CO₂ accumulation could provide valuable mechanistic insights into the physiology of apneic oxygenation. Although the progressive rise in TcPCO₂ during prolonged apnea has been well established in previous studies [8], our study primarily focused on observing changes in tracheal and periglottic oxygen concentrations over time. For this reason, we did not describe the detailed trend of TcPCO₂ accumulation in the current analysis. However, we fully acknowledge that CO₂ buildup may pose potential safety concerns. Therefore, TcPCO₂ was continuously monitored throughout the apneic period. A predefined safety threshold of 100 mmHg was established, and apnea was immediately terminated if TcPCO₂ exceeded this value, in order to ensure patient safety.

Ellis et al. [9] suggested that maintaining 15 L/min after achieving an FE′O₂ ≥ 90% can safely prolong apnea time in obstetric patients, highlighting the role of both preoxygenation and technique. In our study, patients were preoxygenated with a face mask until FE′O₂ reached 90%; the efficacy of different oxygenation strategies under suboptimal preoxygenation conditions warrants further investigation.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, In our study, we specifically chose a relatively healthy patient population, as their longer safe apnea times allowed us to more clearly observe the trend of tracheal oxygen concentration over time and gain a deeper understanding of the physiological mechanisms underlying apneic oxygenation. For special populations, such as patients with obesity, difficult airways, or those in emergency or ICU settings,the safe apnea time may be significantly shorter, and the changes in tracheal oxygen concentration may be more rapid or pronounced [3]. Second, we did not monitor the airway CO2 concentration. We speculated that as the duration of apnea increased, the CO2 concentration in the alveoli increased, diluting and reducing the oxygen concentration in the airways and alveoli.

Conclusions

In conclusion, nasal cannula oxygenation at 10 L/min alone was effective in prolonging safe apnea time and maintaining adequate FgO₂ and FtO₂ in patients preoxygenated to an FE′O₂ of 90%. The addition of a modified nasopharyngeal airway offered comparable benefits, but the simplicity and efficacy of the 10 L/min nasal cannula make it a particularly practical approach in routine clinical settings. Moreover, in the nasal cannula group, increasing the oxygen flow rate was associated with higher FgO₂ and FtO₂ levels, as well as prolonged safe apnea duration.
