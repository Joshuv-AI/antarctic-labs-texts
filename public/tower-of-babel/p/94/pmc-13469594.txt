Modified YOLO for tape-sealing defects in outer packaging of cigarette carton
Authors: Qingming Hou, Fei Ru, Yan Guo, Rui Wang, Jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Defective sealing of outer packaging can result in squeezed, deformed, or fallen-off products, inevitably leading to losses for businesses or individuals. Thus, detecting tape-sealing defects in external packaging is crucial in industrial manufacturing. Systems for detecting surface defects based on visual perception are popular in industrial quality inspection. Nevertheless, traditional surface defect detection methods demonstrate limited precision and slow performance. Multi-scale, multi-type defects, numerous small targets, and complex background interference characterize surface defects in industrial products. Detecting minor, multi-scale defects amidst complex background interference is significantly challenging. Therefore, developing algorithms that can accurately detect industrial defects remains a challenging problem. Consequently, we propose a model that uses YOLOv8 to detect tape-sealing defects of cigarette carton. Using diverse branch blocks (DBB), The backbone extracts multi-scale representations and enlarges the effective receptive field. Secondly, implementing a combined module (CCFM+C2fiRMA) in the Neck enhances the detection accuracy. Also, we adopt focal loss to further enhance the detection of tiny objects. Finally, experimental results show that our model achieves 98.1% mAP50 and 67.8% mAP50−95 on the QZU-DET dataset, outperforming YOLOv8n by 3.2 and 2.2% points, respectively. In addition, our method attains an F1 score of 91.6%, indicating strong detection reliability. Overall, the proposed model enables efficient and accurate tape-sealing defect detection in practical industrial scenarios.

Introduction

In recent years, numerous emerging technologies have been implemented to enhance productivity and product quality in manufacturing. Before, workers were required to be involved in many stages of the production. Today, these positions are gradually being automated by machines and software. Reducing production costs, improving production efficiency, and enhancing product quality are effective strategies for household paper companies to increase their competitiveness. During production, defects, such as more significant gaps, overlaps, or incomplete connections, inevitably appear in the outer packaging. These defects may lead to damage of cigarettes inside the cartons or even cause failure in other equipment on the automated production line. Consequently, quality inspection of carton tape adhesion has become a critical issue requiring immediate resolution. Conventional manual inspection techniques are not only inefficient but also prone to overlooked inspections. Using machine vision for packaging appearance detection can reduce human labor and improve detection efficiency. Incorporating packaging defect detection algorithms can enhance the capabilities of modern intelligent inspection systems and promote higher levels of automation, offering substantial practical value.

Object detection methods are primarily divided into classical and deep learning-based algorithms. The latter can be further classified into two- and one-stage detection methods.

Object detection is crucial in computer vision focused on target localization and classification1. It involves locating a target object in a given image or video, marking its position with a bounding box, and determining its category. The diversity and ambiguity of target items render object detection a fundamental challenge in this domain. The rapid progress of convolutional neural networks (CNNs)2 and the increasing availability of large-scale object detection datasets has recently accelerated the adoption of CNN-based object detection methods. R-CNN3 was proposed by Kirchick et al., and it significantly improved object detection performance by leveraging CNNs to enhance feature extraction and detection accuracy.

Two-stage object detection methods, such as R-CNN, Fast R-CNN4, Faster R-CNN5, and Mask R-CNN6, typically generate candidate regions of interest (ROIs) using a region proposal network (RPN) and then perform bounding box regression and target classification, which enables high detection accuracy but often results in relatively slow inference speed. In recent years, transformer-based object detection methods have further extended this research direction. For instance, DETR formulates object detection as a set prediction problem and models global contextual relationships through self-attention, eliminating the need for anchor boxes and non-maximum suppression; however, it suffers from slow convergence and high computational cost. To improve efficiency, several variants such as Deformable DETR and RT-DETR have been proposed, which introduce sparse attention mechanisms and architectural optimizations to significantly accelerate training and inference while maintaining competitive detection performance. Overall, two-stage and transformer-based detection methods exhibit strong accuracy and global modeling capability, but their computational complexity still limits their applicability in real-time industrial inspection scenarios.

In contrast, one-stage object identification algorithms that treat the detection process as a regression job include the YOLO (You Only Look Once) series7–10 and SSD (Single Shot Multibook Detector)11. They simultaneously complete the tasks of target classification and bounding box localization using a single neural network, enabling end-to-end detection. Although early one-stage detection algorithms had lower accuracy than two-stage algorithms, the formers detection was faster. With the development and improvement of the YOLO series, algorithms like YOLOv412 and subsequent single-stage methods have surpassed two-stage algorithms in some specific scenarios.

In recent years, extensive research and experiments have been conducted on defect detection of industrial products. For example, a Faster R-CNN-based visual inspection method was proposed for surface defect detection in industrial manufacturing, detecting and classifying five defect types with average accuracies of 90.6%, 83.4%, 82.1%, 98.1%, and 84.7%, respectively13. Their method notably surpasses traditional CNN-based approaches in speed, making it suitable for real-time applications.

Similarly, a two-stage detection method was proposed to target the zipper tape defect issue precisely with a CNN-based deep defect detection architecture at each stage14. The initial phase of the YOLOv3++-SPC introduces four detection branches over four feature maps at varying scales, facilitating enhanced detection precision and efficiency for multi-scale errors. During the second phase, minor faults are regarded as significant and identified using the enhanced quick YOLOv3-SPP2. Elsewhere, a real-time defect detection system was introduced using the YOLO algorithm for automatic product quality categorization15. Integrating this system into factories and production lines enhances efficiency and lowers operational costs.

Another research16 presented a detection technique to find packing problems using the ECA-EfficientDet transfer learning algorithm. The complexity of the collected data is increased via the mosaic data augmentation technique. The ECA-Convblock was designed to improve the backbone network’s feature extraction selectivity by integrating the Mish activation function and a channel importance prediction algorithm. Also, RDD-YOLO17, i.e., a YOLOv5-based model for detecting defects on steel surfaces, has been proposed. To increase the receptive field and gather multi-scale data, the backbone component uses Res2Net blocks. Second, by recycling low-level features, a dual feature pyramid network (DFPN) improves the neck by offering a comprehensive representation that is advantageous to the network. A decoupling header improves detection precision by differentiating between regression and classification tasks.

Although the YOLO series has achieved remarkable success in real-time object detection tasks, it still faces several challenges in complex industrial inspection scenarios. Specifically, YOLO-based models often exhibit limitations in detecting multi-scale defects, identifying small and subtle targets, and maintaining robustness under complex background interference, all of which are particularly prominent in packaging defect inspection tasks. In addition, industrial packaging defect datasets typically suffer from a high proportion of small-object samples and severe class imbalance, which can cause easy samples to dominate the training process and consequently degrade the model’s ability to recognize critical defects. Meanwhile, the feature representation capability of the backbone network and the effectiveness of cross-scale feature fusion in the neck remain critical factors affecting detection accuracy.

Methods

YOLOv8 framework

Ultralights, Inc. launched YOLOv818, an object identification and picture segmentation method, on January 10, 2023. The Backbone, Neck, and Head are the three primary parts of the YOLOv8 design. Based on network model size, the models are divided into five groups: YOLOv8n, YOLOv8s, YOLOv8m, YOLOv8l, and YOLOv8 ×19. By replacing the C3 module of the Backbone with the C2f module and eliminating the convolution operation in the Neck section after upsampling in the PAN structure, YOLOv8 improves model efficiency over YOLOv5.

Using various loss functions, the Head component performs bounding box regression and target categorization through two branches. For classification, binary cross-entropy loss is used, and for bounding box regression prediction, distribution focal loss20 and complete intersection over union are used. To dynamically increase detection accuracy and resilience, YOLOv8 replaces the previous IOU matching or unilateral percentage distribution techniques with the Task-Aligned Assigner21 for sample allocation.

The Backbone is a pre-trained network that extracts high-level visual information, increasing feature (channel) resolution while decreasing spatial resolution. It utilizes residual and dense blocks to address the vanishing gradient problem and enable information to reach the deepest layers. Moreover, one advantage of thick and residual blocks is recurring gradients. The Backbone structure comprises repeating convolution layer patterns, C2f blocks, and a spatial pyramid pooling (SPPF) block. This approach decreases parameters and FLOPS, significantly boosting YOLOv8 inference speed, essential for real-time object detection models.

The Neck improves the model’s ability to generalize across objects of various sizes and scales by creating a pyramid-shaped attribute array from the input. The Neck quickly concatenates features, allowing the exact channel dimensions to be relaxed and reducing the tensors overall size and number of parameters. After two CNN layers (kernel size = 3, stride = 1, padding = 1), the Head uses a 2D CNN layer (kernel size = 1, stride = 1, padding = 0) to demonstrate results, including class probability, objectness, and bounding box scores.

Diverse branch block

The backbone architecture of YOLOv8 is based on Darknet53 and incorporates a spatial pyramid pooling fast (SPPF) module to integrate local and global features. In addition, the C2f module enhances network depth and enlarges the receptive field, thereby improving feature extraction efficiency. To further strengthen this capability, the diverse branch block (DBB) module is introduced into the backbone22, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The basic principle of DBB is to increase the structural complexity of convolutional layers during the training phase by introducing convolutional branches with different sizes and structures to enrich feature representations. Specifically, the multi-branch structure used during training is shown in Fig. 1(a), while these branches are equivalently re-parameterized and merged into a single convolutional layer during inference, as shown in Fig. 1(b).

Fig. 1A representative design of diverse branch block (DBB).

A representative design of diverse branch block (DBB).

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the Diverse Branch Block (DBB) adopts a multi-branch architecture during the training phase (Fig. 1(a)), where the input feature is processed in parallel by multiple branches with different structures and receptive fields. Specifically, these branches include a standalone 1 × 1 convolution branch for channel-wise feature reorganization, a sequential 1 × 1–K×K convolution branch that enhances spatial feature modeling by first adjusting channel dimensions and then performing spatial convolution, a 1 × 1–AVG branch in which average pooling introduces local smoothing and contextual information, and a direct K×K convolution branch for capturing local spatial features. Each branch is followed by a batch normalization (BN) layer to stabilize training, and a nonlinearity is applied after branch aggregation to further improve feature representation during training.

During training, the outputs of all branches are fused via element-wise summation to produce the final output feature. At inference time (Fig. 1(b)), the multi-branch structure is equivalently transformed and merged into a single K×K convolutional layer through structural re-parameterization, with the fused convolution kernel and bias retained. This conversion introduces no additional computational overhead during inference, enabling DBB to maintain the same computational complexity and execution efficiency as a standard convolutional layer while inheriting the enhanced representational capacity learned from the multi-branch structure during training.Therefore, DBB effectively enhances the feature representation capability and detection performance of the model without altering the overall network architecture or increasing inference-time computational cost.

By introducing multiple convolutional combinations (including 1 × 1 and K×K convolutions) and equivalently simplifying these structures into a more compact form during inference (e.g., the concatenation-based operation shown as Transform IV in Fig. 3), DBB is able to enhance feature representation while maintaining efficient inference.

Specifically, the core idea of Transform IV lies in the observation that when multiple parallel branches operate on the same input feature map with identical spatial configurations (i.e., the same kernel size, stride, and padding), their outputs are spatially aligned and can therefore be equivalently represented through channel-wise concatenation and re-parameterization. During training, each branch performs convolution independently, and their outputs are fused at the feature level. In the inference stage, this fusion can be reformulated as a single convolution operation by constructing an equivalent convolutional kernel and bias.

The macro-architectural invariance of DBB enables it to be seamlessly integrated into existing networks as a direct replacement for standard convolutional layers, without requiring any modification to the overall network architecture. Specifically, macro-architectural invariance refers to the ability of DBB to adapt to mainstream network topologies, such as ResNet and the YOLO series, without relying on redesign or restructuring of the original architecture. Overall, DBB effectively enhances the feature extraction capability of the model while preserving the original network topology and inference-time computational complexity, thereby achieving an efficient balance between performance and efficiency. This design allows researchers and practitioners to directly apply DBB to existing deep learning models without extensive architectural modifications, demonstrating strong generality and practical engineering value.

CCFM

The cross-scale feature fusion module (CCFM) performs cross-scale feature fusion through convolutional operations, with the core idea of integrating semantic information from different scales to enhance the model capability for multi-scale object detection. In practical detection tasks, single-scale features are prone to missed detections for small objects due to insufficient semantic information, while large objects represented in shallow feature layers often lack adequate contextual cues23. Cross-scale feature fusion is therefore introduced to address the inherent conflict between scale variation and semantic representation.

Specifically, CCFM simultaneously incorporates complementary information from high-resolution feature maps and low-resolution feature maps. High-resolution features preserve rich spatial details, which are beneficial for precise localization of object boundaries and local structures, whereas low-resolution features contain stronger global semantic information, facilitating object category discrimination and holistic structural understanding. As illustrated in Fig. 4, CCFM adopts a structured cross-scale fusion architecture to enable effective interaction and integration between feature maps of different resolutions. Such multi-scale interactions not only alleviate the performance bottlenecks in detecting extremely small or large objects, but also improve the discriminability and robustness of feature representations under complex background conditions.

The cross-scale fusion process of CCFM mainly consists of two steps:

Scale alignment

Assume that multi-scale feature maps with different spatial resolutions are extracted from different network layers. To enable effective fusion, CCFM first employs 1 × 1 convolutions to align the channel dimensions of feature maps from different scales, ensuring consistent feature representations during fusion. C2fiRMA

Tasks involving feature extraction and picture classification are often performed using the C2f module. The expressiveness and performance of the model are improved by using iRMB24 and EMA25. This new module replaces C2f. The parallel substructure enables the network to circumvent excessive sequential processing and deep architectures. The EMA system is enhanced using the CA module, and its complete structure is depicted in Fig. 5. The 1 × 1 convolution of the CA module in EMA is divided into 1 × 1 branches. To consolidate the spatial structure information at several scales, 3 × 3 kernels are positioned alongside 1 × 1 branches to generate a response and create 3 × 3 branches quickly. The effective establishment of short- and long-range dependencies, feature grouping, and multi-scale structure enhance the performance by eliminating the need for EMA to reduce channel dimensionality in the convolution operation. This system also improves pixel-level attention for advanced feature mapping.

For easily classifiable samples where pt approaches 1, the adjustment factor nears 0, consequently reducing the loss weight of these samples. The hyperparameter effectively modulates the penalization of the weight factor, and when set to 0, the focal loss simplifies to cross-entropy.

The following loss function is employed to balance positive and negative samples and hard and easy samples.14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{F}\mathrm{L}\left({p}_{t}\right)=-{\alpha\:}_{t}{(1-{p}_{t})}^{\gamma\:}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}\left({p}_{t}\right)$$\end{document}

In our experiments, the significant class imbalance during the training of dense detectors causes a significant cross-entropy loss. Most of the loss is easily classified as unfavorable, dominating the gradient. Therefore, we replace the loss function with a focus on hard negatives and reduce the number of easy examples.

This paper introduces a modulation factor that can add a tunable focus parameter to the cross-entropy loss.

Dataset

To train and validate the effectiveness of the proposed method, we constructed a dataset named QZU-DET (as illustrated in Fig. 9), which consists of 5,095 images collected from the production line of the Baoji Cigarette Factory in China. The dataset contains appearance images of the four corners of cigarette cartons and covers three typical conditions: normal products (NO), wrong-direction products (WD), and defective products (DE). The wrong-direction samples mainly originate from cartons that are rotated by a certain angle during the packaging process and therefore require manual re-inspection.

During the data annotation process, unified and well-defined labeling guidelines were established and strictly followed to ensure annotation consistency and accuracy. All images were annotated using the Labeling software and were collected in grayscale format, then uniformly resized to a resolution of 1920 × 1200 as the input to the model. Object detection–based bounding box annotation was adopted, with particular emphasis on the tape-sealing regions located at the corners of cigarette cartons. Each annotated instance was categorized into one of three classes: Normal (NO), Wrong Direction (WD), and Defect (DE), which correspond to typical tape-sealing conditions encountered in real industrial production. Figure 9 presents representative examples of annotated images from the QZU-DET dataset, illustrating the labeling strategy and spatial distribution of different defect categories.

To further ensure the quality of the dataset, explicit annotation specifications were defined, including clear criteria for object definition, bounding box placement, and handling of ambiguous samples. All images were first annotated by trained annotators and subsequently reviewed through a manual verification process to identify and correct potential labeling errors. In addition, a data cleaning procedure was conducted to remove samples affected by severe blur, significant occlusion of key regions, or ambiguous defect characteristics. Through a standardized annotation workflow combined with multi-stage manual inspection, the QZU-DET dataset achieves a high level of annotation accuracy and consistency, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent model training and performance evaluation.

GFLOPs (Giga Floating Point Operations) measure the number of floating-point operations required for a single forward pass and serve as an important indicator of computational cost and hardware resource consumption. FPS (Frames Per Second) denotes the number of images processed per second and is commonly used to evaluate real-time inference capability. In general, lower GFLOPs and higher FPS indicate better computational efficiency and greater suitability for practical deployment.

Conclusion

This study proposes a robust method for tape-sealing defect detection by incorporating DBB re-parameterized modules, a composite module (CCFM+C2fiRMA), and Focal Loss to improve detection performance. Specifically, the DBB re-parameterized modules are extensively embedded into the backbone and neck to enhance feature extraction and multi-scale feature fusion, while the composite module is deployed in the detection head to strengthen its representation capability through three-dimensional attention mechanisms without introducing additional computational overhead. To validate the effectiveness of the proposed approach, we construct a tape-sealing defect dataset and conduct ablation and comparative experiments on it. The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method consistently surpasses the baseline YOLOv8 and several mainstream detection algorithms in terms of detection accuracy and stability, highlighting its practicality and feasibility for real-world industrial inspection scenarios.

Nevertheless, this study still has certain limitations and room for further improvement. First, the current model follows a fixed-category supervised learning paradigm, whereas real production lines may involve novel defect types that are not covered during training. Future work may incorporate an open-set/anomaly-detection branch to learn the representation distribution of “normal” sealed cartons and assign anomaly scores to samples that deviate from normality but do not belong to the predefined categories, thereby enhancing the system ability to discover unknown defects and improving deployment robustness. Second, the proposed method primarily relies on deep networks to learn features directly from pixels and does not yet fully exploit quantifiable physical and geometric cues inherent to the sealing structure. Future work can explore a hybrid modeling strategy that fuses physics-/geometry-based priors with deep features, for example by extracting explicit attributes such as tape orientation angle, aspect ratio, edge straightness, and symmetry, and jointly learning them with deep representations to further improve interpretability and engineering applicability.
