Utilization of Critical Care U/S Imaging Tools for Assessment of Cardio-pulmonary Complications in Extracorporeal Circulatory Support in Patients Post Cardiac Surgery and ECMO
Authors: Mohamed A. Ragab, Suzi F. Mikhael, Amr E. Elhadidy, Ihab A. Abdelmoneim, Khaled A. Al faraidy, Abdulrahman I. Alhusil, Mohsen S. Abdelazeem, Abdelraouf Fahmy, Sarah H. Yacoub, Fathia A. Samter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtracorporeal life support (ECLS), including extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), is increasingly used in patients post-cardiac surgery. However, cardiopulmonary complications and delayed weaning remain common.ObjectivesThis study aims to investigate the clinical course of post-cardiac surgery and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation patients using lung ultrasound and echocardiography.Patient and methodsThis prospective multicenter observational study was conducted in the intensive care units of King Fahd Military Medical Complex, Saudi Arabia, and Cairo University, Egypt. A total of 103 adult patients requiring ECMO or post-cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass were enrolled.ResultPostoperative echocardiographic assessment showed significant reductions in EF, TAPSE, and VTI, with increased VTI variability. Extubation time correlated significantly with EF, LVESD, LA diameter, and postoperative VTI variability. Diaphragmatic ultrasound revealed reduced excursion and thickness fraction postoperatively, both strongly associated with prolonged ventilation. LUS detected atelectasis, pleural effusions, and diaphragmatic dysfunction earlier than conventional imaging.ConclusionCCUS is a valuable bedside tool in post-cardiac surgery and ECMO patients. Echocardiographic parameters (EF, LVESD, and VTI variability) and diaphragmatic function indices are strong predictors of weaning success, while LUS facilitates early pulmonary complication detection. Incorporating CCUS into routine monitoring may enhance patient outcomes and optimize weaning strategies.

Introduction

Extracorporeal life support (ECLS) has revolutionized critical care, providing a lifeline for patients with severe heart or lung failure. By temporarily taking over the functions of the heart or lungs, ECLS offers a more robust and controlled support system than traditional methods. This technology is particularly crucial for patients who don’t respond to conventional treatments [1].

ECLS can be configured to provide various levels of support, from purely respiratory to full cardiopulmonary [2]. However, cardiac surgery, especially procedures involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), can disrupt the body’s delicate balance. To monitor patients closely, healthcare providers rely on advanced imaging techniques [3].

Echocardiography is a cornerstone of critical care imaging, which provides invaluable information about cardiac function and structure. It’s particularly useful in post-cardiac surgery patients for assessing complications like pericardial effusion and ventricular dysfunction [4,5].

Recently, lung ultrasound (LUS) has emerged as a powerful bedside tool for evaluating lung health [6]. LUS can detect and assess the severity of various pulmonary complications, including atelectasis, pleural effusions, and pneumonia. It’s also effective in monitoring the impact of treatments and identifying early signs of deterioration [7].

For ECMO patients, LUS plays a crucial role in assessing lung function and predicting outcomes. Studies have shown that LUS can accurately identify patients at risk of developing acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and predict mortality [8,9].

Diaphragmatic dysfunction, a common complication after cardiac surgery, can significantly impact recovery. LUS can help evaluate diaphragmatic function and identify patients who may benefit from targeted interventions [10]. By providing real-time, point-of-care imaging, LUS empowers clinicians to make timely decisions and optimize patient care.

As technology continues to advance, LUS is poised to become an indispensable tool in the management of post-cardiac surgery and ECMO patients. By combining the power of echocardiography and LUS, healthcare providers can gain a comprehensive understanding of a patient’s condition and implement appropriate interventions to improve outcomes.

1.1. Patient and methods

1.1.1. Study design and setting

This was a prospective, multicenter, observational study conducted at the intensive care units (ICUs) of King Fahd Military Medical Complex, Saudi Arabia, and Cairo University’s Faculty of Medicine, Egypt.

1.1.2. Study rationale and hypothesis

The management of patients on extracorporeal life support (ECLS) or recovering from major cardiac surgery is complex, with cardiopulmonary complications being a primary cause of morbidity and mortality. Traditional monitoring (e.g., vital signs, blood gases, chest X-rays) often lags behind the patient’s real-time physiological status. We hypothesize that the implementation of a protocolized, daily critical care ultrasound (CCUS) examination, integrating both lung ultrasound (LUS) and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), will enable the earlier detection of subclinical cardiopulmonary complications, provide a dynamic assessment of the response to therapeutic interventions [12], and objectively guide management decisions, ultimately leading to improved patient care.

1.2. Statistical analysis

Data were coded and entered using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data was summarized using mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum in quantitative data and using frequency (count) and relative frequency (percentage) for categorical data. Correlations between quantitative variables were done using the Spearman correlation coefficient (Chan, 2003). P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Regarding the demographic data, the study included 103 adult patients, with 84.5 % males. Comorbidities: 69.9 % had diabetes mellitus (DM), 66 % had hypertension (HTN), and 69.9 % had dyslipidemia. Smoking history: 58.3 % were non-smokers* (Fig. 1).

LVES diameter decreased significantly from 3.52 ± 0.75 cm to 3.42 ± 0.76 cm (p = 0.046), while EF (2D) declined from 49.44 ± 9.46 % to 44.31 ± 10.21 % (p = 0.007). Diastolic dysfunction increased from 0.76 ± 0.89 to 1.24 ± 0.89 (p = 0.034), and VTI variability rose significantly from 7.49 ± 4.01 to 13.63 ± 3.92 (p = 0.032). In contrast, LVED, LA diameter, and TAPSE showed no statistically significant postoperative differences (Table 1).

There was a significant reduction in diaphragmatic excursion following cardiac surgery. The right hemidiaphragm excursion decreased from 17.85 ± 4.35 mm to 15.08 ± 3.97 mm (p = 0.003), while the left hemidiaphragm excursion declined from 15.57 ± 4.16 mm to 13.52 ± 3.56 mm (p = 0.001). Both reductions were highly significant compared with preoperative values (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

There was a marked reduction in diaphragmatic thickness fraction after cardiopulmonary bypass, with values of 20.2 ± 2.61 % for the right hemidiaphragm and 12.65 ± 4.0 % for the left hemidiaphragm, both showing high statistical significance (p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we explored the use of novel radiological techniques to predict successful weaning from extracorporeal circulatory support. The term “prolonged mechanical ventilation” lacks a consistent definition in the literature [13], so we used the actual duration of mechanical ventilation, providing clear and actionable data for clinical practice. Common endpoints in cardiac surgical studies include time to tracheal extubation and ICU stay duration, which indicate patient recovery and exposure to complications [14]. These measures also have social and economic value, as they reflect the desire for patients to return home and the costs associated with prolonged hospitalization. Lung ultrasound (LUS) is an evolving technology in the postoperative period following cardiac surgery, showing promise in improving the early detection and management of pulmonary complications, potentially leading to better patient outcomes and reduced healthcare costs. As evidence for LUS grows, it may become a standard of care in the future. Diaphragmatic function was reduced in almost all patients, likely due to exposure to cardiopulmonary bypass and inflammatory mediators, requiring varying durations of mechanical ventilation. Diaphragmatic dysfunction is clinically relevant in cardiac surgery patients, impacting ventilator weaning time, extubation success, and ICU length of stay. While several studies have analyzed diaphragmatic dysfunction in this population, few have used ultrasound techniques. Defining normality is crucial for accurate diagnosis and prognosis. Boussuges et al. [15] set the diaphragmatic excursion (DE) threshold for dysfunction at <15 mm in healthy volunteers and <12 mm in patients with neuromuscular diseases. DE is a good predictor of respiratory muscle strength and function. Obtaining appropriate acoustic windows for thickening imaging can be challenging, but excursion is easier to measure and more reproducible. In our study, we measured diaphragmatic excursion and thickness fraction preoperatively and immediately postoperatively. Preoperative right hemidiaphragm excursion was 17.57 mm (SD 4.43), and the left hemidiaphragm was 15.49 mm (SD 4.16). Postoperative excursion was 14.87 mm (SD 3.93) and 13.49 mm (SD 3.71) for the right and left hemidiaphragms, respectively. The thickness fraction of the right hemidiaphragm was 19.36 (SD 3.78), and the left hemidiaphragm was 12.26 (SD 4.09). There was a marked decrease in diaphragmatic function postoperatively, strongly associated with increased mechanical ventilation time. Studies on diaphragmatic function in cardiac surgery patients with an uneventful course are scarce. Lerolle et al. [16] found that patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction (DD) had significantly lower diaphragmatic thickness and excursion, lower maximal inspiratory pressure, and vital capacity. There was a significant correlation between the degree of DD and the duration of mechanical ventilation. Patients with DD were more likely to have prolonged mechanical ventilation. This study provided insights into the role of DD in difficult weaning from mechanical ventilation, though it had limitations such as a small study population and lack of control for factors like age, gender, and comorbidities.

Echocardiography (ECHO) is widely used in critical care, especially post-cardiac surgery, due to its availability, ease of use, and high diagnostic accuracy for postoperative complications. It is also valuable for assessing cardiac function in patients on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), detecting abnormalities like valve dysfunction or pericardial effusions [17]. Mechanical ventilation (MV) reduces left ventricular efficiency, decreasing end-diastolic volume and cardiac output, ultimately lowering cardiac preload and afterload. Patients with left ventricular dysfunction and low ejection fraction require more time to achieve hemodynamic stability and weaning criteria, necessitating longer MV. Left ventricular end systolic diameter (LVESD) can predict prolonged intubation after cardiac surgery. A larger LVESD indicates left ventricular dilation, impairing pulmonary function and systemic perfusion, and increasing arrhythmia risk [14,18]. In our study, preoperative LVESD averaged 3.52 (SD 0.75), and postoperative LVESD averaged 3.42 (SD 0.67), significantly correlating with extubation time (P values 0.016 and 0.046, respectively). Grabowski et al. [13] found preoperative LVESD significantly predicted prolonged intubation, with an adjusted odds ratio of 3.3 for patients in the highest LVESD tertile (>5.5 cm) compared to the lowest tertile (≤4.5 cm). LVESD is dynamic and should be closely monitored and adjusted as needed. Left ventricular end diastolic diameter (LVEDD) can also predict prolonged intubation. Also, a larger LVEDD indicates left ventricular dilation, impairing pulmonary function and systemic perfusion, leading to low cardiac output. In our study, preoperative LVEDD averaged 4.79 (SD 0.76), and postoperative LVEDD averaged 4.45 (SD 0.59), showing no significant correlation with extubation time (P value 0.164). Zhang et al. found no significant association between preoperative LVEDD and prolonged intubation, while Fukuda et al. [19] found preoperative LVEDD >65 mm increased prolonged intubation risk by 2.3 times. There are many reasons for the mixed results, which include: Differences in the study populations:The studies included patients with different underlying medical conditions, different types of cardiac surgery, and different definitions of prolonged intubation. Differences in the measurement of LVEDD: The studies used different methods to measure LVEDD, which may have affected the results. Differences in the LVEDD cutoff values: The studies used different cutoff values for determining enlarged LVEDD, which may have affected the results. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is critical in cardiac surgery patients, reflecting cardiac function and influencing postoperative outcomes. Low LVEF is associated with increased mortality risk, higher postoperative complications, and prolonged hospital stays [18]. In our study, pre-operative LVEF averaged 47.86 (SD 11.14), and post-operative LVEF averaged 43.55 (SD 10.48), showing a strong correlation with mechanical ventilation duration (P value <0.001 preoperatively, 0.07 post-operatively). The correlation was noted with 2D mode rather than the Simpson biplane method, which is more accurate but time-consuming. Ashwin Sankar et al. [18] found prolonged MV occurred in 15 % of participants and was strongly associated with perioperative complications and mortality. Impaired LV systolic function was an independent risk factor for prolonged MV. However, the study was observational, limiting causal relationship establishment, and did not include all possible risk factors or assess long-term outcomes. Francesca Cislaghi et al. [20] found LVEF 30 % associated with prolonged MV, but the study had similar limitations, including single-institution data and lack of long-term outcome assessment. Velocity Time Integral (VTI) measures stroke volume, while VTI variability indicates changes in stroke volume from beat to beat. High VTI variability can signal left ventricular dysfunction, potentially leading to prolonged intubation. In our study, the mean postoperative VTI variability was 13.63 (SD 3.92), significantly correlating with extubation time (P value 0.032). Studies have shown that higher preoperative and postoperative VTI variability increases the risk of prolonged intubation. Zhang et al. [14] found preoperative VTI variability significantly predicted prolonged intubation, with an adjusted odds ratio of 2.3 for patients in the highest VTI variability tertile (>15 %) compared to the lowest tertile ( 5 %). Kim et al. [21] also found both preoperative and postoperative VTI variability to be significant predictors of prolonged intubation. Post-operative VTI variability is dynamic and should be closely monitored and adjusted as needed. Preoperative left atrial diameter (LAD) can predict prolonged intubation, with larger LAD indicating left atrial dysfunction. In our study, the mean preoperative LAD was 3.7 (SD 0.6), significantly correlating with extubation time (P value 0.036). Studies, including one by Lee et al. [22], have found higher preoperative LAD increases the risk of prolonged intubation. However, preoperative LAD is static and may be less useful than postoperative LVEDD and LVESD in predicting prolonged intubation.

3.1. Conclusion

In conclusion, postoperative lung ultrasound (LUS) is a valuable tool for assessing and managing pulmonary complications after cardiac surgery. LUS can detect conditions like atelectasis, pleural effusions, and pneumonia and assess diaphragmatic function, making it superior to traditional chest radiography. Our study demonstrates LUS’s efficacy in timely detection and management of post-operative complications. Echocardiographic parameters such as LVESD, LVEDD, and EF are significant in predicting mechanical ventilation duration, impacting patient outcomes. The correlation between diaphragmatic dysfunction and prolonged ventilation underscores the importance of comprehensive respiratory and cardiac assessments.
