Spatio-temporal modeling of congenital syphilis: a 15-year ecological and population-based study
Authors: Cauan Silva Santos, Rafael Pedro de Souza Nascimento, Thais Silva Matos, Márcio Bezerra-Santos, Carla Pacheco Teixeira, Diana Paola Gutierrez Diaz de Azevedo, Rodrigo Feliciano do Carmo, Carlos Dornels Freire de Souza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital syphilis remains a critical public health concern in Brazil, contributing significantly to neonatal mortality and morbidity. This study analyzes the epidemiologic profile, temporal trend and spatio-temporal dynamics of reported congenital syphilis cases in Brazil from 2010 to 2024.MethodsWe conducted an ecological study using secondary data extracted from the Brazilian National Notifiable Diseases Information System (SINAN, acronym in Portuguese). Clinical and sociodemographic variables as well as epidemiologic indicators were analyzed. The temporal trend was assessed using Joinpoint Regression analysis and the space–time clusters were identified through scan statistics. A 5% significance level and 95% confidence intervals were applied throughout the analysis.ResultsFrom 2010 to 2024, 282,919 cases of congenital syphilis were reported. Most mothers were aged < 30 years (76.2%), were black (56.5%) and had low educational attainment (38.2%). Although 81.1% of the mothers underwent prenatal care, only 17.7% of the sexual partners were treated. Most cases (95.9%) were categorized as early congenital syphilis. The national incidence rate rose from 2.35/1.000 live births in 2010 to 4.65/1.000 live births in 2024 (Annual Average Percent Change: 5.6%; p < 0.001) and all regions presented increasing tendencies, with the steepest increases in the Center-west (AAPC: 9.9%; p < 0.001) and North (AAPC: 8.3%; p < 0.001) regions. Goiânia and Belém showed the highest trends among the capitals (+ 27% e + 20.1% per year, respectively). Scan statistics identified 35 clusters in the purely spatial analysis, with the highest relative risks observed in regions of Rio de Janeiro (RR 3.1 and 3.0) and Rio Grande do Sul (RR 3.4). In the retrospective space–time analysis, 13 clusters were detected, with the highest relative risks found in Rio Grande do Sul and São Paulo (both with RR 2.8). The prospective space–time analysis revealed 4 clusters, with the highest relative risk (RR 3.2) located in a cluster spanning areas of Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais.ConclusionCongenital syphilis incidence in Brazil increased markedly, with a heterogenous spatial distribution. The results point to fragilities in prenatal care, particularly regarding partner treatment coverage, and gaps in epidemiologic surveillance.

Background

Syphilis is a systemic infection caused by Treponema pallidum, a gram-negative spirochete that exclusively infects humans [1, 2]. Signs and symptoms of the infection vary by stage, ranging from localized lesions to systemic manifestations, particularly cardiovascular and neurological complications [3]. Worldwide, an estimated 8 million adults were infected with T. pallidum in 2022, including 3,3 million cases in the Americas [4]. In the same year, Brazil reported 322,000 cases of syphilis (acquired, gestational and congenital combined) [5].

While sexual transmission is generally regarded as the primary route of spread, syphilis can be transmitted vertically at any point of pregnancy [6]. Vertical infection can lead to fetal loss (abortion or stillbirth), preterm birth, early or late congenital syphilis manifestations, and neonatal mortality [7]. In Brazil, around 83 thousand pregnant women were diagnosed with syphilis in 2022 [5].

Congenital syphilis results from T. pallidum infection in newborns. Transmission can occur either transplacentally (via hematogenous spread during pregnancy) or through direct contact with infectious lesions during delivery [8]. The usual clinical presentation includes hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, mucopurulent rhinitis, maculopapular rash, generalized lymphadenopathy and skeletal abnormalities [7].

In 2022, an estimated 700,000 cases of congenital syphilis were reported globally [9]. In Brazil, during the same period, 26,468 cases were notified, reflecting an incidence rate of 10.3 cases/1.000 live births. The Southeast region bore the highest burden (43.8% of cases; 11.8 cases/1.000 live births), followed by the Northeast (27.6%; 10.3 cases/1.000 live births) [5]. Additionally, 1,811 abortions and stillbirths attributed to syphilis were reported, accounting for 6.8% of the total diagnosed congenital syphilis cases in Brazil that year [5].

In 2024, due to the national epidemiologic situation, aligned with the 2030 Agenda of the United Nations (UN) and the initiative of the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) to eliminate vertically transmitted infections, the Brazilian government launched “Healthy Brazil” (“Brasil Saudável”, in Portuguese) program. This national strategy targets the elimination of eleven diseases and five vertically transmitted infections by 2030, including syphilis [10]. Regarding syphilis, the program targets to reduce the incidence of congenital syphilis to ≤ 0.5 cases/1.000 live births. In addition to that, it aims to achieve 95% of prenatal test coverage and treat 95% or more of the pregnant women diagnosed with syphilis [11].

Based on the above, the aim of this study was to analyze the epidemiologic profile, temporal trend and spatio-temporal dynamics of reported congenital syphilis cases in Brazil from 2010 to 2024.

Methods

Study design, population and period

We conducted an ecological study involving all new reported cases of congenital syphilis in Brazil from 2010 to 2024.

Study setting

Brazil comprises five administrative regions (North, Northeast, South, Southeast, and Central-West), which are further divided into 27 federative units (26 states and the Federal District) and 5,570 municipalities. Due to the continental dimensions of the country, national studies face challenges because of the regional disparities, which encompass socioeconomical and populational aspects [12].

Data source and selection criteria

Data were extracted from the Brazilian National Notifiable Diseases Information System (SINAN, acronym in Portuguese) via the Unified Health System’s Computing Department (DATASUS) platform for tabulation of epidemiologic information (Tabnet) (https://tabnet.datasus.gov.br). SINAN is responsible for the storage of syphilis and other notifiable diseases data nationwide [13]. The necessary demographic data to calculate the indicators were extracted from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE, acronym in Portuguese).

The following criteria were utilized for data extraction: i. confirmed cases of congenital syphilis (early congenital syphilis, late congenital syphilis and abortion/stillbirth due to syphilis); and ii. year of diagnosis (2010 to 2024). The period of data extraction and analysis was January to February 2025.

Study variables

Two sets of variables were included. The first set consisted of the following sociodemographic, clinical and maternal characteristics: age, race/color, years of schooling, timing of maternal diagnosis, prenatal care attendance, partner treatment status, case classification and clinical outcomes of the infection. All the confirmed notified cases are interviewed. The second group consisted of an epidemiologic indicator: congenital syphilis incidence rate (cases/1.000 live births).

To calculate this rate, the numerator should be the number of reported cases of congenital syphilis in a certain location and year, and the denominator the number of live births in the same location and period, multiplying the result by 1,000.

Study steps

Step 1

Descriptive analysis of the sociodemographic, clinical and maternal profiles: in this step, the variables were summarized using absolute and relative (%) frequencies.

Step 2

Temporal trend analysis: in this step, the Joinpoint Regression (using the Joinpoint Regression Program, 4.6.0.1, National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, EUA) was used to determine the temporal trend of the incidence rate of new cases of congenital syphilis. The Joinpoint regression model evaluates whether a multi-segmented line provides a statistically superior fit for temporal patterns compared to a straight line or a model with fewer segments. This approach identifies significant trend changes (Joinpoint) in time-series data and determines whether indicators show stationary, increasing or decreasing patterns overt time. Furthermore, the model quantifies trends through two key variables: Annual Percent Change (APC) and the Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC) [14]. The following parameters were utilized for the Joinpoint regression analysis: minimum of 0 joins, maximum of 4; model: test with 4,499 permutations; significance of 5%; confidence interval of 95% and autocorrelation of errors based on the data.

These tools allow the detection of geographic areas and time periods with unusually high rates of congenital syphilis (clusters). This helps pinpoint where and when the disease is most concentrated, which is crucial for targeting prevention efforts.

The analysis scans across Brazil using "windows" that move through both space and time. For each possible cluster, it compares disease rates inside vs. outside the window, employing a Poisson discrete probability model to calculate the relative risk (RR) of each cluster [16]. The test to identify clusters comes from the maximum likelihood method, with the alternative hypothesis being that there is a high risk inside the window compared to outside. We used statistical tests (with 999 Monte Carlo simulations) to confirm which clusters were real patterns (p < 0.05) and not just random chance [16].

The software used to run the spatial analyses was SaTScan (version 10.1.3, National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, USA) and the maps were created using QGis (version 2.14.11, Open Source Geospatial, Foundation (OSGeo), Beaverton, OR, USA).

Ethical aspects

In accordance to the Resolution 510/2016 of the Research Ethics Committee (CEP/CONEP, acronym in Portuguese), the present study does not require ethical assessment because it uses secondary data from the public domain.

Black and mixed-race/pardo women accounted for 56.5% (n = 159,794) of cases, while 38.2% (n = 110,871) had low educational attainment (≤ 9 years of schooling). Epidemiological profile of congenital syphilis cases in Brazil, 2010–2024

Most mothers (55.8%; n = 157,810) were diagnosed during prenatal care, with 81.1% (n = 229,434) receiving some prenatal monitoring. However, only 17.7% (n = 50,041) of partners received treatment. Clinically, 95.9% (n = 271,324) were early congenital cases while 3.9% (n = 11,108) resulted in abortions/stillbirths. Lethality was around 1.5% (n = 4,263). Demographic and clinical patterns remained roughly stable across study periods, although the percentage of cases diagnosed in prenatal care increased over time as well as partner treatment (Table 1).

Brazil showed a significant increasing trend (from 2.35 to 4.65 cases/1,000 live births; AAPC + 5.7%; p < 0.001), with all regions following this pattern but showing distinct variations. The Northeast, Southeast and South regions mirrored national trends with two inflections and three temporal patterns. These regions presented declines after 2022 (−27.8%, −30.0%, 27.9%, respectively), while North and Central-West regions stabilized after 2018 and 2016 respectively. Discussion

The aim of this study was to analyze the epidemiologic profile, temporal trend and spatio-temporal dynamics of reported congenital syphilis cases in Brazil from 2010 to 2024. The predominant maternal profile was younger than 30 years old (76.2%), black and mixed-race/pardo (56.5% vs. 55.5% nationally), women with low educational attainment (< 9 years schooling: 38.2% vs. 36% nationally), highlighting disparities compared to general population demographics [17]. During the period, Brazil and all macroregions showed increasing trends. The spatial analysis revealed heterogeneous distribution patterns, with persistent clusters across all five regions despite nationwide prevention efforts [18].

The epidemiologic profile observed is in line with other investigations carried out on congenital syphilis [18–20]. A study conducted in a maternity hospital in Fortaleza/Ceará involving 250 postpartum women pointed out that women between 18 and 39 years old are 2.07 times more likely to transmit syphilis vertically to their newborns [21]. Multiple factors may be associated with this phenomenon, among which the early initiation of sexual life, issues related to access to health services, as well as poor sex education about sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [22].

Non-white women are at higher risk, as observed in other studies [23, 24]. In addition to the higher risk of infection, black and brown women are more likely to receive inadequate treatment or no treatment for maternal syphilis at all (brown/pardo aOR 1.10 [95% CI 1.01–1.24]; black aOR 1.23 [1.07–1.41]) [22]. In this same study, it was observed that, if all women presented risks equivalent to those of white women with more than 12 years of schooling, 89% of the cases of congenital syphilis would have been avoided [25]. Another national analysis involving 1,851 women showed that black women were 35% less likely to start prenatal care within 12 weeks. Being Black was associated with reduced syphilis testing access [26], reflecting both structural barriers and documented racial disparities in prenatal care quality [26].

Maternal education attainment is another important factor. In our study, women with a low level of education (up to nine years of schooling) are the most affected by syphilis. It is important to highlight that the increase in the average education level in Brazil and sexual education in school environments have not been protective factors against STIs, since it is restricted to a single conception of sex education [27]. The growth of political and moral conservatism observed in recent years has also been pointed out as an obstacle to debates and actions about the sex education of young people in Brazilian schools [28].

Our study found that 13.58% of women did not undergo prenatal care. A study conducted in Fortaleza, Northeast Brazil, demonstrated that inadequate prenatal care increases the risk of congenital syphilis by 3.27 times. However, initiating prenatal care by the 12th week, performing syphilis testing (VDRL) within the first trimester, and receiving proper guidance on STI prevention are protective factors for both mother and child, reducing the risk of vertical syphilis transmission by 73%, 92%, and 81%, respectively [21].

A significant challenge in addressing congenital syphilis lies in the involvement of the pregnant woman's sexual partner. In our study, more than half of the partners did not receive any treatment, and approximately one-fifth of the notifications lacked this information. Notably, in 2016, the Ministry of Health launched the Prenatal Partner Strategy (EPNP, acronym in Portuguese) to encourage active male participation during pregnancy. According to the protocol, the partner should attend at least two prenatal consultations: the first for routine tests to detect infections such as syphilis, hepatitis, and HIV/AIDS, and the second to receive the test results and initiate treatment if necessary [29].

Despite advances in syphilis control, our study revealed an increasing trend in Brazil and across all its regions, consistent with other investigations [30–32]. The rise in congenital syphilis cases has paralleled the increase in acquired syphilis, highlighting their association [5]. One contributing factor may be the 2014 global shortage of benzathine penicillin, the primary treatment for syphilis, due to issues within the pharmaceutical industry [33, 34]. Additionally, Brazil faced a shortage of Crystalline Penicillin, another key medication for syphilis treatment [35]. This situation has also been reported in other countries. For instance, a study in Zambia involving ten health institutions found that 70% experienced benzathine penicillin stock-outs lasting one to six months. Among the 1,231 pregnant women diagnosed with syphilis, only 43% received treatment. Moreover, among the 861 women with childbirth data, preterm birth rates were higher among those not treated with penicillin (43% vs. 32%) [36].

Joinpoint regression analysis identified a significant inflection point in 2022. This shift likely reflects both true epidemiological changes and surveillance artifacts, such as COVID-19 pandemic-related disruptions, including underreporting due to reduced testing and access to prenatal care, which may have temporarily obscured case detection [37] and the underfunding of primary health care following the 2019 termination of Brazil’s fixed/variable funding floor for family health teams [37]. We retained this analysis to transparently document how health system shocks can distort surveillance data, even amid sustained epidemiological trends.

The impact of the Covid-19 pandemic on congenital syphilis records also deserves attention. In the first year of the pandemic (2020), the number of diagnoses dropped significantly. Several studies have reported a decline in infectious diseases, including tuberculosis, leprosy, and HIV, during this period [36, 38–40]. Similar trends were observed globally [41–45].

In a country of continental dimensions, such as Brazil, spatial dynamics deserve special attention. Our study observed a heterogeneous spatial distribution pattern of congenital syphilis in Brazil, with the identification of active risk areas distributed in all regions of the country. In addition, there is a large area of syphilis transmission that covers the lateral axis of the eastern part of the national territory. This spatial behavior may be associated with different factors, such as the influence of the population's living conditions and access to health services, and the underreporting of the number of cases in some areas of the country, which end up resulting in spatial gaps. A study conducted in Minas Gerais found a correlation between primary healthcare and prenatal care coverages with lower congenital syphilis detection rates [46], the influence of socioeconomic and geographic factors on these coverages needs to be further assessed.

A national study analyzed the performance of Primary Care (2022–2023), through a financing model (Previne Brasil), seven indicators observed showed instabilities within the different regions of the country. The capitals of the North region had the worst situation, for example, Macapá/AP and Belém/PA were the capitals that had the lowest prenatal coverage in the country, 17% and 15%, respectively. On the other hand, the Northeast region in general was the one with the best performance [47].

In our study, a large spatial void was identified in the North region and in the state of Mato Grosso. A Brazilian study on the diagnosis and treatment of syphilis in pregnant women, involving 20,286 primary care services in Brazil, showed that the North region was 1.80 times more likely to offer services with inadequate conditions for the diagnosis and treatment of syphilis (95%CI = 1.60–2.03; p < 0.001), with emphasis on the states of Acre and Amazonas, where they had 9.80 and 8.02 times more likely to offer deficient care, respectively. In Mato Grosso, the chance was 0.30 that primary care did not have a rapid test for syphilis and/or had insufficient amounts of benzathine benzylpenicillin (95%CI = 0.19–0.47; p < 0.001) [48].

It is worth highlighting that the retrospective purely spatial and space–time analyses have identified the clusters with the highest relative risk in the state of Rio Grande do Sul. Other studies have shown an increase in congenital syphilis in the states of the South region, especially in the states of Santa Catarina and Rio Grande do Sul, with a high number of high-high clusters (formed by municipalities with a high incidence rate of gestational syphilis surrounded by municipalities that have similar behavior) [49]. In addition, an analysis carried out at the international borders of Brazil showed that there was an average increase of 67.2% in the annual detection rate of gestational syphilis in the bordering areas of the South region from 2010 to 2020 [50].

Despite rigorous methodology, this study has limitations inherent to using secondary surveillance data. Underreporting remains a critical challenge, particularly in vulnerable municipalities, due to inconsistent completion of notification forms per national protocols [51], fragmented health information systems with limited interoperability between primary care, hospital, and epidemiological databases [52] and systemic resource constraints (shortages of trained personnel and diagnostic tools) that disproportionately affect marginalized regions [52].

While SINAN data provide essential foundations for health monitoring [51], these operational gaps likely mask the true congenital syphilis burden, especially where underdiagnosis intersects with socioeconomic barriers to care. A study suggests that strengthening municipal-level capacity for data linkage, case investigation, and laboratory confirmation remains crucial for accurate burden estimation [52]. In addition, it is necessary to consider that the underdiagnosis of congenital syphilis may be a factor that masks the health reality of the country.

Conclusion

To address the increasing trend of congenital syphilis over time, the Brazilian government should prioritize high-risk areas identified through spatial clustering while also focusing on vulnerable regions marked by spatial gaps. These gaps warrant further investigation to determine whether they reflect an actual absence of reported cases or limited access to healthcare services.

Ensuring access to quality prenatal care, timely diagnosis and treatment, and promoting intersectoral initiatives to reduce unplanned pregnancies among young women are essential. Beyond merely disseminating information, it is vital to cultivate collective awareness and a shared sense of responsibility involving all social actors.
