Assessing the role of magician patter on deception in the Three-Card Monte
Authors: Arthur Nguyen, Robert G. Alexander, Ashwin Venkatakrishnan, Stephen L. Macknik, Susana Martinez-Conde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magicians’ speech flow, called “patter,” is considered part of their toolkit to direct audiences’ attention away from the secret method and towards the magical effect. Here, we set out to determine whether patter helps induce inattentional blindness during the Three-Card Monte. As a magic act, the Three-Card Monte features a performer that shuffles three cards and challenges spectators to track the red card, using sleight-of-hand to ensure they fail at this task. Participants viewed a recorded magic performance of the Three-Card Monte. Videos were accompanied by a congruent audio narrative, an incongruent audio narrative, or no sound. Crucially, the red card had a subtle but visible water stain mark: any participants who noticed the mark had a foolproof strategy to always know the red card’s location. We found no differences across conditions (congruent, incongruent, or silent) in how many participants noticed the mark, or how long it took them to notice it. Our results suggest that patter does not significantly misdirect audiences’ attention in the Three-Card Monte, and possibly neither does it in some other close-up magic routines. Despite this, magic patter may intensify the audience’s emotional engagement, strengthen rapport between spectators and performer, and increase the show’s entertainment value.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43656-9.

Introduction

Storytelling is at the core of many magic performances1–4. In close-up magic, performers often deliver a flow of spoken narrative, called “patter,” to accompany their hand and body motions while they manipulate cards, coins, and other small objects. One generally understood purpose of magic patter, along with other social cues such as gaze direction and humour, is to “misdirect” or manage the audience’s attention5–7. For example, the magician may ask the audience to look at a given location onstage to conceal an action happening elsewhere6. More subtly, the magician may use a spectator’s name to attract their attention; intentionally refer (or avoid referring) to a specific object or area (to help direct spectators’ attention towards the magical effect and away from the secret method)5; ask audiences simple questions to turn their attention inward (thus generating internal dialogue that will compete with attentional allocation to the magic act3; or pose leading questions to build a false explanation of the magical effect in the minds of the audience8.

Magic misdirection techniques can support change blindness (in which audiences are unaware of certain changes that may otherwise be noticeable, such as swapping a card with another) and inattentional blindness (in which audiences fail to notice the presence of certain objects or actions that would otherwise be fully visible)1,2,6,7,9–23.

Anecdotal evidence from magicians suggests that verbal misdirection (i.e. jokes or other patter) can effectively direct attention away from locations where a secret move or technique might be visible and/or towards a location that maximizes a magical illusion3,6,24,25. One study also reported that audience questions posed by magicians during a trick helped direct attention towards the magician’s face26. However, no prior research has examined the effectiveness of running narration as verbal misdirection in magic performances.

Some experimental studies unrelated to magic have found that narrative can direct visuospatial attention to task-relevant locations, enhance visual detection of objects, and help sustain attention on a stimulus for an extended period. For instance, sound and verbal cues have been shown to drive attention toward a location or stimulus in both laboratory and applied contexts, such as in-car navigation systems and suspenseful film viewing27–33. Other work has demonstrated that narrative can help direct attention away from a critical location or stimulus34–36 as well as reinforce observer expectations and enhance illusory perception37. Further, non-narrative auditory events can capture or direct visuospatial attention even when irrelevant to a task36,38–44. See45,46 for reviews on using sound to direct attention in additional contexts.

Here, we set out to determine if narrative can serve to misdirect attention in the context of close-up magic. Specifically, we assessed whether a magician’s patter can pull attention to the narrative at the expense of some of the visual aspects of the performance. To test this, we displayed a video recording of a longstanding magic card routine, known as The Three-Card Monte.

When performed as a magic act, the Three-Card Monte features a conjurer that deceptively shuffles three cards (typically two black cards and one red card) and challenges audiences to track one of the cards (typically the red card), using sleight-of-hand techniques (i.e. fake throws) to ensure that spectators fail at this task.

Our experimental setup included the presentation of a single video-recorded performance of The Three-Card Monte, accompanied by one out of three sound conditions: (a) a congruent condition, (b) an incongruent condition, and c) a silent condition. The congruent condition consisted of a “congruent” narrative track, in which the magician told a story that related in a variety of ways to the movement of the cards. The incongruent condition consisted of an “incongruent” narrative track, in which the magician told a story that was unrelated to the movement of the cards. The silent condition did not include an audio track.

At predetermined time points during the performance, participants were asked to identify which of the facedown cards was the red card. Importantly, before recording the video we altered the red card by “marking” it on both sides with a visible water stain. Whereas gamblers may use marked cards to cheat in card games, magicians sometimes use them to secretly identify and track cards before revealing their identity to the audience. Though we did not point out the existence of the mark before or during the experimental sessions, any participants who noticed the mark on their own were able to quickly and accurately identify the red card at all times during the routine, whether or not they attempted to follow the magician’s movements: the mark made the red card easily distinguishable from the other two cards. We set out to assess whether magic misdirection, in the form of patter, would affect participants’ likelihood of noticing the mark. Our prediction was that participants exposed to a spoken narrative during the routine would be less likely to notice the mark than participants who viewed the same routine without simultaneous narration.

We also asked if the effects of narration might differ depending on whether the narration was congruent versus incongruent with the visual elements of the routine. In other words, if spoken narration distracted participants solely based on the presence of speech sounds—perhaps due to the participants’ need to process any speech—the congruent and the incongruent story would have the same effects on performance. However, if verbal patter effectively cued spectators to attend to specific cards or locations (as suggested by3,5,24,25,47), the congruent story might be a more effective distractor than the incongruent story, thereby hindering participants’ ability to detect the mark on the red card. Alternatively, the incongruent narrative might be a more potent distractor if participants found it more cognitively demanding than the congruent narrative. For example, because the incongruent narrative lacked spatiotemporal connections to the magic routine, participants may attempt to seek hidden meanings or links. Indeed, prior research has shown that speech and gesture are harder to understand when they contain different information than when they are mutually congruent48.

Methods

Participants

57 adults (40 female, 17 male; age range of 14–65, average of 34.6 years old) participated in this study, with 19 participants per between-subject condition. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal color vision, by self-report. All were naive to the purpose of the experiment and were paid $15 for their participation.

The experimental protocol was approved by SUNY Downstate Health Sciences University’s Institutional Review Board (protocol number 690152). All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations and conformed with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant and/or their legal guardians.

Experimental design

The following sections describe a magic routine in detail, and in doing so will reveal some magical secrets. Readers who do not wish to be exposed to the secret methods behind the magical effects should skip these sections (in particular, the Magic Routine section).

Participants sat in a dimly lit room and rested their head on a chin/forehead-rest 57 cm from a CRT monitor with a 1280 × 720 screen resolution and a 60 Hz refresh rate. Eye position was recorded non-invasively in both eyes using an EyeLink 1000Plus eye tracker at 500 samples per second. Eye position data was recorded to verify that participants observed the videos throughout and analysed for main effects of narration condition. No significant eye position effects were found, and those analyses are not presented here.

Magic routine

Participants viewed a video of a close-up card magic routine based on the “Three-Card Monte.” In this routine, three cards are presented to a spectator: a target card (referred to as the “red” card hereon), and two distractor cards. At the start of the routine, the performer shows all three cards face up and subsequently turns them face down on a table. Once all three cards are facing down, the magician mixes them in a manner designed to confuse the spectator. The spectator’s task is to identify the red card among the three face-down cards, once the performer stops scrambling them.

In our setup, the ace of hearts served as the red (i.e. target) card, and the 8 and 9 of clubs served as distractors. These distractor cards were selected for their similarity to each other, which facilitated the use of “fake throws” by hampering the spectator’s ability to track the individual position of either distractor card. For instance, the magician might reveal the same distractor card twice and lead the spectator to believe that they saw both distractor cards.

For the magician, the Three-Card Monte is neither gambling nor a game of chance: a competent performer can use misdirection and sleight-of-hand to ensure that the spectator will choose the correct or the incorrect card, as desired. Indeed, when the Three-Card Monte is proficiently performed, the spectator may find it impossible to locate the target card, hence this routine’s infamous use by street hustlers to con passerby into losing significant sums of money.

The main sleight-of-hand technique in this routine requires the performer to pick up two cards in the same hand and then drop either the bottom card (a “normal” throw, as this is the expected move) or the top card (a “fake” throw). If done correctly, the spectator will not be able to tell which of the two cards has been dropped. In other words, to the untrained eye, it is nearly impossible to differentiate a normal throw from a fake throw.

In our experiment, participants were tasked with making six keypress responses throughout a recorded performance of the Three-Card Monte, each time choosing a card after the prompt: “Where is the red card?” The first choice was made deliberately easy. This served the dual purpose of providing a control, and inducing a false sense of security in the participants, by leading them to expect that the following choices would be similarly simple (and not misled by sleight-of-hand). Live performances also typically present straightforward choices early in the routine, thereby lessening potential suspicion and suggesting that all subsequent throws will likewise be “normal.” In the current study, the performer used sleight-of-hand in all choices presented after the initial one, to increase the difficulty of the task.

Unlike some prior magic research in which the conjurer’s face was visible during the performance26, our video purposely did not include the magician’s face. This allowed us to remove any possible effects of face-related social cognition and thus evaluate whether verbal patter alters attentional deployment without the potential confound of such strong social cues.

Audio was recorded separately from the video to remove any differences in background noise across conditions, including the shuffling or ruffling of the cards. Thus, the only difference across conditions was the spoken narrative (or lack thereof).

Target card

The red card was physically marked on both sides with a water stain, which provided an infallible cue to this card’s location to any participant who noticed the stain. We used Adobe Premiere Pro (version 12.0) to modify the contrast, and thus the discernibility of the mark on the video. Through informal piloting of different contrast-altered videos, we selected a level of contrast at which we expected some, but not all, viewers to notice the mark—even though it was plainly visible on both sides of the red card (Fig. 1A–C).

Audio narratives

Both the “congruent” and “incongruent” stories were written and recorded by author A.N.

The congruent story was purposely developed as patter for the Three-Card Monte routine presented in the current study. That is, congruent audio was consistent with the magician’s hand gestures, so that the words used and actions described in the congruent story coincided with events in the card routine. Thus, the narrative in the “congruent condition” explains and justifies the hand and card movements in the same way that magicians use patter when performing for an audience.

Specifically, the three cards were named as characters (i.e. Alice, Bob, and Charlie) in a fictional story, the cards’ positions in space were sometimes mentioned, and the pauses in which participants were asked to select a card made sense within the story. For example, if the magician moved a card back and forth to direct the participant’s attention to that card, the audio likewise referred to that particular card. The congruent narrative also matched the expected difficulty of the task and the timing of fake throws. Thus, at a time when no misleading gestures or throws were used, the narrative described the red card as “not doing anything.” Conversely, at a time when gestures were misleading (because fake throws were used), the narrator stated, “it is starting to get confusing.”

In contrast, the incongruent narrative was written to have no relationship to the movement of the cards. Despite this narrative difference, the two audio tracks (congruent and incongruent) began with the same phrase (“I am going to tell you a story, please listen carefully”), introduced the same three characters (Alice, Bob, and Charlie), were temporally aligned to the video, and had the same approximate word count (217 and 206 words, respectively). Both audio tracks featured high syntactic simplicity and elementary readability, yielding respective Flesch–Kincaid Grade Levels of 1 and 3 per the Text Ease & Readability Assessor (T.E.R.A.49). Critically, only the audio varied across experimental conditions: all participants watched the same video.

Procedure

After consent forms were signed and demographic information was collected, participants were instructed to rest their heads on the chinrest and forehead rest and watch the video display. They were also informed that whenever a prompt for a response appeared, they should press on the left, center, or right arrows to indicate their belief that the red card was on the left, center, or right location.

Audio narratives were presented via a pair of Bluetooth Avantree Audition Pro headphones. Participants were told that the headphones helped reduce potential ambient noises from the room (a function of the noise-reducing over-ear earpads on the headphones); thus, they were not led to expect that the video would necessarily include sound. Background noise was minimized to prevent the additional cognitive effort involved in processing speech amidst noise50,51.

Each experimental session included five identical blocks from a given experimental condition: congruent, incongruent, or silent (i.e., the same video and audio played five consecutive times in each condition). These five blocks were treated as a within-subject variable for later data analysis (to assess any learning effects due to repetition). Each participant was randomly assigned to a single condition.

In each of the five blocks, participants were asked to choose a card six times. At each of these six points, the video was paused (and blurred, to prevent participants from studying the cards and detecting the mark outside of the context of the performance). Text then appeared, prompting participants to respond—see Fig. 1B. The video sequence resumed when participants made a keypress (using the left, down, and right arrow keys for the first five responses, and the left and right arrow keys for the sixth response, which required choosing between two cards only). We note that, though actual blurring of images does not occur in live magic shows, performers typically maintain a fast pace that minimizes the ability of spectators to carefully examine cards and detect any potential marks. Some magicians also believe they can reduce the possibility of detection by speaking rapidly, by repeatedly providing verbal encouragement, or even by explicitly asking the spectator to respond quickly to a question.

Experimental participants completed questionnaires after the first and final blocks. The first questionnaire did not mention the mark or assess whether the mark was detected. Instead, its purpose was to verify that the experiment was running as intended and that participants were paying adequate attention to the card routine. Participants—except for those assigned to the silent condition—were asked if they could hear everything well, whether the characters’ names were audible, and to recall the characters’ names. Additional “filler” questions were presented to all participants: i.e., participants were asked if they were surprised when they chose the incorrect card. The final questionnaire asked a series of increasingly leading questions to ascertain whether the participants noticed the mark or not.

Data analysis

We used repeated-measures ANOVAs for all statistical comparisons, except where otherwise noted. We assumed statistical significance only for values of p < 0.05.

Results

We investigated the potential contribution of spoken narrative to magic misdirection and inattentional blindness during a Three-Card Monte routine. We tracked keypress responses of naïve participants as they indicated the location of a red card (the ace of hearts) during a video recorded presentation of the routine, in which the red card frequently changed position with two other cards. The videoclips were either accompanied by a congruent spoken narrative that matched the movements of the cards and the magician’s hands, by an incongruent narrative that was unrelated to the movement of the cards and the magician’s hands, or by no soundtrack. Participants were prompted to locate the card six times during the routine, which was repeated five times in total.

Because the magician employed no sleight-of-hand prior to the first choice (Choice 1), we expected participants to accurately report the red card’s location at that point in the routine. Thus, accuracy varied across choice, F(5,270) = 55.76, p < 0.0001: as predicted, participants had near-perfect accuracy on Choice 1 and displayed reduced accuracy on subsequent choices.

In line with prior work exploring learning effects in magic2,19, we asked whether repeated presentations of the current video would increase the likelihood—in each successive iteration—that viewers would discover the water stain mark and thus eventually achieve flawless identification of the red card. To reiterate, any participant who discovered the mark had the ability, from that time onwards, to answer correctly every single time by simply choosing the marked card–even without attempting to track the motion of the cards. Moreover, participants received feedback after each choice, granting them the opportunity to potentially memorize each correct response after repeated viewings of the same video in successive blocks. Accordingly, the participants’ accuracy was numerically lower in the first block (Fig. 2A) than in all blocks combined (Fig. 2B), as confirmed by a paired samples t-test (t[56] = 10.5, p < 0.0001). However, they seldom attained perfect accuracy, despite it being theoretically achievable as early as their very first choice. We moreover hypothesized that participants would perform worse in the congruent and incongruent conditions than in the silent condition, due to the audio track drawing attentional resources from the visual stimuli—thereby hindering participants’ ability to notice the mark on the cards. Finally, we predicted that misdirection would be strongest in the congruent condition, where alignment between the card motions and the audio narration might further divert attention from the cards’ visual appearance.

Contrary to our predictions, the vocal narrative had no reliable effect on the participants’ accuracy, F(10,270) = 0.65, p = 0.77—see Fig. 2. We considered that a participant had noticed the mark and used it to perform the task if, at any point during the experiment, they responded accurately on every trial until the end of the routine, with a streak of three or more correct choices. Thus, a participant with an incorrect response on any of the last three choices in the experiment was considered to not be using the mark to perform the task. For participants that noticed the mark, we identified the block of the experiment in which they began using the mark: for example, if the series of correct responses began any time in block 4 (participant’s Choices 19–24), and no incorrect responses were made thereafter, we considered that participant to have noticed the mark in block 4.

Likewise, the debriefing questionnaires showed that comparable numbers of participants explicitly reported noticing the mark across conditions: 63% (7 of 19) in the congruent condition, 53% (9 of 19) in the incongruent condition, and 63% (7 of 19) in the silent condition. However, 100% (19 of 19) of participants in the congruent condition and 79% (15 of 19) of participants in the incongruent condition were able to recall at least one of the names of the characters in the story, suggesting that the congruent narrative may have captured more attention than the incongruent narrative.

Discussion

In close-up magic, the performer strategically exploits and manages the spectators’ perceptual and cognitive mechanisms. Magicians think of narrative and storytelling as important components of this process4, in which patter not only serves to keep the presentation entertaining, but helps direct—and misdirect—the audience’s attention5. Here we asked whether the magician’s patter reduces the observer’s ability to attend to the visual elements of a magic routine, either by increasing the cognitive load involved in viewing the performance or by drawing attentional resources towards the spoken narration and away from visual processing. Specifically, we hypothesized that participants viewing a magic routine would be less likely to notice a visible mark on a card if an audio narration accompanied the routine than if no audio was presented. Moreover, we predicted that congruent narration would be more effective at misdirecting attention, and therefore that participants listening to congruent patter would be less likely to notice the mark than participants listening to incongruent patter. Contrary to our predictions, we found no reliable difference across conditions, either in terms of how many participants noticed the mark per condition, or at what point in the routine they did so.

One possible explanation for these results is a ceiling effect. Because rapidly moving stimuli (i.e. the three cards) are visually salient, they could forcefully draw the viewer’s attention in an automatic or bottom-up manner6, overriding any possible influence of the accompanying audio5,52. That is, observers may have attended to the cards equally across conditions because their motion captured attention equally. Moreover, participants were asked to follow the red card: this task required explicit attention to the cards, thereby potentially suppressing attention to competing sensory stimuli such as the audio tracks. Indeed, attention is known to amplify task-relevant stimuli by suppressing non-task-relevant stimuli or events via inhibitory neural connections. Further, increased task difficulty has been shown to enhance cortical neuronal activity at the focus of attention while suppressing activity in regions surrounding the focus53. Because tracking the fast-moving cards was a cognitively demanding task, suppression of irrelevant stimuli—including the accompanying patter—may have been particularly strong, rendering the presence of audio functionally indistinguishable from its absence.

An alternative possibility is that the specific narratives presented lacked sufficient emotional engagement or cognitive demand to divert attentional resources from the visual aspects of the performance. However, there are several reasons why this explanation is unlikely. First, given that the magician’s hand gestures and the incongruent patter provided different and unrelated information, the incongruent condition may have drawn on additional processing effort and executive resources50,54. Despite this, we found no reliable difference across narration conditions, raising the possibility that participants may have simply ignored the patter. Again, this seems improbable, because even when a task does not require either gesture or speech processing, people still process both modalities in an integrated fashion48. Moreover, more participants in the congruent condition recalled the names of the characters in the narrative than participants in the incongruent condition, consistent with the possibility that congruence enhanced engagement. It is nevertheless conceivable that a different kind of patter might have directed attention away from visual information more effectively, such as if the narration pointed the viewer to certain locations (i.e. if a volunteer is asked to name a card held by the magician, the audience’s attention will be likely drawn to that card’s location). Further, patter in live shows may be more effective than that in recorded performances, which lack the social feedback that would allow a magician to dynamically adjust the patter to better hold the spectator’s attention.

As with any null findings, another possibility is that a small effect may exist but that our study lacked sufficient power to detect it. If so, future work might observe an effect of narrative on misdirection, even though none was found here. Yet, even if such an effect exists, it is likely modest and could be insufficient to produce the substantial performance benefits that magicians might expect.

Despite these caveats, our experimental task offered certain advantages over prior studies of misdirection in magic. Prior magic misdirection research has been criticized for differing from classical inattentional blindness research in four key ways: (a) Observers watching magicians may anticipate experiencing something unexpected; (b) Some magic research lacks a control condition where attention is not misdirected; (c) Magic misdirection experiments often lack a primary attention-demanding task; and (d) Unexpected objects are typically irrelevant to the task in inattentional blindness studies, but functionally relevant to the task in magic misdirection studies16. Our marked card paradigm aligns closely with traditional inattentional blindness studies in all these respects, while placing the participants’ task within the context of a magic performance. Similar to classic inattentional blindness research, the task we presented relied on a critical element (i.e. the water stain mark) that was visible throughout the trial and whose perception depended on the focus of attention. Our participants did not know at the onset of the experiment that they would be watching a magic performance, nor was there any foreshadowing that unusual events might occur: their instructions stated that they would watch an actor manipulate playing cards. Moreover, even if participants came to expect sleight-of-hand or misdirection techniques over the course of the experiment, this expectation presumably did not involve the physical appearance of the individual cards, which did not change throughout the video. The repeated viewings of the videos also likely decreased participants’ anticipation that unexpected events would take place. Further, as in classic inattentional blindness tasks, our study included a control condition (the silent condition), and the participants’ task—following the red card—was attention-demanding. Finally, as in Simons and Chabris’s20 classic study in which observers counted basketballs passes without noticing the unexpected gorilla, our participants could, in principle, choose the red card’s location at each point in time without noticing the unexpected water stain.

Our results are consistent with those of a previous study showing that the magician’s gaze direction did not enhance the effectiveness of a coin toss illusion2. Taken together, the current study and that prior one indicate that social misdirection—whether provided by the magician’s gaze or by patter—may be redundant in robust sleight-of-hand, close-up magic. Notably, both studies also found that their respective magic illusions were resilient to repeated viewings of the performance, suggesting that when a magic trick is sufficiently strong, the illusion will continue to succeed irrespective of social cues such as gaze or patter. Reporting such null results is essential; publishing these findings strengthens the evidence base, constrains theory, and prevents the field from drifting toward assumptions unsupported by data.

If narrative is not critical to guiding attention during well-executed close-up magic, why do magicians often incorporate it in their performances? This question may not have a single answer, given that magicians have multiple responsibilities as performers. They must ensure the success of the magical effects but also craft their presentations in such a way that entertains the audience. The magician Teller (the famously mute half of the magic duo Penn & Teller) asserts that “every magic trick is a story,” implying that magic tricks follow a narrative arc with a beginning, middle, and end4. Narrative can serve an aesthetic role, helping hold viewer engagement and strengthening rapport between audience and performer. Magic storytelling may also help resolve dissonance and draw the audience’s emotions, increase their enjoyment and even play a part in generating the experience of wonder4. As such, even when narrative does not contribute to misdirection, it may still help carry spectators away, allowing them to be engaged and immersed inside of a story, and making the illusion feel like a magical moment.
