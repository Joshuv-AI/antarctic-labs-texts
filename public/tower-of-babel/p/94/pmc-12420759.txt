Serum levels of PECAM-1, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1 in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus: associations with disease activity and clinical features from a single-center study
Authors: Gabriela Rybka, Kazimierz Węglarczyk, Radosław Dziedzic, Maciej Siedlar, Mariusz Korkosz, Joanna Kosałka-Węgiel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by complex disturbances in both innate and adaptive immune responses, often leading to multi-organ involvement. One of the key features of SLE pathogenesis is endothelial dysfunction, which contributes to immune cell infiltration and vascular inflammation. In this context, adhesion molecules such as platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) may reflect the degree of endothelial activation. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate serum levels of PECAM-1, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1 in patients with SLE to investigate their potential role as biomarkers of disease activity and future relapse. We investigated 52 patients with SLE: 15 (28.8%) with disease exacerbation (SLE disease activity index [SLEDAI] ≥ 5 points) and 37 (71.2%) in remission (SLEDAI < 5 points), and 12 controls matched by sex and age. All patients met the 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria for SLE. Serum levels of selected adhesion molecules were determined in all participants with the Luminex Discovery Assay Human Premixed Multi-Analyte Kit. We observed no significant differences in the serum levels of PECAM-1 and ICAM-1 between active and inactive SLE patients or between active/inactive SLE patients and healthy controls, but also considering all SLE cases and the control group. However, VCAM-1 levels were 96.8% higher in the active SLE patients (p < 0.001) and 35.4% increase in inactive SLE as compared to controls (p = 0.016), with a similar level between active and inactive SLE patients (p = 0.11). There were no differences in the selected cytokine levels between patients with renal flare and those without renal flare in the active SLE group, but also in inactive SLE group regarding the presence of lupus nephritis despite from 43.3% higher level of ICAM-1 in patients with lupus nephritis (p = 0.016). There were no observed correlations between the levels of these individual cytokines themselves. Furthermore, regarding clinics, only PECAM-1 correlated with disease duration (rs = 0.42, p = 0.010) and VCAM-1 was associated with SLEDAI (rs = 0.47, p = 0.003). In the follow-up analysis, during a median observation period of 5.5 years, 10 out of 37 enrolled inactive SLE patients developed a disease flare, but no cytokine differences in baseline levels were found between those with or without a flare in the follow-up period. In our study, VCAM-1 levels were elevated in SLE patients compared to controls, with no significant differences between active and inactive SLE; however, they correlated with laboratory markers of disease activity. PECAM-1 and ICAM-1 showed limited diagnostic utility, though PECAM-1 positively correlated with disease duration.Keywords: Lupus erythematosus, systemic; PECAM-1; ICAM-1; VCAM-1; Biomarkers / blood; Disease activity

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disease characterized by the loss of immune tolerance, the production of a wide array of autoantibodies, and the formation of immune complexes that contribute to tissue inflammation and damage [1, 2]. SLE affects various organs such as the skin, joints, kidneys, and several crucial organ systems including central nervous and cardiovascular systems [1]. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, disease flares and long-term organ damage remain significant challenges in the management of SLE [3, 4]. Interestingly, a critical component of SLE pathogenesis is endothelial dysfunction, which plays a central role in promoting vascular inflammation and enhancing the recruitment of immune cells to target tissues [5]. The endothelium, a single layer of squamous endothelial cells lining the blood vessel walls, contributes to many physiological processes, including coagulation with fibrinolysis, maintenance of vascular tone, and angiogenesis [6]. Endothelial dysfunction in SLE results from multiple mechanisms, including impaired clearance of apoptotic cells, oxidative stress, B cell hyperactivity with autoantibody production, T cell subset imbalances, and proinflammatory cytokine cascades, all contributing to vascular damage and accelerated atherosclerosis [7]. Vascular disease is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with systemic autoimmune diseases, particularly SLE [8]. In fact, SLE patients despite recent advancement in management still show a high cardiovascular mortality as compared with the general population [9]. Moreover, as stated in a meta-analysis by Mendoza-Pinto et al. [10] patients with SLE show vascular endothelial dysfunction as reflected by decreased flow-mediated dilatation.

The interaction between leukocytes and the endothelium is mediated by a range of adhesion molecules whose expression is upregulated in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines [11]. These molecules facilitate the firm adhesion and transmigration of leukocytes across the endothelial barrier, contributing to the perpetuation of inflammation and autoimmunity [12]. Among the adhesion molecules implicated in SLE, platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1 or CD31), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) are of particular interest [12, 13]. PECAM-1 is predominantly expressed at endothelial cell junctions and is involved in the diapedesis of leukocytes [14]. In addition to its role in cell trafficking, PECAM-1 modulates immune responses and vascular integrity. Elevated levels of PECAM-1 in circulation have been associated with increased disease activity and vascular complications in autoimmune diseases [11, 15]. ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 are members of the immunoglobulin superfamily and are expressed on endothelial cells in response to inflammatory stimuli, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-1 beta [16, 17]. These molecules promote the adhesion of lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils to the endothelium, a process that precedes transmigration into inflamed tissues. The quantification of soluble forms of these adhesion molecules in serum offers a non-invasive approach to evaluate endothelial activation and inflammation. Several studies have suggested that serum levels of ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and PECAM-1 correlate with disease activity indices and specific clinical features in SLE patients, although findings remain inconsistent [11, 12, 13, 18]. These molecules may also serve as potential biomarkers for predicting flares and monitoring therapeutic response, although there is a lack of papers with a longer follow-up analysis.

Given the generally known in the literature role of adhesion molecules in leukocyte-endothelial interactions and inflammatory cascades, our study evaluated serum levels of PECAM-1, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1 in SLE patients. Furthermore, we explored the associations between these biomarkers and clinical disease activity, including renal involvement, overall flare status, and as possible predictors of future relapses. We believe that understanding the interplay between endothelial activation and immune dysregulation in SLE may offer novel insights into pathophysiological mechanisms and support the development of targeted strategies to improve disease monitoring and patient outcomes.

Patients and methods

Characteristics of the patients

The study cohort consisted of 52 adult patients diagnosed with SLE in accordance with the 2019 classification criteria established by the European League Against Rheumatism and the American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) [19]. Collected demographic and clinical data included sex, current age, age at disease onset, and total disease duration. Blood samples were obtained from all participants to assess cytokine levels. In cases where kidney biopsy had been performed, lupus nephritis (LN) was further characterized histologically, using the classification system of the International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) [20, 21].

Exclusion criteria for both patient and control groups included pregnancy, breastfeeding, and the postpartum period. Participants were excluded if they had received treatment with mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), cyclophosphamide (CTX), rituximab (RTX), or high-dose systemic glucocorticosteroids (sGCS) within the past six months. Individuals with any acute illness unrelated to SLE or medical intervention within four weeks prior to the collection of research material – including infectious disease, surgical intervention, trauma, vaccination, or immobilization for other reasons – were also excluded. Further exclusion criteria comprised a diagnosis of another chronic autoimmune or inflammatory disease (including autoinflammatory syndromes, chronic inflammatory skin or respiratory tract diseases), chronic infectious diseases (such as hepatitis B, hepatitis C, or human immunodeficiency virus infection), malignancy, or a history of bone marrow or solid organ transplantation.

Clinical documentation was reviewed to determine the current dose of sGCS and the cumulative dose of CTX. Disease activity was quantified using the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) and its renal subset (rSLEDAI) [22, 23], with disease flare-ups defined as SLEDAI scores of 5 or higher.

Each patient underwent a clinical evaluation to identify characteristic SLE manifestations, both historical and current. Symptom definitions were based on standardized references [1, 4, 24]. Cutaneous symptoms encompassed malar rash, discoid lesions, or other typical lupus-related skin changes. Oral ulcers were identified as painless mucosal lesions, frequently found on the hard palate, buccal surface, or nasal mucosa. Photosensitivity was defined as a skin reaction to sunlight or UV exposure, potentially triggering erythema, or disease exacerbation. Articular involvement included non-erosive arthritis or arthralgia, primarily affecting small joints of the hands. Evidence of serositis included clinical or imaging findings of pleuritis or pericarditis. Renal symptoms comprised proteinuria, hematuria, or impaired kidney function, sometimes confirmed via biopsy. Neurological involvement referred to central or peripheral symptoms, such as seizures, psychosis, or cognitive dysfunction, attributed to SLE. Hematological findings included anemia (e.g., autoimmune hemolytic), leukopenia, lymphopenia, or thrombocytopenia, in line with recognized diagnostic criteria.

The research protocol received approval from the Bioethics Committee at the Jagiellonian University Medical College (decision No. 1072.6120.34.2018, issued on February 23, 2018). All study activities complied with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Each participant was fully informed about the study procedures and risks and provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Laboratory analysis

Laboratory analyses encompassed a range of hematologic, renal, and immunologic parameters. Standard diagnostic techniques were employed to measure complete blood count (CBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), serum creatinine with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula), 24-hour urinary protein excretion, and urine sediment. The presence of anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) was determined using indirect immunofluorescence (IIF), while anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies were assessed by both IIF on Crithidia luciliae and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; EUROIMMUN, Lübeck, Germany). Complement components C3 and C4 were measured via laser nephelometry. For cytokine profiling, 5 mL of venous blood was drawn into sterile coagulation tubes. Within two hours of collection, the blood was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting serum was transferred into microcentrifuge tubes and further centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min to eliminate cellular debris. The clarified serum was then aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Subsequent analyses were conducted using the Luminex Discovery Assay Human Premixed Multi-Analyte Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and data acquisition was performed with the Luminex 200 System (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocols. This multiplex platform enabled the simultaneous quantification of three specific serum proteins: PECAM-1, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 29.0.0.0 (241). Graphical visualizations were prepared with GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Categorical data were evaluated using either the Chi2 test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on applicability, and results were expressed as counts (n) alongside their corresponding percentages (%). To assess the adequacy of the sample size (15 patients with active SLE, 37 with SLE in remission, and 12 healthy controls), a power analysis was performed. Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.4) and a significance level of α = 0.05, the calculated statistical power was 0.805 (β = 0.195), thus exceeding the commonly accepted threshold of 0.8 for biomedical studies. Since all continuous variables deviated from a normal distribution (checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test), data were summarized using medians and Q1-Q3 ranges. Comparisons between two groups were made using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney test. For cytokine concentration comparisons (PECAM-1, ICAM-1, VCAM-1) across the three groups (active SLE, inactive SLE, and controls), Quantist™ Analysis Software (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was used in conjunction with the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Bonferroni post hoc correction for multiple testing. The same statistical approach was applied to evaluate differences in demographic and laboratory variables between the groups. Relationships between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rs). A p-value below 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of all enrolled participants

A total of 52 individuals diagnosed with SLE (median age: 49.0 years; 88.5% female) and 12 healthy controls (median age: 37.5 years; 66.7% female) were included in the study. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of key demographic variables such as age and sex. Similarly, among patients with active versus inactive SLE, no statistically significant differences were found regarding age, sex, disease onset age, or duration of illness. Among the clinical manifestations of SLE observed in the study population, hematologic abnormalities were the most common (n = 47, 90.4%), followed by joint symptoms (n = 41, 78.8%) and dermatologic involvement (n = 37, 71.2%). In contrast, oral ulcers (n = 5, 9.6%) and neurological complications (n = 3, 5.8%) were relatively infrequent. All participants tested positive for ANA at titers of ≥ 1:160, with anti-dsDNA antibodies detected in 24 individuals (46.2%), making them the most prevalent specific autoantibody. At the time of inclusion, patients with active SLE exhibited a higher frequency of skin involvement, serositis, as well as renal and hematological symptoms compared to those in remission. Lupus nephritis (LN) was confirmed in 24 patients (46.2%), and in 8 of these cases (33.3%), a renal flare was documented during study enrollment. From a clinical standpoint, individuals with active disease demonstrated higher scores on both the SLEDAI and rSLEDAI scales. This group also showed elevated ANA titers and anti-dsDNA antibody levels compared to the inactive SLE group. Complement C4 concentrations and lymphocyte counts were reduced in active SLE patients, while proteinuria was more pronounced. When comparing baseline laboratory findings between active SLE patients and healthy controls, the former exhibited reduced lymphocyte counts, increased CRP levels, and greater proteinuria. Three patients were receiving daily doses of methylprednisolone exceeding 8 mg. In total, 23 individuals (44.2%) had been treated with CTX more than six months prior to blood sampling, with an average cumulative dose of 24.2 g. At the time of enrollment, aside from hydroxychloroquine, only two patients were receiving additional immunosuppressive therapies, specifically azathioprine and mycophenolate mofetil.

Serum levels of PECAM-1, ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 correlate with clinical and laboratory parameters of systemic lupus erythematosus

Notably, there were no observed correlations between the levels of these individual cytokines themselves. Analysis of the associations between adhesion molecules (PECAM-1, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1) and clinical as well as laboratory parameters in patients with SLE revealed several significant correlations. PECAM-1 levels showed a positive correlation with disease duration (rs = 0.42, p = 0.010), lymphocyte count (rs = 0.34, p = 0.040), and cumulative CTX dose (rs = 0.53, p < 0.001). Additionally, an inverse correlation was observed between PECAM-1 and age at SLE onset (rs = − 0.34, p = 0.043). For ICAM-1, significant correlations were found with serum creatinine levels (rs = 0.38, p = 0.019) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), with a negative association (rs = − 0.35, p = 0.033). VCAM-1 levels, in turn, showed a moderate positive correlation with overall SLEDAI (rs = 0.47, p = 0.003) and anti-dsDNA antibody titers (rs = 0.49, p = 0.002). Furthermore, VCAM-1 was negatively associated with complement C3 levels (rs = − 0.40, p = 0.014) and positively correlated with the current systemic glucocorticoid dose (rs = 0.36, p = 0.030). A borderline negative correlation between VCAM-1 and cumulative CTX dose was also observed (rs = − 0.33, p = 0.05). Detailed correlation data are presented in Table 5 below.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the profile of mediators associated with endothelial dysfunction in patients with SLE, in individuals experiencing disease exacerbation, as well as a follow-up assessment of patients who were in remission at the time of enrollment, and healthy controls for a comparison. Notably, serum levels of VCAM-1 differed significantly between the SLE group and healthy controls, whereas ICAM-1 and PECAM-1 levels showed no such difference. Nevertheless, we observed several correlations between endothelial dysfunction mediators and laboratory markers of disease activity.

The demographic profile of our study cohort aligns with typical SLE populations, characterized by a female predominance (n = 46, 88.5%) and a median age at disease onset of 29 years [25]. There were no significant differences between the active and inactive SLE groups regarding age at study enrollment, age at diagnosis, or disease duration. Furthermore, the overall range of previously reported SLE manifestations was comparable between the two groups. Nonetheless, at the time of enrollment, marked differences in clinical and laboratory indicators were evident. As anticipated, patients with active SLE demonstrated elevated anti-dsDNA antibody titers, higher proteinuria, decreased complement C4 concentrations, and more pronounced lymphopenia compared to those with inactive disease. These findings, together with differences in clinical presentation, are consistent with the characteristic features of disease flares in SLE [3].

We found no significant differences in serum levels of VCAM-1, ICAM-1, or PECAM-1 among patients with active SLE, inactive SLE, and healthy controls, except for a higher concentration of VCAM-1 in SLE patients (regardless of disease activity) compared to healthy controls. Additionally, no differences were observed between active SLE patients with and without LN; however, serum ICAM-1 levels were higher in inactive SLE patients in remission with a history of LN compared to those without LN. Moreover, serum levels of these adhesion molecules did not differ between inactive SLE patients who experienced a disease flare during follow-up and those who remained flare-free. This is only partially in line with previously published data, where the authors reported increased serum VCAM-1 levels in patients with active SLE compared to healthy controls; however, in contrast to our findings, they also observed elevated levels in comparison to patients in remission [15, 26, 27, 28, 29]. In contrast to our findings, Yu et al. [26] observed no significant differences in serum VCAM-1 levels between patients with inactive SLE and healthy controls. Surprisingly, the comparison of SLEDAI scores between patients with active SLE and those in remission in this study revealed values comparable to those observed in our cohort. This suggests that specific components of the SLEDAI score may have crucially contributed to the elevated VCAM-1 levels. Moreover, unlike our findings, Yu et al. [26] observed differences in serum VCAM-1 levels between SLE patients experiencing an LN flare and those in remission with a history of LN. Unfortunately, we were unable to assess which specific factors contributed to these disparities, such as the histopathological class of LN according to the ISN/RPS classification [26]. However, this difference is further highlighted by our finding of no correlation between daily proteinuria and serum VCAM-1 levels, in contrast to the reported results [26]. According to published data, longitudinal changes in VCAM-1 levels have shown a temporal association with various indicators of disease activity, including anti-dsDNA antibody titers, complement C3 levels, serum creatinine level, as well as SLEDAI and renal SLEDAI scores [26, 27, 30]. These observations are partially consistent with our results, although there were no differences in VCAM-1 serum concentration between active and inactive SLE, we also identified significant correlations between serum VCAM-1 concentrations and disease activity markers, including SLEDAI scores, anti-dsDNA antibody titers, and complement C3 levels. Additionally, we observed an association between the current dose of sGCS and serum VCAM-1 levels. According to a study by Cieślik et al. [31], soluble VCAM-1 is a key marker of endothelial activation, which is supported by our finding that soluble VCAM-1 levels were significantly elevated in anti-endothelial cell antibody (AECA)-positive SLE patients and positively correlated with AECA concentrations, indicating early endothelial dysfunction independent of vasculitis. However, as noted by Ahmadi et al. [32], vascular endothelial dysfunction is highly prevalent in patients with SLE, and no single factor can fully account for this phenomenon.

Contrary to our observations, several studies have reported elevated serum ICAM-1 levels in SLE cohorts (regardless of disease activity) when compared to healthy controls [33, 34, 35]. Sabry et al. [36] observed that serum ICAM-1 levels were significantly higher in patients with active SLE compared to both healthy controls and patients with inactive SLE. Additionally, Sari et al. [35] reported not only elevated serum ICAM-1 levels in patients with SLE, but also identified a correlation between this parameter and SLE disease activity as measured by the SLEDAI score. Surprisingly, the only difference identified in our study was an increased serum ICAM-1 level in patients with inactive SLE and renal involvement, as compared to those with inactive SLE without kidney involvement. Sfikakis et al. [34] reported that elevated serum ICAM-1 levels were associated with specific clinical manifestations, particularly cutaneous involvement in SLE. Similar to serum VCAM-1, ICAM-1 levels have been associated with various disease activity parameters [34]. This is further supported by a meta-analysis conducted by Bergkamp et al. [29], which demonstrated a correlation between serum ICAM-1 levels and disease activity. Partly in agreement with these findings, Sari et al. [35] reported a correlation between serum ICAM-1 levels and SLEDAI score, although not with all inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein. Similarly, Sfikakis et al. [34] found correlations between serum ICAM-1 concentrations and both erythrocyte sedimentation rate and soluble interleukin-2 receptor levels, but not with key disease activity indicators such as anti-dsDNA antibody titers or complement C4 concentrations. Our findings are consistent with these observations, confirming that serum ICAM-1 levels do not correlate with most conventional disease activity indices, except for serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Furthermore, in line with data reported by Sfikakis et al. [34], serum ICAM-1 levels in our cohort were not associated with current systemic glucocorticosteroids dosage or cumulative CTX dose.

Our findings showed that serum PECAM-1 levels were not useful for distinguishing SLE patients from healthy controls, contrasting with the data presented by Bergkamp et al. [29] but aligning with results reported by Pamuk et al. [37]. However, in this study researchers observed a significantly higher frequency of the AA genotype of the rs1131012 genetic variant in SLE patients compared to healthy individuals [37]. In contrast, atherosclerotic complications were more frequently observed in SLE patients carrying the AG genotype of rs12953 than in those with the AA genotype, despite the lack of significant differences in the distribution of these genetic variants between SLE patients and healthy controls [37]. Further multivariate statistical analysis by Pamuk et al. [37] confirmed that there was no association between genotype and atherosclerotic complications. In agreement with previous data, serum PECAM-1 levels were not associated with overall disease activity, except for lymphopenia, which we found to be significant [37]. Lymphopenia, as a laboratory parameter, is included in the classification criteria for SLE [19]. Moreover, Papachristodoulou et al. [38] highlighted that an elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio correlates with disease activity. Therefore, our study is particularly relevant, as it demonstrates an association between PECAM-1 levels and key markers of SLE activity. Moreover, we identified a correlation between this marker of endothelial dysfunction and SLE onset, disease duration, and cumulative cyclophosphamide dose.

A notable strength of our study lies in the long-term follow-up of patients with inactive SLE, aimed at identifying immunological markers that might predict disease flare (median follow-up time: 5.5 years). Although 10 out of 37 patients who were inactive at baseline developed a flare during the observation period, the relatively small sample size limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions. Notably, no significant differences in serum levels of the endothelium dysfunction markers analyzed were observed between patients who experienced a flare and those who remained in remission. This finding suggests that the investigated mediators may have limited utility as predictive biomarkers of flare in SLE. This challenge has also been underscored in a recent systematic review on potential biomarkers for disease activity and flare prediction, which emphasized the lack of standardized data as a key limitation in the field [39].

Study limitations

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size reduces the statistical power to detect subtle differences or associations, and the single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader SLE populations. However, we performed power calculations demonstrating that the study maintained adequate statistical power to detect relevant differences within the context of biomedical research. Furthermore, the observational and cross-sectional nature of the study constrains causal inferences, particularly concerning the predictive value of the biomarkers assessed. Another limitation is the lack of evaluation of additional immunological factors that may play a role in disease exacerbation. Additionally, although we demonstrated correlations between the measured mediators and disease activity parameters, some of these correlations were weak and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, given the rarity of SLE and the relatively extended follow-up period, we believe that our study provides valuable insights and underscores the need for future research to identify more reliable biomarkers for monitoring disease activity and predicting flares in SLE. Time series analyses of changes in biomarker levels were not performed. Despite these limitations, the rarity of SLE and the relatively long follow-up period strengthen the relevance of our findings. We believe this study offers meaningful insights and highlights the importance of future research aimed at identifying more reliable biomarkers for disease monitoring and flare prediction in SLE.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that VCAM-1 levels were significantly elevated in SLE patients as compared to healthy controls. Although no significant differences were observed between active and inactive SLE subgroups, VCAM-1 levels correlated with selected clinical and laboratory markers of disease activity, such as anti-dsDNA antibodies and complement components. These findings suggest that VCAM-1 may reflect underlying immune processs in the course of SLE rather than serving as a direct marker of disease activity. However, PECAM-1 and ICAM-1 levels showed limited diagnostic utility, though PECAM-1 correlated with disease duration. None of the selected biomolecules were able to predict a lupus flare in inactive SLE patients at baseline. Further research is warranted to identify more robust and consistent biomarkers for evaluating disease activity and predicting flares in patients with SLE.
