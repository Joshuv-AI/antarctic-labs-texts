A novel prognostic model based on MVIRGs identifies ANGPT2 as a key target driving the malignant progression of HCC
Authors: Xian Li, Zhouli Tan, Shanru Feng, Qiang Yu, Jian Zhou, Jia Fan, Yang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a highly lethal malignancy with high recurrence and limited therapies. Microvascular invasion (MVI) is a critical prognostic factor, but its molecular mechanisms and precise predictive tools are lacking. This study aimed to identify key MVI-associated genes (MVIRGs), develop a prognostic model, and elucidate the role of ANGPT2 in HCC progression and therapeutic responses.MethodsMachine learning on multi-cohort data (TCGA, ICGC) identified MVIRGs and constructed a prognostic model. ANGPT2, the core oncogenic MVIRG, was validated clinically (IHC) and functionally (in vitro and in vivo). We analyzed its association with the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), and drug sensitivity.ResultsA powerful six-MVIRG prognostic model was developed, with ANGPT2 identified as the sole oncogenic gene. Clinically, ANGPT2 was significantly overexpressed, correlating strongly with aggressive features like MVI, vascular invasion, and advanced stages, serving as a robust poor prognostic biomarker for OS and PFS. Functionally, ANGPT2 knockdown significantly inhibited HCC cell growth and migration in vitro, and inhibited tumor growth in vivo. Bioinformatics revealed ANGPT2 fosters an immunosuppressive TIME, upregulating immune checkpoints (e.g., PD-L1) to mediate resistance to ICIs. High ANGPT2 expression showed a differential drug response: resistance to targeted therapies (e.g., EGFR-TKIs) but paradoxically increased sensitivity to chemotherapies (e.g., docetaxel, paclitaxel).ConclusionThis study constructed a powerful MVIRG model and identified ANGPT2 as a core oncogenic molecule. ANGPT2 drives HCC progression, mediates immune resistance, and differentially regulates drug sensitivity, making it a key biomarker for prognosis and treatment guidance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12575-026-00347-z.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Microvascular invasion, Prognostic model, MVIRGs, ANGPT2

Introduction

Primary liver cancer is the sixth most commonly diagnosed malignancy globally and ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant histological type, accounting for approximately 80% of all cases. Despite some advances in the diagnosis and treatment of HCC in recent years, the incidence and mortality rates remain high due to inadequate early detection and limited treatment options. Currently, surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality for HCC; however, the two-year disease-free survival rate is only 47.4%, with up to 80% of patients experiencing recurrence, significantly impacting survival outcomes [1, 2]. Therefore, early diagnosis, precise staging, and individualized treatment strategies for HCC continue to pose substantial challenges in contemporary clinical practice. Biomarkers hold critical value in identifying individuals at high risk of recurrence, guiding treatment decisions, and enabling disease monitoring. Biomarkers such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP) may be associated with the recurrence of hepatocellular carcinoma following surgical resection [3–6]. However, elevated AFP levels are observed in only two-thirds of HCC patients, and false positives frequently occur in individuals with other liver conditions, thereby limiting the standalone utility of AFP. Furthermore, the diagnostic performance of DCP varies significantly depending on the etiology of liver disease, showing higher sensitivity but lower specificity in patients with viral infections [7].

Microvascular invasion (MVI) is a significant factor affecting the prognosis of HCC. It refers to the invasion of tumor cells into the small blood vessels of the liver, a process that often marks the early stages of tumor metastasis. MVI is closely associated with the aggressive nature of HCC and plays a role in tumor recurrence and distant metastasis, serving as an independent predictor of poor prognosis [8]. Research indicates that MVI involves various biological processes, including angiogenesis, remodeling of the tumor microenvironment, and immune evasion. However, despite existing studies highlighting the crucial role of MVI in HCC, a comprehensive understanding of its molecular mechanisms remains lacking. Furthermore, most current MVI prediction models still rely on traditional pathological parameters and lack precise, dynamic risk assessment tools that integrate molecular features [9].

Angiopoietin-2 (ANGPT2) plays multiple roles in the tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis. It not only plays a crucial role in angiogenesis but also promotes tumor progression by influencing the phenotypic transformation of tumor cells and the activation of signaling pathways. ANGPT2 binds to the Tie2 receptor, inducing the detachment of vascular endothelial cells, disrupting vascular structure, and increasing permeability, thereby creating favorable conditions for tumor cell invasion and metastasis [10, 11]. Additionally, ANGPT2 participates in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of tumor cells and activates key signaling pathways, such as the AKT and Notch pathways, all of which contribute to tumor growth, invasion, and the development of drug resistance [12–14]. ANGPT2 also alters the structure of tumor blood vessels, reducing the ability of immune cells to traverse the vascular wall and enter tumor tissues, thereby inhibiting the immune clearance of tumors [15–17]. Furthermore, ANGPT2 facilitates tumor immune evasion by recruiting immunosuppressive cells, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs), which suppress the activity of T cells and natural killer (NK) cells [18, 19]. Existing studies indicate that high expression of ANGPT2 is closely associated with tumor malignancy, metastatic potential, and poor patient prognosis, supporting its potential use as a clinical prognostic marker and therapeutic target [20]. However, research on ANGPT2 in HCC is still insufficient.

This study aims to identify specific genes associated with MVI using machine learning methods that integrate gene expression data and clinical information. Based on these genes, we develop a prognostic model that not only predicts progression-free interval (PFI) and overall survival (OS) but also provides a basis for personalized treatment plans in clinical practice. Additionally, this model offers new insights and potential targets for exploring the molecular mechanisms of MVI. Our research focuses on ANGPT2, a key regulatory molecule in angiogenesis that plays a critical role in the occurrence and progression of HCC. In HCC, high expression levels of ANGPT2 are closely associated with increased invasiveness, metastatic potential, and poor clinical prognosis. Evidence suggests that ANGPT2 is involved in regulating tumor-related angiogenesis and may also influence tumor cell survival and metastasis by reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Through a systematic study of the functions and regulatory mechanisms of ANGPT2, this research aims to validate its value as a potential prognostic marker and therapeutic target, providing a theoretical basis for optimizing early diagnosis and targeted treatment strategies for HCC.

Methods and materials

Information sources

This study collected follow-up data from patients who underwent radical surgical resection for HCC at Fudan University Shanghai Zhongshan Hospital between 2009 and 2021. Inclusion criteria were as follows: all patients were pathologically diagnosed with HCC after surgery; no prior treatments were received before surgery; no evident tumor thrombus was found upon preoperative examination; no distant metastases or invasion of adjacent tissues were observed; and postoperative pathological examinations revealed no visible vascular invasion (Supplementary Table 1). Patients were classified into two groups—MVI+ and MVI-—based on microscopic examinations to investigate the impact of microvascular invasion on survival and recurrence in HCC patients. Survival time was defined as the duration from the date of surgery to the follow-up cutoff date, while recurrence time was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the observation of intrahepatic recurrence or extrahepatic metastasis. All participants provided informed consent, and the study received ethical approval from the ethics committee of Fudan University Shanghai Zhongshan Hospital, ensuring adherence to ethical standards. Furthermore, to validate our findings at the protein level, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for ANGPT2 on 151 clinical specimens from the Zhongshan Hospital cohort. Staining intensity was quantified using the H-score method, and the clinicopathological characteristics of these patients are detailed in Supplemental Table 2.

The study utilized multiple public databases, including the TCGA LIHC dataset, ICGC LIRI-JP dataset, and GEO database (e.g., GSE76427), for bioinformatics analyses. To enhance the robustness of the model, the datasets were divided into a training set and an external validation set based on their respective roles. Our methodology enforced a rigorous separation between datasets, whereby all variable selection and parameter tuning were finalized within the training set prior to assessing the model's generalizability on independent validation groups. The TCGA LIHC dataset comprised 402 samples containing transcriptomic data, clinical information, and pathological details. Samples with OS between 90 and 1825 days were selected to minimize the confounding impact of perioperative mortality and to prioritize malignant progression within the first five years. Among these, 285 samples were annotated as Micro (with microvascular invasion) or None (without microvascular invasion), forming the training set for this study (Supplemental Table 3). The ICGC LIRI-JP dataset, characterized by a large sample size and comprehensive transcriptomic chip data along with complete prognostic information, was selected as the external validation set. Another external validation set was the GSE76427 dataset, which is also sourced from the GEO database and contains detailed transcriptomic chip data and thorough prognostic information, making it suitable for further validating the model's effectiveness. The GSE20238 dataset, consisting of 91 cases of HCC tissue and corresponding pathological data, was appropriate for assessing the predictive capability of ANGPT2 regarding microvascular invasion.

For data preprocessing, low-abundance genes (zero expression in > 75% of samples) were filtered out to enhance stability. Expression data were converted from FPKM to TPM and subsequently log2-transformed (log2[TPM + 1]) to address long-tailed distributions and correct for variations in sequencing depth.

Clinical data and survival analysis

We collected clinical and pathological data for all included cases, including age, sex, vascular invasion status, MVI risk classification, the presence of satellite lesions, and follow-up duration. The follow-up data were utilized to evaluate PFS. Patients were divided into high-expression and low-expression groups based on the median H-score of ANGPT2 expression. Kaplan–Meier methods were employed to plot survival curves, and the Log-rank test was used to compare survival differences between the two groups. Additionally, the Cox proportional hazards model was applied to assess the impact of ANGPT2 expression on PFS, calculating hazard ratios (HR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was conducted using the limma package in R. The selection criteria were as follows: a p-value < 0.05 and |logFC| (log fold change) greater than two standard deviations above the mean absolute logFC of all genes. This standard ensures that the selected genes possess both statistical significance and biological relevance. Using this approach, genes that were significantly differentially expressed between MVI-positive and MVI-negative cancer tissues were identified, allowing for a deeper analysis of the biological functions and potential mechanisms of these genes.

Genomic variation analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

We conducted Genomic variation analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to evaluate the relationship between MVI status (positive/negative) and tumors, as well as to examine the association of high ANGPT2 expression with relevant pathways across different tumors. This analysis utilized GSEA v4.1.0 software to assess the enrichment of differentially expressed genes within specific gene sets or pathways, with a particular focus on KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway enrichment analysis. A significance threshold was established at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 to identify pathways and gene sets of substantial biological significance.

Development and evaluation of HCC prognostic model

We developed a prognostic model for HCC using the Mime package in R. The analytical workflow of the study includes: (1) Univariate Cox regression analysis to screen potential prognostic factors and understand the impact of various clinical variables on survival duration. (2) Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) to select key gene features most relevant to prognosis. (3) Lasso regression and Random Survival Forest (RSF) for feature selection to reduce dimensionality and enhance model robustness. (4) RSF is employed to construct a nonlinear survival analysis model that comprehensively considers the contributions of multiple features to survival risk. Model performance is assessed using the Area Under the Curve (AUC) and C-index to ensure optimal predictive capability. (5) We compared the survival differences between high-risk and low-risk groups using Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and statistical testing via Cox proportional hazards regression analysis (P < 0.05) was conducted. Additionally, time-dependent ROC curves were utilized to evaluate the model's sensitivity and specificity in predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival.

Independent prognostic role of prognostic gene features

To evaluate the role of risk scores in independently predicting survival duration, we conducted a multivariable Cox regression analysis, incorporating clinical factors such as age, cancer grade, P stage, T stage, and overall cancer stage. A significance threshold was set at P < 0.05 to ascertain the independent prognostic significance of risk scores after adjusting for clinical factors.

External model validation

To assess the external applicability of the model, we utilized the ICGC LIRI-JP dataset for validation. Risk scores were calculated using the same methodology, and the predictive performance of the model in the external validation cohort was evaluated through Kaplan–Meier survival curves and ROC curves.

Protein expression analysis

We performed a differential protein expression analysis using the CPTAC (Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium) dataset to validate the expression of the target protein in tumor tissues. Furthermore, we integrated immunohistochemistry (IHC) data from The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database to further confirm the expression pattern of the target protein in tumor tissues.

SHAP value analysis

We employed SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) to quantify the importance of MVIRGs within the RSF model. The calculation of SHAP values was utilized to assess the contribution of each gene to the risk score, allowing for a global ranking of feature importance and providing individual prediction explanations. Furthermore, SHAP dependence analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between gene expression levels and survival risk.

AUC value calculation

The calculation of the AUC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) is used to evaluate the predictive capability of ANGPT2 in the diagnosis of HCC. We calculated the AUC values at various expression levels of ANGPT2 based on the ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve to assess its sensitivity and specificity as a biomarker for HCC.

Immune cell infiltration analysis and prediction of immunotherapy response

To assess the role of ANGPT2 in the immune microenvironment of HCC, we conducted an immune cell infiltration analysis to explore the correlation between ANGPT2 expression levels and tumor-infiltrating immune cells, such as CD8+ T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. This analysis utilized CIBERSORT to calculate the relative abundance of immune cells, further revealing the potential regulatory role of ANGPT2 in the immune evasion process of HCC. Additionally, we analyzed the relationship between ANGPT2 expression levels and immunotherapy response using public online databases (such as KMplot). Specifically, this study focuses on the response of patients with high ANGPT2 expression to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors), assessing whether it influences the clinical benefits of tumor immunotherapy.

Drug sensitivity analysis

In the drug sensitivity analysis, we evaluated the sensitivity of the high ANGPT2 expression group to various targeted therapies. By calculating drug response curves, we examined the impact of ANGPT2 on the drug sensitivity of HCC to agents such as Cabozantinib, Docetaxel, Lenvatinib, and Sorafenib. This analysis provides important evidence for clinicians in selecting the most suitable targeted treatment for patients. We utilized the GDSC (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer) database and drug response scoring (IC50 calculation) to assess whether ANGPT2 influences HCC cell responses to different treatment regimens, potentially guiding personalized targeted therapy.

RNA isolation and quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and quantified via spectrophotometry. To ensure uniform normalization, 1 μg of total RNA from each sample was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix. Subsequently, qRT-PCR was performed using BeyoFast™ SYBR Green qPCR Mix, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. The relative expression levels were determined using the 2^-(∆∆Ct) method, with the mean expression levels of the control groups (adjacent normal tissues or shNC-treated cells) normalized to 1. For methodological rigor, clinical tissue validation was conducted using 10 independent biological replicates, while cellular experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates. Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. The primer sequences are displayed in Supplementary Table 4.

Cell culture

The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line SNU449 was obtained from the Liver Cancer Institute, Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China) in May 2024. The cell line was authenticated upon receipt by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling to confirm its identity. All cells were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based assay prior to experimentation and were routinely tested throughout the study. SNU449 cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Establishment of Stable ANGPT2 Knockdown Cell Lines

SNU449 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The optimal puromycin selection concentration was predetermined via a kill curve to be 2.0 μg/mL. Cells were transduced with purchased pre-packaged lentiviral particles encoding shRNA targeting ANGPT2 (shANGPT2) or a non-targeting control (shNC) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 in the presence of Polybrene (8.0 μg/mL). All shRNA target sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table S5. After 24 h, the medium was replaced. At 48 h post-transduction, stable cell lines were selected using 2.0 μg/mL puromycin, with the medium refreshed every 2–3 days until control cells were eliminated. Resistant cell populations were pooled, expanded, and maintained in maintenance medium containing 2.0 μg/mL puromycin. Knockdown efficiency was subsequently confirmed by qPCR and Western blot analysis.

CCK-8 cell proliferation assay

SNU449 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1500 cells per well and treated according to the experimental design. After a 24-h recovery period, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo) was added, and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h. The dehydrogenase activity within the cells converts the CCK-8 reagent, leading to changes in absorbance. The absorbance at 450 nm (optical density, OD) for each well was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Multiskan GO). This value is positively correlated with cell proliferation capacity, allowing for the comparison of cell proliferation differences among different groups by plotting proliferation curves.

Colony formation assay

SNU449 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well to ensure that the cells had sufficient space to form colonies. The cells were cultured under standard conditions for 7 days until visible colonies formed. The colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Excess dye was washed away with PBS, and the number of colonies was observed and counted using a microscope, with precise counting facilitated by image analysis software such as Photoshop.

Wound healing assay

SNU449 cells were cultured in 6-well plates until reaching approximately 90% confluence. A uniform scratch was created on the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip with consistent vertical pressure. Subsequently, the detached cells in the scratched area were gently removed by washing with PBS. To minimize the confounding influence of cell proliferation on wound closure, the remaining adherent cells were further cultured in serum-free medium. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 and 48 h to monitor the migration progression, and the cell migration capacity was quantified by evaluating the remaining scratch area.

Transwell migration assay

First, logarithmically growing cells (SNU449) were resuspended in serum-free medium at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/mL and seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell with an 8 μm pore size. The lower chamber was filled with complete culture medium containing 10% FBS as a chemotactic factor. The cells were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After incubation, cells that migrated to the lower chamber were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Non-migrated cells in the upper chamber were removed using a cotton swab. Finally, five random fields were selected under a microscope to count and calculate the number of migrated cells.

Mouse tumor xenograft experiment

SNU449 cell suspensions with downregulated ANGPT2 (1 × 107 cells in 100 μL) were mixed with Matrigel and injected subcutaneously into the right flank of male BALB/c nude mice (n = 5 per group, 6 weeks of age). Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured with calipers every 2 to 3 days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (L × W2)/2. The study endpoint was defined as the time point when the mean tumor volume in the control group reached approximately 1,500 mm3, in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines. Upon reaching the endpoint, animals were euthanized, and tumors were excised and weighed.

Tissue microarray (TMA) and immunohistochemical staining (IHC)

Tissue microarray (TMA) technology was employed to assess the expression levels of ANGPT2 in HCC tumor tissues and their paired adjacent normal tissues. The TMA samples were provided by the Department of Pathology at Zhongshan Hospital, and staining was performed using standard immunohistochemical procedures. Staining intensity and the percentage of positive cells were evaluated using ImageJ software to determine the semi-quantitative H-score. To ensure a standardized assessment, staining intensity was graded on a scale of 0 to 3: 0 (negative), 1 (weak positive), 2 (moderate positive), and 3 (strong positive). The final H-score was calculated using the formula: H-score = ∑(i × Pi), where i represents the intensity score (0–3) and Pi denotes the corresponding percentage of positively stained cells (0–100%). This resulted in a theoretical H-score range of 0 to 300, reflecting the overall expression level of the target protein in the tissue.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 for statistical analysis. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; if the data did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric tests were employed. All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or t-tests, while comparisons among multiple groups utilized the Bonferroni or Tukey post hoc tests. For survival data, Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Log-rank tests were conducted for analysis, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical prognostic value of MVI and its potential molecular mechanisms.

MVI is a critical adverse prognostic factor in patients with HCC. To systematically validate its clinical value, we performed Kaplan–Meier survival analysis for overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in a cohort of patients who underwent curative resection at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, from 2009 to 2021. The results demonstrated that MVI + patients exhibited significantly shorter OS and PFS compared to MVI- patients (Fig. 1A-B). Furthermore, we analyzed the progression-free interval (PFI) in 87 MVI + and 197 MVI- HCC patients from the TCGA-LIHC dataset. A significantly shorter PFI was observed in the MVI + group relative to their MVI- counterparts (Fig. 1C). Collectively, these findings confirm that MVI is strongly associated with early recurrence and poorer survival outcomes, validating its role as a key adverse prognostic indicator in HCC.

Having established the clinical prognostic significance of MVI, we performed differential gene expression analysis on tumor tissues from MVI + and MVI- HCC patients within the TCGA-LIHC cohort. This analysis revealed significant transcriptional differences between the two groups, with 196 genes upregulated and 269 genes downregulated in MVI + tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of KEGG pathways for these differentially expressed genes showed that pathways such as 'Homologous recombination,' 'Cell cycle,' and 'DNA replication' were significantly upregulated in MVI + samples compared to their MVI- counterparts. In contrast, pathways including 'Cell adhesion molecules,' 'Adherens junction,' and 'Tight junction' were downregulated (Fig. 1D). These results suggest that MVI may enhance tumor cell proliferation and DNA repair efficiency, thereby supporting tumorigenesis, progression, and therapeutic resistance. Concurrently, by altering the adhesive properties of tumor cells, MVI may create a favorable environment for invasion and metastasis.

Construction and validation of a prognostic model based on the six MVIRGs

To develop a robust and clinically applicable prognostic model for HCC patients based on MVI-related genes (MVIRGs), we implemented a stepwise analytical pipeline designed to progressively refine candidate genes and optimize predictive performance. First, using the TCGA-LIHC cohort as the training dataset, we screened for genes that were both stably expressed and significantly associated with MVI, thereby establishing a biologically relevant gene pool for downstream modeling (Fig. 1D). To reduce feature dimensionality while retaining the most prognostically informative genes, we next applied Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE), a machine learning algorithm well-suited for identifying optimal feature subsets. Through fivefold cross-validation, SVM-RFE identified eight candidate genes corresponding to the lowest prediction error rate (0.252), providing an objective basis for subsequent model refinement (Fig. 2A).

To further eliminate redundancy and improve model simplicity, we then performed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) Cox regression analysis on these eight genes. This approach reduced the candidate set to six key prognostic genes (PPP4R4, SLC2A9, ANGPT2, MOGAT2, OTC, and CYP2C9), balancing predictive power with model parsimony (Fig. 2B). Based on these six genes, we subsequently constructed a Random Survival Forest (RSF) model to capture complex nonlinear survival relationships. The RSF convergence curve demonstrated stable model performance, confirming the reliability of the model structure, while variable importance analysis highlighted the relative contribution of each gene, with PPP4R4, OTC, ANGPT2, and CYP2C9 emerging as major contributors (Fig. 2C). These findings validated the suitability of the selected gene signature for prognostic prediction and justified further performance evaluation.

To comprehensively assess the predictive capability and generalizability of the six-gene model, we next conducted validation across multiple independent cohorts, including TCGA, ICGC, and GSE76427 datasets. Concordance index (C-index) analysis demonstrated that the model consistently outperformed conventional clinical variables in predicting both progression-free interval (PFI) and overall survival (OS), supporting its superior prognostic utility (Supplementary Fig. 2A). To further quantify time-dependent predictive accuracy, ROC curve analysis was performed. The model showed excellent performance in the TCGA training cohort, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUC values of 0.851, 0.852, and 0.872, respectively. Importantly, this predictive strength was maintained in the external ICGC validation cohort, indicating strong cross-cohort robustness and generalizability (Supplementary Fig. 2B-D).

Given the strong predictive performance observed, we next evaluated whether the model could effectively stratify patients into clinically distinct risk groups. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated significantly poorer outcomes among high-risk patients across all cohorts, particularly in TCGA PFI and GSE76427 OS datasets, confirming the model’s powerful risk-discrimination capacity (Fig. 2D). Consistently, visualization of risk score distributions and survival outcomes further illustrated a clear association between elevated risk scores and worse prognosis (Fig. 2E). Finally, to determine whether this prognostic signature provided independent clinical value beyond established clinicopathological factors, multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed. Even after adjusting for age, tumor stage, and pathological grade, the risk score remained an independent predictor of both PFI and OS (Fig. 2F).

Collectively, these sequential analyses demonstrate that our MVI-based six-gene prognostic model is clinically meaningful, offering a reliable tool for individualized prognostic assessment and therapeutic decision-making in HCC patients.

Expression levels and clinical prognostic role of the six MVIRGs in hepatocellular carcinoma

To investigate the individual biological behavior of the six key genes in our prognostic model, we first systematically profiled their expression in HCC at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels. At the mRNA level, analysis of the TCGA dataset revealed that ANGPT2 was significantly upregulated in HCC tissues compared to normal tissues, whereas SLC2A9, MOGAT2, OTC, and CYP2C9 were significantly downregulated; no significant difference was observed for PPP4R4 expression (Fig. 3A). These findings were highly consistent with our qPCR validation in tumor and adjacent non-tumorous tissues from the Zhongshan Hospital cohort (Fig. 3B). Subsequently, we extended our analysis to the protein level. Data from the CPTAC and HPA databases confirmed that ANGPT2 protein expression was upregulated, while PPP4R4, SLC2A9, OTC, and CYP2C9 were downregulated in HCC tissues. Notably, a significant discordance was observed for MOGAT2, whose protein level was paradoxically elevated in HCC (Fig. 3C-D).

We next dissected the individual contribution of each of the six genes to patient prognosis. Time-dependent variable importance analysis was performed in the TCGA PFI and ICGC OS cohorts to assess the dynamic weight of each gene within the model. ANGPT2 exhibited high importance across the entire follow-up period, with its contribution becoming particularly prominent in long-term (3–5 years) prediction, strongly suggesting its involvement in late-stage HCC progression and metastasis (Supplementary Fig. 1D-E). This was visually confirmed by partial dependence survival profiles, which demonstrated that high ANGPT2 expression was directly associated with a lower probability of survival (Supplementary Fig. 1F-G). To further validate the independent prognostic value of each gene, we performed Kaplan–Meier analysis in the TCGA PFI cohort. High expression of ANGPT2 was associated with a shorter PFI in HCC patients. In contrast, high expression of the remaining five genes (PPP4R4, MOGAT2, SLC2A9, OTC, and CYP2C9) was associated with a longer PFI, suggesting that ANGPT2 may play an oncogenic role while the others may have protective functions (Fig. 3E).

ANGPT2 as a potential diagnostic and poor prognostic biomarker for hepatocellular Carcinoma

As previously described, ANGPT2 exhibited considerable potential to promote the progression of liver cancer among the six MVIRGs, and thus we selected it as the core molecule for subsequent research and analysis. To further validate the core pro-tumorigenic role of ANGPT2 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we first systematically profiled its protein expression using immunohistochemistry (IHC) on a tissue microarray from the Zhongshan Hospital cohort. The results showed that ANGPT2 protein was primarily localized in the cytoplasm of tumor cells, and both its expression intensity and the proportion of positive cells were significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to paired adjacent non-tumorous tissues. This was robustly confirmed by semi-quantitative H-score analysis (Fig. 4A-B).

Critically, we found that the protein expression level of ANGPT2 was closely associated with a series of key clinicopathological features representing tumor invasion and metastasis. In the Zhongshan Hospital cohort, the H-score of ANGPT2 was significantly elevated in tumor tissues from patients with a higher Microvascular Invasion (MVI) risk ranking (M1/M2 vs. M0), the presence of vascular invasion, and the appearance of satellite lesions (Fig. 4C-D). To further validate these associations at the transcriptomic level, we performed an analysis in the TCGA cohort. The results were highly consistent: the mRNA expression level of ANGPT2 was significantly and positively correlated with multiple adverse clinicopathological parameters, including later pathological N stage (lymph node metastasis), positive vascular invasion, later pathological stage, poorer Child–Pugh grade, 'with tumor' status (indicating residual or recurrent disease), and later pathological T stage (Fig. 4F-K). These highly consistent results from different cohorts and at both the protein and mRNA levels collectively establish high ANGPT2 expression as a core molecular feature of the aggressive HCC phenotype.

Given the strong association between ANGPT2 and tumor progression, we subsequently systematically evaluated its potential as a clinical biomarker. First, regarding its diagnostic value, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis showed that ANGPT2 protein expression could efficiently distinguish HCC from normal liver tissue, with an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.841, suggesting its significant potential as a diagnostic biomarker for HCC (Fig. 4L). Beyond simple tumor diagnosis, we found that ANGPT2 mRNA expression could also effectively predict the presence of microvascular invasion in the independent GSE20238 dataset, with an AUC of 0.688, highlighting its potential utility in assessing the risk of tumor metastasis (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Finally, to establish its prognostic value, we performed Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. The results confirmed that high ANGPT2 expression is a robust biomarker for poor prognosis, consistent across different cohorts and survival endpoints. In the TCGA cohort, high ANGPT2 expression was significantly associated with worse Overall Survival (OS) for patients (Supplementary Fig. 1C), while in our own Zhongshan Hospital cohort, its high expression was significantly associated with worse Overall Survival (OS) and Progression-Free Survival (PFS) (Fig. 4M-N).

ANGPT2 mediates ICI resistance by remodeling the immune microenvironment and differentially modulates drug sensitivity

To systematically elucidate the role of ANGPT2 in tumorigenesis and development, we performed a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis by integrating multiple public databases. The results demonstrated that, compared to corresponding normal tissues, ANGPT2 is significantly highly expressed in the vast majority of tumor types, including hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), and kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), revealing its role as a potential broad-spectrum oncogene (Fig. 5A). To elucidate its molecular mechanisms, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) showed that high ANGPT2 expression is closely associated with the activation of multiple core oncogenic signaling pathways, including the Cell cycle, Wnt signaling pathway, HIF-1 signaling pathway, and ECM-receptor interaction pathway, indicating that ANGPT2 drives tumor proliferation, invasion, and microenvironment remodeling through multiple pathways (Fig. 5B). This finding was supported by gene correlation analysis, which showed a strong positive correlation between the expression of ANGPT2 and that of the key pro-angiogenic factor VEGFA and hypoxia-inducible factor HIF1A (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. 3A).

We further discovered that ANGPT2 is closely associated with the shaping of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). High expression of ANGPT2 is associated with a significant immunosuppressive phenotype, characterized by a positive correlation with the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells such as macrophages and Th2 cells, and a negative correlation with the infiltration of key anti-tumor effector cells like CD8+ T cells and NK cells (Fig. 5D). Mechanistically, this immunosuppressive state may be maintained through the upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules, as ANGPT2 expression was significantly and positively correlated with the expression of multiple key immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-L1, CD276 (B7-H3), VISTA, CTLA-4, and PD-1 (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. 3B).

Given the central role of ANGPT2 in creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment, we hypothesized that its high expression might lead to resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). To validate this hypothesis, we analyzed the relationship between ANGPT2 expression levels and immunotherapy response in an immunotherapy-treated pan-cancer patient cohort using the public online database (Kaplan–Meier Plotter website). The results confirmed that the ANGPT2 expression level in the tumors of non-responders was higher than that in responders, although it exhibited limited predictive accuracy for ICI efficacy (AUC = 0.555) (Fig. 5F). Additionally, within this ICI-treated cohort, survival analysis indicated that high ANGPT2 expression is a indicator of poor prognosis, associated with shorter overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) (Fig. 5G).

Finally, we assessed the relationship between ANGPT2 expression and sensitivity to other drugs, revealing a complex, drug class-dependent regulatory pattern. Drug sensitivity analysis from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database revealed that high ANGPT2 expression was significantly associated with increased IC50 values for multiple targeted drugs, particularly EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as Afatinib, Erlotinib, and Gefitinib, suggesting a potential link to resistance to this class of targeted drugs. In contrast, however, high ANGPT2 expression was associated with decreased IC50 values for multiple chemotherapeutic drugs (such as docetaxel, doxorubicin, bortezomib, and paclitaxel), suggesting that tumor cells with high ANGPT2 expression may exhibit greater sensitivity to these chemotherapies (Fig. 5H).

In conclusion, our study suggests that ANGPT2 may serve as a potential tumor biomarker with a dual associative role. Its expression may be associated with clinical resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors, potentially involving the activation of classical oncogenic pathways and the remodeling of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Furthermore, ANGPT2 appears to correlate with differential responses to various drug classes: while showing a potential link to resistance in specific targeted therapies, it paradoxically correlates with increased tumor sensitivity to certain mainstream chemotherapies.

Knockdown of ANGPT2 inhibits proliferation, migration, and in vivo tumorigenicity of HCC cells

Following our analysis of ANGPT2 expression and its potential mechanisms in clinical HCC samples and public datasets, we performed in vitro and in vivo experiments to further validate its role in hepatocellular carcinoma. We first established stable ANGPT2 knockdown in the SNU449 HCC cell line using a specific shRNA (shANGPT2) and confirmed its high silencing efficiency via qRT-PCR (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, we assessed the impact on cell proliferation. The CCK-8 assay revealed that ANGPT2 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation at multiple time points compared to the negative control (shNC) group (Fig. 6B). Concordantly, the clonogenic capacity of SNU449 cells was markedly diminished following ANGPT2 silencing, as demonstrated by a significant reduction in colony formation (Fig. 6C). Next, we investigated the role of ANGPT2 in cell migration. Transwell assays showed that the number of migrated cells was significantly reduced in the shANGPT2 group compared to the control group (Fig. 6D). These findings were further substantiated by a wound healing assay, in which ANGPT2-knockdown cells exhibited impaired migratory ability and a significantly smaller wound closure area after 48 h relative to shNC cells (Fig. 6E). Finally, we investigated the role of ANGPT2 in tumorigenesis in vivo using a xenograft mouse model. We subcutaneously transplanted SNU449 cells with ANGPT2 knockdown into nude mice and regularly monitored tumor volume and weight. The results showed that the tumors in the shNC group were significantly larger and heavier than those in the shANGPT2 group (Fig. 6F). This result is consistent with our in vitro studies and further confirms that ANGPT2 knockdown inhibits the proliferation of HCC cells.

Discussion

This study integrated machine learning, multi-cohort clinical validation, and systematic in vitro and in vivo functional experiments to reveal the critical role of ANGPT2 in HCC. Our research not only successfully constructed a robust prognostic model based on MVIRGs centered on ANGPT2, but also systematically elucidated the complex biological behaviors of ANGPT2 in driving HCC malignant progression, remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), and differentially regulating therapeutic responses. These findings provide novel molecular targets and a theoretical basis for the precise stratification, prognostic prediction, and development of individualized therapeutic strategies for HCC.

Microvascular invasion (MVI) is widely recognized as a key driver of early postoperative recurrence and poor prognosis in HCC. However, its diagnosis relies on postoperative pathology, which limits its application in preoperative decision-making [8, 9]. Therefore, developing molecular models that can preoperatively predict MVI risk and assess overall prognosis is an urgent need in current HCC clinical research. The prognostic model for HCC patients constructed in this study, based on six MVIRGs (PPP4R4, SLC2A9, ANGPT2, MOGAT2, OTC, and CYP2C9), demonstrates favorable performance in prediction. Methodologically, we employed an advanced machine learning workflow combining Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE), LASSO regression, and Random Survival Forest (RSF). This approach is better equipped to capture complex non-linear interactions among genes, thereby constructing a more robust and precise predictive model. Furthermore, the predictive efficacy of this model was not only favorable in the TCGA training set but was also consistently validated in multiple independent external cohorts.

In our prognostic model, ANGPT2 was identified as a significant pro-oncogenic gene. To validate this, we first consistently confirmed through multi-database analysis combined with experimental data from our in-house cohort that both mRNA and protein levels of ANGPT2 were significantly upregulated in HCC tissues. This finding is highly consistent with numerous external studies—whether detected in tumor tissues or measured in serum, high levels of ANGPT2 have been widely verified as an independent and potent predictor of shortened OS and PFS in HCC patients [21–23]. A large-sample study involving 767 HCC patients clearly indicated that elevated plasma ANGPT2 levels were significantly associated with more advanced clinicopathological features [24]. Furthermore, our study also revealed the significant potential of ANGPT2 as a biomarker for clinical diagnosis and risk assessment. ROC analysis demonstrated that ANGPT2 protein expression can effectively distinguish HCC from para-cancerous tissues, providing a promising new tool for the early screening and monitoring of HCC. The pro-oncogenic role of ANGPT2 is not limited to HCC. A pan-cancer analysis covering 21 tumor types also confirmed that ANGPT2 is highly expressed in the vast majority of these tumors and is closely associated with disease progression and poor prognosis. More importantly, this pan-cancer analysis revealed that its functions are enriched in core oncogenic pathways such as tumor angiogenesis, growth factor signaling, and extracellular matrix remodeling, which is highly consistent with the findings of our present study [25].

HCC is a highly vascularized tumor, and there is complex crosstalk between angiogenesis and immune regulation within its tumor microenvironment [26]. We found that high ANGPT2 expression is closely associated with an immunosuppressive TIME (Tumor Immune Microenvironment) and further revealed its potential mechanism for mediating resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). ANGPT2 may systematically construct an immunosuppressive microenvironment through multiple mechanisms. Firstly, it "physically excludes" T cells by disrupting vascular integrity. Reportedly, ANGPT2 acts as a competitive antagonist to its receptor Tie2, interfering with the vessel-stabilizing effects of ANGPT1. This leads to loosened endothelial cell junctions, reduced pericyte coverage, and the formation of "leaky" and abnormal vascular structures [27, 28]. This ANGPT2-driven vascular instability creates a physical barrier at the tumor margins, thereby effectively preventing the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the tumor core—a phenomenon known as "T-cell exclusion" [29, 30]. The negative correlation we observed in our study between ANGPT2 expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration is a direct manifestation of this mechanism. Therefore, blocking ANGPT2 through pharmacological or genetic means may repair the vascular barrier, promote T cell infiltration, and consequently enhance the anti-tumor immune effect [31, 32]. Secondly, ANGPT2 actively "recruits and induces" immunosuppressive myeloid cells. Our analysis revealed a positive correlation between ANGPT2 expression and macrophage infiltration. The underlying mechanism may be that ANGPT2 acts as a chemoattractant, recruiting Tie2-expressing monocytes (TEMs) into the tumor tissue [33–35]. Induced by signals within the tumor microenvironment, these monocytes preferentially differentiate into pro-tumoral M2-type tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). These TAMs then secrete cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β to directly inhibit the cytotoxic functions of NK cells and CD8+ T cells, and also promote the recruitment of regulatory T cells (Tregs), collectively establishing an immunosuppressive network [36, 37]. Finally, ANGPT2 may directly "arm" immune cells by upregulating the expression of immune checkpoint molecules. Previous studies have confirmed that ANGPT2 can induce the upregulation of PD-L1 expression. Moreover, our bioinformatics analysis also showed that ANGPT2 expression levels are significantly positively correlated with multiple key immune checkpoint molecules, such as PD-L1, CTLA-4, and VISTA [38, 39]. It should be noted that the potential mechanisms mentioned above have not yet been confirmed by in vitro and in vivo experiments in the context of HCC, and we will conduct in-depth validation in our future work.

While this study has yielded a series of important findings, it also has several limitations. First, our prognostic model was primarily constructed and validated using retrospective data from public databases such as TCGA and ICGC. Although these data resources are invaluable, their inherent selection bias, data heterogeneity, and variations in treatment protocols across different regions may pose challenges to the model's generalizability [40, 41]. Therefore, validating the clinical utility of this model through prospective, multi-center clinical trials is an essential step for its translation into clinical application. Second, the functional validations in this study relied primarily on a single HCC cell line and a subcutaneous tumor model. Given the high heterogeneity of HCC, these models may not fully recapitulate the complex tumor microenvironment in vivo. In the future, using patient-derived tumor organoids or orthotopic xenograft models will allow for a more realistic assessment of ANGPT2's function and its impact on therapeutic responses. Finally, the present study did not conduct experimental investigations into the biological role or molecular mechanisms of ANGPT2 in microvascular invasion in hepatocellular carcinoma, nor did it explore potential strategies to reverse ANGPT2-promoted microvascular invasion. Therefore, the specific signaling pathways through which ANGPT2 drives microvascular invasion remain unclear. In future research, we will systematically validate the role of ANGPT2 in microvascular invasion formation through in vitro and in vivo functional experiments, including gene knockout, overexpression, and pharmacological interventions, and will further elucidate its downstream molecular mechanisms. Additionally, we will explore interventional strategies targeting ANGPT2 or its associated signaling pathways, aiming to identify feasible approaches to reverse ANGPT2-mediated microvascular invasion and to provide a new theoretical basis for personalized therapy in hepatocellular carcinoma.

In conclusion, this study positions ANGPT2 as a central molecule in HCC that integrates prognostic prediction, mechanistic driving, and therapeutic guidance, highlighting several key directions for future clinical practice. On the prognostic front, the clinical translation of our MVIRG model and ANGPT2 as a prognostic biomarker should be prioritized. Regarding direct targeting, the prospect of ANGPT2 as a therapeutic target requires further exploration. Although the underlying biological mechanisms are clear, and our study provides new supporting evidence for the application of its inhibitors (e.g., Trebananib), previous clinical translations have been suboptimal. For instance, the study by G. Abou-Alfa et al. observed no PFS benefit [42]. This indicates that while the theoretical rationale for targeting ANGPT2 is strong, optimizing its inhibitory strategies (e.g., selecting specific patient populations or exploring novel combination timings) remains a critical challenge for future research.

Conclusion

Through a comprehensive approach integrating machine learning, multi-cohort clinical validation, and systematic in vitro and in vivo functional experiments, this study provides evidence that ANGPT2 is a critical, multifaceted oncogenic factor in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). We not only developed a robust MVI-related prognostic model but also deeply elucidated the complex biological behavior of its core component, ANGPT2.
