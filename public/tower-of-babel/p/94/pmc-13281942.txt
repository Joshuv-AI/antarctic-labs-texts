The emerging role of prostate-specific membrane antigen–targeted radioligand therapy in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer
Authors: Philipose Mulugeta, Tanya Dorff, Jason Hafron, Mai Anh Huynh, Alicia K Morgans, Ashley E Ross, Rana R McKay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment of metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) has evolved in recent years towards combination therapy, enhancing survival outcomes when compared with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) alone. However, many patients who are eligible for combination therapy are still receiving ADT monotherapy, and a significant proportion of those treated with doublet combinations still experience suboptimal outcomes. Furthermore, the value of adding docetaxel to an androgen receptor pathway inhibitor (ARPI)/ADT doublet in mHSPC has not been delineated, particularly in patients with low-volume disease, and chemotherapy toxicity and tolerability remain a major concern for patients. This review addresses the significant unmet need for additional or novel treatment strategies for mHSPC, utilizing prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)–targeted radioligand therapy (RLT) in a combination regimen that uses a non-chemotherapeutic approach for the treatment of mHSPC. PSMA-targeted RLT has been shown to improve survival outcomes in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) previously treated with ARPIs +/− taxanes and is now under evaluation for the treatment of mHSPC, given its demonstrated success in the mCRPC space. Thus, continued education on RLT and its use for patients with mHSPC is important to optimize patient care. This review provides a brief outline of the mHSPC treatment landscape, focusing on the challenges and unique considerations faced by healthcare professionals when treating these patients. We also evaluate the potential role of RLT in surmounting these challenges, discussing potential barriers and solutions to its integration as a treatment for mHSPC.Keywords: prostate, prostatic neoplasms, castration-resistant, delivery of health care

This review discusses the challenges and unique considerations faced by healthcare professionals when treating patients with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, with an emphasis on the potential role of prostate-specific membrane antigen–targeted radioligand therapy in optimizing clinical outcomes.

Introduction

In recent years, metastatic prostate cancer treatment has moved towards combination therapy, resulting in improved survival compared with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) alone.1 However, with nearly 400 000 prostate cancer-associated deaths reported globally in 2022, and over 500 000 projected to occur by 2030,2,3 there remains an urgent need for alternative, effective treatments.4

Metastatic disease that has not yet developed resistance to ADT is generally referred to as metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC).5 Despite initial high response rates to ADT, mHSPC remains largely incurable.5 Typically, most patients with mHSPC will develop metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) within 1 year of treatment with ADT alone.1,5 In the absence of curative treatments, prolonging survival is the main goal of treatment for mHSPC5; it is also important that treatment is well tolerated and that the patients’ quality of life is preserved as much as possible.6 The standard of care (SoC) for patients with mHSPC has shifted from ADT monotherapy towards combinations of ADT with androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) with or without chemotherapy (eg, docetaxel), which remain underused in clinical practice despite having been shown to improve survival rates.6–8 Treatment options for patients with mHSPC with low-volume disease have also expanded, and now patients may receive local therapy to the prostate in addition to metastasis-directed stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) to limited sites of metastatic disease when all sites can be safely treated to an ablative dose.9–14

Advances in the treatment of mCRPC have guided therapeutic approaches for mHSPC,15 with many therapies first approved for mCRPC showing greater overall survival (OS) benefits when used earlier in the disease course.16 Radiopharmaceuticals (including radioligand therapy [RLT]) are an important treatment approach for patients with mCRPC.4,17 RLTs are systemic therapeutic radiopharmaceuticals consisting of a ligand that binds to the target proteins on the tumor cell, the chelator and linker that attach the ligand to the radioisotope, and a radioisotope, which enables visualization or treatment of tumor lesions (Figure 1).18

Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) is a type II transmembrane protein19 with high expression (approximately 80%) on prostate cancer cells.20 Notably, PSMA is not exclusively expressed in prostate tissues; it has been observed in the cell membranes in other tissues, including breast, ovary, skin, liver, intestines, kidneys, salivary glands, and neovasculature.21–23 Based on the positive results from the phase 3 VISION (NCT03511664) and PSMAfore (NCT04689828) studies, the PSMA-targeted RLT lutetium-177 vipivotide tetraxetan (177Lu-PSMA-617) is approved for use in patients with PSMA-positive mCRPC who have been treated with ARPI therapy and who are considered appropriate to delay taxane-based chemotherapy or have received prior taxane-based chemotherapy.24–26 177Lu-PSMA-617 is the most clinically established RLT and is currently being evaluated in the mHSPC setting, with several ongoing clinical trials investigating its use across differing disease characteristics (Table 1). The latest data from the phase 3 PSMAddition trial, presented at the European Society for Medical Oncology Congress 2025, further support the continued assessment of 177Lu-PSMA-617 as a potential treatment option in PSMA-positive mHSPC.27 Other RLTs have been evaluated for the treatment of mCRPC and mHSPC; although discussion of these is out of the scope of the manuscript (which mainly focuses on FDA-approved agents), a recently published paper by Almedia et al. provides a comprehensive review of the available evidence on RLT in mHSPC and mCRPC.28

Investigating PSMA-targeted RLT in the mHSPC setting leverages the presence of PSMA expression earlier in the disease trajectory, potentially providing benefit from RLT before the emergence of castration-resistance mechanisms that can limit later-stage interventions.29 If proven to provide clinical benefit, the approval of RLTs for the treatment of mHSPC could address unmet clinical needs by providing a new therapeutic option in patients with known PSMA-expressing disease. However, in such a rapidly evolving therapeutic landscape, healthcare professionals (HCPs) managing mHSPC need to remain apprised of the latest advancements and ongoing clinical trials in order to optimize the multidisciplinary implementation of new treatments into everyday clinical practice. Here, we consider the challenges and unique considerations within the mHSPC setting and discuss how future treatment strategies, including PSMA-targeted RLTs, may address these to improve patient outcomes.

mHSPC treatment: current best practice and unmet needs

Treatment guidelines

While initial treatment for mHSPC continues to center around targeting androgen receptor pathways,5 treatment paradigms have evolved considerably.30,31 Current US treatment guidelines (2023-2025) recommend treatment intensification using doublet therapy comprising ADT with an ARPI (abiraterone, apalutamide, or enzalutamide, with darolutamide now also approved).1,32–35 Triplet therapy consisting of ADT with docetaxel and either abiraterone or darolutamide may also be considered in patients with high-volume disease.1,32

Upfront treatment intensification has been shown to offer significant survival benefits compared with previously employed sequential strategies or ADT monotherapy.6,36–42 However, US real-world evidence suggests that a significant proportion of patients are still treated with ADT monotherapy, with HCPs citing costs and treatment sequencing as barriers to treatment intensification with hormone therapy.7,8,43

Existing unmet treatment needs

While ADT+ARPIs +/− chemotherapy have been shown to improve clinical outcomes,7,44 most patients with mHSPC experience disease progression, and survival gains can be modest, especially among patients with suboptimal prostate-specific antigen (PSA) response (>0.2 ng/mL).6,45–47 Chemotherapy toxicity and tolerability remain a major concern for patients; the TRIPLE-SWITCH trial (NCT06592924) and ASPIRE trial (NCT06931340), which aim to investigate whether the addition of docetaxel to ADT and apalutamide improves OS in patients with mHSPC and to assess the tolerability of this treatment regimen, are currently open for accrual and are expected to report out in 2031.48,49 This is a key consideration, given that both treatment side effects and disease progression have significant negative impacts on patients’ quality of life (QoL).50,51

Disease progression despite the introduction of ADT plus an ARPI into the treatment landscape highlights the heterogeneous nature of mHSPC and the limitations of solely targeting the androgen receptor pathway for disease control.6,45–47 There is, therefore, a need for targeted treatments in mHSPC with alternative mechanisms of action to delay progression, and improve disease control and OS, while maintaining QoL.52 Movement towards improved personalization of therapy in mHSPC is the ultimate goal,53 with integrated biomarker-based strategies being tested, 54,55 and RLT, which utilizes PSMA positivity on positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT) as a biomarker.56–67

RLT could offer a new targeted treatment addition that builds upon the efficacy of current SoC regimens. Based on promising results in patients with mCRPC, RLT could allow for taxane-free combination regimen, improve survival outcomes, and delay deterioration in QoL with tolerable safety, as discussed below.

Potential role of RLT in mHSPC treatment

RLT use in the mCRPC setting

There is increasing interest in earlier use of target-directed RLTs for mHSPC, based on promising results in the mCRPC setting (Table 2).68 The phase 3 VISION randomized controlled trial (RCT) was a pivotal trial that first demonstrated the benefit of RLT in mCRPC.72 Treatment with 177Lu-PSMA-617 plus SoC significantly prolonged radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS) and OS, and delayed time to worsening in health-related QoL (HRQoL) in patients with PSMA-positive mCRPC.72 Similarly, in the phase 3 RCT PSMAfore, investigators showed that 177Lu-PSMA-617 prolonged rPFS and delayed time to worsening pain and HRQoL versus a change in ARPI in patients with PSMA-positive mCRPC who had progressed once on prior ARPI therapy and had not been exposed to a taxane-containing regimen.24

Current data for RLT use in mHSPC

Although current data are limited, there is emerging evidence to suggest that patients at an earlier disease stage may benefit from PSMA-targeted RLT. This is supported by findings from the PSMAfore RCT, in which 177Lu-PSMA-617 prolonged rPFS compared with a change in ARPI in the pre-chemotherapy setting.24 Investigating PSMA-targeted RLT in the mHSPC setting leverages the presence of PSMA expression earlier in the disease trajectory and has the potential to provide benefit before the emergence of treatment-induced evolutionary pressure, androgen receptor-independent growth pathways, intra-patient inter-metastatic heterogeneity, and neuroendocrine differentiation, which are frequently observed in mCRPC.48,68,78–82 Additionally, the impact of ADT on PSMA and potential enhancement of RLT efficacy in the hormone-sensitive phase is, of interest, but is yet to be determined.83 While some studies have observed a short-term increase in PSMA within 2-4 weeks of ADT initiation, other findings suggest a reduction in PSMA uptake during this period.84–86 Moreover, studies investigating the long-term use of ADT (median use 230 days) showed significantly reduced PSMA expression and lesion visibility.87 These findings highlight the heterogeneous effect of ADT on PSMA expression and should be considered in PSMA PET imaging.

The application of 177Lu-PSMA-617 is currently being evaluated within the mHSPC setting in several clinical trials of patients with differing disease characteristics (Table 1).57–63,65,67,69–71 This includes the multicenter, open-label, randomized, phase 2 RCT UpFrontPSMA (NCT04343885), which compared the effectiveness of 2 cycles of 177Lu-PSMA-617 followed by docetaxel versus docetaxel alone in patients with mHSPC starting ADT.65,88 Initial findings have shown that patients with high-volume, PSMA-avid disease (diagnosed within 12 weeks of screening commencement) had higher rates of undetectable PSA (≤0.2 ng/mL) at 48 weeks compared with those who received docetaxel alone (41% [25/61] vs 16% [10/61], odds ratio 3.9; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.6-9.4; P = .002; Table 1).65 Moreover, with a median follow-up of 2.5 years, no new safety signals have emerged, but additional data from longer follow-ups are required.65 Other trials are ongoing.56–59,63–67,69,70 The prospective, open-label, phase 3 RCT PSMAddition (NCT04720157) compares 177Lu-PSMA-617 in combination with SoC versus SoC alone in untreated or minimally treated patients with PSMA-positive mHSPC, with results from the second interim rPFS analysis reported early 2025.27,60–62,76 A total of 1144 patients were randomized to receive 177Lu-PSMA-617 every 6 weeks for up to 6 cycles plus modern-day SoC (ARPI and ADT), or SoC alone, with a primary outcome measure of rPFS.27,60–62 Secondary outcomes include HRQoL, pain, and time to castration resistance. At the second interim rPFS analysis (data cutoff January 13, 2025), 177Lu-PSMA-617 plus ADT+ARPI reduced the risk of radiographic progression or death by 28% versus ADT+ARPI alone (hazard ratio [HR] 0.72; 95% CI: 0.58-0.90; P = .002). At the first interim OS analysis (using the same cutoff), a positive but non-significant trend was observed, favoring 177Lu-PSMA-617 plus ADT+ARPI (HR 0.84; 95% CI: 0.63-1.13; P = .125). As data were immature, crossover and follow-up continue. Secondary endpoints at this time point, including overall response rate, progression-free survival (PFS), time to mCRPC, and time to PSA progression, also favored the 177Lu-PSMA-617 plus ADT+ARPI combination, notably the proportion with PSA nadir <0.2 ng/mL at 48 weeks (87% vs 75%).76 Furthermore, in PSMAddition, the safety of 177Lu-PSMA-617 in mHSPC was consistent with its established mCRPC profile, supporting a favorable benefit–risk balance and good tolerability when combined with ADT+ARPI in treatment-naïve or minimally treated patients with PSMA-positive mHSPC.27 These interim results suggest 177Lu-PSMA-617 may be efficacious in earlier disease settings, with the potential to increase disease control compared with ADT and ARPI alone for rPFS. Notably, 177Lu-PSMA-617 combined with ADT and ARPI may offer a new taxane-free combination, benefiting patients with comorbidities or concerns about chemotherapy-related toxicity, an unmet need in real-world practice.89 However, data collection for OS and long-term safety is ongoing. Furthermore, PSMAddition did not include patients with rapidly progressive disease requiring urgent exposure to taxane-based chemotherapy (as defined by the investigators), for whom triplet therapy remains the SoC.48,90 Another key consideration in selecting patients who may benefit from PSMA-targeted RLT is tumor biology. PSMAddition included patients with high-volume metastatic disease (including those with visceral metastases) and supports the finding that PSMA-targeted RLT is a treatment option in this population for patients with sufficient PSMA expression.72,90–92 However, trial data by site of metastasis and PSMA expression intensity are not yet available. Understanding if PSMA-RLT provides clinical benefit over chemotherapy in patients, such as those with visceral disease and lower PSMA expression, will be crucial. Where possible, biopsy of new visceral metastasis should be considered to identify neuroendocrine differentiation.

Treatment of mHSPC is moving towards biomarker-directed strategies. For example, the AMPLITUDE trial (NCT04497844) demonstrated the PFS benefit of abiraterone + niraparib, a poly (ADP ribose) polymerase inhibitor (PARPi) that may be a treatment option for patients with homologous recombination repair-mutated mHSPC; however, data on OS and long-term safety are pending. It has been suggested that combining RLT- and PARPi-based therapy may have a possible synergistic effect, but this approach remains untested.93 Furthermore, patients with phosphatase and tensin homolog-deficient mHSPC demonstrated rPFS benefit from dual blockade of the PI3K/AKT and AR pathways with capivasertib + abiraterone versus placebo + abiraterone in the phase 3 CAPItello-281 trial.55 Until data can confirm efficacy, optimal targeted treatment strategies remain unclear and require individualized decision-making.

The potential role of RLT in delaying castration or disease recurrence in patients with PSMA-positive oligometastatic prostate cancer and avoiding ADT-related adverse events is under active investigation. The multicenter, open-label, phase 2 RCT BULLSEYE (NCT04443062) evaluated 177Lu-PSMA-617 monotherapy versus deferred ADT in patients with oligometastatic HSPC (≤5 metastatic lesions, PSA doubling time <6 months); at a median follow-up of 27 months, 177Lu-PSMA-617 dramatically reduced disease progression (7% vs 93%; P < .0001) and prolonged median PFS to 25 months versus 5 months (HR 0.07, 95% CI, 0.03-0.17; P < .0001), with predominantly low-grade adverse events and no treatment-related serious adverse events.57 In the single-institution, open-label, phase 2 LUNAR trial (NCT05496959), neoadjuvant [177Lu]Lu-PNT2002 followed by SBRT significantly prolonged PFS versus SBRT alone in oligorecurrent mHSPC (median 17.6 vs. 7.4 months; HR 0.37; P < .0001), without increased toxicity.94 Preliminary data from a pilot study of SBRT combined with 177Lu-PSMA-617 in mHSPC with 1–3 metastatic lesions (NCT05079698) further suggest that combining RLT with metastasis-directed therapy could achieve undetectable PSA levels without recourse to hormone therapy.77 Taken together, these results support a potential major shift in treatment options for oligometastatic prostate cancer and have informed the design of the ongoing multicenter, open-label, phase 3 PSMA-DC trial (NCT05939414), which is evaluating 177Lu-PSMA-617 after SBRT in approximately 450 patients.67,95 In parallel, ongoing studies such as the CCTG PR.21 trial continue to expand the evidence base for PSMA-targeted RLT across the prostate cancer disease continuum.96

Considerations for the implementation of RLT in mHSPC

Earlier treatment with RLT may necessitate additional monitoring for late-onset adverse events,97 as patients with mHSPC have a longer median survival than patients with mCRPC (7 years vs 4 years, respectively).6,98 For instance, in a retrospective analysis of long-term renal toxicity in patients with mCRPC undergoing ≥4 cycles of 177Lu-PSMA-I&T (median number of cycles, 6), 45% of patients had moderate (>15%) to severe (≥30%) decreases in estimated glomerular filtration rate from baseline within 12 months of treatment initiation.99 Renal toxicity of 177Lu-PSMA-617 is currently being investigated as part of a global, prospective, long-term follow-up safety study of adult participants with prostate cancer who received ≥1 dose of 177Lu-PSMA-617 from phase 1-4 clinical trials (NCT05803941).100 Current publications have estimated the absorbed renal dose for 177Lu-PSMA-617 is lower than that of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T RLT, reported as 0.4 ± 0.2 to 0.8 ± 0.3 Gy/Gbq versus 0.7 ± 0.2 Gy/Gbq, respectively.101 In patients receiving RLT, renal toxicity can be mitigated by ensuring the patient remains well hydrated and urinates frequently; performing kidney function laboratory tests between treatment cycles; management of tumor-related renal obstruction and withholding, reducing the dose, or discontinuing treatment based on severity of renal toxicity.26,102 Of note, RLT should be avoided in patients with urinary tract obstruction or hydronephrosis due to urinary stasis within the renal collecting system.101 In addition to concerns about renal toxicity, introducing RLT earlier in the disease course raises the possibility of suppressing bone marrow reserve103 and potentially limiting future treatment options.103 In the final safety analysis of the phase 3 PSMAfore trial (median follow-up: 34.27 months) in the pre-chemotherapy mCRPC setting, hematologic adverse events were more common with 177Lu-PSMA-617 compared with an ARPI change; however, these events were predominantly grade 1-2, with a median time to resolution of 1.77 months.34 Moreover, the incidence of grade ≥3 hematologic toxicity was higher in VISION than PSMAfore, suggesting that patients who are more heavily pre-treated may have greater risk of hematologic toxicity.34,104 Long-term follow-up of safety data from VISION and PSMAfore will be used to monitor late myelosuppression.100 Additionally, prospective trials are needed to further characterize long-term toxicities associated with RLTs in order for patients to make fully informed decisions about the risks and benefits of early RLT and to optimize supportive management. Responses to therapies following RLT are also of importance and were recently investigated in a real-world study (PRECISION) of 152 patients with mCRPC who progressed on 177Lu-PSMA-617 and received subsequent guideline-recommended therapies (ARPIs or taxanes). Among evaluable patients (n = 30), 46.7% experienced reduced serum PSA levels by 50% and 40.0% had PSA levels by 80% responses, suggesting that treatment with 177Lu-PSMA-617 does not preclude responses to subsequent therapies.105

While RLT is being investigated in a number of different studies, in both the mCRPC and mHSPC settings, it is important to remember that considerations differ between mCRPC and mHSPC due to underlying clinical differences, including the overall health of the patient. The patient’s age, presence of comorbidities, and ability to tolerate toxicities must be considered, resulting in complex needs for individual patients.6,106 When considering patient populations, it is important to recognize the differences between those in clinical trials and those in real-world clinical practice.107 In the mHSPC setting, clinical trial populations often include more de novo patients, while real-world practice will likely involve a broader patient population, including those who have undergone radical treatment prior to developing metachronous metastases.6 Additionally, patients in clinical practice tend to range more broadly in age, fitness, and competing comorbidities that can complicate application of trial data to real-world settings.108 More research is needed in dosing optimization to maximize therapeutic benefit while keeping toxicities to a minimum.

RLT may augment the current mHSPC treatment paradigm in several ways. Firstly, RLT in the mHSPC setting may allow for taxane-free combination regimens in patients with high-volume PSMA-avid disease, enabling another option for shared decision-making based on patient preferences as head-to-head comparative data are not available. Investigations are ongoing to determine whether oligometastatic disease identified with PSMA PET may be treated with RLT and metastasis-directed treatment and allow for an ADT-sparing approach in this select group of patients. Finally, the potential synergistic effect between ARPI and PSMA-targeted RLT on treatment efficacy in mHSPC is of interest, with results from ongoing trials awaited.

Challenges and proposed solutions associated with implementing RLT into clinical practice in mHSPC

While there is a potential role for RLT in the mHSPC space, there are a number of challenges that will need to be addressed before it can be fully integrated into patient care. Logistics, access to treatment, patient selection, and regulatory requirements represent key considerations for the broader implementation of RLT in mCRPC.97 Below, we discuss unique aspects of moving RLT into the mHSPC setting.

Education

One of the challenges associated with RLT implementation may include HCPs’ reluctance to shift to treatments that are newly used outside of previous treatment settings.108 Real-world data show that ADT monotherapy is commonly used for patients with mHSPC despite data suggesting that it is an inferior approach compared with novel hormone agents or chemotherapy with or without ADT.108 For example, a multicenter international survey of mHSPC treatment demonstrated that up to 47% of patients received ADT monotherapy, while novel hormonal agents ± ADT and chemotherapy ± ADT regimens were used in 31% and 19% of patients, respectively.108 These findings suggest an opportunity for other combination therapies to be integrated and a need for ongoing HCP education.

Enhanced educational resources that clearly communicate new data and guidelines, such as digital and social media campaigns, webinars, and hybrid conferences, serve an important role in supporting urologists and oncologists and can facilitate the integration of emerging evidence into clinical practice. Similarly, patient-focused websites and open-access plain language publications are critical in educating patients on their treatment options, which will in turn support shared decision-making.

Practical considerations

Practical considerations and barriers also exist regarding the incorporation of RLT into standard mHSPC management (Figure 2), with three key steps required: the widespread implementation of PSMA PET imaging (to determine patient eligibility), the expansion of RLT services at current medical centers (to cover the additional patients in the mHSPC population), and the establishment of new RLT centers to ensure equitable access.97,109 Financial, operational, and practical planning will be required for new and expanded centers to provide effective, high-quality services.97 Obtaining licenses for radioactive materials, implementing the necessary infrastructure upgrades, providing dedicated spaces for RLT, training and deploying staff, and disposing of radioactive waste may contribute to substantial upfront costs when starting a new RLT program, which could be an obstacle for many centers.97

With the potential for increased use of RLT, pending its approval in the mHSPC setting, there will be a need to shift towards more coordinated input from the multidisciplinary team, including medical oncologists, urologists, nuclear medicine specialists, radiation oncologists, pharmacists, nurse practitioners, and administrative staff.97 RLT centers should invest in systems of care that ensure all administrative aspects of RLT delivery (such as scheduling, reimbursement, and patient monitoring) run smoothly, and planning documents that clearly delineate the full list of required tasks should be employed to fully optimize patient outcomes.97

Patient selection for RLT has been complicated by lack of standardized reporting for PSMA PET scans.110 Several reporting frameworks for PSMA PET have been proposed, such as the VISION read criteria,111 PSMA-RADS,112 PRIMARY,113 and PROMISE (molecular imaging tumor/node/metastasis system, version 1.0/version 2.0).110,114 More recently, the Standardised PSMA PET/CT Analysis and Reporting Consensus (SPARC) has provided a unified framework for PSMA PET/CT analysis and reporting, serving as a reference standard for future clinical implementation. Integration of these common PSMA PET reporting criteria under one umbrella is expected to improve the interpretation and communication of imaging findings between nuclear medicine specialists and treating clinicians, thereby supporting consistent patient selection for RLT.117 These established PET criteria may be augmented with evolving standards on the use of post-treatment [177Lu]Lu-single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)/CT as a supplementary imaging tool to assess on-treatment response.118 However, prospective trials assessing the value of quantitative SPECT/CT are currently lacking, though this method is being used in phase 2 studies of RLT (NCT06216249 and NCT06288113).119–122

Patients receive RLT in designated, restricted areas of therapy centers.123 These areas need to be staffed with appropriate medical providers, as well as facilities to prepare and administer RLT.123 Currently available RLT agents can be administered in regulated areas without additional shielding (a larger, more isolated room may not require shielding at all).115,124 Another primary consideration should be the availability of a designated restroom for patients receiving RLT or other diagnostic or therapeutic radiopharmaceuticals.115,124 Sufficient room for radioactive waste storage is also strongly recommended.97,115 Finally, a robust treatment and follow-up strategy should be implemented, so that appropriate counseling and educational materials can be provided to the patient and their caregivers, including general and radiation safety instructions for both during and after RLT.123

Licensing

In the US, new medical centers starting RLT and current centers using new radioactive materials require a license from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) or from an agreement state.97,125 To apply for a license, the center must define 1) an authorized user (AU) of the RLT, who should be a board-certified physician who has completed radiation-therapy–specific training per NRC guidelines; 2) a dedicated space for delivery and management of RLT; and 3) a radiation safety and waste management program.97,124

Staff training and deployment

All staff should receive in-house training on radiation safety and familiarize themselves with the RLT workflow to prepare for their new role.97 This training should include the management of common adverse events associated with RLT.97

Day-to-day operations

PSMA PET imaging prior to PSMA-directed RLT is required for visual assessment of sufficient PSMA uptake and to exclude sites of disease on CT without PSMA uptake. Quantitative parameters such as the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) or total tumor volume may have additional roles in patient selection.97,126–128 PSMA expression may differ between mHSPC and mCRPC. A recent single-center retrospective study found that PSMA-targeted PET/CT scans in patients with mCRPC had a higher SUVmax and more frequent distant metastases than those in patients with mHSPC.129 Furthermore, while PSMA expression can be heterogeneous in mCRPC due to the impact of prior therapies and the emergence of resistance mechanisms, PSMA is usually uniformly expressed in mHSPC.130,131

Further considerations for medical centers choosing to offer PSMA PET imaging include establishing a fully integrated multidisciplinary team116 and the standardization of PSMA PET reporting.110 Some smaller, community-based centers may not have PET scanners available, which may make obtaining functional imaging data problematic.97 Allowing patients to be referred to PSMA PET imaging at a larger center could solve this issue; the creation of a “tumor board” meeting to review potential patients for RLT could also bridge this gap. Communication between multidisciplinary team members for imaging interpretation, patient response, and laboratory results will result in the best treatment decisions.

Multidisciplinary team collaboration is necessary for managing RLT treatment schedules, patient scheduling, and aligning with radiopharmaceutical ordering and supply.97 Key learnings from RLT in clinical trials and real-world practice also emphasize the importance of AUs for establishing an appropriate workflow within the clinical team for laboratory checks, ordering drugs, administration, and other considerations.97 Ensuring HCP awareness of the patient radiation instructions is also important, so that these instructions can be effectively reinforced throughout the clinic.97 Appropriate scheduling of laboratory checks prior to treatment is mandatory for patient safety.97 Furthermore, the timeframe for laboratory and clinical checks should allow sufficient time to review and order any necessary doses, with a buffer of time for RLT delivery (1-2 weeks) to allow for any delays.97

Future directions

With ADT use alone, progression from mHSPC to mCRPC typically occurs within 1 year of treatment, and the median OS for patients with mCRPC is 42 months.5,132 There is a need to delay progression to castration-resistant disease in patients with mHSPC, which should not only prolong survival but also delay time to deterioration of QoL due to cancer-related symptoms.5,6,133 Therefore, ongoing HCP education regarding emerging combination treatment strategies for mHSPC is required. Given that up to ∼50% of patients in recent mHSPC clinical trials (TITAN and ENZAMET) did not attain undetectable levels of PSA (defined as ≤0.02 ng/mL and <0.2 ng/mL in TITAN and ENZAMET, respectively) after doublet or triplet therapy,37,38,134,135 emerging combination approaches could address the unmet need for new treatment options. Several ongoing trials are currently assessing novel applications for 177Lu-PSMA-617 in different mHSPC patient populations (Table 1), including PSMAddition, which met its primary endpoint of rPFS at interim analysis (data cutoff January 13, 2025) and demonstrated that treatment with 177Lu-PSMA-617 is associated with prolonged rPFS and a trend towards improved OS in this setting (Table 2).

There remains variation in and uncertainty around the use of treatment intensification strategies for mHSPC in clinical practice.89,136 The addition of new therapies to clinical practice will face similar challenges to those seen with the integration of ARPIs in combination with ADT, such as concerns about drug tolerability, the lack of clinical evidence on OS, and questions on treatment sequencing.89 The implementation of RLT for the treatment of mHSPC could be facilitated by addressing educational gaps and training needs for HCPs and potential issues surrounding reimbursement. In addition, data from other ongoing clinical trials will help to better define patient populations with mHSPC. For example, the PEACE6 program of trials assesses multiple patient groups and treatment combinations for mHSPC for better treatment selection,137 including the PEACE6-Vulnerable trial of ADT with or without darolutamide in de novo mHSPC patients with metastases and frailty138 and the PEACE6-Oligo (PRESTO) trial of ablative radiotherapy in patients with oligometastatic mHSPC.139

Conclusion

Over the past decade, treatment options for mHSPC have improved outcomes, though there is still significant scope for further progress, and new therapeutics and strategies are urgently needed. Recent advances in imaging technology and radiopharmaceuticals are changing the treatment landscape of mCRPC and have the potential to change the treatment paradigm of mHSPC as well. Better understanding of key barriers to the application of new treatment strategies, such as PSMA-targeted RLT, will enable judicious implementation of these treatments into clinical practice when approved and therefore improve future treatment outcomes of mHSPC.
