Quantitative Analysis of the Radial Nerve Pathway Relative to the Humerus Shaft Length: A Cadaveric Study Aimed at Minimizing Nerve Injury in Fracture Treatment
Authors: Chang-Jin Yon, Beom-Soo Kim, Eun-Seok Son, Chul-Hyun Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to provide a detailed anatomical mapping of the radial nerve’s course relative to the humerus and to propose a length-normalized, ratio-based posterior “safe zone” for dual plate fixation in distal shaft fractures to reduce the risk of iatrogenic radial nerve injury.MethodsWe dissected 42 upper arms from 21 embalmed adult cadavers (mean age, 74.1 years) and measured the radial nerve in relation to key humeral landmarks from the posterior aspect. Two orthopedic surgeons performed 2 trials each, and intra- and interobserver reliability were high with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.813 and 0.910, respectively. A posterior safe zone for dual plate fixation using an 8-hole 3.5 locking compression plate (LCP) medially and a 7-hole LCP laterally with at least 2 screws per side was defined by expressing fracture location as proportions of humeral length relative to established landmarks.ResultsThe radial nerve entered the medial humerus at 40.5% and exited laterally at 57.0% of humeral length measured from the proximal end. Key absolute measurements were as follows: proximal humerus to the upper margin of the radial groove, 101.4 mm (range, 58–134 mm); groove length, 43.8 mm (range, 14–72 mm); upper margin of the groove to the olecranon fossa, 127.3 mm (range, 118–159 mm). Expressed as proportions, the posterior safe zone for dual plating spanned 49.1%–76.8% on the posteromedial aspect and 65.5%–88.9% on the posterolateral aspect of the proximal-to-lateral-epicondyle distance, corresponding to 115.3–180.4 mm and 153.7–208.8 mm, respectively, based on the mean humeral length.ConclusionsThis cadaveric study proposes a length-normalized posterior safe zone for dual plating in distal humerus fractures. These anatomical guidelines may assist preoperative planning to avoid radial nerve injury, but clinical validation is required.

Distal shaft fractures of the humerus (DSFHs) are commonly encountered in orthopedic practice, often requiring surgical intervention.12) Due to the lack of space for distal fixation and the unique anatomy of curvature, DSFHs are considered more challenging to treat than other humeral fractures.1) A significant complication arising from the treatment of such fractures is the injury to the radial nerve, which can markedly deteriorate the patient’s recovery trajectory and quality of life.34) The radial nerve traverses along the posterior aspect of the humerus shaft, where its pathway provides crucial guidance for nerve preservation during surgical procedures.5) Despite the clinical significance, detailed investigations into the precise anatomical course and the relative positioning of the radial nerve to the humerus shaft remain limited. This gap in knowledge increases the risk of iatrogenic nerve injury during surgery, complicating postoperative management and patient rehabilitation.

Previous research has predominantly explored the radial nerve’s course in the anatomy field.678910) Notably, these studies have primarily concentrated on measuring distances from the acromion end to the epicondyle, providing insights into specific segments of the humerus shaft.6781011121314) However, such approaches may not fully describe the comprehensive anatomical relationship between the radial nerve and the entire length of the humerus shaft.15) As orthopedic surgeons familiar with the diagnostic and operative implications of radiographic images, we recognize the importance of considering the humerus in its entirety. This general perspective is essential for developing surgical strategies that account for the complete anatomical context, thereby ensuring the precision and safety of interventions.

In the surgical management of DSFHs, posterior dual plating is one viable option for construct stabilization (Fig. 1), particularly when bone quality is compromised or comminution is present. While biomechanical and clinical series have reported greater construct stability and rotational control compared with some single-plate constructs,1617) this must be balanced against the potential for increased soft-tissue dissection and the approach-related risk to the radial nerve. Against this background, we mapped the radial nerve trajectory as a proportion of humeral length and propose a posterior “safe zone” intended to assist preoperative planning. This anatomical guidance is hypothesis-generating and requires clinical validation.

Consequently, this study aims to perform a quantitative analysis of the radial nerve’s pathway and its positional relationship to the humerus shaft using cadaveric specimens. Additionally, this study aims to identify a “safe zone” for dual plate fixation in distal humerus shaft fractures, significantly reducing the risk of radial nerve injury and enhancing surgical safety.

METHODS

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Keimyung University Dongsan Hospital (IRB No. DSMC 2022-11-066), with all procedures performed in accordance with the guidelines for cadaveric research.

We dissected 42 upper arms of 21 embalmed cadaveric specimens without signs of peripheral neurovascular or musculoskeletal diseases. All specimens were from Korean adults, consisting of 12 males and 9 females, with a mean age of 74.1 years (range, 65–86 years). Specimens with a history of trauma, deformity, or surgical intervention in the upper extremity were excluded. Dissections were performed by a well-trained orthopedic surgeon (CHC) using a standard posterior approach. A longitudinal incision was made over the posterior aspect of the arm, and the triceps muscle was carefully split along its fibers to expose the posterior surface of the humerus. The radial nerve was identified proximally as it emerged from the triangular interval and traced distally within the radial groove without disrupting its native position (Fig. 2). All measurements were taken twice and averaged. The proximal end of the humerus, the medial epicondyle, the lateral epicondyle, the olecranon fossa, and the radial groove were used as bony landmarks. We measured the distance to each bony landmark from the posterior aspect of the humerus (Fig. 3): (1) line A, from the proximal end of the humerus to the upper margin of the olecranon fossa; (2) line B, from the proximal end of the humerus to the upper margin of the radial nerve on the humerus; (3) line C, from the upper margin of the radial nerve to the lower margin of the radial nerve on the humerus; (4) line D, from the lower margin of the radial nerve on the humerus to the upper margin of the olecranon fossa; (5) line E, from the upper margin of the radial nerve to upper margin of the olecranon fossa; (6) line F, from the upper margin of the olecranon fossa to the lateral epicondyle; (7) line G, from the upper margin of the olecranon fossa to the medial epicondyle; (8) line H, from the proximal end of the humerus to the lateral epicondyle, measured as the total length of the humerus. All measurements were made using digital calipers with precision up to 0.1 mm.

Identification of the “Safe Zone” for Posterior Dual Plate Fixation

To establish the feasible application of dual plate fixation without risking the radial nerve’s integrity, we meticulously evaluated the compatible lengths of 3.5 locking compression plates (small LCP, Synthes). This evaluation was 2-fold: initially, plate lengths were verified against manufacturer specifications provided in a brochure, followed by empirical measurements to validate these dimensions. In addition to theoretical calculations and manufacturer specifications, 3.5-mm LCP plates were manually positioned on selected cadaveric specimens to assess feasibility in terms of length and anatomical fit. However, actual drilling and screw fixation were not performed, and the plates were not bent to match the humeral curvature. This assessment served as a gross validation of plate placement relative to the radial nerve course.

The critical determination of a “safe zone” for employing posterior dual plate fixation in the context of DSFHs hinged on the precise localization of the fracture relative to established anatomical landmarks. The minimum necessary space for the safe application of a 2-hole 3.5 LCP plate, which measures 29.5 mm in length, served as a foundational parameter. For a fracture to be amenable to posterior dual plate fixation, it must be situated within specific confines: On the posteromedial aspect of the distal humerus, the fracture line must be located from 29.5 mm distally to the upper margin of the radial groove to 29.5 mm proximally to the olecranon fossa. On the posterolateral aspect, the positioning shifts between 29.5 mm distally to the lower margin of the radial groove and 29.5 mm proximally to the lateral epicondyle.

A geometric region delineated by connecting these 4 pivotal points establishes the permissible area for fracture location. Implementation of posterior dual plate fixation is deemed viable within this demarcated “safe zone.” Further, we quantified the proportionate relationship of the fracture line’s location to the total distance from the proximal end of the humerus to the lateral epicondyle, thereby enabling a systematic approach to identifying potential candidates for this surgical technique.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS statistical package version 20.0 (IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the measurements, and inferential statistics were applied to assess the significance of the findings. To increase the accuracy and statistical power, the measurements were performed 4 times, 2 times each by 2 orthopedic surgeons (CJY and BSK) independently and the mean values were used for analyses. We used Cronbach’s alpha value to verify the reliability of the measurements; this value can be between 0 and 1, with higher values indicating higher reliability. This descriptive morphometric study reported outcomes as length-normalized ratios (percentage of humeral length); sex-stratified hypothesis testing was not prespecified, and the study was not powered for subgroup inference.

RESULTS

The anatomical measurements of the radial nerve in relation to key landmarks on the humerus are systematically summarized in Table 1. Two measurement scores showed Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.813 (intraobserver reliability) and 0.910 (interobserver reliability), indicating consistency between the 4 measurements by the 2 surgeons in reliability analysis. There were no statistically significant differences between the measurements obtained from left and right arms in any of the parameters (p > 0.05). Therefore, data from both sides were pooled for analysis. No significant anatomical variations were observed. The mean distance from the proximal end of the humerus to olecranon fossa upper margin (line A) was 234.7 mm (range, 205–265 mm). The mean distance from the proximal end of the humerus to the radial groove upper margin (line B) was 101.4 mm (range, 58–134 mm). The mean distance from the radial groove upper to lower margin (line C) was 43.8 mm (range, 14–72 mm). The mean distance from the radial groove lower margin to olecranon fossa upper margin (line D) was 83.4 mm (range, 47–118 mm). The mean distance from the radial groove upper margin to the olecranon fossa upper margin (line E) was 127.3 mm (range, 118–159 mm). The mean distance from the olecranon fossa upper margin to the lateral epicondyle (line F) was 32.7 mm (range, 12–57 mm). The mean distance from the olecranon fossa upper margin to the medial epicondyle was 34.4 mm (range, 17–61 mm). Further analysis revealed that the radial nerve entered the medial humerus at 40.5% and exited laterally at 57.0% of humeral length measured from the proximal end.

In the posteromedial part of the distal humerus, the distance between the upper margin of the radial groove and the olecranon fossa upper margin would be the minimum length for using the 3.5 LCP plate. The mean distance between the upper margin of the radial groove and the olecranon fossa upper margin was 127.3 mm (range, 118–159 mm). Considering the length of the LCP plate, the minimum plate that can be safely used in the posteromedial part of the distal humerus is an 8-hole LCP plate (111 mm). Then, in the posterolateral aspect of the distal humerus, the distance between the lower margin of the radial groove and the lateral epicondyle would be the minimum length for using the 3.5 LCP plate. The mean distance between the lower margin of the radial groove and the lateral epicondyle was 116.1 mm (range, 78–166 mm). Considering the length of the LCP plate, the minimum plate that can be safely used in the posterolateral part of the distal humerus is a 7-hole LCP plate (98 mm) without radial nerve injury (Fig. 4).

The calculation of the fracture line’s location ratio to the total distance between the proximal end of the humerus and the lateral epicondyle provided critical insights into the feasibility of posterior dual plate fixation. In the posteromedial aspect of the distal humerus, the fracture line was found to be located between 49.1% and 76.8% of this total distance, with a range indicating the variability in anatomical proportions observed across specimens. For the posterolateral aspect, the fracture line’s location ranged between 65.5% and 88.9% of the distance to the lateral epicondyle. These ratios are instrumental in defining a precise “safe zone” for the application of posterior dual plates (Fig. 5). These ratios corresponded to a range of 115.3 mm to 180.4 mm on the posteromedial aspect and 153.7 mm to 208.8 mm on the posterolateral aspect, based on the mean humeral length. This variability highlights the importance of preoperative planning when applying the safe zone in clinical practice. When the fracture line is located within the region of the quadrangle that is created when these 4 points are connected, the posterior dual plate fixation technique can be used.

DISCUSSION

This study revealed that the radial nerve enters the medial humerus at 40.5% and exits laterally at 57.0% of humeral length measured from the proximal end. Quantification of the radial nerve’s course relative to the humerus has led to the identification of a definitive ‘safe zone’ for posterior dual plate fixation in DSFHs, delineating that when the fracture line is situated between 49.1% and 76.8% for the posteromedial aspect and 65.5% and 88.9% for the posterolateral aspect of the distance from the proximal end to the lateral epicondyle. These findings provide a novel “safe zone” for posterior dual plating that can be applied without compromising radial nerve integrity, thereby significantly enhancing surgical safety and outcomes.

Humerus fractures, particularly those involving the distal shaft, pose a considerable risk for radial nerve damage due to the nerve’s intimate anatomical proximity to the surgical field.35) Traditionally, the management of such fractures has necessitated a careful balance between achieving optimal fixation and preserving nerve integrity.1718) The radial nerve’s susceptibility to injury during these interventions highlights the critical need for precise anatomical knowledge and strategic surgical planning.19) To our knowledge, there are currently no systematic reviews or meta-analyses that specifically synthesize cadaveric mapping of the radial nerve pathway or that define a posterior “safe zone” for dual plating in distal humeral shaft fractures. Most evidence syntheses in this field instead compare surgical approaches (e.g., anterolateral vs posterior) or fixation strategies (minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis vs. open reduction and internal fixation) with outcomes such as iatrogenic radial nerve palsy and union. Within that context, our length-normalized, ratiobased mapping is intended to complement—not replace—those clinical comparisons by offering an anatomy-guided corridor for posterior fixation that can be integrated with approach selection and preoperative planning.

Historically, the study of the radial nerve’s anatomical course along the humerus has been approached through various methodologies, particularly regarding the selection of anatomical landmarks for measurement initiation. Notably, the research conducted by Bono et al.15) diverged from the convention by initiating measurements from the humeral proximal end, as opposed to the more commonly used acromion end, as observed in most studies. This variation in starting points for measurements highlights a fundamental challenge in standardizing anatomical studies of the radial nerve’s pathway. In addressing this challenge, our study has adopted a measurement approach that considers the entire length of the humerus, calculated from the proximal end to the distal landmarks. This measurement was guided by the intention to align our findings more closely with the practical considerations and diagnostic practices of orthopedic surgeons, who are accustomed to utilizing radiographs in clinical assessments and surgical planning. By calculating the radial nerve’s pathway in relation to the total humeral length, we aimed to provide a more universally applicable and clinically relevant metric that could facilitate the precise identification of “safe zones” for surgical interventions, thereby minimizing the risk of radial nerve injury.

Our findings are generally consistent with Gerwin et al.,12) who reported that the radial nerve crosses the posterior humerus at approximately 20 cm from the lateral epicondyle in adult cadavers. Although we expressed nerve position as a proportion of humeral length, the average conversion closely aligns with this absolute distance. Additionally, the classic description of Holstein-Lewis fractures involves a spiral pattern at the distal third of the humeral shaft, often associated with radial nerve injury. Our defined safe zone largely avoids this region, providing a possible explanation for why plating within the identified zone may reduce nerve injury risk.

Furthermore, most existing studies on the radial nerve’s course have predominantly focused on Western populations, which may not fully represent the anatomical nuances present in individuals from different racial backgrounds. This discrepancy is highlighted in the reported lengths of the radial nerve’s passage over the humerus’s posterior aspect with Carlan et al.20) reporting 63 mm and Artico reporting 59 mm.6) In contrast, our findings indicate an average length of approximately 44 mm for this segment of the radial nerve. We interpret this variance as indicative of the anatomical differences arising from the distinct body statures between Western and Eastern populations. The comparison study by Chou et al.11) in 2008, which reported a length of 48 mm in a Chinese population, corroborates our results, suggesting that ethnic-specific anatomical variations significantly influence the radial nerve’s course. This insight underscores the necessity for a more diversified approach to studying the radial nerve’s anatomy, ensuring that surgical practices are informed by a comprehensive understanding of these variations.

Given the observed discrepancies in measurements across different ethnicities, evaluating the radial nerve’s course in relation to the humerus on a percentage or ratio basis appears to be a more appropriate and universally applicable method. Accordingly, our study employed this approach, determining that the radial nerve enters the medial humerus at 40.5% and exits laterally at 57.0% of humeral length measured from the proximal end. The absence of previous research utilizing identical landmarks for such precise measurement makes direct comparison challenging. However, our findings closely align with the percentages calculated retrospectively from Guse and Ostrum’s study, which, when adjusted from the acromion end, estimated the radial nerve’s course at 41% to 58%.13) This similarity offers a validation to our results. In contrast, the study by Chou et al.,11) focusing on Chinese population, reported slightly different percentages, ranging from 49% to 65%. Our methodology, involving 2 orthopedic specialists performing measurements twice to ensure accuracy, has demonstrated statistical significance in terms of reliability. This approach supports the correctness of our measurements and the use of ratios as a reliable indicator of the radial nerve’s anatomical course, regardless of individual or ethnic variations.

The identification of a “safe zone” for posterior dual plating in distal humerus fractures represents a novel contribution to the field, previously unexplored in existing literature. Clinical evidence has already established the efficacy of posterior dual plating for achieving favorable outcomes. Through cadaveric simulation of dual plate application, our study not only corroborates these clinical findings but also pioneers the establishment of a “safe zone.” Assuming a scenario where fractures occur within this zone, our research demonstrates the feasibility of applying an 8-hole LCP on the medial side and a 7-hole LCP on the lateral side, offering a viable fixation strategy. This advancement provides surgeons with valuable insights and new challenges in considering surgical options for DSFHs, significantly expanding the selection of available treatment methods.

The clinical relevance of posterior dual plating has been supported by several prior studies. Cho et al.16) reported a 91.7% union rate and satisfactory functional outcomes in patients treated with posterior dual plating without requiring radial nerve dissection. Similarly, Prasarn et al.21) documented successful fixation and early rehabilitation in a series of patients using the same approach. From a biomechanical standpoint, Shih et al.22) demonstrated that posterior dual plating provides comparable stiffness and load-to-failure characteristics to parallel plating, and superior fixation stability compared to single posterior Y-plate constructs, particularly in sagittal loading. These findings offer strong justification for the anatomical analysis presented in our study and reinforce the viability of posterior dual plate application in distal humerus shaft fractures.

Our study introduces significant advancements in the understanding of the radial nerve’s anatomy and the application of dual plate fixation. However, the first limitation lies in the inherent variability of anatomical structures across individuals, which may affect the generalizability of our “safe zone” findings. Additionally, our study’s focus on cadaveric specimens may not fully capture the dynamic conditions encountered during live surgical procedures. Moreover, all dissections were performed in a single static position (prone with elbow extended), which does not account for the positional variability encountered in actual surgical settings. Tissue elasticity, intraoperative traction, and joint flexion could alter nerve position dynamically, and these factors could influence the clinical applicability of our static anatomical findings. Third, biomechanical testing was not performed to assess the mechanical strength and stability of the proposed plate configuration, which limits the evaluation of fixation performance. Another limitation is that while plates were applied to cadaveric specimens for positional assessment, no actual drilling or screw fixation was performed, and plate contouring was not carried out. Therefore, the feasibility evaluation did not fully replicate intraoperative fixation dynamics. We did not perform plate contouring, drilling, or screw insertion; therefore, we could not evaluate how plate bending and screw trajectories might modify the local relationship to the radial nerve. As a next step, computed tomography-based 3-dimensional modeling with virtual plate contouring and simulated screw vectors (e.g., collision/proximity analyses or finite-element–based sensitivity testing) could assess whether the proposed ratio-based safe zone holds under realistic fixation conditions. Such modeling, ideally combined with posture-dependent cadaveric experiments, was beyond the scope of the present study but represents a priority for future work. We did not perform sex-stratified analyses and the study was not powered for subgroup inference; therefore, larger, adequately powered clinical cohorts are required to verify the generalizability of the proposed length-normalized (“ratio-based”) safe zone across sex and body habitus. In addition, we were unable to identify systematic reviews or meta-analyses that directly address radial nerve pathway mapping or posterior dual-plate “safe zones”; therefore, extrapolation should be cautious, and prospective clinical validation remains essential. Despite these limitations, our study has some strengths, including the rigorous methodology, the novel identification of a “safe zone,” and the practical implications for surgical practice.

In conclusion, this cadaveric study identified a consistent anatomical safe zone for posterior dual plate fixation in distal humeral shaft fractures. Based on proportional measurements from the proximal humerus to the lateral epicondyle, the safe zone ranges from 49.1% to 76.8% on the posteromedial aspect and from 65.5% to 88.9% on the posterolateral aspect. These findings may assist surgeons in avoiding radial nerve injury during posterior fixation of distal humeral shaft fractures. These anatomical guidelines can be used preoperatively with radiographic planning to evaluate the suitability of posterior dual plating while minimizing the risk of radial nerve injury. Future studies should aim to validate our findings in clinical settings and explore the applicability of the “safe zone” across diverse patient populations, further refining the precision and safety of orthopedic interventions.
