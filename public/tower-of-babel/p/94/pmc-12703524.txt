Impact of Clinical Pharmacy Team Intervention on Naloxone Fills in Patients on Chronic Opioid Therapy
Authors: Rosy S Sijapati, Erin R Himes, Stephanie A Hale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpioids are highly effective for pain management but carry risks. Naloxone quickly reverses opioid overdoses by blocking opioid receptors in the brain. Despite its effectiveness, naloxone remains underutilized. Kaiser Permanente created the Interregional Pharmacy Opioid Use Improvement Group to support safe opioid prescribing and increase appropriate naloxone prescribing.MethodsThis retrospective study included Kaiser Permanente Georgia members who were 18 years or older and filling chronic opioid therapy of ≥ 50 morphine milligram equivalents (MME) averaged over the previous 90 days. The primary objective was to compare the number of patients who filled a naloxone prescription within the last 24 months as of March 31, 2023 (preintervention) vs as of December 31, 2023 (postintervention). The secondary outcome was to compare the naloxone fill rates based on intervention type.ResultsAs of March 31, 2023, 122 out of 399 patients (30.6%) had filled naloxone prescriptions compared to 241 out of 356 patients (67.7%) as of December 31, 2023 (30.6% vs 67.7%; P < .0001). Out of 56 patients who received only a secure email, 42 (75%) filled their prescriptions. For patients who spoke directly with a pharmacist or technician, 91 (91%) filled their prescriptions. Among patients who were not directly reached and received a voicemail, 15 (41%) filled their prescriptions.ConclusionsPharmacy-led interventions, particularly telephone outreach, were effective at improving naloxone fill rates in patients on chronic opioid therapy of ≥ 50 morphine milligram equivalents per day. Voicemails had a lower fill rate, highlighting the importance of direct interaction in improving patient outcomes.

Introduction

Opioids are a class of medications widely used for pain management due to their effectiveness in blocking pain signals. Although effective, these medications, such as morphine, oxycodone, hydrocodone, and synthetic opioids like fentanyl, carry great risks. In 2021, approximately 51.6 million people reported experiencing chronic pain. 1 Chronic use of opioids can lead to addiction, physical dependence, and severe complications like overdose, which can cause respiratory depression and death. In 2022, the number of opioid overdose deaths was 14,716, which was slightly decreased from 2020. 2 While the overall national prescription opioid dispensing rate has declined since 2019, opioid dispensing rates continue to be highest in southern states, including Georgia where this study was conducted. 3

Naloxone, an opioid antagonist, is crucial for reversing opioid overdoses. It works by binding to opioid receptors in the brain to reverse or block the effects of opioids. Naloxone acts quickly, typically within minutes, to reverse opioid effects, making it highly effective in overdose emergencies. Additionally, it has minimal effects on individuals who are not using opioids, making it a safe option for emergency use. 4 Naloxone prescribing in primary care for patients taking opioids has been associated with reductions in emergency department visits and is considered an effective risk-mitigation strategy. 5 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Chronic Pain recommends prescribing naloxone when factors that increase risk for opioid overdose, such as history of overdose, combination of opioids and benzodiazepines, history of substance use disorder, and higher opioid dosages (≥ 50 morphine milligram equivalents (MME) per day), are present. 6 Although overall volume of naloxone prescriptions dispensed increased from 0.3% in 2019 to 0.5% in 2023, challenges remain and further efforts are needed to enhance access and utilization. 7 Challenges such as stigma, lack of awareness, and barriers to access continue to limit its use. Prior studies have focused on distribution of naloxone and dispensing trends within community-based programs, 8,9 but there has been less research on naloxone dispensing rates in health care settings.

Due to a strong focus on safe prescribing practices, Kaiser Permanente developed the Interregional Pharmacy Opioid Use Improvement (OUI) Group. This committee involves pharmacy teams across all Kaiser Permanente regions and supports safe opioid prescribing efforts across the program while sharing best practices among different regions. This group, which meets monthly, is comprised of physicians, pharmacists, data analysts, and compliance representatives at both national and local levels. In 2023, their primary goal was to enhance naloxone fill rates and reduce negative overdose outcomes. Previous work published in The Permanente Journal explored a multidisciplinary approach to naloxone education and demonstrated the feasibility of reaching high-risk patients using this model. 10 This research project is designed to evaluate the impact of clinical pharmacy team interventions on naloxone fills in Kaiser Permanente Georgia patients on chronic opioid therapy of ≥ 50 MME/d. Clinical pharmacy specialists (CPSs) obtained physician approval for a naloxone nasal spray for eligible patients taking ≥ 50 MME/d before making any interventions.

Methods

Study setting and design

The Kaiser Permanente Interregional Institutional Review Board determined that this quality improvement project did not qualify as human participants research and therefore did not require Institutional Review Board review. This was a retrospective cohort study conducted within an integrated health care delivery system to evaluate the number of patients on chronic opioid therapy who filled a naloxone prescription. Kaiser Permanente Georgia is an integrated health care system serving more than 330,000 members that offers services in a variety of specialties, such as urgent care, orthopedics, primary care, and pain management. These specialties are relevant because naloxone prescriptions were most frequently prescribed within these areas during this intervention period. CPSs at Kaiser Permanente work closely with practitioners to deliver evidence-based patient care for various chronic conditions by utilizing cost-effective medication. This collaborative approach helps improve patient outcomes by ensuring comprehensive management of their medical needs.

Study population

Inclusion criteria included Kaiser Permanente Georgia members who were 18 years or older and filled chronic opioid therapy of ≥ 50 MME averaged over the last 90 days between April 1, 2023 and September 30, 2023. In this study, chronic opioid therapy is defined as ≥ 90 days of cumulative supply of any opioid or combination of opioid products dispensed during the study period. Exclusion criteria included patients with a cancer diagnosis or those who were enrolled in hospice care. The population was a rolling cohort allowing for patients to enter or exit the study as they met or did not meet inclusion criteria.

Study outcomes

The primary objective was to compare the number of patients meeting inclusion criteria who filled a naloxone prescription within the last 24 months as of March 31, 2023 (preintervention) vs as of December 31, 2023 (postintervention). The secondary outcome was to compare the naloxone fill rates based on intervention type.

Study procedure

Data reported by the OUI Group from April 1, 2023 to September 30, 2023 were utilized. Data collection included patient characteristics (age, patient-reported gender, and race), opioid prescriber area (Kaiser Permanente internal vs external practitioners), specialty areas (pain management, adult primary care, orthopedics, urgent care, etc), average MME, and last naloxone prescription dispensed date. CPSs identified patients who could benefit from a naloxone prescription through monthly reports. Patients were randomly distributed among different CPSs, who then performed chart reviews and pended the naloxone order for the prescriber to sign. Once the prescriber signed the naloxone order, initial outreach was conducted via secure email. If the prescription was not filled within 1 week, either a CPS or a technician called and spoke directly with patients. In cases where patients could not be reached, a general voicemail was left to check secure messages on the patient portal or to return call if they have questions.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to determine the similarities in patient characteristics. Mean and standard deviation was calculated for continuous variables, while percentage was used to report categorical data. The χ2 test was used to determine the statistical significance of categorial variables; P < .05 was considered significant for all statistical analyses.

Results

A total of 496 unique patients were included in this study. The baseline demographics were similar between the 2 groups. The mean patient age was 60 years, with a median age of preintervention and postintervention groups of 61 and 62 years, respectively. The majority of patients were White (57%) and women (55%). As of March 31, 2023, 399 patients were eligible for a naloxone prescription compared to 356 patients eligible as of December 31, 2023. Most naloxone prescriptions were prescribed by Kaiser Permanente internal practitioners, primarily from adult primary care, followed by pain management. Other specialties included urgent care, neurology, palliative care, sleep medicine, and rheumatology. Notably, most patients prescribed naloxone were receiving opioids with an average MME ranging between 50 and 89 (Table 1). The primary outcome was to compare the number of patients on chronic opioid therapy ≥ 50 MME/d who filled a naloxone prescription within the last 24 months as of March 31, 2023 vs as of December 31, 2023. As of March 31, 2023, 122 out of 399 patients (30.6%) had filled naloxone prescriptions compared to 241 out of 356 patients (67.7%) who had filled naloxone prescriptions as of December 31, 2023. This represents an absolute increase of 119 naloxone prescriptions (37.1%) between the pre- and postintervention periods (P < .0001; Table 2).

Primary endpoint

The study looked at several secondary outcomes (Figure 1). A total of 193 patients received an outreach from the clinical pharmacy service. Out of 56 patients who received only a secure email, 42 (75%) filled their naloxone prescriptions. Out of 100 patients who were spoken to directly, 28 were successfully reached by a CPS and 72 were contacted by a pharmacy technician. Of patients who were successfully reached and spoke directly via telephone outreach with either a CPS or a technician, 91 (91%) filled their naloxone prescriptions. Of the patients who spoke with a CPS, 100% filled their naloxone prescriptions, compared to 88% of those who spoke with a technician (Figure 2). Among patients who were not directly reached by either a CPS or a technician and received a voicemail, 15 (41%) filled their naloxone prescriptions.

Discussion

This study highlights the pivotal role of clinical pharmacy teams in improving naloxone prescription fill rates among patients on chronic opioid therapy. The significant increase in naloxone fills from 30.6% preintervention to 67.7% postintervention shows that direct pharmacy-led interventions, particularly telephone outreach, were the most effective method. There was no clinical reason behind which patients were contacted by a CPS vs a technician. CPSs were unable to make all outreach calls due to lack of resources (limited time and workload). Regardless of who conducted the outreach, patients who received direct telephone outreach had naloxone fill rates > 85%. This demonstrates that direct patient engagement and tailored approaches could help address barriers and misunderstandings about naloxone. Additionally, higher fill rates could have also been due to the OUI Group’s emphasis on naloxone prescribing, with reinforcement and encouragement from dispensing pharmacists and practitioners on clinic visits.

The study further demonstrates effectiveness across different intervention methods. While direct phone calls were the most successful method, secure emails had a moderate success rate of 75%, whereas voicemail messages had a lower fill rate of 41%. Due to HIPAA regulations, CPSs or technicians were unable to leave a detailed voicemail but instead left a general voicemail encouraging patients to check their secure email or return the call. As a result, patients were required to take an additional step to access the information, which could have contributed to the lower prescription fill rate.

These findings support the existing literature regarding the multidisciplinary approach in optimizing medication adherence and patient safety, particularly in patients who are at increased risk for adverse outcomes. The Raffel et al findings showed that utilizing the nursing team to conduct outreach in a primary care setting increased access to naloxone, resulting in a 91% training rate. 10 Building on this approach, we conducted a larger study involving the clinical pharmacy team and it led to a similar outcome, as 91% filled their naloxone prescriptions after speaking directly on the phone. This supports the claim that clinical pharmacy–led approaches have potential in combating the public health crisis, including the opioid epidemic, and reducing the risks associated with long-term opioid use. A key strength of this study is that it was conducted within an integrated health care delivery system and included a large and diverse patient population. The current epidemiology of opioid overdose that affects patients across various demographics is consistent with this wider representation of patient population. CPSs and pharmacy technician resources were utilized using different intervention methods to enhance patient care and evaluate the importance of clinical pharmacy teams. Patients were provided education about naloxone, including signs of opioid overdose and how to properly administer the medication. They were also advised to call for a refill 2 weeks before the product expiration date. Although the data indicated an increase in naloxone fills after pharmacy intervention, further research is needed to determine whether this increase in naloxone fills corresponds to a decrease in the number of opioid overdoses.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that clinical pharmacy team interventions can greatly improve naloxone prescription fill rates among patients on chronic opioid therapy. Direct outreach via telephone by either a CPS or a pharmacy technician was more effective than leaving a voicemail or secure email, which may not carry the same level of personalization. This shows how important it is to have a direct conversation with patients in addressing barriers about naloxone and improving its access. It reinforces that patient care can be optimized by leveraging the clinical pharmacy team and their expertise in medication safety. However, as naloxone is now available over the counter, future efforts should focus on providing sufficient information about naloxone by different health care practitioners. Prescribing practitioners should assess each patient who is on chronic opioid therapy and take time to provide naloxone education at the visit before refilling their medications. These patients should be further counseled on naloxone benefits and its role in harm reduction by frontline dispensing pharmacists upon medication pickup. This multidisciplinary approach could help clinicians better understand patients’ concerns/barriers and develop tailored interventions.
