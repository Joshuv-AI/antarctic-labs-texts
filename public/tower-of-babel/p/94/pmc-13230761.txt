Biodegradable chitosan–gelatin films incorporating Zanthoxylum limonella (Dennst.) Alston essential oil for antibacterial and hemostatic applications
Authors: Dhammawit Haemanwichian, Sarunpron Khruengsai, Teerapong Sripahco, Chanokchon Srimatra, Patcharee Pripdeevech
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of biodegradable and multifunctional films is a promising approach toward sustainable biomedical materials. In this study, chitosan–gelatin composite films incorporated with varying concentrations (0–2.5% w/w) of Zanthoxylum limonella essential oil were successfully fabricated and systematically characterized. The incorporation of essential oil increased film porosity and microstructural heterogeneity, as observed by scanning electron microscopy. Mechanical analysis revealed that tensile strength significantly improved from 17.21 ± 1.19 MPa in the control film to 39.04 ± 1.06 MPa at 2.0% essential oil concentration, while elongation at break increased from 70.41 ± 1.21% to 88.62 ± 1.72%. Essential oil addition enhanced surface hydrophobicity, indicated by the increase in contact angle from 56.41° to 72.99°, while water vapor permeability remained statistically unchanged. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy and thermal analyses confirmed the physical incorporation of the essential oil without altering the chemical structure of the polymer matrix. The films exhibited dose-dependent antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) analyses demonstrated synergistic effects at higher essential oil concentrations. Preliminary hemostatic evaluation showed enhanced blood absorption capacity from 325 ± 10% to 651 ± 20% and a reduction in whole blood clotting time from 10.53 ± 0.41 to 4.18 ± 0.38 min, comparable to the positive glass control. These biodegradable chitosan–gelatin films demonstrate promising antibacterial functionality and preliminary hemostatic potential.Keywords: Biodegradable film, Chitosan, Essential oil, Antibacterial activity, HemostasisSubject terms: Biotechnology, Chemistry, Materials science, Microbiology, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The development of sustainable, biodegradable, and multifunctional materials has attracted increasing attention in recent years due to growing environmental concerns and the urgent need for safer alternatives to conventional synthetic polymers1. Many petroleum-based plastics used in packaging and biomedical products contain chemical additives such as phthalate plasticizers, bisphenols (e.g., bisphenol A), stabilizers, antioxidants, flame retardants, and residual monomers, which are not covalently bound to the polymer backbone and can leach into the environment during use and disposal. These substances have been associated with ecotoxicity, endocrine disruption, and long-term environmental persistence, while degradation of synthetic plastics further generates microplastics that act as carriers for toxic additives and environmental pollutants. In this context, biodegradable films represent an environmentally responsible strategy to reduce persistent plastic waste while enabling functional performance for biomedical and packaging applications1,2.

Biodegradable films can be fabricated from a wide range of natural and synthetic biodegradable polymers, including polysaccharides (e.g., cellulose, starch, alginate, chitosan), proteins (e.g., gelatin, collagen, soy protein), and biodegradable polyesters such as polylactic acid (PLA), polycaprolactone (PCL), and polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs). Among these materials, chitosan, derived from chitin, has emerged as a widely used film-forming biopolymer owing to its biodegradability, biocompatibility, mechanical integrity, and inherent biological activities2,3. Chitin itself is a chemically well-defined linear polysaccharide composed exclusively of repeating N-acetyl-D-glucosamine units linked by β-(1→4) glycosidic bonds. Partial deacetylation of chitin yields chitosan, introducing free amino groups along the polymer backbone, which impart cationic character and are responsible for many of chitosan’s physicochemical and biological properties3.

Chitosan-based materials have been extensively applied in wound care, drug delivery, and food packaging because of their antibacterial and hemostatic properties, which arise primarily from the presence of these cationic amino groups that promote electrostatic interactions with microbial cell membranes and blood components. However, the antibacterial efficacy of chitosan alone may be limited under certain conditions, particularly against resistant or high microbial loads, which can restrict broader clinical and industrial applicability4. These limitations have motivated the modification of chitosan-based materials through incorporation of natural bioactive agents capable of synergistically enhancing functional performance5.

Biodegradable films offer distinct advantages in both biomedical and food-related applications by combining environmental sustainability with functional versatility1. Unlike petroleum-based plastics, biodegradable materials can undergo natural degradation, thereby reducing long-term environmental accumulation and associated ecological risks1–4. In biomedical applications, biodegradable films are generally biocompatible and may eliminate the need for secondary removal after use, minimizing patient discomfort and the risk of chronic inflammation⁶. Their adaptable structure also enables incorporation of antimicrobial or hemostatic agents to support wound healing and infection control. In food systems, biodegradable films can serve as active packaging materials by providing antimicrobial protection and moisture regulation while reducing dependence on non-degradable plastics5.

In wound care, achieving rapid hemostasis together with effective protection against bacterial infection represents a critical clinical requirement6. Although various commercial hemostatic products are available, many suffer from high production costs, limited biodegradability, or potential cytotoxic effects7. Chitosan-based materials are attractive candidates for blood-contact applications due to their ability to promote platelet aggregation, bioadhesiveness, and accelerated clot formation2. Nevertheless, in wounds with a high risk of secondary infection, additional antimicrobial functionality is required to improve overall efficacy and safety.

A common strategy to impart antimicrobial functionality involves incorporation of essential oils (EOs) into chitosan-based films. Essential oils are plant-derived volatile compounds known for broad-spectrum antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activities8, and many are classified as Generally Regarded As Safe (GRAS)9. Previous studies have shown that EO incorporation can enhance antibacterial efficacy, surface hydrophobicity, and mechanical flexibility of biopolymer films, properties relevant to both wound-contact materials and active packaging systems10. However, the use of essential oils in active packaging has also been critically discussed due to challenges such as volatility, strong aroma, and limited control over release and migration. Accordingly, in the present study, essential oil incorporation is not intended to propose an immediately deployable packaging solution, but rather to serve as a natural model bioactive agent for investigating structure–property–function relationships in biodegradable chitosan-based films.

Among various EOs, Zanthoxylum limonella (Dennst.) Alston, a plant native to Southeast Asia, has attracted attention due to its potent bioactivity. The essential oil of Z. limonella, rich in limonene and sabinene, has demonstrated antimicrobial effects against common pathogens such as Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus11. Previous studies have primarily explored Z. limonella essential oil in chitosan-based films for food-packaging applications, reporting improved antibacterial performance and water vapor barrier properties12. However, its application in biodegradable films for biomedical use, particularly with respect to blood–material interactions and hemostatic behavior, remains insufficiently explored.

Incorporation of essential oils into chitosan matrices is expected to influence physicochemical, mechanical, and biological properties of the resulting films, including microstructure, surface hydrophobicity, fluid absorption capacity, and antibacterial activity, parameters critical for wound healing and blood-contact performance13. While previous studies have often examined antibacterial or mechanical properties in isolation, relatively few investigations have simultaneously evaluated antibacterial activity and preliminary hemostatic behavior within a single biodegradable film system. Establishing such multifunctionality is important for advancing sustainable material platforms with application flexibility.

In this study, chitosan–gelatin composite films incorporating Z. limonella essential oil were fabricated via a solvent-casting method and systematically characterized. Gelatin was incorporated to improve film flexibility, mechanical stability, and processability through complementary intermolecular interactions with chitosan. The combination of chitosan and gelatin enables hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction, resulting in a more cohesive and elastic film structure compared to chitosan alone2,3. The films were evaluated for structural, physicochemical, mechanical, and biological properties, including antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus and preliminary in vitro hemostatic behavior assessed by blood absorption and clotting assays. This work provides a proof-of-concept demonstration that a biodegradable chitosan-based film system can integrate antibacterial functionality with blood-interactive behavior. Importantly, the inclusion of preliminary hemostatic assays represents a key distinguishing feature of this study compared with previous EO–chitosan film reports, which are typically limited to packaging-oriented antimicrobial and barrier evaluations.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chitosan powder (CAS No. 9012-76-4, purity ≥ 85% deacetylation, molecular weight ≈ 8.97 × 10⁵ Da) was obtained from AP Operations Co., Ltd. (Chonburi, Thailand) and sieved through a 200-mesh screen prior to use. Glacial acetic acid, glycerol, Tween 20, and other analytical-grade reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), while nutrient agar was supplied by Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Penicillin, used as a reference antibiotic, was acquired from a local pharmaceutical supplier in Thailand. Fresh pig blood, used for hemocompatibility assays, was procured from a local market. The bacterial strains E. coli (TISTR 073) and S. aureus (TISTR 2329) were provided by the Thailand Institute of Scientific and Technological Research (TISTR). Penicillin (CAS No. 69-57-8) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA; lot no. SLBV1234; expiry date: December 2026).

Plant materials of Zanthoxylum limonella were procured from a local market in Mae Chan District, Chiang Rai Province, Thailand, in April 2025. The species is commonly cultivated and commercially available, and no protected or endangered plant material was involved. Taxonomic identification was confirmed by Dr. Jantrararuk Tovaranonte, Head of the Mae Fah Luang Botanical Garden. A voucher specimen (MFU 10063) was prepared and deposited at the Mae Fah Luang Botanical Garden, Mae Fah Luang University, Chiang Rai, Thailand. Although the samples were obtained from a commercial source, all procedures followed relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations. The study also adheres to the principles of the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Policy Statement on Research Involving Species at Risk of Extinction and the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES). Following procurement, the plant material was air-dried at ambient temperature for approximately 7 days. The dried samples were subsequently sealed in airtight plastic bags and stored under dry conditions to minimize exposure to air and moisture prior to further analysis.

Extraction of Z. limonella essential oil

Dried of Z. limonella (250 g) were subjected to hydrodistillation using 1000 mL of distilled water in a round-bottom flask equipped with a Clevenger-type apparatus. The distillation was conducted continuously for 5 h to facilitate the release and condensation of volatile compounds. The resulting essential oil was separated and dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate to eliminate moisture residues. After filtration, the oil was transferred into seglass vials and stored at 4 °C under dark conditions to prevent oxidative degradation. The oil was used within 30 days of extraction to ensure consistent physicochemical properties for all experimental procedures.

Preparation of formulated films

Composite films were prepared following the method described by Chankachang et al.14, with slight modifications. The chitosan–gelatin ratio was kept constant across all formulations to isolate the effects of essential oil concentration on film properties, while different essential oil loadings were used to evaluate concentration-dependent structure–property–function relationships. In the first step, 2.0 g of chitosan and 0.23 g of glycerol (used as a plasticizer) were dissolved in 100 mL of 1% (v/v) aqueous acetic acid under continuous stirring at 45 °C for 4 h. Concurrently, 0.3 g of gelatin was dispersed in 30 mL of distilled water and heated at 120 °C for 10 min until fully dissolved. The gelatin solution was then gradually introduced into the chitosan mixture and stirred at 45 °C for an additional 3 h to ensure homogeneous blending. Subsequently, Z. limonella essential oil was added in various concentrations and stirred at 25 °C for 10 min to allow uniform dispersion within the biopolymer matrix. The final film-forming solutions were cast into 100 mm diameter polystyrene Petri dishes and dried at 40 °C for 10 h. After complete drying, the solidified films were detached from the molds. The resulting samples were designated as ZO0, ZO1, ZO2, ZO3, ZO4, and ZO5, corresponding to essential oil contents of 0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 g, respectively. Film characterization

All film characterization techniques employed in this study were modified from the comprehensive methodology reported by Khruengsai et al.11, with minor modifications to suit the specific properties of the present film formulation. Each technique was selected to evaluate critical physicochemical, structural, mechanical, and functional attributes of the bioactive films.

Mechanical properties

The tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (Ɛ) of the composite films, with an average thickness of approximately 0.80 mm, were evaluated using a universal testing machine (Instron, MA, USA). Film samples were cut into rectangular strips measuring 2 cm × 2 cm. A 1 kN static load cell was employed, with an initial grip separation set at 50 mm. The specimens were stretched at a crosshead speed of 60 mm/min until failure. All tests were conducted at ambient temperature (27 °C) under controlled relative humidity conditions (~ 40%).

SEM

The films were affixed to aluminum stubs and coated with a thin layer of gold using a sputter coater to improve surface conductivity. Both surface and cross-sectional views were obtained using a field emission SEM (FEI Quanta 450; ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 10–15 kV to ensure optimal resolution and contrast. The microscope was operated with an aperture size of 30 μm and a working distance of 10 mm to achieve high-quality visualization of surface topography and internal film structure.

Thermal analysis

The thermal behavior of the chitosan-based composite films was assessed using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) on a Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC1 instrument (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Approximately 5.00 mg of each film sample was placed into an alumina crucible and subjected to heating from room temperature to 500 °C at a constant rate of 10 °C/min under a continuous flow of nitrogen gas to prevent oxidative degradation. The analysis was conducted in a nitrogen atmosphere to evaluate the thermal degradation profile and estimate the thermal stability of the composite films.

FTIR

Before FTIR analysis, the dried film samples were gently cleaned using a stream of nitrogen gas to remove loosely attached surface particles. No solvent or water washing was performed to avoid changing the surface characteristics or chemical structure of the films. Dried film samples were analyzed using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA) equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory. Spectral data were collected over the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹. The presence of functional groups such as hydroxyl (–OH), amino (–NH₂), carbonyl (C = O), and ether (C–O) was confirmed by identifying their characteristic absorption peaks. Variations in peak intensity or position were used to assess potential chemical interactions or structural modifications resulting from the incorporation of Z. limonella essential oil.

Water vapor permeability (WVP) analysis

WVP of the chitosan-based films was evaluated using a modified gravimetric method. The experimental setup consisted of a sealed permeation chamber containing a cup with 5 mL of distilled water. Each film sample was securely sealed over the mouth of the cup to prevent leakage. The system was stabilized at room temperature (27 °C) and allowed to equilibrate for 3 h. The cup was placed at the center of an analytical balance, and its weight loss was recorded at 10-minute intervals. Contact angle measurement

A 5 µL droplet of distilled water was gently placed on the surface of each film using a goniometer (Krüss DSA100, Germany). The contact angle was recorded after 10 s at ambient temperature (approximately 25 °C). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the average values were reported.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of chitosan–gelatin composite films incorporated with varying concentrations of Z. limonella essential oil was evaluated using the agar disk diffusion method against both Gram-negative (E. coli) and Gram-positive (S. aureus) bacteria. Both bacteria were selected as representative model organisms due to their distinct cell wall structures and their relevance to wound infection and food contamination. These strains are commonly used as benchmark bacteria for evaluating antibacterial performance of biopolymer-based films.Circular film discs (6 mm in diameter) were aseptically prepared and placed on the surface of nutrient agar plates previously inoculated with bacterial suspensions. For comparative analysis, two positive controls were employed including pure Z. limonella essential oil applied in a volume of 6 µL onto sterile paper discs of the same diameter, and commercially available penicillin discs containing 10 µg of active compound. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions. Following incubation, the diameters of the inhibition zones surrounding each sample were measured in millimeters using a calibrated digital caliper.

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of chitosan–gelatin composite films (ZO0–ZO5) and Z. limonella essential oil was determined using the broth microdilution method with resazurin as a growth indicator, following the protocol adapted from Raphaël and Meimandipour12. For the preparation of film extracts, each film sample (100 mg) was cut into approximately 0.5 × 0.5 cm pieces and soaked in 2 mL of sterile Müller-Hinton broth at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm for 24 h. This extraction process was designed to allow the release of the bioactive components, including chitosan and essential oil, into the broth medium. The soaking step simulates the diffusion of active compounds from the film into an aqueous environment. The resulting film extracts were sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm syringe filter to remove undissolved film particles. The initial concentration of the film extract was calculated to be 50 mg/mL based on the film-to-broth ratio. For standardization in FICI calculations, the starting concentration of the film extract was adjusted to 22.5 mg/mL, matching the essential oil solution.

For the essential oil solution, Z. limonella essential oil was dissolved in 0.5% (v/v) Tween 80 to prepare a stock solution at 2.5% (v/v), which was equivalent to 22.5 mg/mL based on the assumed essential oil density of 0.9 g/mL. This starting concentration was selected to ensure a direct and comparable analysis between the film extract and the essential oil solution in the FICI evaluation. Serial two-fold dilutions were prepared for both the essential oil solution and the film extracts, starting from 22.5 mg/mL, to achieve final concentrations of 22.5, 11.25, 5.63, 2.81, 1.41, 0.70, and 0.35 mg/mL. Each well of a sterile 96-well microtiter plate contained 50 µL of the test sample (film extract or essential oil solution), 10 µL of bacterial suspension (approximately 10⁶ CFU/mL), and 10 µL of 0.675% resazurin solution. The plates were covered with sterile lids and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. 0.5% (v/v) Tween 80 and chloramphenicol were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that retained the resazurin’s blue color, indicating complete bacterial growth inhibition. The fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) was calculated to evaluate potential synergistic interactions between the chitosan–gelatin composite films and Z. limonella essential oil, following the method described by Moussii et al.15. Mixtures of film extract and essential oil solution were prepared at sub-MIC levels using two-fold serial dilutions in 96-well microtiter plates containing 150 µL of Müller-Hinton broth and 10 µL of bacterial suspension (10⁶ CFU/mL). Blood clotting test

A preliminary hemostatic assay was conducted to evaluate the blood absorption capacity of the chitosan–gelatin composite films. This assay was performed following the study of Chankachang et al.14 with some modification. Fresh pig blood obtained from a local food industry source was used for this experiment. As no live animals were involved and the blood was sourced post-mortem from an industrial supplier, formal animal ethics approval was not required for this study. Composite film samples (2 × 2 cm, approximately 40 mg) were placed into Petri dishes containing 2 mL of pig blood supplemented with anticoagulant to prevent coagulation during the test. The interaction between the films and blood was allowed to proceed for 10 min at room temperature. Images were captured before and after exposure to visually assess absorption behavior. Whole blood clotting time (WBCT) test

The whole blood clotting time (WBCT) of the chitosan–gelatin composite films (ZO0–ZO5) was evaluated using fresh porcine whole blood without anticoagulant, following established protocols for biomaterial assessment as described by Shao et al.16 with adaptations. The blood was collected from a local slaughterhouse and used within two hours to ensure physiological clotting activity. Each film sample, sized 2 cm × 2 cm, was placed in a sterile Petri dish and pre-warmed at 37 °C for 10 min prior to testing. A fixed volume of 200 µL of whole blood was carefully dropped onto the center of each film sample, and the stopwatch was started immediately upon contact. The Petri dishes were maintained at 37 °C throughout the test, and the blood mobility was assessed by gently tilting the dishes at 30-second intervals. The clotting time was defined as the time point at which the blood drop ceased to flow upon tilting. A clean glass Petri dish was used as the positive control because glass surfaces are known to activate the intrinsic coagulation pathway through contact activation of Factor XII (Mann et al., 2003; Bagot and Arya, 2008). The whole blood placed on a hydrophobic polystyrene Petri dish served as the negative control.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were statistically analyzed using SPSS software version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate significant differences among treatment groups, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post-hoc comparisons. Statistical significance was defined at a confidence level of P < 0.05. All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Additionally, the pork blood absorption data from the preliminary hemostatic test were analyzed using the same statistical approach.

Results and discussion

Film composition and morphology

Chitosan–gelatin composite films were successfully prepared with varying concentrations of Z. limonella essential oil ranging from 0% to 2.5% (Table 1). The primary polymer matrix, consisting of 2.0 g of chitosan and 0.3 g of gelatin plasticized with 0.23 g of glycerol, was kept constant across all formulations to ensure that the observed variations were solely attributable to essential oil incorporation. SEM analysis revealed distinct, concentration-dependent alterations in both surface and cross-sectional morphology following essential oil addition (Fig. 1). Incorporation of Z. limonella essential oil was associated with a slight increase in film thickness and a reduction in apparent density, which can be attributed to microstructural changes occurring during solvent casting. Dispersed hydrophobic oil droplets promote partial phase separation during drying, leading to the formation of internal voids and pores. These features reduce packing compactness within the polymer matrix and are consistent with the SEM observations showing progressively increased porosity at higher essential oil concentrations. The control film (ZO0) exhibited a smooth, compact, and homogeneous internal structure, characteristic of well-miscible chitosan–gelatin blends with strong intermolecular interactions. Upon incorporation of essential oil, the film morphology progressively evolved toward increased surface roughness and internal heterogeneity, with the appearance of discrete pores, interfacial voids, and localized structural irregularities. These features became more pronounced as the essential oil concentration increased, particularly in the ZO4 and ZO5 formulations. The observed morphological changes are attributed to partial phase separation between the hydrophilic polymer matrix and the hydrophobic essential oil, resulting in the formation of dispersed oil-rich domains within the film. At moderate essential oil concentrations, these domains were relatively well distributed, whereas higher oil loading promoted droplet aggregation and microstructural discontinuities. Similar morphology evolution has been reported in essential oil–loaded chitosan systems, where increasing oil content induces pore formation and internal heterogeneity due to limited polymer–oil compatibility15–17. A qualitative decrease in film transparency was observed with increasing essential oil content. This behavior is consistent with increased light scattering associated with heterogeneous microstructures and dispersed oil domains within the polymer matrix, as also reported for other essential oil–incorporated biopolymer films15–17. These structural modifications are closely associated with changes in mechanical performance, surface hydrophobicity, and fluid absorption behavior, as discussed in subsequent sections. The results confirm that incorporation of Z. limonellaessential oil systematically modulates the internal and surface architecture of chitosan–gelatin films in a concentration-dependent manner. This behavior is consistent with previous studies employing other essential oils, such as rosemary18 and thyme19, which reported comparable microstructural transitions.

Mechanical and barrier properties

Mechanical and barrier properties are critical parameters governing the practical applicability and handling stability of biodegradable films in both wound-contact and active packaging contexts. For wound-care applications, adequate tensile strength and flexibility are required to ensure that the film can be handled, conform to irregular wound surfaces, absorb fluids, and be removed without tearing. In packaging applications, mechanical robustness is equally important to maintain film integrity during processing, handling, and storage. Therefore, mechanical testing was conducted to evaluate whether incorporation of Z. limonella essential oil could enhance functional performance while preserving or improving structural stability.

The tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EAB) of the chitosan–gelatin films are summarized in Table 2. The control film (ZO0) exhibited a TS of 17.21 ± 1.19 MPa. With increasing essential oil concentration, TS increased progressively, reaching a maximum value of 39.04 ± 1.06 MPa at 2.0% essential oil loading (ZO4), corresponding to an increase of approximately 127% compared to the control. At the highest essential oil content (ZO5), TS decreased slightly to 35.45 ± 1.15 MPa, although it remained higher than that of the control film. Statistical differences among formulations are indicated in Table 2 (p < 0.05). A similar concentration-dependent trend was observed for elongation at break (EAB). The EAB increased from 70.41 ± 1.21% in the control film to 88.62 ± 1.72% at 2.0% essential oil (ZO4). At 2.5% essential oil (ZO5), EAB decreased slightly to 85.23 ± 1.23%, although it remained higher than the control film (p < 0.05). Overall, these results indicate that incorporation of Z. limonella essential oil increases tensile strength and elongation at break up to an optimal concentration (2.0%), beyond which slight reductions are observed.

Surface hydrophobicity, assessed by water contact angle measurements, increased steadily with increasing essential oil content, rising from 56.41° for ZO0 to 73.16° for ZO5. This enhancement in hydrophobicity is attributed to the hydrophobic nature of the major constituents of Z. limonella essential oil, such as limonene and sabinene. During solvent casting and drying, these low-polarity molecules preferentially migrate toward the film–air interface to minimize interfacial free energy, resulting in surface enrichment that reduces polarity and increases water repellency. Additionally, microstructural roughness induced by dispersed oil domains may further amplify apparent hydrophobicity by limiting water spreading on the film surface. Increased surface hydrophobicity is advantageous for both wound protection and food packaging, as it can reduce moisture penetration and surface wetting20,21. In contrast, water vapor permeability (WVP) values showed no statistically significant differences among formulations (p > 0.05), remaining within a narrow range of 1.48–1.53 × 10⁻⁹ g s⁻¹ m⁻¹ Pa⁻¹. This indicates that, despite increased surface hydrophobicity and microstructural modifications, the internal barrier structure of the films remained sufficiently intact to prevent notable changes in water vapor transport. Similar behavior has been reported for thyme oil–chitosan films, where improvements in mechanical properties and surface hydrophobicity did not translate into significant changes in WVP11. These results demonstrate that incorporation of Z. limonella essential oil enhances mechanical performance while maintaining barrier stability, supporting their suitability for multifunctional biodegradable applications2,11.

Thermal and chemical characterization

The thermal degradation behavior of the chitosan–gelatin films, with and without Z. limonella essential oil, was assessed using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), as shown in Fig. 2. All film samples exhibited a characteristic three-stage thermal degradation profile. The first weight loss, occurring below 150 °C, was attributed to moisture evaporation. The second, and most significant, weight loss occurred between 250 and 350 °C, corresponding to the thermal decomposition of chitosan and gelatin molecular chains. A final degradation step above 400 °C was associated with the carbonization of residual biopolymers. Notably, films containing moderate to high concentrations of essential oil (ZO3–ZO5) exhibited a slight rightward shift in the onset of the main degradation peak, indicating improved thermal stability compared to the control film (ZO0). This thermal enhancement may result from physical interactions between the essential oil constituents and the polymer matrix, which could delay the onset of thermal degradation. Similar stabilization effects have been reported in chitosan films incorporating oregano22, clove23, and cinnamon essential oils24, where phenolic compounds are known to improve thermal resistance by scavenging free radicals and limiting polymer chain scission. Chemical structure analysis using FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 3) confirmed the presence of functional groups characteristic of chitosan and gelatin across all film formulations. Key peaks included a broad O–H/N–H stretching band at approximately 3270 cm⁻¹, amide I (C = O stretching) at around 1655 cm⁻¹, and amide II (N–H bending) near 1554 cm⁻¹. Increasing essential oil content did not cause significant peak shifts, indicating that the essential oil was physically incorporated rather than chemically bonded to the polymer matrix. However, the essential oil-loaded films exhibited new absorption bands and increased intensity in the 1400–1500 cm⁻¹ region, which are likely associated with aromatic C–H bending and C = C stretching vibrations from monoterpenes and lignans present in Z. limonella essential oil25. These subtle spectral changes confirm the successful dispersion of essential oil within the film while preserving the chemical integrity of the chitosan–gelatin backbone. Comparable observations have been reported in chitosan films loaded with rosemary and thyme essential oils, where physical incorporation occurred through hydrogen bonding and Van der Waals interactions11,26.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of chitosan–gelatin films incorporated with Z. limonella essential oil was evaluated against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, as summarized in Tables 3 and 4. The control film (ZO0) exhibited minimal antibacterial activity, with inhibition zone diameters of 7.45 ± 1.17 mm for E. coli and 7.76 ± 1.04 mm for S. aureus. Both the pure essential oil and the essential oil–loaded films showed increasing antibacterial activity with increasing essential oil content (Table 3). For the pure essential oil, inhibition zones increased from 7.22 ± 1.29 mm to 13.23 ± 1.44 mm against E. coli and from 7.56 ± 0.84 mm to 14.34 ± 1.23 mm against S. aureus as the oil concentration increased from 0.5% to 2.5%. A similar trend was observed for the essential oil–loaded films, confirming retention of antibacterial activity within the film matrix. Compared to the control film (ZO0), all essential oil–loaded films exhibited significantly enhanced antibacterial activity (p < 0.05), with inhibition zone diameters increasing progressively with essential oil concentration. The highest activity was observed for the ZO5 formulation, with inhibition zones of 15.09 ± 1.11 mm for E. coli and 15.38 ± 0.56 mm for S. aureus, corresponding to approximately a twofold increase relative to the control. Consistent trends were observed in the MIC results (Table 4). For E. coli, MIC values decreased from 11.25 mg/mL in ZO1 to 1.41 mg/mL in ZO4 and ZO5, while for S. aureus, MIC values decreased from 22.50 mg/mL in ZO1 to 0.70 mg/mL in ZO5. The FICI values indicated additive effects at lower concentrations and synergistic effects at higher essential oil contents (ZO3–ZO5), with FICI ≤ 0.5.

Overall, these results demonstrate that incorporation of Z. limonella essential oil markedly enhances the antibacterial efficacy of chitosan–gelatin films compared to the control, with the greatest effectiveness observed at higher essential oil concentrations. The antibacterial activity of Z. limonella essential oil is primarily associated with its hydrophobic bioactive constituents, including limonene, sabinene, and linalool. These compounds can partition into bacterial cell membranes, disrupt membrane integrity, and increase permeability, leading to leakage of intracellular components and inhibition of essential cellular functions27. In addition, the chitosan matrix may contribute through electrostatic interactions between protonated amino groups and negatively charged bacterial cell surfaces, further promoting membrane destabilization28. Notably, S. aureus was more susceptible than E. coli across all formulations, which is consistent with previous reports on biopolymer-based antimicrobial films8. This difference is commonly attributed to the presence of an outer membrane in Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli, which can limit the penetration of hydrophobic compounds29. Similar antibacterial behavior has been reported in chitosan-based films incorporating essential oils such as rosemary30, clove10, and cinnamon31. In addition to biomedical relevance, the antibacterial properties of the developed films suggest potential applicability as active materials. However, this potential application remains preliminary, and further studies involving real systems, storage conditions, sensory evaluation, and migration analysis are required to validate practical performance and safety.

Blood absorption and clotting time

Hemostatic properties are a critical requirement for biomedical materials intended for wound dressing applications, as rapid blood clot formation is essential for controlling bleeding, preventing excessive blood loss, and initiating the wound healing process. Effective hemostatic materials can also stabilize the wound environment and reduce the risk of infection. Therefore, evaluation of the blood absorption capacity and clotting performance of the developed films is important for assessing their suitability for wound management. The blood absorption capacity and preliminary in vitro hemostatic performance of the essential oil–loaded chitosan–gelatin films were evaluated using porcine blood, and the results are summarized in Table 5. The control film (ZO0) exhibited a blood absorption capacity of 325 ± 10%. Blood absorption increased significantly with increasing essential oil content, reaching a maximum of 651 ± 20% for the ZO4 formulation (p < 0.05). The ZO5 film also demonstrated substantial absorption (551 ± 17%), although slightly lower than ZO4. Whole blood clotting time measurements (Table 5) showed a clear improvement in clotting efficiency with essential oil incorporation. The control film exhibited a clotting time of 10.53 ± 0.41 min, whereas clotting time decreased progressively with increasing essential oil content, reaching 4.18 ± 0.38 min for the ZO4 film, which was comparable to the positive glass control (4.01 ± 0.13 min). The ZO5 film maintained a short clotting time of 4.87 ± 0.28 min, indicating sustained hemostatic performance at higher essential oil concentrations. The enhanced blood absorption is associated with microstructural changes induced by essential oil incorporation, including increased porosity, internal voids, and surface roughness, as observed by SEM. These features facilitate capillary-driven fluid uptake, which is characteristic of porous hemostatic materials26. In addition, the intrinsic hydrophilicity of chitosan and gelatin contributes to effective blood retention within the film matrix. The improved clotting performance is primarily attributed to the intrinsic hemostatic properties of chitosan. The positively charged amino groups of chitosan can interact with negatively charged membranes of red blood cells and platelets, promoting cellular aggregation and platelet activation, thereby accelerating clot formation32. Increased surface roughness and porosity may further enhance blood–film contact, facilitating faster clot initiation. The role of Z. limonella essential oil in coagulation appears to be indirect. While essential oil incorporation modifies the film microstructure and may influence surface interactions, direct biochemical involvement in the coagulation cascade cannot be confirmed based on the present data and requires further investigation12,33 When compared with previously reported essential oil–incorporated biodegradable films, the hemostatic performance of the present system is within the upper range of reported values. Blood absorption capacity (up to ~ 651%) and clotting time (~ 4 min) are comparable to those reported for porous chitosan-based and essential oil–modified biopolymer films, where structural modifications enhance fluid uptake and clot formation34,35. These results suggest that incorporation of Z. limonella essential oil improves hemostatic functionality without compromising structural integrity. These findings indicate that incorporation of Z. limonella essential oil enhances blood absorption and reduces clotting time of chitosan–gelatin films, highlighting their potential as multifunctional biodegradable materials for wound-related applications. However, further studies on hemocompatibility, cytotoxicity, and in vivo performance are required to validate their biomedical applicability.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the development of a sustainable, biodegradable chitosan–gelatin composite film incorporating Z. limonella essential oil and its multifunctional performance. From a sustainability perspective, the work contributes to green biomaterials research by employing renewable, biodegradable polymers (chitosan and gelatin) and a plant-derived bioactive agent as an alternative to synthetic polymers and conventional antimicrobial additives. The results show that functional enhancement can be achieved through formulation-driven design without reliance on petroleum-based materials or hazardous chemicals, supporting environmentally compatible material development. Incorporation of Z. limonella essential oil systematically modulated film properties in a concentration-dependent manner. At an optimal essential oil loading of 2.0% (ZO4), the films exhibited a 127% increase in tensile strength (from 17.21 ± 1.19 MPa to 39.04 ± 1.06 MPa) together with enhanced flexibility, with elongation at break increasing from 70.41% to 88.62%. Surface hydrophobicity increased steadily, with water contact angles rising from 56.41° (control) to 73.16° at the highest oil concentration, while water vapor permeability remained stable within a narrow range of 1.48–1.53 × 10⁻⁹ g s⁻¹ m⁻¹ Pa⁻¹, indicating preserved barrier performance. The composite films exhibited clear antibacterial activity against both E. coli and S. aureus, with inhibition zones reaching approximately 15 mm at higher essential oil loadings. In addition, the films showed enhanced blood absorption capacity, increasing from 325 ± 10% for the control film to 651 ± 20% for ZO4, and a marked reduction in whole blood clotting time from 10.53 ± 0.41 min to 4.18 ± 0.38 min, comparable to the glass positive control (4.01 ± 0.13 min). These results indicate promising preliminary in vitro hemostatic behavior, primarily driven by chitosan’s intrinsic bioactivity and essential oil–induced microstructural modifications. The combination of mechanical robustness, antibacterial activity, and hemostatic performance suggests that the developed films may be suitable for use as multifunctional wound dressing materials, particularly in applications requiring rapid bleeding control and infection prevention. The high blood absorption capacity and reduced clotting time support their potential for managing acute or superficial wounds, where rapid hemostasis is essential. In addition, the observed antibacterial activity, stable barrier properties, and increased surface hydrophobicity indicate potential applicability as active biodegradable food packaging materials. In such applications, the films may help inhibit microbial growth on food surfaces and contribute to improved product stability during storage.This work provides a proof-of-concept demonstration that a biodegradable chitosan-based film system can integrate antibacterial functionality with blood-interactive performance while maintaining mechanical integrity and barrier stability. Although the present study is limited to in vitro evaluation, the findings highlight the potential of this material platform for sustainable biomedical and packaging applications. Future studies should address essential oil release kinetics, cytotoxicity, hemocompatibility, sterilization compatibility, and in vivo performance to support regulatory translation and application-specific validation.
