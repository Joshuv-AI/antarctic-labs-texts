Between scarcity and surfeit: a familial tale of contradictory iron disorders (β -thalassemia and iron-refractory iron deficiency anemia)
Authors: Maha AlRiyami, A. AlSayegh, M. ALRawahi, J. Balasuburamanian, M. Rohlfs, L. Peterson, C. Klein, Y. Wali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
β-thalassemia and iron refractory iron deficiency anemia (IRIDA) are distinct hereditary causes of microcytic anemia. Their co-occurrence in the same individual is extremely rare and complicates diagnosis and management. This case series emphasizes on the importance of performing molecular analysis to define personalized therapeutic strategies. We report an Omani family with high level of interfamily marriages presenting with persistent microcytic hypochromic anemia, low ferritin levels, and partial transient response to intravenous iron therapy. Initial investigations suggested non-transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia due to persistently elevated HbF and a homozygous HBB promoter variant (NM_000518.5 (HBB):c.-121 C > T). Whole-exome sequencing subsequently identified a novel homozygous TMPRSS6 missense variant (NM_001374504.1(TMPRSS6):c.1070G > C, p.Cys357Ser), classified as likely pathogenic for IRIDA. Extensive family history revealed multiple relatives with unexplained iron deficiency requiring intravenous iron therapy and two cousins unnecessarily treated as transfusion-dependent thalassemia. After molecular confirmation, transfusions were discontinued, and the affected siblings were successfully managed with periodic IV iron therapy. This case series highlights the importance of comprehensive genomic evaluation in complex anemia presentations, particularly in consanguineous populations. The coexistence of β-thalassemia and IRIDA can obscure the clinical picture, delay appropriate therapy, and result in suboptimal therapies. Early integration of genomics is crucial for accurate diagnosis and optimal patient management.

Background

Anemia is one of the significant global health burdens attributed to both genetic and acquired factors. Beta (β) thalassemia and iron-refractory iron deficiency anemia (IRIDA) are two hereditary causes of severe anemia. β-thalassemia is an inherited hemoglobinopathy caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in the hemoglobin subunit beta (HBB) gene. This results in reduced or complete absence of the β-globin chains, causing hemolytic anemia with a clinical presentation in the first year of life with variable severity, ranging from asymptomatic carriers to regularly transfusion-dependent cases. Mutations in the HBB gene, although uncommon, could increase in communities with consanguineous marriages [1]. One example is the NM_000518.5 (HBB):c.-121 C > T (also referred to as -71 C > T in a previous nomenclature), a homozygous mutation located in the promoter region of the HBB gene, within an evolutionarily conserved sequence responsible for transcription regulation. This mutation was first reported in Oman (2011) [1] in a homozygous state. Cases of homozygous NM_000518.5 (HBB):c.-121 C > T mutation were found to behave as a mild β+ -thalassemia and do not necessitate regular transfusion [1, 2].

IRIDA is a rare autosomal recessive form of iron deficiency anemia caused by mutations in the TMPRSS6 gene that encodes matriptase-2 enzyme, a key regulator of hepcidin hormone expression that controls the body’s iron homeostasis [3]. Patients with IRIDA have persistent microcytic, hypochromic anemia that is caused by dysfunctional intestinal iron absorption and impaired release of iron stores. This results in patients being unresponsive to oral iron therapy [4].

Here, we report the first cases of homozygous NM_000518.5 (HBB):c.-121 C > T β-thalassemia and IRIDA co-occurrence in consanguineous first-cousin marriages.

Methods

High-molecular-weight genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The amplification by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and Sanger sequencing of the β-globin gene promoter region and α-globin were previously described in [1].

Exome Sequencing was performed using NGS-Star (Hamilton) with Illumina DNA prep with Exome 2.5 Enrichment library preparation and an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) sequencing platform. The bioinformatics analysis pipeline uses Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA 0.7.15), Genome Analysis ToolKit (GATK3.6), Variant Effect Predictor (VEP 89), and frequency filters with public and in-house databases.

Case presentation

The index patient is a female child born in 2017 to consanguineous first cousins; both parents were assumed to have thalassemia trait. She initially presented at six months of age with pallor. Further workup revealed microcytic hypochromic anemia and increased levels of HbF. Due to a positive family history of transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia (TDT), the patient was advised to undergo chronic transfusion/chelation therapy by a peripheral hospital. However, her guardians declined the recommended transfusion therapy and sought a second opinion from our hospital.

The index patient was first presented at our clinic at the age of one year during an episode of viral infection and was found to have a baseline Hb level of 6 g/dL; however, she was clinically stable and was not transfused. She was screened for genetic mutations associated with thalassemia as per the regulations in Oman when thalassemia hemoglobinopathy is suspected. A homozygous NM_000518.5 (HBB):c.-121 C > T mutation was detected at the promoter region in the HBB gene [5]. Moreover, using Gap polymerase chain reaction (GAP-PCR), the seven most common α-globin deletions associated with thalassemia were analyzed [6], and she was found to be heterozygous for the alpha plus 3.7 kbo deletion [2]. Of note, all the affected family members were found to be heterozygotes for the same deletion.

After discussion with the family, a shared decision was made that she will be assigned a non-transfusion-dependent thalassemia (NTDT) and to keep her on folic acid prophylaxis. Her Hb rose to 9 g/dL, and she continued follow-up and folic acid treatment. Two years later, the family had another baby girl who showed an identical clinical presentation.

A comprehensive review of the family history revealed that the mother was suspected of having severe iron deficiency anemia, which has not been responsive to oral iron therapy. Moreover, she did not have history of heavy menstruations, and her coagulation profile was normal. Her genetic testing was not performed, and she has been treated for episodes of symptomatic anemia by intravenous (IV) iron therapy. Furthermore, the mother has a total of seven siblings, of which three are also known cases of severe iron deficiency anemia, and all were on an irregular IV iron therapy. While the other four siblings are normal. After confirming the IRIDA mutation in the mother, her three siblings on IV Iron therapy were clinically suspected to have the same mutation, however, they were not tested. Furthermore, one of the mother’s female siblings has two kids diagnosed with β-thalassemia major and are on chronic transfusion therapy (Table 1; Fig. 1). They have microcytic hypochromic anemia, and they were to be homozygous for HBB promotor mutation. They were put off transfusion and maintained a hemoglobin of 9.5–10.5 g/dL. They did not need any IV iron therapy for the last three years possibly because they do not have the TMPRSS6 mutation. Their serum ferritin and percent transferrin saturation were elevated due to their previous history of regular transfusion.

Interestingly, on follow up both the index patient and her sister, had low serum ferritin and percent saturation of transferrin levels (2 ng/ml and 3% respectively), and they dropped their HB to 6–7 g/dL with no infection. Given the family history of severe iron deficiency anemia in many members of the family, it was decided to give a trial of IV iron to both daughters, and there was marked rise in HB. Next, we performed whole exome sequencing of the nuclear family of the index patient (the parents and two daughters) was analyzed. In both daughters, a homozygous missense variant was identified in the Transmembrane Serine Protease 6 (TMPRSS6) gene (GRCh37.p13, chr22:37480783 C > G, transcript NM_001374504.1(TMPRSS6):c.1070G > C, p.Cys357Ser, leading to a protein change p.Cys366Ser. PolyPhen and SIFT algorithms suggest that this variant is probably damaging (0.999) and deleterious (0) for the protein. Moreover, the Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) score also suggests a damaging effect for the variant. Furthermore, Alpha Missense predicts that the clinical relevance score of p.Cys366Ser (0.992) is likely pathogenic. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, this variant can be classified as likely pathogenic for IRIDA. This variant has not been described before, but variants affecting the TMPRSS6 gene have been previously associated with IRIDA.

The parents’ genetic profile revealed that they are both heterozygous carriers for the TMPRSS6 mutation. Moreover, the WGS analysis also revealed that the asymptomatic father and the two daughters are homozygous for the intronic 5’UTR variant in the HBB gene NM_000518.5 (HBB): c.-121 C > T, which we previously identified by GAP-PCR. The mother was found to be a heterozygous carrier of the same HBB variant.

Two cousins previously treated as transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) were re-evaluated and found to have the same homozygous NM_000518.5 (HBB): c.-121 C > T promoter mutation. Transfusions were discontinued, and they stabilized at 9.5–10.5 g/dL, confirming the benign nature of this HBB variant when not compounded by TMPRSS6 mutations.

Outcome and follow-up

After the genetic results, the proband was initiated on 15 mg/kg IV iron therapy with Ferric carboxymaltose IV (CSL Vifor) targeting an Hb level above 9 g/dL, alongside oral folic acid supplementation. One month later, at the patient’s follow-up visit, she showed a favorable response with a sharp rise in her hemoglobin levels to 9.5–10 g/dL. Thereafter, she has continued IV iron every 3 months to maintain her baseline hemoglobin levels (Fig. 2). The same treatment protocol was also used for her sister. They were both monitored every 3 months for their Hb, serum ferritin and percent transferrin saturation. Their last Hb values on 23/04/2026 were 10.2 and 10.3 g/dl respectively. Their serum ferritin was in the normal range (42 and 59 ng/ml, however, their percent saturation of transferrin remained low (5 and 7% respectively). If their Hb is lower than 9 g/dl, IV iron was given. IV iron therapy has never been discontinued.

The adult hematologist treating the mother was informed about her genetic findings and has been treated accordingly and maintained on IV iron therapy based on her Hb level. The father, heterozygous for TMPRSS6 mutation and homozygous for the HBB promoter variant, maintains stable hemoglobin levels with persistent microcytosis and hypochromia consistent with NTDT β-thalassemia phenotype, and leads a normal life without any hematinic support.

Discussion

Here, we report the first documented co-occurrence of homozygous HBB promoter mutation (NM_000518.5 (HBB): c.-121 C > T) and a novel homozygous TMPRSS6 variant (p.Cys357Ser) resulting in overlapping phenotypes of mild β-thalassemia and IRIDA in two siblings from a highly consanguineous family. This unprecedented clinical scenario involved multiple members of an extended family with consanguineous marriages resulting in these complex patients’ phenotypes.

Thalassemia and IRIDA represent opposite ends of iron pathophysiology. Thalassemia syndromes are associated with iron overload even in NTDT forms [7]. IRIDA is associated with severe iron-deficiency anemia that is not responsive to oral iron therapy [4]. The co-occurrence of the two disorders in our cases makes the clinical presentation particularly challenging, necessitating genetic investigation to uncover the uncertainty of what accounts for the severe iron deficiency associated with very low serum ferritin levels despite the thalassemic status. It’s worth noting that all the tested family members have been found to have α-thalassemia trait, which is prevalent in the Omani population. It is well known that α-thalassemia mutations can ameliorate the clinical severity of β-thalassemia by reducing α-globin chain production, therefore, improving the α:β globin chain imbalance that drives ineffective erythropoiesis. As a result, patients may have a milder course, and present with a phenotype consistent with NTDT [8].

We previously reported the HBB promoter mutation NM_000518.5 (HBB): c.-121 C > T in our index patient [1, 2], yet we were surprised to find that she was dropping her hemoglobin. Upon further investigations using WGS, a p. Cys357Ser mutation in the TMPRSS6 gene was uncovered in our case. TMPRSS6 consists of 44,278 nucleotides with 18 exons coding for Transmembrane Serin Protease 6, an 811 amino acid protein [9]. This protein is essential for regulating iron metabolism by modulating hepcidin expression, a hormone that controls iron absorption and distribution in the body. The observed variant p.Cys357Ser disrupts a conserved cysteine residue and replaces a polar, neutral amino acid, Cysteine (Cys) at position 357 with Serine (Ser), which is another polar, neutral amino acid. This change results in alteration of the CUB domains, which are extracellular regions involved in protein-protein interactions and are critical for the proper functioning of developmental processes and complement activation [10]. Cysteine residues within these domains often participate in disulfide bond formation, which is essential for maintaining the proteins’ structural integrity. A substitution at this site could disrupt disulfide bonding, potentially leading to misfolding or loss of function of the protease.

Based on this new molecular diagnosis, we started IV iron therapy, an a priori counterintuitive option since beta-thalassemia patients usually require iron chelation therapy to reduce systemic iron overload. Of note, we observed a remarkable improvement in IV iron substitution, supporting our hypothesis that not the HBB promoter mutation but rather the TMPRSS6 mutation caused the significant anemia in our patient. Moreover, the significant improvement of Hb level happened along with the initiation of IV iron. Of interest, the father with a homozygous mutation in HBB has never required any treatment and maintains a reasonable hemoglobin level ranging from 9.5 to 11 g/dL, further supporting the benign nature of this homozygous HBB mutation. While the mother is heterozygous for both TMPRSS6 and HBB mutations, she has always been anemic with severe iron deficiency and dependent on IV iron. To the best of our knowledge, the heterozygosity for both mutations has never been reported previously. A plausible explanation for her case is that heterozygosity for both genes might lead to severe impairment of iron absorption, as TMPRSS6 mutations can lead to higher hepcidin levels, which in turn inhibit iron absorption and utilization.

Thus, our findings highlight the need to perform genome-wide sequencing whenever phenotypic features (clinical presentation, laboratory data, response to therapy) do not fit classical patterns. In our case, the mild promotor HBB mutation, the continuing drop of Hb of these patients, and the routine laboratory investigations that showed very low serum ferritin and percent saturation of transferrin have raised a high index of suspicion to a defect in iron metabolism and IRIDA. Furthermore, particularly in consanguineous populations, the co-occurrence of more than one pathogenic mutation should be considered. Our patient illustrates that a correct molecular diagnosis is of utmost importance to tailor personalized therapeutic strategies and to avoid over-therapies. This study is subject to the inherent limitations of a case series design, and we could not do the genetic analysis for some of the study members.
