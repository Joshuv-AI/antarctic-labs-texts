ABSTRACT
In a decade marked by unprecedented challenges, the Kaiser Permanente Scholars Academy has redefined what it means to prepare nurse leaders for the future, serving as a transformative force through strategic academic partnerships, innovative leadership programs, and a steadfast commitment to Caring Science. The Academy has created clear pathways for professional growth from the bedside to executive leadership. Initiatives such as the RN-to-BSN, MSN, and DNP programs, coupled with targeted leadership development experiences, have not only expanded knowledge and skills but have fostered resilience, compassion, and systems thinking. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the Academy demonstrated agility and purpose, integrating virtual learning, resilience strategies, and Caring Science principles to sustain leadership capacity. The recent evolution into the Quantum Caring Leadership Academy builds upon this legacy, emphasizing equity, innovation, and human-centered leadership as essential pillars for the future. This manuscript reflects on the Academy’s achievements, lessons learned, and the vision for its next chapter, offering a model for other health care organizations seeking to cultivate leadership excellence while honoring the art and science of nursing.

General Johnson-Brown was the mentor I needed to think of myself in a new light. And from that time forward, I did not look back.General Johnson-Brown was generous with her time and resources, including her own expertise. And that’s what mentors do. They look for leadership attributes, they call them out, share of themselves and their resources, and incite a call to action. This is what I have come to characterize in my life as “generous leadership”—the characteristics of which I have tried to not only practice in my own life but also be a role model for others. And for me, this has also included speaking and writing widely to advocate for patients and the profession of nursing. When my first husband received end-of-life care, I quickly learned that the truly great nurses were the ones who practiced compassion. Indeed, one cannot be a great nurse without compassion. In these divisive times, it is incumbent upon nurses to stand together, and with a generous and compassionate leadership stance, advocate for what will make life better for patients and the profession alike.

Luminary Reflection

Sue Hassmiller, PhD, RN, FAAN

I did not start out my nursing career thinking I was a leader. What I had however was passion, drive, a thirst for knowledge, and a mindset of what was possible, rather than impossible. These attributes always seemed to open up new opportunities for me. And it was one such opportunity that led me to a PhD program where faculty member Dr Hazel Johnson-Brown, the country’s first Black female army general, and Chief of the U.S. Army Nurse Corps, stared me in the face one day and said, “this is it … you are the leader now … so look straight ahead and start leading.” It was at that moment that another human being saw something in me and compelled me to take note … and to take action!! General Johnson-Brown was the mentor I needed to think of myself in a new light. And from that time forward, I did not look back.

General Johnson-Brown was generous with her time and resources, including her own expertise. And that’s what mentors do. They look for leadership attributes, they call them out, share of themselves and their resources, and incite a call to action. This is what I have come to characterize in my life as “generous leadership”—the characteristics of which I have tried to not only practice in my own life but also be a role model for others. And for me, this has also included speaking and writing widely to advocate for patients and the profession of nursing. Mentoring younger nurses using a generous leadership approach has always been one of the highlights of my nursing career.

Thus, when I retired from the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation in 2022, I began my encore career as an accredited executive leadership coach to continue my strong desire to help develop the next generation of nurses. I not only coach and mentor my clients with their own challenges but also teach nurse leaders how to coach the nurses they oversee as a leadership strategy. With my leadership company, SuLu, I partnered with other organizations, such as the UCSF Academy for Healthcare Leadership, to help nurse leaders foster self-understanding, deploy strengths, and improve relational strategies. They do this by listening intently to staff, being curious and discerning rather than judging (asking rather than telling, or even demanding), and embracing both/and thinking. These strategies empower nurses to thrive in complexity, enhance workplace well-being and contribute to a resilient health care culture. The other attribute I stress in my coaching and mentoring is the need for nurses to be compassionate. As technology and artificial intelligence revolutionize efficiencies in care, nurses must retain their human touch and compassion for patients. When my first husband received end-of-life care, I quickly learned that the truly great nurses were the ones who practiced compassion. Indeed, one cannot be a great nurse without compassion. In these divisive times, it is incumbent upon nurses to stand together, and with a generous and compassionate leadership stance, advocate for what will make life better for patients and the profession alike.

HONORING THE PATH, ILLUMINATING THE FUTURE

Over the past 10 years, the Kaiser Permanente Scholars Academy has emerged as a national model for visionary nursing leadership development. Through bold academic partnerships, innovative leadership programs, and the infusion of Caring Science into professional practice, we have nurtured a generation of nurse leaders to thrive in complexity, drive change, and lead with integrity. Quantum Caring Leadership emerges from the synthesis of quantum theory, complexity science, and Watson’s Caring Science, offering a leadership model that embraces the interrelatedness of people, systems, and outcomes.1 Rather than viewing leadership as a linear process of command and control, Quantum Caring Leadership recognizes the dynamic, fluid nature of human and organizational relationships. It emphasizes presence, coherence between values and actions, and the intentional creation of healing environments—principles that resonate deeply within our organizational mission. This framework provides a unifying thread for the evolution of our Scholars Academy and the development of the Quantum Caring Leadership Academy (QCLA), ensuring that our leaders are prepared to guide with both competence and compassion in an increasingly complex world.

This chapter honors our journey—and looks forward. As we enter the next decade, we recognize the call for a new paradigm of leadership: one that unites operational rigor with relational wisdom, and evidence with heart. This vision is exemplified by the establishment of our QCLA, which represents the next generation of leadership development at Kaiser Permanente. This vision has come to life most powerfully in the Associate Chief Nurse Executive (ACNE) Education and Mentorship Program—a living example of how intentional, competency-based pathways can shape the next generation of leaders.

THE PAST DECADE: CULTIVATING LEADERS THROUGH ACADEMIC AND PROFESSIONAL EXCELLENCE

One of the most impactful exemplars of our leadership development philosophy is the ACNE Education and Mentorship Program. Conceived as a deliberate succession pathway, this fellowship prepares future nurse executives to navigate the complexity of modern health care while remaining grounded in relational leadership. Rooted in the American Organization for Nursing Leadership (AONL) Nurse Executive Competencies,2 the program blends structured education, immersive mentorship, and project-based learning to build readiness for executive roles. Fellows engage in strategic planning, governance exposure, and high-level operational decision-making while also benefiting from individualized coaching informed by Liz Cunningham’s framework for providing a “fresh perspective at precisely the right time,” recognized as the way adults learn best.3

The ACNE curriculum is also aligned with Korn Ferry’s research on leadership agility and self-awareness, which identifies these qualities as critical predictors of high performance in complex environments.4 This approach ensures that emerging leaders are not only technically proficient but also able to lead with adaptability, emotional intelligence, and systems thinking. Throughout the fellowship, participants build competencies in financial management, talent development, operational leadership, and strategic influence, with a focus on professionalism and integrity within the framework of Caring Science. Each module of the ACNE program was mapped to the AONL Nurse Executive Competencies2, ensuring alignment with national leadership standards. Coaching components drew on Cunningham’s adult learning approach,3 while Korn Ferry’s leadership agility model4 informed experiential and reflective activities. Evaluation data from the program show that graduates consistently advance into senior leadership roles within the organization, demonstrating both immediate and sustained impact. As such, the ACNE Fellowship serves as a living example of how intentional, competency-based leadership development can transform individuals, teams, and systems.

The journey of the past 10 years began with a vision: to create a place where every nurse, regardless of where they stood in their career, could see a clear, attainable path to leadership. That vision took root in strong academic partnerships, built not just to check the box of higher education but to transform how our nurses thought, acted, and led.

It started with the RN-to-BSN program at Samuel Merritt University, where Caring Science was not simply an add-on but woven into the fabric of every class. Nurses were encouraged to pause and reflect, to see their patients as whole beings, and to see themselves as part of that wholeness. The MSN in Clinical Nurse Leadership at the University of San Francisco took that foundation and pointed it toward change—teaching nurses to transform microsystems, drive quality and safety, and foster collaboration across teams. At the doctoral level, our DNP in Systems Leadership with University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) aligned with our organization’s succession strategies, preparing future nurse executives to lead with clarity, courage, and vision. Together, these academic-practice partnerships created a coherent pathway where nurses at every level could see themselves as part of a leadership journey. Education by itself does not produce leaders; it also requires the presence of opportunity. Over the years, the Scholars Academy became a launchpad for leadership, offering programs for every stage of the journey. New leaders found their footing in the Foundations for Excellence in Healthcare Leadership Program, where they learned the AONL competencies and how to navigate the complexities of people and process. Mid-career nurses honed their skills in the Academy for Healthcare Leadership, building expertise in change management and strategic communication. Nurse educators deepened their craft through the Excellence in Nursing Education Certificate, and high-potential leaders experienced immersive mentorship with Chief Nurse Executives through our Nurse Executive Fellow Program. Even frontline nurses found a place to grow through our Foundations of EBP Workshops, learning how to turn curiosity into measurable improvement.

These programs, anchored in Caring Science, have reached hundreds of nurses across our system, providing structured opportunities for growth while cultivating resilience and innovation. This foundation has brought us to our next evolution: the Quantum Caring Leadership Academy (QCLA). While the Scholars Academy began before the term “Quantum Caring Leadership” entered the lexicon, its evolution mirrors this philosophy—blending relational presence with systems thinking to address the complexities of modern care. Built on a decade of learning, QCLA recognizes that today’s leaders face a different reality—one of constant complexity, rapid change, and the need to connect deeply in a fragmented world. It blends quantum theory, systems thinking, and relational leadership into a model that values not only excellence and innovation but also wholeness, equity, and human connection.

QCLA provides tailored development, learning paths, and structured advancement for leaders at every stage. It builds on the Caritas programs we know and love, adds innovative experiences like equine leadership and leadership simulation, and partners with academic institutions to create interprofessional, cross-boundary leadership development. It treats mentorship as a sacred practice, advocacy as a core responsibility, and recognition as essential to sustaining the joy of leading.

Today marks the 10th anniversary of the Scholars Academy, which has contributed to preparing a workforce in nursing equipped for future needs. QCLA is not just a program—it is a movement. It is a declaration that leadership in nursing is not defined solely by expertise but by the courage to lead with humanity. As we step into this next chapter, we do so together—open-minded, open-hearted, and committed to leading with wisdom, purpose, and care.

Our next chapter in leadership development is intentionally grounded in 2 complementary frameworks—quantum caring leadership and human-centered leadership—that together offer a holistic, future-ready approach to nurse leadership. This forward-looking vision is echoed by Dr Susan Hassmiller, whose reflection in Appendix A underscores the enduring influence of mentoring, generous leadership, and compassion as essential qualities for cultivating the next generation of nurse leaders.

Quantum Caring Leadership, as described by Watson and colleagues,1 blends the principles of quantum theory with Caring Science, urging leaders to recognize the interconnectedness of all systems and the transformative potential of authentic human presence in organizational life. It moves leadership beyond transactional management into a realm where values, vision, and relationships shape outcomes as much as policies and procedures.

In parallel, human-centered leadership, as articulated by Leclerc, Kennedy, and Campis,5 emphasizes compassion, trust, and the creation of psychologically safe environments as essential to unlocking team potential. It calls on leaders to prioritize well-being and belonging alongside productivity and performance. Embedding human-centered leadership practices not only fosters team trust and psychological safety but also mitigates burnout and turnover—critical outcomes for sustaining leadership pipelines.

Together, these frameworks position the QCLA as more than a skills program—it becomes a movement for cultural transformation. By aligning development programs with these principles, we prepare leaders to navigate uncertainty, foster resilience, and cocreate healing environments where both care teams and patients thrive. Over the next decade, effective nursing leadership will necessitate a balanced commitment to courage, compassion, and innovative approaches. The QCLA offers a blueprint—one we invite other organizations to adapt, refine, and share as we shape the future together.
