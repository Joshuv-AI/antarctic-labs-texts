Analysis of antennal transcriptome and gustatory receptor genes in Apis cerana cerana collecting pollens from different nectar source plants
Authors: Lina Guo, Chang Song, Yu Zhang, Yuan Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTaste perception is crucial for guiding food selection and regulating insect feeding behaviors. This study aimed to identify taste-related genes in Apis cerana cerana by analyzing antennal transcriptomes through RNA sequencing and functional annotation. Results showed 241, 135, and 102 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the YC (rapeseed pollen foragers) vs. CC (non-foraging controls), LC (pear pollen foragers) vs. CC, and LC vs. YC groups, respectively. GO annotation indicated these DEGs were enriched in functions like defense response to Gram-positive bacteria, integral component of plasma membrane, and signal transduction. KEGG analysis revealed involvement in pathways such as tyrosine metabolism and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction. Five gustatory receptor genes were identified from the antennal transcriptome, all with complete open reading frames and seven transmembrane domains, showing high homology with those of other hymenopteran insects like Apis mellifera. Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that these genes clustered with counterparts from Apis mellifera and Apis dorsata, indicating significant functional conservation. It was hypothesized that AcerGR28b and AcerGR68alike-X1 belong to the bitter receptor family, while AcerGR64f-like, AcerGR64f-likeX2, and AcerGR43a are sweet receptors. QRT-PCR verification confirmed the reliability of the transcriptome data. This study elucidated the antennal transcriptome response pattern of Apis cerana cerana to different pollens, identified key gustatory receptor genes and related pathways, providing a theoretical basis for researching the regulatory role of the taste system in feeding behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12179-4.Keywords: Apis cerana cerana, Antennal transcriptome, Gustatory receptor gene, Pollen collection, Differential expression analysis

Introduction

The antennae are an important component of the insect chemosensory sensory system, insects rely on their chemosensory systems to perceive environmental cues, enabling essential life activities such as host seeking, feeding, mating, and oviposition. Among these systems, the gustatory system specifically recognizes non-volatile phytochemicals, which critically determine host-plant selection [1], gustatory receptors (GRs) are the essential receptors mediating insect gustatory system. Insect GRs are distributed in antennae, mouthparts, pharynxes, legs, wings and ovipositors. These receptors specifically recognizing chemical substances such as sugars, amino acids, and plant secondary metabolites (such as phenols, terpenoids) [2, 3], thereby converting gustatory signals into neural electrical signals. Insect GRs can be functionally categorized into three classes: sugar receptors, bitter receptors, and carbon dioxide receptors.

In addition to GRs, honeybee antennae harbor a wide repertoire of other chemosensory proteins, including odorant receptors (ORs), odorant binding proteins (OBPs), and chemosensory proteins (CSPs). ORs are mainly responsible for detecting volatile compounds and play a central role in locating floral resources through olfaction, while OBPs and CSPs are soluble proteins that facilitate the transport of hydrophobic odorants and tastants through the sensillar lymph to their corresponding membrane receptors [4–6]. Together, these proteins form an integrated sensory network: ORs provide long-range recognition of floral odors, OBPs and CSPs enhance ligand accessibility, and GRs perform final tastant discrimination. Such synergistic interactions highlight the functional diversity of antennal proteins and ensure that honeybees can rapidly assess both the attractiveness and edibility of floral resources, thereby optimizing foraging efficiency.

Sugar GRs are primarily responsible for detecting carbohydrates such as glucose, fructose, and sucrose; bitter GRs respond to plant-derived secondary metabolites including alkaloids and glycosides; and carbon dioxide GRs are involved in environmental sensing and host location in some insect species. In honeybees, the gustatory receptor repertoire is markedly reduced compared to other insects. For example, while Drosophila melanogaster encodes approximately 68–69 GR genes and Anopheles gambiae up to 75, Apis mellifera possess only about 10 GR genes [7–9]. Despite these findings, functional characterization of GRs in Apis cerana cerana remains limited, and the molecular basis linking floral chemical composition to differential GR expression has not been fully elucidated. Honeybees are central-place foragers and play a crucial role in pollination, directly affecting ecosystem balance and agricultural productivity. Their foraging preferences are influenced not only by ecological factors such as floral resource availability but also by the chemical composition of pollen and nectar.

As an important pollinating insect, the honeybee obtains carbohydrates primarily from nectar or honeydew and essential protein and lipids from pollen, necessitating sophisticated gustatory discrimination to assess floral resource quality [10–12]. Compared to other insects such as Drosophila melanogaster, which has 69 gustatory receptor genes, or Anopheles gambiae, which has up to 75, honeybees possess only 10 gustatory receptor genes [6, 9]. Gustatory perception is central to the precise feeding strategy of honeybees. Their antennae are densely covered with a diverse array of olfactory and gustatory trichoid, including trichoid and basiconic sensilla [13]. The honeybee’s gustatory system is highly differentiated, with different gustatory receptor subtypes exhibiting significantly different affinities for specific chemicals. Some GRs are highly sensitive to monosaccharides, such as glucose, fructose, found in nectar, while others respond to amino acids in pollen or defensive compounds, such as bitter substances. This fine molecular recognition mechanism enables honeybees to distinguish the quality of nectar from different plants, assess the nutritional value of pollen, and identify toxic or contaminated food sources. Consequently, honeybees can optimize their energy intake and avoiding survival risks.

The gustatory perception of honeybees does not operate in isolation but rather works synergistically with other sensory systems, such as olfaction and mechanoreception. ORs and GRs on the antennae jointly encode complex chemical information. Honeybees locate floral resources through olfaction, while gustation confirms the edibility of nectar or pollen [14]. This dual perception mechanism ensures the efficiency and accuracy of foraging behavior. Moreover, gustatory perception is crucial for learning and memory information - honeybees can quickly adjust their foraging preferences by associating taste with reward (such as sweetness) or punishment (such as bitterness) signals [15].

The gustatory receptor gene family of Apis cerana cerana contains multiple functional subtypes. These receptor proteins typically contain seven transmembrane domains. Upon ligand binding, they activate downstream signaling pathways, such as the regulation of transient receptor potential channel TRP family members, including the mushroom bodies of the brain [16]. The differences in the chemical composition of pollen from various plants may trigger specific expression patterns of gustatory receptor. This molecular-level adaptive recognition mechanism enables Apis cerana cerana to efficiently utilize native nectar source plants. This study investigates the gustatory regulation mechanism of Apis cerana cerana when foraging for pollen from pear and rapeseed flowers. Utilizing comparative transcriptomic analysis, we systematically analyze the differential expression and functional differentiation of antennal gustatory receptor genes. Using Illumina high-throughput sequencing technology, we constructed an antennal transcriptome database corresponding to different pollen collection behaviors. We identified multiple GR genes that were significantly differentially expressed and validated the association between their expression patterns and the chemical characteristics of pollen through qRT-PCR. Further functional annotation and evolutionary analysis show that these key receptor genes are highly conserved among Hymenopteran but exhibit adaptive expression patterns in response to specific nectar source plants. This study elucidates the molecular network underlying Apis cerana cerana, gustatory system for pollen recognition and provides a new insights into the coevolutionary dynamics between insects and plants.

Materials and methods

Test insects

The Apis cerana cerana colonies used were non-specially selected strains. These bee colonies were sourced from local natural populations in the ecological pear orchard area of Hong zhiyi Town, Yanhu District, Yuncheng City, Shanxi Province from March 23 to March 29, 2022 (longitude: 110.99827°, latitude: 35.0151°), without undergoing artificial directional selection or genetic improvement procedures. The orchard, covering approximately 54 hm², primarily consisted of pear trees. A 0.2 hm² rapeseed field was situated 1000 m away from the pear trees, with a competition plant ratio of 0.4%. The bee colonies were placed in 6 Langstroth hives respectively, each housing approximately 3000–3500 worker bees, adjusted to ensure consistent population size. Each hive contained 1 queen, a small number of larvae, and minimal pollen and nectar reserves. When approximately 25% of the pear blossoms had opened, the bee colonies were transported to the orchard, and the hives were arranged in a single row midway between the pear trees and the rapeseed field. From 11:00 am to 15:00 pm the next day, 200 worker bees from three groups-carrying pear pollen, rapeseed pollen and no pollen-were captured at the hive entrance. The antennae of these worker bees were dissected on ice, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80 °C.

RNA Extraction, library construction and transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the antennae of Apis cerana cerana using the TRIzol kit (Invitrogen, USA). Three RNA samples were extracted from each experimental group: LC-1, LC-2, LC-3 (collecting pear pollen), YC-1, YC-2, YC-3 (collecting rapeseed pollen), and CC-1, CC-2, CC-3 (non-foraging controls). Antennae from 30 worker bees were pooled for each RNA sample. RNA purity and concentration were first assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was further verified with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) to ensure stringent quality standards.

Poly(A) + mRNA was enriched from total RNA using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation with Fragmentation Buffer. Using the fragmented mRNA as templates, first-strand cDNA was synthesized with random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase, and second-strand cDNA was synthesized subsequently. The purified double-stranded cDNA underwent end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. Fragment size selection was performed using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA). The cDNA library was enriched by PCR amplification, quantified with a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; concentration > 1 ng/µL), and fragment size distribution was assessed using the Qsep400 system (BiOptic, Taiwan). Accurate quantification of the effective library concentration (> 2 nmol/L) was performed by qRT-PCR. After passing all quality control checks, the libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) in PE150 mode at Hangzhou Lianchuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The same RNA samples were returned and used for subsequent qRT-PCR validation.

RNA-seq data processing and bioinformatics analysis

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using Cutadapt v1.9 (default parameters) to remove adapter contamination, polyA/T and polyG tails, low-quality reads (containing > 20% bases with Q ≤ 20), and reads with > 5% ambiguous bases (N). The quality of clean reads was further assessed with FastQC v0.11.9 to calculate Q20, Q30, and GC content distributions, ensuring the reliability of downstream analyses.

High-quality clean reads from nine libraries were aligned to the Apis cerana cerana reference genome (GCF_001442555.1) using HISAT2 v2.2.1 (https://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2/, version: hisat2-2.2.1) with default parameters (allowing up to two mismatches per read, reporting up to 20 alignments). The aligned reads were assembled into transcripts using StringTie v2.1.6 (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/,version:stringtie-2.1.6), and transcript assemblies from all samples were merged with gffcompare v0.9.8 (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/gffcompare.shtml,version:gffcompare-0.9.8). to obtain a comprehensive reference transcriptome. Expression levels were quantified as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) using StringTie and Ballgown.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups were identified with DESeq2 v1.30.1 (for comparisons with biological replicates) and edgeR v3.32.1 (for pairwise comparisons without replicates). Genes with |log2FoldChange| ≥1 and adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

For functional annotation, assembled transcripts were aligned to the NCBI non-redundant (NR) database using BLASTx (BLAST + v2.12.0) with an e-value cutoff of 1e − 5. Gene Ontology (GO) annotation was performed using Blast2GO v5.2, classifying functions into the biological process, molecular function, and cellular component categories. KEGG pathway annotation was carried out with the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS, 2021 release) to map genes to biochemical and signaling pathways. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using hypergeometric tests (p < 0.05), identifying key metabolic and signaling pathways regulated by differentially expressed genes.

Differential expression analysis

The gene expression data under different treatments were statistically analyzed relative to the control group, and gene with significant expression changes was identified. For experiments with biological replicates, the differential expression analysis software DESeq was employed to perform differential expression analysis. In contrast, for experiments lacking biological replicates, EBSeq was utilized to analyze differential expression between sample groups. During the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEG), HTSeq was used to count the number of reads mapped to each gene, which served as the raw expression level of the gene. The results were then normalized and expressed as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilo bases of transcript per Million mapped reads) values, allowing for comparability of gene expression levels across different genes and samples. The criteria for screening differentially expressed genes were set as qValue < 0.05 and fold change > 2.

Screening of gustatory differential genes

Candidate gustatory receptor (GR) genes were identified from transcriptome assemblies and functional annotation results by searching with the keyword “gustatory receptor”. The longest open reading frames (ORFs) of candidate transcripts were predicted using the NCBI ORF Finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/). Deduced amino acid sequences were further analyzed for conserved features. Amino acid sequences were aligned against the NCBI non-redundant (nr) database using BLASTp (E-value ≤ 1e − 5) to confirm homology with known insect GRs. Transmembrane domains were predicted using TMHMM v2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/) with default settings to confirm the presence of seven transmembrane helices, a hallmark of insect GR proteins. Conserved protein domains were annotated against the Pfam database using HMMER v3.3.2. Genes containing the 7tm_7 chemosensory receptor domain (Pfam08395) and seven predicted transmembrane helices were confirmed as candidate GRs.

Construction of phylogenetic tree of gustatory-related genes

To elucidate the evolutionary relationship between the five AcerGRs and GRs from other insects, we selected nucleotide sequences of GRs from several well-characterized Hymenopteran species to construct a phylogenetic tree incorporating nucleotide sequences of these five AcerGRs. Initially, we retrieved the nucleotide sequences of GRs from four known Hymenopteran insects (Apis mellifera, Apis dorsata, Apis laboriosa, Apis florea) from the NCBI database. These sequences were then aligned with the nucleotide sequences of Apis cerana cerana GRs using MEGA 12.0 software. A phylogenetic tree was subsequently constructed using the neighbor joining (NJ) method available in MEGA12.0. The AcerGRs were also named accordingly.

Real-time fluorescence quantitative analysis

Five GRs (AcerGR28b, AcerGR68alike-X1, AcerGR64flike-X2, AcerGR64f, and AcerGR43a) were selected for qRT-PCR verification. One reference genes, β-action gene (XM.017065464) was used for normalization to ensure data stability, and the primer information is shown in Table 1. Primer design was conducted using Primer 5.0 software with the following specific parameters: primer length of 18–22 bp, annealing temperature (Tm) ranging from 55 to 60 °C (with the Tm difference between upstream and downstream primers not exceeding 2 °C), GC content of 40%−60%, and amplicon length of 80–200 bp; All designed primers were further validated for specificity via the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool to ensure no non-specific amplification. To verify the specificity of the designed primers, PCR products were subjected to 1% agarose gel electrophoresis for nucleic acid staining. Electrophoresis was carried out at 120 V for 20 min in 1× TAE buffer, and gels were visualized using a gel documentation system under UV illumination. A single clear band at the expected size indicated primer specificity without non-specific amplification (Fig. 1). RNA samples provided by the transcriptome sequencing company were reverse transcribed into cDNA, which was then used as a template for the reaction with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa). The thermal cycling program was as follows: 95 °C pre-denaturation for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C 5 s, 57 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and a final melting curve analysis (95 °C for 10 s, 52 °C for 5 s). The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT and normalized by log2FoldChange. Each experimental group included 3 biological replicates and 3 technical replicates. Gene expression data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method, and results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 software. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, and t-tests were used to evaluate statistical differences between two groups. Graphs were generated accordingly.

Results and analysis

Transcriptome sequencing evaluation

To determine the gene expression profile of the antennae of Apis cerana cerana collecting pollen from different nectar source plants, the antennal transcriptome of Apis cerana cerana was sequenced using the Illumina sequencing platform. Nine cDNA libraries were constructed, comprising samples from Apis cerana cerana collecting pear pollen (LC1, LC2, LC3), Apis cerana cerana collecting rapeseed pollen (YC1, YC2, YC3), and Apis cerana cerana antennae without pollen collection (CC1, CC2, CC3). In this study, a total of 220,779,398 raw data reads were generated. After quality control, 212,731,904 clean reads were obtained. The GC content of these reads ranged from 35.50% to 36.00%, and the Q20 value of each group of dataset exceeded 99.97%, while the Q30 value was above 96.38% (Table 2), These results demonstrate that the filtered data quality was very high and could be used for subsequent bioinformatics analysis.

The screening of differentially expressed genes

Differential expression gene analysis was conducted on three groups of samples. Clustering analysis revealed that the antennae of Apis cerana cerana collecting different types of pollen in each group were clustered into categories, with clear separation between groups (Fig. 2). This result indicates good uniformity and correlation within each group, and suggests that subsequent analysis is feasible. Transcriptome sequencing was performed on the three groups of samples, and DESeq was used for differential gene expression analysis. The results showed that in the YC vs. CC group, 241 differentially expressed genes were identified, comprising 86 up-regulated and 155 down-regulated genes, in the LC vs. CC group, 135 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 56 up-regulated and 79 down-regulated genes, and in the LC vs. YC group, 102 differentially expressed genes were identified, consisting of 81 up-regulated and 21 down-regulated genes (Fig. 3).

Functional classification of differentially expressed genes between antennal transcriptomes of apis cerana cerana collecting pear pollen and rapeseed pollen

The GO classification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the antennal transcriptomes of Apis cerana cerana collecting pear pollen and rapeseed pollen comprises 50 functional categories (as shown in Fig. 4). These categories are organized into three major classes: biological process, molecular function, and cellular component. The biological process classes describes the biological activities in which gene-encoded products are involved. The molecular function class characterizes the biochemical activities of gene products. The cellular component class specifies the subcellular locations where these gene products are found. Within these classes, the primary functions highlighted include defense response to Gram-positive bacterium, integral component of the plasma membrane, and molecular function. These GO terms—particularly integral component of plasma membrane, signal transduction, and binding—are congruent with the canonical properties of insect gustatory receptors as seven-transmembrane chemosensors. They point to membrane-embedded ligand recognition and downstream transduction events that likely underlie antennal processing of floral chemical cues.

GO functional annotation of differentially expressed genes between the antennae transcriptomes of Apis cerana cerana collecting pear pollen and rapeseed pollen

The results of the KEGG database alignment revealed that 5 genes were involved in tyrosine metabolism pathway, 2 genes were involved in neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, 2 genes were involved in lysosome formation, and 2 genes were involved in phosphatidylinositol signaling pathway (as shown in Fig. 5). These results imply that tyrosine metabolism and signal transduction pathways may play crucial roles in gustatory perception of Apis cerana cerana, linking pollen chemical cues to antennal sensory processing. Enrichment of tyrosine metabolism, neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, lysosome, and phosphatidylinositol signaling suggests coordinated modulation of biogenic-amine pathways and receptor/second-messenger systems during pollen foraging, linking peripheral tastant detection to antennal neuromodulation and cellular turnover.

Sequence analysis of candidate gustatory genes in Apis cerana cerana

Analysis of the Apis cerana cerana antennae transcriptome identified five genes annotated as gustatory receptors. These five candidate gustatory genes were subjected to BLAST alignment, open reading frame (ORF) identification, and transmembrane domain prediction. The results demonstrated that all five genes possess complete ORFs. Five gustatory receptor (GR) genes were identified in Apis cerana cerana, each encoding proteins with the typical seven-transmembrane architecture characteristic of insect gustatory receptors. The longest ORF of LOC108004342 spans 1248 bp, encoding a 415 aa protein (48.5 kDa) that shows 100% identity to GR28b (Accession: XM_028664013.2), with a 7tm_7 domain (Pfam08395) located between aa 42–397. LOC108000611 spans 1233 bp, encoding a 410 aa protein (47.9 kDa) that is 100% identical to GR68a-like-X1 (Accession: XM_017061050.3), containing a 7tm_7 domain (Pfam08395, cl19976) between aa 134–397. LOC108003288 comprises 1413 bp, encoding a ~ 470 aa protein with 99.79% similarity to GR64f-like (Accession: XM_017065455.3), in which the 7tm_7 domain (Pfam08395) spans aa 40–400; hydrophobic transmembrane helices, extracellular loops for sugar recognition, and intracellular loops for signal transduction were observed. Similarly, LOC107997799 spans 1458 bp, encoding a ~ 480 aa protein with 99.70% similarity to GR64f-like transcript variant X1 (Accession: XM_017056640.3), also containing a 7tm_7 domain (Pfam08395) with hydrophobic transmembrane helices, extracellular loops likely involved in sugar binding, and intracellular loops in signal transduction. Finally, LOC108004158 is 1428 bp in length, encoding a ~ 475 aa protein with 100% identity to GR43a transcript variant X1 (Accession: XM_028669909.2), with a 7tm_7 domain (Pfam08395) spanning aa 40–420; hydrophobic transmembrane segments support membrane localization, while extracellular loops likely mediate fructose recognition and intracellular loops facilitate signal transduction. Based on the above information and the presence of seven transmembrane domains, a characteristic of the insect gustatory receptor gene family, in LOC108004342, LOC108000611, and LOC108004158, these five genes were further confirmed to be gustatory receptor genes. This identification highlights the conservation of canonical GR structural features while suggesting their potential involvement in floral chemical discrimination. The convergence of complete ORFs, seven-transmembrane signatures, and high homology to hymenopteran GRs supports their functional candidacy in tastant recognition; extracellular loops implicated in ligand binding and intracellular loops implicated in signaling provide a structural rationale for the observed expression shifts across floral sources.

Construction of the phylogenetic tree for gustatory receptor acerlgrs

To explore the evolutionary relationships between the five gustatory receptor genes (AcerGRs) of Apis cerana cerana and gustatory receptor genes (GRs) of other insects, we constructed a phylogenetic tree based on nucleotide sequences. Specifically, GR nucleotide sequences from several well-studied Hymenopteran insects were selected and analyzed together with the nucleotide sequences of the five AcerGRs. The results of the phylogenetic tree are shown in Fig. 6. AcerGR28b (XM_028640132.2) clustered closely with Apis mellifera AmelGR28b (XM 016918080.2), forming a distinct subclade. AcerGR68alike-X1 (XM_017061050.3) belonged to the same branch as AmelGR32a-like (XM_016914041.2). AcerGR64f-like (XM_017056455.3) clustered with Apis mellifera AmelGR64f-like (XM_397125.7) and Apis dorsata AdorGR64f-like (XM_012490378.2). The nucleotide sequences of the obtained AcerGR64f-like, AcerGR64f-likeX2, and AcerGR43a-X1 were determined to be sweet receptor genes through BLAST analysis.

Construction of the phylogenetic tree for candidate gustatory receptors (AcerGRs) of Apis cerana cerana and gustatory receptors (GRs) of other Hymenopteran insects

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the amino acid sequences of 50 gustatory receptor nucleotide sequences from Drosophila melanogaster and the five gustatory receptor nucleotide sequences identified in this study from Apis cerana cerana, as shown in Fig. 7. AcerGR64f-likeX2 was found to be homologous to DmelGR47a. AcerGR28b was homologous to DmelGR28b and clustered within the same branch as AcerGR68a-likeX2. AcerGR64f-like was homologous to DmelGR93d and clustered within the same branch as AcerGR43a-X1. Given that DmelGR28b in Drosophila, is involved in the avoidance response to saponins (bitter substances), it can be inferred that AcerGR28b and AcerGR68a-likeX1 are more likely to be bitter receptor genes. Such clustering provides molecular evidence that honeybee GRs have undergone functional divergence, enabling adaptation to diverse floral secondary metabolites.

qRT-PCR validation results of candidate gustatory genes

To further verify the accuracy of the transcriptome data, qRT-PCR was performed on selected gustatory genes identified in the transcriptome. The results were consistent with the trend of FPKM values, indicating that the transcriptome sequencing data are accurate and highly reliable (Fig. 8). This validation strengthens the reliability of transcriptomic insights and underscores the potential regulatory role of GRs in pollen foraging behavior. The concordance between qRT-PCR and RNA-seq trends reinforces the robustness of differential GR expression and provides a quantitative foundation for interpreting tastant-specific sensory tuning during foraging.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically analyzed the antennal transcriptome of Apis cerana cerana foragers collecting pear and rapeseed pollen and identified five GR genes with conserved seven-transmembrane domains, the identification of additional GR genes may have been limited by their extremely low expression levels in gustatory organs [17]. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that differentially expressed genes were mainly involved in categories such as integral component of plasma membrane, binding activity, and signal transduction, as well as pathways including tyrosine metabolism, neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, lysosome, and phosphatidylinositol signaling (Fig. 4). These terms and pathways are highly consistent with the canonical role of insect GRs as transmembrane chemosensors that convert chemical stimuli into intracellular signaling. In particular, enrichment of tyrosine metabolism implies involvement of biogenic amines derived from aromatic amino acids, such as dopamine and tyramine, in regulating antennal sensitivity [18, 19]. This provides a mechanistic link between peripheral gustatory detection and neural modulation.

Moreover, the GO term “integral component of plasma membrane” corresponds well with the molecular characteristics of the five identified GRs, all of which encode seven-transmembrane proteins embedded in the antennal membrane. Genes such as AcerGR28b and AcerGR68a-like-X1, which were significantly upregulated during rapeseed pollen foraging, exemplify this enrichment by highlighting the role of membrane-localized bitter receptors in detecting glucosinolates and other secondary metabolites. Similarly, the GO category “binding activity” reflects the ligand-recognition properties of sweet receptors like AcerGR64f-like and AcerGR43a, which showed higher expression in pear pollen foragers and are likely tuned to fructose and glucose, the main sugars present in pear nectar. Finally, the enrichment of “signal transduction” resonates with the downstream functional role of these GRs: upon ligand binding, they initiate intracellular signaling cascades that regulate neuronal excitability. The expression patterns of AcerGR64f-like-X2 and AcerGR64f-like—both elevated in foragers—are consistent with enhanced signal transduction capacity when bees assess nutritional quality of pollen and nectar. Together, these associations suggest that the enriched GO categories are not only broad descriptors but also capture the specific molecular activities of the differentially expressed GRs in response to contrasting floral chemical environments.

Sixty-eight GRs have been identified in Drosophila, primarily mediating bitter, sweet, and carbon dioxide perception. Among these, DmelGR64 and DmelGR5a encode sweet receptor that detect glucose, sucrose, maltose, maltitol, stachyose, and raffinose [7, 20]. DmelGR32a, DmelGR33a, and DmelGR66a encode bitter receptors responsive to quinine, lobeline, and caffeine. Notably, the GR28b gene cluster mediates saponin avoidance independently of core bitter receptors, such as GR32a, GR33a, and GR66a [21]. Anopheles gambiae possesses up to 75 GR genes, including receptors specialized for detecting host-associated cues such as CO₂ and lactic acid, essential for blood-feeding behavior [22]. In stark contrast, Apis cerana cerana maintain a much smaller GR repertoire of only about 10 genes [23]. Although Apis cerana cerana have a comparatively small GR gene repertoire, Apis mellifera studies demonstrate strong functional specialization. For example, AmGr1-3 display distinct sugar sensitivity profiles, and AmGr10 functions as an amino acid receptor responding to glutamate and aspartate, but not bitter compounds [24, 25]. These findings indicate that reduced gene number does not preclude fine chemical discrimination. Phylogenetic analysis of GR genes in Apis cerana cerana and Drosophila melanogaster (Fig. 6) revealed that DmelGR47a and AcerGR64f-like-X1 are homologs. However, DmelGR47a mediates strychnine detection in Drosophila, whereas AcerGR64f-like-X1 functions as a sweet receptor in honeybees. This functional divergence likely reflects ecological adaptation: nectar-feeding honeybees depend on sweet receptors for foraging, while Drosophila must detect diverse chemicals, including bitter toxins, to avoid toxic ingestion [26]. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that AcerGR28b and AcerGR68a-likeX1 were significantly upregulated in the rapeseed flowers (YC) group and clustered with the Drosophila bitter receptor DmelGR28b. Given that rapeseed pollen contains high concentrations of glucosinolates, which are plant secondary metabolites with bitter or pungent properties, these receptors likely function as bitter taste receptors [27, 28]. Among the five candidate gustatory gustatory genes analyzed by qRT-PCR, AcerGR64f-like-X2 showed significantly higher expression in Apis cerana cerana workers collecting both pear and rapeseed pollen compared to the non- flower collection control group (Fig. 7C). Quantitative analysis revealed that AcerGR64f expression was generally higher in foragers collecting pear pollen compared to those collecting rapeseed pollen (Fig. 7D). Specifically, Apis cerana cerana proboscises exhibited elevated AcerGR64f levels during pear pollen collection relative to non-foraging controls, whereas Apis cerana cerana showed reduced expression when foraging on rapeseed pollen (Fig. 7D). Notably, gustatory receptor genes exhibited higher expression levels during pear pollen collection, despite established preferences of bees for rapeseed pollen. Pear nectar is characterized by a relatively low sugar concentration (approximately 9.6–10.8%), consisting primarily of glucose and fructose with only limited amounts of sucrose [29]. Such a weak sweet cue is unlikely to strongly stimulate the expression of honeybee sweet receptor genes. In contrast, rapeseed pollen (Brassica napus) contains a diverse array of glucosinolates (GSLs), well-known bitter and defensive secondary metabolites [30]. Although most quantitative studies focus on seeds and seed meal—where GSL levels may reach 60–100 µmol/g dry weight in traditional cultivars—multiple GSL compounds have also been confirmed in rapeseed pollen [31]. In addition, S-methyl-L-cysteine-sulfoxide (SMCSO) has been detected in both rapeseed pollen and nectar, imparting bitter and pungent sensory properties [32]. Collectively, these defensive compounds are capable of activating gustatory pathways and likely contribute to the upregulation of honeybee bitter receptor genes, such as GR28b and GR68a-like, during rapeseed foraging. We hypothesize that this upregulation may reflect aversive or stimulatory compounds in pear pollen that activate gustatory sensory pathways, potentially serving as a defensive mechanism against non-preferred floral resources.

Despite significant advances in characterizing insect gustatory receptor families, functional studies in non-drosophilid species remain limited. Apis cerana cerana exhibits highly diversified gustatory receptor genes in the genome, penabling sophisticated gustatory perception that facilitates precise discrimination of plant nectar chemistry across flowering seasons [33].

Through antennal transcriptome sequencing, this study systematically analyzed the gene expression profiles of Apis cerana cerana foragers during pear and rapeseed pollen collection, identifying five gustatory receptor genes. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed AcerGR64f-like, AcerGR64f-likeX2, and AcerGR43a as canonical sweet receptors, while providing the first evidence that AmelGR28b and AcerGR68a-likeX1 may represent novel bitter receptors. These finding implicate functional divergence of specific GRs in mediating nectar-foraging preferences, offering critical molecular targets for dissecting the honeybee gustatory regulatory network and guiding future genetic breeding programs.
