Diversity representation in the development of respiratory society guidance documents
Authors: Josephine Younan, Tong Hieu, Emma Marshall, Latisha E. Witjaksono, Arwel W. Jones, Seema Green, Yuxin Jiao, William Soon, Kirushallini Saravanan, Pulasthi V. Wettesinghe, Christopher J. Ryerson, Kevin C. Wilson, Yet Hong Khor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSocietal guidance documents, typically developed by diverse and multidisciplinary committees, are pivotal in supporting patient care and advancing research to improve respiratory health. This study evaluated panel member composition of guidance documents developed by two international respiratory societies, the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and the European Respiratory Society (ERS).MethodsGuidance documents from society inception until December 2024 were identified using online repositories and journal websites for ATS and ERS. For each document, characteristics of panel members were extracted with verification by a second reviewer. Trend of panel member composition was assessed, pooling at 5-year intervals from 2005.ResultsOf the 318 included documents, there were 3838 contributing unique panel members (43% respiratory physicians; 64% male; 59% from North America). The involvement of different medical specialists and nonmedical healthcare professionals was unchanged over time, with increased patient representation from none to 57%. The percentages of panel members with conflicts of interest consistently met the recommended threshold of <50%. There was an increase in the percentage of female representation as panel members from 27% between 2005 and 2009 to 41% between 2020 and 2024. Panel members from different continents beyond North America and Europe continued to increase, with 24% of documents having at least one member from low- or middle-income countries between 2020 and 2024.ConclusionThere is increasing diversity in panel member composition in the development of international guidance documents in respiratory medicine. Ongoing efforts to maintain growing representation of panel members in international guidance documents are needed to incorporate broader perspectives and support applicability of clinical and research recommendations.

Introduction

Societal guidance documents, including clinical practice guidelines and statements, from professional organisations, are crucial in informing clinical practice for high-quality patient care and identifying areas of research need. The involvement of multidisciplinary panels of experts and other relevant stakeholders, including patients, is fundamental in the development of high-quality societal guidance documents [1–3]. The World Health Organization Handbook recommends balance in gender and geographical representations for guideline development [4]. Of note, societal guidance documents developed by leading international professional organisations are often adopted for local and regional application with varying healthcare resources [5]. Multidisciplinary involvement of experts and stakeholders from diverse backgrounds in the development of societal guidance documents may enhance their relevance and implementation globally.

The number of societal guidance documents in the field of respiratory medicine has increased over time for various topics [6]. Collectively, the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and European Respiratory Society (ERS) have contributed to the majority of societal guidance documents for advancing respiratory health [5], with international memberships spanning the globe [6–8]. Nevertheless, the types of panel members who participated in developing the societal guidance documents remains uncharacterised. This study aimed to evaluate the composition of panel members and stakeholder involvement in societal guidance documents published by ATS and ERS and their trends over time. This knowledge is essential for informing future development of societal guidance documents to ensure diverse perspectives and relevance for wide implementation.

Methods

Identification of ATS and ERS societal documents

Published societal documents were identified from society inception up to December 2024 via the online repositories of the websites for ATS and ERS, as well as the official society journals, including the American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, American Journal of Respiratory Cell and Molecular Biology, Annals of the American Thoracic Society, and the European Respiratory Journal. The only exclusion criterion was societal documents related to COVID-19, as they were developed using distinct procedures during the pandemic. Duplicates of the same documents identified from different sources were excluded.

Data extraction

Data were extracted with independent verification by a second reviewer (J. Younan, T. Hieu, E. Marshall, L.E. Witjaksono, S. Green, Y. Jiao, W. Soon, K. Saravanan or P.V. Wettesinghe). Any differences were resolved by a third reviewer (Y.H. Khor).

Extracted data included document characteristics (type, participating societies, publication year, topic covered and target audience), as well as panel member information (profession, declared conflict of interest (COI), gender and geographical location). All data were collected from published societal documents. Declared COI was categorised as presence (for any declared relationships) or absence (for none declared). The only exception was panel member gender, which was determined by their gendered title in the documents (e.g. Ms, Mr) or publicly accessible professional biographies from institutional and/or professional association websites.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using descriptive statistics and presented as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and median (interquartile range (IQR)) for continuous variables. Separate analyses were performed for the overall characteristics of unique panel members and the panel composition representation for included documents.

Deduplication was performed to identify unique author statistics for overall assessment of profession, declared COI, gender and geographical location. Geographical representation of panel members was analysed by continent and country, with the latter being classified into high-income countries (HICs) and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) using the World Bank income classification [9]. Results

Document characteristics

409 ATS and ERS guidance documents were identified (figure 1). After deduplication and exclusion of COVID-19-related documents, 318 were included. Most included documents were guidelines (n=84, 26%), followed by consensus statements/position papers (n=77, 24%) and workshop reports (n=75, 24%) (table 1). Most documents were published since 2015 (n=238, 75%), with only a small proportion from the period between 2005 and 2009 (n=20, 6%). There were 149 (47%) ATS-only documents and 64 (20%) ERS-only documents, with 105 (33%) being joint documents by ATS and/or ERS with or without the involvement of other societies (supplementary tables S1 and S2). The most common non-ATS/ERS society for joint documents was the American College of Chest Physicians (supplementary table S3).

Characteristics of unique panel members

There were 3838 unique panel members who contributed to the included documents. While contributions from multiple professions existed, a significant proportion of panel members were respiratory physicians (n=1639, 43%) and other medical specialists (n=1063, 28%). Contributions from scientists (n=466, 12%), allied health professionals (n=255, 7%), nurses (n=127, 3%), and other professions and stakeholders (collectively n=288, 8%) were relatively low. Most panel members (n=2440, 64%) disclosed the absence of COI and were male (n=2438, 64%). Panel members from different continents were represented, with most from North America (n=2260, 59%). This was followed by Europe (n=1333, 35%), Oceania (n=117, 3%), Asia (n=62, 2%), South America (n=26, 1%), Africa (n=25, 1%) and transcontinental countries (n=15, 0.4%). The LMIC representation was low at 3% (n=117) of unique panel members.

Panel profession representation

Overall, the vast majority of included documents had involvement of at least one respiratory physician (n=314, 99%) and a medical specialist from other disciplines (n=302, 95%) (figure 3 and supplementary table S6). Nurses were involved as panel members in 96 (30%) included documents. Other health professionals were represented in 188 (59%) included documents (supplementary table S7). Other experts, such as educators, researchers, scientists and public health professionals or agencies, were also involved. The involvement of either patient representatives (n=100, 31%) or policy-makers (n=33, 10%) was low (supplementary table S7). While the contributions of respiratory physicians and other medical specialists were consistently high across the different time periods, there was an increase in patient representation over time, from none between 2005 and 2009 to 67 (57%) documents between 2020 and 2024, which was also observed for nonmedical healthcare professionals (figure 4). The involvement of policy-makers remained largely unchanged.

COI declaration and gender representation

The median (IQR) percentage of panel members with COI across all included documents was 41% (22–59%). The percentage of panel members with the presence of COI among documents remained relatively stable over time at <50%, with the largest percentage in the most recent years between 2020 and 2024 at 48% (supplementary table S8 and supplementary figure S1). Overall, the median (IQR) percentage of female panel members for all included documents was 31% (20–45%), along with 35% (n=110) as the first author and 32% (n=101) as the last author. There was an increase in female representation for the panel members over time, reaching a mean of 41% published between 2020 and 2024 (supplementary table S8 and supplementary figure S1). Notably, the percentage of female first authors increased from 30% (n=6) between 2005 and 2009 to 47% (n=55) between 2020 and 2024 (supplementary figure S2). The female last author representation also increased over time, from 20% (n=4) between 2005 and 2009 to 38% (n=44) between 2020 and 2024.

Geographical representation

North America had the highest representation for 283 (89%) documents, followed by 245 (77%) documents for Europe, 105 (33%) documents for Oceania, 81 (25%) documents for Asia, 52 (16%) documents for South America and 22 (7%) documents for Africa (supplementary table S9). However, there were varying representations within continents (supplementary table S9). For North America, the United States of America and Canada were represented in 274 (86%) and 150 (47%) documents, respectively, while Mexico was only represented in 12 (4%). The greatest representation for Europe, Oceania, South America, Asia and Africa was the United Kingdom (n=164, 52%), Australia (n=100, 31%), Brazil (n=34, 11%), Japan (n=20, 6%) and South Africa (n=13, 4%), respectively. Involvement of at least one panel member from a LMIC among the included documents was generally low (n=68, 21%) (supplementary table S9).

The panel member representations from Africa, Asia and Oceania continued to increase over time (figure 5 and supplementary table S10). There was a reduction in the panel member representation from North America, although it remained >80% of documents published between 2020 and 2024. The panel member representation from Europe remained stable since its increment during the period between 2010 and 2014, while South America remained largely unchanged across different time periods. Nevertheless, there was a rise in the number of countries with panel member representation from 21% between 2005 and 2009 to 62% between 2020 and 2024. Representation from LMICs also increased from 4 (20%) documents between 2005 and 2009 to 28 (24%) documents between 2020 and 2024 (supplementary figure S3).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of panel member composition for guidance documents developed by leading international respiratory societies, which play a pivotal role in informing patient care and research practice globally. Overall, the majority of panel members contributing to the included documents were respiratory physicians, who were predominantly male and from the United States of America. Most had no declared COI. A variety of medical specialists and nonmedical healthcare professionals from different disciplines were consistently involved as panel members, with a growth in the engagement of patient representatives over time. It is noteworthy that there has been increasing diversity with improved female and geographical representation in the panel member composition in recent years.

Unequal representation in panel composition for societal guidance documents is not unique to respiratory medicine, as has also been reported in other fields, such as endocrinology [10], gastroenterology [11] and rheumatology [12]. Of note, our work expanded beyond the evaluation of gender disparity, which has been the primary focus of previous work, to cover profession, declared COI, and geographical representation, as well as their trends over the past two decades. This approach provides an in-depth understanding of panel member composition for key societal guidance documents in respiratory medicine. Despite the observed disparities in different aspects of panel member composition across included documents, there was a notable increase of diversity in panel composition over time. This may be due to a combination of factors, including the implementation of inclusion and equity policies [13, 14], increased awareness of the importance of diversity in panel members, and growing international and diverse societal memberships [13–19]. In addition, both ATS and ERS provide specific guidance for diverse composition in the formation of panel member, to encompass all relevant disciplines and career stages, backgrounds and stakeholders [15, 16]. Ongoing efforts are required to ensure sustained improvement in the representation of diverse panel members in the development of societal guidance documents in respiratory medicine.

Given that the focus of included societal guidance documents was respiratory medicine, the target audience and profession representation of panel members were primarily respiratory physicians. A large majority of included documents had contributions from other relevant medical specialists involved in patient management and research related to the health issue of interest. Among nonmedical healthcare professionals, nurses, physiologists and physiotherapists were most represented. Nurses play a key role in the care of acute and chronic respiratory diseases across various healthcare settings [20–22]. Pulmonary rehabilitation and exercise training are well-established effective nonpharmacological interventions for patients with chronic respiratory disease [1]. Ongoing cross-disciplinary collaboration approach in the development of societal guidance documents for respiratory medicine is essential for informing the delivery of optimal comprehensive care in complex respiratory diseases.

Overall, panel member representation from HICs was substantially greater than that from LMICs. This may reflect the varying levels of expertise, experience and resources across different regions, as well as the leading roles of ATS and ERS, for contributing to the development of societal guidance documents. Research outputs in the field of respiratory medicine predominantly originate from HICs, in particular the United States of America and United Kingdom [23]. Both acute and chronic respiratory conditions pose significant health burden globally, with some disproportionately affecting LMICs with rising trends for noncommunicable diseases [24, 25]. However, the availability of local and national guidance documents for respiratory diseases is low in LMICs, as shown in COPD and interstitial lung disease [5, 26]. There is also considerable disparity in the distribution and access of healthcare resources within countries, even in HICs [27]. Thus, diverse representation of panel members from different regions and healthcare settings in societal guidance documents is needed to consider implementation feasibility and address modifiable gaps. Potential measures in future development of societal guidance documents may include collaborations between international and local or regional societies, as well as provision of support for members from underrepresented regions.

Across the different aspects of panel member composition that we evaluated, the median percentages of members with COI remained largely unchanged over time, meeting the recommended threshold of <50% having COIs [1]. Of note, the reporting and evaluation of COI primarily focus on relationships with commercial entities, particularly with pharmaceutical companies. Given the nature of physician–industry relationships and participation of industry in research activities, the involvement of panel members with COI is not unique to societal guidance documents for respiratory medicine, which is consistently reported across various fields, with the percentages ranging between 42% and 86% [28–32]. Both ATS and ERS have stringent procedures for disclosure and management of COI [15, 33], which is a crucial step to ensure transparency and integrity of societal guidance documents. Other types of COIs, such as intellectual COI [34], are less commonly considered and may similarly influence subjective expert opinions, although this may be mitigated by ensuring diversity in the panel member composition.

As the ultimate goal of societal guidance documents is to improve patient health outcomes, incorporating lived experience perspectives into the development of these documents is necessary for alignment with the needs of people most impacted. While literature and systematic reviews synthesise available evidence for specific areas of interest as the foundation for societal guidance documents, patients’ views often take into account a wider scope of overall wellbeing and life [35–37]. Societal guidance documents with patient-informed preferences facilitate patient–clinician communication and shared decision-making, which may lead to improved acceptance and implementation [37–39]. It is encouraging that there was increasing patient involvement as panel members in included documents, although their roles and participation were not evaluated. Previous studies have demonstrated different approaches to patient involvement in the development of guidance documents, with identified barriers including appropriate patient training, empowerment, and support from other panel members [35, 40]. Further work is needed to establish processes and guidance for meaningful patient representation in developing preference-informed societal guidance documents for respiratory medicine.

There are a few limitations to our study. First, only two respiratory societies, ATS and ERS, were included in our analysis. However, both societies are world-leading professional organisations for lung health, with their guidance documents having major influence globally [6]. Furthermore, a comprehensive search was performed, including >300 societal guidance documents covering a wide range of topics. Second, the gender of panel members was inferred based on the pronouns used and information available from institutional and/or professional association websites, which could not be verified and may be subject to error. Nevertheless, this similar approach was consistently applied to all included documents, which would not affect the analysis for trend over time. Third, the evaluation of career stage representation for panel members could not be performed, as this could not be determined reliably at the time of document development. The ethnicity of panel members was also not evaluated, given the lack of available data for accurate assessments. These are important areas for consideration of transparent reporting in future clinical guidance documents. Fourth, our assessment of COIs was based on voluntary reporting accompanying the included societal guidance documents, without independent verification, and details of COIs were not assessed.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that diversity is increasing in the composition of panel members for international guidance documents from leading respiratory societies, although inequalities persist. Involving panel members from low-resource settings in developing societal guidance documents should be considered, which may facilitate effective implementation. Incorporation of patient values and preferences in societal guidance documents through meaningful engagement with adequate support is an ongoing endeavour. Strengthening societal guidance documents through broadening diversity in the development process may improve the relevance and facilitate implementation across different healthcare settings and backgrounds.
