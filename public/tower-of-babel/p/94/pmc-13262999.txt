Physical Fitness and Physical Activity Levels One Year after Total Hip Arthroplasty in Older Hip Osteoarthritis Patients: A Longitudinal Observational Study
Authors: Manne Godhe, Anders Stålman, Johnny Nilsson, Eva Andersson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTotal hip arthroplasty (THA) effectively alleviates pain and improves quality of life in patients with severe hip osteoarthritis (OA). However, there are limited comprehensive longitudinal data on physical function and objectively measured physical activity (PA) patterns after THA. The purpose of this study was to establish the test-retest reliability of physical fitness measures in patients with hip OA and to evaluate changes in physical fitness and activity over time following THA.MethodsSeventy-eight OA patients (mean age 73.7 ± 4.2 years, 81% women) scheduled for THA underwent comprehensive physical fitness testing and accelerometry-based PA monitoring. Assessments were conducted at two preoperative time points and at 4-month (n = 57) and 1-year postoperative follow-up (n = 48). The test battery included measures of muscle strength, motor fitness, and cardiorespiratory fitness. PA was objectively measured using hip-worn triaxial accelerometers. Self-reported Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS) were collected pre- and postoperatively.ResultsGood to excellent test-retest reliability was demonstrated across 23 of 24 fitness parameters. At 1-year post-op, significant improvements were observed in 23 of 45 measured parameters, including functional mobility (timed up-and-go: −29%), walking distance (Six-Minute Walk Test: +16%), trunk endurance-strength (+71 to 82%), shoulder press (+32%), various leg strength tests (+13 to 42%), 4-m max walking speed (+42%), moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (+63%) and daily step count from 6,004 to 7,558 steps/day (+26%). Ipsilateral-to-contralateral step height asymmetry decreased from 27% preoperatively to 8% at 1-year follow-up. VO2peak demonstrated a modest but significant improvement from 22.7 to 23.6 mL/kg/min (+4%) 1 year postoperatively.ConclusionThis study revealed low fitness and activity levels in patients with severe hip OA before surgery, with marked improvements in physical fitness and activity measurements 1 year after surgery. The high reliability of the comprehensive assessment battery supports its usefulness for both research and clinical practice. These findings contribute to the existing evidence on simple, field-based fitness tests for patients with hip OA, while also offering methodological insights and recovery trajectories.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a common surgical procedure that provides immediate pain relief and significantly improves the quality of life for patients with severe hip conditions, such as osteoarthritis (OA) [1, 2]. However, ensuring the long-term success of THA requires more than surgical intervention alone; long-term outcomes depend substantially on the restoration of physical function and habitual physical activity (PA) through effective rehabilitation [3].

While patients typically report satisfaction and improved quality of life in the first year following THA, research indicates that functional mobility deficits can persist post-surgery, including impaired mobility and reduced PA levels [4]. These deficits can increase the risk of falls and reduce mobility, which impact individual patient health and also impose a significant burden on healthcare systems [5]. Moreover, few studies have combined comprehensive functional fitness assessments with accelerometry-based activity monitoring across the full recovery trajectory, leaving the broader picture of physical recovery following THA incompletely characterized.

Historically, the success of THA has mainly been assessed through surgical results, prosthesis longevity, and patient self-reports [6, 7]. However, self-reported measures of function and PA correlate poorly with objective assessments and tend to overestimate true functional capacity [3, 8, 9]. This discrepancy highlights the need for a more comprehensive evaluative strategy in THA follow-up testing. Comprehensive objective evaluation, incorporating both physical fitness testing and accelerometry-based activity monitoring, offers a more accurate picture of recovery and can identify modifiable targets for postoperative rehabilitation [3]. In addition, a comprehensive test battery spanning multiple physical domains, including muscular strength, cardiorespiratory fitness, and motor function, may better capture the multifaceted nature of functional decline and recovery in this population. Despite growing interest in functional outcomes, population-specific reliability data for physical fitness tests in hip OA patients remain limited; most available evidence comes from knee OA or general older adult populations [10–12], and it cannot be assumed that reliability established in those groups transfers directly to patients with hip OA, where pain and compensatory movement patterns may affect test performance in distinct ways.

This study therefore had two primary objectives: (1) to establish the test-retest reliability of a comprehensive battery of physical fitness measures, including novel fitness tests, in older adults with hip OA scheduled for THA, and (2) to assess longitudinal changes in physical fitness and objectively measured PA from preoperative to 1-year postoperative assessment.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This investigation was a prospective longitudinal study. Older adults (≥65 years) with hip OA undergoing THA were recruited between March 2022 and August 2024 (see Table 1 for anthropometric data). The inclusion criteria were patients with unilateral OA and no other severe diseases (ASA score interval 1–2), a BMI below 30, and aged 65–84 years. Exclusion criteria included other severe joint disorders in the lower extremities that affected motor abilities.

Several steps were taken to minimize the influence of factors known to affect physical performance. The ASA score 1–2 inclusion criterion excluded patients with significant metabolic conditions (including insulin-dependent type II diabetes), cardiovascular, pulmonary, or neuromuscular disorders. Patients with BMI below 18.5 kg/m2 were not included given the potential for malnutrition to compromise performance. Cognitive impairment was not formally screened, but patients with documented dementia were not referred to the study by the orthopedic surgeon. Participants were instructed to avoid vigorous exercise and alcohol the day before and caffeine 2 h prior to each assessment, and those reporting acute illness or substantially elevated pain on the day of testing were rescheduled. Use of prescribed pain medication was recorded but not controlled for, as withholding analgesics was considered ethically inappropriate.

This study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (Approval No ID: 2021-00948). Patients were screened by an orthopedic surgeon at Artro Clinic in Stockholm and informed about the study as soon as they received a decision for THA. Eligible participants were contacted, provided with detailed study information, and scheduled for initial data collection based on individual availability and clinical status. No additional preoperative instructions were given beyond standard care for hip OA.

Test Procedures

Before surgery, participants completed two identical preoperative fitness assessments at the Swedish School of Sport and Health Sciences (GIH), separated by approximately 2 weeks (pre-op 1 and pre-op 2). Assessments were conducted at the same time of day (±2 h) to control for circadian variation. Participants maintained usual activity levels and medications between sessions. At pre-op 1, participants completed a health status questionnaire and were fitted with accelerometers for PA monitoring.

The test-retest reliability analysis was based on the two preoperative assessments (pre-op 1 and pre-op 2), conducted approximately 2 weeks apart. Of the 78 participants who completed pre-op 1, 68 returned for pre-op 2. All physical fitness tests included in the longitudinal analysis were administered at both preoperative time points, with the exception of the submaximal and maximal cycling test given its physiological demands and accelerometry measures as the logistics of a 7-day wear period precluded repetition within the 2-week intersession interval. The 2-week interval was selected to minimize both learning effects and true physiological change. The test battery was then repeated at 4-month and 1-year post-THA follow-ups (Fig. 1).

Participant recruitment flowchart.

Anthropometric data were collected at each visit to GIH (height, weight, BMI, and body fat percentage (Tanita BC-418MA, Tanita Corporation, Arlington Heights, IL, USA). This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational research [13].

Physical Fitness Tests

The test battery comprised assessments of muscular endurance, muscular strength, cardiorespiratory fitness, and motor fitness (see Fig. 2 and descriptions below). The muscular endurance tests included isometric trunk flexion, performed while seated at a 45° incline of the upper body relative to the level floor. Participants maintained a static trunk position with arms crossed over the chest and knees bent at 90°, using a wooden frame as a reference for the correct angle and receiving ankle support from the test leader [14]. The isometric trunk extension (Sorensen’s test) required participants to maintain a prone horizontal position with crossed arms, with their upper body positioned hanging over the edge of a bench at the iliac crest, supported by their ankles [15]. Both tests were timed from correct positioning until exhaustion. The sit-to-stand test involved completing 50 repetitions as quickly as possible on a 46-cm chair, with participants instructed to briefly touch or fully sit between rises. Time and repetitions completed in the first 30 s were recorded [16], along with the total number of chair touches [14]. When a brief touch of the chair was not possible, a full sit-down was performed between each ascent. The total number of sit-to-stands per second was recorded. The shoulder press test involved participants alternately pressing held-in-hand weights (5 kg for men, 3 kg for women) from shoulder to full arm extension at a one-second cadence until exhaustion or loss of rhythm [14].

The field fitness tests studied: isometric trunk flexion endurance (45° in the hip joint) (a), isometric trunk extension endurance (b), 50 sit-to-stand (50 sit-to-stand speed; number of successful chair-bounces and also total number chair-raises (n out of 50); 30-s sit-to-stand (c), alternating shoulder presses (d), 5 sit-to-stand (sitting on a chair between each stand up) (e), maximal step height test (MST), left and right leg (L and R), respectively (f), handgrip strength (L = left and R = right) (g), 6-min walk test (6MWT) (h), Ekblom-Bak cycle ergometer test (measuring heart rate during two workloads) (i), time-up-and-go (TUG) (j), stand-and-reach (k), one-leg standing balance test (left-L and right-R, open and closed eyes) (l), maximal cycle ergometer test for VO2peak (m), 4-meter walk tests at self-selected and maximal speed (n), 3- and 5-min pyramid test (o), and floor-to-stand (p). Figure modified from Godhe et al. [14] in the journal Gerontology (see descriptions in the Method section).

Muscular strength was evaluated through three tests. The time to complete five sit-to-stands with complete sitting between each rise was recorded on a 46-cm chair [16]. The maximal step height test (MST) was performed on a specialized step box for both the ipsilateral (with hip OA) and contralateral leg, requiring proper posture without support [17]. For grip strength (N), a dynamometer (Sagitta, Sagitta Pedagog AB, Mariestad, Sweden) was used, recording the best of three trials per hand with the elbow fully extended while standing [18].

Cardiorespiratory fitness was assessed using the six-minute walk test (6MWT), during which participants rapidly walked around an indoor course consisting of 50 m laps [16, 19], and a submaximal cycling test following the Ekblom-Bak protocol [20]. The cycling test involved two 4-min periods at 60 rpm: initially at 32 watts, then at a personalized workload aiming for RPE ∼13–14. Heart rate was monitored with a heart rate monitor (Polar 400, Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland), and the average heart rate during the last minute of each load was recorded. After a brief rest, a maximal oxygen cycle test was performed to determine peak VO2. Participants wore a face mask (Hans Rudolph, Germany), and their expired air was collected and analyzed in a mixing chamber (Oxycon Jaeger Pro, Hoechberg, Germany). Gas concentrations and flow were calibrated before each test following the manufacturer’s instructions using external gas supplies (15% O2 and 6% CO2). The test started at 32 W and increased by 10–30 W per minute based on the participant’s capacity until exhaustion [21, 22]. Verbal encouragement was provided throughout. Peak VO2 was calculated as the highest 30-s average during the test [23]. A 5-min pyramid test was also performed, during which the number of laps over pyramid-shaped boxes (maximum height 0.62 m, lap distance 4.5 m) was recorded at both 3 and 5 min [19].

Motor fitness evaluation comprised the timed up-and-go test (TUG), requiring participants to rise from a 46-cm chair, walk 3 meters around a mark, and return to sitting [16]; the stand-and-reach test, measuring fingertip-to-toe distance while standing with straight legs [14], one-leg balance tests (60 s) recording support instances with eyes open, and time to first support with eyes open and eyes closed [24] (ipsilateral leg and contralateral leg), and the time (s) to walk 4 meters at self-selected and maximum pace (the middle 4 meters over a total walking distance of 7 meters). The time to stand up from lying down flat on the back on the floor was also measured (“floor-to-stand”) [25]. For participants unable to complete specific tests due to pain or limitations, only the affected test data were excluded from analyses while retaining all other valid measurements.

Accelerometer Recordings

Hip-worn accelerometer recordings were performed following the pre-op 1 test and twice following THA surgery (post-op 4 months and post-op 1 year). The GT3X accelerometer (ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola, FL, USA), a compact electronic device (3.8 × 3.7 × 1.8 cm; 27 g), was used to monitor participants’ PA patterns. This device quantifies PA and sedentary (SED) behavior by assessing intensity, frequency, and duration, expressed in units called “counts”, and summarized as counts per minute (CPM). Participants received their accelerometers after completing fitness tests at GIH, along with verbal instructions on device use. The initial measurement period lasted at least 1 week (∼7–9 days) and had a predetermined return date. This process was repeated at 4 months and 1 year postoperatively.

Participants were instructed to wear the accelerometer during all waking hours for at least seven consecutive days, including weekends, and to remove it only for water-based activities. The device, attached via an elastic belt, was positioned over the right hip. Data collection and processing were performed using ActiLife v.6.10.1 software, with data extracted in 60-s epochs using a proprietary low-frequency extension filter. Non-wear time was defined as 60 or more consecutive minutes with 0 CPM. To be included in analyses, participants needed to wear the device for at least 4 days with a minimum of 10 h per day.

The raw accelerometer data (30-Hz sampling rate) were examined for vertical axis accelerations to measure SED behavior and PA at different intensities. Results are presented as both mean minutes per day (min/day) and percentage of wear time (% of wear time).

The following parameters were established for vertical axis acceleration analysis: Total SED behavior was defined as 0–99 CPM [26, 27]. SED bout was classified as ≥10 consecutive minutes within 0–99 CPM [26]. Light-intensity PA was set at 100–2,019 CPM [26, 27] while moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) was defined as ≥2,020 CPM, with vigorous activity specifically categorized as >5,999 CPM [26, 28]. The Freedson bout (MVPA bout) was defined as a consecutive period of >1,952 CPM lasting ≥10 min, with a 2-min drop time [26]. Additionally, vector magnitude counts per minute [28], and the number of steps per day were analyzed. These accelerometer parameters were assessed and compared to a previous study on healthy older adults [29].

Self-Reported Measures

Participants were assessed using the Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS) at pre-op 1, post-op 4 months, and post-op 1 year, a validated, hip-specific questionnaire developed by [30]. The HOOS consists of 40 items divided into five subscales: pain (10 items), symptoms and stiffness (5 items), activities of daily living (ADL; 17 items), function in sport and recreation (4 items), and hip-related quality of life (QOL; 4 items). Each subscale was scored independently using the established HOOS scoring system, resulting in normalized scores ranging from 0 to 100. The overall mean HOOS score was also recorded. Higher scores indicate more favorable outcomes, with 100 representing optimal hip function and 0 indicating maximum disability. The HOOS has demonstrated high test-retest reliability (Intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] > 0.78) [31]. There were no cases in which more than 50% of items were missing from any subscale; therefore, no data were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Retrospective data were registered regarding the frequency and duration of both structured rehabilitation exercises with a physical therapist and self-directed strength exercises. Questions captured exercise habits over the preceding 6 months before surgery and up to 1 year after THA. These data were then analyzed for the 48 participants who completed all four assessments from preoperatively to 1 year post-THA.

Statistical Analysis

The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Reliability analysis between the two pre-op tests in hip OA patients included paired t-tests, ICC (2.1) with 95% confidence intervals, and the standard error of measurement derived from ANOVA analyses. ICC values were interpreted as: excellent (>0.90), good (0.75–0.90), moderate (0.50–0.75), and poor (<0.50). Additionally, limits of agreement were calculated using the formula d ± (1.96 × SD difference), where d represents the mean difference between pre-op test scores, and coefficients of variation were also computed. To examine changes over time (preoperative to postoperative) in physical fitness tests and accelerometry parameters, a repeated-measure ANOVA was conducted. Significant main effects of time were analyzed, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc tests to evaluate differences between measurement time points. The effect size over time was assessed using partial eta squared (η2p) from the RM-ANOVA, where 0.01 indicates a small effect, 0.06 a moderate effect, and 0.14 a large effect. The significance level in the statistical analyses was set at p > 0.05, with multiple testing adjustments (p < 0.01). All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi (Version 2.4, Computer Software).

Results

Test-Retest of Physical Fitness Tests in Patients with Hip OA

Paired t-tests showed no significant improvement from pre-op 1 to pre-op 2 in any fitness tests, except for women who improved in their time to first miss during the open eyes balance tests on the ipsilateral leg (online suppl. Table; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551919). ICCs indicated good to excellent test-retest reliability, with most values exceeding 0.75. In a few cases, the ICC ranged to moderate values (e.g., 5 sit-to-stand and ipsilateral step height in men, and in open-eye first-miss balance tests). Physical Fitness Test Scores

Isometric trunk endurance improved significantly throughout the follow-up period. Trunk flexion endurance increased by 46% at 4 months and 82% in 1 year, while trunk extension endurance improved by 71% in 1 year (all p < 0.0001). Sit-to-stand performance improved across multiple parameters. The 30-s test increased from 16.5 to 21.2 repetitions at 1-year post-op (28% increase, p < 0.0001), while the time for five sit-to-stands decreased by 32%, from 11.9 s to 8.1 s (p = 0.0004), and sit-to-stand speed improved by 27% over the year (p < 0.0001). Step height assessment revealed distinct improvement patterns between limbs. The ipsilateral side showed greater improvements (24% at 4 months and 42% at 1 year) compared to the contralateral limb, which showed 8% and 13% improvements, respectively (all p < 0.0001).

Walking performance improved significantly across all measures. TUG times decreased by 29% at the 1-year follow-up (8.3s–5.9s, p = 0.0020), while 6MWT distance increased by 16% from 532 m to 615 m (p < 0.0001). Both self-selected and maximal 4-meter walking speeds improved by 45% and 42%, respectively, at 1-year follow-up (p < 0.0001).

Cardiorespiratory fitness showed modest yet significant improvements. VO2peak increased by 4% from 22.7 to 23.6 mL/kg/min at 1 year (p = 0.0020). Both the 3-min and 5-min pyramid tests showed improvements of 13% and 12%, respectively, at the 1-year follow-up (p < 0.0001). Aerobic fitness improvements were generally not observed at 4 months postoperatively.

PA Measurements

PA patterns showed notable improvements at 1 year postoperatively. MVPA increased significantly by 63%, from 18.7 to 30.5 min daily (p = 0.0002), with the proportion of wear time rising from 2.3% to 3.6% (p = 0.0011). Freedson-defined MVPA bouts almost doubled from 9.7 to 19.0 min daily (p = 0.0006). Daily step count increased by 26% from 6,004 to 7,558 steps (p = 0.0007).

These postoperative PA levels represented a clinically meaningful threshold: at 1 year following THA, participants accumulated a mean of 30.5 min of MVPA per day, equivalent to approximately 213 min per week, thereby meeting the international PA recommendation of at least 150 min per week of moderate-to-vigorous activity [32]. Prior to surgery, participants averaged 18.7 min of MVPA per day (approximately 131 min per week), falling below this recommended threshold. This transition from below to above the recommended activity guideline represents one of the key findings of this study.

SED behavior and light PA remained stable throughout the study period. SED time showed a small, nonsignificant change from 551 to 573 min daily (p = 0.1534), while light PA remained essentially unchanged at approximately 245 min daily (p = 0.9507). Device wear time was consistent across all measurement periods: 13.6, 13.7, and 14.2 h at preoperative, 4-month, and 1-year follow-up, respectively. No significant changes in PA patterns were observed at the 4-month assessment.

Self-Reported Outcomes

HOOS

Patient-reported outcomes, assessed using the Hip disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS), showed significant improvements across all measured domains (symptoms and stiffness, pain, activities of daily living, function in sports and recreational activities, and quality of life), as determined by repeated measures ANOVA (p < 0.001). The most substantial gains were observed at the 4-month postoperative assessment, with continued significant improvements in the 1-year follow-up. The composite HOOS score increased notably from 40.3 at baseline to 82.6 4 months postoperatively and further improved to 88.6 at 1 year post-op, as shown in Figure 5. This suggests a successful recovery and sustained rehabilitation early after surgery, with high HOOS scores at 4 months, compared to more marked continued improvements in objective physical fitness measures up to 12 months post-surgery.

Physiotherapy and Self-Directed Exercise

Preoperative Period

Preoperative physiotherapy treatment (within 6 months before surgery) was performed by approximately half (n = 23) of all 48 hip OA patients who completed all pre- and postoperative tests. On average, one physiotherapy session per week was conducted, with a mean duration of 35 min per session, over a period of 3–4 months. Self-directed strengthening exercises were generally performed by all participants (n = 48), with an average of every-other-day sessions (30 min per session) maintained over at least 5 months.

Postoperative Period

At the 1-year follow-up, all participants (n = 48) reported generally engaging in structured physiotherapy, with an average of one 35–40-min session each week over 3–4 months. Self-directed strength exercises demonstrated a similar frequency to that before the operation, with participants completing 30–35-min sessions four times a week over approximately 5 months. This exercise adherence was consistent across both groups (n = 23 and n = 48), regardless of preoperative physiotherapy.

Discussion

Main Findings

Test-retest analysis demonstrated high measurement reliability across fitness parameters, confirming the validity of subsequent longitudinal comparisons. Following THA, significant improvements in physical fitness and activity levels were observed over the course of 1 year, with patients meeting international guidelines for MVPA. Longitudinal analysis revealed substantial gains across multiple functional domains after surgery, including several assessments not previously studied in THA patients, such as maximal cycling to determine VO2peak, pyramid tests, maximum step height, isometric trunk endurance, shoulder presses, and speed in 50 sit-to-stand repetitions. While functional improvements were evident at the 4-month post-test with continued progress at the 1-year follow-up, aerobic fitness, and PA levels only significantly improved after 1 year. Step height analysis revealed notable asymmetrical patterns and compensatory mechanisms. At baseline, the affected limb achieved only 72–76% of the contralateral height, with postoperative assessments indicating different improvement patterns between limbs.

PA patterns showed significant changes after surgical intervention. MVPA increased by 63% at the 1-year follow-up, along with a 26% rise in daily ambulatory activity (6,004–7,558 steps). The combination of maintained SED behavior with increased moderate activity indicates a behavioral shift, replacing light-intensity activities rather than decreasing SED time [33].

Improvements in the 3- and 5-min pyramid tests, walking distance, and cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2peak) show increased aerobic capacity 1 year after surgery, which is important for maintaining independence in daily activities, as aerobic capacity relates to functional performance in older adults [34]. These improvements, combined with increases in self-reported functional outcomes, provide evidence of the effectiveness of THA in addressing functional deficits associated with hip OA.

Test-Retest Reliability

The physical performance test battery showed measurement consistency between the two pre-op tests, with no significant differences observed across 23 of 24 fitness parameters. One balance test was an exception. ICCs mainly indicated good to excellent test-retest reliability, supporting the validity of the assessment protocol.

These findings address a previously identified need for reliability assessment in OA populations and complement earlier research showing acceptable to good reliability coefficients (ICC) for specific functional measures: knee-extension-strength (ICC = 0.79–0.99), 8-feet walk time (ICC = 0.83–0.99), stair ascend/descend performance (ICC = 0.84–0.99), and the 5-repetition sit-to-stand test (ICC = 0.84–0.99) [10, 12]. Additionally, systematic review data have documented favorable ICC values for the timed up-and-go test, the 30-s sit-to-stand, and various walk tests ranging from 6 min to 13 m [11]. However, it should be noted that these reports did not include explicit statistical comparisons of performance measures between sessions.

The reliability of these assessments increases confidence in future long-term comparisons and provides methodological validation for their use in both research and clinical settings. This thorough reliability analysis contributes to the current evidence. It examines newer fitness tests that have not been studied before, supporting the usefulness of standardized physical performance measures in OA populations.

Physical Fitness

Previous meta-analyses of THA recovery primarily relied on patient-reported outcomes and basic functional tests, such as walking performance, the timed-up-and-go test, and the sit-to-stand test. Most studies had limited follow-up periods, up to 6 months. [3]. Our comprehensive assessment extended the evaluation using novel fitness parameters and a longer follow-up.

Comparative analyses revealed significant preoperative deficits in hip OA patients across various areas, including lower levels of MVPA, reduced functional mobility, decreased walking capacity, weaker strength in the trunk and extremities, and impaired postural control on the affected limb, as well as reduced strength and endurance in trunk, arm, and leg muscles, compared to healthy older adults [14, 29, 35].

Our cohort showed a gradual reduction in limb asymmetry following surgery. The step height difference between the affected and opposite leg decreased from a 24% deficit before surgery to 8% 1 year after the procedure. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which also found similar strength deficits before surgery that improve over time [36]. However, muscle size analyses suggest that some deficits may persist, with reductions in the cross-sectional area of certain muscle groups, including the iliopsoas (7%) and the hip adductors (8%) [37]. These findings support consideration of earlier surgical intervention to prevent progressive muscle atrophy in patients with severe hip OA.

Balance deficits persisted at 1 year, with our findings showing 7–23% differences between operated and non-operated limbs, consistent with previous reports of postural stability deficits following THA [38]. Recent investigations have demonstrated variable recovery patterns, with improved functional mobility (as measured by TUG performance) but persistent bilateral strength deficits compared to healthy controls [39]. However, direct comparisons require caution given differences in study populations, measurement methods, and control group characteristics.

The modest but significant 4% increase in VO2peak at 1 year marks a notable deviation from the expected age-related decline, which typically occurs in older adults, resulting in a reduction of approximately 3.5 mL/kg/min over 5 years [40, 41]. This improvement has clinical relevance, as each 1 mL/kg/min increase in VO2peak associates with meaningful reductions in cardiovascular disease risk and all-cause mortality [40]. Previous THA studies reported similar increases in VO2peak at 1 year, although direct comparisons are limited by methodological differences and younger study populations [42].

Our investigation revealed improvements in 18 of 33 measured fitness parameters, consistent with previous studies reporting enhancements in eight of ten parameters, despite our cohort’s older age [43]. The newly evaluated pyramid test, which assesses functional lower extremity capacity through stair-climbing movements, demonstrated consistent improvements and may have clinical implications warranting further investigation [19]. The observed improvements in isometric trunk extension (from 71 s to 122 s) sharply contrast with typical age-related decline patterns [44], suggesting that successful THA may offer broader health benefits beyond improved hip function, as supported by concurrent gains in the patient-reported outcomes.

Physical Activity

PA measurements showed significant improvements after surgery. Most notably, MVPA increased by 63% from 18.7 to 30.5 min per day at the 1-year post-op assessment. This was accompanied by a 26% rise in daily steps from 6,004 to 7,558. The World Health Organization recommends at least 150 min per week of MVPA for older adults, with additional benefits at higher volumes [32]. Preoperatively, our participants averaged approximately 131 min of MVPA per week below this threshold. At the 1-year follow-up, mean weekly MVPA had increased to approximately 213 min, surpassing the guideline recommendation.

This transition is clinically relevant: meta-analytical evidence demonstrates that achieving 150–300 min of weekly MVPA is associated with substantial reductions in all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease risk, and functional decline in older adults [45, 46]. The finding that this threshold was not met prior to surgery highlights the extent to which advanced hip OA restricts habitual PA. Moreover, it suggests that for patients with severe OA, conservative management may be insufficient to restore adequate activity levels, and that timely surgical intervention may be a prerequisite for achieving health-promoting PA patterns.

Limited longitudinal data exist on objective PA patterns from pre- to post-THA, and substantial methodological heterogeneity exists across studies. Our MVPA improvements align with previous findings, which show increases from 20 to 25 to 30–45 min per day at 1 year [47]. However, some studies report no significant changes at 6 months [48]. The approximate 1,500 steps/day increase observed 1 year after surgery exceeds established thresholds for clinical significance, with previous research linking increases of 1,000 steps/day to a reduced risk of functional limitation and 6–36% decreases in all-cause mortality [49]. The daily step count at the 1-year follow-up exceeds recommended levels for PA guidelines, confirming a more active lifestyle after surgery.

Our preoperative PA levels (MVPA: 18.7 min daily; daily steps: 6,004) align with recent accelerometry studies in older OA populations using similar methods [47] but are lower than meta-analytical estimates, which included predominantly younger cohorts [50]. Recent large-scale analyses have demonstrated that post-arthroplasty patients achieve MVPA levels comparable to those of healthy controls, thereby resolving preoperative deficits [51].

Meta-analytical evidence shows a significant reduction in mortality risk of up to 60%, at MVPA levels of 24 min daily among older adults [52], with 30–40 min of daily MVPA effectively decreasing mortality risks associated with SED behavior [46]. The clinical importance extends beyond mortality, as accelerometer-measured MVPA strongly correlates with functional performance metrics in older adults [53].

Hip OA patients achieved recommended MVPA levels after THA, highlighting the significant activity limitations caused by advanced joint disease. This finding suggests that timely surgical intervention, when clinically appropriate, could be beneficial, as earlier arthroplasty may help maintain healthy PA habits and reduce the systemic health impacts of prolonged activity restriction.

THA Rehabilitation

Current evidence on the best post-THA rehab protocols remains limited, with systematic reviews showing no significant difference between progressive resistance training and standard rehab protocols [54, 55]. Meta-analytical evidence from six intervention studies showed similar results for cardiorespiratory endurance, functional mobility, and lower extremity power output across different rehabilitation protocols, with consistent adverse event rates across approaches [55].

While some studies have reported early benefits of intensive strength training protocols at 3 to 6 months postoperatively, these differences usually resolve by 1 year [56]. Similarly, specialized interventions, such as functional strength integration have not demonstrated better performance results than traditional rehabilitation 6 months after surgery [57]. These findings suggest that standard rehabilitation protocols may be sufficient to achieve optimal functional recovery after THA; however, the potential effects of more intensive rehabilitation on overall fitness improvements, as observed in our study, warrant further investigation in randomized controlled trials.

Previous investigations of nonsurgical interventions in OA populations have shown mixed results, with exercise programs providing limited lasting improvements in objectively measured PA [58–60]. Large-scale cross-sectional studies show that patients with preoperative OA take approximately 1,129 fewer steps per day than healthy controls, while post-arthroplasty patients reach activity levels similar to those of the controls [61].

Our longitudinal findings show that surgical intervention helped patients achieve international PA recommendations, unlike their sub-threshold preoperative levels. This indicates that, while conservative treatments may relieve symptoms, surgery might be necessary to enable meaningful activity participation in advanced OA cases.

THA Self-Reported Outcomes

Patient-reported outcomes showed significant improvements throughout the follow-up period. The composite HOOS score steadily increased from the preoperative baseline of 40.3–82.6 at 4 months post-operation, with further improvement at 1-year follow-up to 88.6. The most affected preoperative domains were Function in Sports and Recreation (24.3) and Quality of Life (25.1), with quality of life experiencing the greatest postoperative improvement, reaching a score of 81.4 1 year after surgery.

These HOOS improvements align with previously published data for similar patient populations and follow-up periods [43, 48, 62]. The self-reported functional improvements align with gains observed in functional fitness tests, with significant progress evident as early as 4 months after surgery in measures such as sit-to-stand performance and walking speed.

All participants adhered to standard rehabilitation protocols without any modifications. About half of the participants (23 out of 48 patients) received preoperative supervised physiotherapy, while all patients consistently performed unsupervised strength exercises both before and after surgery. Participation in postoperative supervised physiotherapy remained consistent, regardless of whether they had received preoperative treatment.

Strengths and Limitations

The current study highlights several methodological strengths that enhance its contribution to existing research. The comprehensive assessment protocol examined test-retest reliability across multiple functional domains using field-based measurement tests in patients with hip OA, including innovative assessments with potential clinical usefulness. The longitudinal design, which features two pre-op tests and both a 4-month and a 1-year follow-up, allows for analysis of recovery patterns. Additionally, we compare postoperative results to the second pre-op test, which helps lessen learning effects, a unique aspect of this pre- to post-THA comparison.

Our year-round recruitment strategy, spanning 2.5 years, minimized potential seasonal fluctuations in PA patterns during longitudinal comparisons. However, several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The absence of a randomized control group introduces the possibility of confounding variables. Selection bias must also be acknowledged, as participants who completed the extended follow-up may represent a more motivated subgroup of the initial cohort. Additionally, the small number of older men with hip OA in the study warrants caution when interpreting sex-specific preoperative comparisons between men. Furthermore, the limited follow-up sample size restricts sex-specific analyses of postoperative recovery trajectories and our ability to detect potential sex differences in recovery patterns.

The study’s attrition rate of 38% at 1-year follow-up may introduce selection bias, as participants who completed the extended follow-up may represent a more motivated or healthier subgroup. However, baseline characteristics were similar between completers and non-completers, suggesting limited systematic bias.

A notable finding was the high reliability of many physical performance measures between preoperative assessments, with no significant differences between pre-op tests across the test battery, except in one balance test. This indicates that a single preoperative assessment may be sufficient, enabling shorter and less time-consuming protocols in future studies.

This study was conducted at a single specialist clinic (Artro Clinic, Sophiahemmet, Stockholm), which may limit the generalizability of findings. Patients referred to and operated at a specialized arthroplasty center may represent a more health-literate, motivated, and socioeconomically advantaged subset of the broader hip OA population. Activity levels, rehabilitation adherence, and functional outcomes may differ in lower resource settings.

A further limitation concerns potential expectation bias. Participants were aware that physical fitness assessments would be repeated at multiple time points, which may have influenced their motivation to exercise and rehabilitate between sessions. This knowledge of repeated testing could have contributed to greater rehabilitation engagement than would be observed in routine clinical care, potentially inflating the observed improvement.

The absence of body composition assessment represents an additional limitation. Changes in lean body mass and lower limb muscle mass were not quantified, which constrains interpretation of the observed improvements in muscle strength and functional performance. It remains unclear to what extent improvements reflect true muscle hypertrophy, neural adaptations, or reductions in pain-related inhibition. Similarly, the presence and severity of sarcopenia were not assessed, despite its high prevalence in this age group and its known association with poor surgical outcomes and impaired functional recovery. Future studies should incorporate dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or equivalent methods to provide a more complete picture of musculoskeletal recovery following THA.

Several of the assessments included in the test battery represent methodological contributions beyond existing THA research. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine maximal aerobic capacity using a cycle ergometer VO2peak protocol, the 3- and 5-min pyramid test, the maximal step height test, isometric trunk endurance, shoulder presses, and the 50-repetition sit-to-stand speed test in the context of pre- to post-THA evaluation. Their inclusion provides a more comprehensive characterization of physical recovery trajectories and may offer additional clinical value in identifying patients who remain functionally limited despite satisfactory scores on conventional outcome measures.

Conclusion

This study found low fitness and activity levels in patients with hip OA, with significant improvements in physical fitness and activity measurements 1 year after surgery. The high reliability of the comprehensive assessment battery supports its usefulness for both research and clinical practice. These findings add to the existing evidence on simple, field-based fitness tests for patients with hip OA, while also providing validated methodological insights and recovery trajectories.
