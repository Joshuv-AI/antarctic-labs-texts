Rebuilding ability acquisition in medical imaging teaching: a multi-level AI-driven teaching paradigm based on cognitive-information theory
Authors: Jie An, Xi Leng, Yujie Liu, Jie Zhou, Yi Liang, Yuna Chen, Shijun Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMedical imaging education faces challenges like learner variability and delayed feedback, hindering efficiency and skill transfer. While AI shows promise, no previous studies have systematically tested multi-layer AI teaching frameworks using dual-level randomized experiments. This study evaluates a cognitive informatics–driven “three-level–four-module” AI teaching paradigm.MethodsSixty postgraduate imaging students participated in a course-level RCT (AI paradigm vs. traditional teaching, 8–12 weeks) and a 2 × 2 factorial experiment to dissect the effects of “explainable guidance” and “adaptive scheduling.” Data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models and DeLong tests.ResultsThe AI paradigm significantly improved diagnostic performance (AUC: β = 0.072, P < 0.001; accuracy: +5.8%, P = 0.004) and reduced cognitive load (NASA-TLX, η²=0.12, P < 0.01). Factorial analysis revealed both components independently increased AUC (Δ ≈ 0.05, P < 0.01) with a positive synergistic interaction (β = 0.021, P = 0.03), and cognitive load reduction was maximal when combined (Δ=–17.6, P = 0.01).ConclusionThe AI-driven paradigm enhances learning effectiveness and reduces cognitive load, addressing a critical gap by providing scalable, evidence for multi-layer AI integration in medical imaging instruction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09002-3.

Introduction

Medical imaging education is central to training diagnostic physicians and research-oriented talents. Current teaching models, however, face unresolved challenges: learner variability in prior knowledge and cognitive load tolerance, delayed feedback leading to persistent cognitive biases (error rates of 20–35% in chest X-ray/CT interpretation [1]), and overemphasis on pattern recognition over reasoning chain construction—limiting skill transfer to rare diseases or emerging modalities [2, 3]. These issues are exacerbated by the lack of dynamic adaptation to individual learning trajectories, a gap highlighted in recent meta-analyses [4, 5].

From the perspective of cognitive-information theory, these limitations stem from insufficient learner state modeling and fragmented integration of AI functions. This theory integrates cognitive science and information theory to optimize human information processing [6], and recent advances in AI-driven medical education have shown promise: a 2024 systematic review [4] confirms AI can improve learning efficiency by 15–25%, but notes critical gaps: (i) most studies focus on single-module interventions (e.g., only case annotation or only feedback [6, 7]) rather than closed-loop multi-module integration; (ii) few studies combine explainable AI (XAI) with adaptive scheduling to synergistically reduce cognitive load [8]; (iii) longitudinal tracking of skill transfer across training cycles is rare [9]. Complementary research [5, 10] further points out that existing adaptive systems rarely incorporate individual forgetting parameters or confusion pair training, limiting their ability to correct systematic errors.

To address these gaps, we propose a cognitive informatics-driven “three-level–four-module” AI teaching paradigm. Unlike prior work [11, 12] that focuses on single-task optimization or static case push, our paradigm integrates: (i) a micro-meso-macro three-tier architecture (covering task-level feedback, course-level scheduling, and longitudinal ability transfer); (ii) four closed-loop modules (cognitive diagnosis → case generation → explainable guidance → adaptive scheduling); and (iii) dual experimental validation (course-level RCT + component-level factorial experiment). This design not only operationalizes cognitive-information theory but also addresses the fragmentation of existing AI educational systems, offering scalable, transferable evidence for medical imaging instruction.

Relevant work

AI in medical imaging education and assessment

Artificial intelligence has rapidly advanced in medical imaging education, encompassing automatic case annotation, learning path optimization, skill assessment, and ability prediction. Deep learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks and transformers, have enabled the construction of diverse, high-representativeness image libraries, overcoming limitations of static case sets in sample size and difficulty gradients [1]. Zhou et al. demonstrated in a multi-center chest X-ray teaching study that AI-driven personalized case selection improved learners’ sensitivity and specificity by 12% and 9% over 8 weeks [2]. AI scoring systems further provide high-frequency, consistent feedback by analyzing diagnostic decisions and visual fixation trajectories, supporting scalable intelligent teaching platforms across institutions [3, 4].

Recent studies (2022–2024) have expanded AI applications: Li et al. [13] developed a 3D Slicer-integrated platform for anatomy teaching, improving spatial understanding but lacking dynamic difficulty adjustment; Wang et al. [14] used virtual simulation for practical training, enhancing clinical familiarity but ignoring individual cognitive differences. A critical gap is that most existing systems [3, 13] focus on single-modal optimization (e.g., only case libraries or only scoring) rather than cross-task, cross-course integration. They also rarely link AI functions to long-term skill transfer, a key demand in medical education [4]. In summary, while AI shows promise in automating tasks, existing systems largely focus on single-module optimization. This fragmented approach fails to integrate AI functions across tasks and courses [15], a critical gap this study addresses with a multi-level, closed-loop framework.

Cognitive diagnosis, knowledge tracing, and adaptive learning

Cognitive diagnostic models (e.g., DINA, GDINA) infer mastery of fine-grained knowledge points from response patterns, identifying learners’ weak links [5, 6]. Knowledge tracing methods (e.g., BKT, DKT) dynamically model learners’ evolving knowledge states [7]. Integrating these approaches enables continuous learner state monitoring and targeted interventions. Reinforcement learning–based adaptive scheduling has demonstrated improved learning gains in CT cerebral hemorrhage training, alleviating cognitive overload and accelerating skill acquisition [8], providing a methodological foundation for dynamically regulated AI-driven teaching models. Cognitive diagnosis and knowledge tracing technologies lay the groundwork for precise learner state modeling, but few studies have integrated these tools into a hierarchical teaching framework to achieve cross-task, cross-course ability evolution tracking—consistent with the cognitive informatics emphasis on optimizing human information processing through systematic state modeling.

Recent work has advanced these techniques: Zhang et al. [16] used DKT to predict medical students’ learning gaps, but did not integrate reinforcement learning for scheduling; Chen et al. [17] applied PPO algorithm for adaptive task selection, but lacked cognitive diagnosis-driven personalization. A key limitation is that existing studies [7, 16] treat cognitive diagnosis and adaptive scheduling as separate components, failing to leverage their synergy—for example, not using fine-grained knowledge mastery data to optimize RL reward functions.

We integrate BKT/DKT models with PPO-based adaptive scheduling, where the reward function (learning gain + efficiency - cognitive load) directly incorporates cognitive diagnosis outputs (e.g., weak knowledge points, forgetting parameters). This closed-loop design ensures that scheduling is not only efficient but also targeted to individual cognitive states, addressing the “state-scheduling mismatch” in prior work [8, 17]. Collectively, cognitive diagnosis and adaptive scheduling have been treated as separate entities. Our study innovates by integrating them into a closed loop, where diagnosis outputs directly inform the RL reward function, enabling a “state-scheduling” synergy absent in prior work.

Explainable AI and human–computer collaborative prompts

Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, including saliency maps, Grad-CAM, feature visualization, and decision-path reconstruction, make the “black box” of image interpretation partially transparent [9]. Studies have demonstrated that XAI-guided case interpretation can increase lesion detection by junior radiology residents while reducing time spent on irrelevant information in medical imaging training [10]. Human–computer collaborative diagnostic models, where AI provides initial interpretable prompts and learners perform secondary reasoning, improve both accuracy and structured understanding, supporting skill transfer and reasoning habit formation [11]. Explainable AI and human–computer collaboration effectively reduce extraneous cognitive load and strengthen reasoning chains, but their synergistic effects with adaptive scheduling remain understudied—echoing the cognitive informatics goal of minimizing inefficient information processing through targeted guidance.

Collectively, AI in medical imaging education offers dynamic, personalized learning materials and enhances diagnostic accuracy, cognitive transfer, and reasoning. However, three key research gaps persist: ① Previous studies have not systematically validated the multi-layered AI teaching framework using two-level randomized experiments (course-level RCTS + component-level factorial experiments); ② There is a lack of synergy effect analysis for the closed-loop module of “cognitive diagnosis - case generation - interpretable guidance - adaptive scheduling”. ③ Most of the existing research focuses on the optimization of a single teaching link and has not achieved a full-process dynamic adaptation from micro tasks to macro training cycles. This study addresses these gaps by designing a three-layer, four-module AI teaching paradigm and evaluating its causal effects and mechanisms at both course and component levels, establishing the foundation for the Methods section. Thus, while XAI effectively reduces extraneous cognitive load, its synergistic potential with adaptive scheduling for long-term knowledge consolidation remains unexplored. This study is designed to investigate this precise interaction, as detailed in Experiment B.

Method

Multi-layer paradigm (micro-meso-macro)

To systematically optimize medical imaging learning, we propose a Micro-Meso-Macro three-level paradigm, which aligns with core educational theories [18]: micro-level focuses on real-time feedback (constructivism), meso-level on load balance (cognitive load theory), and macro-level on skill transfer (skill acquisition theory). It tracks capabilities across tasks, courses, and time to achieve refined intervention.

This three-tier paradigm achieves a closed loop through a unified data flow and feedback mechanism: the micro level outputs real-time task performance, the meso level adjusts the course strategy accordingly, and the macro level uses long-term profiling to guide the update of meso strategy weights. The system performs global parameter updates at the end of each cycle (week) to balance short-term task optimization with long-term training goals.

Four major AI modules

To achieve multi-level and dynamically adapted medical imaging teaching, this study designed four core AI modules to form a closed-loop teaching system (cognitive diagnosis → case generation/screening → explainable guidance → adaptive scheduling), supporting the improvement of capabilities in micro tasks, course units, and macro cultivation. The detailed construction of each module is as follows:

Cognitive diagnosis module

This module captures learners’ knowledge mastery and misjudgment types to support personalized teaching (cognitive diagnosis theory core). Key components:

Knowledge tracking and misjudgment modeling

We use Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) for real-time mastery updates. Case generation & selection module

This module is responsible for providing image cases of gradient difficulty, covering key knowledge points and confusion pairs for training. The specific construction is as follows:

Basic data sources and amplification strategies

Confuse the strategies

Highly confused disease pairs (such as nodules vs. scars, inflammation vs. tumors) are arranged in a control manner to intensify identification training. The generation algorithm prioritizes cases related to the learner’s weak knowledge points to enhance its pertinence.

Explainable guidance module

This module aims to reduce cognitive load and strengthen reasoning (cognitive load theory). Construction details:

Visual cues

Use Grad-CAM and saliency maps to highlight key image areas — this directly reduces time spent on irrelevant information [21], which is proven effective in prior medical education studies.

Language prompt strategy

LLM generates three-level cues (local sign, diagnostic, contrastive) — we design this to “scaffold” learning: start with hints, then reduce support, which aligns with Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development theory.

Adaptive scheduling module

This module combines cognitive diagnosis, case difficulty, and prompt dependency to optimize scheduling (adaptive learning theory).

Strategy algorithm

Task selection is based on reinforcement learning using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. The scheduler was implemented using a custom-coded PPO in PyTorch. While the core PPO framework follows standard implementations, the state space (comprising 15 features: knowledge mastery vector, task difficulty, hint dependency, etc.) and the reward function (see Appendix A for full mathematical formulation) were custom-engineered for this medical imaging teaching context. Closed-loop iterative optimization

After each training cycle, the scheduling module updates task sequence, difficulty weighting, and prompt allocation to achieve joint optimization across micro, meso, and macro levels. Using cognitive diagnosis outputs, historical learning curves, and prompt usage, it predicts the optimal subsequent training combination, enhancing individualized learning. Task execution results feed back into cognitive diagnosis, enabling continuous adaptation. This closed-loop mechanism balances cognitive load, task diversity, and long-term retention, providing quantifiable intervention metrics for Experiments A and B. Trial registration: Not applicable.

To ensure fairness and reproducibility, interface isomorphism was maintained across experimental arms, differing only in AI module activation. Scoring was fully blinded to expert reference values to prevent bias. All learning data—including task performance, course trajectories, and longitudinal ability profiles—were automatically captured and archived. Learner privacy was safeguarded via de-identification and hierarchical access control. These measures ensure the validity and attributability of experimental outcomes, providing a robust technical and methodological foundation for subsequent course-level RCTs.

Experiment a: course-level randomized controlled trial (RCT)

Following system implementation and experimental controls, a course-level RCT was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the multi-level AI-driven teaching paradigm in real-world medical imaging instruction.

Research questions and hypotheses

Based on theoretical analysis and prior evidence, five hypotheses were proposed:H1 (Effectiveness): Learners in the AI-driven experimental group will demonstrate superior diagnostic performance, reflected in higher AUC, interpretation accuracy, and structured report completeness.H2 (Efficiency): The experimental group will achieve comparable performance with reduced decision time and fewer cases, indicating accelerated learning and improved training efficiency.H3 (Cognitive Load): Post-course NASA-TLX scores will be lower in the experimental group, suggesting reduced psychological and information processing burden.H4 (Transferability): In cross-modal and cross-lesion transfer tests, performance decline will be smaller in the experimental group, reflecting enhanced knowledge and skill transfer.H5 (Calibration): Diagnostic confidence will align more closely with actual performance in the experimental group, evidenced by lower Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier scores, indicating improved decision-making calibration.

Study design and participants

A parallel-group randomized controlled trial was conducted with postgraduate imaging students at Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (target N ≈ 60). Participants had completed foundational imaging courses and possessed basic film-reading experience but had not begun clinical rotations, minimizing confounding from prior clinical exposure.

Inclusion criteria: age 20–30 years; completion of core imaging anatomy and medical imaging courses; basic computer skills for independent web-based learning; no formal clinical film-reading training within the past year; voluntary participation with informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: prior AI-assisted interpretation or intelligent teaching exposure; severe visual or color vision deficits; cognitive, neurological, or psychiatric disorders; planned clinical internships or training likely to enhance imaging skills during the study; noncompliance with protocol or < 80% early trial completion; prior participation in related studies with familiarity of materials.

Research subjects

Through the platform’s backend, they were randomly assigned to the control group or the experimental group (in a 1:1 ratio) to ensure that the two groups were balanced and comparable in key variables such as age, gender, and baseline image interpretation ability (pre-test scores). The randomization process was supervised by independent statisticians to avoid researcher bias.

The control group followed traditional imaging teaching

(1) Two weekly lectures covering imaging anatomy and key diagnostic points, plus one collective film-reading session led by senior physicians; (2) Fixed case sequence using a standardized, stratified case library (10 cases/week); (3) Standardized review with uniform feedback and common error analysis after each practice.

The experimental group adopted a four-module AI teaching platform based on a three-layer architecture of “micro - meso - macro” to achieve full-process dynamic optimization from single tasks to cross-cycle cultivation. Indicators and measurements

Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint focused on the efficacy and efficiency of learners in medical imaging diagnosis tasks. Firstly, diagnostic efficacy is measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and Accuracy. The system compares the interpretation results of each learner with the standard answers, calculates the true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) using the case-level binary classification results, and then generates the ROC curve and obtains the AUC:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AUC=\int_0^1TPR\left(FPR\right)d\left(FPR\right)$$\end{document}

Meanwhile, the accuracy rate is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Accuracy}\mathrm{=}\frac{\mathrm{TP}\mathrm{+}\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TP}\mathrm{+}\mathrm{FP}\mathrm{+}\mathrm{TN}\mathrm{+}\mathrm{FN}}$$\end{document}

Among them, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{TP}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{TN}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{FP}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{FN}$$\end{document} respectively represent true positive, true negative, false positive, and false negative cases. Secondly, the completeness of structured reports is quantified by the rate of missing items. The research platform will compare the structured reports submitted by learners with the standard reports, and count the number of key diagnostic items missed. The rate of missing items is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Missing}\;\mathrm{rate}=\frac{\mathrm{The}\;\mathrm{number}\;\mathrm{of}\;\mathrm{omitted}\;\mathrm{iteams}}{\mathrm{The}\;\mathrm{total}\;\mathrm{number}\;\mathrm{of}\;\mathrm{entries}\;\mathrm{should}\;\mathrm{be}\;\mathrm{inculuded}}$$\end{document}

AUC comparison

The intergroup comparison of AUC will adopt DeLong’s Test for Two Correlated ROC Curves to ensure the unbiasedness and variance control of the area difference estimation under the ROC curve. When it comes to multi-time point or multi-modal analysis, the Holm-Bonferroni method will be adopted for multiple comparison adjustments to control the first type of error rate.

Cognitive load and subjective experience were evaluated using the six dimensions of the English version of the NASA-TLX Scale (mental demand, physical demand, time pressure, effort level, frustration, and overall satisfaction) with a Likert 6-level score (1 = extremely low, 6 = extremely high), with a total score range of 6 to 36 points.

Missing data processing

The missing values will be handled by the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method and combined with the hierarchical strategy: introducing learner baseline ability stratification and experimental groups in the imputation model to reduce the bias of missing data on causal inference. The interpolation result will be combined with Rubin’s rule to obtain the final estimate.

Heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

To test the robustness of the intervention effect under different learner characteristics, subgroup analyses will be conducted. Subgroup variables include: baseline performance level, grade, gender, and self-reported learning strategies. Subgroup analysis will be accomplished by introducing the group × subgroup interaction term in the main model. If the interaction term is significant, further hierarchical modeling will be carried out.

Experiment B: 2 × 2 factorial component analysis

Following the overall effectiveness verification in Experiment A, Experiment B aimed to dissect the independent and interactive effects of the core modules—explainable guidance and adaptive scheduling—on learning outcomes and cognitive load. The selection of these two modules is theoretically driven:

From the cognitive-information theory perspective, they target the two most critical bottlenecks in medical imaging learning: ‘interpretation opacity’ (solved by explainable guidance) and ‘state-dynamic mismatch’ (solved by adaptive scheduling) [27].

Their synergy is hypothesized to amplify learning gains: explainable guidance reduces immediate extraneous cognitive load by focusing attention, while adaptive scheduling optimizes long-term knowledge consolidation to maximize germane load, together aligning perfectly with Cognitive Load Theory.

Dissecting their effects thus provides mechanistic insights into the AI paradigm’s effectiveness.

Factors and grouping

A 2 × 2 factorial design was employed: Factor A, explainable guidance (with vs. without); Factor B, adaptive scheduling (with vs. without), yielding four arms: A0B0 (baseline, neither module), A1B0 (explainable guidance only), A0B1 (adaptive scheduling only), and A1B1 (both modules). This design allows separation of individual and combined module effects.

Design and process

Participants were randomly allocated to four arms, ensuring balanced baseline characteristics. All groups accessed the same case database and scoring system as Experiment A, with strict control of question overlap to minimize cross-experiment transfer. The 2–4-week centralized training ensured equal learning duration and case exposure per participant. Each session comprised pre-test, training, post-test, and delayed test, allowing direct evaluation of individual module effects on performance, efficiency, and cognitive load.

Endpoint and statistical analysis

Primary endpoints mirrored Experiment A, including diagnostic performance (AUC, accuracy), structured report completeness, decision efficiency, and confidence calibration. Analyses focused on the main and interaction effects of Factor A (explainable guidance) and Factor B (adaptive scheduling). Depending on data distribution, repeated measures were analyzed via linear or generalized linear mixed models, or 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA. The effect size will be reported by partial \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} squared (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta}_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{2}}$$\end{document}) or Cohen’s d, and post hoc comparisons (Holm-Bonferroni correction) will be conducted when necessary to control for multiple comparison errors.

Discussion

Key findings summary

This study verified that the cognitive informatics-driven “three-level–four-module” AI teaching paradigm produces statistically significant improvements in medical imaging learning outcomes—including AUC (Δ = 0.072, P < 0.001), accuracy (Δ = 5.8%, P = 0.004), report completeness (Δ = 7.2%, P = 0.002), decision time reduction (Δ=-18.5 s/case, P = 0.008), and lower cognitive load (NASA-TLX η²=0.12, P < 0.01). Core modules “explainable guidance” and “adaptive scheduling” independently improve AUC (Δ = 0.045 and 0.052, P < 0.01) and exhibit a synergistic interaction (β = 0.021, P = 0.03). Three statistical observations underpin these outcomes: reduced cognitive load, corrected error patterns, and enhanced short-term memory retention via individualized interval repetition. However, the translation of these training gains to clinical practice and long-term skill retention remains preliminary, and the paradigm’s scalability requires further validation given the study’s limitations.

Reduced cognitive load: The synergistic burden reduction effect of explicable guidance and adaptive scheduling

Guided by cognitive load theory, the paradigm reduced extraneous load through module interaction. Explainable guidance (Grad-CAM + three-level prompts) shortened non-critical information search time (dwell time Δ=-18.3 s, clicks Δ=-4.7 vs. controls; both P < 0.001) and reduced high-order prompt dependence (from 21.4% to 12.3%, P < 0.001). Adaptive scheduling optimized task difficulty (0.3–0.7 range) via reinforcement learning, yielding NASA-TLX reductions of 7.5 points (single module) and 17.6 points (combined; interaction Δ=-3.9, P = 0.01). Ablation analysis confirmed both modules contributed to these gains (AUC reduction 3.5–4.1%, decision time prolongation 9.5–11.2 s when either removed).

However, clinical relevance remains unproven. Real-world diagnoses involve contextual factors (e.g., patient history, comorbidities) not captured in training cases. Whether reduced cognitive load during training translates to clinical efficiency—by freeing mental resources for contextual integration—requires validation in post-training follow-up studies. Additionally, the observed synergy may not persist in resource-limited settings or with diverse learner populations.

Error pattern correction: targeted training effects and limitations

The closed-loop mechanism targeted three common error types (feature omission, positioning offset, confusing misjudgment). Cognitive diagnosis (BKT/DKT) identified weak knowledge points, guiding case generation to prioritize confounding pairs (e.g., nodules vs. scars). After 8 weeks, the experimental group’s misjudgment rate for confusing lesions decreased by 11.3% (vs. 4.6% in controls, P < 0.001), and report completeness increased by 12.3% in the combined module group (P = 0.04). Controlled transfer tests showed preliminary cross-scenario generalization (new disease error rate Δ=-9.2% vs. controls, P < 0.01).

These effects should not be overstated. Training used standardized cases; improvements may not translate to clinical practice where lesions are atypical or co-occur with other pathologies. The paradigm does not replace nuanced clinical judgment from real patient interactions. Long-term error correction stability beyond 4-week follow-up is unknown, and the link between training report completeness and clinical documentation quality remains speculative given additional regulatory and communication requirements in practice.

Interval repetition: a memory consolidation mechanism based on individual forgetting parameters

Based on the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve and spaced repetition theory, adaptive scheduling optimized review timing using individual forgetting parameters (λi=0.12–0.35). This strategy enhanced short-term retention: 4-week delayed test accuracy decreased by only 4.2% (vs. 9.5% in controls, P < 0.01), and transfer gain reached 0.18 (vs. 0.07 in controls). It also accelerated skill automation (decision time reduction Δ=-6.3 s/week vs. -1.5 s/week in controls, P = 0.008) and inhibited error solidification (secondary misjudgment rate 12.3% vs. 21.8% in controls, P < 0.001), with improved confidence calibration (Brier score 0.181 vs. 0.219, P < 0.05).

Crucially, these are short-term gains only. Evidence for long-term retention (e.g., 6–12 months post-training) or sustained clinical performance is lacking; the 4-week follow-up is insufficient to support claims about lifelong learning. Individual forgetting parameters were calibrated in a single-institution sample, limiting generalizability to undergraduates or practicing clinicians. Furthermore, automated diagnostic skills in training may not directly translate to clinical efficiency, as real-world decision-making involves more than pattern recognition.

Practical implications for educators

Educators can adopt and extend this AI-driven teaching system through three key steps:① System Integration: Connect the four-core-module framework to existing clinical imaging databases (e.g., PACS) and teaching platforms, retaining the “cognitive diagnosis + adaptive scheduling” core to avoid redundant development.② Targeted Adaptation: Adjust difficulty weight parameters (α/β/γ) and confusion pair libraries based on curriculum objectives (e.g., undergraduate foundational training vs. postgraduate specialized training).③ Clinical Linkage: Expand the macro-level ability profile to include clinical case types (e.g., emergency imaging, chronic disease follow-up) and integrate real-world error cases to enhance practical relevance. For resource-limited institutions, modular deployment (e.g., prioritizing the explainable guidance module for junior learners) is feasible to balance effectiveness and cost.

Conclusion

This study presents a three-tier “micro–meso–macro” teaching architecture with four AI functional modules in medical imaging education. Course-level RCTs and 2 × 2 factorial experiments demonstrate that AI-driven interventions enhance learning outcomes, efficiency, cognitive load management, and skill transfer, with both “explainable guidance” and “adaptive scheduling” yielding independent and interactive benefits. Limitations include a single-institution master’s student sample, reliance on synthetically perturbed cases, residual teacher effects, and transfer tests not fully reflecting complex clinical scenarios. Future work should optimize prompt timing through micro-randomized interventions, expand AI interpretability strategies, and advance cross-language and cross-cultural adaptation to improve global applicability and clinical relevance.

Educators can adopt and extend the AI-driven teaching system through three key steps: ① System Integration: Connect the four-core-module framework to existing clinical imaging databases (e.g., PACS) and teaching platforms, retaining the “cognitive diagnosis + adaptive scheduling” core to avoid redundant development; ② Targeted Adaptation: Adjust difficulty weight parameters (α/β/γ) and confusion pair libraries based on curriculum objectives (e.g., undergraduate foundational training vs. postgraduate specialized training); ③ Clinical Linkage: Expand the macro-level ability profile to include clinical case types (e.g., emergency imaging, chronic disease follow-up) and integrate real-world error cases to enhance practical relevance. For resource-limited institutions, modular deployment (e.g., prioritizing the explainable guidance module for junior learners) is feasible to balance effectiveness and cost.
