Overlapping mycoses in a case of Seborrheic dermatitis with coexistent Malassezia folliculitis
Authors: Jayaprakash Sasikumar, Shankar Prasad Das, Manjunath M. Shenoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Seborrheic dermatitis (SD) is a chronic inflammatory skin condition likely caused by several factors, including overgrowth of the lipophilic yeast Malassezia, increased sebaceous gland activity, and a potential genetic predisposition, whereas Malassezia folliculitis (MF) is a fungal infection of hair follicles. In this case, a 22-year-old male initially presented with erythematous papules on the malar region and was clinically diagnosed with SD. Following partial improvement with topical and oral antifungals, the patient developed new pustular lesions involving the malar and chin regions, raising suspicion of MF. The absence of comedones and cysts helped rule out Acne vulgaris. Potassium hydroxide mount from pustular swabs revealed numerous fungal spores, prompting further investigation. Culture on modified Leeming and Notman agar yielded yeast colonies, which appeared as budding yeast cells under light and fluorescence microscopy with Calcofluor white stain. Molecular identification using PCR for the ITS regions and RFLP analysis confirmed the isolate as Malassezia furfur. This case highlights the clinical progression from SD to MF, underscoring the pathogenic potential of Malassezia and the importance of integrating clinical, microscopic, and culture-based molecular diagnostic approaches for accurate diagnosis and targeted treatment in dermatological mycoses.

Introduction

Seborrheic dermatitis (SD) and Malassezia folliculitis (MF) are common inflammatory skin disorders that predominantly affect sebaceous gland-rich regions of the body. Seborrheic dermatitis is a chronic relapsing dermatosis characterized by erythematous patches and plaques with greasy scales, commonly involving the scalp, face, and upper trunk [1]. In contrast, Malassezia folliculitis is an acneiform eruption characterized by monomorphic follicular papules and pustules resulting from fungal proliferation within hair follicles [2]. Although both disorders have distinct clinical manifestations, they share several epidemiological and pathogenic features, including a strong association with lipophilic yeasts of the genus Malassezia and a predilection for areas with high sebaceous activity.

Malassezia folliculitis (MF) is frequently misdiagnosed as acne vulgaris (AV) because both conditions present follicular papules and pustules. However, in contrast to AV, MF presents in the trunk, chest, and back, and is distinguished by the absence of comedones [3]. Consequently, many patients experience delayed diagnosis and inappropriate treatment before the underlying fungal etiology is identified. Diagnosis is commonly established through clinical examination supported by direct microscopic detection of fungal elements, while culture and species-level molecular identification are rarely performed in routine practice. Among the recognized species, Malassezia furfur is one of the most frequently implicated in human skin diseases and has been associated with both seborrheic dermatitis and Malassezia folliculitis [4].

Increasing evidence suggests that multiple Malassezia-associated dermatoses can coexist in the same individual. Previous reports have described the simultaneous occurrence of Malassezia folliculitis with other fungal skin conditions, particularly in patients with altered local or systemic immune responses [5]. Factors such as increased sebum production, disruption of the skin barrier, corticosteroid exposure, and underlying inflammatory dermatoses have been proposed to facilitate excessive Malassezia proliferation and disease progression [6]. However, reports describing the coexistence of seborrheic dermatitis and microbiologically confirmed Malassezia folliculitis remain limited, especially those supported by culture and molecular identification of the causative species.

Here, we report a case of seborrheic dermatitis complicated by the subsequent development of facial Malassezia folliculitis caused by Malassezia furfur in a young adult male. The diagnosis was established through microscopy, culture on modified Leeming and Notman agar, and molecular confirmation using ITS-PCR followed by restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis. The case is of particular clinical interest because the follicular lesions became evident during treatment for seborrheic dermatitis, highlighting the possibility of overlapping Malassezia-associated dermatoses and the need to consider concurrent fungal conditions in patients who develop recurrent or persistent papulopustular eruptions despite therapy. Furthermore, this report provides microbiological evidence supporting the role of M. furfur in coexisting facial dermatoses and emphasizes the importance of tailoring antifungal treatment strategies to address multiple Malassezia-mediated disease manifestations when present.

Case presentation:

A 22-year-old adult male presented to the Department of Dermatology, Venereology, and Leprosy, Yenepoya Medical College, Mangalore, India, with complaints of itching and redness localized to the malar region of the face, as well as complaints of dandruff associated with scalp itching. A detailed clinical examination was performed, and based on the characteristic distribution and morphology of the lesions, a differential diagnosis of Acne vulgaris and Seborrheic Dermatitis was considered. The patient was prescribed a treatment regimen comprising topical Tretinoin 0.025% for comedolytic action; short-term use of a topical drug containing a combination of Clotrimazole and Beclomethasone to address potential fungal colonization and inflammation; and Itraconazole SB 100mg, an oral antifungal containing Itraconazole.

After adhering to the prescribed therapy, the patient reported improvement in scaling and itching. However, he revisited the outpatient department with new complaints of recurrent erythematous papules with pustules, involving both the malar and chin regions (Fig. 1A and B). The new presentation raised concerns about a possible secondary infection or a coexisting dermatological condition. Close examination of the lesions revealed the absence of comedones, nodules, and cystic eruptions, effectively ruling out acne vulgaris from the differential diagnosis. The pattern and inflammatory nature of the lesions suggested a possible follicular involvement. Skin scappings and pustular swabs were collected for microscopic examination to investigate the etiology further. A potassium hydroxide (KOH) mount prepared from the sample revealed the presence of numerous fungal spores, pointing toward a fungal etiology. Given these findings, the patient was advised to discontinue Tretinoin, as it could aggravate irritation and potentially mask fungal involvement. To isolate and identify the causative organism, clinical samples of scappings from scaly facial lesions and swabs from pustules were collected and cultured on modified Leeming and Notman agar (mLNA), a lipid-rich medium optimized explicitly for the growth of lipophilic yeast species. After an incubation period, visible colonies were observed in the samples, which were subsequently examined under both conventional light and fluorescence microscopy. Calcofluor white (a fluorescent dye that binds to chitin and cellulose in fungal cell walls) staining was employed to enable better visualization. Both microscopy techniques revealed budding yeast cells (Fig. 2A and B), suggesting the presence of Malassezia species. For molecular confirmation, genomic DNA was extracted from the cultured colonies. The DNA samples were subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis, and a distinct band of approximately 560 base pairs was observed, corresponding to the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region, using ITS3 and ITS4 primers, characteristic of Malassezia furfur (Fig. 3A). A restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis was performed using the AluI restriction enzyme to strengthen identification. The digestion profile obtained from the patient-derived isolate was compared with that of a known M. furfur control strain. The resulting restriction band patterns were identical, confirming that the isolate was Malassezia furfur (Fig. 3B). Discussion

Malassezia folliculitis, formerly known as Pityrosporum folliculitis, is an inflammatory disorder resulting from the proliferation of lipophilic Malassezia yeasts within hair follicles. Although increasingly recognized, MF remains substantially underdiagnosed because its clinical appearance closely resembles acne vulgaris and other papulopustular dermatoses [2,3]. The disease typically presents as monomorphic follicular papules and pustules affecting sebaceous gland-rich areas, including the face, chest, back, and upper arms. A distinguishing feature repeatedly emphasized in the literature is the absence of comedones, which helps differentiate MF from acne vulgaris.

The present case is clinically significant because it demonstrates the coexistence of seborrheic dermatitis (SD) and laboratory-confirmed Malassezia folliculitis (MF) caused by Malassezia furfur. Both disorders are closely associated with Malassezia species and predominantly affect sebaceous gland-rich regions of the skin. Previous studies have shown that increased Malassezia colonization correlates with disease severity in seborrheic dermatitis, supporting the role of these yeasts in disease pathogenesis [7]. The occurrence of multiple Malassezia-associated dermatoses in the same patient has also been documented. For example, concomitant Pityriasis Versicolor and Malassezia folliculitis have been reported in immunosuppressed individuals receiving long-term azathioprine and prednisolone therapy, suggesting that alterations in host immunity can facilitate the development of overlapping Malassezia-mediated diseases [8]. A similar mechanism may have contributed to the clinical presentation observed in the present case. Following the diagnosis of seborrheic dermatitis, the patient was treated with a topical combination containing Clotrimazole and Beclomethasone. While Clotrimazole targets fungal growth, Beclomethasone is a medium-potent corticosteroid that reduces inflammation through local immunomodulatory effects. Although the treatment duration was relatively short, corticosteroids are known to suppress local immune responses and may create a favourable microenvironment for fungal proliferation. Previous studies have identified corticosteroid exposure as a potential risk factor for the development or exacerbation of Malassezia folliculitis, particularly in susceptible individuals [5]. Therefore, it is possible that the underlying Malassezia colonization associated with seborrheic dermatitis, combined with corticosteroid-mediated local immunosuppression, contributed to the emergence of follicular infection in this patient.

Another important aspect of this case is the definitive identification of M. furfur through culture and molecular characterization. In routine clinical practice, MF is frequently diagnosed based solely on clinical findings, whereas species-level identification is rarely performed. The successful isolation and molecular confirmation of M. furfur in this case provide strong microbiological evidence supporting its role in the observed lesions and contribute valuable data regarding species-specific disease associations. In conclusion, this case highlights the potential coexistence of multiple Malassezia-associated dermatoses in a single patient and underscores the importance of considering MF in patients with seborrheic dermatitis who develop recurrent papulopustular lesions despite treatment. Careful clinical evaluation, awareness of potential predisposing factors such as corticosteroid exposure, and appropriate mycological investigations are essential for accurate diagnosis. Furthermore, treatment strategies should account for the possibility of overlapping Malassezia-mediated conditions to ensure effective management and prevent disease persistence or recurrence.
