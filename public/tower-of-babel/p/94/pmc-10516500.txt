Climate amelioration, abrupt vegetation recovery, and the dispersal of Homo sapiens in Baikal Siberia
Authors: Koji Shichi, Ted Goebel, Masami Izuho, Kenji Kashiwaya
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The dispersal of Homo sapiens in Siberia and Mongolia occurred by 45 to 40 thousand years (ka) ago; however, the climatic and environmental context of this event remains poorly understood. We reconstruct a detailed vegetation history for the Last Glacial period based on pollen spectra from Lake Baikal. While herb and shrub taxa including Artemisia and Alnus dominated throughout most of this period, coniferous forests rapidly expanded during Dansgaard-Oeschger (D-O) events 14 (55 ka ago) and 12 to 10 (48 to 41 ka ago), with the latter presenting the strongest signal for coniferous forest expansion and Picea trees, indicating remarkably humid conditions. These abrupt forestation events are consistent with obliquity maxima, so that we interpret last glacial vegetation changes in southern Siberia as being driven by obliquity change. Likewise, we posit that major climate amelioration and pronounced forestation precipitated H. sapiens dispersal into Baikal Siberia 45 ka ago, as chronicled by the appearance of the Initial Upper Paleolithic.

INTRODUCTION

Understanding the process of Homo sapiens’ first dispersal across Europe and Asia remains a major objective in paleoanthropological science. The current prevailing model postulates that this event occurred during a succession of millennial-scale warm phases of the last glacial period, 45 to 40 thousand calendar years (ka) ago, immediately following an intense cold phase that caused a decline in resident Neanderthal populations (1–3). At odds with this view, however, are local proxy records from some of the earliest European Upper Paleolithic sites, suggesting a cold-phase dispersal (4–5), as well as new claims of migrations occurring as early as 54 ka ago (6–7) and even earlier (8).

Far to the east in Siberia, the initial migration of H. sapiens is interpreted to have occurred about 45 to 40 ka ago, with the direct dating of a modern human femur at Ust’-Ishim (9) and the appearance of Initial Upper Paleolithic (IUP) industries at archaeological sites like Kara-Bom in the Altai Mountains; Kamenka A, Podzvonkaia, Makarovo-4, and Varvarina Gora in the Lake Baikal region; and Tolbor-4 and Tolbor-16 in neighboring northern Mongolia (10–14). The IUP site cluster in the Baikal region is especially noteworthy, in that it attests to the importance of this area in the early migration of H. sapiens despite a much earlier appearance in Southeast Asia and southern China (15). We hypothesize the appearance of the IUP in this area is the product of a rapid eastward dispersal from southwestern Asia that eventually reached as far as northern China (14, 16–18), an event separate from the earlier eastward dispersal across southern Asia. Establishing the climatic and environmental context of this event in Siberia is key to interpreting why and how H. sapiens successfully extended their range to include all of the Eastern Hemisphere’s varied environments.

Recent regional paleoenvironmental reconstructions of marine isotope stage (MIS) 3, when H. sapiens with “modern” anatomy and genetics first migrated into central Eurasia, have been conducted primarily through analysis of lacustrine sediments [e.g., (19–20)]. Lake Baikal’s diatom record indicates a 50-ka warm signal for early MIS 3 (21), and pollen and diatom records from neighboring Lake Kotokel indicate climate amelioration ~45 to 42 ka ago and again ~35 to 31 ka ago (22). Corresponding biological responses to climate change between Lake Baikal and northern Europe during MIS 3 confirm persistent teleconnections (23); however, it is still difficult to consider them in the context of millennial-scale climate variability derived from the North Atlantic, particularly Heinrich and D-O events because of poor dating or low time resolution in previous studies.

In this study, we provide new palynological data from sediments taken from the southern part of Lake Baikal (influenced by the upper Selenga River basin; Fig. 1), specifically the 113-m-long Baikal Drilling Project 99 (BDP-99) core from Posolskoe Bank, analyzing the upper 15.3 m of the core to reconstruct detailed environmental change during MIS 3. We then present a chronological analysis of 272 radiocarbon dates (265 previously published, seven new) from 53 Upper Paleolithic and five Middle Paleolithic sites in the Baikal region spanning MIS 3 and early MIS 2, pairing traditional methods of analyzing large radiocarbon datasets with a kernel density model. The combination of these analyses permits us to consider the influence of environmental change on H. sapiens dispersal and settlement, from the onset of the IUP to the last glacial maximum (LGM).

RESULTS

Vegetation history

Figure 2 presents the lithology and age model for the BDP-99 core (table S1). The precision of an age model derived from peak-matching grain size fluctuations depends on the interval of grain size analysis. In the BDP-99 core, the uncertainty for ages between 20 to 32 ka ago and 38 to 62 ka ago is small, less than a few hundred years, but for ages between 32 to 38 ka ago and 64 to 81 ka ago, it exceeds more than a thousand year because of the inclusion of missing core sections. Nonetheless, it is possible to correlate the core’s record at the level of millennial events during the earlier time periods. Figure 3 presents the core’s pollen and spore spectra (table S2) by MIS, with the boundary age between each MIS based on Lisiecki and Raymo (24). Pollen concentrations ranged from 3 to 104,000 grains cm−3 and were more than 10,000 grains cm−3 in the 14.3- to 14.1-m, 8.9-m, and 0.7- to 0-m sections. In the 3.0- to 1.7-m section, however, only pollen concentration was plotted because the pollen counts were less than 200 grains. The pollen accumulation rate (PAR; Fig. 4) is very similar to the fluctuation of the pollen concentration. The regression curve using the locally weighted least squares error method with 20% correlates well with the LR04 stack (24). The concentration and accumulation rate of pollen of Lake Baikal reflect the amount of vegetation cover in the watershed (25). Thus, the fluctuation of vegetation cover corresponds well with global climate change, although the vegetation data also express short-term regional climate fluctuations.

Overall, nonarboreal pollen (NAP) was dominantly detected through the core, with percentages reaching 93% of the total pollen count in the 12.8-m section but decreasing to less than 10% in MIS 1 (middle and late Holocene) sections. Artemisia pollen was the most dominant, with percentages up to 70% among NAP. Then, Asteraceae excluding Artemisia (17%) and Chenopodiaceae (16%) are most common.

Amounts of coniferous pollen among the arboreal pollen were more than 40% of the total in the 14.3- to 14.1-m section of MIS 5.1, the 10.2-m and 8.1- to 7.3-m sections of MIS 3, and the 0.7- to 0-m section corresponding to the Holocene. During these periods, Pinus subg. Diploxylon (mostly Pinus sylvestris) was dominant, making up more than 40% during the middle and late Holocene. It was detected to be at 27% in the 10.2-m section, and three peaks of more than 5% were seen in the 8.1- to 7.3-m section of MIS 3. Pinus subg. Haploxylon (including Pinus sibirica and Pinus pumila) reached more than 40% during MIS 5 and the middle Holocene. Furthermore, there were three peaks reaching more than 10% during MIS 3. Picea remarkably increased in the layers around 8-m depth and the early Holocene. It also increased during MIS 5.1, with percentages reaching 15%. Abies pollen was detected at levels above 1% during MIS 5.1 and up to 5% in the early Holocene. Larix pollen was detected at levels less than 4% in MIS 5.1 and the early Holocene. Broad-leaved pollen increased in the latter half of MIS 4 and also from the latter half of MIS 3 to the former half of MIS 2, with percentages reaching 50%. This pollen group notably decreased when coniferous pollen dominated during MIS 5 and the middle Holocene. Alnus was the most dominant among the broad-leaved pollen, and it dominated in MIS 4 to 2, with percentages up to 40%. Betula undiff. (cf. Betula alba type identified by Russian palynologists) was detected at levels of approximately 5 to 10% during MIS 5.1 up to the early part of MIS 2. There were two peaks of this pollen reaching 20% during the early and middle Holocene. Betula nana type (dwarf-type Betula) was about 5 to 10% except for the Holocene, and the fluctuation was similar to that of B. undiff. The PAR tended to be high in layers where the frequency of coniferous pollen, such as Pinus and Picea, was large, and tended to be low when herb pollen, such as Artemisia and Chenopodiaceae, increased. Pollen taxa that are currently absent in the upper Selenga region such as Tsuga and Tilia were detected at levels less than 8%, and these pollen types could be attributed to reworked pollen from older deposits because those pollen grains were generally damaged (20). The reworked pollen increased from MIS 4 to 2, during glacial periods, and remarkably decreased in MIS 5.1 and 1, during interglacial periods.

Paleolithic radiocarbon chronology

We first analyzed the entire assemblage of Paleolithic radiocarbon dates for the Baikal region (Fig. 5) and then separately analyzed the more specific chronologies of the Cisbaikal and Transbaikal subregions (Figs. 6 and 7). Our results are presented in three ways: (i) the sum of radiocarbon dates by millennium, (ii) the sum of dated occupations per millennium [following (26)], and (iii) a kernel density estimation (KDE) model. The first two analyses allowed the inclusion of finite and infinite dates; the KDE model allowed the inclusion of only finite dates.

The results of the entire Baikal analysis present some important trends (Fig. 5, A to C; see also fig. S1). The suite of dates represents an unbroken sequence of human occupation from 44 to 21 ka ago but with several “peaks” and intervening “valleys” evident. Most obvious are peaks that recur in all three analyses at ~42, 35, 31, 26, and 22 ka ago, with intervening valleys, especially ~39, 27, and 24 ka ago. The earliest peak at ~42 ka ago is coincident with the beginning of the Upper Paleolithic, the IUP, which persisted as a complex from ~44 to 40 ka ago, as represented by occupations, for example, at Podzvonkaia, Kamenka, Tolbaga, Makarovo-4, and Varvarina Gora (table S3). This is followed by a sharp decline in radiocarbon-dated occupations between ~40 and 38 ka ago and then a long period of steady early Upper Paleolithic (EUP) occupations from ~38 to 28 ka ago, again peaking ~31 ka ago, with characteristic occupations being Priiskovoe (layer 2), Gerasimova-1, and the Bol’shoi Naryn sites. After this, occupation intensity drops gradually, reaching a nadir ~27 ka ago but with sites like Igeteiskii Log-1 and Voennyi Gospital attesting to continued human occupation of the region. The subsequent peak ~26 to 25 ka ago is largely a product of the extensive Mal’ta site and its various archaeological features; however, even when Mal’ta is excluded, the radiocarbon distribution still documents persistent human occupation during this time. The sequence ends with a minor peak ~22 ka ago, representing 13 different cultural layers typically represented by just one or two dates; this signals the start of the late Upper Paleolithic (LUP).

Differentiating the chronologies of the Cisbaikal (Fig. 6, A to C) and Transbaikal (Fig. 7, A to C) regions indicates some important similarities and differences. Their radiocarbon distributions share an IUP peak ~42 to 40 ka ago, with the available evidence suggesting a possibility of more IUP occupations in the Transbaikal region. A second EUP peak in the Transbaikal record occurs ~35 to 34 ka ago, but it is not evident in the Cisbaikal record. Both share a middle Upper Paleolithic (MUP) peak at ~31 ka ago; however, it is much more obvious in the Cisbaikal record, being well represented at sites like Bol’shoi Naryn-1 and 2, Buret’, Cheremushnik (layer 8), Gerasimova-1 (layer 2), Tuiana, and Ust’-Ëdarma-2 and 3 (table S3). Another important difference between the two records is temporal placement of their latest peaks: For the Cisbaikal, it is immediately before the LGM, ~26 to 25 ka ago, being obviously dominated by Mal’ta but still represented by nine other MUP sites; for the Transbaikal, it is immediately after the LGM, ~22 ka ago, including LUP cultural layers at Mamakan-2, Sokhatino-4, Studenoe-1 and 2, and Ust’-Menza-2 (table S3).

DISCUSSION

Climate and environmental change during MIS 3 and early MIS 2 in Baikal Siberia

A comparison of the pollen diagram from the BDP-99 core, the δ18O curve (27), and orbital variations (28) is shown in Fig. 8. Expansion of coniferous taxa, which, in the past, has been observed during major interglacial periods such as MIS 11 and 5.5, indicates warm climate conditions in southern Siberia (20, 29). The pollen spectra reported here from Posolskoe Bank show expansions of coniferous elements not only since 10 ka ago (the Holocene) but also during 78 to 72 ka ago, 53 ka ago, 45 to 41 ka ago, and 14 ka ago (Fig. 8). These millennial-scale increases in conifers also indicate rapid climate amelioration. The initial peak in coniferous taxa occurs during MIS 5.1. In addition, from our age model, the peak at 53 ka ago and three peaks during 45 to 40 ka ago of coniferous pollen potentially correlate to D-O 14 and 12 to 10, respectively. Conifer expansion around 14 ka ago suggests a relationship with D-O 1 (Bølling-Allerød period), as previously reported (22, 30). Conversely, almost the only time herb or sparse vegetation communities expanded around Lake Baikal was under severe climate conditions during the LGM (20, 22). It is judged that warming was most remarkable during D-O 14 and 12 to 10, with the exception of events during interglacial periods of MIS 5 and 1.

The abrupt forestation events found during D-O 14 and 12 to 10 were consistent with orbital obliquity maxima. Obliquity dominates high-latitude total insolation because the effect of obliquity on insolation increases with latitude, while precession dominates low-latitude insolation (31). The Lake Baikal and upper Selenga region are situated at a high-enough latitude (ca. 48° to 58°N) to expect obliquity-driven climate changes, and this is actually confirmed by physical and biological data from Lake Baikal (19). Most recently, the abrupt expansion of coniferous trees is consistent with the obliquity maxima during the Holocene (Fig. 8), as previously mentioned (19). Before this, for MIS 3, the expansion of coniferous trees during D-O 14 and 12 to 10 was notably greater than any other D-O events in the region. This indicates that climate and vegetation during MIS 3 were strongly driven by obliquity, as shown in Fig. 8.

In Europe during MIS 3, the development of temperate forest, which is dominant at present, differed between southernmost European latitudes below 40°N and at latitudes above 40°N during specific D-O events (32). At latitudes above 40°N, forest development was stronger during D-O 14 and 12 than other D-O events. These D-O 14 and 12 developments were synchronous with maxima in obliquity, which promoted increased annual temperatures (33). Simultaneously, a near precession maximum during these two events (Fig. 8) may have brought wetter conditions to higher latitudes (32). These orbital elements hence likely enhanced expansion of coniferous forests in the region.

In the Baikal region during MIS 3, tundra or steppe vegetation was typically dominant (Fig. 3), indicating cool and dry conditions. Expansions of coniferous trees ~53 ka ago and 45 to 40 ka ago, however, indicate remarkable climate amelioration, as noted above. Specifically, expansion of Picea indicates warm and humid conditions after melting of mountain glaciers and/or permafrost (34–35). In particular, the rapid and long-term expansion of Picea in D-O 12 indicates the most humid conditions during MIS 3, and the expansion of P. subgen. Haploxylon also suggests warm and humid conditions. Coniferous trees continued to dominate with the two brief declines 45 to 40 ka ago of D-O 12 to 10, indicating the warmest and most humid conditions in the region during MIS 3.

To provide a more accurate depiction of the climate conditions around 45 ka ago, we applied the LegacyClimate 1.0 dataset to the BDP-99 core for this time. This dataset, developed by Herzschuh et al. (36), uses the modern analog method for the Northern Hemisphere. By applying this method, we were able to calculate that the annual precipitation and the mean temperature of July (the warmest month) were approximately 370 mm and 16°C, respectively. When compared to the current climate in the Baikal region, the annual precipitation remained nearly at the same level as it is today, but the mean temperature of July was lower by approximately 3°C. From this, we infer that humid climate conditions ~45 ka ago were sufficient to support the expansion of the forest-steppe vegetation, thereby facilitating human dispersal.

In other Baikal studies, climate amelioration also has been shown during a peak of Cyclotella ocellata, Cyclotella bodanica, and Aulacoseira ambigua, as well as total diatom concentration in Lake Kotokel 44 to 40 ka ago (18), and an increase in total diatom concentration associated with D-O 12 recorded from Buguldeika Saddle, which was influenced from Selenga River inflow (21). This period of extraordinary climate amelioration has been widely observed elsewhere across Siberia, for example, through forest-steppe and coniferous forest development in the lower Yenisei River basin and southern Angara River valley [e.g., (37–38)], larch woodland development in the Indigirka Lowland (39), expansion of pine trees in Alut Lake of northeastern Siberia (39), and the formation of a strong chernozem (i.e., the Karginsk soil) in western Siberia (40–41).

Following 40 ka ago, reduction in forest and increase in steppe and tundra elements indicate that climate became cooler and drier. Pollen spectra between ~25 and 18 ka ago were not calculated because of low pollen concentrations, so little can be said about vegetation and climate during this interval, the LGM, but other diatom and pollen records in the Baikal region indicate extreme cold and continued arid conditions (21, 35).

The pollen record from Posolskoe Bank reported here likely indicates vegetation response for the upper Selenga catchment including the Transbaikal and northern Mongolia, although we consider it to primarily reflect vegetation change near Lake Baikal. Judging from modern vegetation distributions, steppe and forest-steppe mainly covered the upper Selenga catchment during D-O 12 to 10, while coniferous trees expanded their distribution near Lake Baikal during the period. Nonetheless, the period of 45 to 40 ka ago is characterized by the most stable climate and vegetation conditions in the Baikal region during MIS 3.

Human dispersal and settlement during MIS 3 and early MIS 2 in Baikal Siberia

Precise impacts of MIS 4 and early MIS 3 environmental change on Middle Paleolithic populations of the Baikal region currently cannot be addressed, given the small sample and issues in dating sites of such antiquity, despite the growing sample of known sites dating to this time (42–44). However, the climatic optimum of MIS 3 documented here, 44 to 40 ka ago, coincides with the appearance of the IUP in the Baikal region (fig. S3). Technologically, Baikal’s IUP is characterized by (i) flat-faced parallel cores prepared for the unidirectional and bidirectional production of long, slender blades, (ii) detaching of small blades from a larger blade using a burin-like technology, (iii) dorsal retouching of blades into distinctive points often with proximal ventral thinning, and (iv) production of bone tools and items of personal adornment (45–51). These features tie Baikal’s IUP to the important 44.5-ka-ago IUP industry preserved at Kara-Bom in the Altai Mountains, southwestern Siberia (11, 52–53), as well as other IUP industries in southwestern Asia and southeastern Europe [e.g., (2, 54–57)].

Knowing precisely who, as in which hominin, manufactured the IUP in these regions is still an open question. Although at Bacho Kiro Cave, Bulgaria, the earliest skeletal remains of modern H. sapiens in Europe have been recovered from an IUP cultural occupation dating to at least 45 ka ago (58), such associations have not been made in southwestern Asia, Siberia, and Mongolia. Nonetheless, the isolated human femur dating to 45 ka ago from Ust’-Ishim (with no associated archaeological material) in western Siberia yielded an ancient genome of a modern human (9), providing evidence that H. sapiens had arrived in the region by the time of the appearance of the IUP in southern Siberia and Mongolia. As argued by Zwyns (57), the synchronicity of the IUP and Ust’-Ishim modern human is likely not a coincidence.

The long-term, stable environmental conditions of D-O 12 to 10 not only facilitated human migration eastward from southwestern Asia, across central Asia, and into the Baikal region of Siberia ~45 to 44 ka ago but also appear to have sustained IUP settlement in the Cisbaikal and Transbaikal until 40 ka ago. The forest-steppe environment would have promoted a rich ecotone for these early hunter-gatherers, as further suggested by diverse faunal assemblages recovered from most of Baikal’s IUP sites [e.g., (47, 59)]. This may also explain why the southern Baikal region, which during MIS 3 was sandwiched between more homogeneous life zones to the south (inner Asian steppe) and north (taiga), today contains such a dense concentration of IUP sites and why neighboring central Mongolia to the south saw equally early IUP occupation (14).

The data presented here indicate that the spread of H. sapiens in central Eurasia was not a response to the marked Campanian Ignimbrite volcanic eruption in Italy or successive cooling of Heinrich event 4; instead, it predates these events by several thousand years [e.g., (2, 55)] and is more closely associated with intense warming of D-O 12 to 10. The origin point and route (or routes) of dispersal are still not known, but given the strong techno-typological similarities between the Siberian IUP and western Eurasian IUP, dispersing humans likely moved via inner Asia’s mountainous zone from the Levant and Iran in the southwest to the Siberian Altai in the northeast (Fig. 9) (14, 16–17). On the one hand, our results suggest that, at least for the Baikal region, climate amelioration may have directly facilitated H. sapiens dispersal into an otherwise normally cold, arid region; on the other hand, our results add to our understanding of the adaptive flexibility of early H. sapiens, as they expanded into the diverse environments of the European and Asian land masses during MIS 3.

The hypothesized dispersal of Homo sapiens.

This dispersal is defined by initial Upper Paleolithic industries, originating from southwestern Asia and extending into Europe in the west and Siberia in the east.

After the obviously strong IUP pulse of human occupation in the Baikal region 44 to 40 ka ago, the area saw continued human occupation 40 to 33 ka ago, except with some variation in the density of dated Upper Paleolithic occupations. Especially noteworthy is a dearth of EUP occupations 40 to 38 ka ago, coincident with the marked decrease in forest vegetation at Lake Baikal and concomitant increase in steppe vegetation indicative of the onset of cool, dry conditions immediately following D-O 9 and persisting through late MIS 3. After this, the number of EUP occupations climbs steadily, reaching a minor peak ~35 ka ago, perhaps an indication of gradually recovering early H. sapiens populations. Sites like Bol’shoi Naryn-1 and Gerasimova-1 in the Cisbaikal and Kamenka, Khotyk-3, and Tolbaga in the Transbaikal, likely representative of occupational palimpsests (fig. S3), chronicle this continued human occupation of the region.

The chronological model shows another influx in the number of radiocarbon-dated occupations ~31 ka ago, for both Cisbaikal and Transbaikal, potentially relating to the warm event recorded in the vegetation record of Lake Kotokel (22). This period, which Siberian paleoecologists have labeled the Lipovsko-Novoselovo interstadial (60), is represented by numerous MUP occupations in the Baikal region, for example, Sedova and Ust’-Ëdarma-2 and Ust’-Ëdarma-3 in the Cisbaikal and Chitkan (layer 4), Kunalei, and Priiskovoe in the Transbaikal (table S3). Lithic industries are characterized by a variety of primary-reduction strategies including subprismatic to flat-faced core-and-blade technology, discoidal flake technology, and microcore and bladelet technology, used to produce unifacial tools including scrapers, gravers, notches, burins, and expedient flake tools (61).

MIS 2 in the Baikal pollen record is marked by a lack of pollen spectra, which is the result of low pollen concentration in sedimentation on the Posolskoe Bank, 26 to 17 ka ago, coincident with full-glacial conditions of the LGM. On the basis of other pollen records, however, the Baikal region during this time became open and almost treeless, characterized by sparse tundra-steppe vegetation and the expansion of permafrost [e.g., (22, 35)]. When comparing the herb pollen spectra for the LGM in Cisbaikal and Transbaikal, however, Kobe et al. (62) found that Transbaikal was notably drier than Cisbaikal, with the Poaceae-enriched grassland of Cisbaikal potentially sustaining the mammoth-steppe fauna. Human populations persisted, too, especially in Cisbaikal where some of the largest MUP settlements interpreted as residential base camps [not only Mal’ta but also possibly Alekseevsk, Buret’, and Igeteiskii Log-1 (63–64)] continued to be occupied right up to the LGM, 26.5 to 24.5 ka ago. Although our chronological model does not reveal a hiatus in human occupation of the Baikal region during the height of MIS 2 [contra (65–68); see also (69)], there are still strong signs of post-LGM “rebound” of human populations in both Cisbaikal and Transbaikal starting 23 ka ago, as climate ameliorated and LUP industries marked by microblade technologies became prevalent in the region (e.g., Krasnyi Iar, layer VIc; Sokhatino-4, layers 8 and 10; and Studenoe-2, layer 4/5) (66, 68, 70).

Overall, throughout the time considered here, 44 to 22 ka ago, the radiocarbon models for Cisbaikal and Transbaikal show general conformity, except that, overall, in Transbaikal, there is less density of radiocarbon-dated occupations and identified peaks in the model are typically more muted, potentially an indication of more severe aridity and more open steppe conditions in Transbaikal throughout much of late MIS 3 and MIS 2. The chronological records of the IUP for both regions, especially Transbaikal, are among the strongest measured in our analysis, contrary to expectations of models of taphonomic bias against oldest events in a radiocarbon chronology (71). The IUP represents a special time for the Baikal region, one in which optimal climatic conditions facilitated rapid human dispersal and sustained settlement.

Thus, detailed pollen records for the past ~80 ka from southern Lake Baikal demonstrate vegetation response to D-O events in the Lake Baikal and upper Selenga regions, the strongest being the rapid expansion of coniferous forest during D-O 14 and 12 to 10. Forest-steppe and steppe vegetation also expanded in the upper Selenga catchment of the Transbaikal and northern Mongolia during this climatic maximum of MIS 3, and its effect was likely also felt in the Angara and Lena valleys of Cisbaikal. These warmer and more humid conditions of D-O 14 and 12 to 10 resulted from maximum of obliquity and precession affecting high-latitude Eurasia above 40°N. Although we hesitate to suggest that these orbital elements ultimately facilitated the eastward dispersal of H. sapiens from southwestern Asia into Siberia and Mongolia 45 to 40 ka ago, with the evidence presented here, we do conclude that the consequent warm, humid climate of the time proximately created new ecological opportunities facilitating the rapid human expansion expressed by the sudden appearance of the IUP in the Baikal region. Further clarification of orbital and suborbital climate and environment changes in central Eurasia will require paleoenvironmental data from additional lakes and paleolakes in Russia, Mongolia, and China, and additional Paleolithic chronology building using state-of-the-art pretreatment techniques will be needed to more fully document and explain early H. sapiens settlement across greater northern Asia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Lake Baikal and the upper Selenga region

Lake Baikal, which is the deepest lake in the world, belongs to the Baikal Rift Zone of southeastern Siberia and is surrounded by the Khamar-Daban, Barguzin, and Baikalsky mountain ranges (Fig. 1). There are more than 300 rivers that flow into Lake Baikal, and the largest is the Selenga River, which contributes ~50% of the total annual river inflow into the lake (23). The Selenga and its tributaries drain the western Transbaikal (the Uda, Khilok, and Chikoi rivers) and north-central Mongolia (the Orkhon, Tuul, and Eg rivers) regions. Lake Baikal drains into the Angara River, which flows north through the Cisbaikal and eventually empties into the Enisei River and Arctic Ocean. Within Lake Baikal, Posolskoe Bank is the prominent underwater ridge that forms the boundary between its southern and central basins. The deposition of sediment on Posolskoe Bank is controlled by river input from the Selenga River, although the bank is separated from the Selenga’s delta slope by a trough and is located beyond the zone directly affected by Selenga riverine sediment load (Fig. 1) (72). The sediment of Posolskoe Bank contains preserved records of basin evolution since the mid-Miocene (72).

Climate around Lake Baikal and the upper Selenga region is strongly continental with severely cold and dry winters resulting from the Siberian Anticyclone. The Inner Asiatic Depression, however, brings warm summers with hot air from China and Mongolia. Meteorological data in Ulan-Ude, located 100 km southeast of the Selenga River mouth, indicate average temperatures of −25.4°C in January and 19.4°C in July and mean annual precipitation of ~250 mm (73). Annual precipitation rates for the lake’s coast and adjacent uplands range from ~400 to 500 mm, while those of its lowlands and southern area are only ~200 mm (73).

Modern vegetation communities in the region include forest, forest-steppe, and steppe, with coniferous forest dominating the slopes of the mountain ranges. Deciduous coniferous forest of Larix gmelinii is typical in the northern and northeastern coastal area of Lake Baikal (74), while evergreen coniferous forest composed mainly of P. sibirica, Abies sibirica, and Picea obovata is prevalent in the southern mountainous areas of the lake (75). Coniferous forest dominated by L. sibirica with P. sibirica and A. sibirica characterize the mountainous area of the Transbaikal region east of the lake. On relatively dry positions of ridges, Larix is mixed with P. sylvestris. Steppe composed mainly of Poaceae and Asteraceae and P. sylvestris forest-steppe occur in the upper Selenga basin to the south, as well as in valleys and depressions of the Barguzin River basin to the north (30).

Paleolithic archaeological research has a long history in the Baikal region, starting with the initial discovery and recognition of Pleistocene-aged artifacts at Voennyi Gospital 150 years ago (76). Since then, numerous Upper Paleolithic sites have been investigated, for example, the famous Mal’ta site, initially excavated in 1928–1931 and located in the southern Angara valley (70). In addition, clusters of sites have been excavated in both the Cisbaikal (Irkutsk city, the Bratsk Reservoir, and upper Lena valley) and Transbaikal regions (Vitim, Chikoi, Khilok, and Ingoda valleys; Fig. 1C). This work has led to the development of a tripartite organization of Upper Paleolithic chronology, with an EUP phase starting with the IUP and spanning from before 40 to ~33 ka ago, an MUP phase ~33 to 22 ka ago, and an LUP phase ~22 to 12 ka ago (65). In this study, we focus on the EUP and MUP, the cultural phases spanning much of MIS 3 and early MIS 2, with particular attention paid to the very beginning of the Upper Paleolithic (i.e., the IUP). The preceding Middle Paleolithic of the Baikal region is not well established, with only a few documented sites with corresponding radiometric dates (e.g., Igeteiskii Log III, layer 4; Shchapovo-1, layer 6; and Baruin-Alan 1, Slavin Iar, and Khotyk-3, layer 5/2; table S3).

Palynological analysis

We used the 113-m-long BDP-99 core, which was drilled in winter 1999 from the Posolskoe Bank (52°05′23″N, 105°50′24″E) at a water depth of 201 m by the BDP members (Fig. 1B). We analyzed the upper 15.3-m part, which, by diatom analysis, includes sediments since MIS 5 (77). The lithological profile based on semiquantitative observations by smear slide (Fig. 2) shows high contents of clay particles with some terrigenous silt and sand (72). The core also includes some diatom-rich layers. The upper part was not affected by turbidite, although deposition was mainly controlled by input from the Selenga River (65).

We used the age model for the BDP-99 core (78), which was established by linear interpolation of peak-matching results of the grain size fluctuations and insolation curve at 65°N in June (79) based on three age-control points from the diatom assemblage (72, 77). According to the model, the age at a depth of 15.3 m (the lowest part of this segment of the core) was estimated to be 81.4 ka (Fig. 2).

We prepared subsamples for pollen analysis every 2 cm and analyzed them at about 4-cm intervals (a ~220-year interval between the analyzed levels). The 2-cm3 samples were processed using a treatment technique including potassium hydroxide, heavy liquid separation by an aqueous solution of zinc chloride, hydrofluoric acid, and acetolysis mixture procedures (80). To calculate pollen concentration (grains cm−3) and PAR (grains cm−2 year−1), a known concentration of microspheres (81) was added to the samples before treatment. The extracted fossil pollen and spores were mounted on microscope slides, and >200 pollen grains were counted per sample using a transmitted light microscope with magnifications of 400× or 630×. The percentage values per taxon were based on the total pollen count, and the pollen diagram was drawn using Tilia software (82). The percentages presented here represent the relative amounts of each pollen taxon with respect to the total pollen count, including both shrubs and conifers.

Archaeological radiocarbon analysis

The objective of the radiocarbon analysis was to interpret temporal variability in the intensity of cultural occupations in the Baikal region by measuring the probability density of the radiocarbon sample and considering it in relation to regional paleoenvironmental proxy records. We conducted three sets of analyses, the first using the full suite of radiocarbon dates for all of Baikal (Fig. 5), the second using only dates from Cisbaikal (Fig. 6), and the third using only dates from Transbaikal (Fig. 7).

We assembled 282 radiocarbon dates from 58 archaeological sites in the greater Baikal region (table S3), ranging in age from 47,800 ± 3500 to 17,390 ± 220 radiocarbon years ago (14C BP), for complete coverage from the earliest radiocarbon-dated site in MIS 3 to the end of the LGM, early MIS 2. The majority of the radiocarbon dates used were compiled from existing published sources, with seven new dates being published here (table S5). In addition to the seven new dates, we have included nearly 200 dates that were measured since the late 2000s, many of which were subjected to the ultrafiltration or XAD pretreatment methods. Therefore, we argue that our results are robust, although we have included earlier-obtained dates with larger SDs (18, 68–69). We excluded ten of the dates from the analysis because they were not from cultural layers or were obviously inconsistent with their stratigraphic contexts or with other dates obtained from the same context. A 10th date, from Kurla-3 (24,060 ± 5700 14C years BP), had such a large SD that at two sigma, it was meaningless to the analysis and therefore also excluded. Of the remaining 272 dates, 267 provided chronological data for 72 cultural layers and/or loci identified at 53 Upper Paleolithic sites, while five dated five cultural layers at five Middle Paleolithic sites.

Calibration of the 272 dates was hampered by the presence of 14 infinite dates in the sample. For the finite dates, we conventionally calibrated them in OxCal v4.4 (83) using the IntCal20 curve (84) to generate means, two-sigma ranges, and medians (table S3). For the infinite dates, we simply used the IntCal20 curve to estimate their correlative minimum ages in calendar years. We did not calculate mean ages for cultural layers with multiple dates, given their oft-poor agreement when applying the R_Combine command in OxCal 4.4. Poor agreements at these EUP and MUP sites likely relate to recognized cultural layers representing variably-aged palimpsests of human activity or mismeasures from conventional dating of contaminated bone or large samples of charcoal (69). Given well-known issues of dating soil organics and humic acids, we also reran the analysis without these dates; results are largely the same and presented in fig. S1.

For graphic representation of the resulting chronology, we created three correlative charts. First, we summed individual dates in batches of 1000 years, from 50,000 to 21,000 cal BP so that every accepted radiocarbon date in the sample was counted (Figs. 5A, 6A, and 7A). Second, we calculated the number of dated occupations per millennium (26) to neutralize the impact of sites with numerous synchronous dates (e.g., Gerasimova-1 and Mal’ta), but, at the same time, capturing the full range of ages for potential palimpsests spanning multiple millennia (e.g., Kamenka Complex A and Tolbaga), a common issue in dating Siberian Upper Paleolithic sites [e.g., (18)] (Figs. 5B, 6B, and 7B; for comparison, fig. S2 also presents the first and second graphs pooled in larger 2000-year intervals, for the entire Baikal sample of dates). To calculate the number of dated occupations, we simply tallied the number of calibrated median ages per millennium, so that if a certain occupation’s suite of dates all fit within a single millennium, then it was counted once, but if the individual occupation’s median dates fell across two millennia, then it was counted twice, once per millennium represented. This method of pooling calibrated median ages also allowed us to include infinite dates in the resulting charts (Figs. 5B, 6B, and 7B). Third, we used the KDE_Model command [KDE_Model(“site_name”, N(01), U(0,1))] in OxCal 4.4 to produce a kernel density distribution chart of the finite dates (Figs. 5C, 6C, and 7C). The KDE_Model command applies the Silverman bandwidth estimate (84) to calculate a kernel density distribution graphically depicting trends in the distribution of the date series, reducing “noise” resulting from the radiocarbon-calibration process that often leads to extreme peaks and valleys in a summed-probability analysis (85). OxCal code for running the KDE model is presented in table S4. In each of the resulting charts, the kernel density distribution is shown in dark gray, overlaying the correlative standard summed-probability distribution in light gray. This combination of approaches ensured a more balanced representation, minimizing potential skewing of the data due to multiply-dated sites and permitting inclusion of infinite dates.

The peaks and valleys evident in the resulting charts are used as proxies for occupation intensity, rather than solely for chronological distribution. While a higher peak in the distribution indicates a higher intensity of occupation for the region, a valley implies a less intensive occupation. While the peaks and valleys might be linked to various factors (e.g., regional population density or geomorphic conditions of site preservation), in our research context and based on our data, we interpret them directly as markers of episodes of human activity at archaeological sites and only indirectly as reflections of population density or demographic trends.
