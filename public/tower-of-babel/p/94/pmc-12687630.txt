An interdisciplinary approach in teaching RNA secondary structure prediction to first-year undergraduate students using an automated deep learning RNA 3D model prediction tool
Authors: Kamanasish Bhattacharjee, Adi Idris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe use of artificial intelligence (AI) in biologics drug design is interlaced into the fabric of the drug discovery pipeline for many in the biotechnology industry. The use of AI tools in RNA therapeutic drug design has gained traction in recent years to develop more effective therapeutics in a short period of time, revolutionizing rapid-response therapeutics. Indeed, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) are streamlining RNA therapeutic design in ways we never thought were possible just a decade ago. These advances are accompanied by a plethora of new AI tools for drug design that continue to barrage the research space at unprecedented speed. As biology educators, we bear the responsibility for keeping up with technological advances in the biotechnology space, as it is up to us to prepare and equip the next generation of scientists with the use of AI platforms in this space. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapeutic design remains a complex challenge, despite several of them being currently in clinical use for various genetic diseases. The application of AI and ML models can predict potent and longer-lasting siRNA drug candidates for therapeutic development. Additionally, it is imperative that siRNA candidates are screened for their propensity to form secondary structures, as this can reduce targeting efficacy and result in unwanted immune responses. Though siRNA technology is commonly taught at the undergraduate level across life sciences disciplines, there remains a disconnect between the use of AI and siRNA design in the teaching curriculum. We previously described an innovative approach for teaching students the use of a generative AI tool called Biomod AI to design siRNAs. Here, we designed an inquiry-based non-wet-lab workshop for students to explore the use of an automated DL-based RNA 3D structure prediction tool called trRosettaRNA to determine the secondary structures of siRNAs. Importantly, the interdisciplinary design of this activity amalgamates both AI and RNA science concepts in a simplified format in a single workshop tailored for first-year health sciences undergraduate students.

INTRODUCTION

The rise of RNA-based therapeutics and the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in RNA drug design demand a rethinking of how university curricula prepare future scientists for this emerging field. Reforming tertiary-level healthcare education to include AI and ML is becoming increasingly essential (1).

Interest in RNA 3D structure prediction has grown due to the discovery of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs); their functions are influenced by their secondary and tertiary structures. To address this, Wang et al. (2) developed trRosettaRNA, a deep learning (DL)-based tool that predicts RNA 1D and 2D geometries and generates full-atom 3D models via energy minimization. This free online tool (https://yanglab.qd.sdu.edu.cn/trRosettaRNA/) has become valuable for RNA researchers. While DL in RNA structure prediction is still developing (3), tools like trRosettaRNA and newer ones like RhoFold (4) offer accessible options for those without advanced coding skills, making them suitable for undergraduate teaching.

Traditionally, RNA structure prediction has been confined to advanced mathematics (5) and biochemistry (6) students. Current life sciences curricula lack exposure to AI-driven RNA tools. To address this, we designed an interdisciplinary in silico workshop for first-year undergraduates, co-led by AI and RNA experts. This is, to our knowledge, the first such hands-on use of a DL RNA tool at the introductory undergraduate level.

PROCEDURE

Rationale

We designed this workshop as part of the first-year undergraduate course (Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Healthcare), offered under the B.Tech in Health Sciences and Technology program at Vellore Institute of Technology (VIT), India. Integrated into the final module, “Contemporary Issues of AI in Healthcare”, the student lecture series, AI in Biotechnology, introduces AI-based RNA therapeutic design. The interdisciplinary curriculum blends engineering, humanities, computer science, and life sciences to foster innovative thinking from the outset. To complement theoretical content, a hands-on session on AI and RNA therapeutics was introduced to enhance conceptual understanding. While these students have been introduced to AI and ML concepts early in the semester, the workshop is designed to be accessible to those with a first-year level understanding of biology.

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) silence genes by binding to target mRNAs, but their efficiency can decline if they form secondary structures (7). To address this, our workshop focused on predicting the 3D structures of siRNAs using trRosettaRNA. We selected siRNAs from the Chen et al. (8) study, as they determined 2D siRNA secondary structures using Mfold (9), a widely known nucleic acid folding and hybridization prediction tool. Therefore, following an authentic inquiry-based learning approach (10), students can explore whether siRNA 3D predictions by trRosettaRNA align with Mfold-generated 2D structures.

We began with an introductory lecture on siRNA therapeutic design, followed by an in silico session using trRosettaRNA. Each student was randomly assigned an anti-sense siRNA sequence from the Chen et al. (8) study. As we have 51 students, some students were assigned a duplicate siRNA sequence. Unbeknownst to them, five of the 31 sequences were known to form secondary structures via Mfold. Students submitted their sequences to trRosettaRNA, and during the 15–30 min processing time, we explained the DL algorithm behind the tool (2) via a mini-lecture. At the end of the session, the five siRNAs that form secondary structures are revealed to the students before they are asked to complete in-session inquiry-based questions to reflect on and discuss their findings.

Expected outcome

The students will correctly predict siRNA 3D structures with high confidence using trRosettaRNA. We expect that DL-generated 3D siRNA structures may not be the same as those predicted by Mfold.

Student findings

A list of siRNA sequences from the Chen et al. (8) study can be found in Supplemental material S1. siRNAs that do not form secondary structures as determined by trRosettaRNA are depicted as a circular 2D (example shown in Fig. 1). Out of the five siRNA sequences that Chen et al. (8) predicted using Mfold to form secondary structures, only one siRNA sequence (ITPR2-1 (IDT)) was predicted to form the same secondary structure by trRosettaRNA (Fig. 2). On the other hand, there were siRNAs predicted by Mfold not to form secondary structures that were predicted to form secondary structures by trRosettaRNA (i.e., IDH1-B-direct, IDH1-B-design-2, IDH1-1 (IDT), IDH1-3 (IDT), and TRIM28-1 (Sigma)) (Fig. 3). The difference in data output is likely because trRosettaRNA and Mfold fundamentally differ in how they predict the secondary structure.

Student feedback

Feedback was collected via an online form (Supplemental material S2), with 24 of 51 students responding (47.1% response rate; Supplemental material S3). The response was overwhelmingly positive, whereby 95.8% found the workshop useful, 75% found it easy to follow, and 87% rated the technical content as appropriate. Notably, 95.9% reported improved understanding of the AI model behind trRosettaRNA. While over 60% found the tool easy to use, 37.5% found it somewhat complex but manageable. All respondents agreed the workshop improved their understanding of RNA structure prediction and recommended it to peers. Many described the session as “interactive” and “engaging,” and requested similar workshops on other therapeutic RNAs like mRNA.

CONCLUSIONS

The integration of AI in RNA therapeutic design is rapidly advancing (11). It is incumbent that tertiary educators are in line with these trends. We used trRosettaRNA to expose students to both AI and RNA design concepts in a single session hands-on interdisciplinary workshop, who have had no prior exposure to RNA structure prediction tools (>95%, Supplemental material S3). Building on this, we plan to merge this workshop with another we recently developed using the generative AI tool called Biomod AI (12), enabling students to design and validate siRNAs in a complete in silico workflow.

One limitation is that trRosettaRNA may not perform well with novel RNAs, including synthetic siRNAs (e.g., siRNAs or mRNAs). Another DL model, ATOM-1, may have a better capability to predict therapeutic RNA structures such as siRNAs and antisense oligonucleotides (ASO) (13). Unlike trRosettaRNA, ATOM-1 is trained on data obtained through in-house chemical mapping experiments. However, neither an online server platform nor code sources are yet publicly available for ATOM-1. The other limitation is the trRosettaRNA server load capacity (i.e., the volume of queries the server can handle). Generally, it takes 15–30 min for a query to be resolved by the trRosettaRNA server. But, when all the 51 students submitted their queries simultaneously, it took longer to generate the results. The best speculation is that the remote server is facing scalability issues. In future workshops, we can access the source codes of trRosettaRNA and deploy them on a local server or local machine so that dependency on the internet strength and scalability issues of the remote server can be bypassed. Another limitation is that the students were not previously exposed to recurrent neural network (RNN) or Transformer concepts in the course. RNN and Transformer are DL models that handle sequential data. During the workshop, the need for these concepts and knowledge became evident, which will be added in the revised version of the course syllabus.

Though RNA secondary structure biology is taught at the undergraduate level (14), our approach is unique in that students get to experience how this is done using an automated DL platform. Moreover, our workshop provides simple theoretical RNA biology and AI technological concepts in the form of lecture sessions sandwiched in the in silico session. Beyond siRNA secondary structures, efficacy is also shaped by features of the target site and mRNA architecture (15). Future workshops could focus on target mRNA structure to inform siRNA design. Moreover, trRosettaRNA can also be adapted to screen ncRNA structures in advanced genomics and bioinformatics undergraduate courses. We also plan to deploy a summative assessment post-completion of the workshop to help us evaluate student understanding of secondary RNA structures and to reveal where they may need additional support. In a fast-evolving biotechnology world shaped by AI, emerging AI tools offer opportunities for fellow educators to amalgamate AI and drug design concepts into their teaching practices.
