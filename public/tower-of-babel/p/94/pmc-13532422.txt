The High‐Sensitivity C‐Reactive Protein−Albumin−Lymphocyte Index Is a Prognostic Marker for All‐Cause and Cardiovascular Mortality in Patients With ST‐Elevation Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Xinuo Ma, Wei Zhang, Ningli Zhang, Qinghua Zhong, Tao Shi, Sirui Yang, Fazhi Yang, Yujuan Peng, Lixing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundInflammation and immune dysfunction may worsen the prognosis of patients with ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).AimsThis study aimed to assess whether the high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein–albumin–lymphocyte index (hsCALLYI) could be used to predict the prognosis of STEMI patients who received emergency percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) treatment.MethodsParticipants were categorized into three groups based on hsCALLYI tertiles. To assess the prognostic significance of hsCALLYI in predicting both all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed, followed by Cox proportional hazards regression modeling and time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsA lower level of hsCALLYI demonstrated a significant correlation with elevated mortality risk from all causes (hazard ratio [HR] = 9.826, 95% CI = 3.091–31.235, p < 0.001) and cardiovascular mortality (HR = 15.035, 95% CI = 3.353–67.408, p < 0.001). The area under the ROC curve of hsCALLYI for all‐cause mortality was 0.696 (p < 0.05), and the area under the ROC curve of hsCALLYI for cardiovascular mortality was 0.695 (p < 0.05).ConclusionhsCALLYI emerged as a significant independent prognostic indicator for both all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in STEMI patients undergoing emergency PCI.Keywords: albumin, hs‐CRP, inflammation, lymphocytes, prognosis, STEMI

Introduction

ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remains one of the leading causes of death globally, with an increasing prevalence annually [1]. Estimates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study suggest that the total number of disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) attributable to ischemic heart disease (IHD) has risen steadily since 1990, reaching 182 million (95% UI: 1.7 to 194 million) DALYs and 9.14 million (95% UI: 8.40 to 9.74 million) deaths in 2019 [2]. In China, due to the prevalence of unhealthy lifestyles among the Chinese population, a large population base, and accelerated population aging, it is estimated that there are currently about 330 million patients with CVD in the country, of whom 11.39 million have coronary heart disease (CHD) [2, 3]. Therefore, early diagnosis and accurate prognostic prediction are crucial for improving patient prognosis.

Acute STEMI is primarily attributable to the rupture of atheromatous plaques, resulting in the obstruction of at least one coronary artery and the formation of an intravascular thrombus. This leads to the onset of myocardial ischemia, and as the infarcted area expands further, myocardial injury or necrosis ultimately manifests clinically as chest discomfort. The most effective treatment is reperfusion therapy [1]. The role of inflammation in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and the changes in diagnostic findings during the onset and recovery of STEMI are highly important in this context. Although the Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) and Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) risk scores are useful in assessing the prognosis of myocardial infarction (MI) [4, 5], the complexity of their calculation process hinders their efficiency and limits their wider clinical use. Chronic low‐grade inflammation, which is central to the pathogenesis and prognosis of coronary artery disease, also suppresses hepatic albumin (ALB) synthesis. Consequently, serum ALB serves not only as a nutritional marker but also as an indirect indicator of systemic inflammatory burden [6]. In previous studies, several markers of inflammation, including ALB, high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hs‐CRP), the lymphocyte count (LYM), the white blood cell count (WBC), the C‐reactive protein/albumin ratio (CAR), and the lymphocyte‐to‐C‐reactive protein ratio (LCR), are effective in prognosticating patients with STEMI [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. However, to date, no study of combined high C‐reactive protein, LYM, and ALB levels has been able to confirm the predictive utility of prognosis in STEMI patients. Accordingly, the present study was a retrospective investigation into the impact of LYM, high C‐reactive protein, and ALB, a composite inflammatory‐national index, on the prognosis of patients with STEMI.

Methods

Study Population

This retrospective cohort analysis enrolled 1341 STEMI patients undergoing urgent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) within 24 h of symptom onset, all of whom were treated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University during the period from June 2018 to January 2023. A diagnosis of STEMI was made when the patient presented with acute chest pain (or equivalent signs or symptoms) and persistent ST‐segment elevation (or ST‐segment elevation equivalent) on an electrocardiogram (ECG) [1]. Patients with significant valvular cardiac pathology, atrial fibrillation, decompensated heart failure, peripheral arterial disease, other serious diseases such as malignancy, hematological disorders, autoimmune diseases, or severe liver or kidney disease, patients with missing clinical data, and patients lost to follow‐up were excluded. The final analytic cohort consisted of 761 patients with complete hsCALLY data.

Data Collection

During the initial hospitalization period, a range of general demographic data, including age, sex, heart rate (HR), body mass index (BMI), systolic/diastolic blood pressure, and the Gensini score, was collected. Additionally, medical history data were gathered, encompassing information regarding smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, previous stroke, previous atrial fibrillation, and previous heart failure. Information regarding the use of any medication, including statins, aspirin, and P2Y12 receptor antagonists, was also obtained. A bedside ECG was then immediately performed to obtain the patient's ECG data on admission. In addition, before emergency PCI, the patient was admitted to the emergency department where blood was immediately drawn from the anterior elbow vein and were transported to the clinical laboratory of the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University for quantitative assessment of multiple biomarkers, including myoglobin (Mb), cardiac troponin (cTnI), creatine kinase isoenzyme (CK‐MB), brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), WBC, LYM, red blood cells (RBC), hs‐CRP, ALB, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TB), creatinine (Cr), fasting blood glucose (FBG), urea, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), triglycerides (TG), and D‐dimer (DD) levels. The patient was subsequently admitted to the cardiac catheterization operating room at the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University. There, an experienced senior cardiologist performed a coronary angiogram to obtain information on the extent of the lesion, its location, the degree of stenosis, the site and number of stents to be implanted, as well as a pre‐procedure TIMI grading. The first loading dose of aspirin and clopidogrel was administered as soon as possible before PCI, following the latest guidelines. PCI is performed according to the coronary angiographic findings of each patient. Successful and complete revascularisation, residual stenosis of less than 20%, and intraprocedural TIMI flow grade 3 were defined as successful stenting. All patients were advised to follow the recommended regimen of aspirin (100 mg/day) and P2Y12 inhibitor (75 mg/day) indefinitely following the procedure. After the procedure, transthoracic echocardiography was performed by a senior sonographer to obtain information on the diameter of the left ventricle during end‐diastole (LVEDD), systolic function as represented by left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), thickness of the interventricular septum, as well as the internal dimensions of both left and right atria.

Follow‐Up and Endpoint Events

The median follow‐up duration was 1053 days (IQR: 659–1466), with a maximum follow‐up of 1984 days. The present investigation primarily relied on telephone‐based follow‐up assessments, which were conducted with either participating patients or their designated family members. In cases where contact could not be established, the observation period was terminated based on the most recent clinical documentation available for each participant. This study had a primary endpoint of all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular death.

Calculation of High‐Sensitivity C‐Reactive Protein−Albumin−Lymphocyte Index (hsCALLYI)

hsCALLYI was calculated as ALB (IU/L) × LYM (10^9/L)/hs‐CRP (mg/L).

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analyses in this investigation were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0), MedCalc software, and R version 4.4.0. Based on the 33rd and 66th percentile thresholds of hsCALLYI measurements, study participants were stratified into tertiles for comparative analysis: Group 1 (hsCALLYI < 7.03), Group 2 (7.03 ≤ hsCALLYI < 32.51), and Group 3 (hsCALLYI ≥ 32.51). Continuous data were presented as mean values with standard deviations for normally distributed parameters, while non‐normally distributed variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical data were reported as frequency counts accompanied by corresponding percentages. The baseline characteristics across the three groups were evaluated using appropriate statistical methods: continuous variables with normal distribution were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA, while nonparametric data were examined using the Kruskal−Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared through χ 2 tests. To investigate the relationship between hsCALLYI levels and mortality outcomes, survival probabilities were estimated using Kaplan−Meier methodology, with between‐group differences evaluated by log‐rank testing. The predictive performance of hsCALLYI for both all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality was quantified by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Preliminary screening of potential covariates was conducted through univariable Cox regression, with statistically significant variables (p < 0.05) subsequently incorporated into multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to determine the independent prognostic value of hsCALLYI for mortality endpoints. To evaluate the incremental prognostic value of hsCALLY beyond established clinical indicators, we constructed two nested Cox proportional hazards models: Base model: GRACE score, LVEF plus glomerular filtration rate (GFR); Extended model: Base model plus hsCALLY (as a continuous variable). Model comparison was performed using the likelihood ratio test. Discrimination was assessed by C‐index, and reclassification improvement was evaluated using time‐dependent continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Sensitivity analyses were performed by modeling hsCALLYI as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines and as a continuous linear term. A significance threshold of α = 0.05 was applied for all statistical tests.

Results

Survival Analyses Based on hsCALLYI

To evaluate the prognostic value of the hsCALLYI in patients with STEMI, we conducted a Kaplan–Meier analysis. Figure 1 shows that among STEMI patients with all‐cause mortality, the low‐hsCALLYI subgroup had the highest risk of death, the intermediate‐hsCALLYI subgroup had the second highest risk of all‐cause mortality, and the high‐hsCALLYI subgroup had the lowest risk of death (log‐rank χ 2 26.012, p < 0.0001). Figure 2 shows that, among STEMI patients with cardiovascular mortality, those in the low‐hsCALLYI subgroup presented the highest cardiovascular mortality, whereas those in the intermediate‐hsCALLYI subgroup presented the second highest risk of cardiovascular mortality. Conversely, the high‐hsCALLYI subgroup presented the lowest risk of cardiovascular mortality (log‐rank χ 2 20.882, p < 0.0001).

hsCALLI Is an Independent Predictor of All‐Cause and Cardiovascular Mortality

Univariate and Multifactorial Cox Analysis in STEMI Patients

Univariate Cox proportional risk analysis identified several significant predictors of both all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality among STEMI patients, including age, sex, SBP, the number of stenotic vessels, the Gensini score, the use of aspirin, the international normalized ratio (INR), AST, TB, Cr, and FBG. Notably, hsCALLYI levels also emerged as an independent prognostic factor in this high‐risk population.

As demonstrated in Table 2, a multivariate Cox proportional risk analysis of all‐cause mortality, adjusted for age, sex, SBP, number of stenotic vessels, Gensini score, aspirin, DD, INR, AST, TB, Cr, and FBG, revealed that age (HR = 1.059, 95% CI = 1.027–1.092, p < 0.001), aspirin (HR = 15.872, 95% CI = 5.297–47.557, p < 0.001), INR (HR = 2.172, 95% CI = 1.035–4.555, p < 0.05), and hsCALLYI (using the high‐hsCALLYI group as a control, low‐hsCALLY group: HR = 9.826, 95% CI = 3.091–31.235, p < 0.001; intermediate‐hsCALLY group: HR = 4.617, 95% CI = 1.417–15.042, p < 0.05) emerged as an independent prognostic indicator for all‐cause mortality among individuals diagnosed with STEMI.

Cox Proportional Risk Model in Patients With STEMI

Three adjusted models were used for Cox multivariate analysis. In Model 1, the adjusted covariate was age; in Model 2, the adjusted covariates were age and sex; and in Model 3, the adjusted covariates were age, sex, DD, AST, TB, FBG, and LDL‐C. In Model 3, the risk of all‐cause mortality in the low‐hsCALLYI group was 4.949 times greater than that in the high‐hsCALLYI group (95% CI = 1.841–13.300, p < 0.05); the risk of all‐cause mortality was 3.947 times greater in the intermediate‐hsCALLYI group than in the high‐hsCALLYI group (95% CI = 1.427–10.915, p < 0.05) (Table 4). As shown in Table 5, when the high‐hsCALLYI group was used as a reference, in Model 3, the risk of cardiovascular mortality in the low‐hsCALLYI group was 6.079 times greater than that in the high‐hsCALLYI group (95% CI = 1.921–19.231, p < 0.001); the risk of cardiovascular death in the intermediate‐hsCALLYI group was 5.614 times greater than that in the high‐hsCALLYI group (95% CI = 1.743–18.080, p < 0. 05).

Predictive Ability of the hsCALLYI for All‐Cause and Cardiovascular Mortality in STEMI Patients

To investigate the predictive value of the hsCALLI for all‐cause and cardiovascular deaths in STEMI patients, we generated ROC curves. These curves demonstrated that hsCALLYI exhibited superior predictive value compared with ALB, LYM, and hs‐CRP. As illustrated in Figure 3, the AUCs for all‐cause mortality according to the ROC curve for ALB, LYM, hs‐CRP, and hsCALLYI were 0.630 (p < 0.05), 0.544 (p < 0.05), 0.683 (p < 0.05), and 0.696 (p < 0.05), respectively. As illustrated in Figure 4, in the ROC curves for cardiovascular mortality, the AUCs for ALB, LYM, hs‐CRP, and hsCALLI were 0.638 (p < 0.05), 0.549 (p < 0.05), 0.684 (p < 0.05), and 0.695 (p < 0.05), respectively.

Incremental Prognostic Utility Analysis

To evaluate the incremental prognostic value of hsCALLY beyond established clinical indicators, we constructed two nested Cox proportional hazards models. The base model included GRACE score, LVEF, and GFR, while the extended model additionally incorporated hsCALLY as a continuous variable.

The extended model demonstrated a C‐index of 0.826 (95% CI: 0.784−0.867), which was higher than that of the base model of 0.794 (95% CI: 0.745−0.843), with a ΔC‐index of +0.031, indicating improved discriminative ability after adding hsCALLY. The likelihood ratio test showed that the extended model was significantly superior to the base model (χ 2 = 21.73, p < 0.001), confirming that the addition of hsCALLY significantly improved model fit. Time‐dependent reclassification analyses revealed that at 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year follow‐up, the continuous NRI values were 0.340, 0.385, and 0.406, respectively, indicating that hsCALLY correctly reclassified 34.0% to 40.6% of patients. The corresponding IDI values were 0.496, 0.524, and 0.555, further confirming that hsCALLY significantly enhanced model discrimination (Supporting Information S2: Table 1).

hsCALLY provides significant incremental prognostic value beyond GRACE score, LVEF, and renal function, and may serve as a useful complementary biomarker for risk stratification in STEMI patients.

Sensitivity Analyses

As shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure 1, the restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association between hsCALLYI and all‐cause mortality (p for nonlinearity < 0.001). When modeled as a continuous linear term, each 1‐unit increase in hsCALLYI was associated with a 3.6% lower risk of all‐cause mortality (HR 0.964, 95% CI: 0.940−0.988, p = 0.003) and a 4.1% lower risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.959, 95% CI: 0.932−0.988, p = 0.005) (Supporting Information S2: Table 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we introduced a new inflammatory trophic index, hsCALLYI, by combining LYM, hs‐CRP, and ALB. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the inaugural research endeavor to evaluate the predictive capacity of this composite index for both all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular death among STEMI patients undergoing urgent PCI. The analysis revealed that STEMI patients exhibiting reduced hsCALLYI levels were at significantly greater risk of both all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular fatalities. Kaplan−Meier analyses showed that low hsCALLYI was significantly associated with shorter survival; in multivariable‐adjusted Cox proportional risk analyses, hsCALLYI was an independent predictor for STEMI patients regardless of whether the endpoint event was all‐cause or cardiovascular mortality. After multivariate analyses using the three correction models, low hsCALLYI still exhibited the most pronounced susceptibility to both all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality. ROC curves showed that hsCALLYI demonstrated superior predictive accuracy for mortality outcomes in STEMI patients than LYM, hs‐CRP, and ALB.

Atherosclerotic plaque rupture is an inflammatory process. When LDL accumulates in the intima, it activates endothelial cells to express leukocyte adhesion molecules and chemokines, which promote monocyte and T‐cell recruitment. T cells in lesions recognize local antigens and acquire a Th1 phenotype through the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, which promotes local inflammation and plaque growth [13]. The increased activation of inflammation may lead to local protein hydrolysis and thrombosis, thereby triggering ischemia and infarction [13]. This process is driven primarily by innate and adaptive immune responses that modify lipoproteins and damaged vessel wall components [14]. Several lines of experimental evidence suggest that LYM reduction is associated with the development of complications and mortality after acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [15, 16, 17]. In a 2015 study, Boag et al. retrospectively analyzed LYM in 1377 UK STEMI patients discharged after PPCI, with an endpoint event of death within 1200 days of follow‐up, confirming that lymphopenia is associated with reduced long‐term survival. They also prospectively recruited 59 STEMI patients to observe the temporal evolution of leukocyte subpopulation counts and microvascular occlusion (MVO) via cardiac MRI and reported that transient depletion after reperfusion is due mainly to the loss of T cells and NK cells [18].

C‐reactive protein (CRP), a key acute‐phase inflammatory marker, is primarily produced by hepatocytes in response to proinflammatory cytokines. And can be quantified via a high‐sensitivity (hs) assay. There is compelling evidence that hs‐CRP levels are associated with a poor prognosis after AMI [11, 12, 18, 19]. In endothelial cells, CRP exerts a pro‐inflammatory effect by reducing the levels of nitric oxide and prostacyclin while increasing the levels of endothelin‐1, tissue factor, and fibrinogen activator inhibitor‐1. In monocyte–macrophages, CRP induces the secretion of tissue factors, enhances the release of reactive oxygen species and proinflammatory cytokines, promotes monocyte chemotaxis and adherence, and increases the uptake of oxidized low‐density lipoprotein (ox‐LDL), which leads to foam cell formation. Furthermore, CRP in vascular smooth muscle cells has been demonstrated to promote Ang II‐induced migration, proliferation, and remodeling of vascular smooth muscle, thereby playing a role in promoting atherosclerotic thrombosis [20, 21, 22]. Furthermore, evidence indicates that CRP facilitates LDL uptake and impedes cholesterol efflux from human macrophage‐derived foam cells [23, 24, 25]. In the initial stages of atherosclerosis, CRP has been demonstrated to influence monocyte adhesion and vascular migration. In addition, CRP is a proinflammatory factor that promotes plaque deposition in atheromatous plaques, ultimately leading to atherosclerotic lesions. Recent studies have shown that hs‐CRP stabilizes plaques and directly influences the progression of atherosclerosis [26]. Therefore, hs‐CRP can be used to identify plaque rupture in patients at high risk for significant atherosclerotic recurrence [27]. In a 2017 study of 10,061 patients with prior MI and hs‐CRP levels ≥ 2 mg/L randomized double‐blindly to low, medium, or high doses of canakinumab monoclonal antibody or placebo, with the primary efficacy endpoints of nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, or cardiovascular death, the results revealed that a significant reduction in the recurrence rates of adverse cardiovascular events in the low‐dose hs‐CRP cohort after receiving anti‐inflammatory therapy was associated with a significantly lower recurrence rate of adverse cardiovascular events [28]. Furthermore, hs‐CRP has shown greater prognostic accuracy than CRP levels obtained through conventional testing methods [29]. The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study collected two hs‐CRP measurements from over 10,000 participants across a 6−8 year observation window. After adjusting for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, individuals exhibiting elevated hs‐CRP concentrations showed a 1.51‐fold higher likelihood of developing CHD compared to that in patients with persistent low/moderate hs‐CRP levels (95% CI = 1.23–1.85) [30]. Furthermore, evidence indicates that CRP is a valid predictor of mortality independent of and in combination with cTnT in acute coronary syndrome patients [31].

Human serum albumin (HSA), which constitutes the predominant protein component in blood plasma, serves crucial physiological functions. It is a monomeric alpha‐alpha protein synthesized in hepatocytes that binds endogenous and exogenous molecules, including fatty acids, hemoglobin, and acidic drugs [32]. First, ALB binds to lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) and transports it to the liver, where it is converted to phosphatidylcholine (PC) by hepatic enzymes and finally dropped onto the surfaces of very low‐density lipoproteins (VLDLs), LDLs, and HDLs [33]. It has been demonstrated that hypoproteinemia facilitates the accumulation of LPC in LDL [34]. Furthermore, LPC levels exhibit an approximately fivefold elevation within ox‐LDL particles under hypoproteinemic conditions [35]. Second, LPC increasingly binds to erythrocytes in hypoalbuminemia, which results in decreased erythrocyte deformability and, consequently, increased whole blood viscosity. Therefore, hypoalbuminemia results in elevated levels of LPC in lipoproteins and erythrocyte membranes, which enhance the atherosclerotic properties of LDL and blood viscosity, respectively [36]. Second, ALB can influence lipid profiles by binding to free fatty acids (FFAs), which can induce hypertriglyceridemia by inhibiting FFA production from TG via the inhibition of endothelium‐bound lipoprotein lipase activity by circulating angiopoietin‐like 4 (ANGPTL4) [37]. Other functions of ALB include binding to cholesterol‐containing vesicles, which regulate cholesterol transport. This is an important factor in the pathogenesis of STEMI patients [38]. Furthermore, ALB possesses antioxidant properties and binds NO molecules, thereby inhibiting lipoxygenase‐dependent liposome and LDL oxidation and, consequently, influencing the atherosclerotic process [39]. Additionally, serum ALB has been demonstrated to inhibit platelet activation and aggregation [40]. The bioavailability of certain antiplatelet aggregation molecules, such as prostacyclin, is dependent on the ALB concentration [41].

A substantial body of contemporary research indicates that the prognosis of STEMI is associated with nutritional status [42, 43, 44]. In a retrospective cohort study, 220 patients with STEMI who underwent their first PCI within 12 h of symptom onset demonstrated an association between low serum ALB and an increased risk of worsening hospitalized chronic coronary syndrome and long‐term adverse events [44]. Furthermore, in a study of 1282 80‐year‐old community‐dwelling individuals over 4 years to ascertain the causes of death, researchers measured ALB levels at the beginning of the trial and after 4 years of follow‐up. They reported that the serum ALB level served as an independent prognostic indicator for both all‐cause and cardiovascular disease mortality in community‐based older adults of advanced age [7].

Beyond its role as an indirect inflammatory marker, serum ALB integrates hepatic synthetic function, oxidative stress buffering, and nutritional reserve, functioning as a longitudinal risk signal in CAD [6]. Lower ALB levels in STEMI patients may reflect pre‐existing chronic disease burden. The prognostic value of ALB is enhanced when combined with CRP. The CRP‐to‐ALB ratio has been shown to predict outcomes in COVID‐19 pneumonia [45], heart failure [46], and atrial fibrillation [47], and other composite indices have demonstrated value in NSTEMI [48]. Although these conditions are clinically distinct, they share common pathways of systemic inflammation and catabolic stress. Our study extends this evidence to STEMI, supporting hsCALLYI as a practical prognostic tool.

Emerging evidence from recent investigations has increasingly focused on evaluating the association of inflammatory biomarkers and nutritional indicators with clinical outcomes in cardiovascular disease. Ji et al. initially combined the CRP‐ALB‐LYM ratio to predict short‐term MACE in patients with STEMI [49]. However, no studies have combined hs‐CRP‐LYM‐ALB to assess the prognostic value of all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in patients with STEMI. Our study confirms that hsCALLYI serves as an autonomous prognostic indicator for both all‐cause and cardiovascular death among STEMI patients after PCI and that hsCALLYI predicts it better than ALB, LYM, and hs‐CRP.

Limitations

This investigation possesses several inherent limitations that should be acknowledged. Primarily, the research design was retrospective and relied on observational data, while the sample size was moderate, which may not have provided sufficient statistical power for all analyses (n = 761). Future investigations should employ multicenter prospective designs with extended follow‐up periods and larger sample sizes. Second, the laboratory data of hs‐CRP, LYM, and ALB levels of STEMI patients at the time of admission do not fully reflect the dynamic process of prognosis in STEMI patients. To enhance the study, it would be beneficial to collect laboratory data on patients on multiple occasions in the future.

Conclusion

The findings of our study indicate that hsCALLYI is an independent predictor of all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals diagnosed with STEMI. Furthermore, hsCALLYI demonstrated superior predictive efficacy compared with high C‐reactive protein levels, ALB, and LYM values.
