Imaging for Pediatric Head and Neck Emergencies: Critical Insights
Authors: Gayoung Choi, Bo-Kyung Je
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNontraumatic head and neck emergencies in children are common and often infectious, with overlapping and nonspecific clinical presentations that can delay accurate diagnosis. Imaging plays a critical role in distinguishing self-limited conditions from conditions requiring urgent intervention. This review provides an anatomically organized overview of pediatric orbital, sinonasal, oral cavity, deep neck, temporal bone, and skull base emergencies and foreign-body events. We highlighted the role of radiologists in emergent clinical decision-making, from appropriate modality selection (radiography, contrast-enhanced CT, MRI, and angiographic techniques) to the identification of key imaging findings that alter the management and recognition of common diagnostic pitfalls.Keywords: Pediatric Head and Neck Emergency, Orbital Cellulitis, Retropharyngeal Abscess, Foreign Bodies

INTRODUCTION

Pediatric patients frequently present with nontraumatic head and neck emergencies, wherein infectious etiologies are predominant (1,2). Distinct head and neck pathologies often have overlapping clinical presentations, and young children may be unable to provide a reliable history or to cooperate with examinations (3). Therefore, imaging is pivotal for a rapid and accurate diagnosis, to guide management, and to assess complications. Radiologists must be familiar with the spectrum of pediatric head and neck emergencies and their imaging features, because timely recognition can prevent serious outcomes such as airway compromise, vision loss, or intracranial spread of infection. This comprehensive review, which is organized by anatomic region, emphasizes the critical radiological findings that influence clinical decision-making. Appropriate imaging modalities, such as radiography, US, CT, and MRI, are selected based on clinical indications, with the careful consideration of radiation exposure in children. Although MRI serves as a valuable problem-solving tool, CT remains essential in many acute settings where rapid and detailed evaluation is required. The key conditions in each region are discussed with a focus on distinguishing the imaging characteristics and emergent management considerations.

ORBITAL DISEASE

PRESEPTAL AND POSTSEPTAL CELLULITIS: THE ORBITAL SEPTUM AS CLINICAL DIVIDER

The orbital septum, a fibrous connective tissue layer extending from the orbital periosteum to the tarsal plates of the eyelids, represents the critical anatomic boundary dividing preseptal (periorbital) and postseptal (orbital) cellulitis (2). Although the orbital septum is rarely visualized on imaging, its location can be inferred on contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) by drawing a line between the anteriormost aspects of the lateral and medial orbital rims to the tarsal plate level on axial images (4). This anatomic distinction has profound management implications because orbital cellulitis may result in severe complications such as abscess, visual impairment, venous thrombosis, intracranial involvement, and death (5). The disruption of the orbital septum, contiguous spread from the adjacent sinus disease, and valveless venous pathways facilitate the extension of the infection into the postseptal orbital space (5).

Preseptal cellulitis, typically arising from local skin trauma, such as insect bites or contiguous soft tissue infection, presents with eyelid and periorbital swelling anterior to the septum. On CT, inflammatory changes appear superficial to this reference line and manifest as fat stranding and soft tissue edema limited to the preseptal compartment. These cases generally respond to oral antibiotics on an outpatient basis (6,7). Conversely, postseptal cellulitis involves deeper orbital structures and mandates intravenous antibiotic therapy with potential surgical drainage of larger abscess collections (6,7).

This clinical distinction is reinforced by imaging findings. Postseptal inflammation may occur within the extraconal (external to the extraocular muscle cone) or intraconal (within the muscle cone) compartments, with subperiosteal abscesses being the most common complications (8). These rim-enhancing fluid collections appear most clearly in the coronal or sagittal planes as lenticular collections closely apposed to the orbital walls, particularly medially from the ethmoid sinusitis or superiorly from the frontal sinusitis (Fig. 1) (6,9). Critical to radiologic assessment in pediatric patients, orbital cellulitis is most commonly a sinogenic complication. Therefore, when orbital inflammation is encountered in the absence of paranasal sinus disease, alternative diagnoses such as idiopathic orbital inflammatory disease must be considered, prompting different therapeutic approaches (Table 1) (10).

Complications of orbital infections include superior ophthalmic vein thrombophlebitis extending to cavernous sinus thrombosis, epidural abscess, and subdural empyema. CEMRI is particularly valuable for detecting intracranial involvement including brain parenchymal abscesses and meningitis.

DACRYOCYSTITIS: NASOLACRIMAL DUCT OBSTRUCTION WITH RIM-ENHANCING ABSCESS

Dacryocystitis is characterized by the inflammation of the lacrimal sac secondary to nasolacrimal duct obstruction and infection. Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction occurs in up to 20% of neonates and frequently resolves spontaneously by 1 year of age (11). In older children, other causes, such as rhinosinusitis, tumors, or trauma can result in dacryocystitis (5). When obstruction persists or secondary narrowing occurs at the valve of Rosenmuller (creating a one-way valve mechanism leading to dacryocystocele formation), the risk of infection increases.

On imaging, dacryocystitis appears as an enlargement of the nasolacrimal sac extending from the medial canthus to the inferior nasal meatus with surrounding fat stranding and inflammatory changes (Fig. 2). While most cases are treated with antibiotics, underlying congenital lesions or rim-enhancing abscess formation require surgical intervention such as probing (11).

ORBITAL COMPARTMENT SYNDROME: “GUITAR PICK” SIGN INDICATING VISION-THREATENING PRESSURE

Acute proptosis, which is defined as an anterior globe protrusion from the bony orbit, is a hallmark of orbital compartment syndrome, an emergent vision-threatening condition requiring immediate recognition and intervention. To assess proptosis on axial CT or MRI, an image that includes both lenses is selected. An interzygomatic line is drawn connecting the right and left zygomatic arches, and a perpendicular distance from this baseline to the anterior margin of the globe >21 mm or asymmetry >2 mm, is indicative of proptosis (12). With proptosis, the stretching of the optic nerve and tenting of the posterior globe (“guitar pick” sign) are pathognomonic for orbital compartment syndrome (Fig. 3) (2,13). This acute vision-threatening state results from an elevated intraorbital pressure and can lead to optic nerve ischemia (13). Recognition of these findings requires immediate clinical intervention; the delayed recognition of orbital compartment syndrome results in irreversible vision loss.

Orbital compartment syndrome.

The differential diagnosis of acute proptosis in pediatric patients is broad, including infectious (orbital cellulitis and abscess), vascular (hemangioma, arteriovenous malformation, and lymphatic malformation), neoplastic (rhabdomyosarcoma, optic glioma, and neurofibromatosis type 1 optic pathway glioma), inflammatory (idiopathic orbital inflammatory disease), and traumatic etiologies (12,13). Idiopathic orbital inflammatory disease, which is not accompanied by adjacent sinus disease, may present with acute proptosis and can manifest as a unilateral or bilateral disease. MRI is particularly useful for characterizing the nature and extent of proptosis-inducing lesions.

PAPILLEDEMA: MRI MARKERS OF INTRACRANIAL HYPERTENSION

Papilledema, an elevation of the optic nerve head observed on fundoscopic examination, reflects sustained elevated intracranial pressure. The role of the radiologist is to identify the imaging signs of papilledema and investigate the potential underlying causes. MRI demonstrates the following characteristic findings: flattening of the posterior globe contour, elevation of the optic papilla, and tortuosity with dilation of the optic nerve sheath complex (14). These findings prompt the investigation of hydrocephalus, hemorrhage, neoplasm, infection, cerebral edema, venous thrombosis, and idiopathic intracranial hypertension.

Distinguishing papilledema from pseudopapilledema, which is the structural elevation of the optic disc that is unrelated to increased intracranial pressure, is essential. Optic disc drusen, visible as punctate calcifications on CT, indicates pseudopapilledema (15), averting unnecessary additional imaging and investigation.

OPTIC NEURITIS: IMAGING LOCATION AND EXTENT GUIDE DEMYELINATING DISEASE CLASSIFICATION

Optic neuritis, the inflammation of the optic nerve, presents with acute or subacute vision loss. Critically, pediatric patients more frequently present with bilateral involvement and less commonly report pain with eye movement than adults (16). CEMRI of the orbits reveals optic nerve enlargement with T2 prolongation and enhancement, often accompanied by optic nerve sheath enhancement and perineural inflammation (17,18).

The location and longitudinal extent of the optic nerve involvement narrow the differential diagnosis of the underlying demyelinating disease. The intraorbital involvement of the short (<2–3 cm) extent favors multiple sclerosis. Conversely, myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) antibody-associated disease and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder present with longer longitudinal involvement, often including chiasmal or optic tract involvement, and characteristically demonstrate bilateral disease (19,20). These distinctions directly affect the management and screening of associated demyelinating lesions on craniospinal MRI.

SINONASAL DISEASE

ACUTE RHINOSINUSITIS: CLINICAL DIAGNOSIS AND IMAGING FOR COMPLICATION ASSESSMENT

Acute rhinosinusitis in children is very common, is usually viral in etiology, and is typically self-limited (21). However, the bacterial superinfection of the paranasal sinuses can lead to severe complications that present as emergencies. The clinical signs of acute sinusitis include fever, purulent rhinorrhea, nasal congestion, and facial pain; however, younger children may only show nonspecific irritability or swelling (22). Imaging is not routinely needed for uncomplicated sinusitis; however, it is essential when complications are suspected or when clinical deterioration occurs despite appropriate therapy. Signs warranting imaging include proptosis, extraocular motility restriction, visual disturbance, severe facial edema, or neurologic signs suggesting intracranial involvement (23,24). MRI excels at detecting bone marrow edema indicating osteomyelitis, identifying intracranial complications (dural venous sinus thrombosis, epidural abscess, subdural empyema, meningitis, cerebritis, and brain abscess), and facilitating the differential diagnosis of leukemic or lymphomatous presentations mimicking sinusitis (25).

POTT PUFFY TUMOR: FRONTAL SINUSITIS COMPLICATED BY OSTEOMYELITIS AND EPIDURAL ABSCESS

Pott puffy tumor, while historically named for prominent forehead swelling, represents a serious infectious complication: frontal sinusitis with adjacent bone osteomyelitis and subgaleal or epidural abscesses (26). This entity typically emerges in adolescents after the pneumatization of the frontal sinus and carries a high risk of associated intracranial complications (50%–75% of cases) (2). Recognition on imaging, as demonstrated by frontal sinus opacification with associated osteomyelitis and overlying soft tissue or epidural abscess, requires urgent surgical drainage to prevent intracranial spread (27).

ACUTE INVASIVE FUNGAL RHINOSINUSITIS: IN IMMUNOCOMPROMISED PATIENTS

Acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis is a medical emergency, particularly in immunocompromised pediatric populations (25). Unlike indolent chronic fungal diseases, the acute invasive variant progresses rapidly and has the potential for extensive bone destruction, vascular invasion, and fatal intracranial complications (25). Causative organisms vary by immunological status: Aspergillus predominates in neutropenic patients and cases of hematologic malignancies, whereas Mucor species predominate in cases of diabetic ketoacidosis (28).

Imaging features include aggressive bony erosion or dehiscence, lack of mucosal enhancement (“devitalized mucosa” or “black turbinate” sign), periantral fat infiltration, and intracranial or vascular involvement. MRI is superior to CT for defining soft tissue extent and marrow edema (2,25,29). Critically, clinical suspicion must remain high and imaging findings of severe sinusitis with aggressive bony destruction or rapid clinical deterioration despite appropriate bacterial antibiotics warrant urgent endoscopic sampling for fungal identification (30).

JUVENILE NASOPHARYNGEAL ANGIOFIBROMA (JNA): HYPERVASCULAR MASS CENTERED AT THE SPHENOPALATINE FORAMEN

Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (JNA) typically presents in adolescent males with epistaxis and nasal obstruction (31). Although benign, this vascular neoplasm is highly vascular and locally aggressive, with frequent extensions beyond the nasopharynx. The key imaging finding is its characteristic location, centered at the sphenopalatine foramen, with widening of the pterygopalatine fossa (Fig. 4) (32). The mass demonstrates avid enhancement on both CT and MRI, with prominent vascular flow voids evident on MRI (32).

ORAL CAVITY AND AERODIGESTIVE TRACT DISEASE

LUDWIG ANGINA: EMERGENT FLOOR-OF-THE-MOUTH CONDITION

Ludwig angina is a rapidly progressive and typically bilateral cellulitis of the floor of the mouth that primarily involves sublingual and submandibular spaces (often with submental extension) and can precipitate a life-threatening airway compromise (34). The infection most commonly arises from an odontogenic source, particularly the mandibular second and third molars. When the dental root apices lie below the mylohyoid attachment, the infection can spread directly into the submandibular space and across contiguous deep fascial planes (35). Clinically, patients present with painful floor-of-mouth swelling, “woody” induration, dysphagia/odynophagia, a muffled (“hot-potato”) voice, drooling, trismus, and variable respiratory distress; adverse outcomes are largely related to delayed recognition and delayed airway control (36).

When imaging is indicated, CECT is usually the first-line cross-sectional modality in acutely ill patients, because it rapidly defines the extent of deep-space involvement, evaluates airway narrowing, and predominantly differentiates cellulitis from drainable abscesses. Typical CT findings include diffuse submandibular and sublingual edema with fat stranding, fascial thickening, loss of normal fat planes, reactive lymphadenopathy, and, when present, rim-enhancing fluid collection tracking along the deep neck spaces (Fig. 5) (35). MRI provides superior soft tissue characterization and can better assess complications, such as vascular thrombosis, osteomyelitis, and mediastinal extension; however, its use is often limited in unstable children because of longer acquisition times and the frequent need for sedation. From a decision-making standpoint, imaging should address three questions: 1) is there an imminent threat to the airways? 2) is there a drainable abscess versus predominant cellulitis/phlegmon? 3) which deep spaces require surgical access?

TONSILLAR INFECTION: ABSCESS VERSUS PHLEGMON DETERMINING DRAINAGE

In children, acute pharyngotonsillar infection is predominantly viral and self-limited, and imaging is reserved for cases where clinical progression raises concerns of suppurative disease (37). Group A Streptococcus is the most common bacterial etiology and requires antibiotic treatment to prevent local and systemic complications (2).

The principal role of CECT is to differentiate a drainable peritonsillar abscess from a non-drainable inflammatory phlegmon, because this distinction directly affects the management (Fig. 6). On CECT, acute infectious tonsillitis typically presents with enlarged tonsils and/or adenoids demonstrating a striated (“tigroid”) pattern of enhancement, reflecting inflamed lymphoid tissue with interspersed edema (38,39). Tonsillar phlegmon appears as an ill-defined region of low attenuation, higher than that of simple fluid, without a well-formed enhancing rim, which is consistent with inflammatory infiltration rather than suppuration. Progression to a peritonsillar abscess is suggested by a rim-enhancing fluid collection adjacent to or within the tonsil, with surrounding parapharyngeal inflammatory changes and potential airway narrowing (37,38,39).

In addition, CECT is essential for assessing the extent of the disease, particularly its spread beyond the pharyngeal constrictor muscles into the parapharyngeal or retropharyngeal spaces, and for delineating surgically relevant anatomies, including the position and medialization of the internal carotid artery (38,40).

Imaging should also be used to identify alternative or concurrent causes of clinical deterioration, such as retropharyngeal lymphadenitis or retropharyngeal abscesses, which may mimic peritonsillar infection, but require different therapeutic and surgical considerations (38,40).

UPPER-AIRWAY INFECTIONS

Croup (acute laryngotracheobronchitis) is a common viral upper-airway infection in infants and young children that is most frequently caused by parainfluenza and influenza viruses (41). Clinically, it is characterized by a barking cough and inspiratory stridor. Typical radiographic findings are the tapered narrowing of the subglottic airway (“steeple sign”) on anteroposterior neck views and hypopharyngeal dilatation on lateral views (Fig. 7) (42,43).

Croup.

A, B. Neck radiographs of a 2-year-old girl with barking cough demonstrate tapered subglottic airway narrowing (“steeple” sign) (arrow) on the anteroposterior view (A) and air trapping within the hypopharynx (arrow) on the lateral view (B), which are consistent with croup.

Bacterial tracheitis, also known as membranous/pseudomembranous/bacterial croup, is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening cause of upper-airway obstruction that most often affects preschool and early school-aged children (41). It frequently arises as a secondary bacterial infection after viral croup, and Staphylococcus aureus is the predominant pathogen (44). Children typically present with higher fever, more severe respiratory distress, and a toxic appearance, and often fail to improve with standard croup therapy. Imaging may show irregular or circumferential tracheal wall thickening, luminal narrowing, and intraluminal soft tissue densities representing sloughed mucosa or pseudomembranes, and pneumonia is commonly associated (41).

Acute epiglottitis in children was historically most commonly caused by Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib); however, its incidence has markedly declined following widespread Hib vaccination, and the condition is now relatively more frequent in adults (41). Clinically, patients present with high fever, drooling, a muffled “hot potato” voice, and signs of impending respiratory distress. On lateral neck radiographs, a markedly thickened and edematous epiglottis produces a characteristic “thumbprint” sign (Table 2) (42).

NECK DISEASE

RETROPHARYNGEAL INFECTION: ABSCESS VS. EFFUSION

The retropharyngeal space is a potential space extends from the skull base to the superior mediastinum; in children, it contains active lymphatic chains that predispose them to secondary suppurative lymphadenitis and subsequent abscess formation after upper respiratory tract infection. On lateral neck radiographs or sagittal cross-sectional imaging, true retropharyngeal soft tissue widening is suggested when the prevertebral soft tissue thickness exceeds approximately 7 mm at C1–C4 or 14 mm at C5–C7 in children, after excluding technical factors such as neck flexion or expiration (45).

Distinguishing retropharyngeal abscesses from reactive effusions is crucial (Fig. 8). True retropharyngeal abscesses most often arise from the rupture of a suppurative retropharyngeal lymph node. They may extend across the midline, demonstrate convex borders with peripheral rim enhancement, and obliterate normal nodal architecture—often described as a “football” shaped collection (46). If the infection extends further into the danger space, the deep neck infection can descend into the mediastinum and result in life-threatening complications. In contrast, retropharyngeal effusions represent nonsuppurative fluid and tend to show a biconcave “bow tie” configuration, with the anteroposterior diameter smaller centrally than laterally and without definite rim enhancement (46).

INFECTED CONGENITAL CYSTS

The most frequently encountered congenital cervical lesions that become secondarily infected are branchial apparatus anomalies, thyroglossal duct cysts, and dermoid/epidermoid inclusion cysts, each with a characteristic anatomic predilection, but often overlapping clinical presentations (Table 3) (47). When uninflamed, these cysts typically appear as well-circumscribed, thin-walled fluid collections; superimposed infection is suggested by wall thickening and enhancement, the development of internal debris with a “pseudosolid” or heterogeneous appearance, and conspicuous inflammatory fat stranding and hyperemia in the adjacent soft tissues. In an acute setting, US and CT are the primary problem-solving modalities, allowing the assessment of cyst content, mural changes, and surrounding inflammation; MRI is best reserved for the post-acute phase to delineate the full extent of the malformation and its relationship with adjacent structures for surgical planning (2). Therefore, accurate interpretation relies on integrating lesion location and embryologic pathways (to classify the underlying congenital anomaly) with the imaging stigmata of infection, which helps distinguish inflamed congenital cysts from their non-inflamed counterparts and from important mimics such as suppurative lymphadenitis, infected lymphatic malformations, necrotic or cystic tumors, and sialoceles (2).

TEMPORAL BONE AND SKULL BASE DISEASE

COALESCENT MASTOIDITIS: BONY EROSION AS SEVERITY INDICATOR

In young children, coalescent otomastoiditis is the most common suppurative complication of acute otitis media (Fig. 9) (48). “Coalescence” refers to the erosion and loss of the delicate mastoid air-cell septa and/or the bony cortex, resulting in the confluence of normally separate air cells and creating larger, irregular cavities that may contain fluid or pus. Once a cortical breach occurs, rim-enhancing collections within the mastoid and adjacent soft tissues indicate coalescent mastoiditis with abscess formation, which can extend to form subperiosteal abscesses, epidural empyema, or dural venous sinus thrombosis; a demonstration of these findings typically prompts surgical intervention rather than medical therapy alone.

Acute labyrinthitis reflects the spread of infection into the membranous labyrinth and is suggested on MRI as an abnormal post-contrast enhancement of the labyrinthine structures and, in more advanced cases, as the loss of the normal high T2 signal within the fluid-filled inner ear (49). If diagnosis and treatment are delayed, organizing inflammation may progress to labyrinthitis ossificans with new bone formation within the cochlea and vestibule, resulting in irreversible sensorineural hearing loss (50). Therefore, the early recognition of coalescent changes and inner-ear involvement in imaging is critical for timely surgical drainage and hearing preservation.

PETROUS APICITIS AND GRADENIGO SYNDROME

Pneumatized petrous apices can become secondarily infected when the mastoid inflammation spreads to the central skull base, resulting in petrous apicitis (51). Clinically, patients often complain of deep facial pain due to trigeminal nerve irritation in Meckel’s cave, with the variable association of ipsilateral otorrhea and sixth nerve palsy. When these features occur together, they constitute Gradenigo syndrome; however, in practice, only a subset of the triad is frequently present, which can delay diagnosis if imaging is not carefully scrutinized. On CT, petrous apicitis is suggested by the opacification of a pneumatized petrous apex with associated osseous erosion; MRI typically shows T2/STIR hyperintensity and strong postcontrast enhancement of the petrous apex with or without abscess formation, helping to distinguish infection from simple effusion wherein the bony septa remain intact (51). Recognizing petrous apicitis is critical, because it may progress to skull base osteomyelitis, cranial neuritis, dural venous or cavernous sinus thrombosis, meningitis, subdural or epidural abscesses, and potentially catastrophic carotid artery involvement with arteritis, occlusion, pseudoaneurysm, or rupture (2).

FOREIGN BODIES

RADIOPAQUE AND RADIOLUCENT FOREIGN BODIES: BUTTON BATTERY AS EMERGENCY ENTITY

Children, driven by innate curiosity and a constant desire to explore and experiment, are particularly prone to foreign-body incidents, regardless of whether objects are ingested, aspirated, or inserted into various orifices. In the radiologic evaluation, both the location and material of the suspected foreign body are crucial as they determine the choice of imaging modality, potential complications, and the urgency and route of removal. The initial assessment typically includes anteroposterior and lateral neck radiographs, followed by chest and abdominal radiographs, to detect and track radiopaque foreign bodies and to evaluate secondary signs such as airway narrowing or soft tissue swelling. Radiopaque foreign bodies (coins, buttons, and batteries) are readily identified on radiography based on their radiodensity, while radiolucent materials (food, cloth, and plastic) appear as air or intermediate density.

The distinction between ingested coins and button batteries is critical; button batteries pose a catastrophic risk, whereas coins generally do not (Fig. 10, Table 4). Although similar in size, button batteries demonstrate distinctive radiographic appearance: a characteristic “double-ring” appearance on frontal radiographs (representing the two battery terminals) and a “beveled-edge” appearance on lateral radiographs (52,53,54). Button battery ingestion requires immediate endoscopic removal within 1–2 hours to prevent caustic mucosal injury, potential esophageal perforation, and life-threatening complications, including tracheoesophageal fistula, vascular injury, and spondylodiscitis (55).

Coins Versus Batteries

Ingested coins lodge predictably at three esophageal locations: the thoracic inlet, aortic arch level, and lower esophageal sphincter. Coin impaction may be managed nonoperatively via endoscopy (including Foley catheter balloon extraction in selected cases) or spontaneous passage; prolonged impaction risks esophageal perforation (56).

Nasal foreign bodies, typically beads, buttons, food, or batteries, present with epistaxis or unilateral nasal obstruction (57). While many are removable without imaging, complex cases of edema, bony destruction, or tissue reactions require preoperative imaging for planning (54).

CONCLUSION

The imaging assessment of pediatric nontraumatic head and neck emergencies requires a comprehensive anatomic understanding, a recognition of age-specific pathology, and an awareness of complications that develop insidiously in young patients. The radiologist serves not only to confirm suspected diagnoses but also to guide critical management decisions affecting vision preservation, airway patency, and prevention of life-threatening complications. A systematic approach—identifying key anatomical landmarks (orbital septum, sphenopalatine foramen, and retropharyngeal space anatomy), recognizing pathognomonic imaging findings (guitar pick sign, double-ring appearance, and coalescence), and understanding management implications of diagnostic distinctions (preseptal versus postseptal, suppurative adenitis versus abscess, and intratonsillar versus peritonsillar)—enables rapid and accurate diagnosis and optimal patient outcomes.
