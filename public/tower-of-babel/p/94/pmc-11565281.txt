How Oxygen Deficiency and Its Ordering Control Electrical Conductivity in Sr2–xCaxFe2O6−δ Perovskites as Related to Water Oxidation Electrocatalysis
Authors: Jiam Vuong, Shahab Derakhshan, Hadi Tavassol
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We report on the analysis of oxygen vacancies (OVs) content and ordering of Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ perovskites and explain how OVs change the electrical conductivity and oxygen evolution catalytic activity of these compounds. The structure and OV content are tuned by controlling the A-site composition and the reaction atmosphere. X-ray diffraction (XRD) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) are used to identify the crystal structures and to quantify bulk oxygen vacancy contents. Our analysis shows that OV content and crystal structure govern the electronic transport properties of the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ system. The electrical conductivity of oxygen-deficient perovskites (ODPs) is significantly higher than those in brownmillerites (by at least 2 orders of magnitude). Seebeck coefficient measurements identified that the Sr-rich ODPs and brownmillerites are p-type semiconductors, while Ca-rich brownmillerites are either insulators (within the experimental temperature range) or p-type semiconductors at lower temperatures (<750 K). Electrical conductivity of p-type semiconductors (Sr-rich compounds) reduces with higher OV content, and in brownmillerites with x ≥ 1.25, a transition to n-type semiconductor is observed at temperatures above 750 K. Our analysis shows that the hole and electron concentrations are similar in these brownmillerites, indicating major contributions from ionic transport. Finally, we show how oxygen deficiency alters the electrical conductivity and catalytic activity of the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ system in noncomplementary ways.

Introduction

The oxygen evolution reaction (OER) is important in many chemical processes relevant to sustainable storage and conversion of energy, e.g., water-splitting. Oxygen-deficient perovskites (A2B2O6-δ) are promising materials for the OER electrocatalysis. The crystal lattice of an ideal perovskite-type (ABO3, or A2B2O6) structure consists of a network of corner-sharing BO6. In general, oxygen-deficient perovskites are referred to systems whose crystal lattices are composed of randomly distributed oxygen vacancies, resulting in corner-sharing octahedra and motifs with lower coordination numbers, such as square pyramidal BO5 and tetrahedral BO4. In brownmillerites, oxygen vacancies are crystallographically ordered. The brownmillerite-type structure consists of alternating layers of BO6 octahedra and BO4 tetrahedra.

Oxygen vacancies could potentially control the electronic transport properties of transition metal oxides by changing the charge carrier concentration and charge carrier mobility.1,2 For example, hematite (α-Fe2O3), an n-type semiconductor, exhibits increased conductivity with increasing oxygen vacancy.3 In contrast, the conductivity of p-type semiconductors such as Sr2Fe2O6-δ decreases with increasing oxygen vacancy (δ).4−7

Iron-based oxygen-deficient perovskites8−10 and brownmillerites9,11−13 have been recently studied for OER activity. Multiple studies have attributed high OER activity to either high electrical conductivity, oxygen vacancies, or both.9,10,12,14−18 The covalency of the M–O bonds is known to enhance the electrical conductivity and the OER activity.11,20,21 However, the role of oxygen vacancies and transport properties in the OER is not fully understood. Our previous study demonstrated increased activity in the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ system as oxygen vacancy at the surface (δs) approaches one.18

In this work, we analyze the transport properties of the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ series to understand the relationship between oxygen vacancy, electrical conductivity, and the OER activity. The content and structural ordering of oxygen vacancy in the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ system was controlled by synthesis condition. We use transport properties measurements and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) to quantify bulk vacancy contents and explain their relationship to the OER catalytic activity.

Experimental Section

Synthesis

The synthesis of Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ (x = 0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, 1.25, 1.50, 1.75, 2.00) follows conventional solid-state synthesis. First, stoichiometric mixtures of CaCO3 (Alfa Aesar), SrCO3 (Alfa Aesar), and Fe2O3 (Alfa Aesar) were grounded, mixed, and pelletized. The platinum sheets were placed between the pellets and the alumina boats. Next, the samples were heated at 1250 °C for 48 h in an argon atmosphere. The same procedure was followed for the air-synthesized samples, too. The rate for both cooling and heating was 110 °C/h. Repeated grinding and heating were performed to achieve single-phase samples.

Phase Analyses

The crystal structures and purity of the samples were determined by employing the powder X-ray diffraction technique. The data were collected using a PANalytical X’Pert Pro MPD diffractometer, equipped with a linear X’Celerator detector and Cu Kα1 radiation. The unit cell parameters were obtained, and the peaks were indexed using HighScorePlus (4.1).22

Thermogravimetric Analyses

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on a Discovery SDT650 instrument. The powder samples were placed in 90 μL alumina pans and heated at 110 °C isothermally for one h to remove any moisture. Subsequently, the measurement was performed at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, up to 900 °C, and a cooling rate of 5 °C/min, down to 110 °C in argon flow.

Electrochemical Measurement

The inks were prepared by mixing 8 mg of synthesized powder, 2 mg of Ketjen Black EC-600JD carbon, 4 mL of 95% ethanol, and 200 μL of Nafion (Sigma-Aldrich). The mixtures were subsequently sonicated for 1.5 h. For each experiment, 5 μL of the slurry was drop-casted onto a glassy carbon electrode, which was allowed to dry overnight.

Rotating disk electrode (RDE) experiments were performed in a three-electrode cell using a Pt wire counter electrode and a leakless Ag/AgCl reference electrode (eDAQ) in different compartments separated by frit membranes from the working electrode compartment. Before each experiment, the potential of the Ag/AgCl reference electrode was measured vs. a Pt wire while purging with H2 (g) to adjust the potential vs. RHE. A 0.1 M KOH solution was used as the electrolyte and was purged with oxygen gas before the electrochemical measurement. Electrochemical measurements were performed under O2 environments between 1.1 to 1.9 V vs RHE. The reported data here was from the third electrochemical cycle across all samples. All experiments were performed by using a CH Instruments potentiostat, and the rotation was performed by using a Pine Research rotator. RDE measurements were performed in O2 saturated 0.1 M KOH solutions at 1600 rpm.

Electronic Transport Property Measurements

The four-probe DC measurement was performed on a ULVAC ZEM-3. The samples were cold-pressed into rectangular shapes and then sintered in a furnace to achieve higher mechanical strength. Temperature-dependent electrical conductivity and Seebeck measurements were performed under a low-pressure He atmosphere (to enhance heat transfer through convection, also providing a nonreactive environment), from 25 to 700 °C at an interval of 25 °C.

Results and Discussion

Crystal Structure

Oxygen vacancy content and the vacancy ordering of the compounds were controlled by performing synthesis under air or argon flow at ∼1250 °C. Figure S1 shows XRD patterns of all compounds synthesized under argon and air. The A-site in the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ system was varied by nominal increments of x = 0.25. Figure S2 shows details of how the structural transition occurs in this group of compounds. Table S1 lists the unit cell details of these compounds. We have previously reported the synthesis of this series of compounds,18 and here we provide further structural details.

Samples synthesized under argon crystallize in brownmillerite-type structures, which are indicated by the presence of the low-angle 020 peak (Figure S1a). For the sample with x ≥ 1.25, the 131 and 151 peaks represent the Pcmn space group.19,26,27 A phase impurity is observed at x = 1.00, which suggests a transition from Pcmn to the Ibm2 space group.

Assigning a space group to x ≤ 1.00 is difficult as the discrepancy between Icmm and Ibm2 space groups is minimal.28 Using both XRD and neutron diffraction data, Hona et al. showed that x = 1.00 is best described based on the Ibm2 space group.19 The most controversial brownmillerite structure is Sr2Fe2O5 as it is assigned to either Icmm(29−31) or Ibm2.32−35 Most recently, a single crystal of Sr2Fe2O5 grown by the floating zone method identifies the crystal structure as Pbma. However, it should be noted that some reports have shown that the resultant structures from floating zone synthesis are not always the same as those from the conventional high-temperature solid-state synthesis method.36 This argument has been made based on fewer oxygen contents in samples produced from melting techniques.

The brownmillerite-type structures, with ordered OVs, adopt one of the Pcmn, Pbcm, Ibm2, or Icmm orthorhombic space groups. The difference between these space groups originated from various possibilities of the orientation of the tetrahedral chains, i.e., right- or left-handed which are shown in Figure 1. In the Pcmn space group, right- and left-handed orientations alternate between tetrahedral layers (Figure 1a). In the Ibm2 space group, the tetrahedral chains adopt the same orientation (Figure 1b), while in the Icmm space group, the orientation of tetrahedral chains is random (Figure 1c). In the Pbma space group, the orientation of the tetrahedral chains alternates both in the layers and between the layers (Figure 1d).28,37−39

Our previous reports on structural analyses of air-synthesized samples are in agreement with the other studies (Figure S1b).19,40,41 For samples with x ≥ 1.00, the low-angle 020 peaks suggest a brownmillerite-type structure. The 131 and 151 peaks are indicative of the orthorhombic Pcmn space group.19,26,27 The sample with x = 1 adopts another orthorhombic space group, namely Ibm2.19 A phase impurity is observed at x = 1.25, which suggests a transition from Pcmn to the Ibm2 space group. The perovskite-type structures, in general, have higher symmetry than brown millillerites, which is highlighted by the presence of fewer peaks in their XRD patterns. The samples with x ≤ 0.75 adopt I4/mmm tetragonal space group.29

Depending on the oxygen vacancies, the Sr2Fe2O6-δ (SFO) class of compounds adopt four different known structures, three of which belong to the perovskite family, and one is brownmillerite.29,42 The three perovskites are cubic Pm-3m, tetragonal I4/mmm, and orthorhombic Cmmm, while the brownmillerite is orthorhombic Ibm2.29 The three perovskites structures show similar XRD patterns; however, neutron diffraction experiments have been employed to distinguish between them.29,42,43 SFO is known to adopt the I4/mmm space group when synthesized in air at 1250 °C.29

Oxygen Stoichiometry

Figure 2 shows the estimated values for the total or bulk oxygen deficiency, δb, in the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ series as a function of A-site composition, obtained by TGA measurements. In general, for brownmillerites, the δb is very close to one, and for perovskites, the δb values are lower than one. We have previously reported surface oxidation states of Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ compounds acquired from XPS measurement which we assign to surface oxygen deficiency, δs.18 We compare bulk and surface oxygen deficiencies δb and δs in Figure 2. Interestingly, surface oxygen deficiencies are different from δb particularly for 0<x ≤ 1. Phase analysis of SFO has shown that the perovskites are associated with lower δb than the corresponding brownmillerites which show δb → 1.23,44 Similarly, the δb values for Ca-based brownmillerites have been reported to be very close to one.26,45,46 As expected, Ar-synthesized compounds have δb values higher than those of the air-synthesized members. As δb → 1, the concentration of Fe3+ in the compound increases. Hence, perovskites contain relatively more Fe4+ cations than brownmillerites.

Bulk oxygen deficiency, δb from TGA measurements (closed symbol and solid line, compared with surface oxygen deficiency and δs from XPS analysis18 (open symbol and dash lines). The black and red symbols represent compounds synthesized under Ar and air flow, respectively.

The XPS measurements are performed under ultrahigh vacuum conditions at room temperature, while TGA experiments are done under an Ar atmosphere across a wide range of temperatures. Hence, the differences in calculated δ from these methods can be assigned to the differences between the surface and the bulk properties and experimental conditions. Differences in surface and bulk oxygen deficiencies are particularly important in the catalytic properties of these materials. Although the surface electronic structure controls the catalytic activity, the electronic transport properties of the materials are mainly determined by the electronic structure of the bulk.

The Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ system is known for its oxygen absorption and desorption properties.23,40,41,47−49 As the temperature increases under an Ar-saturated environment, the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ system may show weight loss, which is associated with the loss of oxygen from the material. Hence, the oxygen vacancies increase with temperature for materials that show weight loss at higher temperatures. To maintain charge neutrality, the oxidation state of Fe reduces from 4+ to 3+ as these compounds lose oxygen. The air-synthesized compounds generally show a lower bulk oxygen deficiency, whereas δb shows a stronger correlation to the temperature (Figure 3a and c). Notably, δb changes drastically with temperature in SFO, which could be linked to the various reported crystallographic phase transitions, namely, from tetragonal I4/mmm → orthorhombic Cmmm (300 °C) → orthorhombic Ibm2 (550 °C).23 The Ar-synthesized compounds (Figure 3b and d), which are all brown millillerites, mostly show near unity δb values. However, Ar-synthesized compounds with x → 1.25, show lower oxygen deficiency than other compounds synthesized under the same condition.

Transport Properties

Electrical conductivity is proportional to the charge carrier concentration and charge carrier mobility. In transition metal oxides, the transition metal ion has more than one stable oxidation state. The conductivity in transition metal oxides is often through a polaronic mechanism.1 In a small polaron mechanism, conduction occurs by the charge carrier “hopping” along the M-O-M pathway.1,19 In transition metal oxides, the electronic conductivity is determined by the overlap between the metal (M) 3d orbitals and oxygen (O) 2p orbitals.4,19 The charge carrier hopping occurs in the M–O–M pathways.4,19 Shorter M–O bond lengths and greater M–O–M bond angles (closer to 180°) result in more effective orbital overlaps and more dispersed band shapes. The slope of the bands is directly proportional to charge carriers’ mobility. Therefore, in general, fewer structural distortions in the crystalline lattice are desirable for higher electronic conductivities.4,20

As discussed earlier, different crystal structures were obtained for the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ family, depending on the chemical composition and reaction condition. So different electronic transport properties are expected. Hence, detailed electrical conductivity measurements were performed on these compounds. Transport properties of Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ compounds with x = 0, 1.0, and 2.0 have been previously reported.6,7,19,27,45,50,51 In Figure 4, we report the electrical conductivity of Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ compounds as a function of composition where x is varied by a 0.25 increment to further investigate changes in electrical conductivity. In general, higher Ca content corresponds to lower electrical conductivity. In fact, Ca-rich compounds are insulators at room temperature. This is likely due to the smaller ionic radius of Ca than Sr. The lattice distortion increases as Ca content increases, which results in less effective covalent interactions among Fe–O–Fe links, lowering the charge carrier mobility and electrical conductivity.4,19 It is also noted that perovskite-type materials show electrical conductivity higher than that of their brownmillerite counterparts with the same A-site composition. This trend can be attributed to the greater orbital overlaps between the Fe–O–Fe bonds in layers of corner-shared octahedra in perovskite structures.19

Electrical conductivity measurement of (a) air- and (b) Ar-synthesized compound. The transition refers to the electronic transition from semiconductor to metallic.

The argon- and air-synthesized Ca-rich brownmillerites adopt the orthorhombic Pcmn space group, while Sr-rich brownmillerite adopts the orthorhombic Ibm2 space group. The difference in conductivity is largely due to the Fe–O bond overlap, notably higher degree of distortion in Pcmn,52 which lowers the electrical conductivity. The alternating layers of octahedral and tetrahedral in the brownmillerite type structure hinder polaron hopping.51 The structural distortion in brownmillerite-type compounds introduces lattice polarization,19 which decreases the mobility of the predominant charge carriers.1

Most samples were confirmed to exhibit a transition to metallic behavior at elevated temperatures, which is summarized in Figure 5d. For argon-synthesized samples with x = 1.50, 1.75, and 2.00, the transitions are not detected within the temperature range of the experiment. Below the transition temperatures, all compounds show semiconducting behavior. As shown in Figure 5d, increasing Ca content and higher bulk oxygen deficiency result in higher transition temperatures.

Oxygen vacancy content changes charge carrier concentration and mobility of semiconducting oxides since the presence of randomly distributed vacant sites disrupts the long-range order in the crystal structure, resulting in shortening the mean free path of charge carriers. Vacancy content can be controlled by the partial pressure of oxygen (PO2) during the synthesis. The oxygen vacancies are compensated for by the reduction of transition metal ions (for example Fe4+ → Fe3+). In compounds with very high concentrations of oxygen vacancies, n-type semiconductors exhibit transitions from nonmetal to metal as observed in CaMnO3,53,54 SrTiO3,55−60 ZnO,61 TiO2.62,63 In the case of p-type semiconductors, the transition from nonmetal to metal is observed as oxygen vacancy concentration decreases, for example, in SFO,6 LaNiO3-δ,64,65 and La1–xSrxFeO3-δ.66

Correlation to OER Activity

Our previous studies examined the role of surface oxygen deficiency, δs in the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ system for OER activity.18 XPS analysis was employed to examine the link between the δs and the OER activity, which showed that the OER overpotential decreases as δs approaches one. Notably, the Ar-synthesized samples demonstrated much lower overpotentials than those of the similar composition of the air-synthesized samples. Other studies have also reported a boost in OER activity by the introduction of oxygen vacancies.8,11,17,74−80 However, in many cases additional oxygen sites dopants (S,74 and Cl76) or B-site dopants (Co,11−13,17,81−84 and Mn9) also contribute to the OER activity. In situ analysis of perovskite oxide electrocatalysts reveal that these surfaces undergo dynamic self-reconstruction during OER.69,85

The plot of the log(conductivity) vs. overpotential at 450 °C is presented in Figure S3, which also shows that there is no clear correlation between reduced overpotential and higher electrical conductivity. It should be noted that there are brownmillerites with low electrical conductivities that show relatively higher catalytic activities than their perovskite with the same A-site composition. This shows that bulk electrical conductivity and oxygen vacancy content, δb does not necessarily correlate with higher electrochemical activity in these compounds. However, we have shown that OER overpotential decreases as surface oxygen vacancies, δs approaches unity.18

As discussed earlier, both the crystal structure and bulk oxygen vacancies affect the charge transport properties of the materials. Despite the overall lower total conductivity of Ar-synthesized compounds (Figure 7b), they tend to show lower overpotential, i.e., higher OER activities than their similar A-site composition air-synthesized counterparts. When comparing the similar A-site composition from x = 0.00 to 0.75, the Ar-synthesized brownmillerites exhibit lower overpotential than the air-synthesized brownmillerites despite the structural differences. The high oxygen deficiency of Ar-synthesized samples particularly at the surface appears to contribute to the higher OER activity when compared with their counterpart with the same A-site composition synthesized in air. Higher bulk electrical conductivity generally does not seem to improve catalytic properties in this group of oxides. Catalytic properties of these materials seem to be controlled by surface oxygen vacancy content,18 the resulting surface orbital occupancy changes, Fe4+ → Fe3+,21 and structural and electronic changes during reaction conditions.86

Conclusion

We show that the crystal structure and bulk oxygen vacancy content control the transport properties of the Sr2–xCaxFe2O6-δ system. We report considerably higher electrical conductivity in the oxygen-deficient perovskite than in their brownmillerite counterparts, which we explain by the M-O bond distance and formation mechanism of charge carriers (holes) as the oxygen vacancy content changes. However, we show that surface oxygen vacancy controls the catalytic activity of iron perovskites toward OER. Higher electrical conductivity (lower bulk oxygen vacancy content) does not correlate with OER activity. We also explain inconsistencies in the reported OER catalytic activities of SrFeO3-δ with variations in the structures and transport properties resulting from the vacancy content changes under different synthesis and reaction conditions. Finally, this study provides further insight into the interplay between bulk electrical conductivity and oxygen vacancy, surface oxygen vacancy content, and OER activity in iron perovskites.
