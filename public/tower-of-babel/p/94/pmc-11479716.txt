Mediation analysis in longitudinal study with high-dimensional methylation mediators
Authors: Yidan Cui, Qingmin Lin, Xin Yuan, Fan Jiang, Shiyang Ma, Zhangsheng Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMediation analysis has been widely utilized to identify potential pathways connecting exposures and outcomes. However, there remains a lack of analytical methods for high-dimensional mediation analysis in longitudinal data. To tackle this concern, we proposed an effective and novel approach with variable selection and the indirect effect (IE) assessment based on both linear mixed-effect model and generalized estimating equation. Initially, we employ sure independence screening to reduce the dimension of candidate mediators. Subsequently, we implement the Sobel test with the Bonferroni correction for IE hypothesis testing. Moreover, we apply our methodology to explore the mediation mechanisms involving over 730 000 DNA methylation sites with potential effects between the paternal body mass index (BMI) and offspring growing BMI in the Shanghai sleeping birth cohort data, leading to the identification of two previously undiscovered mediating CpG sites.

Introduction

In recent years, mediation analysis has surged in popularity as a potent instrument for revealing potential pathways that establish connections between exposures/treatments and outcomes with clinical significance. Building upon this foundation, Baron and Kenny [1] described several prerequisites for mediation relationships, effectively integrating mediation analysis into the realm of psychological theory and research. Subsequently, the fascination with mediation analysis has permeated various scientific domains, including but not limited to social sciences [2–4], public health [5–7], and genome analysis [8–10].

With modern medicine increasingly focusing on clinical research, the data collected in studies are becoming progressively more comprehensive. Particularly in prospective studies, there are concerted efforts to maximize follow-up waves and expand the follow-up duration. To enhance mediation analysis and capture random effects within subjects inherent to this type of data, researchers have developed mediation analysis methods for repeated measurements and longitudinal data. The structural equation model (SEM) can effectively manage variables measured repeatedly and accommodate measurement error by utilizing latent variables [11–13]. The multilevel SEM offered a method to estimate two levels of regression coefficients [14, 15]. Blood et al. in 2010 and 2011 [16, 17] assessed the efficacy of the linear mixed-effect model (LMM) and SEM in analyzing mediated longitudinal data. Nevo et al. applied generalized estimating equation (GEE) to estimate mediation proportion in duplicate data. Muthén and Asparouhov [18], Vuorre and Bolger [19] developed Bayesian methodologies that enhance the performance of effect estimation. Zheng and van der Laan [20], Didelez [21], and Tanner et al. [22] illustrated techniques for identifying and inferring treatment effects on survival outcomes through repeatedly measured mediators. However, new mediation analysis methods are still needed to handle the high-dimensional nature of increasingly comprehensive longitudinal clinical cohort studies.

The previously mentioned methods are formulated for single or multiple mediators. However, with the accelerating momentum of electronic and information technology, alongside with popularity of sequencing technologies, the demand for mediation analysis with high-dimensional mediators is on the rise. Zhang et al. [8] introduced a framework to assess and validate high-dimensional epigenetic indirect effect (IE) linking smoking to decreased lung function. Derkach et al. [23] proposed a high-dimensional mediation analysis approach incorporating latent variables. Loh et al. [24] introduced an innovative nonparametric strategy for estimating IEs in the context of high-dimensional mediators. Fang et al. [25] proposed an omnibus test for both linearity and nonlinearity assumption of gene-based high-dimensional mediation analysis in epigenetic studies. Luo et al. [26] and Cui et al. [27] focused on the survival outcomes associated with high-dimensional DNA methylation sites. Zhao et al. [28] developed a sparse high-dimensional mediation analysis approach that incorporates sparse principal component analysis within the mediation setting. Song et al. [29] proposed two Bayesian hierarchical models for discerning active mediators from a vast array of correlated high-dimensional candidates. These investigations employed high-dimensional mediators such as DNA methylation and biomarkers (like serum metabolites).Recent studies have additionally explored medical images (like brain MRI and mammogram images) as intermediary variables, aiming to identify specific image regions which serve as mediators between treatments and diseases [30–32]. However, these high-dimensional works focused on cross-sectional studies, and there is still no work in longitudinal research.

Longitudinal data for repeated collected data such as cohort studies and clinical trials are often accompanied by high-throughput data collection including DNA methylation or medical imaging which mediate predictors’ effect on the outcome. Nevertheless, there is a significant demand for the analytical approach for high-throughput mediators in longitudinal studies. To tackle this challenge, we proposed a procedure designed for identifying and estimating high-dimensional mediation effects associated with longitudinal outcomes in epigenetic and clinical studies based on LMM and GEE. In this study, we assume that the clinical data would be measured repeatedly, whereas the high-dimensional mediators would not, due to the substantial costs of measuring DNA methylation data. We utilize a sure independence screening (SIS)-based variable selection method to identify mediators from a candidate pool. The SIS method was initially proposed by Fan and Lv [33] to reduce the dimensionality of variables in sparse ultra-high dimensional datasets. Motivated by their idea of marginal correlation variable selection, we apply an interactive SIS-based method designed for dimension reduction in high-dimensional mediation analysis. Subsequently, we conduct the IE test and implement multiple hypothesis corrections to identify significant mediators. Sobel method [34] is opted to test the IEs of mediators, while Bonferroni adjustment is unitized for correcting multiple hypothesis testing [35].

The subsequent sections of this article are organized as follows. In the section on statistical methods and materials, we provide methodological details and outline the workflow of high-dimensional mediation analysis for longitudinal data. In the section on simulation studies, the efficacy of the proposed procedure is assessed through comprehensive simulation studies, additionally compared with the linear method, which ignores the correlation within repeated measures for individuals. In the section on case study, this novel framework is applied to analyze longitudinal clinical data and high-throughput DNA methylation data from the Shanghai sleep birth cohort study (SSBCS). Finally, the conclusion and discussion are provided.

Simulation studies

We carry out comprehensive simulation studies to assess the selection and estimation performance of two proposed methods. We make comparisons with the linear method which does not account for correlations within individuals’ repeated measures. For each scenario, we generate 200 replicates. The simulation sample size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n$\end{document} ranges from 150 to 500, with the dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $p$\end{document} of mediators set as 10 000. In the first step, we set hyperparameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\omega _{\alpha } = \omega _{\beta } = 2$\end{document} to maintain a moderate number of selected mediators. We then perform intersect screening by selecting the top mediators based on the absolute values of the regression coefficients estimated from the marginal models.

To evaluate the mediator’s selection performance, we utilize the F\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{1}$\end{document} score, true positive rate (TPR), and false discovery rate(FDR). For estimation performance evaluation, we consider the estimated IE, coverage probability (CP), and mean squared error (MSE). In Supplementary Materials, we give the calculation of the above evaluation criteria. To control the multiple hypothesis test errors, we apply the Bonferroni correction to adjust the estimated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P$\end{document} values [35].

Results of additional studies

In addition to the foregoing scenarios, we explored alternative methods, including the Joint method and FDR-BH method, to compare with our proposed procedure. The Joint method employs a joint significance test (details provided in Supplementary Materials) instead of the Sobel test during the IE testing step, while the FDR-BH method replaces Bonferroni with FDR-BH correction. For comparison, we also included the linear method, which does not account for the correlation among repeated measures but incorporates either joint significance testing or FDR-BH adjustment. Results of these comparisons are detailed in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5 for the LMM-based procedure, and Supplementary Tables S6 and S7 for the GEE-based procedure, respectively. Notably, under the joint method and FDR-BH method, longitudinal methods consistently yield higher F\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{1}$\end{document} scores compared to the linear method but fall short of the performance demonstrated by our proposed procedures. While Bonferroni correction is more stringent, we found that with smaller sample sizes, the FDR-BH correction exhibits a higher TPR while maintaining an acceptable false discovery rate (below 0.05). As a result, we included both correction methods in our R package, providing users the flexibility to select their preferred approach.

Case study

The SSBCS is a prospective study conducted at Renji Hospital in Pudong New District, Shanghai, spanning from May 2012 to July 2013. This study recruited parents and their newborns who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate. Follow-up visits for the children were conducted at multiple time points, including 42 days, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 months, as well as 3, 4, and 6 years of age. Throughout the study, data on various factors were collected, including demographics, physical examinations, sleep assessments, developmental and psychiatric assessments, diet records, and biological samples. A total of 277 parents participated in this study, along with their offspring [45]. Genome-wide DNA methylation was measured using the Illumina Infinium Human Methylation EPIC BeadChip array, which covers more than 850 000 CpG loci across the entire human genome [46].

The methylation data collected from infants participating in the SSBCS study underwent standard preprocessing using the chip analysis methylation pipeline R package [47]. After the preprocessing, we employed the beta mixture quantile dilation method to compute and normalize the resulting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta $\end{document} values [48]. These \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta $\end{document} values serve as quantitative measures of methylation levels at each specific CpG locus across individuals, ranging between 0 (indicating no methylation) and 1 (indicating 100% methylation).

Several studies demonstrate that increased pre-pregnancy paternal body mass index (BMI) is linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes, including altered fetal growth, placental development, and long-term health risks for offspring, such as being small for gestational age, macrosomia, and childhood obesity [49, 50]. In this real-world data application, we focus on investigating potential epigenetic pathways connecting fathers’ BMI before pregnancy with their offspring’s BMI during growth (from birth to 4 years old). After performing quality control on the DNA methylation data and removing individuals with missing clinical data, we obtained a dataset consisting of 234 samples (113 boys and 121 girls) and 731 154 DNA methylation sites. The number of repeated measured BMI per individual varies from 6 to 10 times. Fathers’ BMI exhibits a wide range, spanning from a minimum of 15.94 to a maximum of 53.63 with a median of 24.12 and an average of 24.37. According to standard BMI ranges, our sample includes 13 underweight individuals (BMI <18.5), 136 healthy individuals (18.5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\leq $\end{document} BMI <25), 73 overweight individuals (25 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\leq $\end{document} BMI <30), and 12 obese individuals (BMI \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\geq $\end{document} 30).

In our analysis, we employed fathers’ BMI as the continuous input (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $X$\end{document}) and utilized the repeated measurements of offspring BMI during growth as the continuous output (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Y$\end{document}). The TE between father’s BMI and newborns’ BMI during their growth is estimated to be 0.0332 (95%CI: 0.0021, 0.0644), indicating that a higher father’s BMI is associated with an increase in their offspring’s BMI. The CpG site cg05668674 is located on chromosome 16 within the ADGRG1 gene. ADGRG1 encodes the GPR56/ADGRG1 protein, a member of the adhesion G protein-coupled receptor family, which is crucial for normal brain development [51, 52]. During embryonic brain development, ADGRG1 is expressed in neural progenitor cells and migrating neurons. In later stages of brain development and throughout postnatal life, it exhibits high expression in glial cells [53]. The CpG site cg12152068 is situated on chromosome 3 within the BSN gene. BSN is a protein-coding gene responsible for the Bassoon protein, a substantial scaffolding protein highly expressed in the mammalian brain, particularly in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus. It plays a critical role in presynaptic terminal development and the regulation of neurotransmitter release [54, 55]. These findings strongly suggest that fathers’ BMI may impact the encoding of GPR56/ADGRG1 and Bassoon proteins in their offspring. This influence can affect brain development from the embryonic stage throughout one’s lifetime, potentially influencing brain function, which, in turn, may have quantifiable effects on children’s growth and development.

Conclusion and discussion

In recent years, the analysis of high-dimensional data has gained increasing importance, driven by advancements in sequencing technologies. High-dimensional mediation analysis has emerged as an effective tool for uncovering potential biomarkers that link exposures/treatments to clinical outcomes. This study introduces a novel framework for detecting significant mediators and estimating their corresponding IEs in longitudinal studies involving high-dimensional DNA methylation data. Our approach employed the intersection SIS technique, designed to reduce the dimensionality of candidate mediators efficiently. To assess multiple IEs and reduce false positives, we utilized the Sobel test in conjunction with the Bonferroni correction. Extensive simulation studies demonstrated the effectiveness of our proposed framework. Additionally, when applied to the SSBCS data, our approach successfully identified two DNA methylation sites, cg05668674 and cg12152068, which exhibit significant IEs in the relationship between fathers’ BMI and offspring’s BMI. These findings significantly improve our understanding of the complex interactions between genetics and health status.

There are several improvements worth consideration in the future research. On the one hand, while our study assumed that high-dimensional mediators were not repeatedly measured due to the high cost of DNA methylation measurements, the decreasing cost of sequencing opens up opportunities to explore the role of longitudinally measured DNA methylation sites in high-dimensional mediation analysis. This could illustrate how changes in DNA methylation over time relate to the indirect pathways. On the other hand, our study focuses on data with a single measured input. Therefore, future research could explore methodologies for handling repeated measured input to gain a more comprehensive understanding of dynamic changes and their IEs within potential pathways over time. Moreover, in our current study, we treated all mediators as independent of each other. However, in reality, CpG sites may exhibit correlations with nearby sites. Future research could delve into the complexities of correlated high-dimensional mediators within longitudinal data, drawing on approaches such as those proposed by Vansteelandt and Daniel [56] and Song et al. [57]. Additionally, while our analysis controlled for observed covariates like age and gender, it’s worth exploring the situation with more in-hospital covariate data. Propensity score-related approaches applied in high-dimensional mediation analysis area, as discussed by Yu et al. [58] and Luo et al. [59], provide a meaningful direction for further research to more comprehensively address multiple confounding factors. Some studies, such as Liu et al. [60] and Dai et al. [61], have highlighted concerns about the potential underpowered of the Sobel test and joint test for IE testing. Exploring alternative hypothesis tests for IEs in the context of high-dimensional longitudinal mediation analysis presents an interesting and valuable research direction.
