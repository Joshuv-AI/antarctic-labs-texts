The pager explosions: lessons learnt from a hybrid-warfare mass casualty incident
Authors: Eveline Hitti, Imad Bou Akl, Tharwat El Zahran, Amin Kazzi, Layal Hamdar, Rana Saleh, Gladys Honein- AbouHaidar, Pager Explosions MCI Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospitals responding to mass casualty incidents (MCIs) must rapidly scale up operations to accommodate high casualty counts, with little lead time. On 17 September 2024, 4000 pager devices were detonated across Lebanon, injuring 2323 individuals. This paper reports on a single-centre MCI response to this unprecedented hybrid warfare tactic, describing success features and identifying areas for improvement.MethodsA qualitative analysis of 10 debrief meeting minutes held within 2 weeks of the incident, as part of the standard quality improvement process post-MCI response that included an after-action review conducted in accordance with the 2019 WHO guidelines. Group-based debriefs, facilitated by the emergency department (ED) chairperson, addressed different response elements and engaged relevant stakeholders. Conclusions were derived from the qualitative analysis of the meeting minutes.ResultsOur hospital received 182 casualties, 66% of whom were triaged to urgent-level care (yellow), 21% to delayed-level care (green) and 13% to immediate-level care (red), with 35 requiring emergent surgery within the first 12 hours. The MCI response was activated 34 min postincident, initiating security lockdown protocols, expedited patient registration, MCI triage protocol implementation, opening of surge areas and command centre activation. Key success features included: shifting from ED-based response to a whole-hospital mobilisation to accommodate the influx; early secondary triage of urgent-level (yellow) patients for immediate transfer to inpatient wards, resulting in 22.5% being managed in the inpatient setting; and optimised information system workflows for admission and order entry. Recommended improvements include simplifying operating theatre transfer workflows, shifting to paper-based documentation of assessments/interventions, optimising surgical and diagnostic prioritisation processes and developing minimal diagnostic imaging standards of care.ConclusionThis study highlights key successes and challenges of a hospital response to a large hybrid-warfare MCI. Future research on reliability of key success features, specifically use of ED secondary triage protocols, is needed to support wider adoption. In addition, developing minimal imaging standards in MCIs is needed to reduce bottlenecks in this area.Keywords: mass casualty incidents, disaster planning, emergency responders

Background

On 17 September 2024, approximately 4000 pager devices detonated unexpectedly across Lebanon, injuring over 2300 patients.1 The synchronised timing and scale of the incident overwhelmed healthcare facilities, with thousands of casualties quickly arriving to over 150 hospitals across Lebanon.

The pager devices had been planted with small amounts of explosives and subsequently detonated through a coded message after distribution to users. This hybrid warfare tactic is unprecedented, combining traditional methods with emerging technologies, resulting in unique injury patterns.2 3 Of the 2323 total casualties across the country, there were 12 fatalities and only 824 discharges from emergency departments (EDs) after initial care, with the remaining patients requiring admission for further management. Patients primarily sustained severe injuries to eyes, face, hands and pelvis, with many requiring highly specialised operative care.1

While there have been other hybrid warfare incidents that have resulted in devastating injuries, like the September 11 attack,3 the nature of this incident demonstrates the challenges of anticipating the injury patterns and preparing for mass casualty incidents (MCIs) within this evolving landscape. In this report, we detail the response of the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC) to the pager explosions. Our objective is to explore both the successful strategies employed as well as the areas that require improvement in the hospital MCI response plan.

Methods

Study design

This study relies on a qualitative analysis of debrief meeting minutes held within 2 weeks of the incident, between 19 September and 24 September 2024, as part of a quality improvement process that included an after-action review (AAR) conducted in accordance with the 2019 WHO guidelines (online supplemental Appendix A).4 This project was determined to be a quality improvement activity—not human subjects research—and therefore was exempt from formal ethics board review.

Facility

AUBMC is the largest academic tertiary-care facility in Beirut, with 317 operational inpatient beds, 14 operating theatres (OTs) and 43 ED beds. Over the last 10 years, AUBMC’s ED has responded to 12 MCIs. Each MCI is followed by an AAR that is used to refine the hospital plan.

Description of the MCI response

AUBMC’s MCI response plan includes three levels: alert, partial activation and full activation (figure 1). Activation messaging relies on redundant systems including SMS, paging, email and internet-based messaging to reach the preprepared disaster staff list.

Flowchart of AUBMC’s MCI response plan. AUBMC, American University of Beirut Medical Center; ED, emergency department; MCI, mass casualty incident.

Once activated, a command centre is established and downstream processes kick in, activating department-level responses, securing lockdown of main entrances and designating areas for family and media communication. At the ED level, the response includes expedited patient registration using preprinted bracelets containing preassigned mass casualty record (MCR) numbers that require barcode scanning for activation within the information system. The MCR numbers are unique identifiers that allow patient tracking and identification throughout the response. A mobile triage area is established, using the modified Care-Flight System.5 Additional measures include expanding designated ED surge areas, deploying disaster supply and medication carts, expediting the discharge and admission of existing ED and ward patients and the use of vests by emergency section leads to facilitate identification.

Since the 2020 Beirut port blast, AUBMC’s disaster plan focused on expanding capacity to respond to larger scale MCIs. This required transitioning from an ED-centred approach to a hospital-wide response, with early shifting of stable urgent-level (yellow) patients from the ED to inpatient units for primary management and care. This required using an internally developed triaging system in the ED to identify appropriate patients for inpatient care. This was coupled with the training of 88 non-surgical residents on trauma care in MCI and 54 managers on MCI-unit-level preparedness. Additional changes focused on information system enhancements. These included: streamlined workflows for diagnostic ordering and admission requests, improvements to the registration process and adding on-site information technology (IT) support to the response team.

The event

On 17 September 2024, around 15:30, approximately 4000 pagers loaded with explosives detonated across Lebanon.6 AUBMC received the first patient at 16:01 and activated its emergency response plan at 16:04 initially as an alert, followed by partial activation at 16:07 and full activation at 16:25. By 07:00 the next morning, a total of 182 patients had arrived at AUBMC, with 80% of those arriving during the first 2 hours (figure 2). The majority of patients were triaged to urgent-level care (yellow) (120, 66%), followed by delayed-level care (green) (39, 21%) and immediate-level care (red) (23, 13%), and 35 required surgeries in the first 12 hours (figure 3). The disaster code was deactivated at 20:16.

Data source

After the MCI, 10 debrief sessions following the WHO AAR framework were conducted as part of our routine quality improvement process.4 The debriefs were group-based, 1 hour in duration, facilitated by the ED chairperson. Key informants (physicians, nurses, pharmacists, administrators and staff) from the following areas were included: ED, inpatient units, OT, surgery, pharmacy, patient registration, security, diagnostic radiology and medical informatics. A total of 62 stakeholders contributed to these debriefs held from 19 to 29 September 2024. Each of the 10 debriefs focused on specific response elements, with participation ranging from 10 to 28 individuals per session. Meeting minutes were recorded by the quality officer and circulated to participants for comment and final approval.

Data analysis

Each of the 10 meeting minutes was assigned a unique identifier (eg, CC, ED1, OR, IM2) and uploaded to Quirkos software for analysis.

A directed content analysis approach was used to identify key findings related to processes, gaps and challenges in protocol implementation. The analysis involved an iterative process of coding and categorising the data. Two research team members (LH, EH) conducted the initial line-by-line coding, that is, assigning a meaning or label to each code within each process of the MCI response. After completing three meeting minute documents, the team grouped the codes into themes. A data collection matrix was developed and used for coding the remaining meeting minutes. We revisited the matrix one final time to make sure results were comprehensive and meaningful.7

The trustworthiness of the analysis was enhanced through iterative review and reflexive practices, while maintaining a critical stance and adhering only to the content of the debriefing meeting minutes.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research because the study focused on evaluating the hospital’s internal operational response to an MCI.

Activation

Activation went as planned with prompt notification of personnel through the different messaging systems. A few technical issues were identified related to the low volume of overhead announcements and some staff not receiving the Wi-Fi activation alerts.

Response governance/roles and responsibilities

The emergency command centre (ECC) promptly convened in the predesignated location and oversaw the execution of MCI workflows, the deployment of resources across the hospital and coordination with external agencies. Medical team leads assumed their roles within their respective clinical areas. Low compliance with vest usage by ED section leads led to challenges with identifying key personnel.

Communication

Internal

The ECC communicated with ED and other key unit leaders through two-way radio and Wi-Fi messaging. Within the ED, different section leads were not assigned two-way radio devices and thus relied on messengers, referred to as runners, to communicate with the ED incident command. The OT team reported technical issues with the two-way radios, so they shifted to internet-based messaging.

Family

The patient affairs team was handling communication with families but did not use the predesignated area. A major challenge with updating patient families was related to the lack of actual name use, as only MCR identification was available.

Media

As per protocol, release of information to the public was handled by the communication office. The media assembly area was not used as reporters stationed themselves around the ED where families had congregated.

Triage

Patients were triaged at the ED entrance by a senior emergency medicine (EM) physician to the different acuity surge areas within the ED. After rooming in the ED, secondary triage of urgent-level (yellow) patients for primary management on inpatient wards was completed. This helped expand surge capacity and accommodate the large influx of patients presenting to the ED. Most patients triaged to the delayed-level care (green) section were stable with the exception of two who deteriorated but were quickly attended to and moved to a higher acuity section.

Security/crowding

Immediate lockdown of the ED using existing barriers was effective in preventing overcrowding from external crowds (family and visitors). A large internal security team was deployed, which was later reinforced by governmental security forces. The discharge area, however, which had glass doors, was hard to secure. In addition, unsolicited response staff contributed to ED crowding by presenting to the ED rather than their designated unit staging areas.

Surge capacity

Space capacity

Immediately after the activation of the MCI alert, internal medicine (IM) teams opened up inpatient capacity by sending dischargeable patients home. Additional capacity was opened by deploying IM teams to wards with open bed capacity.

To expand the ED care areas further, two additional outpatient clinic areas were used for delayed-level (green) patients.

Supply and equipment

Supplies were mostly readily available, except in inpatient units where suture sets/supplies were missing. The increased load during the MCI unveiled some maintenance problems in existing equipment, specifically related to central-sterilisation and CT injectors.

Personnel

General feedback was that medical staff, trainees, nursing and ancillary staff were sufficient. Given the unique injury patterns, ophthalmology, orthopaedics and plastics teams were stretched, and shortages in housekeeping and transport teams were reported.

Information system

Patient registration

Expansion of the registration staff along with the use of preprepared MCR numbers allowed for quick patient registration. Some MCRs failed to register in the information system on barcode activation, and some bracelets tore, requiring the use of new MCRs, occasionally leading to MCRs’ duplication. Relying on MCRs, without actual names, led to challenges with patient identification.

Workflow

Improved registration processes ensured smooth downstream workflows. Physician ordering and admission processes were noted to be improvements since last activation. Workflow issues related to moving patients who required emergent surgery from the ED to OT were identified. Within the admission workflows, several hard stops were identified that delayed processing bed requests. On the ground, IT support was rotating between sections and assisting with challenges.

Documentation

Limited availability of workstations made documentation through the information system challenging, particularly for medications, with patients often having to tell the physician what they already received. Teams converted to paper documentation that they attached to patients. Some section leads also resorted to assigning task residents, responsible for order entry.

Clinical operations

Flow

Although unidirectional flow of patients on admission and discharge from the ED was preplanned, bottlenecks at elevators were noticed. In addition, patients were unable to navigate back to the designated discharge area from the various surge areas across the hospital.

Clinical coordination

Injured patients were admitted to non-surgical wards under the care of internal medicine teams and other specialists. This necessitated close coordination with surgical teams who had to round on all patients, not just those admitted under the surgical team. Response from nursing teams was considered timely.

Surgical prioritisation

The arrival of many patients needing emergent surgery created unique challenges for the prioritisation of OT cases. As per the MCI protocol, the chair of surgery assumed the role of triaging patients to OT, which was a daunting task with the numerous surge care areas. This led to a few patients arriving at the OT unannounced. There was agreement that each surge care area required a surgical lead to pull surgical resources as needed and coordinate with the chair of surgery on operative priorities.

Resource utilisation

Bottlenecks at diagnostic imaging emerged. While the introduction of secondary triage with early transfer of patients to the floor helped reduce imaging requests in the ED, it was reported that queuing of patients for CT imaging occurred. Some solutions were implemented on the spot, such as dedicating a physician to prioritise patients within the imaging queue. Informants suggested developing protocols to standardise practice for imaging during MCIs.

Discussion

This study highlights key success features and challenges of a hospital MCI response to an unprecedented hybrid warfare MCI incident. The shift away from an ED-based response to a whole-hospital approach with use of secondary triage of urgent-level (yellow) patients for immediate transfer to inpatient wards was critical to rapidly scaling up capacity. Key areas for future improvement focused on further simplifying information system workflows, shifting to paper-based documentation of assessments/interventions, optimising surgical and diagnostic prioritisation processes and developing minimal diagnostic imaging standards of care to reduce imaging related bottlenecks.

Our study relied on a rigorous AAR process followed by a methodical qualitative analysis of stakeholder debrief meeting minutes to support the key findings. While our findings are specific to the unique circumstances of this MCI incident and the single-centre context, we provided detailed descriptions of the setting and the MCI protocol8 to enhance the transferability. In addition, we involved wide stakeholder engagement in the debrief process and employed reflexivity with member checking, maximising inclusion of the perspectives of the various disciplines central to the response.

The scale of these explosions was unprecedented, overwhelming the prehospital transport system and hospitals alike. The 2323 injuries were transported to hospitals across the country, with our medical centre receiving a total of 182 casualties. The first casualty arrived at our ED within 34 min of the incident, with 80% of the subsequent patients arriving within the first 2 hours of activation of our MCI plan. The speed of arrival of patients and the large volume received reinforce the need for a differentiated approach to hospital MCI response in incidents with such high injury loads, requiring hospitals to be able to ramp up capacity within minutes and to scales requiring a whole-system approach well beyond the ED alone.

Unlike other reported explosive-related MCIs, where the overwhelming majority of cases are typically lower-acuity, walking wounded delayed-level (green) cases,9,12 this incident involved an unusually high number of more complex urgent-level (yellow) patients. Leveraging of inpatient capacity early in the response was therefore instrumental to accommodating the large volumes received. This required quick discharge of stable patients from both ED and inpatient units on activation of the MCI alert, followed by use of inpatient beds/teams to oversee the initial care of stable complex patients. While use of outpatient clinics for initial care of low acuity patients is common practice in MCIs,9 13 shifting the primary assessment of complex patients to alternate non-ED settings is not well described. Essential to the success of this model is the ability of non-surgical inpatient teams to safely assess and manage trauma patients. In preparation for this approach, 88 non-surgical residents had undergone structured training on trauma care in MCI. Exploring the safety of this model for wider adoption into hospital MCI plans requires additional research and outcome analysis of the cohort of patients triaged for inpatient primary evaluation.

Secondary triage was based on the use of a two-tiered ED triaging system, with initial use of the modified Careflight system for door triage by a senior EM physician, followed by secondary triage of urgent-level care (yellow) patients in the ED to either inpatient primary care or continued ED care, with the latter reserved for those requiring further diagnostics/interventions to identify life-threatening conditions beyond a medical screening examination. Most MCI triaging tools have been developed for prehospital settings with little data on ED performance.14 The need for in-hospital quick triaging tools is paramount for MCIs, especially in hybrid warfare incidents like this one where the scale and rate of influx of patients makes use of the lengthier traditional triaging systems such as the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) prohibitive. Use of modified Careflight for in-hospital MCI triage has been reported in prior studies;5 however, it has not been assessed for its reliability and validity in determining resource needs within the ED.15,17 Our protocol for secondary triage of urgent-level (yellow) patients is another area that requires further research on reliability and outcome prognostication.

This MCI had a high proportion of patients requiring operative interventions. In fact, 35 (19.23%) required operative care within the first 12 hours, compared with other reported explosion-related MCIs where the percentage of cases requiring emergent operative care ranged between 8.8% and 11.8%.9 10 This reflects the unique aspect of this incident, where hybrid warfare was used, combining technological sabotage of personal-use devices with explosions.2 While the existing plan requires all surgical cases to be channelled through the surgical chair/delegate, the number of surge locations used was challenging to keep up with. This highlights the need for surgical section leads supporting the surgical chair/delegate in pulling surgical resources and prioritising cases within different areas of care. Further, the replicated injury patterns, with high ophthalmological and hand injuries, strained the respective specialties in ways not typically experienced in other MCIs. Ophthalmology faculty from affiliated organisations, located farther from the incident, were called in to reinforce the primary team. Ophthalmology teams conducted rounds on all patients to prioritise more salvageable cases, and all elective ophthalmological procedures were cancelled for several days to focus resources on managing explosion-related casualties.

Diagnostic imaging emerged as another clinical bottleneck. While directing physicians to minimal standards of usage was done throughout the MCI training, no clear criteria or protocols were used. The secondary triage to the floor reduced imaging requests in the ED. Nevertheless, a bottleneck remained that was addressed by designating an emergency physician to work with the radiologist stationed in the CT room to prioritise patients within the imaging queue. The challenge of overwhelming diagnostic imaging has been well described in other studies on MCIs and raises the need for standardised protocols for minimal care standards for imaging in MCI.18 19

Few studies have looked at information system performance in MCI. One study identified challenges with patient registration, tracking, documentation and order entry.20 After the Beirut Port Blast, similar issues with information systems in our MCI response were identified, with patient registration being a dominant challenge, as well as workflows related to admissions and lack of on-site IT support.21 Optimising these workflows and the integration of on-site IT support role during MCI led to improvements in these areas since the last response. However, information system workflows related to transfers to the OT and documentation emerged as persistent challenges. Limited availability of workstations made documentation through the information system unfeasible. Formalising paper-based documentation of assessments/interventions and further simplifying transfer workflows emerged as key recommendations.

Conclusion

This study highlights the strengths and challenges of a hospital response to a large hybrid-warfare MCI. Whole-hospital mobilisation, early secondary triage and optimised information systems emerged as key success features that supported patient flow and operational efficiency. Challenges faced were related to surgical transfer workflows, documentation processes and surgical and diagnostic prioritisation protocols. Future research on the reliability of some of the key success features, specifically use of ED secondary triage protocols, is needed to support wider adoption of this approach. In addition, developing minimal imaging standards in MCIs is needed to reduce bottlenecks in this area.
