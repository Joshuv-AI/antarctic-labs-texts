Frailty predicts the onset and dynamic progression of cardio-renal-metabolic multimorbidity: a multistate modeling analysis from the UK Biobank
Authors: Lulu Liu, Xuhou Ji, Guangzan Yu, Yunlong Wang, Hua He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty has increasingly been recognized as an important contributor to cardio-renal-metabolic (CRM) diseases. However, most previous studies have focused on isolated outcomes or cross-sectional disease status, lacking a dynamic perspective on disease progression. This study aimed to evaluate the predictive value of frailty for CRM onset and its multistage progression using multistate modeling.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study of 85,003 UK Biobank participants free of CRM diseases at baseline, frailty was categorized as robust, prefrail, and frail. Associations with CRM incidence and progression were examined using Cox proportional hazards and multistate models. The predictive performance of frailty was further assessed using the area under the curve (AUC), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsOver a mean follow-up of 12.0 years, 39.6% of participants were robust, 51.9% were prefrail, and 8.5% were frail. Frailty was independently associated with higher risks of CRM multimorbidity in both incidence and progression analyses. Compared with the robust group, the prefrail group had 28.2%, 36.5%, and 38.0% higher risks of first, double, and triple CRM outcomes, respectively (all p < 0.01), while the frail group had corresponding increases of 81.2%, 118.2%, and 171.1% (all p < 0.001). In multistate models, prefrail participants had 27.5% and 9.1% higher risks of transitioning from a healthy state to the first and from the first to double CRM diseases, respectively (both p < 0.05), whereas the frail group showed respective increases of 78.9% and 32.0% (both p < 0.001). Frailty demonstrated modest discriminative ability for CRM outcomes and provided statistically significant and meaningful improvements in NRI and IDI.ConclusionFrailty was independently associated with both the onset and progression of CRM diseases. Incorporating frailty assessment into risk stratification and preventive frameworks may enhance early identification and mitigation of CRM multimorbidity. Further large-scale prospective studies are warranted to validate these associations and clarify their clinical and public health implications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02153-9.Keywords: Frailty, Cardio-renal-metabolic multimorbidity, Multi-state model, UK Biobank

Introduction

With the acceleration of global population aging and the continuous rise in chronic disease prevalence, multimorbidity, defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions within an individual, has become one of the major public health challenges worldwide [1–3]. Among the various patterns of disease clustering, cardio-renal-metabolic (CRM) multimorbidity, which encompasses cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and chronic kidney disease (CKD), is one of the most prevalent and detrimental combinations. This cluster not only increases the risk of functional impairment and premature death but also imposes a substantial healthcare and socioeconomic burden. The cardiac, renal, and metabolic systems are intricately interconnected through overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms, creating a self-perpetuating cycle that accelerates disease development and adds complexity to clinical management [4–7].

Current evidence indicates that the development of CRM multimorbidity follows an ordered and cumulative process rather than occurring by chance, generally progressing from an initially healthy state to the onset of a single chronic disease and eventually advancing to multiple concurrent conditions [8, 9]. However, most previous studies have focused on single outcomes, such as incident cardiovascular disease or diabetes, with limited attention to the dynamic process of disease accumulation. Therefore, exploring the transition process of CRM multimorbidity is crucial for identifying high-risk populations and developing stage-specific prevention and intervention strategies.

Recently, several important studies have applied multistate models to characterize CRM progression. For example, Tang et al. [10] investigated the association between triglyceride glucose-body mass index and CRM trajectories, while Tian et al. [11] evaluated multiple insulin resistance-related indices in relation to cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) development. These studies have substantially advanced understanding of metabolic risk in CRM or CMM evolution. However, they primarily focused on biochemical or metabolic indicators, and whether broader measures of systemic vulnerability provide additional prognostic insight remains uncertain.

Frailty is a common syndrome among the older people, defined by reduced physiological resilience and heightened susceptibility to external stressors [12, 13]. It typically manifests as reduced muscle mass and strength, decreased endurance, and progressive decline across multiple physiological systems. Frailty is often defined as a clinical syndrome comprising symptoms such as weight loss, exhaustion, low physical activity, slow walking speed, and low grip strength [14–16]. Positioned between robust health and overt disease, frailty predisposes individuals to a range of adverse outcomes, including multimorbidity, functional impairment, and mortality [17, 18]. With the accelerating pace of global aging, frailty has become a pressing public health concern, substantially increasing the risk of both cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular diseases [19, 20]. Moreover, frailty and CRM multimorbidity share several pathophysiological mechanisms, such as chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and endothelial impairment, which may jointly drive deterioration in the cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic systems [21, 22]. Unlike traditional metabolic indices, frailty captures cumulative decline across multiple physiological domains and may therefore reflect the organism’s global susceptibility to disease progression. From the perspective of the American Heart Association (AHA) cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) framework [5], such vulnerability may complement established risk stratification strategies by integrating functional status with organ-specific pathology. Despite their close interrelationship, the causal role of frailty in the development and progression of CRM multimorbidity remains poorly understood.

Therefore, taking advantage of the extensive longitudinal data available in the UK Biobank, we intended to: (1) explore the relationship between frailty and the onset of CRM diseases, and (2) assess the predictive performance of frailty using several discrimination measures; (3) examine how frailty influences disease progression through the application of multistate modeling. By moving beyond metabolic biomarkers and incorporating a multidimensional measure of physiological reserve, our study aims to extend existing CRM research and evaluate whether frailty provides incremental value in identifying individuals at high risk of transition across disease stages. Our findings contribute to improving CRM disease prediction and may help guide targeted prevention and management strategies.

Materials and methods

Data sources

This research utilized data from the UK Biobank, a large prospective cohort that enrolled more than 500,000 adults across England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010. Participants provided extensive baseline information via touchscreen questionnaires, underwent physical assessments, and contributed biological samples. The study design and data collection procedures have been comprehensively described elsewhere [23]. The UK Biobank study received ethical clearance from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The current analysis was conducted under UK Biobank Application Number 177,024.

Among the 501,954 participants enrolled in the UK Biobank, individuals with a history of CVD, including ischemic heart disease (IHD) and stroke, T2DM, or CKD were first excluded (n = 48,101). We further excluded participants lacking complete data on frailty (n = 339,618), as well as those with missing information on covariates (n = 29,232). Following these exclusions, 85,003 participants remained in the final sample for the association analyses (Fig. 1A). To assess potential selection bias, baseline characteristics of included and excluded participants were compared, with results presented in Table S1.

Physical frailty assessment

We assessed physical frailty using the Fried’s phenotype, which is the most widely applied operational definition of frailty in epidemiological research and offers greater clinical practicality compared with the frailty index or frailty scale [14, 20]. The frailty phenotype was defined based on five components: weight loss, exhaustion, low physical activity, slow walking speed, and low grip strength. The operational definitions of these criteria were adapted to align with the variables available in the UK Biobank dataset, as described in previous studies [14–16]. Comprehensive definitions of the criteria can be found in Table S2. Consistent with prior studies [14–16], participants were categorized as robust (meeting none of the criteria), prefrail (meeting one or two criteria), or frail (meeting three or more criteria).

Evaluation of outcomes

In this study, the primary outcome was defined as the incidence of CRM diseases during the follow-up period. Disease events were ascertained using diagnostic codes from the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). The classification of CRM conditions was based on established definitions reported in previous research [7, 9, 24]. The first CRM event was defined as the initial occurrence of any of the following: CVD, including IHD (I20-I25) and stroke (I60-I69), T2DM (E11), or CKD (N18) [10, 25, 26]. Participants with two concurrent CRM conditions were categorized as having double CRM, whereas those with all three conditions simultaneously were classified as triple CRM. Participants who had any CRM prior to baseline were excluded from the risk set for incident CRM from a disease-free state (left censoring). Follow-up for each participant began at the date of UK Biobank enrollment and continued until the earliest of incident CRM outcome, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period. Participants without an observed event during follow-up were right-censored at the last date of observation (May 31, 2024), with outcome information obtained via linkage to national death registries.

Covariates

Drawing on prior studies and clinical considerations [10, 11, 27, 28], we included the following covariates: age (continuous, in years), sex, race, body mass index (BMI), Townsend deprivation index (TDI) (continuous, with larger values reflecting increased deprivation), education levels (high (college or university degree, nursing, teaching and others), intermediate (A (advanced)/AS levels or equivalent, O (ordinary) levels/General Certificate of Secondary Education or equivalent and Certificate of Secondary Education or equivalent), low (National Vocational Qualification or Higher National Diploma or equivalent) or other (none of the above)) [29], smoking status (never, ever, current), drinking status (never, ever, current), sleep duration (< 7 h/day, 7–8 h/day, > 8 h/day), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP, continuous, in mmHg), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c, continuous, in mmol/L), C-reactive protein (CRP, continuous, in mg/L), total cholesterol (TC, continuous, in mmol/L), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, continuous, in mmol/L), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, continuous, in mmol/L). Sex and race were obtained from baseline self-reported data. Most sociodemographic and lifestyle variables were self-reported, whereas physical measurements and biochemical markers were assessed through standardized examinations and central laboratory assays conducted by the UK Biobank.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population were described according to the occurrence of the first CRM disease. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using t-tests for parametric continuous variables, Kruskal-Wallis tests for non-parametric continuous variables, and Pearson’s chi-square tests for categorical variables. The relationship between frailty and the risk of CRM diseases, both cumulatively (first, double, triple) and individually (IHD, stroke, T2DM, CKD), was evaluated using multivariable Cox regression. Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and race; and Model 2 was additionally adjusted for the following covariates, including BMI, TDI, education level, smoking status, alcohol status, sleep duration, HbA1c, CRP, TC, HDL-C, and LDL-C. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and the global test was used to evaluate overall model validity. For covariates that violated the PH assumption, time-dependent effects were modeled by introducing interactions with follow-up time. Detailed results of the PH assumption tests are presented in Tables S3-S9. Additionally, the Kaplan-Meier (KM) approach was used to estimate and compare the cumulative incidence of CRM diseases among different frailty groups, with differences assessed using the log-rank test. Consistent with prior research [30, 31], we evaluated the incremental predictive value of frailty by developing a standard model incorporating the covariates from Model 2 and an extended model that additionally included frailty. The net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were then calculated to evaluate whether the inclusion of frailty improved the prediction of CRM disease outcomes [32]. The discriminative ability of frailty for CRM disease risk was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and predictive accuracy was quantified by estimating the time-specific area under the curve (AUC) at 5, 10, and 12 years. Extending the competing risk approach, the multi-state model provides a framework to explore the influence of risk factors across multiple stages of disease development and outcomes. This methodology has been described in previous studies [25, 33]. In the present study, we applied a multi-state model to explore the influence of frailty on the temporal progression of CRM diseases, tracking transitions from a disease-free state to the development of first, double, and triple CRM conditions. Detailed result of the PH assumption tests is presented in Table S10. Figure 1B depicts the progression of CRM diseases, which was classified into three transition stages: (1) baseline (disease-free) to the first CRM event, (2) first CRM to double CRM, and (3) double CRM to triple CRM. When stage transitions occurred on the same day, the earlier stage was assigned a start date 0.5 days before the next stage, as done in prior research [25, 34]. In secondary analyses, we further explored whether the associations between frailty and disease progression varied according to the subtype of the initial CRM condition (IHD, stroke, T2DM, or CKD). This assessment covered transitions from baseline to each specific first CRM subtype, from first to double CRM diseases, and from double to triple CRM diseases. Death was considered an absorbing state across all transitions to accurately reflect participants exiting the progression process. We performed subgroup analyses to assess whether the observed associations were consistent across key demographic and lifestyle factors, including age (< 60 vs. ≥60 years), sex, overweight/obesity status (BMI < 25 vs. ≥25 kg/m²), smoking history (never vs. ever), and alcohol consumption (never vs. ever). These subgroups may warrant particular focus in future public health strategies targeting the early prevention and management of CRM multimorbidity. Multiplicative interaction terms between frailty and each subgroup variable were included in Cox proportional hazards models, with p values for interaction reported. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the findings, including excluding participants who developed CRM within the first two years of follow-up to reduce potential reverse causality, further adjusting for baseline use of antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering medications, and applying Fine-Gray competing risk models to account for all-cause mortality. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study individuals

Table 1 demonstrated the baseline characteristics of the research participants. Among 85,003 participants, 39.6% were robust, 51.9% were prefrail, and 8.5% were frail. The mean follow-up time to the first CRM event was 12.0 years. Compared with the robust group, frail individuals were older, prone to being women, had lower educational attainment, and experienced greater socioeconomic deprivation (all p < 0.001). Unhealthy lifestyle behaviors were also more common among frail individuals, including current smoking, former drinking, and both short and long sleep duration (all p < 0.001). Regarding anthropometric and clinical indicators, frail participants had significantly higher levels of BMI, HbA1c, and CRP, but lower levels of HDL-C, LDL-C, and TC (all p < 0.001). In addition, frail individuals exhibited slightly lower SBP compared with the robust group, while DBP did not differ significantly across groups. Overall, frailty was closely associated with adverse sociodemographic characteristics, unhealthy lifestyle factors, and metabolic disturbances.

Correlations of frailty with CRM diseases

In Cox regression analyses, frailty was independently linked to elevated risks of CRM outcomes, whether only demographic variables were adjusted (Model 1) or additional lifestyle and clinical factors were further included (Model 2) (Table 2). In Model 2, prefrail individuals had a 28.2% higher risk of developing a first CRM condition, whereas frail individuals had an 81.2% higher risk. The excess risk increased with the accumulation of conditions: for double CRM outcome, the risks rose by 36.5% in prefrail and 118.2% in frail individuals; for triple CRM outcome, the risks further increased to 38.0% and 171.1%, demonstrating a clear dose-response relationship. For specific conditions, prefrail was associated with 27.8%, 33.4%, 36.9%, and 20.3% higher risks of IHD, stroke, DM, and CKD, respectively, whereas frail conferred substantially greater risks of 85.9%, 86.5%, 83.3%, and 77.1%. Among these outcomes, the risk increase was most pronounced for DM, while the increases for IHD, stroke, and CKD were of similar magnitude. These results demonstrate that frailty may represent an independent risk factor for CRM outcomes, with risks rising progressively according to frailty severity and the accumulation of comorbid conditions.

Frail participants consistently exhibited the highest event rates, prefrail individuals had intermediate risks, and robust individuals had the lowest, demonstrating a clear dose-response relationship across frailty categories. Role of frailty in the trajectory of CRM multimorbidity progression

Table 3 presents the associations between frailty and the trajectory of CRM based on a multi-state model. Frailty appeared to be linked to an elevated risk of the first CRM event, with a tendency toward a dose-response relationship. Compared with the robust group, the risk of the first CRM event was estimated to be approximately 27.5% higher in the prefrail group and 78.9% higher in the frail group, suggesting that frailty may play a role in the early stages of multimorbidity development. In disease-specific analyses, frailty seemed to have a particularly notable association with the risk of stroke and T2DM, with increases of about 93.9% and 82.6%, respectively, in the frail group. These observations may imply that frailty contributes to the onset of specific CRM diseases, potentially through mechanisms such as metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, or vascular dysfunction. Frailty also appeared to be related to a higher risk of progression from the first CRM to double CRM, particularly in the context of IHD and stroke. However, its association with the transition from double CRM to triple CRM was less evident, suggesting that its influence may be more pronounced in the earlier stages of disease progression.

Incremental predictive values of frailty for CRM diseases incidence

Incremental predictive value analysis (Table 4) demonstrated that incorporating frailty into conventional risk prediction models modestly but significantly improved the prediction of the first CRM event. Specifically, frailty contributed additional information beyond traditional risk factors, enhancing both risk classification and discrimination. These findings suggest that frailty captures dimensions of vulnerability not fully reflected by conventional clinical markers, and its inclusion in prediction models may help identify high-risk individuals more accurately, thereby informing early preventive and personalized intervention strategies.

Subgroup, interaction, and sensitivity analyses

The observed associations between frailty and CRM risk were generally consistent across subgroups (Fig. 4, Figure S1). Stronger associations were noted in older participants, females, individuals without overweight/obesity, and those with a history of smoking; these differences were supported by significant interaction p values for age, sex, BMI, and smoking (all p for interaction < 0.001). Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of our findings. Excluding participants who developed CRM within the first two years of follow-up did not materially change the associations (Figure S2). Additional adjustment for baseline use of antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering medications yielded results consistent with the main analyses (Figure S3). Applying Fine-Gray competing risk models for all-cause mortality also produced similar effect estimates (Figure S4). Overall, these analyses reinforce the potential value of frailty in predicting CRM risk across different demographic and clinical settings, though further validation in independent cohorts is warranted.

Discussion

Drawing on data from 85,003 participants in the UK Biobank, this large prospective cohort study investigated how different frailty relate to the onset and progression of CRM multimorbidity. Frailty was consistently linked to an elevated risk of CRM diseases, showing a clear dose-response relationship, and frailty demonstrated modest predictive performance. Multistate Cox regression analyses further indicated that frailty was independently related to the progression of CRM multimorbidity, particularly from baseline to first CRM, while the association with the transition from double to triple CRM was less pronounced. Disease-specific analyses indicated that frailty was especially linked to higher risks of stroke and T2DM. In addition, frailty achieved modest performance in predictive measures, including AUC, NRI, and IDI. Taken together, these results stress the relevance of incorporating frailty assessment into early risk stratification and preventive strategies for CRM multimorbidity.

This study demonstrated that frailty is a critical independent risk factor for CRM diseases and their individual components. Higher risks were observed in individuals with prefrail or frail, and the magnitude of risk increased progressively with frailty severity, indicating a clear dose-response relationship. In particular, the frail group exhibited substantially independent associations compared with the prefrail group, highlighting frailty as a more pronounced vulnerability phenotype. When disease-specific outcomes were examined, frailty was consistently linked with increased risks of IHD, stroke, T2DM, and CKD, with the greatest relative increase observed for T2DM, while the risks for IHD, stroke, and CKD were of comparable magnitude. These findings suggest that frailty may contribute to the onset of diverse cardiometabolic and renal diseases through shared biological pathways, such as chronic inflammation, impaired metabolic regulation, vascular dysfunction, and reduced physiological reserve across multiple organ systems [21, 22]. Our results are consistent with previous studies indicating that frailty is linked to elevated risks of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease, and plays a crucial role in multimorbidity clustering [35–38]. Importantly, even prefrail was linked to increased risks, underscoring the value of early detection and management of frailty for the primary prevention of CRM conditions.

Numerous studies have investigated links between frailty and individual or combined CRM conditions; however, most have been limited to cross-sectional evaluations of disease status [39–41]. Most of these studies focus on the cross-sectional presence of single or multiple conditions, overlooking the dynamic role of frailty in the development and progression of CRM diseases across stages. This cross-sectional perspective hinders understanding of frailty’s dynamic role in disease development. Extending this work, a multistate modeling framework was implemented in this study to comprehensively examine the stage-specific associations between frailty and CRM disease progression. Our findings indicate that the associations between frailty and disease transitions exhibit notable stage-specific differences. In subtype analyses, the transition from baseline to the first CRM event was particularly significant, with stroke showing the strongest association. Participants in the stroke group were tended to progress to double CRM, highlighting the predictive role of frailty at this stage. By contrast, in the T2DM and CKD groups, frail and prefrail were not significantly associated with transitions to double CRM; similarly, in the IHD group, prefrail lost its predictive significance. Furthermore, the transition from double CRM to triple CRM was generally not significant, suggesting that frailty may exert a more prominent influence in the earlier stages of multimorbidity development rather than in later stages. These findings suggest that frailty is not only associated with the occurrence of CRM diseases, but also plays a particularly important role in the early phases of disease transition. This underscores the importance of incorporating frailty assessment into clinical risk stratification, with potential implications for developing timely and stage-specific prevention and intervention strategies.

Our incremental predictive value analysis underscores that incorporating frailty into conventional prediction models offers modest but statistically significant improvements in forecasting the first CRM event. While the absolute gains in discrimination and reclassification were limited, they reveal that frailty captures dimensions of risk beyond traditional factors, reflecting a multidimensional vulnerability that encompasses physiological decline, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation. The modest magnitude of improvement likely reflects both the strength of existing predictors in conventional models and the heterogeneous, dynamic nature of frailty, which a single baseline assessment may not fully capture. Nonetheless, even small enhancements in predictive accuracy can translate into meaningful clinical and public health benefits, particularly in aging populations where frailty prevalence and multimorbidity burden are rising. Incorporating frailty into risk assessment frameworks may thus improve early identification of high-risk individuals, enabling targeted prevention and intervention strategies. Future work should investigate dynamic frailty assessments, integration with molecular biomarkers, and the real-world utility of frailty-informed prediction models to enhance CRM disease prevention and management.

From a clinical application standpoint, our results indicate that assessing frailty could provide a useful complement to current risk stratification approaches for CRM multimorbidity. Frailty, as a multidimensional construct reflecting physiological decline, systemic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and reduced homeostatic reserve, demonstrated a consistent and stage-specific association with CRM progression. Notably, frailty showed independent predictive value in the early stages of disease transition, particularly from baseline to the first CRM event and from stroke to double CRM, suggesting heightened sensitivity to early metabolic and systemic disturbances. These findings imply that incorporating frailty assessment into clinical decision-making and public health screening strategies could enhance early risk identification and enable more tailored prevention and intervention approaches. Given that frailty can be assessed through simple, non-invasive clinical measures and functional tests, it represents a practical and cost-effective addition to current risk prediction frameworks. Such integration may be particularly valuable in aging populations, where frailty prevalence is high and multimorbidity burden is increasing.

Because frailty in our study was defined using the Fried phenotype, a standardized and clinically feasible instrument, we propose that frailty could be incorporated as an additional layer of risk stratification within the AHA CKM framework. From an operational perspective, frailty screening could be performed in outpatient or primary care settings alongside routine evaluations of individuals with obesity, diabetes, hypertension, or early kidney dysfunction. Identification of prefrail or frail status may then serve as a trigger for escalation of management intensity, including closer follow-up, earlier referral to multidisciplinary care teams (e.g., cardiology, nephrology, endocrinology, rehabilitation), prioritization of lifestyle and exercise interventions, and adaptation of therapeutic targets according to reduced physiological reserve. Importantly, frailty captures dimensions of biological aging and vulnerability not fully reflected by conventional cardiometabolic markers, and its integration into CKM risk evaluation may enhance prognostic precision, improve shared decision-making, and support more personalized allocation of preventive resources.

The present research offers multiple significant advantages. Firstly, the large-scale UK Biobank cohort affords strong statistical power and improves the robustness of our results. Secondly, the extensive control for diverse demographic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates enhances the credibility of the observed associations. Thirdly, the use of a prospective cohort design with long-term follow-up allows for a robust examination of the temporal relationship between frailty and CRM multimorbidity. Finally, the robustness and generalizability of the results within the studied population are further supported by consistent findings across sensitivity and subgroup analyses. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the relatively low response rate of the UK Biobank may introduce potential selection bias and affect the generalizability of our findings. To evaluate this concern, we compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants. Although many comparisons reached statistical significance given the very large sample size, the standardized mean differences (SMDs) were generally small. Most demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, blood pressure, lipid levels, and frailty status, showed SMDs below 0.1. Moderate differences were observed for BMI, TDI, and educational attainment. Importantly, these factors were comprehensively adjusted for in the multivariable models, which should mitigate their potential influence on the effect estimates. Therefore, while selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out, the overall comparability between included and excluded participants suggests that its impact on the main conclusions is likely to be limited. Secondly, although data from non-Western populations remain relatively sparse, some evidence provides supportive signals. For instance, results from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, a nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older adults, have indicated that both baseline frailty and worsening frailty transitions are associated with increased cardiometabolic risk, whereas improvement in frailty may confer risk reduction [39]. Nevertheless, differences in healthcare systems, disease detection, and survival patterns across regions mean that caution is still required when extrapolating our findings. More broadly, we emphasize that validation of frailty-integrated CRM risk stratification in more diverse cohorts, particularly in low and middle-income countries, is warranted. Populations in these settings may exhibit distinct frailty burden, healthcare access, and disease trajectories, underscoring the need for targeted research to ensure global applicability of our findings. Thirdly, frailty status was assessed only at baseline, which restricts insight into its temporal dynamics and may overlook changes over time. Although repeated assessments could provide a more accurate depiction of frailty trajectories, in the UK Biobank such data were available only for a relatively small subset of participants who attended repeat visits, thereby reducing statistical power and potentially introducing selection bias. As a result, some misclassification of frailty over time may have occurred, which could attenuate the observed associations [19]. Therefore, our analyses relied primarily on baseline measurements to maximize sample size, representativeness, and analytical stability. Future studies with more complete longitudinal frailty data should incorporate repeated measurements and trajectory modeling to better capture frailty dynamics, improve causal inference, and examine their impact on CRM risk and progression. Different frailty definitions might also yield varying results, and the lack of a standardized approach remains a challenge for large-scale studies. Lastly, despite consistent associations observed in our analyses, further research is warranted to examine the clinical utility of frailty assessment for CRM multimorbidity prevention and management. Future studies with diverse populations and repeated frailty assessments are needed to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

Frailty was independently associated with both the onset and progression of CRM diseases. Incorporating frailty assessment into risk stratification and preventive frameworks may enhance early identification and mitigation of CRM multimorbidity. Further large-scale prospective studies are warranted to validate these associations and clarify their clinical and public health implications.
