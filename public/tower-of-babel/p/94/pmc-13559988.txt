CATS-BIT-ME Score: A Machine Learning and Conventional Risk Model for Prognosis in Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Chun-Ting Ho, Elise Chia-Hui Tan, Pei-Chang Lee, I-Cheng Lee, Jiing-Chyuan Luo, Yi-Hsiang Huang, Teh-Ia Huo, Ming-Chih Hou, Jaw-Ching Wu, Chien-Wei Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsThe prognosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is influenced by various factors. This study aimed to develop and validate a novel machine learning (ML)-based risk score to stratify HCC patients into prognostic groups and compare its predictive accuracy with conventional staging systems and biomarkers.MethodsThis retrospective study included 4038 HCC patients diagnosed between 2012 and 2023. Patients were randomly divided into training (n = 2827) and validation (n = 1211) cohorts in a 7:3 ratio. Prognostic factors for overall survival were identified using conventional Cox proportional hazards models and ML-based least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression. Variables significant in both methods were incorporated into a risk score, with each parameter scaled from 0 to 100, based on multivariable Cox regression coefficients. Patients were categorized into 3 risk groups using the 33rd and 66th percentiles.ResultsAfter a median follow-up of 42 months (interquartile range: 33.6–50.4), 1931 deaths occurred, with a 5-year overall survival rate of 26.0%. Eleven significant prognostic variables were identified: age, maximum tumor size, extrahepatic metastasis, macrovascular invasion, serum albumin, bilirubin, creatinine, aspartate aminotransferase, lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio, alpha-fetoprotein level, and treatment modality. The CATS-BIT-ME score demonstrated high predictive accuracy for overall survival (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve: 0.800) and effective risk stratification within the 5 years (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve range: 0.852–0.884). It outperformed 13 conventional staging systems and biomarkers in prognostic precision.ConclusionThe ML-based CATS-BIT-ME score provides an accurate and reliable tool for stratifying HCC patients into distinct prognostic groups, enhancing personalized disease management and clinical decision-making.Keywords: Hepatocellular Carcinoma, Machine Learning, CATS-BIT-ME Score, Prognostic Model, Survival

Introduction

Primary liver cancer ranks as the sixth most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide, with an estimated 866,136 new cases reported in 2022.1 Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the predominant form of primary liver cancer, remains a significant global health burden.2 Risk factors for liver carcinogenesis, such as chronic viral hepatitis and excessive alcohol consumption, are well established.3 While progress has been made toward eliminating viral hepatitis, the rising prevalence of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease has become a major driver of the increasing incidence of HCC.4 Despite advancements in treatment strategies, HCC prognosis remains suboptimal, with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate in the United States improving modestly from 3% during 1975 to 1977 to 22% during 2014 to 2020.5 Similarly, data from the Taiwan Cancer Registry showed gradual year-by-year improvements in HCC outcomes, with median survival rising from 2.25 years in 2011 to 3.00 years in 2019.6 To improve survival and quality of life for HCC patients, identifying key prognostic risk factors and optimizing treatment modalities is essential.

The prognosis of HCC was determined by various factors, including tumor characteristics, viral etiology, performance status, liver functional reserve, portal hypertension, and treatment modalities.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Tumor-related factors, such as size, macrovascular invasion, and extrahepatic metastasis, have long been considered key prognostic indicators and formed the mainstream of currently used Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system.12 However, additional factors impacting HCC prognosis have been identified. For instance, our previous study demonstrated that in patients with hepatitis B virus–related HCC who underwent surgical resection, tumor factors were associated with early recurrence within 2 years postsurgery, whereas high HBV viral load and active hepatic inflammation correlated with late recurrence.8 Antiviral therapies have been shown to reduce the risk of HCC occurrence, recurrence following surgical resection, and mortality in patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors.13, 14, 15 Additionally, our recent studies have highlighted the crucial role of nutritional status, muscle mass, and adipose tissue in determining HCC prognosis.16,17

Recent studies have also explored predictive tools for HCC outcomes. For instance, Serenari et al18 developed a nomogram incorporating liver stiffness, age, and liver function tests to predict postoperative complications in HCC patients following surgical resection. Campani et al19 reported that liver functional reserve, alongside serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) dynamic changes, correlated with outcomes in HCC patients treated with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab. These findings emphasize the multifactorial nature of HCC prognosis. However, the interplay among these factors remains incompletely understood.

Various serum biomarkers and scoring systems, such as albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade, fibrosis 4 (FIB-4) score, and the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score, have been proposed for HCC prognosis.20,21 While the BCLC staging system is widely used and had robust performance in patient stratification, it does not incorporate factors such as inflammation and nutrition, leading to variability in prognosis among patients within the same stage.22 These limitations highlight the need for improved prognostic models that integrate additional clinical and biochemical factors.

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a promising tool for liver cancer research.23 Our recent research demonstrated the potential of ML in identifying critical prognostic factors and predicting outcomes in patients with early-stage HCC.24 Another ML-based model incorporating clinical and imaging features has also shown promise in stratifying HCC patients.25 This study aims to identify key prognostic factors and develop an ML-based risk score to stratify HCC patients into distinct risk groups, enhancing personalized disease management and prognostic accuracy.

Materials and Methods

Data Source and Study Population

This retrospective cohort study used HCC registry data obtained from Taipei Veterans General Hospital (TVGH), a major medical center in northern Taiwan with 3160 beds and approximately 8000 outpatient visits per day, handling a substantial volume of patient care. Newly diagnosed HCC patients are prospectively enrolled in the registry data, which collects comprehensive information, including demographics, etiology, laboratory results, tumor characteristics, treatments, and health outcomes. Further details of the HCC registry have been described in previous studies.24 After excluding patients with incomplete datasets, a total of 4038 consecutive treatment-naïve HCC patients were enrolled between January 2012 and December 2023 and randomly assigned to training and validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio (Figure 1).

Study flowchart.

Regarding ethical considerations, this study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of TVGH, Taiwan (IRB case number: 2024-06-029CC). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB of TVGH because of the retrospective design and the use of deidentified patient data.

Outcome Measure

The primary outcome of the study was OS. Vital status of each patient was obtained from the electronic health records and follow-up telephone interviews conducted by trained nurses. The index date was defined as the date of HCC diagnosis. Patients were followed until death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period (June 30, 2023).

Treatment Modalities and Covariates

Patients who received surgical resection, local ablative therapy, or liver transplantation were defined as curative treatment. In contrast, noncurative treatments included trans-arterial chemoembolization, molecular targeted therapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and best supportive care. Baseline information was collected at the time of diagnosis and included demographics (age and sex), tumor characteristics (BCLC stage, number of tumors, tumor size, single large hepatocellular carcinoma [SLHCC]), viral hepatitis status (hepatitis B surface antigen–reactive and anti–hepatitis C antibody–reactive), presence of extrahepatic metastasis and macrovascular invasion, as well as baseline laboratory data (platelet count, albumin, creatinine, total bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase/aspartate aminotransferase [AST], lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio [LMR], prognostic nutritional index [PNI], AFP, ALBI grade, and FIB-4 index). SLHCC was defined as a solitary tumor ≥5 cm in diameter, without vascular invasion, distant metastasis, or high tumor burden.26 Binary cutoffs for variables were determined based on clinical and statistical properties through 3 approaches. For standard biochemical markers, such as alanine aminotransferase, AFP, etc., cutoffs adhered to institutional clinical reference limits. For composite parameters (LMR and PNI), optimal cutoffs were derived via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the Youden index, with minor adjustments for clinical utility. Lastly, baseline demographics and tumor characteristics (age, ALBI grade, tumor number, and tumor size) were stratified according to clinical consensus and validated literature.24

Construction and Validation of Predictive Model and Risk Score

A novel predictive model was developed using a rigorous, two-pronged variable selection workflow. First, all candidate variables were screened via univariable Cox proportional hazards regression, with statistically significant factors subsequently entered into a traditional multivariable Cox model. In parallel, all baseline variables were subjected to the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox model, which is recognized as a foundational ML technique employed to mitigate overfitting and multicollinearity in high-dimensional clinical data.24 Risk factors identified as significant in both models were used to construct the final predictive model. Third, points for each risk factor were assigned based on its regression coefficient from the multivariable Cox model, with the weights individually scaled within a 0 to 100 range for each parameter. Fourth, the total risk score for each individual was calculated and categorized into low-, medium-, and high-risk groups based on the 33rd and 66th percentiles.

Cox-based and ML-based risk scores were validated in both cohorts with 3 steps. We assessed the predictive performance of OS by comparing homogeneity, the Akaike information criterion, and the time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Calibration plots for predicted and observed survival were generated.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile range, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Mann–Whitney U test, Chi-square test, and Fisher exact test were used to compare the distribution of baseline characteristics between the 2 cohorts. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank tests was performed to assess outcomes of interest among the low-, medium-, and high-risk groups. All the analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 24.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and R software (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). LASSO Cox regression and related plots were conducted using the “glmnet” package in R, and time-dependent ROC curves were generated using the “timeROC” package. A two-tailed P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic Characteristics

Among the 4038 HCC patients, 224 were in BCLC stage 0, 1559 in stage A, 909 in stage B, 1036 in stage C, and 310 in stage D. Patients in both the training and validation cohorts were predominantly male, with median ages of 67 and 66 years, respectively. Chronic hepatitis B and C were the primary etiologies of HCC. Approximately 60% of patients had a single tumor, and around 20% presented with SLHCC. Over 80% of patients were classified beyond ALBI grade 1 at diagnosis. No significant differences in baseline demographic characteristics were observed between the 2 cohorts. Detailed baseline clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Biomarker Selection

After a median follow-up of 42.0 months (interquartile range: 33.6–50.4), 1931 patients died, and the 5-year OS rate was 26.0%. As shown in Table 2, multivariate analysis by conventional Cox regression identified several factors significantly associated with OS, including age, maximum tumor size, extrahepatic metastasis, macrovascular invasion, serum levels of albumin, bilirubin, creatinine, AST, AFP, LMR, and treatment modality. LASSO Cox regression was also performed on all clinical variables for optimal factor selection (Supplementary Figure 1A and B), yielding the same key prognostic factors aligned with the multivariable Cox regression model results. When these concurrently selected variables were refitted into the definitive multivariable model, a comparison of their unpenalized coefficients demonstrated perfect alignment along the identity line (Supplementary Figure 2).

Development of the Risk Scores

The β-coefficients from the traditional Cox model for each selected factor, reflecting their influence weight on patients’ OS, were simplified based on their ratios and summed to create a risk score. This resulted in a clinically applicable, ML-based model, the CATS-BIT-ME score (an acronym representing the contributing factors listed in Table 3). The newly developed ML-based CATS-BIT-ME score demonstrated excellent predictive performance for OS in the training cohort with an AUROC of 0.810 (95% confidence interval: 0.795–0.826). Patients were then stratified into 3 risk groups based on 33rd and 66th percentiles of the CATS-BIT-ME score (106 and 260 points, respectively). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis in the training cohort showed excellent stratification ability of this score (P < .001) (Figure 2A).

Calibration, Validation, and Performance of the Machine Learning–Based CATS-BIT-ME Score

The predictive performance of the ML-based CATS-BIT-ME score for OS was further validated in the validation cohort. In this cohort, the CATS-BIT-ME score demonstrated excellent predictive accuracy for OS, with an AUROC of 0.800 (95% confidence interval: 0.775–0.825), effectively differentiating low-, medium-, and high-risk groups. The 5-year OS rates for the 3 groups were 78.1%, 41.9%, and 5.1%, respectively (Figure 2B, P < .001). The calibration plot demonstrated an excellent profile, with predicted mortality probabilities tightly aligning with observed clinical outcomes across the entire risk spectrum (Supplementary Figure 3; Hosmer–Lemeshow test: χ2 = 3.113, P = .927). In subgroup analyses, the CATS-BIT-ME score maintained robust and statistically significant discriminatory power across key clinical populations, including 588 patients (48.6%) at high risk of cirrhosis (FIB-4 score >3.25), albeit with a marginal performance attenuation in those without curative treatment (Supplementary Table 1).

Furthermore, we compared the performance of the ML-based CATS-BIT-ME score with other established prognostic factors and scoring systems for HCC and liver diseases. As shown in Table 4, the CATS-BIT-ME score achieved the highest AUROC and the lowest Akaike information criterion value, outperforming all other factors and scoring systems, including the ALBI score, AST-to-platelet ratio index, LMR, PNI, FIB-4, and the MELD score, BCLC staging system, Japan Integrated Staging score, Taipei integrated scoring system, Hong Kong liver cancer staging system, tumor-node-metastasis staging, Okuda staging, and Tokyo score.27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 The time-dependent ROC curve for the CATS-BIT-ME score demonstrated superior predictive accuracy during the first 5 years following diagnosis, with the AUROC values ranging from 0.852 to 0.884 (Figure 3). These findings underscore the strong predictive performance of the novel CATS-BIT-ME score.

Discussion

This study identified 11 independent risk factors associated with HCC prognosis using conventional Cox regression analysis, which were further validated through an ML-based LASSO Cox regression model. The resulting CATS-BIT-ME score demonstrated superior prognostic accuracy compared to existing HCC staging systems and serum biomarkers. By effectively stratifying patients into distinct risk groups, the score provides a more refined and personalized approach to risk assessment, highlighting the transformative potential of ML in biomarker selection and prognostic modeling for HCC.

In addition to tumor-related factors, field factors—including hepatic functional reserve, systemic inflammation, steatosis, and comorbidities—play critical roles in determining HCC prognosis.15,16,33, 34, 35 The ALBI grade, a widely used indicator of hepatic function in patients with HCC or cirrhosis, has been recognized as an important prognostic factor for OS.20 In line with this, our study identified serum albumin and bilirubin levels, the 2 components of the ALBI grade, as independent predictors for HCC outcomes. Systemic inflammation, indicated by the LMR, was indicated as a survival predictor of HCC,36,37 and was incorporated into the CATS-BIT-ME score. Additionally, our study also found that serum creatinine levels significantly impacted prognosis, aligning with existing evidence that chronic kidney disease is linked to poorer outcomes in HCC.38,39

While the BCLC staging system remains the standard for HCC staging and stratification, prognosis within each stage varies.12,22 The Japan Integrated Staging, Tokyo score, and Okuda staging system have been proposed to address this gap.27, 28, 29 Moreover, by including serum AFP levels, the Cancer of the Liver Italian Program and Taipei integrated scoring system scores revealed good performance on prognosis stratification.30,32 However, these systems are limited by the number of parameters and lack of universal applicability. Recent studies highlight the importance of field factors such as inflammation, fibrosis, and nutritional status in HCC prognosis.17,24,37 The novel CATS-BIT-ME score combines tumor characteristics, liver functional reserve, and field factors to provide a comprehensive assessment of patient prognosis. By outperforming established staging systems, it offers a more accurate and individualized approach to risk stratification in HCC.

The FIB-4 score and the MELD score are commonly used in clinical practice to assess hepatic fibrosis and predict outcomes in end-stage liver diseases, respectively.40,41 However, their prognostic utility in HCC patients has not been fully validated. The CATS-BIT-ME score, which integrates noninvasive serum biomarkers with clinical data, demonstrates significantly superior survival prediction compared to these conventional biomarker scores. This improved prognostic accuracy underscores its potential as a practical and comprehensive tool for personalized disease management in HCC patients.

ML has demonstrated its effectiveness in predicting outcomes across various diseases.42,43 In this study, ML techniques facilitated the development of a robust survival-prediction model for HCC. Compared to our previous work focusing on early-stage HCC,24 we identified additional aggressive tumor characteristics in this study, such as large tumor size, macrovascular invasion, and extrahepatic metastasis, as significant prognostic factors, reflecting the inclusion of patients with more advanced tumor stages. Notably, ML also emphasized the significance of field factors in determining HCC outcomes, reinforcing the concept that tumor development and progression are multifactorial processes influenced by a complex interplay of both tumor-specific and host-related factors.

Despite the excellent performance of the novel CATS-BIT-ME score, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study relied on a cohort from a single, tertiary medical center; therefore, further external validation in prospective, multicenter cohorts across different geographical regions is warranted to verify its global generalizability. Second, chronic viral hepatitis was the predominant etiology of HCC in our cohort, necessitating further investigations to evaluate the model's performance in nonviral HCC populations. Additionally, cutoff values of certain serum markers were determined using ROC curves and the Youden index, and their broad clinical applicability warrants further validation. Moreover, historical hepatic decompensation events were not fully captured, and histologic or imaging data for cirrhosis were incomplete in this retrospective cohort. Consequently, the validated FIB-4 index (cutoff: 3.25) was employed as an objective surrogate for cirrhosis during subgroup analysis.44 Finally, emerging ML techniques, such as Random Survival Forest, XGBoost, and neural networks, may provide additional insights, and their comparative performance should be evaluated in future studies.

Conclusion

The ML-based CATS-BIT-ME score integrates comprehensive factors, effectively stratifying HCC patients into distinct prognostic groups. These findings underscore the potential of ML in advancing personalized treatment strategies and improving outcomes for HCC patients.
