Sputum-negative pulmonary tuberculosis in Shanghai, China: notification, clinical characteristics and outcomes
Authors: Zheyuan Wu, Yaohan Zhou, Jing Chen, Lixin Rao, Xiao Xiao, Ping Zu, Xin Shen, Wenhong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the scaling up of WHO-recommended diagnostics, 40% of pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) cases were still clinically diagnosed without sputum biological confirmation in China. To better understand this early paucibacillary disease, we explored notification, clinical characteristics and outcomes of sputum-negative PTB in Shanghai, China.MethodsWe accessed PTB surveillance data in Shanghai, China, from 2010 to 2019. All presumptive PTB patients underwent at least one sputum biological diagnostic (i.e., smear, culture, GeneXpert MTB/RIF). Any positive was defined as sputum-positive, otherwise sputum-negative. Data of newly diagnosed secondary PTB patients was extracted for analysis. The annual notification of sputum-negative PTB was calculated. Clinical characteristics and treatment outcomes were compared between sputum-negative and positive PTB. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation. Log-binomial, logistic and Cox models were used to adjust potential confounders.ResultsOf 56,851 PTB patients in 2010–2019, 26,858 (47.2%) were sputum-negative. The annual notification declined from 12.2 per 100,000 in 2010 to 7.9 per 100,000 in 2019 (P for trend < 0.001). The median age of sputum-negative PTB patients was 35 (interquartile range 25, 55), with 34.1% of females, 50.7% of local residents. Compared to patients with sputum-positive PTB, sputum-negative PTB patients had a shorter delay in health seeking (10 vs. 12 days, Padj<0.001), but a longer delay in diagnosis (11 vs. 7 days, Padj<0.001). Besides, they had a higher proportion of detection through active screening (Padj<0.001), pleurisy (Padj<0.001), and a lower proportion of pulmonary cavities (Padj<0.001), diabetes (Padj<0.001), bronchial tuberculosis (Padj<0.001). 53,204 PTB patients were successfully treated (95.7% in sputum-negative vs. 91.7% in sputum-positive) and were followed for 368804.5 person-years. Compared to sputum-positive patients, sputum-negative PTB patients showed lower rates of treatment failure (0.2% vs. 0.6%, ORadj=0.56, 95%CI 0.52–0.61), death (1.4% vs. 3.9%, ORadj=0.43, 95%CI 0.38–0.49) and recurrence (2.1% vs. 3.3%, HRadj=0.71, 95%CI 0.63–0.79).ConclusionsAs a less severe disease, sputum-negative PTB had fewer comorbidities, radiological abnormalities, and better treatment outcomes, but a longer delay in diagnosis, which indicated the urgent need for novel biomarkers to enhance early detection of sputum-negative PTB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13162-4.

Background

Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB), a respiratory infectious disease caused by pathogen mycobacterium tuberculosis, is one of the world’s top ten causes of death. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) [1], globally in 2023, there are an estimated 10.8 million cases of PTB, China ranks third in the world in the number of new TB patients, and about 1.25 million people die from PTB in 2023, posing a major threat to the life and health of all mankind. In recent years, tuberculosis prevention and control work has become more difficult, considering that it may be related to the increase in regional population mobility, the destruction of ecological environment, the production of drug-resistant tuberculosis bacteria caused by irregular use of drugs and the double infection caused by AIDS [2].

Active pulmonary TB is usually confirmed by a sputum bacteriological test (e.g., molecular test, smear, culture). Globally in 2023, 2.6 million (38%) of the 6.9 million people notified with PTB were not sputum biologically confirmed [1]. Sputum negativity may arise due to diminished bacillary populations, temporal fluctuations in the quantity of expelled bacilli, HIV infection, and inaccuracies in specimen processing [3]. The presence of subtle symptoms or atypical radiographic signs in these individuals can result in overlooked or delayed diagnoses [4]. The identification of this particular group is crucial as early intervention can potentially halt the progression to more severe disease, thereby reducing morbidity and curbing disease transmission.

Shanghai, a low TB incidence area in China, has provided sputum smear, culture, and conventional DST to all presumptive PTB patients since 2010 [5]. Prior to the adoption of WHO-endorsed rapid diagnostics, the bacteriological confirmation rate for PTB was suboptimal, at around 40% [6]. In an effort to improve this, Xpert MTB/RIF testing was added to the initial diagnostic protocol (smear and culture) for all presumptive PTB cases in Shanghai since 2018. In this study, we explored notification, clinical characteristics and outcomes of sputum-negative PTB in Shanghai, China to better understand this early paucibacillary disease, and improve the diagnosis and management of sputum-negative PTB patients.

Method

Study design and population

Primarily, this study conducted secondary analysis using routinely collected programmatic data. We accessed PTB surveillance data in Shanghai, China, from 2010 to 2019, which included diagnosis, clinical characteristics and outcomes of PTB. Data are collected by TB designated hospitals in Shanghai via a web-based platform, following the standardized electronic format specified by the national TBIMS [7]. Authorized healthcare personnel are responsible for data entry. The collected patient variables encompass a broad spectrum of information, including demographic details (name, ID, gender, date of birth, address), comprehensive clinical records (dates of symptom onset, initial hospital visit, diagnosis, and start and end of anti‑tuberculosis treatment; imaging and laboratory findings such as sputum smear, culture, and molecular testing results; HIV status), treatment documentation (therapeutic regimen, management approach, outcomes, and adverse reactions), as well as specialized follow-up data for suspected drug-resistant TB cases, including dates and outcomes of detailed drug susceptibility testing. A total of 78,298 PTB case have been reported in Shanghai. After excluding 17,236 cases with extrapulmonary TB, TB pleurisy, retreated PTB, or repeated registration, 61,162 case were registered as newly diagnosed PTB. 4311 cases were excluded, due to primary PTB; hematogenous disseminated pulmonary PTB; changed diagnosis, including nontuberculous mycobacteria, pneumonia, lung cancer, old tuberculosis, pneumoconiosis, pulmonary nodules, preventive treatment, silicosis, BCG reaction, pulmonary tuberculosis with insufficient diagnostic basis; human immunodeficiency virus (HIV); sputum biological unconfirmed. After applying the exclusion criteria, a total of 56,851 newly diagnosed secondary PTB patients were ultimately included in the analysis and categorized into sputum-negative (n = 26,858) and sputum-positive (n = 29,993) groups for comparative analysis (Fig. 1).

The provincial TB control program

All presumptive patients with PTB, defined as individuals experiencing cough for more than 2 weeks, identified in a general hospital or community health center are referred to the district PTB hospital. At the referral hospital, sputum smear examination, liquid culture testing, chest radiography, and other necessary diagnostic tests are conducted. GeneXpert MTB/RIF testing was fully implemented from 2018 [8]. Culture-positive strains are subsequently transferred to the TB reference laboratory at Shanghai Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) for species identification using Lowenstein-Jensen culture method and drug susceptibility testing (DST) for first-line drugs (isoniazid/rifampicin/ethambutol/streptomycin). Additionally, Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital conducts liquid culture-based species identification and DST not only for first-line drugs but also three second-line drugs (aminoglycosides/ofloxacin/cycloserine). The TB reference laboratory at Shanghai CDC is responsible for ensuring quality control across all TB laboratories in the surrounding area encompassing 30 PTB hospitals.

Relevant definition

According to the national diagnostic standard for PTB [9], confirmed PTB cases include individuals who test positive for sputum smear microscopy, culture, molecular tests, or have pulmonary lesions of tuberculosis that are confirmed by pathological examination (lung biopsy). Those who do not meet the criteria for confirmed PTB can be clinically diagnosed if other pulmonary diseases are excluded and their chest radiograph supports active PTB. In addition, they must exhibit any of the following: (1) Symptoms of PTB such as cough, expectoration, hemoptysis; (2) Immunological evidence such as a strong purified protein derivative (PPD) skin test reaction, positive Interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA), or positive MTB antibody test; or (3) Extra-pulmonary TB confirmed by pathological examination.

All presumptive PTB patients underwent at least one sputum biological diagnostic (i.e., smear, culture, GeneXpert MTB/RIF). Any positive was defined as sputum-positive, otherwise sputum-negative.

Delay in health seeking: days from the onset of tuberculosis symptoms to the first medical consultation for this case of tuberculosis.

Delay in diagnosis: days from the first medical consultation for this case of tuberculosis to the confirmation of diagnosis.

Treatment outcomes were defined as per the definitions for tuberculosis from the World Health Organization [10] and the “Guideline for Tuberculosis Prevention and Control in China (2020 Edition)” [11]. Treatment failure: A patient whose sputum smear or culture is positive at month 5 or later during treatment. Death: A patient who dies for any reason during the course of tuberculosis treatment. Recurrence: A patient who has previously been treated for tuberculosis, was declared cured or treatment completed at the end of their most recent course of treatment, and is now diagnosed with a new episode of tuberculosis (either a true relapse or a new infection) [12]. The follow - up period ended on December 31, 2021.

Data collection and statistic analysis

The data was extracted from the National TB management information system and imported into Microsoft Excel® (Redmond, Washington, USA).

The analysis was conducted using R® software (version 3.5.1). The annual notification of sputum-negative PTB was calculated. Demographic, clinical, and diagnostic characteristics were analyzed and presented as frequencies for categorical variables and medians with an interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables.

The missing data in the “occupation” variable were addressed using multiple imputation [13]. To verify the robustness of our conclusions, we compared the pooled results from the multiple imputation analysis with the original complete-case analysis. These sensitivity analyses have been supplemented in the Appendix.

Log-binomial regression models were fitted to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) for the association between patient characteristics (independent variables) and sputum-negative status (dependent variable). Logistic regression models were used to analyze the association between sputum-negative statue and binary treatment outcomes (failure and death, respectively), with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs), in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses. For the time-to-event outcome of recurrence, survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and subgroup differences were assessed with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to examine the association between sputum-negative status and recurrence, with the proportional hazards assumption verified using Schoenfeld residuals; results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) before and after adjusting for potential covariates. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Notification of sputum-negative PTB in Shanghai, 2010–2019

From 2010 to 2016, the annual notification of new sputum-negative PTB in Shanghai fluctuated around 2,900 cases, and the annual notification rate fluctuated around 12 per 100,000. From 2016 to 2019, the number of annual notification declined from 2,955 to 1,921, and the annual notification rate declined from 12.2 per 100,000 to 7.9 per 100,000(χ² trend =264.15, P < 0.001, Fig. 2). During the same period, the number of annual notification of sputum-positive PTB fluctuated around 3000 cases, and the annual notification rate fluctuated around 12 per 100,000 (χ² trend = 5.15, P = 0.02).

Annual notification of new PTB in Shanghai from 2010 to 2019, stratified by sputum-positive and negative. Bars represent absolute counts, and the line shows the corresponding rate

The sputum-positive proportion of new PTB patients in Shanghai showed a significant increase from 51.9% to 62.2% between 2010 and 2019, indicating a notable trend (χ² trend = 153.83, P < 0.001, Fig. 3). The proportion of sputum smear positivity exhibited a consistent decline over the years, decreasing from 39.4% in 2010 to 30.0% in 2019. The prevalence of patients exhibiting negative sputum smear but positive sputum culture has shown a consistent annual increase, rising from 12.6% in 2010 to 25.8% in 2019. In 2019, the implementation of sputum Xpert testing resulted in a 6.4% increase in the sputum-positive proportion (i.e., cases where sputum smear and culture were negative or not conducted, but GeneXpert MTB/RIF test yielded positive results).

Characteristics of sputum-negative PTB patients

Compared to patients with sputum-positive PTB, those with sputum-negative PTB were younger (Padj=0.005), with a median age of 35 (IQR 25, 55) years; had a higher proportion of females (34.1%)(Padj=0.026); a higher proportion of local residents (50.7%) (Padj<0.001) and registered at referral hospitals (61.1%) (Padj<0.001) and detected through active screening (4.3%) (Padj<0.001) and with comorbid pleurisy (11.3%) (Padj<0.001); a shorter delay in seeking care (Padj<0.001), with a median of 10 (IQR 3, 23) days; a longer delay in diagnosis (Padj<0.001), with a median of 11 (IQR 5, 21) days; a lower proportion of pulmonary cavities (12.1%) (Padj<0.001) and with comorbid diabetes (4.0%) (Padj<0.001) and with comorbid bronchial tuberculosis (1.2%) (Padj<0.001). Treatment outcomes of sputum-negative PTB patients

53,204 PTB patients were successfully treated (95.7% in sputum-negative vs. 91.7% in sputum-positive) and were followed for 368804.5 person-years. (Table 2 and Table 3) Each patient was followed for an average of 6.93 years. Compared to sputum-positive patients, sputum-negative PTB patients showed lower rates of failure (0.2% vs. 0.6%, ORadj=0.56, 95%CI 0.52–0.61), death (1.4% vs. 3.9%, ORadj=0.43, 95%CI 0.38–0.49) and recurrence (2.1% vs. 3.3%, HRadj=0.71, 95%CI 0.63–0.79). The survival curve of recurrence showed a significant difference, with a p-value less than 0.001 according to the log-rank test (Fig. 4). The results of the sensitivity analysis were similar to those obtained from the imputation.

Discussion

PTB is a chronic infectious pulmonary disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, primarily transmitted through the respiratory tract. It continued to pose a significant threat to human health and remains a major public health concern [14]. Given that the proportion of sputum-negative PTB patients constituted approximately 55% of all PTB patients in China [1], an understanding of the trends and characteristics of sputum-negative PTB is crucial for guiding tuberculosis elimination efforts. While several studies have explored the notification, clinical characteristics and outcomes of PTB in different periods and regions in China [15], this study distinguished itself through its large sample size and extended temporal scope. Our research found that from 2010 to 2019, the annual notification rate of new sputum-negative PTB declined from 12.0 per 100,000 to 7.9 per 100,000 in Shanghai, China. At the mean time, the sputum-positive proportion rose from 51.9% to 62.2%. Compared with sputum-positive PTB, sputum-negative PTB had fewer comorbidities, radiological abnormalities, and better treatment outcomes, but a longer delay in diagnosis.

This study found that since 2017 in Shanghai, both the annual notification number and notification rate of sputum-negative PTB among newly diagnosed PTB patients showed a clear decline. During the same period, both the notification number and notification rate of sputum-positive PTB exhibited a significant increase, accompanied by a marked rise in the proportion of sputum-positive cases among all new PTB patients. These local trended aligned with research outcomes from settings including South Africa and Peru [16], which confirmed the high feasibility, accuracy, and effectiveness of decentralized Xpert deployment for diagnosing PTB, leading to improved early diagnostic efficiency. With the intensification of PTB case-finding, an increasing number of cases were discovered at an early stage with a low bacillary load, leading to a diminishing role for the less sensitive smear microscopy in case detection. In 2018, Shanghai initiated a large-scale project, funded by the local government, to implement Xpert testing for all presumptive pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) patients during their initial visit to designated TB hospitals. The primary objectives were to enhance bacteriological confirmation and drug susceptibility testing (DST) coverage among TB patients while reducing the delay in detecting rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB). This phenomenon accounted for the decline in sputum-negative PTB cases and the increase in the rate of sputum-positive PTB cases. However, the current rate stands at only 60%, which still presents a significant disparity compared to the National Disease Control and Prevention Administration report’s benchmark of 70% [17]. Therefore, it remains imperative to develop and implement novel detection technologies to enhance the standard of etiological diagnosis.

In this study, patients with sputum-negative PTB had a shorter delay in health seeking (Padj < 0.001), with a median of 10 (IQR 3, 23) days and longer days for delay in diagnosis (IQR 11 vs. 7, P < 0.001) and multivariate analysis showed that the possibility of delays of 1–2 weeks, 2 weeks to 1 month, 1–2 months, and more than 2 months was significantly higher in sputum-negative PTB than in sputum-positive PTB (Padj < 0.001). One contributing factor to the shorter health seeking delay in sputum-negative PTB patients might be the nature of their symptoms. It is plausible that these patients experience symptoms that are relatively atypical or less severe, which paradoxically could prompt an earlier decision to seek medical attention. For instance, research indicates that the clinical presentation and radiological characteristics of sputum-negative PTB patients differ from those with smear-positive PTB, influencing patients’ perception of their illness and their early health seeking behavior [18]. Due to the lack of biological confirmation, sputum-negative PTB is often diagnosed through trial-of-antibiotics [19] and anti-tuberculosis treatments, resulting in a longer diagnosis period. An observational study in South Korea [20] showed that over 50% of patients with sputum-negative PTB had a delayed diagnosis. A study in Turkey [21] demonstrated that the number of days of diagnosis delay in sputum-negative PTB was significantly higher than that in sputum-positive PTB (IQR 41 days vs. 16 days, P < 0.001). Older individuals are more prone to sputum-positive PTB due to several interconnected factors. First, diminished immune system function associated with aging reduces the ability to limit bacterial replication and spread effectively [4]. Second, atypical symptom presentation is common among elderly patients, often resulting in delayed diagnoses and more advanced disease stages by the time of detection [22]. Third, practical challenges such as difficulty in obtaining high-quality sputum samples from older adults further compound diagnostic obstacles [23]. In contrast, younger individuals with more robust immune responses are often able to better suppress bacterial proliferation, resulting in higher occurrences of sputum-negative PTB presentations. These age-related disparities highlight the need for tailored diagnostic strategies to improve early detection in elderly populations.

In this study, patients with sputum-negative PTB demonstrated less proportion of pulmonary cavities (12.1% vs. 35.4%, Padj<0.001), a lower comorbidity proportion of diabetes mellitus (6.8% vs. 14%, Padj < 0.001), and fewer adverse outcomes such as treatment failure, death, and recurrence (all Padj < 0.001). These findings may be attributed to the earlier disease stage and less severe pulmonary damage in sputum-negative PTB. This interpretation is consistent with our observations that sputum-negative patients had a shorter care-seeking delay (10 days vs. 12 days) and were more frequently detected through active screening (4.3% vs. 2.7%). The analysis [24] conducted in 1981 on sputum-negative PTB patients in Hong Kong revealed that sputum-negative PTB patients exhibited radiographic abnormalities compared with sputum-positive PTB patients. A cross-sectional study in New York City (NYC), NY, USA, from 2011 to 2013, found that, among PTB patients without HIV infection, sputum-negative patients had fewer symptoms and less advanced radiographic evidence of disease than sputum-positive patients [4]. A study from South Korea [25] found that sputum Xpert-negative PTB patients exhibited significantly lower rates of consolidation (21.7% vs. 39.4%, Padj = 0.012) and cavitation (23.6% vs. 37.9%, Padj = 0.045) on chest CT compared with their counterparts. Similarly, an analysis of U.S. surveillance data [26] also observed that sputum-negative PTB patients had a lower proportion of cavitation on chest imaging (13.1% vs. 38.8%, Padj < 0.01) and a reduced risk of mortality during treatment (2.0% vs. 9.6%, P < 0.01) when compared with sputum-positive patients. Delays in the diagnosis or treatment, whether initiated by patients or healthcare professionals, can result in diminished therapeutic efficacy and compromised hospitalization outcomes for PTB patients [27], and unfavorable prognoses for those patients with sputum-negative PTB in areas with a high prevalence of HIV infection [28, 29]. Studies in areas with high HIV prevalence, such as South Africa, Benin, and Peru, have shown that the death rate of sputum-negative PTB is higher than that of sputum-positive PTB [30–33]. Studies conducted in China [12, 34] reported that sputum-negative pulmonary tuberculosis was associated with a lower rate of recurrence. In a setting of relatively low prevalence of HIV infection like Shanghai, we proposed that the majority of sputum-negative PTB patients exhibit a disease progression between incipient and active PTB along the continuum of this intricate illness [35, 36] and with non-severe tuberculosis. This might also support the conception of a shortened treatment regimen for non-severe susceptible PTB [37–39].

Although the infectious intensity of sputum-negative PTB cases is lower compared with sputum-positive PTB cases, its significance in the transmission of tuberculosis cannot be overlooked. According to a U.S. study [40], sputum-negative PTB had a transmission rate that was 22% of that of smear-positive PTB, accounting for 17% of secondary cases. Furthermore, a similar study conducted in the Netherlands [41] reported a corresponding proportion of 12.6%. Studies conducted in certain developed countries have demonstrated that 9.0% and 5.8% of close contacts associated with a 9-year-old boy and a 15-year-old girl, respectively, manifest active tuberculosis, while the infection rates among these close contacts were reported to be 42.5% and 32.7%, correspondingly [42, 43]. A study conducted in Germany reported that out of 143 contacts (hospital medical workers) of an 18-year-old patient with sputum-negative PTB, 13 individuals (9.1%) tested positive for tuberculosis using the Quantiferon-TB-Gold (QFT-G) test [44]. Furthermore, previous research has demonstrated that close family contacts living with sputum-negative PTB patients at home have a 1.7 times higher risk of developing PTB compared to those without PTB patients at home [45]. A study from China [46] showed that sputum-negative cases accounted for at least 30% of secondary cases, with these patients being more frequently under 25 years of age and new cases, while typically presenting fewer clinical features such as cough and cavitary disease. Given its high proportion among active pulmonary tuberculosis patients and its risk of transmission to close contacts, the enhancement of early diagnosis and treatment for patients with sputum-negative PTB holds significant implications in the prevention and control of tuberculosis epidemics.

There are some limitations in our study. Firstly, we have insufficient data regarding the clinical symptoms exhibited by PTB patients, including those recommended by the World Health Organization: cough, expectoration, hemoptysis, and so on, which hindered the thorough explorer for clinical characteristics. Secondly, our follow-up data on recurrence comes from the surveillance data system, where recurrence records are searched for again based on individual information, which may lead to underestimation of recurrence. In the current surveillance data, information on other common immunosuppressive factors was unavailable, precluding analysis in this area and limiting the generalizability of our conclusions to PTB populations with impaired immune function.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we found that the annual notification rate of new sputum-negative PTB declined from 2010 to 2019 in Shanghai, China. As a less severe disease, sputum-negative PTB had fewer comorbidities, radiological abnormalities, and better treatment outcomes, but a longer delay in diagnosis, which indicated the urgent need for novel biomarkers to enhance early detection and diagnosis of sputum-negative PTB, accelerating the progress toward End TB targets. In the future, we will conduct specialized investigations to determine the impact of these factors on PTB and address the limitations of the surveillance system. Looking forward, we plan to conduct a nested case-control design focusing on the close contacts of sputum-negative PTB patients in Shanghai. This study design is essential for accurately detecting the PTB infection rate and incidence risk among this exposed population. Ultimately, this research will allow us to quantify the local transmission contribution of sputum-negative PTB, a critical step for evaluating and optimizing the current public health interventions in our community.
