Persistent NET states link lesion-related microbial signals to the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment: a context-dependent working model
Authors: Fanying Meng, ZhenLim Wong, Junyi Chen, Zishu Zhang, Zhe Deng, Xuefei Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCold tumors often show poor effector immune cell entry or impaired effector function and therefore respond poorly to immunotherapy. Growing evidence suggests that neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are not simply by-products of tumor-associated inflammation. In some tumor settings, they can persist within lesions and act as retained local mediators. Among the upstream factors that may sustain this process, microbial signals warrant particular attention. In selected cancers, persistent lesion-related microbial signals may convert an initially transient NET response into persistent NET states. These states are linked to restricted effector immunity, stromal remodeling, vascular abnormalities, and other tumor-promoting consequences. This review reorganizes current evidence around the axis of lesion-related microbial signals, persistent NET states, and the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment. On this basis, a context-dependent working model is proposed in which lesion-related microbial signals contribute to the formation and maintenance of this environment through persistent NET states. Support for this sequence is not uniform across tumor types. Colorectal cancer currently provides the clearest line of support. Hepatocellular carcinoma is better understood as a setting in which the same framework is expressed more through organ conditioning and local amplification. Evidence from other tumors remains more limited and is often partial rather than fully resolved. Most available studies are still preclinical. Even so, current findings suggest that reducing sustained microbial stimulation or directly intervening in persistent NET states may help relieve this restrictive environment and inform future translational strategies.Keywords: Microbial signals, Neutrophil extracellular traps, Cold tumor microenvironment

Background

The cold tumor microenvironment (cTME) is usually associated with poor infiltration of effector immune cells, the development of an immunosuppressive milieu, and weak antitumor activity [1]. Beyond classic mechanisms such as defective antigen presentation and limited tumor antigen availability [2], growing evidence suggests that cold tumors do not always exist as simple immune deserts. Alongside immune deserts, T-cell-excluded tumors also represent a major form of cold tumors [3–5]. In these settings, local immune activity may still be present, yet effector T cells remain largely confined to the tumor margin or stromal regions and fail to enter the tumor core [4, 5]. Immunosuppressive cell networks, a dense extracellular matrix (ECM), and abnormal tumor vessels can further limit effector cell infiltration and weaken effector function, thereby restricting antitumor immunity and treatment response [6–9]. The defining feature of cold tumors is therefore not simply the absence of inflammation, but the failure of local immune activity to translate into effective tumor clearance. Clarifying the cellular and molecular basis of this cold tumor-related local restrictive environment is therefore important for improving cancer treatment.

Neutrophils are key innate immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, and their functional state can be reshaped by tumor-related signals, with downstream effects on other immune populations and overall antitumor immunity [10, 11]. Among neutrophil-derived processes, Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) have drawn increasing attention because they can limit effector immune cell access and cytotoxic function, promote immune escape, and remodel the local tissue environment [12]. In this way, NETs are increasingly recognized as active mediators that help shape the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment rather than as passive by-products of tumor-associated inflammation. NET formation can be induced by multiple classes of stimuli, including chemical signals [13, 14], metabolic byproducts [15, 16], inflammatory mediators [17], and microbial signals [18–20].

Among these upstream factors, microbial signals deserve particular attention. Current evidence suggests that microbial signals can influence the tumor microenvironment through inflammatory and immunoregulatory pathways. Microbial structural components or metabolites may also reach tumor-related niches through barrier disruption, circulatory transfer, or local colonization, and may then affect the formation and transition of local microenvironmental states [21–23]. Under conditions such as dysbiosis, barrier damage, and chronic inflammation, microbial signals may not only trigger NET formation but may also sustain NET activity through persistent stimulation, local enrichment, or intratumoral colonization. In this setting, an initially transient defensive response may shift into persistent NET states. The key question is therefore not simply whether microbial signals can induce NET release, but which signals, and under which tissue conditions, can sustain persistent NET states and thereby support the formation and maintenance of the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment. Within this framework, NET-related processes are better understood as factors that help shape this environment, whereas epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), ECM remodeling, and vascular abnormalities more often reflect downstream tissue remodeling and tumor-promoting consequences that emerge as the environment continues to evolve. The strength of evidence linking microbial signals, persistent NET states, and the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment is also not uniform across tumor types. Current evidence is still insufficient to support a single unified mechanism across cancers. On this basis, this review proposes a context-dependent working model to explain how lesion-related microbial signals may, in selected cancers, contribute to the formation and maintenance of the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment through persistent NET states. Colorectal cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma are then discussed as representative settings in which this model shows different patterns and potential translational relevance.

NETs as context-dependent organizers of the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment

NETs are increasingly understood not as passive by-products of inflammation, but as active organizers of the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment. Their importance lies not only in their presence within tumor tissues, but also in their ability to connect several key components of cold tumor biology within the same local setting. Although NETs may exert antitumor effects under certain conditions, these effects may at least partly reflect settings in which NETs remain coupled to acute or N1-like neutrophil effector programs, such as direct tumor cell injury, transient restraint of tumor-cell movement, or, in selected contexts, association with local immune activation [24–28]. These observations do not contradict the tumor-promoting role of NETs in most solid tumors. Rather, they suggest that the biological meaning of NETs depends on the state in which they are generated and retained. In established solid tumors, NETs are often observed as spatially retained and tumor-conditioned structures. In this setting, their dominant effects more commonly shift away from short-lived effector activity and toward immune restriction, tissue remodeling, vascular dysfunction, and support of tumor progression. A major way in which NETs contribute to cold tumors is by restricting effective antitumor immunity. NETs can be detected both in the tumor core and at the invasive margin, where they form dense extracellular structures around malignant cells. These structures limit the access of cytotoxic lymphocytes to tumor cells and weaken contact-dependent killing by CD8⁺ T lymphocytes (CD8⁺ T cells) and natural killer (NK) cells [29, 30]. At the same time, NET-rich microenvironments can promote T-cell dysfunction and exhaustion, which further weakens antitumor immunity [31]. NETs therefore not only drive immune exclusion, but can also aggravate immune paralysis. Both are key features of cold tumors. Beyond immune restriction, NETs are also linked to a series of parallel tissue changes and tumor-promoting consequences. In colorectal cancer, NETs are associated with polarization of tumor-promoting M2 macrophages [32]. In lung cancer and colorectal cancer models, NETs can trap circulating tumor cells, help them evade immune clearance, and promote intrahepatic seeding and metastasis [33]. In pancreatic cancer, NET-derived components can activate pancreatic stellate cells and promote tumor growth, thereby generating a stromal state that is less permissive to immune cell infiltration [34]. In oral squamous cell carcinoma and gastric cancer, NETs can promote ECM degradation and EMT, enhance invasiveness, and support a more aggressive tumor phenotype [35, 36]. NET-associated angiogenesis has been reported in gastric cancer, while NET-driven metastatic and prothrombotic effects have also been observed in liver cancer and gastric cancer [18, 37, 38]. These findings suggest that NETs do not simply coexist with matrix remodeling or vascular abnormalities. They participate in the continued progression of the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment. In this process, immune restriction, tissue remodeling, vascular abnormalities, and tumor invasiveness become intertwined and evolve together.

These divergent effects are unlikely to be incidental. Instead, they reflect the context-dependent biology of NETs within tumors. Because NETs are produced by activated neutrophils in specific local settings, heterogeneity in tumor-associated neutrophil (TAN) states provides one important upstream basis for variation in NET release and NET-mediated effects. TANs show marked functional heterogeneity and can adopt different activation states under different microenvironmental conditions. This heterogeneity is often described as N1-like and N2-like polarization [39]. The composition and function of NETs themselves can also change according to the triggering stimulus and the mode of release. Different forms of NETosis involve different intracellular signaling pathways and may generate extracellular structures with distinct functions [40, 41]. In addition, cytokine signaling, metabolic stress, and other local microenvironmental cues can further shape neutrophil behavior and modify NET formation and function [28]. NET-driven cold tumor biology is therefore better understood as a context-dependent process rather than as a uniform phenomenon that appears in the same way across different cancer types and disease settings. Overall, current evidence supports NETs as important participants in the formation of cold tumors. Their specific effects, however, depend on the state of neutrophils, the mode of NET release, and the local microenvironmental context. The biological significance of NETs in tumors cannot therefore be judged by abundance alone. How microbial signals induce NET formation and promote persistent NET states

NET formation is an important innate immune response by which neutrophils react to microbial signals. Under dysbiosis or inflammatory conditions, microbe derived pathogen associated molecular patterns can be recognized by several receptors on the neutrophil surface, such as TLR4, NOD1/2, and other pattern recognition pathways. These signals activate intracellular programs that promote reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) dependent histone citrullination, chromatin decondensation, and NET release [32, 42, 43]. Dysbiosis can also enhance neutrophil recruitment and increase NET formation, and IL-8 can further drive this process through NOX- and MAPK-related signaling [44, 45].

Differences among microbes are first reflected at the level of NET induction. Bacteria, fungi, viruses, and their related products can all induce NET formation, but the conditions that support this process are not the same across microbes. Larger pathogens, such as fungal hyphae, are more likely to trigger NETosis. Smaller microbes are more often cleared by phagocytosis. Extracellular aggregates are also more likely to induce NET release [46]. In Candida albicans, hyphal forms induce NET responses more readily than yeast forms [46]. Size and morphology are not the only determinants. Microbial aggregation state and the local matrix environment can also shape NET formation. Studies have shown that Candida albicans can rapidly induce NET formation under matrix dependent conditions, whereas its mature biofilms and ECM can suppress NET release and affect later clearance [47, 48]. By contrast, the development of persistent NET states depends more on whether microbial stimulation can be repeatedly transmitted and retained in the local environment. NET formation is not a single process. In addition to suicidal NETosis, neutrophils can also undergo vital NETosis and release extracellular traps while remaining alive [49]. Regardless of the release mode, extracellular NET structures can retain inflammatory and antimicrobial components in the local environment [50, 51], which becomes more biologically important when stimulation is repeatedly renewed and insufficiently cleared. It can then become linked to sustained NET associated inflammation, tissue injury, and chronic inflammatory processes [52].

This process is also shaped by the ability of pathogens to evade NET mediated host clearance. Some pathogens can weaken NET mediated capture and clearance by secreting nucleases, forming biofilms, or suppressing neutrophil adhesion [53–55]. This not only supports microbial survival, but also makes NET associated responses easier to amplify through sustained stimulation, local retention, and impaired pathogen clearance. The key significance of microbial signals therefore lies not only in their ability to induce NET formation, but also in the fact that some forms of microbial stimulation make NET associated responses more likely to shift from transient release to persistent NET states, thereby providing more stable local conditions for later pathological processes. Why does microbial signal-driven NETosis concentrate in specific lesion regions?

In tumor settings, microbial signal-driven NETosis does not occur evenly throughout the lesion. It is more likely to concentrate in regions where microbial signals can become locally enriched and remain active over time. Current evidence points to two related but functionally distinct processes: systemic priming and local intratumoral activation. The former mainly changes the overall susceptibility of neutrophils to NETosis. The latter more directly determines where microbial signals become enriched, retained, and repeatedly active.

Systemic priming can increase the inflammatory activation state of neutrophils before they reach the tumor-bearing organ. When the epithelial barrier is disrupted, microbial products such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), together with other pathogen associated molecular patterns, can enter the circulation and raise neutrophil responsiveness through relevant receptors and inflammatory pathways [56]. This process is not limited to the classic setting of microbial products entering the bloodstream. Specific gut microbes and their metabolites can also shape neutrophil responsiveness before these cells arrive at the lesion. A recent study showed that Parasutterella excrementihominis is enriched in ulcerative colitis and colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC), and that it induces GSDMD dependent NETosis in LPS primed neutrophils through the production of succinic acid and 6-hydroxyhexanoic acid, thereby aggravating chronic colitis and colitis-associated tumor progression [57]. These findings suggest that systemic priming can arise through the combined effects of microbial signals, the metabolic environment, and the inflammatory background. In this way, it may increase neutrophil susceptibility to pathological NET release.

Local intratumoral activation more directly explains why this process becomes spatially restricted within the lesion. Growing evidence indicates that tumors are not always sterile. In some cancers, viable microbes can be detected within tumor tissue [23]. Their distribution is not random. Instead, they form focal microbial niches within the lesion. Studies of the intratumoral microbiota have shown that bacteria can cluster in discrete microniches with distinct epithelial and immune features, whereas cancer cells affected by bacteria may invade surrounding tissue as single cells and at the same time recruit myeloid cells into bacteria-enriched regions [58]. These observations indicate that microbes, and likely their associated signals, can be spatially organized rather than evenly distributed across the lesion. This further suggests that microbial signals do not act equally in all parts of the tumor. Instead, they are more likely to create conditions for sustained exposure and repeated action in specific local regions. One of the best studied examples is Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn) in colorectal cancer. This organism is enriched not only in colorectal cancer tissue but also persists in metastatic lesions [59]. Further studies showed that Fn can selectively colonize tumor tissue through Fap2 dependent recognition of tumor expressed Gal-GalNAc [60]. This suggests that tumor-associated surface features can themselves provide adhesion and enrichment advantages for specific microbes. At the same time, tumor-associated microbes do not always remain in a free extracellular state. Studies have shown that tumor-associated microbes can also adopt intracellular locations within cancer cells and, in some tumor contexts, immune cells, suggesting that some microbes may gain relatively protected survival sites within tumor systems [23, 61]. Other studies suggest that solid tumors can provide niche-specific conditions that support the preferential growth of some bacteria, including facultative anaerobes [62]. These findings are not themselves equivalent to persistent NET states. Even so, together they support a more important point. Some tumor lesions may provide favorable conditions for the local enrichment and continued action of microbial signals, rather than merely passively receiving them. In this setting, NETosis should not be understood as a diffuse response occurring evenly throughout the lesion. Neutrophils entering tumor tissue do not encounter the same stimuli in all regions. Instead, they are more likely to be activated where microbes or microbial signals are enriched. In colorectal cancer, Fn-enriched lesions are associated with higher NET levels, which further supports the view that microbial signal-driven NETosis is more likely to occur as a locally restricted response in specific lesion regions [32]. Therefore, systemic priming changes the response threshold of neutrophils, whereas local intratumoral activation, together with the lesion conditions on which it depends, more strongly determines whether this response can become spatially restricted and maintained, thereby contributing to local pathological consequences.

A context-dependent working model: lesion-related microbial signals shape the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment through persistent NET states

Current studies have separately shown that lesion-related microbial signals can induce NET formation, and that NETs can affect immune clearance, tumor cell states, and local tissue structure. Even so, placing these findings side by side still does not explain how persistent local effects arise within lesions. Based on current evidence across cancer types, we propose a context-dependent working model. In this model, some microbial inputs that can act continuously within lesions are converted, through persistent NET states, from brief stimuli into lasting lesion effects. We refer to these inputs as lesion-related microbial signals. They may arise from local microbial presence within tumors or near lesions. They may also derive from microbial components, metabolites, or other molecular signals that reach the lesion after transport from distant reservoirs such as the gut. In this setting, NETs are no longer just residual structures left after a single neutrophil response. They become local mediators that remain within lesions and continue to act on nearby cells. Extracellular DNA, histones, elastase, myeloperoxidase, and other inflammatory components can therefore persist at the lesion site. Tumor cells, stromal cells, and immune cells remain exposed to these signals. In this way, persistent NET states provide a durable local condition for immune imbalance, tissue remodeling, and tumor-promoting consequences.

The most direct support for this central step in the working model comes from functional studies showing that NETs restrict effective immune clearance. The cold tumor-related local restrictive environment is more often marked by persistent inflammation than by an absence of inflammation. The problem is that effector immune cells fail to achieve effective contact and cytotoxic clearance. In current studies, EBV-related epithelial cancers provide relatively direct functional support for the link between NET-rich local states and restricted cytotoxic function. EBV-positive tumor cells upregulate CXCL10, which induces NET formation through CXCR3-positive neutrophils. These NETs weaken antigen-specific CTL killing both in vivo and in vitro [75]. Granzyme B, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and perforin are all reduced, whereas DNase I restores CTL killing efficiency [75]. These findings suggest that persistent NET states can keep lesions in a condition where immune cells remain present but effective clearance fails, without requiring immune cell loss. This is why persistent NET states matter. Their importance lies not only in their persistence, but also in their ability to maintain a local state in which inflammation remains present but ineffective.

Beyond these direct functional data, current studies also suggest that persistent NET states do more than impair immune clearance. They can also alter tumor cell programs and are accompanied by tissue-level change. In an E. coli -related colorectal cancer model, NET signals increase TRPC1 and NFATC3 activity in colorectal cancer cells, promote calcium-dependent assembly of the STAT3/S100A8/9 complex, stabilize enhancer-promoter looping, and sustain TNS1 transcription [72]. This suggests that NET-related signals can be translated into durable tumor cell programs rather than remaining short-lived stimuli. In Fn-related models, NET formation is accompanied by increased tumor cell migration and invasion, MMP2 and MMP9 upregulation, basement membrane degradation, and enhanced EMT-like programs [32]. In oral Streptococcus salivarius-related models, NET formation is likewise linked to tumor-promoting change that supports metastasis [74]. These findings more directly support a link between persistent NET states, EMT-related change, and downstream tissue consequences. As MMP upregulation, basement membrane damage, and ECM remodeling accumulate, the local conditions required for stable and effective contact between effector immune cells and tumor cells may be progressively disrupted. Local inflammation therefore remains present, but becomes less likely to translate into effective clearance. This interpretation is also consistent with broader tumor immunology studies showing that EMT signatures are associated with lower T-cell infiltration and more evident immune exclusion, while stromal TGF-β signaling can further restrict T-cell penetration into tumors [83, 84]. In this way, EMT, basement membrane damage, and ECM remodeling are no longer just parallel tumor-promoting findings. They are more likely to work together to sustain a local state in which inflammation persists but clearance fails.

Across cancer types, the available evidence does not point to one identical NET-related outcome. What it supports more clearly is an upstream logic: lesion-related microbial signals can leave persistent lesion effects through persistent NET states, and the dominant form of those effects depends on the disease context. In the hepatocellular carcinoma setting, current evidence mainly points to vascular and tissue-level change, whereas direct functional evidence that persistent NET states suppress cytotoxic function is still lacking. In HBV-related disease and dysbiosis-driven hepatocellular carcinoma, NET-rich states are associated with increased VEGF, MMP2, and MMP9, together with enhanced angiogenesis, tissue injury, and support for intrahepatic dissemination. HBV can activate the TLR4/RAGE-ROS cascade in neutrophils through S100A9 and promote NET formation [81]. Patient-derived microbiota can also enhance neutrophil inflammatory activation and excessive NET formation, and NET-rich regions are colocalized with CD31-related vascular growth, tissue necrosis, and intrahepatic dissemination [18]. These findings suggest that, in the hepatocellular carcinoma context, the main consequences of persistent NET states are more closely linked to remodeling of the vascular and tissue environment and to support for intrahepatic spread.

The point of this context-dependent working model is not that lesion-related microbial signals simply amplify local inflammation. Rather, through persistent NET states, they can convert a brief input into a lesion effect that persists. The central consequence is that local inflammation remains present while effective clearance becomes progressively limited. At the same time, structural support and tumor-promoting consequences continue to accumulate within lesions. What emerges is not a silent state without inflammation. It is a lesion environment in which inflammation persists, local organization continues, and clearance ultimately fails. NETs therefore serve as the key mediating step between transient microbial input and persistent lesion effects. EMT, ECM remodeling, vascular abnormalities, and metastatic support are better understood as the tissue and functional consequences that accumulate under sustained local restrictive conditions (Fig. 3).

Organ-specific expression and scope of the context-dependent working model

This working model is not expressed in the same way across tumor types. It is most strongly supported in settings where three levels can be linked within the same disease context: lesion-related microbial signals, persistent NET states in the relevant tumor space, and downstream tumor consequences connected to those persistent NET states. Across cancers, these three levels are not resolved to the same degree. In some settings, the chain is relatively continuous within lesions. In others, support lies more in long-term shaping of the organ background followed by local amplification. In still others, only part of the chain is visible, while fuller continuity remains unresolved.

Current evidence still places lesion-related microbial signals more often upstream of persistent NET states and the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment. As tumors progress, tumor-associated niche conditions, together with necrosis, metabolic remodeling, vascular abnormalities, and immunosuppressive pressure, may in turn favor microbial enrichment, repeated stimulation, and intratumoral colonization [62, 23]. The relation is therefore more likely to be mutually reinforcing than strictly one way. Even so, support remains stronger for lesion-related microbial signals as initiating drivers than for the reverse process.

Colorectal cancer: the clearest setting for closure within lesions

Colorectal cancer provides the clearest support for this model within lesions. Colonic tumors arise at a mucosal surface with dense microbial exposure. Barrier disruption, altered luminal contact, and enrichment of lesion-related microbes place abnormal epithelium under sustained microbial influence [85, 58]. In this setting, gut microbes and intratumoral microbes are better viewed as different levels of the same process than as fully separate categories [86, 87, 58]. Microbial signals can therefore act directly within lesions on tumor cells and recruited myeloid cells [58]. Some lesion-related microbes, represented by Fn, may also remain relevant across the transition from precancerous lesions to established tumors, which makes support for initiating drive in colorectal cancer relatively strong [88, 89, 59]. In colorectal cancer, the strongest downstream support is concentrated in lesion-level continuity, invasion-related change, and dissemination-related consequences [32, 72, 74]. As an epithelial malignancy, colorectal cancer progression depends on breaching the basement membrane and adjacent stromal barriers, which gives lesion-level invasion and dissemination particular weight in this setting [90, 91]. Model systems have traced a relatively continuous sequence from microbial colonization and neutrophil activation to persistent NET formation and later tumor-promoting outputs [72, 74, 32]. Human studies are less resolved in causal depth, but they still support continuity within lesions through the spatial organization of intratumoral microbes and the persistence of Fn between primary tumors and liver metastases [58, 59]. Portal venous continuity between the colon and the liver may further increase the relevance of this local program during metastasis [59, 92]. Even in colorectal cancer, neutrophil responses are not uniform. Integrated single cell analysis has identified distinct normal-associated neutrophil (NAN) and TAN states with different differentiation paths and prognostic associations [93]. Even so, colorectal cancer remains the setting with the clearest closure of this model at the lesion level.

Hepatocellular carcinoma: a supportive setting defined mainly by organ conditioning and local amplification

Hepatocellular carcinoma also supports this model, but in a different form. Here, lesion-related microbial signals do not usually appear first as a tightly localized microbe–NET sequence within lesions. More often, gut-derived microbial signals reach the liver through the gut–liver axis, portal venous transport, altered bile acid metabolism, increased intestinal permeability, and chronic inflammatory conditioning [94–96]. What changes first is therefore less often the lesion itself than the susceptibility of the liver as an organ to NET-related consequences. Intratumoral microbes in liver cancer are broader in source and more heterogeneous [96]. Together with continuous portal input, abundant immune cell residence, sinusoidal specialization, altered blood flow, fibrosis-related ECM deposition, hypoxia, and necrosis, these features make local amplification of NET-related consequences more likely [96–98]. The dominant outputs in hepatocellular carcinoma also differ from those in colorectal cancer. Current evidence points more strongly to vascular support, necrotic remodeling, inflammation amplification, and intrahepatic dissemination [18]. In this setting, microbial signals are more likely to be translated through hepatic stellate cells, myeloid suppressive populations, regulatory circuits, and NKT cell pathways [94, 95]. The strongest support still comes mainly from preclinical models, especially studies showing that gut-derived microbial products or dysregulated gut microbiota can drive NET-dependent hepatocellular carcinoma progression and intrahepatic dissemination [56, 18]. Human evidence is less continuous than in colorectal cancer and still depends mainly on microbiome profiling, tumor versus adjacent tissue comparison, and clinicopathologic association [18, 96, 99]. Hepatocellular carcinoma is therefore compatible with this model, but its main strength lies in organ conditioning and local amplification rather than in the clearest closure within lesions.

Other tumors: related components are present, but closure remains incomplete

Beyond colorectal cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma, current studies more often show organ-specific components of NET biology than a continuous sequence from lesion-related microbial signals to persistent NET states and downstream consequences. Gastric cancer remains below the level of stronger closure. Current studies support local colonization-related microbial activity and marked remodeling of the intratumoral microbial community. Intratumoral Fn can promote TAN recruitment and drive immune escape and tumor progression through an IL17A–PD-L1-related program [100]. The IL-8/CXCR1/2 axis can also induce NET formation and promote tumor progression through a BRF1-cyclin-related program [101]. Even so, direct continuity among initiating input, persistent NET states, and the local restrictive environment has not yet been established within the same disease setting.

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) provides a different type of support. Its value lies less in a complete chain than in showing how tissue context shapes the dominant consequence of NET-rich biology. In PDAC, IL-17-driven NET formation can exclude cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and mediate resistance to immune checkpoint blockade, while collagen-rich stroma activates DDR1 signaling and induces CXCL5-dependent neutrophil recruitment, which further promotes NET formation, invasion, and metastasis [102, 103]. PDAC is therefore better understood as a supportive setting that shows how a fibrotic tissue background can direct persistent NET states toward immune exclusion and stroma-directed progression rather than as a setting with complete closure of this model.

Overall, current evidence supports a context-weighted view rather than a universal template. Colorectal cancer shows the clearest closure within lesions. Hepatocellular carcinoma supports the same framework mainly through organ conditioning and local amplification. Other tumors still provide partial or supportive expression. Whether this model can be extended further will depend on whether lesion-related microbial signals, persistent NET states, and downstream consequences can be resolved together within the same disease setting. Potential translational relevance of targeting microbial signals and intervening in persistent NET states

Within this context-dependent working model, potential intervention mainly falls into two layers. One is to reduce the persistent stimulation provided by microbial signals. The other is to intervene in persistent NET states once they have formed. Current evidence still comes mainly from preclinical studies, and support remains uneven across tumor settings. Even so, existing findings suggest that stratified intervention at these two levels may help relieve the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment and provide a basis for later therapeutic development.

Upstream intervention by targeting microbial signals

The main aim of upstream intervention is to reduce microbial signals that can continuously drive persistent NET states, or to weaken the ecological conditions that support their maintenance. Based on current evidence, this strategy is more directly supported in hepatocellular carcinoma. In dysbiosis-driven hepatocellular carcinoma-related models, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from healthy donors reduced NET levels, suppressed angiogenesis, and lowered the intrahepatic metastatic burden [18]. These findings suggest that correcting gut microbial imbalance may do more than ease the inflammatory background. It may also weaken the upstream conditions required for the development of persistent NET states. In colorectal cancer, direct evidence for upstream intervention within this context-dependent working model remains more limited. Most available findings come from inflammation-driven intestinal tumorigenesis. For example, in a Parasutterella excrementihominis-related model, microbiota transplantation from aged donors accelerated tumor formation, whereas dietary intervention markedly affected colonization by this bacterium. A ketogenic diet showed a relatively clear inhibitory effect on its expansion [57]. These results suggest that, for lesion-related microbes that can enter the sequence from lesion-related microbial signals to persistent NET states, limiting the conditions that support their expansion may itself be a feasible way to reduce sustained stimulation. At the same time, this evidence mainly comes from CAC models and cannot yet be directly extended to sporadic colorectal cancer as an established therapeutic approach.

Taken together, upstream intervention against microbial signals acts by weakening the basis on which persistent NET states develop. At present, the clearest support comes from microecological remodeling in hepatocellular carcinoma and from regulation of the ecological conditions that support lesion-related microbes in inflammation-driven colorectal tumorigenesis. At this stage, this strategy is better understood as a way to reduce the likelihood that the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment will form and persist.

Downstream intervention against persistent NET states

Compared with upstream intervention, NET-directed strategies act on persistent NET states that are already present, together with the related local restrictive structure. At present, the clearest evidence comes from DNase I-mediated NET hydrolysis and from blocking key molecular programs required for NET formation. Within this working model, the more direct downstream evidence that falls on the sequence from microbial signals to persistent NET states is still concentrated in a limited number of tumor settings.

In colorectal cancer, this therapeutic tractability has already been shown more directly. Fn-related studies indicate that DNase I can partly reverse the NET-dependent migration, invasion, and metastatic phenotypes induced by Fn, and can also reduce tumor burden in mice [32]. Studies centered on E. coli further show that, in colorectal cancer liver metastasis models, DNase I or the PAD4 inhibitor GSK484 can reduce liver metastatic burden, suppress tumor growth, and decrease Vimentin expression. At the same time, NOD1/2 antagonist-1 or RIPK2 deficiency can suppress E. coli-induced NET release and metastatic progression [72]. Taken together, these findings suggest that, in settings driven by specific microbial signals, persistent NET states are themselves amenable to intervention. This tractability is reflected not only in blocking NET formation, but also in dismantling NET-related structures that have already formed. Findings in hepatocellular carcinoma provide complementary support for the same translational direction. HBV-related studies show that DNase I can suppress NET-mediated tumor growth and metastasis [81]. This suggests that, in settings driven by virus-related microbial signals, once NETs have formed and become part of tumor progression, structural dismantling of NETs can itself alter disease outcome. Compared with the gut-derived microbial signal-driven colorectal cancer models, such evidence more directly supports therapeutic tractability once persistent NET states have already been established. By contrast, in the narrower translational sequence of gut-derived microbial signals, persistent NET states, and therapeutic intervention, the colorectal cancer models remain the more direct examples.

Overall, upstream regulation of microbial signals and downstream intervention against persistent NET states do not represent two competing strategies. They act at different levels of the same process. The first targets the source of sustained stimulation. The second acts on the local restrictive state once it has formed. Current evidence therefore supports a stratified combination approach more strongly than a single-node treatment strategy. At the same time, therapeutic evidence that falls directly within the sequence of microbial signals and persistent NET states remains limited and is still concentrated in a small number of preclinical models. This suggests that the translational value of this strategy still needs to be tested across more tumor settings and with more direct human evidence (Fig. 5).

Two-level translational intervention within the lesion-related microbial signals–persistent NET states sequence. Potential intervention can be placed at two levels within this sequence. Upstream intervention acts on lesion-related microbial signals and the ecological conditions that sustain them, thereby reducing the likelihood of persistent NET-state formation. Downstream intervention acts on established persistent NET states through NET degradation, inhibition of NET formation, or blockade of key NET-related programs, thereby disrupting the local restrictive state once it has formed. Both levels may help relieve the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment, reduce angiogenesis, invasion, and metastatic support, and improve the basis for combination treatment. Current evidence provides relatively stronger support for upstream microecological remodeling in hepatocellular carcinoma and for downstream NET-directed intervention in colorectal cancer

Conclusion

Current evidence suggests that the role of microbial signals in tumors extends beyond the induction of NET formation alone. Their key effect also lies in converting otherwise transient lesion-related stimulation into persistent local effects through persistent NET states. On this basis, we propose a context-dependent working model to explain how lesion-related microbial signals contribute to the formation and maintenance of the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment through persistent NET states, and why this process leads to different dominant tumor-promoting consequences and different degrees of closure across organs.

At present, colorectal cancer provides the strongest continuous support for this context-dependent working model. In this setting, lesion-related microbial signals, persistent NET states, and downstream consequences are more readily linked within the same local environment. Hepatocellular carcinoma is biologically compatible with this model, but its dominant pattern is more one of organ background conditioning followed by local amplification. In other tumors, current evidence more often supports only individual components of the model rather than a similar degree of continuous support. The key issue at this stage is not to place different tumor types into one fixed template, but to define in which lesion context this model is dominant and at which level closure is achieved.

From a translational perspective, current evidence favors stratified intervention along this sequence rather than treating NETs as a universal target. Upstream, modulation of lesion-related microbial signals and the conditions that sustain them may reduce the likelihood that persistent NET states will form. Downstream, intervention against persistent NET states may weaken their continued effects on the lesion environment. Because available evidence remains largely preclinical and is still uneven across tumor settings, the present value of this context-dependent working model lies mainly in providing a theoretical basis for relieving the cold tumor-related local restrictive environment and guiding later therapeutic development, rather than in supporting a uniformly validated therapeutic outcome.

Several questions still need clearer answers. One is how to define which microbial drivers truly have lesion-related significance, rather than giving equal weight to all microbial changes. Another is how to establish marker systems that can capture the spatial features and dynamic changes of persistent NET states, so that stratified assessment and clinical translation become possible. The temporal relationship between microbial dysbiosis and cold tumor development also needs clearer definition, especially whether developing tumors can in turn reinforce microbial dysbiosis. Direct support for this context-dependent working model still comes mainly from selected bacterial settings, although virus-related studies have already provided key functional support. By contrast, the roles of fungi and other microbial signals in persistent NET states and cold tumor biology still need to be defined.
