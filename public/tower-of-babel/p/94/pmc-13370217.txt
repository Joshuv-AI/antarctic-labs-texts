PVA-based microneedle systems for precise molecular delivery in plants
Authors: Mingzhuo Li, Aditi Dey Poonam, Deepjyoti Singh, Jin Xu, Hongwei Jing, Ziwei Zuo, Yipeng Huang, Nana Liu, Yajun Liu, Liping Gao, Tao Xia, Anna E Whitfield, Qingshan Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional crop delivery methods, such as foliar spray and soil application, face significant limitations, including nutrient loss, environmental impacts, and low delivery efficiency. Recent advances in nanomaterials have enabled novel molecular delivery platforms; however, challenges such as synthesis complexity, long-term stability, and compliance with rigorous biosafety regulations persist. To provide a simpler, lower-cost, and safer alternative, we developed a polyvinyl alcohol-based microneedle (MN) delivery system that can be precisely applied to various plant tissues, including stems, lateral branches, or petioles. This system demonstrates high delivery efficiency compared with conventional methods (3.5× higher tissue accumulation) while reducing the application dose (>90% reduction). It facilitates the delivery of diverse small molecules, ranging from fluorescent dyes to growth promoters and antiviral hormones, into plant tissues while causing minimal wounding stress. In particular, salicylic acid injection using MNs induced resistance to tomato spotted wilt virus in Nicotiana benthamiana, opening a new possibility for plant immunity engineering without gene editing. Overall, this easily fabricated and cost-effective MN system offers a promising tool for precision agriculture, enhancing plant health and productivity while significantly reducing the use of agrochemicals.Key words: plant delivery, microneedle, phytohormone, pathogen resistance, precision agriculture

Introduction

As global food demand continues to rise to feed 10 billion people by 2050, and natural resources become increasingly limited, more efficient agricultural delivery methods are needed to produce higher yields with fewer inputs. One promising approach is precision delivery, which enables farmers to apply water, fertilizers, and pesticides with greater accuracy and efficiency. Rather than treating entire fields uniformly, these methods employ high-resolution, variable-rate delivery approaches to target specific areas or even individual plants based on soil conditions, crop requirements, and environmental factors. This targeted strategy reduces waste, lowers production costs, and minimizes environmental impacts while improving crop health and yield. In contrast, two of the most widely used methods for active delivery in agriculture are foliar spraying and soil application (Nkebiwe et al., 2016; Niu et al., 2021). These methods are popular due to their simplicity, speed, and ease of scaling up. However, with the continued development of the agricultural industry, the limitations of these traditional delivery methods have become increasingly apparent (Zheng et al., 2020; Sinha et al., 2022). As larger areas of cropland and a wider variety of crops are integrated into the agricultural industry to meet rising food demands driven by rapid population growth, conventional compound application methods face growing challenges (Davis et al., 2016; Fukase and Martin, 2020).

In foliar spraying, most applied compounds fail to reach internal plant tissues because deposition, retention, and penetration are primarily governed by aerodynamic drift, surface wetting, and cuticular permeability. During application, a substantial proportion of aerosol droplets is lost to air dispersion before reaching leaf surfaces. Even after deposition, limited adhesion, runoff, evaporation, and restricted diffusion across the cuticle further reduce uptake into plant tissues. Previous studies have shown that 30%–50% of sprayed chemicals are lost to the air due to spray drift, resulting in a low deposition efficiency of ∼20% under unfavorable conditions (Boonupara et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023).

Soil application is similarly constrained by environmental transport processes. After entering the soil, nutrients and agrochemicals partition among adsorption, leaching, volatilization, and microbial transformation before being take up by plants (Zeng et al., 2021). Because root absorption competes with these processes, only a fraction of applied compounds is ultimately utilized, while measurable losses through runoff and leaching commonly occur across crop systems and soil types.

Foliar application and soil-based treatments also pose significant environmental concerns when applied at high volumes or under suboptimal timing. Although these methods are convenient for crop uptake, runoff and leaching during rainfall or irrigation can transport active compounds into adjacent water bodies or deeper soil layers. As a result, larger application volumes are required to compensate for these losses and to achieve effective concentrations within plant tissues, thereby increasing environmental pressure, including groundwater contamination and ecosystem disturbance. Soil-applied fertilizers commonly lead to nitrate leaching into groundwater (Hina, 2024). Excessive chemical inputs can also cause water contamination, soil acidification, salinization, structural degradation, and reduced soil biodiversity (Alkharabsheh et al., 2021; Young et al., 2021; Brichi et al., 2023). The frequent detection of pesticides in protected surface waters further highlights the systemic environmental dispersion associated with conventional soil applications. These inefficiencies are therefore not specific to particular crops or agrochemicals but represent inherent limitations of current delivery methods that mainly rely on uncontrolled plant–environment interfaces for transport.

In addition to generating waste and imposing environmental pressures, traditional fertilization methods face further limitations due to the crop’s internal structure. The leaf cuticle, which acts as a barrier, slows down nutrient and chemical absorption (Krishnasree et al., 2021; Vasundhara and Chhabra, 2021; Barłóg et al., 2022). As such, foliar spraying often provides only short-term relief from nutrient deficiencies and requires frequent reapplication. Similarly, soil fertilization is inefficient, as plant uptake is delayed while nutrients move through the soil matrix into root tissues, and it can also lead to uneven nutrient distribution (Dimkpa et al., 2020; Barłóg et al., 2022).

In recent years, several approaches have been developed to address these limitations in traditional crop delivery systems and improve molecular delivery in plants. One emerging approach involves the use of nanomaterials as plant delivery systems (Liu et al., 2021; Salama et al., 2021; Beig et al., 2022; Jakhar et al., 2022). Nanomaterials possess properties that protect labile compounds from rapid degradation, enhance adhesion of payloads to plant surfaces, enable controlled release, and improve permeability through the cuticle or internalization into plant tissues and cells (Nair et al., 2010; Cunningham et al., 2018). For example, nanocarriers (e.g., carbon nanotubes, quantum dots, and metal/metal oxide nanoparticles) have been designed to deliver DNA molecules for plant genome engineering, such as gene transformation, small interfering RNA silencing, and gene editing (Nair et al., 2010; Cunningham et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020, 2021, 2022; Wang et al., 2021). However, most nanoscale genetic delivery methods are limited by the complexity of nanomaterial design. In addition, the potential toxicity of nanoparticles to plants and animals remains a significant concern (Cunningham et al., 2018). Furthermore, these methods are commonly applied via spraying, which similarly suffers from low uptake efficiency (∼0.1% for root application and <30% via leaves) (Tudi et al., 2021). Alternative delivery methods, such as foliar infiltration, are labor-intensive and restricted to leaves, limiting their use in the field and across diverse plant species.

Microneedles (MNs) are an emerging technique that has recently been applied in precision agriculture (Nagarkar et al., 2020; Jung and Jin, 2021). MNs are microscopic needles, typically ranging from 150 to 1500 μm in length, 50 to 250 μm in base width, and 1 to 25 μm in tip diameter. They can be applied to plant tissues to deliver essential compounds such as nutrients, metabolites, and agrochemicals (Ece et al., 2023; Faraji Rad, 2023). One example is biocompatible silk-based MNs developed for the precise delivery of pesticides and other essential compounds into plants (Cao et al., 2020, 2023; Han et al., 2025). For instance, these silk-based MNs successfully delivered the plant hormone gibberellic acid (GA3) into Arabidopsis and promoted stem growth (Cao et al., 2023). They have also been used to precisely deliver physiological doses of the plant hormone melatonin into vegetables (Brassica rapa subsp. chinensis), significantly extending shelf life by 4 days at room temperature or 10 days under refrigeration (Han et al., 2025). Although silk-based MNs provide a promising tool for delivering physiologically active compounds into crops, their material source and fabrication process limit large-scale agricultural applications. Recently, a versatile core–shell MN platform has been developed, which enabled the targeted delivery of diverse cargoes—ranging from biomolecules to active microorganisms—into both terrestrial and aquatic plants (Shen et al., 2025). While this amphibious MN design overcomes species and humidity-related barriers, it also increases the complexity of MN fabrication.

In our previous study, we developed a different MN system using water-soluble and biocompatible polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as the base material (Paul et al., 2020, 2021, 2022). PVA-based MNs effectively isolated genetic molecules (e.g., DNA and RNA) from plant tissues, demonstrating their potential for early and in-field crop disease diagnostics (Paul et al., 2020, 2022). More recently, we applied PVA MNs to crop seeds (e.g., soybeans) and demonstrated their capability for rapid seed DNA extraction for genotyping and whole-genome sequencing—a greatly simplified trait analysis technology that can substantially accelerate the breeding process (Li et al., 2025). While nucleic acid extraction relies on the rapid swelling behavior of PVA (<1 min contact time), which facilitates the absorption of intratissue biomarkers onto the needle surface, PVA can also be completely dissolved in an aqueous environment, albeit on a slower timescale (≥10 min contact time). This latter property forms the basis of the PVA MN system, making it an excellent delivery platform. The concept was recently demonstrated by Le et al., who developed a PVA-based dissolvable MN system to deliver biofertilizers directly into plant leaves and stems, significantly enhancing the growth of vegetables (Le et al., 2026).

Despite substantial progress in delivering various cargoes into host plants using different MN systems, one important application that has not been widely demonstrated is the enhancement of plant immunity against pathogen infections. Currently, preventive chemical applications and gene editing to introduce pathogen-resistant traits are the primary strategies for plant disease control. However, the former leads to excessive chemical waste and environmental pollution, while the latter is extremely expensive and difficult to scale. The concept of plant vaccination is emerging (Holmes et al., 2019) but remains limited in practical applications due to inefficient delivery approaches.

In this work, we systematically investigated the capability of cost-effective and easily scalable PVA MNs to deliver various small molecules, including dyes, the growth promoter GA3, and the antiviral hormone salicylic acid (SA), into model plant systems. In addition to analyzing molecular release dynamics and the phenotypic effects of GA3 in promoting plant growth and restoring lateral flowering in Arabidopsis ft-10 mutants, we carefully characterized the wounding effects of PVA MNs and quantified the in situ concentrations of delivered chemicals in plant tissues via high-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS). More importantly, we evaluated the delivery of SA in Nicotiana benthamiana to inhibit the spread of tomato spotted wilt virus (TSWV), providing a promising example of induced immunity mediated by MN delivery. Together, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of PVA MNs in delivering small molecules, particularly bioactive plant hormones, into plants. This PVA MN system holds great promise for future applications in precision agriculture.

Results

Tomato was chosen as the model system due to its well-defined vascular structure and long petioles. Previous studies have shown that the distance between the vasculature and the epidermis ranges from 840 to 1040 μm for xylem and 707 to 925 μm for phloem in tomato plants, with xylem and phloem diameters in the tens and hundreds of micrometers, respectively (Cao et al., 2020). Based on these parameters, we designed and fabricated a 1000-μm-long PVA MN as a microinjector to study payload delivery in planta (Figure 1A).

Effect of MN-induced mechanical stress on plant growth

PVA is a synthetic polymer with several advantageous properties, such as water solubility, biodegradability, non-toxicity, and tunable mechanical strength, making it a promising candidate for applications in plant delivery. To test whether PVA MNs can be used for chemical delivery into plant tissues, we first examined their potential mechanical damage to plant health. Due to the strong hygroscopic nature of PVA, leaf tissues punctured by PVA MNs dehydrated rapidly if the MNs were not removed immediately after penetration (e.g., contact time >20 s), causing the surrounding tissues to wilt within one hour post-puncture (Supplemental Figure 1). In contrast, when MNs were applied to the stem, although localized dehydration occurred at the puncture site, the vascular bundles remained largely unaffected and continued to function properly (Supplemental Figure 1). These results indicate that MN contact time must be carefully controlled for leaf delivery, whereas the stem is a more robust and suitable delivery site for PVA MNs.

The morphological changes in MN tips before and after stem penetration were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (Supplemental Figures 2A and 2B). We found that after 30 s inside the stem, the MN tips were softened, and some needle tips were partially dissolved by cellular fluids within the stem. The weight loss of PVA MNs was also monitored. Before MN insertion into the tomato stem, the average weight of a small PVA MN patch (9 × 6 array) was approximately 2.5 mg (Supplemental Figure 2C). After MN injection, the average weight of the used MNs was about 7%, 10%, or 13% lower than that of new MN patches after contact times of 1, 2, and 15 min, respectively, indicating that the PVA material gradually dissolves during MN treatment (Supplemental Figure 2C). Additionally, we tested the mechanical properties of PVA MNs under compression on the stem. The results showed that the average needle-tip break force required for tomato stem penetration was approximately 0.2 N (Supplemental Figure 2D). These observations indicate that PVA MNs exhibit an excellent combination of rapid water solubility and sufficient mechanical strength for plant delivery.

Next, we investigated whether PVA MN puncture of the stem had a negative impact on plant health and growth over time. We examined the damage caused by MN puncture in tomato stems under a microscope (Supplemental Figure 3). On the punctured surface, the stems appeared slightly hardened, but no visible oxidation or darkening was observed at the puncture sites. This phenomenon may be attributed to the water-absorbing property of PVA, which induces local cell dehydration and the formation of a protective layer. Microscopic images of tomato stem cross-sections showed that the pith region at the center of the stem developed several void zones at 2 min after MN puncture, indicating acute cell damage (Supplemental Figure 3B, middle column). However, over time, the pith cells repaired themselves and filled these gaps within 48 h after puncturing (Supplemental Figure 3B, right column). Meanwhile, vascular bundle cells at the puncture site formed a protective layer upon cell death, helping to prevent further internal damage. In addition, no excessive tissue oxidation or darkening was observed around the puncture site (Supplemental Figure 3, top row).

The tomato respiratory burst oxidase homolog (SlBOH1) gene is a commonly used indicator of abiotic stress. We analyzed SlBOH1 expression in the stem after PVA MN puncturing. The results showed that the transcript level of SlBOH1 was induced 2- to 6-fold immediately after MN treatment (30 min) (Supplemental Figure 4A). However, SlBOH1 expression decreased to baseline levels 48 h after MN puncturing (Supplemental Figure 4A). At the same time, we also monitored TomLOXD, a classical lipoxygenase gene induced by mechanical damage. Gene expression analysis showed that the relative expression level of TomLOXD was induced 3- to 4-fold at 30 min after MN wounding; after 48 h, its expression returned to the normal range (Supplemental Figure 4B). These molecular results are consistent with the previous microscopic observations (Supplemental Figure 3), suggesting that MN-treated stems experienced a certain level of acute mechanical damage that was subsequently recovered.

Meanwhile, we compared the growth of wild-type (WT) tomato plants (without MN puncturing) with that of plants subjected to three rounds of PVA MN puncturing. Phenotypically, no significant differences in plant height were observed between WT and MN-treated plants (Supplemental Figure 5A). Quantitative measurements of plant height taken at 1, 6, and 15 days after puncturing also revealed no noticeable differences compared with WT plants (Supplemental Figure 5B). Together, these findings (Supplemental Figures 3–5) suggest that PVA MN puncturing of tomato stems, even with repeated treatments, does not significantly inhibit normal plant growth.

Delivery of fluorescent molecules in plant tissues

Fluorescent or colorimetric dyes were chosen as the initial model small molecules for MN-assisted plant delivery (Figure 1A). Because PVA is water-soluble, we speculated that small molecules are more likely to be transported through the xylem. Safranin, a commonly used histological dye for staining xylem ducts, was loaded into the PVA MNs at a concentration of 1%. After stem injection, we observed a faint reddish accumulation around vessel cells, suggesting that MN-delivered dyes were mainly transported through the xylem system after MN penetration (Figure 1B).

We also incorporated fluorescent dyes, such as rhodamine 6G (maximum absorption at 526 nm) and 5(6)-carboxyfluorescein diacetate (CFDA; maximum absorption at 492 nm), into the PVA MNs (Figure 1A). Diffusion of CFDA into surrounding tissues was observed within 5 min of PVA MN injection (Figure 1C). To further analyze molecular transport in the xylem, we performed tangential sectioning of the stem and monitored dye diffusion at 1, 5, and 10 min post-injection using fluorescence imaging. We observed a significantly more widespread distribution at 5 min compared with 1 min (Figure 1C). Quantification of relative fluorescence intensity revealed a 32% and 77% increase in the diffusion area in stems at 5 and 10 min post-injection, respectively (Supplemental Figure 6). Additionally, the dyes were observed to move along the xylem flow within the tomato stem (Supplemental Figure 7). These findings strongly suggest that the PVA MN tips effectively reach the vasculature and that embedded chemical compounds can diffuse and be transported through vascular tissues, especially via the xylem vessel system in tomato plants.

Delivery of the growth regulator GA3 promotes tomato plant growth

To demonstrate the utility of PVA MNs for delivering plant growth regulators to crops, we investigated the delivery of GA3 to tomato plants. GA3 is a plant hormone that plays a crucial role in regulating various growth and developmental processes, including seed germination, stem elongation, flowering, and fruit development (Gupta and Chakrabarty, 2013). GA3 has previously been delivered to Arabidopsis plants via silk MNs (Cao et al., 2023). In the current study, GA3 was selected as a model cargo for lateral comparison between different MN platforms (silk vs. PVA), as well as for direct comparison with other delivery methods such as leaf spraying and stem injection, in both tomato and Arabidopsis plants (see next section).

PVA MNs were injected into the stem tissues of 25-day-old tomato plants (n = 12; Figure 2A and Supplemental Figure 8). Following the initial MN injection, secondary and tertiary injections were performed 3 and 6 days later, respectively. Plants treated with PVA MNs without GA3 (negative control, “Control-MN”), plants receiving direct GA3 injection into the stem via syringe (“GA3-Injection”), and plants sprayed with GA3 on their leaves (positive control, “GA3-Spray”) were included in the experiment (Figure 2A). In addition, untreated healthy WT plants were used as the control (“WT,” Figure 2A). The PVA MN patch contained 500 μM GA3, which is higher than the concentration used in the spray method (100 μM). For direct stem injection, we used 100 μL of GA3 solution with a concentration of 100 μM. However, due to the much smaller delivery volume of the PVA MN method (only an array of ∼4 × 10 needles penetrating the stem) compared with the 5–10 ml applied in the spray method, three PVA MN treatments delivered an estimated total of 0.05–0.075 μmol GA3, which is only approximately 10% of that delivered by the spray method and 50% of that delivered by direct stem injection (Table 1).

After 6 days, tomato plants treated with GA3, including GA3-MN, GA3-Spray, and GA3-Injection, exhibited faster growth compared with control plants (both WT and Control-MN; Figures 2B, 2C, and 2E). Stem lengths (labeled in red in Figures 2B and 2C) were measured quantitatively at the same time. After 3 days, the heights of GA3-MN- and GA3-Spray-treated tomato plants were 18%–21% higher than those of WT or Control-MN plants (Supplemental Figure 8). In contrast, GA3-Injection plants showed an increase of approximately 10% in height (Supplemental Figure 8), indicating lower efficacy than the GA3-MN and GA3-Spray treatments. After 6 days, GA3-treated plants (including GA3-MN, GA3-Spray, and GA3-Injection) showed a 20%–43% increase in height compared with untreated controls (Figure 2E), with GA3-Injection again being slightly less effective than GA3-MN or GA3-Spray. A similar trend was observed on Day 9 (Supplemental Figure 8B) and Day 15 (Supplemental Figure 9). Notably, by Day 15, the GA3-MN group eventually outgrew all control groups (Supplemental Figure 9). In our experiments, direct stem injection of GA3 using a syringe did not promote plant growth to the same extent as leaf spray or MN delivery. This is probably due to the fact that direct stem injection is difficult to control and requires precise regulation of the injection rate to avoid liquid overflow. In contrast, the MN approach is much easier to apply, does not require flow rate control, and is therefore more suitable for field or greenhouse applications.

We also tested the effect of application frequency of the GA3-MN treatment on tomato growth (Supplemental Figure 10). Tomato plants used for the MN injection experiment were divided into three groups, receiving one (1×), two (2×), or three (3×) injections. All plants were injected on Day 1; the second and third groups received an additional injection after 3 days, and only the third group received a final injection after 6 days. Plant height was measured throughout the experiment. The results showed that for GA3 delivery, a single MN treatment is sufficient to promote tomato growth (Supplemental Figure 10). On Days 6 and 9, plants in groups 2 and 3, which received additional injections, exhibited greater variation in height compared with group 1 (1× MN injection) (Supplemental Figure 10B). These results demonstrate that, for GA3 delivery via PVA MNs, the application frequency can be as low as one treatment.

Furthermore, GA3 delivery by PVA MNs could also be performed on lateral branches using 40-day-old tomato plants (Supplemental Figures 11A and 11B). The injection procedure was the same as that used for stem treatment. Due to differences in the initial length of each lateral branch, we calculated relative lateral branch elongation to evaluate GA3-induced stimulation. The results showed that, after 3 days of treatment, the average lateral branch length of GA3-MN- and GA3-Spray-treated tomato plants was approximately 5% greater than that of WT and Control-MN plants (Supplemental Figure 11C). At 6 days post-injection, the average lateral branch length in the GA3-MN group increased by nearly 10% compared with the control groups (Supplemental Figure 11D).

In addition to differences in stem or branch length, we observed that tomato plants in the GA3-treated groups (GA3-MN or GA3-Spray) showed a noticeable color difference from the fourth to sixth compound leaves (newly emerging leaves) (Supplemental Figure 12A). We assume that this phenotypic difference is likely due to the accelerated growth triggered by GA3. The concentrations of chlorophyll a (p = 0.0049) and chlorophyll b (p = 0.0025) in the fourth to sixth leaves (mixed samples) were also quantified. The results showed that, in GA3-treated tomato plants, leaf chlorophyll a and b contents decreased by 6%–10% compared with WT and Control-MN plants (Supplemental Figures 12B and 12C), suggesting a faster growth rate in the GA3-treated groups. To investigate whether GA3-MN treatment triggers intrinsic transcriptional changes in tomato plants, we conducted a series of reverse transcription–quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) experiments using tomato leaf samples collected after different treatments. We selected two GA signal receptor genes, GID1 and GID1b1 (Shohat et al., 2021), as targets for gene expression analysis. The results revealed that, after GA3-MN or GA3-Spray treatments, both GID1 and GID1b1 showed significant increases in transcription levels (2- to 15-fold) compared with WT and Control-MN plants (Figure 2F). The expression levels in the GA3-MN group were generally comparable to those in the GA3-Spray group (Figure 2G). Interestingly, in individual plant #9, MN treatment promoted higher GID gene expression than conventional spraying. Overall, these data strongly suggest that PVA MN-mediated GA3 delivery promotes stem or branch growth similarly to foliar spraying while reducing the application dose by 10–20-fold. Consistently, genes involved in GA3 signaling were also upregulated, confirming the effectiveness of GA3 delivery via the MN patch in tomato.

PVA MNs for GA3 delivery to Arabidopsis ft-10 mutants

Besides tomato, we also investigated the effects of PVA MN injection on GA3 signaling in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana. A. thaliana offers several advantages for phenotypic analysis, such as well-characterized biological processes (including development, metabolism, hormone signaling, and stress responses), ease of genetic manipulation, and comprehensive genomic information.

Numerous studies have shown that the application of gibberellins (such as GA3) in A. thaliana promotes the transition from vegetative growth to reproductive development, regulates flower formation, accelerates bolting, and reduces the number of rosette leaves while increasing the number of cauline leaves (Bao et al., 2020). Under normal conditions, WT A. thaliana Col-0 plants produce around 14 rosette leaves during vegetative growth before inflorescence emergence at approximately 23 days under a 16-h light regime. Other studies report 11–13 rosette leaves between Days 21 and 24 (Cao et al., 2023). Cao and colleagues investigated the Arabidopsis ft-10 mutant, a loss-of-function mutant of the FT gene that exhibits a clear late-flowering phenotype under long-day conditions, to evaluate their silk-based MN delivery system (Cao et al., 2023). This mutant disrupts pathways that predominantly regulate flowering under long-day conditions independently of GA3 (Supplemental Figure 13A). Unlike the previously reported silk-based MNs, our study applied PVA MNs to the petiole of a single rosette leaf in Arabidopsis plants (Supplemental Figures 13B and 13C). The PVA MNs used in this experiment were 800 μm in length.

To assess the effectiveness of PVA MNs in delivering GA3 to Arabidopsis plants, we compared MN-mediated delivery with foliar spray. Additionally, empty PVA MNs (without GA3) were used as the negative control (Control-MN; Figure 3A). GA3 was delivered via PVA MN injections at multiple leaves every 3 days, for a total of 3 applications (3×), similar to the frequency of foliar sprays. For MN injection on an Arabidopsis vein, a small array of 2 × 10 needles was used, whereas the leaf spray method used 2–4 ml of GA3 solution. The total GA3 amount delivered by the spray method was nearly 10 times that used in the MN injection method (Table 1). Both GA3-Spray and GA3-MN treatments resulted in early bolting compared with the ft-10 control and the Control-MN group (MNs without GA3) (Figures 3B and 3C). Although GA3-MN-treated plants bolted earlier than the controls, their response was slower than that of GA3-Spray-treated plants. This difference likely reflects the distinct two-stage developmental pattern of Arabidopsis, in which vegetative growth precedes reproductive growth. During the vegetative phase, the plant dedicates energy to rosette formation, with the main stem internodes remaining compressed. Upon receiving appropriate environmental and internal cues, the plant undergoes a critical phase transition that initiates rapid, dramatic elongation of the central stem, known as bolting. In our experiment, PVA MN injection was performed at the petiole, which differs from the stem injection used in tomato plants. The diffusion of GA3 from the petiole to the whole plant is likely slower than that from direct stem injection. Furthermore, MN treatment targeted only 3 of more than 20 inflorescences, meaning that the total GA3 applied in the GA3-MN group was substantially lower than that in the GA3-Spray group. Together, these factors likely contribute to the differences in flowering time observed between the GA3-MN and GA3-Spray groups.

In addition, rosette and cauline leaf numbers were recorded throughout the growth period. GA3-MN injections in ft-10 produced phenotypes similar to those observed in GA3-Spray plants, including changes in rosette and cauline leaf numbers and leaf size (Figures 3D and 3E). These phenotypes were significantly different from those of ft-10 mutants not treated with GA3 (Figures 3D and 3E), confirming that GA3 delivered via MNs effectively alters plant metabolism. Interestingly, Control-MN plants (without GA3) produced fewer rosette leaves than untreated ft-10 mutants, suggesting that PVA MN injection may induce some stress even in the absence of GA3 (Figure 3D). These phenotypic findings demonstrate that PVA MNs effectively deliver GA3 to Arabidopsis plants and alter their growth behavior accordingly. In Arabidopsis, 3 GA receptor isoform genes have been identified: AtGID1a, AtGID1b, and AtGID1c. Using RT-qPCR, we analyzed the transcript levels of these genes with and without GA3-MN treatment (Figures 3F and 3G). We found that AtGID1a showed low transcript levels in the ft-10 mutant background, whereas AtGID1b and AtGID1c showed a significant increase in mRNA levels (2- to 10-fold) following GA3-MN and GA3-Spray treatments (Figures 3F and 3G). These molecular results are consistent with the phenotypic observations, further supporting that PVA MNs are an effective tool for delivering plant hormones in Arabidopsis.

Delivery of SA induces TSWV resistance in N. benthamiana plants

To test whether plant hormones can be successfully delivered into plants using MNs, we selected SA as a testing model and fabricated a PVA-MN patch loaded with 5 mM SA. SA is an important plant hormone that plays a significant role in triggering pathogen resistance responses. To measure SA delivery efficiency, we used a 3 × 18 MN array applied to the N. benthamiana petiole with a 2 min contact time (“SA-MN”). At the same time, a 500 mM SA solution was sprayed on the leaf surface (“SA-Spray”) or infiltrated into the leaf (“SA-Infiltration”) as controls (Figure 4A). Each N. benthamiana plant was sprayed with 5–10 ml of SA solution, and the injection volume for the SA-Infiltration method was approximately 1 ml. After 24 h of treatment, 100 mg of leaf tissue was collected and ground in liquid nitrogen. SA was extracted from plant tissues using an extraction buffer and analyzed by HPLC-MS.

(B) HPLC–MS analysis showing the presence of SA (retention time: 7.29 min) in leaf tissues under different delivery methods.

The HPLC-MS/MS results showed that WT and Control-MN leaves contained very little SA (Figure 4B and Supplemental Figure 14). In contrast, leaves in the SA-MN, SA-Spray, and SA-Infiltration groups all showed relatively high SA concentrations (Figure 4B and Supplemental Figure 14A). Notably, the SA-MN group showed a higher peak than the SA-Spray group (Figure 4B), indicating higher delivery efficiency. We also quantified SA concentrations in different samples. The results showed that leaves with SA-Infiltration had the highest SA content (40 ng/g; Figure 4C). In comparison, MN-treated leaves accumulated 14 ng/g SA, while the spray method resulted in only 4 ng/g SA, the lowest among the three treatments (Figure 4C). Overall, these results verify that the PVA MN approach achieves high delivery efficiency despite its low loading capacity.

TSWV is a plant virus that belongs to the family Tospoviridae and is one of the most economically significant plant viruses affecting crops worldwide (Best, 1968). Infected plants often show necrotic spots, wilting, chlorosis (yellowing), stunted growth, leaf deformation, and, in some cases, ring spots or concentric patterns on leaves and fruits. These symptoms lead to reduced yield and poor fruit quality. The plant hormone SA has been shown to play a significant role in triggering resistance responses to TSWV (Turina et al., 2016). SA is a crucial hormone involved in systemic acquired resistance, a defense mechanism that provides broad-spectrum resistance to a variety of pathogens, including viruses such as TSWV (Durner et al., 1997). In the following experiment, we used PVA MNs to deliver SA directly into plant tissues and investigated whether MN microinjection could confer resistance to TSWV in the model plant N. benthamiana. Normally, the virus moves within plants through a well-coordinated process involving both cell-to-cell movement and systemic transport via the vascular system. To monitor TSWV movement in plants, a TSWV infectious clone generated by Liu et al. was engineered, in which Cas9 was replaced with the RUBY reporter (Liu et al., 2023), making viral movement visible to the naked eye (Supplemental Figure 15).

We compared the SA delivery and treatment efficiency of four different methods: Control (no SA delivery), SA- MN, SA-Spray, and SA-Infiltration (Supplemental Figure 16A). For SA-MN, the MN patch loaded with SA was applied to N. benthamiana petioles four times in total (1× before and 3× after TSWV inoculation). The SA-Spray method was applied twice (2×, before and after inoculation), whereas the SA-Infiltration method was performed only once (Supplemental Figure 16B). For PVA MN injection, a small MN array of 4 × 10 needles was used. The spray and infiltration treatments used 20–25 ml and 8–10 ml of SA solution, respectively. The absolute amounts of SA delivered to tobacco plants by different methods are summarized in Table 1.

In the first trial, the SA-MN group contained 2 mM SA, while the SA-Spray and SA-Infiltration groups used only 500 μM SA, considering their larger application volumes. Still, the estimated amount of SA delivered to the plants by the MN approach was only about 0.5%–1% of that delivered by the spray or infiltration methods (Table 1). Four N. benthamiana plants were used for each treatment, and three leaves were randomly selected from each plant for viral inoculation. During the infection process, a gradual deepening of RUBY pigmentation was observed, indicating the progression of viral infection. On Days 3 and 6 post-inoculation, we recorded the numbers of lightly and deeply colored leaves to estimate the strength of the immune response in the tobacco leaves. Three days after inoculation of N. benthamiana plants with TSWV, virus infection was observed in all test groups. However, the percentages of light-red (indicating strong resistance) and dark-red (indicating weak resistance) leaves differed markedly among the groups. In the Control group, 8 of 12 (∼67%) infected leaves turned dark red. In contrast, only 2–3 of 12 (∼21%) leaves showed dark coloration in SA-treated plants (Figure 5A). There were no major differences among the SA-MN, SA-Spray, and SA-Infiltration groups. After 6 days, nearly 92% of the infected leaves in the Control group were dark red. In contrast, the percentage of dark-red leaves in SA-treated groups remained lower, at around 50% (Figure 5A).

SA-MN delivery restricts TSWV movement in Nicotiana benthamiana.

(A) TSWV infection in N. benthamiana leaves at 3 and 6 days post-inoculation, as indicated by RUBY expression. Light red indicates lower TSWV infection, whereas dark red indicates higher TSWV load and reduced pathogen resistance.

(B) Phenotypes of TSWV-infected N. benthamiana leaves at 15 days post-infection. SA-MN delivery inhibited TSWV movement from the leaf to the petiole. The red arrows indicate the movement direction of Rubby-labeled TSWV.

These results indicate that MNs are a highly effective platform for delivering SA into leaf tissues and inducing TSWV resistance. Notably, after 15 days of viral infection, all infected leaves turned dark red and eventually died. The petioles of the Control, SA-Spray, and SA-Infiltration groups all turned red (Figure 5B), indicating the transport of TSWV through the plant vascular system. However, the SA-MN-treated petioles remained green (Figure 5B), indicating stronger viral resistance and reduced viral transport in SA-MN-treated tissues.

The comparison experiments were repeated by increasing the SA concentration in the SA-Spray and SA-Infiltration groups to 1 mM instead of 500 μM (Supplemental Figure 17A). After 20 days of infection, the Control group clearly showed that TSWV moved from the leaf tissue to the stem along the veins in WT N. benthamiana plants (Supplemental Figure 17B). However, in SA-MN-, SA-Spray-, and SA-Infiltration-treated plants, no red coloration was observed in the stem (Supplemental Figure 17B). After 30 days of infection, the Control plants exhibited a dwarf phenotype compared with the SA-treated plants (Supplemental Figure 18A). The stems of Control plants showed tissue wilting. In contrast, the SA-MN- and SA-Spray-treated plants remained healthy, whereas some viral infection (red coloration) was still observed in SA-Infiltration-treated plants (Supplemental Figure 18B), which may be due to the lower total SA dose applied in the infiltration method (infiltration: 5 ml of 1 mM SA; spray: 10 ml of 1 mM SA, repeated 2×).

These experiments strongly suggest that SA can be effectively delivered to N. benthamiana plants using the MN delivery method, thereby inducing resistance to TSWV infection. Compared with the traditional spray method, although the SA-MN method requires more frequent applications (a total of 4×) and uses a higher SA concentration (2 mM), it significantly reduces the total application dose by ∼99% (Table 1) while achieving comparable protective effects. Moreover, the spray method requires multiple application sites, including both the adaxial and abaxial leaf surfaces, whereas PVA MN injection can be applied directly to the stem or petiole. This suggests that our PVA MN delivery method not only reduces the amount of active ingredient required but may also allow faster and more convenient application.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that PVA-MN patches can be effectively utilized as a precision plant delivery system for various compounds, including small-molecule dyes, growth factors, and antiviral hormones (Figure 6). Compared with conventional delivery methods, such as foliar spray and soil application—which suffer from high chemical losses, environmental impacts, and low absorption (Zeng et al., 2021; Sinha et al., 2022; Boonupara et al., 2023)—PVA MNs offer a promising alternative that directly delivers target molecules into plant tissues with high precision, low application doses, and reduced environmental side effects.

Comparison between conventional agrochemical delivery and MN-based precision delivery in plants.

The right panel summarizes the different cargos delivered by the PVA-MN platform into living plants in this study and their functions.

Our results show that PVA MNs successfully delivered fluorescent dyes into tomato stem vascular tissues, demonstrating their ability to penetrate plant tissues and promote compound diffusion (Figure 1). This finding highlights the advantage of PVA MNs in enhancing the uptake and transport of bioactive molecules, which are often limited by environmental factors and plant surface barriers. Wounding assessments indicate that although MN insertion causes initial mechanical damage, its negative effects on stems are temporary and recoverable (Supplemental Figures 1–5). Delivery of GA3 via PVA MNs accelerates tomato stem growth and alters chlorophyll levels in newly developed leaves, confirming effective hormone delivery (Figure 2). Compared with foliar spraying, MNs produced comparable or slightly improved growth responses while reducing GA3 usage by nearly 90%, indicating a more controlled and efficient delivery approach. The upregulation of GA receptor genes further confirmed successful activation of hormonal signaling. In Arabidopsis ft-10 mutants, MN-mediated GA3 delivery induced early bolting similar to foliar spraying, validating the efficiency of MNs in the model species Arabidopsis (Figure 3). The increased expression of GA receptor genes underscores the potential of PVA MNs for fine-tuning plant growth and development in agricultural applications. Finally, the application of SA via PVA MNs in N. benthamiana demonstrated the ability of MNs to enhance plant resistance against TSWV, offering a new strategy for plant immunization with high accuracy (Figure 5). The restricted viral movement observed in SA-treated plants highlights the effectiveness of MNs in delivering antiviral compounds directly to infection sites, thereby strengthening the plant’s natural defense mechanisms. This targeted approach holds promise for controlling viral infections while potentially reducing reliance on broad-spectrum chemical treatments that may have adverse environmental effects.

Our PVA MNs differ from several previously reported MN systems used in agriculture. For example, Cao and colleagues designed a silk-based MN platform (Cao et al., 2020, 2023) that can deliver small molecules and Agrobacterium to leaf tissues. However, a major limitation of their approach is that the fabrication process for silk MNs is more complex, and the production cost is substantially higher than that of our PVA MNs. A recent core–shell MN delivery platform based on a sodium hyaluronate core is also promising (Shen et al., 2025). However, it increases fabrication complexity, and hyaluronate MNs are less mechanically robust than PVA materials, which may limit their application to softer plant tissues. In terms of deliverable molecules, antiviral or vaccine agents have not yet been tested with MN platforms.

On the other hand, several emerging nanomaterial-based delivery methods have been developed for delivering small molecules, DNA fragments, and even proteins to plants (Demirer et al., 2021). However, these approaches are more suitable for small-scale laboratory studies. Their overall cost is higher than that of PVA MNs, and their scalability is more limited (Demirer et al., 2019, 2020; Beig et al., 2022). In addition, although nanomaterials may enhance delivery at the cellular level, many still rely on foliar spray or infiltration-based methods at the tissue level. As an initial demonstration of using PVA MNs for molecular delivery in plants, we selected several small molecules, such as plant hormones, for validation in this study. In the near future, MNs also hold strong promise for delivering larger biomolecules—including DNA, RNA, proteins, and even gene-editing systems—into plant tissues.

The MN approach offers clear advantages over existing and emerging plant delivery methods. Owing to their simple fabrication, low cost, and environmental safety, MN patches can be directly attached to plant stems, petioles, or fruit pedicels for localized delivery of nutrients, hormones, or protective agents. In greenhouse settings, MNs can be applied manually or via automated dispensers to achieve controlled and reproducible dosing. In field applications, MN systems could be integrated with existing agricultural machinery for large-scale, site-specific treatment. Furthermore, the smart polymer matrix allows the design of time-controlled or humidity-responsive release formulations, enabling sustained molecular delivery under fluctuating environmental conditions. After use, PVA MNs can be dissolved in water or degraded by various microorganisms under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions, producing CO2 and H2O rather than persistent plastic fragments, thereby minimizing microplastic contamination in plant tissues and surrounding environments (Nair and Laurencin, 2007; Tokiwa and Calabia, 2007; Malka and Margel, 2023). Together, these features highlight the feasibility of applying MN technology in precision agriculture to enhance crop health and productivity while minimizing environmental impacts.

Despite these promising results, several challenges remain for the future implementation of MN-based delivery methods. For instance, the dehydration effect observed in MN-treated leaf tissues suggests that MN application may be better suited to stems or stronger plant tissues. Stem delivery may also accommodate larger payloads and enable faster transport to other parts of the plant compared with leaf delivery. Moreover, due to the hydrophilic properties of PVA, it is essential to maintain the stability and activity of loaded biomolecules within the PVA matrix, which requires further optimization of MN formulations.

Looking forward, integrating PVA MN technology with agricultural robotics represents a promising direction for next-generation precision crop management. As field robots and autonomous platforms continue to advance in sensing, navigation, and manipulation (Vougioukas, 2019; Oliveira et al., 2021; Wakchaure et al., 2023), MN-based delivery systems could be incorporated into robotic arms or end-effectors to enable fully automated, site-specific treatment of individual plants. Such systems could autonomously diagnose plant nutrient deficiencies, pathogen infections, or growth abnormalities using onboard cameras and sensors, followed by immediate, localized intervention through MN-mediated delivery with minimal human involvement. Despite this potential, several practical challenges remain. First, although various agricultural robotic systems—including gantry-based greenhouse robots, wheeled ground platforms, and legged robots—have been demonstrated recently, their costs remain relatively high for large-scale applications (Durán et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). Second, the operational speed of current robotic systems is generally slower than that of humans, which may limit their time-saving advantages in practice. Third, advances in robotic hardware must be coordinated with software development, including the integration of artificial intelligence-assisted object recognition, precise robotic gripper path planning, and post-treatment evaluation. In the short term, MNs could be integrated with carefully selected robotic systems that balance cost and performance (e.g., precision and speed) to target high-value crops. In the long term, the economic feasibility of such systems is expected to improve with the development of more affordable smart robots and the realization of tangible gains from field adoption and trials.

In summary, our findings suggest that PVA MNs are a viable and effective tool for plant compound delivery, offering advantages over traditional methods in terms of precision, efficiency, and reduced environmental impact. Future research should focus on scaling up MN fabrication, automating delivery processes, and expanding the range of compounds that can be effectively delivered using this innovative system. With continued development, MN-based delivery could revolutionize precision agriculture by enabling more sustainable and targeted crop management.

Methods

Plant materials and growing conditions

A. thaliana ft-10 mutant seeds (TAIR Germplasm CS9869) and ecotype Columbia (Col-0) seeds were sterilized by soaking in 70% ethanol for 1 min, followed by 10 min in 20% bleach, and then rinsed five to seven times with sterile distilled water. Sterilized seeds were placed on Murashige and Skoog plates and kept at 4°C for 2 days. Plates were then transferred to a plant growth chamber (Caron 7304-22-1) at 22°C under long-day conditions (day/night 16 h/8 h), with 70%–100% relative humidity and a light intensity of 100 μmol m−2 s−1. Seedlings were transferred to pots (2.5 × 2.5 × 3 in3) containing soil (Miracle-Gro Moisture Control Potting Soil Mix) on day 7 and watered regularly. Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum var. Beefsteak) seeds were purchased from The Seed Plant. Seeds were grown in pots (3.5 × 3.5 × 3 in3) in a plant room under long-day conditions (day/night 16 h/8 h), with a light intensity of 80 μmol m−2 s−1, 70%–90% relative humidity, and day/night temperatures of 26°C/20°C, respectively. N. benthamiana plants were grown in a growth chamber under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle at 26°C and a light intensity of 275 μmol m−2 s−1.

TSWV infection

Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 carrying recombinant plasmids (pL, pM-RUBY, pS-NSs, and triple VSR [viral suppressor of RNAi]) was grown overnight in Luria broth medium containing appropriate antibiotics (rifampicin 50 μg/ml, kanamycin 50 μg/ml, and gentamicin 50 μg/ml). Cells were harvested and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES [pH 5.6], and 100 μM acetosyringone) to an OD₆₀₀ of 1, followed by incubation at 28°C for 3 h. Before infiltration, Agrobacterium cultures carrying pL, pM-RUBY, pS-NSs, and triple VSR plasmids were mixed to a final OD₆₀₀ of 0.25. After 3 h of incubation, fully expanded N. benthamiana leaves were co-infiltrated with Agrobacterium cells harboring the pL, pM-RUBY, pS-NSs, and triple VSR plasmids at a final OD₆₀₀ of 0.25 (Liu et al., 2023).

PVA MN fabrication process

The MN patch was fabricated using a simple centrifugation method with polydimethylsiloxane-based negative molds (Blueacre Technology) and a 10% (w/v) PVA solution (MW 31,000–50,000, 98.0%–98.8% hydrolyzed; Acros Organics). To remove dust and debris from the micrometer-scale crevices of the molds, each chip was first cleaned by repeated rinsing with deionized (DI) water, followed by two rounds of sonication for 5 min each. The molds were then dried at 35°C for 10–20 min to evaporate any remaining water droplets on the needle edges. Next, 1.2 ml of PVA solution was aliquoted into 1.7-ml centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 6260 g (Fisher Scientific accuSpin Micro 17) for 10 min to remove trapped air bubbles. The molds were then submerged in the degassed PVA solution with the needle cavities facing inward and centrifuged again at 6260 g for 10 min to fill the molds. The molds were then removed and sealed with an additional layer of PVA to form a strong base upon drying. Finally, the MN patches were partially covered (to allow slow evaporation) and cured at room temperature for 24 h to achieve complete solidification.

MN patch fabrication with GA3

GA3 (34.6 mg) was dissolved in 500 μl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and 500 μl of DI water to prepare a 100 mM stock solution. Six microliters of this solution was added to 1.2 ml of 10% (w/v) PVA to obtain a final GA3 concentration of 500 μM. Each MN patch (20 × 20 array) was fabricated using 500 μl of the PVA–GA3 solution. In tomato experiments, only a 4 × 10 array was applied to the stem due to its small size. The estimated total amounts of GA3 delivered to different plants by MN are summarized in Table 1.

MN patch fabrication with SA

SA (11.04 mg) was dissolved in 15 ml of DI water to prepare a 5.33 mM stock solution. This solution was then mixed with 16% (w/v) PVA to obtain a final solution containing 2 mM SA and 10% (w/v) PVA for MN fabrication.

MN patch fabrication with rhodamine and CFDA

Rhodamine (52 mg) was dissolved in 1.2 ml of DI water to prepare a 0.09 M stock solution. To prepare a 0.27 M stock solution, 25 mg of CFDA powder was dissolved in 200 μl of DMSO. Thirty-three and eleven microliters of the respective stock solutions were added to 30 ml of 10% (w/v) PVA to obtain final concentrations of 0.1 mM rhodamine and CFDA, respectively. This solution was used to fabricate the MNs.

Delivery of fluorescent dyes into tomato plants

CFDA (1 mM) and rhodamine 6G (1 mM) were mixed in DMSO at a volume ratio of 1:100 during the fabrication of PVA MNs. To monitor fluorescence signals from the delivery and transport of CFDA and rhodamine 6G, cross sections upstream and downstream along the petiole were observed under a microscope with fixed light intensity and an exposure time of 20 ms. The petiole was also sliced longitudinally to image the distribution profile of CFDA in the xylem. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ 1.52i. Fluorescence intensity was used as a proxy for CFDA concentration within the tested range, and fluorescence signals were integrated radially.

Preparation of plant hormone solutions

Preparation of GA3 solutions

A 0.1 M stock solution of GA3 was prepared by dissolving 34.6 mg GA3 in 1 ml of 75% ethanol. The GA3 spray solution was prepared by diluting the stock solution 1000× to a final concentration of 100 μM. Silwet-77 was added to achieve a final concentration of 0.01% in the GA3 spray.

Preparation of SA solutions

A 100 mM stock solution of SA was prepared by dissolving 138 mg of SA in 1 ml of 75% ethanol. The SA spray solution was prepared by diluting the 100 mM stock solution 1000× to a final concentration of 0.1 × 10−3 M. Tween 20 was added to achieve a final concentration of 0.01% in the SA spray and was used as a surfactant to promote SA absorption.

RT-qPCR-based gene expression analysis

RT-qPCR analysis was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) on a CFX96™ system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Gene-specific primer pairs and optimized thermal programs (Supplemental Table 1) were developed to compare expression levels in Arabidopsis, tomato, and N. benthamiana plants. The Arabidopsis UBQ10 gene and tomato Actin gene were used as reference controls for normalization. A preliminary RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that reference gene expression was stable under plant hormone treatments, including GA3. Amplified products were monitored using an optical reaction module (SYBR Green), and the resulting gene expression levels were normalized to those of the reference genes according to a previously described plant protocol (Li et al., 2021). The qPCR analysis was carried out as described previously (Li et al., 2021). The reaction mixture (12 μl total volume) consisted of 10 μl SYBR Green premix, 1.0 μl of 10 μM forward and reverse primers, and 1.0 μl cDNA template (Li et al., 2017). The thermal cycling program followed the protocol of the Bio-Rad SYBR Green PCR Master Mix: 95°C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 1 min; followed by a melt curve from 65°C to 95°C with 0.5°C increments every 2 s; and a final hold at 4°C. Three biological replicates were performed for each gene expression analysis. All primers are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

SA extraction from tobacco leaves and detection using HPLC–MS/MS

For SA analysis, approximately 100 mg of fresh leaf tissue was collected and ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. The powder was transferred to a 2-ml microcentrifuge tube and extracted with 1.0 ml of extraction solvent (methanol:water:formic acid, 80:19:1, v/v/v), followed by the addition of a known amount of internal standard. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min, sonicated in an ice-cooled ultrasonic bath for 10–15 min, and then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube. The pellet was re-extracted with an additional 0.5 ml of extraction solvent, and the supernatants from both extractions were combined (total volume ∼1.5 ml). The extract was stored at −80°C before analysis or subjected directly to solid phase extraction (SPE) purification if needed.

The SA content was measured using an HPLC–MS/MS system (SCIEX Triple Quad 5500) equipped with a C18 column (Phenomenex, 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). The injection volume was 5 μl, with a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min. The column pressure was 1800 bar and the column temperature was maintained at 40°C. The mobile phases were A (100% water) and B (100% acetonitrile). The 12-min gradient program was as follows: initial 95% A/5% B; 1 min, 95% A/5% B; 6 min, 5% A/95% B; 7.5 min, 5% A/95% B; 10 min, 95% A/5% B; and 12 min, 95% A/5% B. The mass spectrometer was operated in negative ion mode with electrospray ionization. The gas temperature was set to 320°C, with a gas flow rate of 6 l/min. The sheath gas temperature was 350°C, with a flow rate of 11 l/min. Data were acquired and analyzed using Analyst software. SA was identified by comparison with standard compounds. For data analysis, the characteristic ion transitions of SA were set as 137/93 or 137/65. The retention time of SA was 7.29 min, and the 137/93 transition represented the dominant primary and secondary ion fragments under our analytical conditions.
