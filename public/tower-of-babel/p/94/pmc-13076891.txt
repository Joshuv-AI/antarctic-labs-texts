The synergistic effects of chronic gas condensate pollution and warming on the survival, performance and reproduction of the mussel Brachidontes pharaonis
Authors: Noga Pressman Tal, Peleg Astrahan, Tamar Guy-Haim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing prevalence of multiple environmental stressors in marine ecosystems necessitates understanding their interactive effects. We investigated the individual and combined effects of chronic gas condensate exposure and warming of 3.1 °C on the survival, respiration and clearance rates of the mussel Brachidontes pharaonis and the mono/polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (M/PAHs) bioconcentration patterns in the mussels’ tissues. Mussels were exposed to gas condensate (0, 0.5, 5, 30, and 100 ppb) under both ambient and warmed conditions for 77 days. Mortality was negligible, while gas condensate exposure significantly and negatively affected metabolic rates. The combined effect of gas condensate and warming led to synergistic impacts on respiration rates. M/PAH bioconcentrations varied, showing a complex interaction between temperature and pollutant exposure. Mussels exposed to 100 ppb accumulated benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) at levels reaching 1215 ng/g — exceeding the regional regulatory threshold by a factor of 208. Our findings highlight the vulnerability of marine ecosystems to the combined threats of hydrocarbon pollution and climate change, emphasizing the need for further research on the long-term consequences of gas condensate exposure in a warmed environment. Introduction

Climate change and hydrocarbon pollution are among the most widespread and pressing environmental stressors, posing serious threats to marine ecosystems1. While each of these stressors can impact ecosystems separately2,3, growing evidence suggests that their combined effects may be particularly severe, leading to synergistic disruptions in physiological processes, survival, and ecosystem stability4–8.

Despite increasing awareness of the risks associated with oil pollution, research has primarily focused on crude oil, with limited understanding of gas condensate, a lighter hydrocarbon by-product of natural gas production. With offshore gas platforms expanding worldwide, accidental spills and chronic discharges of gas condensate into marine ecosystems are becoming more frequent9. Relative to crude oil, gas condensate dominant components have a higher volatility and lower viscosity10, allowing rapid dispersion but also increasing the potential for acute toxicity in marine organisms11,12. Understanding how climate change interacts with gas condensate pollution is critical for assessing the vulnerability of marine ecosystems to these emerging threats.

Gas condensate contains a complex mixture of hydrocarbons, including monocyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (MAHs) and to a lesser extent – polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)11. MAHs show higher volatility, water solubility and lower persistence relative to PAHs13. On the other hand, PAHs are well known for their environmental stability and are frequently described as persistent organic pollutants (POPs), demonstrating substantial bioaccumulation potential14. PAHs and MAHs are lipophilic in nature and have the potential to integrate into biological systems through uptake and accumulation by marine organisms. Their accumulation in marine organisms has been linked to oxidative stress15, immune suppression16, disturbance to endocrine regulation17, developmental impairment18, and reproduction disorders19. Considering these impacts and the varying levels of these potentially harmful components in the gas condensate mixtures, it is crucial to investigate their impact on organisms across different trophic levels, particularly in regions subject to multiple anthropogenic stressors.

Over the past four decades, the Eastern Mediterranean Sea (EMS) has experienced increasing anthropogenic pressures, including coastal development, fisheries, and invasion of Lessepsian alien species via the Suez Canal20–23. Additionally, the EMS faces increasing hydrocarbon pollution due to intensified oil and gas exploration and transport24,25. Among the most alarming stressors is climate change. Since the beginning of the 1980s, average Mediterranean Sea surface temperatures have increased throughout the basin at rate of 0.29–0.44 °C per decade, with stronger warming trends in the eastern Mediterranean. This warming rate is two to three times higher than the average global sea water warming rate, leading to the identification of the Mediterranean Sea as a climate change hotspot26. The rate of reaction in chemical and biological processes generally increases two- to fourfold for every 10 °C increase in temperature27. This principle, known as Q10, is expected to affect toxicity in ectothermic (cold-blooded) organisms by modulating uptake, metabolism, and elimination rates21. Consequently, LC50 values typically decrease with increasing temperature, reflecting heightened toxicity under warmer conditions21,27.

Bivalves play a crucial role in marine food webs as both primary consumers and prey for a wide variety of organisms. As filter feeders, they consume plankton and organic particles suspended in the water, converting them into biomass that becomes available to higher trophic levels28. Bivalves contribute to marine ecosystem stability by filtering water, which improves clarity, increases light penetration, and supports the growth of seaweeds and other organisms. Their presence and abundance in ecosystems can influence the structure of benthic communities, making them integral to the overall health and productivity of coastal and estuarine environments29,30. Warming may have major effects on mussels performance and growth, by influencing filtration, clearance rates, absorption and utilization of food31–33. While data regarding MAHs accumulation is scarce, benthic organisms, such as mussels, are prone to PAH uptake due to their contact with contaminated sediments, which are considered to be the final organic pollutant sink in marine environments34–36. PAHs in mussels have been found to affect survival, activity of detoxification enzymes, immune function, genetic damage, general morphology37–39, as well as reproductive and neoplastic disorders19,40,41. Information regarding the impacts of MAHs which are more dominant components in gas condensates, is absent and is yet to be defined.

In this study, we investigated the individual and combined effects of chronic gas condensate pollution and warming on the survival, physiological and immunological responses of Brachidontes pharaonis, a Lessepsian mussel species, which has become a dominant component of the intertidal and subtidal rocky reef communities. Additionally, we assessed the levels of selected MAHs and PAHs bioconcentration in mussel tissues, providing insights into the toxicological risks posed by gas condensate pollution in a rapidly warming EMS. We hypothesize that (1) the chronic exposure of B. pharaonis to gas condensate will lead to sublethal effects, including impaired performance, i.e. decreased respiration and filtration rates; (2) the exposure to the dual stressors of gas condensate and warming will have negative synergistic effects on performance; and (3) the bioconcentrations of MAHs and PAHs will exhibit different patterns under ambient and warming conditions.

Results

Mortality

The overall mortality was negligible during the experiment (2 mussels), occurring in the middle of the experiment (day 33, warming and 30 ppb gas condensate) and at the end of the experiment (day 77, ambient temperature and 0 ppb gas condensate).

Respiration rates

Rl (Respiration rate by length, µmol O2 h− 1 cm− 1) was significantly and negatively affected by gas condensate exposure (p = 0.0016), but not by temperature (p = 0.9484). The interaction between gas condensate concentrations and temperature was significant (p = 0.0480). Post-hoc tests showed that respiration rates were significantly higher in the 0.5 ppb treatment versus 30 and 100 ppb treatments in the warmed temperature only (Fig. 1; Table 1). Similarly, Rw (Respiration rate by weight, µmol O2 h− 1 g WW− 1) was significantly and negatively affected by condensate exposure (p = 0.0025), but not by temperature (p = 0.8061) or their interaction (p = 0.1671) (Fig. S1, Table S1). GAMs (Generalized Additive Models) analysis revealed a significant non-linear effect of condensate concentration on respiration rates per weight (Rw) under warming conditions (p = 0.002, edf = 1.568), while no significant effect was observed under ambient conditions (p = 0.2730, edf = 1.0), with the model explaining 26.87% of the total variance (Fig. S2).

Clearance rates

Clearance rates by weight (CRW, L gWW− 1 h− 1) and by length (CRl, L cm− 1 h− 1) were significantly and negatively affected by condensate concentrations, but not by temperature or their interaction (Fig. 2, Fig. S3, Table 2, Table S2). Post-hoc tests showed that in the ambient treatment, the clearance rate of the control was higher than all gas condensate levels. In the warming treatment, clearance rate was significantly lower in the 0.5 ppb level compared to control. GAMs analysis for clearance rates per length (CRl) revealed a significant non-linear effect of condensate concentration under ambient conditions (p = 0.004, edf = 2.788), while no significant effect was observed under warming conditions (p = 0.284, edf = 1.0), with the model explaining 48.96% of the total variance (Fig. S4).

MAHs and PAHs measurements

Figure 4 presents the dominant MAHs and PAHs levels measured in mussels whole body tissues following various MAHs and PAHs treatments (see also Supplementary Table S3). As shown in Fig. 3, benzene and toluene exhibited the highest whole-body concentrations among all hydrocarbons analyzed. Following the exposure of the mussels to 100 ppb gas condensate treatment, at ambient temperature, benzene and toluene levels showed levels of 8165 and 5566 ng/g respectively. When mussels were exposed to elevated temperatures, the 5-ppb gas condensate treatment showed again the highest whole-body concentrations of benzene and toluene, reaching levels of 2505 and 3225 ng/g respectively (Fig. 3a and c). Benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) was recorded at considerably high levels of 1215 ng/g following exposure to 100 ppb at ambient temperature, however in the warming treatment, the highest concentration was 310 ng/g after exposure to 30 ppb gas condensate (Fig. 3j).

In general, at ambient temperature, most MAHs and PAHs bioconcentration levels at whole-body tissue increased with the increase in gas condensate concentrations. However, in the warming treatment, at low gas condensate levels, the concentrations of MAHs and PAHs increased considerably, whereas at higher levels of condensate a marked decrease in the concentrations of MAHs and PAHs was recorded. Water PAHs and MAHs levels measured in the seawater are detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

At the ambient temperature treatment, a negative correlation was found between most of the MAHs and PAHs concentrations and the clearance rates (Fig. 4A, Table S5). In contrast, in the warming treatment, there was a negative correlation with the small two and three -rings PAHs, naphthalene, acenaphthylene and anthracene and a positive correlation in the heavier four and five ring PAHs, fluoranthene, pyrene, chrysene, benzo(b)fluoranthene and benzo(k)fluoranthene (Fig. 4B). Benzene, a mono-cyclic carbohydrate, was positively correlated with clearance rates in both the ambient and warming treatments (Fig. 4, Table S6). Respiration rates showed a similar trend.

Discussion

Gas condensate is a petrochemical mixture that, while compositionally similar to crude oil, differs notably in its chemical profile. To date, only a handful of studies have investigated the effects of gas condensate exposure on marine animals and those that have focused on bacteria and plankton, including copepods, coral and sea urchin larvae11,12,42,43. Furthermore, no studies have previously tested the combined impacts of gas condensate pollution and warming, highlighting the necessity of further research on the response to gas condensate and warming in other marine organisms, as well as bioaccumulation patterns and biomagnification potential along the food web.

We tested the individual and combined effects of chronic exposure to gas condensate and warming on the survival, performance and reproduction of Lessepsian mussel Brachidontes pharaonis. By integrating ecotoxicological and physiological assessments, we have demonstrated significant impacts of gas condensate exposure on respiration and clearance rates, as well as synergetic effects of gas condensate and elevated temperatures on respiration. Under ambient and warming treatments, the PAHs and MAHs bioconcentrations followed different patterns.

Bivalves are widely used as bioindicators of pollution due to their sedentary life mode, broad distribution, high abundance, filtering feeding mode, sensitivity to environmental disturbances and their ability to accumulate high concentrations of chemicals in their tissues44. In our long-term experiment, mortality of B. pharaonis was negligible, showing high tolerance to both warming and condensate exposure. These results are in accordance with previous studies. Read & Cumming showed thermal tolerance (37–40 °C) of B. deraissu, a widely distributed tropical mussel45. Moreover, Diaz-Jarmilo et al. showed tolerance of B. rodriguezii, a southwestern Atlantic mussel, to harbor pollution and temperature elevation44.

Respiration rate was negatively and significantly affected by gas condensate concentrations, as well as the interactive effect with elevated temperatures. It was demonstrated that under stressful conditions mussels reduce cellular respiration in an attempt to conserve energy46. Moreover, mussels are known to respond to temperature elevation by altering metabolic rates and enzymatic activities, leading to a reduction in fitness-related functions such as growth, activity and reproduction33,47,48. It was demonstrated that elevated temperature treatments in Mytilus galloprovincialis from a chronically polluted bay, led to enhanced tolerance to seasonal elevated temperatures in the summer, despite a reduction in metabolic rate49.

We did not find a significant effect of temperature on the clearance rates of B. pharaonis. Similarly, experiments showed that changes in temperature had no effect on clearance rates of Mytilus edulis32. However, we found that clearance rates were negatively and significantly affected by gas condensate exposure. Several mechanisms have been suggested to cause a reduction in filter feeding rates by exposure to hydrocarbon pollutants50. It is yet to be determined whether the reduction in clearance rate is caused by physiological impairment due to the narcotic effects of PAHs or MAHs, such as cilia impairment and lesions on the gills50, or if it is a biological response to minimize ingestion of pollutants like PAHs51.

In the warming treatment, respiration and clearance rates showed a biphasic dose response curve, i.e., an initial increase in low gas condensate levels (at 0.5 ppm and 5 ppm respectively), following a decline with increasing concentrations. Our results may reflect hormesis, an adaptive response of organisms to moderate levels of stress52. Indeed, hormetic responses in marine organisms have been demonstrated to a wide range of PAHs and other types of hydrocarbons53,54, including benzo[a]pyrene (BaP)55–58, naphthalene56,59, dimethylnaphthalene59, phenanthrene60,61, as well as PAHs mixtures62,63. It has been suggested that exposure of an organism to a mild or moderate environmental stressor, including contaminants, improves its ability to cope with stress and enhances resilience to subsequent exposures64. This mechanism could also explain the mussel’s high resilience to both warming and gas condensate exposure.

The extent to which PAHs and MAHs bioconcentrate in marine organisms depends on their uptake, depuration metabolism, and the biochemical properties of the different pollutants as such65–67. Mussels tend to concentrate high levels of lipophilic contaminations, such as PAHs due to their inability to metabolize and excrete them68,69. As a result, mussels are used worldwide to monitor marine pollution, not only because they exhibit cellular and physiological responses, but also because they indicate the level of bioavailable contaminants in the environment70. Hydrophobic pollutants’ bioavailability depends on their solubility in water, which decreases with increasing molecular weight. While many hydrophobic PAHs are practically insoluble in the water phase, for instance PAHs with > 4 rings (e.g., benzo[a]pyrene, benzo[b]fluoranthene). Naphthalene, which is the smallest PAH (2 rings) shows a minimal solubility level of 31.6 mg/L at 25°C71. The even smaller hydrophobic molecular family MAH (1 benzene ring) includes members such as benzene and toluene, having higher solubility levels, reaching 1.8 and 0.5 gr/L under the same conditions. Several reports focused on PAHs accumulation in filter feeders described the shift in adsorption levels toward low molecular and less hydrophobic pollutants in low turbidity water > 1 mg/L (e.g., the air-water interface), and vice versa in environments with high particulate matter or turbidity < 1 mg/L or in sediments65,70,72. Since the mussel specimens analyzed in this experiment were sampled from shallow rocky regions, the results presented here are consistent with and further extend the previously discussed observations concerning PAHs containing more than three rings.

Most of the PAHs and MAHs examined were detected at ppb levels. Nevertheless, under combined stressor conditions—specifically the interaction between temperature and M/PAH concentrations—an enhanced accumulation in mussel tissues was observed. Notably, elevated tissue concentrations (ppm range) were measured for the most water-soluble compounds within these groups (i.e., benzene and toluene). These single-ring aromatics are also the most abundant aromatic constituents of the gas condensate mixture. In addition to their strong affinity for mussel tissues, benzene and toluene are highly volatile, consequently they were largely absent from the aqueous phase when analyzed at the end of the exposure period. In contrast, the high-molecular-weight PAH benzo[a]pyrene also exhibited increased tissue concentrations when mussels were exposed to the highest gas condensate level (100 ppb) also. Its similarly low or undetectable concentrations in the water phase at the end of the experiment are expected, as this compound—although present at relatively low levels in the condensate mixture—is extremely hydrophobic, characterized by very low aqueous solubility and strong bioaccumulation potential.

Every petrochemical mixture is unique, differing not only among product types (e.g., gas condensates, crude oils), but also among mixtures within the same product category. Accordingly, we adopt an individual-compound approach, assessing the effects of each molecule separately in parallel with a bulk-composition approach (i.e., ΣPAHs and ΣMAHs). Future experimental designs that build on these complementary approaches may, if successful, lead to the development of a compound-based predictive framework capable of forecasting the biological impacts of petrochemical spills on selected organisms.

As presented here, the low molecular weight MAHs toluene and benzene were found in the highest concentrations in mussel tissues, in both thermal conditions. MAHs and low molecular weight 2-ring PAHs are considered acutely toxic73. MAHs have been shown to induce nonspecific narcosis and change in the permeability of the cell membranes, especially of the gills74,75, while naphthalene and its derivatives can cause tainting of fish and bivalves76. The heavier 4- to 6-ring PAHs are considered mutagenic and carcinogenic (i.e., benz[a]anthracene, benzo[a]pyrene, dibenz[a, h]anthracene)73. Poulsen et al. found a negative correlation between M. edulis health (condition index) and high molecular weight PAH levels, although the low molecular weight PAHs bioconcentration level was higher39. Hellou & Law found a positive correlation between immunological stress response and bioconcentrations of PAHs in M. edulis and M. trossulus exposed to elevated temperatures and oil pollution77. Similarly, Kamel et al. found higher bioconcentration levels of benzo[a]Pyrene in higher temperatures in M. galloprovincialis78. Specifically, the levels of this compound are monitored worldwide due to its high toxicity and persistence. Considering B. pharaonis as a representative filter feeder, and based on the feeding experiment presented here, we conclude that following gas condensate addition to a final concentration of 100 ppb under the described ambient temperature conditions, the levels of benzo[a]pyrene detected exceeded both Israeli and European regulatory limits for edible tissues. It is assumed that organisms at higher trophic levels that feed on this mussel may accumulate even higher levels of contaminants.

In the ambient treatment, the bioconcentration of most PAHs increased with increasing gas condensate levels and was negatively correlated with the clearance rates. In contrast, in the warming treatment, we found a positive correlation between the heavier, less soluble PAHs, and clearance rates. These results are in agreement with Jeong & Cho, who suggested that reduction in clearance rate is a behavioral response to increased stress in order to minimize ingestion of PAHs51.

The Mediterranean Sea is particularly susceptible to hydrocarbon pollution, due to its semi-enclosed nature, relatively small size and rich biodiversity79. Between 1977 and 2023, more than 125 oil spills have been reported (MEDGIS-MAR, https://medgismar.rempec.org/), including a recent medium-size oil spill and tar beaching in the Eastern Mediterranean80. Moreover, a recent model-based risk analysis report predicts that the probability of medium or even large-scale oil spills in the EMS is high both on spatial and temporal scales81.

In the short term, the tolerance of B. pharaonis to gas condensate pollution is a pathway for bioaccumulation and biomagnification of PAHs along the food web. Mussels are prey for marine organisms at different trophic levels, including crabs, gastropods, starfish, fish, and birds. Heat stress compromises mussels’ valve sealing, which may increase their vulnerability to predators82. In the EMS, the predatory marine gastropods Hexaplex trunculus and Stramonita haemastoma feed on Mediterranean mytilids, including B. pharaonis83. Sara et al. showed that a decrease in B. pharaonis density significantly changed the abundance and species richness of the associated macrobenthos in a large sea-water lagoon in Sicily84.

We showed that warming enhanced the sublethal effects of gas condensate exposure on physiological performance in B. pharaonis. Similarly, Shmutz et al. have demonstrated that exposure of M. edulis to crude oil did not significantly affect survival, but led to decreased clearance rates, impaired gametogenesis and impaired larval metamorphosis85. In the long term, impaired gametogenesis will result in a reduction in mussel numbers. Given their role as nutrient cyclers via filter-feeding86, this may affect water transparency, as well as particulate and sedimentary organic matter, with clear repercussions on primary production, sedimentation, decomposition rates, nutrient levels and flux87. Moreover, as ecosystem engineers that provide refuge and habitat structure to other species, it may have consequences on marine biodiversity87,88. Based on these observations, we advocate for increased attention in environmental research toward gas condensates and their enriched volatile M/PAH fractions, focusing on their distribution, bioaccumulation, and toxicological impacts in marine ecosystems.

Exposure of Brachidontes pharaonis, a sentinel species, to warming and gas condensate, caused significant and synergetic physiological and immunological responses, with potential impact on community structure. While several methodological considerations should be acknowledged, including the use of single-container temperature–pollutant combinations (which nonetheless allow trend detection in a regression framework) and a single mussel population, these should be considered alongside the strong and consistent physiological and immunological patterns found in our study. Overall, our findings indicate that warming can amplify the sublethal effects of gas condensate exposure in B. pharaonis, with consequences that may extend beyond individual performance to reproductive output and the transfer of aromatic hydrocarbons through coastal food webs.

Methods

Mussel collection and experimental setup

One hundred individuals of the mussel Brachidontes pharaonis were manually collected from the intertidal population of Neve-Yam beach. The annual mean of seawater temperature at the collection site is 22.6 ± 5.1°C89. The mussels were collected on 6 Mar 2024, while ambient seawater temperature was 21.4 °C. Length (the longest antero-posterior distance) and wet weight were measured individually. Average weight and length were 2.00 g (± 0.38) and 2.40 cm (± 0.18), respectively. The mussels were acclimated for one week to laboratory conditions (ambient temperature 21.4 °C) prior to the start of the experiment. The temperature in the warmed treatment group was increased by 1.0 °C per day until reaching 24.5 °C, 3.1 °C above ambient temperature (following the intermediate future warming scenario, Representative Concentration Pathway 4.590). Ten sealed 5 L glass containers were filled with 2 L Mediterranean seawater, pumped from 1 m depth, and placed in two experiment tanks. Circulation pumps were used to homogenize the temperature in each tank (Fig. 5A). Seawater with pollutants at different concentrations were manually changed twice a week to maintain stable water quality and pollutant exposure levels over time. The experiment lasted 77 days, from 14 March 2024 to 23 May 2024. During both the acclimation period and the experiment, mussels were maintained in non-filtered seawater pumped from a depth of 1 m in the southeastern Mediterranean Sea and were fed once a week with Tetraselmis tetrathele. Temperatures were continuously monitored and logged hourly during the whole experimental period using temperature loggers (EnvLogger, Electric Blue, Portugal). The temperature logs are available at 10.5281/zenodo.15342372.

The multi-stressor experimental setup. (A) Two thermobaths of ambient (21.4 °C) and warmed temperatures (24.5 °C), containing five glass containers each, with seawater, gas condensate (0, 0.5, 5, 30, 100 ppb) and 10 Brachidontes pharaonis mussels. (B) Respiration rate incubations (n = 3, 30 mussels in total). (C) Clearance rate assay (n = 5, 50 mussels in total).

Gas condensate, obtained from a local gas refinery, was added in increasing concentrations of 0 (control), 0.5, 5, 30 and 100 ppb to each container. 10 mussels were placed in each container. Aeration in each container was achieved by air pump, maintained throughout the experiment to eliminate oxygen stress and prevent the formation of a surface slick. At the end of the 77-day experiment, 60 mussels were analyzed for MAHs and PAHs whole-body levels. Water samples from the lowest 10 cm of each container were sampled using a hand pump for MAHs and PAHs analysis.

Measurements of physiological parameters

Mortality was monitored daily by visual inspection of the mussels. Individuals with slightly or fully open valves could be inspected and classified as alive or dead. Respiration rate was measured in individual mussel incubations (n = 5 from each treatment, 50 mussels in total, Fig. 5B) in 285-ml glass jars in the beginning (day 1), middle (day 39) and the end of the experiment (day 77).

Dissolved oxygen was measured in each chamber at the beginning and end of the incubation using a handheld sensor (Multiline 3420, WTW, Wellheim, Germany) following Jiao et al.91 and Belivermiş et al.92. Control jars with no mussels were used to account for microbial respiration. Due to limited biomass and the need to preserve samples for M/PAHs analysis, mussels could not be dried, and wet weight was used instead for standardization, following Pieper et al.93.

Clearance rate was measured by a one and a half hours incubation of each mussel (n = 3 from each treatment, 30 mussels in total, Fig. 5C) with the microalgae Tetraselmis tetrathele. Each mussel was placed in a separate chamber, containing 50 ml filtered seawater and initial concentration of 4.6 × 107 algal cells L− 1. Three chambers without mussels were used as control. The chambers were positioned on a multi-stirrer at 120 RPM, to keep the water mixed and oxygenated. At the end of the incubation, algal cells were counted under a microscope (BX43, Olympus, Japan) using a hemocytometer (Hydro-Bios, Germany) following Falfushynska et al.94 and Hubert et al.95. Analysis of MAHs and PAHs in the mussels

At the end of the experiment, a total of 60 mussels were allocated for the bioconcentration assays. Mussel tissues were carefully dissected from their shells, weighed, and stored at -20 °C until further analysis. To meet the minimum tissue mass required for reliable extraction and quantification (≥ 0.2 g wet weight), tissues from three mussels were pooled per sample, resulting in two pooled replicates per treatment. The samples were extracted at 100 °C / 1500 psi with Hexane: Acetone (1:1) using a Speed Extractor E-916 (Büchi). Prior to extraction, samples were fortified with deuterated PAH surrogates (RESTEK). Following the extraction, the solvent was concentrated to 1 ml using the SyncorePlus Parallel Evaporator system (Büchi). Samples were then cleaned using a silica column containing 1 g MgSO₄ and 10 g silica, extraction was performed using 100 ml of DCM and reduced to 1 ml again. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC/MS, Agilent 6890/5973) was used to identify and quantify the concentrations of each MAH and PAH. The average of M/PAHs levels from each treatment (two pooled replicates) is presented.

Analysis of MAHs and PAHs in the seawater

Water samples were stored in dark glass bottles at -20 °C until extraction. Prior to the extraction, the samples were thawed at room temperature. The water was extracted using a solid-phase extraction (SPE) method similar to that described in our previous report12. Briefly, water samples were passed through a Strata-X33µm cartridge (Phenomenex), connected to a vacuum pump. The column was reloaded by repeating the extraction process three times. MAHs and PAHs were extracted using 50 ml of acetone. The solvent was reduced to 1 ml using a rotary evaporator (Büchi) and dried using a Pasteur pipette containing glass wool and Na2SO4. Finally, samples were analyzed using an Agilent GC/MS (6890/5973) against external selected MAHs standard (BTEX, RESTEK) and 16 PAHs standard (RESTEK).

Statistical analyses

Two-way ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc were used to test the effect of gas condensate concentrations and temperature and their combination on respiration rates and clearance rates using with R v. 4.2.2 using the package “stats”97. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test within each treatment group. In respiration rate data, all groups showed normality (p > 0.05). In clearance rate data, most groups did not deviate significantly from normality (p > 0.05), although one group (30 ppm, ambient temperature) showed a deviation, likely influenced by the small sample size (n = 3). Homogeneity of variances was confirmed for all datasets using Levene’s test (p > 0.3). Given the regression-based experimental design and the robustness of ANOVA to minor violations of normality, parametric analyses were considered appropriate.

The main and interactive effects of warming and gas condensate on the respiration and clearance rates of the mussels were evaluated using generalized additive models (GAMs). The modeling and post hoc comparison of trends were done using the mgcv package in R98. See a more extended version in the Supplementary Material. To test the correlations between the concentrations of individual MAHs and PAHs in the mussels and their respiration (Rl, Rw) and clearance (CRl, CRw) rates Pearson correlations were calculated and visualized in a correlogram using the package “corrplot” in R.
