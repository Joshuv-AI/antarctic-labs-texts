Zoning of agroclimatic and productive areas for quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) in Peru; an integration of F-AHP, TOPSIS, and GIS
Authors: Katerin M. Tuesta-Trauco, Angel J. Medina-Medina, Jhon A. Zabaleta-Santisteban, Abner S. Rivera-Fernandez, José A. Sánchez-Vega, Teodoro B. Silva-Melendez, Marlen A. Grandez-Alberca, Cecibel Portocarrero, Rolando Salas López, Lily Juarez-Contreras, Samuel Pizarro, Manuel Oliva Cruz, Elgar Barboza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) is an Andean crop of high nutritional value that is resistant to adverse climatic conditions. However, its sustainable expansion in Peru faces limitations due to the lack of detailed spatial information on areas suitable for cultivation, especially under climate change scenarios. The objective of this study was to define the agroclimatic and productive zones suitable for quinoa cultivation in Peru, taking into account current conditions (1970–2000) and projected climate change scenarios (2041–2060 and 2081–2100). Multicriteria evaluation techniques were integrated using the Fuzzy analytic hierarchy process (F-AHP) method to weight the criteria and the technique of Order of Preference by Similarity to the ideal Solution (TOPSIS) to classify the alternatives in a Geographic Information System (GIS) environment. Future projections were assessed under Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5). The results indicated that 45.71% of the national territory is marginally suitable and 10.95% is moderately suitable. Under future scenarios (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5), a slight reduction in marginal areas and an increase in moderately suitable and highly suitable areas are projected, especially towards the end of the period 2081–2100. For the SSP5-8.5 scenario, highly suitable areas could increase to 1.09% of the territory and moderately suitable areas to 14.08%. These results show a spatial redistribution of suitability, highlighting the need for adaptive agricultural policies, climate resilient planning strategies, and targeted territorial management to support sustainable quinoa expansion in Peru.

Introduction

Over the last few decades, interest in quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) has grown significantly internationally, driven by its nutritional properties, balanced essential amino acid profile, and high mineral content, including iron, calcium, and phosphorus1–3. These characteristics position it as a key functional food for combating malnutrition and promoting healthy diets in vulnerable contexts4,5. Quinoa, belonging to the Amaranthaceae family, is widely recognized for its remarkable adaptability to extreme environmental conditions1,6,7. This characteristic, together with its high nutritional value and productive potential3,8, makes it a strategic crop both regionally and globally9. In South America, its distribution covers an extensive latitudinal gradient of approximately 4,828 km from Colombia to southern Chile, reflecting its ability to adapt to different ecological environments2,10. Recent studies in Africa, specifically in Malawi, have also demonstrated its potential to grow in both warm and cold areas3,11.

In countries such as Peru, characterized by a marked climatic diversity and soil heterogeneity, the identification of optimal areas for quinoa cultivation is essential not only to strengthen food security, but also to promote sustainable agricultural development and improve the income of local producers12,13. It also stands out for its resilience to adverse agroclimatic conditions, such as nutrient poor soils, high salinity14 and limited water availability. These qualities allow it to thrive in marginal ecosystems affected by drought or soil stress11. It also tolerates wide ranges of soil pH and shows optimal growth at various altitudes and rainfall regimes, from 0 to 4,000 m.a.s.l15,16.

Agricultural planning requires tools that integrate climatic, soil, topographic and socioeconomic data17 with spatial analysis and decision-making methods. The application of multi-criteria methodologies, such as the Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (F-AHP) and the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS), allows areas to be prioritized according to their suitability for quinoa cultivation under different climate change scenarios. F-AHP is an extension of the traditional Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) method, designed to address the uncertainty inherent in human judgment when assigning weights to multiple criteria18,19. Unlike traditional AHP, which is based on precise comparisons between pairs of criteria, F-AHP introduces fuzzy logic to represent the ambiguity and vagueness inherent in subjective assessments20. This approach uses fuzzy triangular numbers to quantify experts’ preferences, allowing for greater flexibility and realism in the decision process. In agricultural planning studies, F-AHP has proven effective in ranking agroclimatic, edaphological, topographical, and socioeconomic variables21, providing a solid basis for multi-criteria assessment of land suitability.

Sustainable agricultural planning in the context of climate change requires the precise identification of the most suitable areas for the cultivation of strategic species. Quinoa has demonstrated a high tolerance to adverse soil and climatic conditions and its nutritional value, positioning it as a viable alternative to the challenges of food security in Andean regions. However, the expansion of the crop faces limitations due to the lack of integrated tools22 to spatially denineate potential production areas, especially in terms of future climate scenarios23,24.

Several research studies have applied multi-criteria decision methods for crop suitability assessment24–27, most notably the combined use of F-AHP and TOPSIS. These methods have been successfully employed in the agricultural zoning of crops such as coffee, maize and sugar cane in countries such as Iran, India, Colombia and Ethiopia, demonstrating their ability to integrate multiple variables in geospatial environments21,28–30. In particular, F-AHP allows incorporating the uncertainty inherent in expert judgements through fuzzy logic, overcoming the limitations of classical AHP, while TOPSIS facilitates the ranking of alternatives according to their closeness to a positive ideal solution and their distance from a negative one31,32. The combination of F-AHP and TOPSIS allows for a more robust and objective assessment28. Its implementation in Geographic Information Systems (GIS) environments has proven to be effective for the generation of thematic maps oriented to decision making in agriculture, territorial planning and sustainable land management28,33.

Climate change represents a growing threat to agriculture34, due to alterations in the patterns of temperature, precipitation and frequency of extreme events23,30. These variations can displace areas traditionally suitable for certain crops, affecting their productivity4. In the case of quinoa, its adaptive capacity positions it as a resilient crop, but it is crucial to anticipate the effects of climate change on its potential distribution2,10. Identifying new suitable areas under future scenarios will contribute to climate resilience and adaptation-oriented agricultural planning35. In view of the projected effects of climate change, which could significantly modify the distribution of agricultural areas, it is necessary to identify areas with potential for quinoa cultivation under future conditions36.

The central problem addressed by this study lies in the absence of agroclimatic zoning at the national scale that integrates climatic, soil, topographic and socio-economic criteria under current and future conditions, using advanced multi-criteria methodologies. This gap limits the formulation of adaptive agricultural policies and the timely identification of regions with high productive potential for quinoa.

This study is carried out at the national level in Peru, a country noted for its high ecological heterogeneity, altitudinal diversity and socio-economic complexity. Carrying out the assessment at this scale makes it possible to capture more accurately the opportunities and constraints for quinoa cultivation in different regions, from the coast to the highlands and the high jungle. This perspective is fundamental for guiding public investment, defining priority areas for agricultural expansion and promoting differentiated territorial planning in the face of climate variability.

Therefore, the objective was to identify potential agroclimatic and productive areas for quinoa cultivation in Peru under climate change scenarios. To this end, a multi-criteria modelling approach was employed using the F-AHP and TOPSIS, implemented through raster-based spatial analysis within a Geographic Information System (GIS) platform (ArcGIS Pro 3.1.5).The integration of climatic, edaphological, topographical and socio-economic variables in combination with climate change projections made it possible to determine the area’s most suitable for sustainable quinoa production, providing key information for agricultural planning and decision-making in a context of climate variability.

Materials and methods

Study area

Peru is located in the intertropical zone of the South American continent, between 0° and 18° south latitude and between 68° and 81° west longitude, this location extends from sea level up to 6768 m.a.s.l. (Fig. 1) offering remarkable physiographic and climatic diversity31,32. Peru has an area of approximately 1,285,215 km2, making it the third largest country in South America and one of the largest in the world31,37. The irregularity of the terrain, together with the variability of climatic factors, favors the development of crops adapted to different altitudinal zones39,40. In this context, quinoa is a strategic crop due to its tolerance to adverse soil and climate conditions, and it is traditionally grown in high Andean areas2. Departments such as Apurímac, Arequipa, Ayacucho, Cusco, Junín, La Libertad, and Puno stand out for having the largest productive areas, generally located above 2,500 m.a.s.l, where cold and temperate climates prevail with moderate to low rainfall31,38.

In addition to its agroclimatic resilience, quinoa is highly nutritious, being a rich source of high biological value proteins, essential amino acids, fiber, and minerals. For this reason, its incorporation into the national diet has been promoted as part of strategies to combat malnutrition and improve food security, especially in rural Andean communities. It is also an important source of income for thousands of rural families, due to growing demand in international markets. In fact, Peru has positioned itself as one of the world’s leading exporters of quinoa, which contributes significantly to the regional economies of the high Andean areas.

Methodological process

Figure 2 describes the methodological approach used to identify agroclimatic areas suitable for current and future quinoa cultivation in Peru, considering different climate change scenarios. The methodological process began with the identification and selection of climatic, edaphic, topographic and socio-economic criteria, followed by the geospatial processing phase in the ArcGIS Pro 3.1.5 environment41, which included obtaining a base map, as well as sampling, reprojection and cutting of the thematic layers according to the defined sub criteria. For the assignment of weights, the F-AHP method was used, through a panel of experts. This procedure consisted in the construction of fuzzy pairwise comparison matrices, whose consistency was checked by means of the consistency ratio (RC < 10%). After validation, the fuzzy weights were calculated and then defuzzified, normalized and finally adjusted. With the weights obtained, the TOPSIS method was applied for the classification and selection of optimal areas. This stage included the construction and normalization of the decision matrix, the identification of the ideal and anti-ideal solutions, and the calculation of the relative proximity coefficient for each unit of analysis. In the next phase, GIS spatial analysis was carried out, which consisted of the reclassification of thematic layers into suitability levels, the generation of suitability maps by weighted overlay, and the incorporation of physical and legal constraints into the model. The validation and evaluation of the model ensured the reliability and internal consistency of the results through expert-based consistency analysis and spatial coherence assessment. Because the available occurrence data correspond to presence-only records of quinoa cultivation and do not include confirmed absence data, standard quantitative accuracy metrics such as confusion matrices, overall accuracy, or Kappa statistics were not methodologically applicable. Instead, model evaluation was performed by verifying the consistency of F-AHP weights (CR < 0.10) and by assessing the spatial agreement between high-suitability areas and known quinoa-producing locations. Finally, the future climate component was incorporated by downloading data from the HadGEM3-GC31-LL global climate model (GCM), developed by the UK Met Office Hadley Centre and participating in the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6), under the SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 emission scenarios of the IPCC’s Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP), corresponding to the periods 2041–2060 and 2081–2100. These projections were integrated into the climate database for the study area to quantify spatial and temporal changes, enabling the estimation of future climate suitability for quinoa cultivation.Fig. 2Methodological flow used in the identification of potential areas for quinoa cultivation.

Identification and selection of criteria and sub-criteria

Different criteria and sub criteria were identified based on the availability of national and international spatial data, measurability, and comparability. For both methods, specific thresholds were needed that met the appropriate conditions for quinoa production. The selection of these criteria was based on technical manuals6,15,16,42–44 and scientific articles reviewing the methodologies used to identify the most promising areas24,45–48. Four criteria were selected: climatic, edaphic, topographic, and socioeconomic, and 14 sub criteria, such as: average annual temperature, average annual precipitation, relative humidity, soil texture, soil organic matter, soil pH, cation exchange capacity, altitude, slope, aspect, LULC, distance to urban centers, distance to roads, and distance to rivers (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Hierarchical structure of criteria-sub-criteria selected in the identification of potential agro-climatic and productive areas for quinoa cultivation in Peru.

Source and processing of data sets

Geospatial data on mean annual temperature and mean annual precipitation were obtained from WorldClim version 2.1 (https://www.worldclim.org/), which provides monthly climatological averages for the period 1970–2000 at a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds (~ 1 km)49. Relative humidity data were retrieved using the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform50 from available climate data collections. Edaphic variables, including soil texture, pH, cation exchange capacity (CEC), salinity, and soil organic carbon (SOC), were obtained from https://soilgrids.org/ with a resolution of 250 m51.

Topographic layers including elevation, slope, and aspect were derived in ArcGIS Pro from a Digital Elevation Model (DEM) with an original spatial resolution of 30 m, subsequently resampled to 250 m. The DEM was obtained from the Alaska Satellite Facility Distributed Active Archive Center (DAAC), based on Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) data (www.srtm.csi.cgiar.org/; accessed on March 3, 2025). For the evaluation of accessibility and land-use-related criteria, Euclidean distance raster layers were generated from road network and urban center vector layers using the Euclidean Distance tool in ArcGIS Pro41. Vector layers representing roads, urban areas, population centers, and water bodies were first converted into raster format. Subsequently, for each criterion, the Euclidean Distance tool was applied to calculate the straight-line distance from each raster cell to the nearest feature (e.g., roads, urban polygons, or water bodies), producing continuous distance-to-feature surfaces. These distance rasters were then used as proxies for accessibility and land-use influence in the multi-criteria evaluation.

Land use and land cover (LULC) data were obtained from national databases52, while urban polygons were extracted from the Land Use Classification (CUS) map, and population centers were represented as point features provided by the Ministry of Education52. Road network data and hydrographic features were derived from national cartographic datasets produced by the National Geographic Institute and the Ministry of Transport and Communications52. Spatial data on protected natural areas and their official buffer zones (updated to 2022) were obtained from the National Service of Natural Areas Protected by the State53. All raster layers were standardized to a spatial resolution of 250 m and harmonized under the WGS 84 geographic coordinate system (EPSG:4326) to ensure spatial consistency across the national territory of Peru. A resampling procedure was applied to align cell size and grid structure across all criteria layers, enabling their integration into a unified spatial framework for subsequent multi-criteria analysis45.

The soil suitability thresholds for organic matter content were selected based on documented agronomic responses of quinoa to soil fertility conditions. Although quinoa is tolerant to marginal soils, low levels of organic matter are associated with limited nutrient availability, poor soil structure, and reduced water-holding capacity, all of which can substantially constrain yield potential without soil amendments61. Studies have shown that increases in soil organic matter through compost or manure application significantly enhance quinoa grain yield and soil health, indicating that very low organic matter (< 1%) may be functionally restrictive for sustained production compared to soils with higher organic matter (e.g., ~ 1–2% or more)62. Modelling approach with the fuzzy analytical hierarchical fuzzy process - F - AHP

Construction of F-AHP sets

Triangular fuzzy numbers were used due to their simplicity in calculation and their effectiveness in representing and handling fuzzy logic. In the initial stage, a group of twelve experts was formed to be in charge of the decision-making process. The expert panel was composed of twelve specialists with academic and professional backgrounds in agronomy (with emphasis on quinoa and Andean crops), agroclimatology, soil science, geographic information systems, and agricultural land-use planning. All experts had more than ten years of experience in research, technical consultancy, or applied projects related to agroclimatic zoning, land suitability assessment, climate change impacts on agriculture, or territorial planning.

The selection criteria included demonstrated expertise through peer-reviewed scientific publications, participation in national or regional agricultural or environmental projects, and familiarity with the Peruvian agroecological context. Experts were selected to ensure a multidisciplinary perspective and to reduce individual bias in the weighting process. Their task was to make pairwise comparisons and establish the relative importance of the criteria, using the linguistic scale proposed by Gumus64.

The expert panel included researchers affiliated with public universities, specialists from governmental agricultural and environmental institutions, and technical professionals involved in regional land-use planning and climate change adaptation programs in Peru. Their experience covered major quinoa-producing departments such as Puno, Cusco, Ayacucho, Junín, and Arequipa, ensuring representation of diverse Andean agroecological conditions.

Although small-scale producers or farmer cooperative representatives were not directly involved in the pairwise comparison process due to the methodological requirements of the F-AHP framework the selection of criteria and suitability thresholds was based on national technical guidelines and agronomic studies that incorporate field-based knowledge from quinoa-producing communities.

The consistency of the decision-making process was evaluated using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). Assessing climate trends under shared socio-economic trajectories

The assessment was carried out taking into account the most suitable areas for quinoa. For the analysis of climate trends for quinoa cultivation in Peru, data from the HadGEM3-GC31-LL global Earth model (part of Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6—CMIP6), available through the World Clim portal, was used. This analysis was carried out at the national level and in the areas with the highest potential for production, under different climate change scenarios. Climate trends were assessed using the set of scenarios of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project 6 (CMIP6), a global effort of the World Climate Research Programme (WCRP).

Two of these scenarios were used in this study, selected for their relevance to greenhouse gas emissions and potential impact on climate change; SSP2-4.5, an intermediate scenario in which mitigation policies are moderate, with moderate population growth and controlled emissions compared to other more extreme scenarios, and SSP5-8.5, a high emissions scenario, associated with strong economic growth based on overexploitation of fossil fuels, and with a significant increase in environmental problems. These two scenarios were analysed for two projected periods, the first being period 1: 2041—2060 and the second period 2: 2081—2100. For each period, variations in mean annual temperature (°C) and mean annual precipitation (mm) were analysed using data from the HadGEM3-GC31-LL model, one of the most sensitive CMIP6 models.

Historical data for the period 1970–2000, obtained from WorldClim, was the basis for comparing projected changes in future periods. From these projections, changes in temperature and precipitation in the different quinoa-producing regions of Peru were calculated in order to identify the areas that could experience more favorable or unfavorable conditions for the crop.

Future climate projections were obtained from the HadGEM3-GC31-LL global climate model (CMIP6) through the WorldClim platform. This model was selected due to its high sensitivity to changes in temperature and precipitation and its frequent application in climate impact assessments in complex topographic regions. In this study, HadGEM3-GC31-LL was used as a representative model to evaluate climate change impacts under two contrasting emission scenarios (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5). The projected climate variables were reprojected and resampled to a spatial resolution of 250 m to ensure consistency with the other GIS layers. Although a multi-model ensemble was not employed, this approach allows for a consistent assessment of future agroclimatic suitability, while acknowledging associated uncertainties.

Hierarchization and classification of departments

Finally, the departments were ranked according to their Ci values, identifying the territories most suitable (values close to 1) and least suitable (values close to 0) for quinoa cultivation. For the cartographic representation of the results, the Ci values were linked to a vector layer of departments in the GIS environment and classified into categories of suitability using natural intervals.

Although no formal quantitative validation analysis was performed, the spatial distribution of areas classified as moderately and very suitable shows strong agreement with Peru’s main quinoa-producing regions located along the Andean belt, particularly in departments such as Puno, Cusco, Junín, and Ayacucho. This correspondence reflects the concentration of extensive suitable areas within these traditional production zones, rather than the highest departmental Ci values derived from the TOPSIS ranking. Such spatial consistency provides indirect support for the plausibility of the model outputs and reinforces their usefulness for guiding regional agricultural policies and strategic planning.

Figure 8c shows the projection of potential habitat for quinoa cultivation under future climate scenarios, which reveals an increase in the “moderately” and “very suitable” suitability categories compared to current conditions (1970–2000). According to the results obtained, the “moderately suitable” areas show a progressive increase in area in all scenarios analysed. Under the SSP2-4.5 scenario, the area with moderate suitability increases by 2.79% during the period 2040–2060, reaching an increase of 3.22% by the end of the century (2081–2100). Similarly, the SSP5-8.5 scenario projects an increase of 2.82% in the intermediate period and 3.13% by 2100.

The “very suitable” category, which currently has no areas in the baseline scenario, undergoes a gradual expansion under both scenarios. In the case of SSP2-4.5, an increase of 0.52% is observed for the period 2040–2060, rising to 0.80% for the period 2081–2100. In the highest severity scenario (SSP5-8.5), the very suitable area reaches 0.71% by mid-century and increases to 1.63% by the end of the century (2081–2100). Similarly, there is a reduction in areas classified as “marginally suitable”, with a decrease of between −5.49% and −7.81%, suggesting a progressive transition towards more favorable conditions for cultivation. Although the area classified as “not suitable” shows a slight upward trend in most scenarios, this increase does not compensate for the growth observed in the higher suitability classes.

Taken together, these results indicate that, under the different climate change scenarios (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5), an expansion of potentially more favorable habitat for quinoa is expected in the study territory, especially towards the end of this century. This pattern suggests that quinoa cultivation could benefit from the projected new climatic conditions, particularly in regions currently classified as marginal or moderately suitable.

Discussion

This research evaluated the suitability of Peruvian territory for quinoa cultivation using geospatial information technologies and a multi-criteria evaluation approach based on F-AHP and TOPSIS. The identification of suitable areas is essential, given that this crop requires specific conditions in terms of temperature, precipitation, pH, soil texture, and organic matter, as well as adequate topographical and access conditions. The results showed that agroclimatic factors had the greatest influence on territorial suitability, while edaphic and socioeconomic criteria acted as constraints on the potential cultivation area.

In this context, the results obtained using the F-AHP model made it possible to identify the spatial distribution of land suitability for quinoa cultivation, integrating agroclimatic, edaphic, topographic, and socioeconomic criteria. The variables with the greatest positive influence were average annual temperature and annual precipitation, with more than 70% of the area classified as “moderately” or “very suitable.” However, other variables such as relative humidity (84.9% “not suitable”) and soil organic matter (88.8% “not suitable”) significantly limited the productive potential in large areas of the country. This result does not contradict the rustic nature of quinoa, but rather reflects the distinction between physiological tolerance and productive suitability at national scale, where extremely low organic matter levels are associated with low and unstable yields. The results of the classification by criteria showed that soil restrictions are critical for crop adaptation, coinciding with the findings of65, who determined that soil pH, texture, and organic matter content are decisive for the successful adaptation of quinoa in regions such as Extremadura, Spain.

The application of the TOPSIS method allowed the departments to be ranked according to their similarity to the ideal solution. The department of Puno was ranked as the most suitable for quinoa cultivation, in line with the findings of Alandia et al3. who point out that the Andean region of southern Peru has historically presented favorable agroclimatic conditions for cultivation. In contrast, departments such as Loreto and Ucayali showed significantly lower levels of suitability compared to other departments, due to unfavorable tropical conditions, high relative humidity, and acidic soils, which has been previously documented by Bedoya-Perales et al66. when analyzing the limitations on the territorial expansion of quinoa into the Peruvian Amazon.

In terms of surface area, the results showed that the departments of Puno, Cusco, and Ayacucho not only have the highest levels of suitability, but also concentrate the largest areas classified as “moderately” and “very suitable,” reinforcing their role as strategic areas for the sustainable expansion of cultivation. Together, these departments represent more than 50% of the total area considered suitable at the national level. This spatial concentration can be attributed to their favorable altitude, thermal stability, and agricultural tradition linked to the management of Andean crops. In contrast, departments such as Loreto, Ucayali, and Tumbes have the smallest areas suitable for cultivation, with a predominance of areas classified as “unsuitable” due to factors such as low altitude, high relative humidity, and unfavorable soils, which significantly limit their productive potential. These findings coincide with those reported by El-Madany et al67., who highlight that the high Andean regions of southern Peru have the most stable agroecological conditions for quinoa, while the Amazon region has severe soil and climate restrictions that hinder its adaptation.

From a practical perspective, the departmental suitability ranking derived from the TOPSIS method provides a decision-support framework for agricultural zoning and territorial planning. Departments with higher relative similarity index (Ci) values, such as Puno, Cusco, and Ayacucho, may be prioritized for strategies focused on productivity improvement, technological innovation, and climate-resilient agricultural development, rather than for crop introduction alone. Conversely, departments with lower suitability scores may require targeted interventions, including soil management improvements, irrigation infrastructure, or adaptive agronomic practices, or may be better suited for alternative crops. Therefore, the suitability maps and rankings generated in this study can support public investment decisions, regional agricultural planning, and the formulation of policies aimed at strengthening food security and sustainable land use.

The projected climate change scenarios (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5) show a trend toward a reduction in areas classified as “marginally suitable” and “unsuitable,” with a slight increase in ‘moderately’ and “highly suitable” areas in medium- and long-term projections. For example, in the SSP5-8.5 scenario for 2081–2100, the “very suitable” area increases to 1.63%, while the “unsuitable” areas decrease slightly. This behavior could be related to a change in the altitude of suitable areas, as explained by Lozano et al56. describe the altitudinal resilience of quinoa in the Andes. These results coincide with those reported by58,68,69 who showed that quinoa adapts well to temperature variations, although it may be sensitive to extreme changes in radiation and photoperiod.

Identifying areas with productive potential under future climate scenarios is a key tool for decision-making in agricultural planning. As Tarqui et al24., point out, multi-criteria models make it possible to anticipate spatial changes in land suitability and guide agricultural adaptation policies based on geospatial evidence. Likewise, they highlight the importance of strengthening irrigation infrastructure and local technical capacities, especially in regions with potential suitability but socioeconomic limitations, as discussed in relation to the adoption of irrigation in highland communities. This absence of areas can be attributed to various factors which, when considered in models for identifying potential areas, limit the possibility of achieving this category. As a result, most suitable areas are classified as moderately suitable and marginally suitable, indicating an average level of compatibility with optimal conditions for crop development.

Among the main limitations are the spatial resolution of edaphic and socioeconomic variables, as well as the subjective aspect involved in assigning weights in the F-AHP model, despite its methodological consistency. Additionally, future climate suitability was assessed using a single Global Climate Model (HadGEM3-GC31-LL), which does not capture the full range of structural uncertainty associated with climate projections. Although this model was selected due to its high sensitivity and frequent application in climate impact studies in the Andean region, the projected suitability patterns should be interpreted as plausible scenarios rather than deterministic predictions. In addition, variables such as access to irrigation or availability of improved seeds were not included, aspects that could enrich future research. Despite this, the F-AHP and TOPSIS methodological combination proved to be an effective tool for evaluating and prioritizing productive areas, coinciding with studies such as that of Geerts et al70. who applied similar approaches for the agroclimatic zoning of quinoa in the Bolivian highlands. This study offers a comprehensive analytical contribution to anticipating and managing the impacts of climate change on quinoa cultivation in Peru, proposing a technical basis for the formulation of adaptation strategies and sustainable use of the Andean territory.

The results are particularly relevant in the context of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 2: Zero Hunger, SDG 13: Climate Action, and SDG 15: Life on Land. The identification of agroclimatic suitable areas for quinoa cultivation not only improves agricultural planning and food security, but also promotes the resilience of traditional production systems in the face of climate change scenarios, by providing spatially explicit evidence to guide agricultural planning and policy-making at national and regional scales. Quinoa, as a native crop with high nutritional value and adaptable to extreme conditions, represents a strategic alternative for rural communities in the high Andes, many of which are economically and climatically vulnerable. Likewise, the information generated by this study can be used to guide public policies that promote sustainable land management, encouraging agricultural practices compatible with the conservation of fragile ecosystems and the reduction of rural poverty. In this way, the integrated spatial assessment approach adopted not only provides technical evidence for decision-making, but also contributes to global efforts to achieve sustainable and inclusive development in rural areas.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that the integration of F-AHP and TOPSIS methodologies in a GIS environment constitutes an ideal tool for the evaluation of the agro-climatic and productive suitability of quinoa in Peru. The results highlight the determining role of climatic variables such as temperature and precipitation in the delimitation of suitable zones, and show significant limitations associated with relative humidity and edaphic parameters such as organic matter; In addition, the department of Puno was identified as the most suitable for cultivation, in contrast to regions such as Loreto, which are less suitable due to unfavorable conditions; The projected climate scenarios suggest an upward shift of optimal zones, with a slight expansion of moderately and highly suitable areas; this information is of great relevance for agricultural planning, the formulation of adaptation policies and land use planning, being advisable in future studies to integrate complementary variables such as access to irrigation, availability of improved seeds and management practices, in order to optimize the accuracy of the modelling and strengthen its application in local contexts in the face of the challenges of climate change.
