Electroacupuncture enhances nerve regeneration and functional recovery in a rat model of median nerve transection
Authors: Hongyu Zhou, Jin He, Ainizier Yalikun, Hui Chen, Yu Si Aierken Rehemulatu, Li li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms of electroacupuncture (EA) in promoting peripheral nerve regeneration following delayed repair of a median nerve transection in a rat model.MethodsThirty male Sprague-Dawley rats were randomly assigned to five groups: sham operation, model (1- and 2-weeks post-transection), and EA treatment (1- and 2-weeks post-repair). Median nerve transection was performed, followed by delayed microsurgical repair. EA was applied at Jianyu (LI15) and Neiguan (PC6) acupoints following nerve repair. Nerve regeneration and tissue response were evaluated through histological staining (Hematoxylin and Eosin [H&E], Luxol Fast Blue [LFB], Masson’s Trichrome [Masson]), immunofluorescence (S100β, Sox10), western blotting (phosphorylated phosphoinositide 3-kinase [p-PI3K], receptor for advanced glycation end products [RAGE], triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 [TREM2]), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; interleukin-6 [IL-6], C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 3 [CXCL3], matrix metalloproteinase-3 [MMP3], reactive oxygen species [ROS], superoxide dismutase [SOD], malondialdehyde [MDA]), and transcriptome sequencing with gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)ResultsEA significantly enhanced axonal regeneration and remyelination, reduced inflammatory infiltration, and suppressed fibrotic collagen deposition. EA treatment markedly downregulated p-PI3K and RAGE expression and upregulated TREM2, indicating inhibition of inflammatory signaling and promotion of M2 macrophage polarization. Serum biomarkers showed reduced levels of IL-6, ROS, and MDA, along with increased SOD activity. Transcriptomic analysis revealed EA-induced activation of reparative and metabolic pathways, including lysosome function, endocytosis, and protein processing, while reversing injury-induced suppression of oxidative phosphorylation and ribosomal function.ConclusionEA facilitates peripheral nerve regeneration through multi-target regulation of inflammation, oxidative stress, and neuroglial activity. These findings support the translational potential of EA as a complementary therapeutic strategy following nerve repair for upper limb peripheral nerve injuries.Keywords: Peripheral nerve injury, Electroacupuncture, Median nerve, Rat, Peripheral nerve regeneration

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) constitutes a significant neurological pathology with profound socioeconomic implications, with an annual incidence of 11.2–23 cases per 100,000 population (Zhang et al., 2022, Aman et al., 2022). Epidemiological analyses reveal a distinct demographic predominance in young males, with penetrating trauma and lacerations constituting the principal etiological mechanisms, accounting for 54.6 % of documented cases (Aman et al., 2022). PNI often results in motor and sensory dysfunction, with only about 30 % of patients achieving full recovery, while most experience varying degrees of neurological impairment or permanent disability (Murphy et al., 2023). Current treatment approaches for PNI include neurorrhaphy and nerve grafting, as well as physical therapies such as electrical stimulation and ultrasound therapy. Pharmacological interventions, including methylcobalamin, B vitamins, and neurotrophic factors, are also commonly used (Lopes et al., 2022). However, due to the inherently slow rate of nerve regeneration and the potential formation of traumatic neuromas, functional recovery often requires several months, and in some cases, complete restoration of target tissue function is cannot be achieved (M et al., 2014, Barnes et al., 2022).Recent reviews further emphasize that despite advances in surgical repair, adjunctive strategies, such as peri-operative electrical stimulation, biomaterial conduits, and pharmacological support, remain insufficient to achieve consistent functional recovery, underscoring the need for novel neuromodulatory interventions (Cheng et al., 2025, Zhou et al., 2024, Stasiowski et al., 2024).

Electroacupuncture (EA), a modern adaptation of traditional acupuncture, enhances therapeutic efficacy through electrical stimulation. In recent years, EA has demonstrated significant potential in PNI treatment and pain management. Studies have shown that EA stimulation at the Zusanli (ST36) acupoint activates the vagus nerve-mediated cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, reducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α by 40–60 % (Torres-Rosas et al., 2014). Regarding oxidative stress regulation, research using a rat model of complex regional pain syndrome type I has found that EA enhances superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and reduces mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation via the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase-1 pathway, thereby mitigating inflammation and pain (Li et al., 2022). Furthermore, EA promotes nerve repair by downregulating microRNA-1b expression, which in turn increases brain-derived neurotrophic factor expression in Schwann cells, thereby facilitating nerve regeneration (Liu et al., 2020).

In recent studies, Tian et al. reported that EA promoted axonal regeneration in a sciatic nerve transection model by downregulating long non-coding RNA GAS5 and activating the microRNA-1b/phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) pathway (Tian et al., 2024). Chen et al. further demonstrated that EA alleviated PNI in rats by modulating autophagy-related signaling (Chen et al., 2024). Ji et al. highlighted mitochondrial biogenesis and adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)/ribosomal protein S6 kinase beta-1 activation as critical mechanisms underlying EA-mediated functional recovery in nerve-injured mice (Ji et al., 2025).

While most studies have focused on lower limb nerves, particularly the sciatic nerve, research on upper limb nerve injury models remains limited. An analysis from the European Trauma Register (TraumaRegister DGU®) suggests that upper limb nerve injuries are more prevalent than lower limb injuries among trauma patients (Huckhagel et al., 2018).

However, recent preclinical work has begun to focus on upper limb nerve.For example, Liu et al. (2025) demonstrated that EA significantly enhanced median and ulnar nerve regeneration in a rat forelimb injury model, suggesting its translational potential for upper limb nerve injuries (Liu et al., 2025).

In this study, we established a rat model of median nerve transection and investigated the effects of EA intervention on nerve regeneration, inflammatory regulation (IL-6, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 3 [CXCL3], matrix metalloproteinase-3 [MMP3]), oxidative stress markers (ROS, SOD, malondialdehyde [MDA]), and key signaling pathways (PI3K/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), receptor for advanced glycation end products [RAGE], triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 [TREM2]). Using morphological and molecular biological analyses, we aim to elucidate the multi-target synergistic effects of EA. The objective of this study is to clarify the mechanisms by which EA facilitates upper limb nerve repair and to provide a theoretical foundation for the treatment of upper limb nerve injuries.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (200 ± 20 g, n = 30) were provided by Wuhan Hengyisai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (SCXK (E) 2020–0018), and all animal experiments were conducted at Wuhan Bessie Model Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (SYXK (E) 2022–0124). All animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free laboratory conditions (12-h photoperiod, 22 ± 2 °C, 55 ± 5 % relative humidity). Following a 7-day acclimatization period with daily health monitoring, animals were randomized into five experimental groups (n = 6 per group): (1) sham surgery, (2) 1-week post-injury model, (3) 2-week post-injury model, (4) 1-week EA intervention, and (5) 2-week EA intervention. Standard laboratory diet and purified water were provided ad libitum, with cage bedding replaced every 48-h intervals. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee with the approval number 2025–03–20B.

Surgical Procedures

Median Nerve Transection and Delayed Repair Model

Following anesthesia induction (sodium pentobarbital, 40 mg/kg, intraperitoneal), animals were positioned supine with limbs immobilized. Using aseptic technique, a longitudinal incision exposed the median nerve in the mid-to-distal segment of the left forelimb. Complete nerve transection was performed in experimental groups using microsurgical scissors, while sham-operated animals underwent nerve exposure without transection. Primary wound closure was accomplished using 4–0 non-absorbable monofilament sutures.

Seven days post-injury, secondary surgical intervention was performed under identical anesthetic conditions. The initial incision was reopened, and neurorrhaphy was performed using 10–0 monofilament nylon with an epineural technique under microsurgical magnification. Sham-operated animals underwent surgical re-exploration without further nerve manipulation. Post-operative analgesia consisted of buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg, subcutaneous) administration for 48 h.

EA Protocol

EA intervention commenced immediately following neurorrhaphy. Sterile acupuncture needles (0.25 mm × 25 mm, Huatuo, Suzhou Medical Appliance Factory, China) were inserted at standardized acupoints: Jianyu (LI15) at the anteroinferior depression of the acromion, and Neiguan (PC6) located 2 cun proximal to the transverse wrist crease between the palmaris longus and flexor carpi radialis tendons. Electrical stimulation was delivered via HANS-200A EA apparatus (Nanjing Jisheng Medical Technology, China) with the cathode positioned at the distal acupoint (PC6) and anode at the proximal acupoint (LI15). Stimulation parameters were precisely calibrated (2 Hz frequency, sparse-wave configuration) with current intensity (0.1–1.0 mA) titrated to elicit visible fasciculation without discomfort. The therapeutic regimen consisted of 15-min bilateral sessions administered at 12-h intervals (08:00 and 20:00). Control groups received no intervention following neurorrhaphy (Figure S1).

Tissue Acquisition and Processing

Median nerve tissue was fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for over 24 h, trimmed, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Tissue was oriented in the coronal plane, rapidly solidified at –20 °C, and sectioned into 3 μm slices. Sections were flattened in a 40 °C water bath, mounted on slides, and baked at 62 °C for 90–120 min for histological analysis.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) Staining

Paraffin sections were dewaxed with xylene, stained with hematoxylin for 1–5 min, differentiated with 1 % acid alcohol for 3–5 s, and neutralized with ammonia water. Cytoplasmic counterstaining was performed with eosin for 1–3 min, followed by graded ethanol dehydration, xylene clearing, and sealing with neutral gum. Nerve cell nuclei appeared blue, while the cytoplasm appeared pink.

Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) Staining

After dewaxing, sections were hydrophobically sealed and incubated in 0.1 % LFB at 60 °C for 2 h in the dark. Differentiation was performed with a 0.1 % lithium carbonate solution for 5–10 s under microscopic control, with 75 % ethanol used to terminate differentiation. After dehydration, sections were mounted with neutral gum. Myelinated regions appeared dark blue, while demyelinated areas appeared light blue.

Masson Staining

Following dewaxing, sections were incubated with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin overnight at room temperature, followed by a 30-min treatment at 62 °C. Nuclei were stained with Ponceau acid fuchsin-phosphomolybdic acid for 1 min, differentiated with 1 % acid alcohol, followed by aniline blue staining of collagen fibers and Orange G counterstaining of muscle fibers. Sections were differentiated with 1 % glacial acetic acid and mounted with neutral gum to assess collagen fiber deposition after nerve injury.

Immunofluorescence

Antigen retrieval was performed using a 0.01 M citrate buffer with microwave heating. After blocking with 5 % bovine serum albumin, sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies: S100β (1:300, PTG, China), Sox10 (1:300, PTG, China), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies (horseradish peroxidase 1:500, fluorescein isothiocyanate 1:1000) and tyramide signal amplification. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, and images were captured using laser confocal microscopy.

Western Blot Analysis

Tissue samples were rinsed 2–3 times with pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline to remove blood and lipid contaminants, then minced and homogenized in tissue protein extraction reagent on ice. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected as the total protein extract. Protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit. Based on the determined concentration, an appropriate volume was taken to ensure 40 μg of protein per well. Samples were mixed with 5 × loading buffer and denatured at 95–100 °C for 5 min.

Proteins were separated using gel electrophoresis, transferred to membranes, and blocked for 1 h. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: p-PI3K polyclonal antibody (1:1000, Abcam, UK), PI3K polyclonal antibody (1:2000, Affbiotech, China), mTOR monoclonal antibody (1:500, CST, USA), and RAGE monoclonal antibody (1:1000, Wuhan Sanying, China). Hematoxylin (Sigma, Germany) was used as a counterstain.

Secondary antibody incubation at room temperature for 30 min, followed by enhanced chemiluminescence (detection and darkroom exposure. Densitometric analysis of target protein bands was performed using AlphaEaseFC software.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Whole blood samples were immediately centrifuged at 2000 x g at 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected and stored at –80 °C for subsequent analysis. Serum samples were processed according to the ELISA kit instructions to quantify oxidative stress markers (ROS, SOD, and MDA) and inflammation-related immune regulatory factors (IL-6, CXCL3, and MMP3), assessing the effects of EA intervention on inflammation reduction and oxidative stress mitigation. The ELISA kits used in this study included: SOD assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China), ROS assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China), MDA assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China), rat IL-6 ELISA kit (ELK Biotechnology, China), rat MMP3 ELISA kit (ELK Biotechnology, China), and rat GROγ ELISA kit (ELK Biotechnology, China).

RNA Sequencing and Differential Expression Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the median nerve tissues using a standard TRIzol-based protocol, and RNA quality was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform to generate paired-end 150 bp reads. Raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp, and clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2. Transcript abundance was estimated using StringTie, and gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups were identified using DESeq2, with |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value (false discovery rate [FDR]) < 0.05 as the threshold for significance.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

To investigate the biological significance of transcriptomic changes, GSEA was performed using the clusterProfiler R package. Gene sets were ranked according to their expression changes, and enrichment was assessed based on the curated Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway database. Enriched pathways were identified using the normalized enrichment score (NES), with significance thresholds set at FDR < 0.05. Comparative analyses were conducted between Sham vs. Model and EA-treated vs. Model groups to explore both injury-induced and EA-responsive regulatory pathways.

Statistical Analysis

All collected data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.2.0. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used for statistical analysis, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

H&E Staining

Nerve specimens from the sham surgery group exhibited optimal cytoarchitecture with densely packed nerve fibers, intact axonal ultrastructure, and complete absence of inflammatory infiltrates, edema, or pathological alterations. The model group demonstrated severe axonal disorganization with disrupted fascicular architecture at 1 week post-injury, followed by modest amelioration of inflammatory infiltration by 2 weeks, although persistent axonal disarray, neovascularization, and fibroblastic infiltration remained evident. In contrast, the EA intervention group showed marked attenuation of inflammatory cell extravasation and axonal degeneration at 1 week, with early manifestations of axonal regeneration and diminished perineural edema. By 2 weeks, nerve fascicles exhibited substantially restored cytoarchitectural organization with prominent axonal regeneration characterized by parallel alignment of nascent axon bundles, accompanied by near-complete resolution of inflammatory infiltrates and interstitial edema (Fig. 1A).

LFB Myelin Quantification

In the sham surgery group, myelin sheaths displayed complete structural and functional integrity, with quantitative morphometric analysis revealing a myelinated nerve fiber density of 54.01 ± 6.364 fibers/100μm². At 1-week post-injury, the model group exhibited severe demyelination characterized by extensive myelin fragmentation, abundant myelin ovoids, and expansive unstained or weakly LFB-positive regions, with significantly reduced myelinated nerve fiber density (12.95 ± 2.167 fibers/100μm²). This pathological state persisted through 2 weeks, with minimal evidence of spontaneous remyelination (11.75 ± 3.163 fibers/100μm²).

The EA intervention group demonstrated significantly reduced myelin degradation at 1 week, with distinct histological evidence of early remyelination processes and significantly higher myelinated nerve fiber density (35.69 ± 3.817 fibers/100μm², P = 0.0010) compared to the contemporaneous model group. By 2 weeks, newly synthesized myelin sheaths exhibited enhanced structural continuity and approached physiological thickness parameters, with a quantified fiber density of 29.72 ± 6.344 fibers/100μm², significantly exceeding that of the parallel model group (P = 0.0059) (Fig. 1A, B).

Masson’s Trichrome Collagen Assessment

Sham surgery specimens demonstrated physiological collagen distribution, comprising 37.1 % ± 8.919 % of the tissue parenchyma, with fibers predominantly localized to the epineurial and perivascular compartments and minimal intrafascicular deposition. The model group exhibited pathological collagen hyperproduction at 1 week (66.96 % ± 3.78 %), characterized by extensive blue-green fibrillar matrix deposition and disorganized collagenous architecture. This fibrotic process intensified by 2 weeks (70.57 % ± 7.564 %), manifesting as dense scar tissue formation, pathological expansion of interfascicular spaces, and circumferential encapsulation of regenerating nerve fibers.

The EA intervention group significantly attenuated this fibroproliferative response at 1 week (31.86 % ± 6.169 %, P = 0.0005 vs. model group) and further mitigated fibrotic progression at 2 weeks (47.01 % ± 5.471 %, P = 0.0097), resulting in reduced scar tissue density and diminished perineural collagenous encapsulation surrounding regenerating axons (Fig. 1A, C).

Immunohistochemical Analyses

Immunofluorescence analysis of S100β and Sox10, established biomarkers of Schwann cell activation and myelination capacity, demonstrated robust nuclear and cytoplasmic expression in Schwann cells within the sham surgery group. At 1 week post-injury, Schwann cell functionality was severely compromised in the model group, with quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis revealing significant attenuation of Sox10 (68.76 ± 10.58 arbitrary units) and S100β (18.09 ± 1.686 arbitrary units) expression (P < 0.0001 vs. sham group). By 2 weeks, limited endogenous recovery was observed (Sox10: 98.47 ± 5.267, S100β: 37.65 ± 7.447 arbitrary units).

EA intervention significantly enhanced Sox10 (127.6 ± 15.77 arbitrary units, P = 0.0004) and S100β (75.89 ± 9.279 arbitrary units, P = 0.0007) immunoreactivity at 1 week compared to the model group, with increased nuclear co-localization indicating enhanced transcriptional activity. This effect was further potentiated by 2 weeks, with sustained upregulation of fluorescence intensity (Sox10: 141.1 ± 8.612, P = 0.0049; S100β: 101.6 ± 22.1 arbitrary units, P = 0.0003), indicating progressive enhancement of Schwann cell activation and promotion of remyelination processes (Fig. 2).

Western Blot Analysis

Quantitative immunoblot analysis revealed significant dysregulation of key inflammatory and immunomodulatory signaling molecules following nerve injury. Specifically, p-PI3K and RAGE demonstrated marked upregulation, while TREM2, a critical regulator of macrophage/microglial phenotypic polarization, was significantly downregulated in the model group.

EA intervention induced time-dependent normalization of p-PI3K and RAGE expression, with protein levels progressively approximating those of the sham group. Concurrently, EA promoted significant upregulation of TREM2 expression with sustained intervention. These molecular modifications collectively suggest that EA attenuates neuroinflammatory processes and oxidative injury through inhibition of the PI3K/Akt signaling cascade and downregulation of RAGE-mediated inflammatory pathways, while simultaneously promoting macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype via TREM2 upregulation, thereby establishing a microenvironment conducive to neural regeneration (Fig. 3).

Pro-inflammatory Cytokine Profiles

Quantitative analysis of inflammatory mediators demonstrated significant elevation of IL-6 concentrations in the model group at both 1 week (P = 0.0012) and 2 weeks (P = 0.0155) compared to sham controls. EA intervention induced significant attenuation of IL-6 levels at 1 week (P = 0.0313), with normalization to baseline concentrations by 2 weeks (P > 0.05 vs. sham group). CXCL3 expression exhibited significant upregulation at 1 week (P = 0.0074) with sustained elevation at 2 weeks in the model group. EA intervention produced moderate, though statistically nonsignificant, reductions in CXCL3 levels at both time points.

MMP3, a key mediator of extracellular matrix remodeling and inflammatory signaling, demonstrated significant elevation in the model group at 1 week (P = 0.0067) with partial spontaneous reduction by 2 weeks. EA intervention accelerated the normalization of MMP3 expression at both time points, with 2-week levels approaching physiological baseline values (P > 0.05 vs. sham group), indicating enhanced resolution of inflammatory processes (Table 1, Fig. 4A).

Oxidative Stress Biomarker Assessment

ROS quantification revealed marked elevation in the model group at 1 week (P = 0.0007) with persistent upregulation at 2 weeks (P = 0.0026). EA intervention significantly attenuated ROS generation at 1 week (P = 0.0241), demonstrating early antioxidant efficacy. MDA concentrations, a validated biomarker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative membrane damage, were significantly elevated in the model group at both 1 week (P = 0.0002) and 2 weeks (P = 0.0012). EA intervention induced a significant reduction of MDA levels at 2 weeks (P = 0.0305), demonstrating time-dependent amelioration of oxidative injury. SOD enzymatic activity, a critical component of the endogenous antioxidant defense system, was significantly suppressed in the model group compared to sham controls at both time points, indicating compromised antioxidant capacity. EA intervention progressively enhanced SOD activity, with 2-week levels approximating those of the sham group (P > 0.05), suggesting restoration of endogenous antioxidant mechanisms and consequent attenuation of oxidative stress-induced neuronal damage (Table 2, Fig. 4B).

In the comparison between the sham group and the model group, a total of 565 DEGs were identified, including 229 upregulated and 336 downregulated genes in the model group. A total of 160 genes (68 upregulated and 92 downregulated) were identified in the comparison between EA and model groups (Fig. 5A-C).

GSEA analysis revealed that, compared to the sham group, the model group exhibited significant activation of pathways related to cellular stress responses, metabolic compensation, and immune signaling. Notably enriched pathways included aminoacyl-transfer RNA biosynthesis, epidermal growth factor receptor signaling, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and T/B cell receptor signaling (normalized enrichment score [NES] > 1.6, FDR < 0.05), indicating a shift toward increased protein synthesis, metabolic demand, and immune activity in response to nerve injury (Fig. 6A). Additionally, pathways involved in tissue remodeling and angiogenesis, such as vascular endothelial growth factor signaling and the proteasome pathway, were also upregulated, potentially reflecting endogenous repair efforts.

In contrast, multiple core metabolic and homeostatic pathways were markedly downregulated in the model group, including ribosome function, cytochrome P450-mediated metabolism, smooth vascular muscle contraction, DNA replication, and N-glycan biosynthesis (NES <-1.8, FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 6B). These alterations suggest that neural injury leads to suppressed translational activity, impaired energy metabolism, and disrupted cellular integrity.

Compared to the EA group, the model group also showed significant downregulation of key biosynthetic and mitochondrial pathways, such as ribosome, oxidative phosphorylation, and cardiac muscle contraction (NES <-2, FDR < 0.05), highlighting profound metabolic suppression post-injury (Fig. 6C).

In contrast, EA treatment significantly upregulated pathways associated with immune clearance, repair, and protein processing, including N-glycan biosynthesis, spliceosome, DNA replication, lysosome, endocytosis, and Fc gamma receptor-mediated phagocytosis (NES > 1.7, FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 6D). Additionally, enhanced enrichment of glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchor biosynthesis suggests improved cell recognition and neuronal connectivity.

These findings collectively indicate that EA promotes neural regeneration by reversing injury-induced suppression and activating coordinated immune and reparative pathways.

Discussion

This study employed the median nerve model as the primary experimental paradigm, representing a methodologically significant shift from the conventional sciatic nerve model. This choice was based on several compelling translational and clinical considerations. Epidemiologically, upper extremity nerve injuries occur at significantly higher rates than lower extremity injuries in clinical practice. Additionally, manual dexterity functions, particularly prehensile movements, exhibit remarkable phylogenetic conservation between rodent and human models, thereby substantially enhancing the translational validity of our experimental approach (Jager et al., 2014).

While the sciatic nerve predominantly subserves locomotor and load-bearing functions in the lower extremities, the median nerve is instrumental in both somatosensory perception and fine motor coordination in the upper extremities, rendering it exceptionally suitable for investigating nuanced motor control mechanisms and neuroregeneration processes (Tos et al., 2008). It is important to emphasis that the median nerve injury model requires advanced microsurgical skills, as its procedural complexity can introduce methodological variability, necessitating refined technical expertise to ensure reproducible and valid results (DeLeonibus et al., 2021).

Although immediate neurorrhaphy represents the optimal clinical approach for PNIs, pragmatic clinical management is frequently subject to temporal delays attributable to multiple factors, including acute inflammatory edema, potential infectious complications, and resource limitations (particularly insufficient microsurgical infrastructure in primary care institutions) (Menorca et al., 2013, MacKay et al., 2021). To accurately recapitulate this prevalent clinical scenario, our study established a delayed-repair model of median nerve injury in mice, involving complete nerve transection followed by microsurgical coaptation after a precisely standardized 1-week delay.

EA therapy has demonstrated substantial efficacy across a spectrum of neurodegenerative pathologies, metabolic disorders, and inflammatory conditions, emerging as a significant focus in contemporary translational medicine. In our study, EA intervention significantly facilitated histomorphological restoration of the median nerve through multiple complementary mechanisms. Comprehensive histological analyses revealed substantially attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration in the EA group, with enhanced remyelination mediated through significant upregulation of Schwann cell-specific S100β/Sox10 expression. Masson trichrome staining further corroborated that EA therapy markedly reduced collagen deposition and fibrotic scarring within the neural parenchyma, establishing a microenvironment more conducive to axonal regeneration.

Our findings corroborate and build upon previous experimental evidence. Ho et al. similarly demonstrated that EA promoted accelerated nerve regeneration and enhanced neurophysiological recovery in a comparable rat median nerve injury model (Ho et al., 2013). In a complementary investigation, Hu LN et al. documented that EA and moxibustion stimulation targeting specific acupoints, Huantiao (GB30) and Zusanli (ST36), significantly augmented Schwann cell proliferation, upregulated neurotrophin synthesis, and improved electrophysiological parameters of nerve conduction (Hu et al., 2018). These convergent findings substantiate the therapeutic potential of EA in facilitating peripheral nerve regeneration and functional recovery. Li et al. using intravoxel incoherent motion magnetic resonance imaging, demonstrated that EA significantly improved nerve perfusion recovery compared to bone marrow stromal cell transplantation, with perfusion fraction strongly correlating with histological and functional outcomes (Li et al., 2025). These imaging data further substantiate EA’s role in restoring neural microcirculation during regeneration.

At the molecular level, our investigation elucidated several interconnected signaling pathways through which EA exerts its neuroregenerative effects. Our results demonstrated that EA intervention effectively suppressed PI3K phosphorylation. Given that activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling axis potentiates pro-inflammatory gene expression via nuclear transcription factor kappa B modulation (Ma et al., 2022), we postulate that EA attenuates tissue injury-induced inflammatory cascades primarily through inhibition of this critical phosphorylation event. RAGE, a transmembrane pattern recognition receptor belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily, activates nuclear transcription factor kappa B, mitogen-activated protein kinase, and related signal transduction pathways through interaction with diverse ligands, including advanced glycation end-products, amyloid-beta, and high mobility group box 1. This activation cascade induces the expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α and IL-6 (Lin et al., 2016). Following EA intervention, we observed significant RAGE downregulation, which correlated with attenuated inflammatory responses. Reduced circulating levels of IL-6 and CXCL3 further confirmed the anti-inflammatory efficacy of EA, providing biochemical validation of the observed histomorphological improvements. TREM2 is a transmembrane receptor of the immunoglobulin superfamily, primarily expressed on myeloid cells such as macrophages and microglia (Tanaka et al., 2022). In this study, TREM2 expression was upregulated in the EA intervention group, suggesting that EA promotes the transformation of macrophages to the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype, enhances phagocytic function, and improves the local microenvironment.

Our biochemical analyses revealed that EA intervention significantly enhanced SOD enzymatic activity while concomitantly reducing MDA levels, indicating substantial enhancement of endogenous antioxidant capacity. These findings complement previous investigations by Lv E et al., who demonstrated that high-frequency (100 Hz) EA stimulation significantly mitigated oxidative stress in the nigrostriatal dopaminergic system of Parkinsonian mice through activation of the Nrf2/antioxidant response element signaling pathway (Lv et al., 2015). Similarly, in a lipopolysaccharide-induced endotoxemic lung injury model in rabbits, EA preconditioning at specific acupoints, Zusanli (ST36) and Feishu (BL13), significantly attenuated pulmonary inflammation through Nrf2/antioxidant response element pathway activation, confirming acupoint-specific modulation of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory signaling networks (Yu et al., 2014).While EA also alleviated PNI in rats by modulating autophagy-related pathways, thereby reducing fibrotic remodeling and creating a favorable regenerative niche (Chen et al., 2024).

Extensive research has shown that EA upregulates the expression of key neurotrophic factors, including nerve growth factor and glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor (Ozkan et al., 2021). In a facial nerve injury model, Fei J et al. demonstrated that EA stimulation of craniofacial acupoints, including Yifeng (TE17) and Jiache (ST6), promoted nerve regeneration through multiple complementary mechanisms: upregulating glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor expression and its receptor N-cadherin in facial motor neurons, inhibiting neuronal apoptotic pathways, and reducing peripheral axonal demyelination and inflammatory infiltration (Fei et al., 2019).

By synthesizing our experimental findings with existing scientific literature, we propose that EA synergistically promotes median nerve regeneration through four complementary molecular and cellular mechanisms: (1) inhibition of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pro-inflammatory signaling axis, (2) upregulation of TREM2 expression to facilitate macrophage M2 polarization and subsequent anti-inflammatory effects, (3) enhancement of endogenous antioxidant defense systems to counteract oxidative stress, and (4) stimulation of Schwann cell proliferation and differentiation to facilitate remyelination and axonal regeneration.

In addition, extracellular vesicles have recently been recognized as key mediators of nerve repair; a 2025 review by Yu et al. proposed that EA may regulate extracellular vesicle cargo release from Schwann cells and macrophages, thereby facilitating intercellular signaling and contributing to the regenerative microenvironment (Yu et al., 2025).

Despite the promising results, several methodological and conceptual limitations warrant acknowledgment and provide opportunities for future investigation. First, our study lacks a comprehensive characterization of molecular regulatory effects on critical fibrotic pathways, particularly the transforming growth factor-beta/Smad signaling axis, which plays a fundamental role in determining the quality and functional outcomes of nerve regeneration. Second, the long-term durability of EA-mediated therapeutic effects beyond our experimental timeframe requires systematic longitudinal evaluation to determine the sustainability of the observed functional and histological improvements. Third, at the mechanistic level, several complex molecular interactions remain incompletely elucidated, including RAGE-TREM2 signaling crosstalk, temporal dynamics of Nrf2 pathway activation, and the precise dose-response relationships between therapeutic outcomes and EA parameters such as stimulation frequency, amplitude, and duration.

Future investigations should leverage advanced multi-omics technologies, including single-cell transcriptomics, spatial proteomics, and phosphoproteomics, to precisely identify EA-responsive molecular targets with enhanced spatiotemporal resolution (Wang et al., 2021). Additionally, systematic optimization of EA parameters through rigorously designed dose-response studies (frequency range 2–100 Hz, intensity 0.1–1.0 mA) could identify optimal treatment protocols for diverse neuropathological conditions, ultimately facilitating the development of evidence-based, standardized clinical treatment guidelines (Zhou et al., 2023).

The translational potential of our experimental findings should be validated through multicenter randomized controlled clinical trials with appropriate statistical power, designed to assess the reproducibility of therapeutic efficacy, population-specific responses, and procedural safety profiles across diverse demographic groups and healthcare settings (Shin et al., 2018). This comprehensive translational research approach would bridge fundamental laboratory discoveries with clinical applications, potentially establishing EA as a standardized complementary intervention for PNIs in contemporary neurological rehabilitation protocols.

Conclusions

EA intervention significantly promotes axonal regeneration and remyelination while reducing collagen scar formation within the neural tissue. EA exerts its effects by inhibiting the PI3K/RAGE pro-inflammatory axis and activating TREM2-dependent macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby significantly reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and alleviating post-injury inflammatory responses. EA intervention markedly enhances the antioxidative capacity of neural tissues, mitigating oxidative stress-induced damage. Therefore, EA therapy provides a stage-specific treatment strategy for PNI through its multi-target synergistic effects on neural repair promotion, inflammatory regulation, and oxidative balance.
