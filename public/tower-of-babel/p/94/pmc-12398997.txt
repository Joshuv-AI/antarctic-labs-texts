Detection and antimicrobial susceptibility of E. coli O157:H7 in the milk supply chain, milking environments and humans in Woliata Sodo, Ethiopia
Authors: Seblewengel Ayichew, Ashagrie Zewdu, Bekele Megersa, Teshale Sori, Fanta D. Gutema
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEscherichia coli O157:H7 is a strain that produces Shiga toxin, which can cause infections ranging from mild to severe in humans. Cattle are a primary reservoir for the bacteria, and the contaminated milk and milk products can transmit these bacteria to humans, posing a risk of infection. The present study aimed to evaluate the occurrence of E. coli O157:H7 and determine its antimicrobial susceptibility profile across the milk supply chain, milking environments, and humans in Woliata Sodo, Southern Ethiopia.MethodologyA total of 644 samples, including raw milk, hand swabs, teat swabs, equipment swabs, floor swabs, and human stool, were collected through a cross sectional study. Bacterial detection was performed according to the ISO 16,654 standard method, and the antimicrobial susceptibility of the isolates was evaluated using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method against 14 antimicrobials representing ten different classes.ResultsThe overall detection rate of E. coli O157:H7 was 3.1% (20/644). The highest detection rate was observed in milk samples collected at milk collecting centers, at 10% (5/50). In comparison, detection rates were 6.8% (4/59) in samples from milkers’ hands, 5.4% (2/37) in farm floor swabs, and 2.9% among outpatients presenting with diarrhea. All the isolates showed resistance to doxycycline, erythromycin, tetracycline, clindamycin, and vancomycin. Additionally, 95%, 90%, and 90% of isolates were resistant to amoxicillin, ampicillin, and streptomycin, respectively. Resistance was lower for ceftriaxone (20%) and chloramphenicol (15%). A significant proportion (100%) of the isolates exhibited multidrug resistance.ConclusionThis study confirmed the presence of multidrug resistant E. coli O157:H7 in the tested sample. Notably, the highest prevalence was detected in milk samples from collection centers, underscoring post harvest contamination as a critical point of intervention. Identification of the pathogen on milkers’ hands, farm floors, and human stool samples highlights potential transmission pathways and suggests a tangible risk of foodborne infections. These findings emphasize the critical need for targeted public health strategies, including comprehensive education on hygienic practices and the implementation of effective contamination control measures, to reduce the risk of infection and protect consumer health.

Background

Diarrhea remains the leading cause of early death and is the second most significant contributor to the overall disease burden in Ethiopia [1]. Humans can contract milk borne infections from consuming unpasteurized milk and milk products contaminated with pathogens such as Salmonella, E. coli O157:H7, and Staphylococcus aureus [2, 3]. E. coli O157:H7 is a highly significant pathogenic strain of E. coli that poses serious public health concerns and leads to substantial economic impacts in the dairy sector. It is estimated to cause approximately 2,801,000 serious infections worldwide, with an incidence rate of 43.1 cases per 100,000 person years [4]. Human infections linked to E. coli O157:H7 strains have been reported in many developing nations, including Ethiopia [5–7].

E. coli O157:H7 is a rod-shaped, gram negative, flagellated bacterium that does not form spores and is recognized for its production of Shiga-like toxins [8]. The Shiga toxins (Stx1 and Stx2) produced by E. coli O157:H7 disrupt protein synthesis and induce necrosis of endothelial cells, leading to severe health complications such as kidney failure, hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), abdominal pain, bloody diarrhea, and potentially fatal enterohemorrhagic colitis (HC). These infections are typically more severe in vulnerable populations, including children, the elderly and immunocompromised individuals [9–11].

Cattle are the primary reservoirs of E. coli O157:H7 [12]; however, they are resistant to the disease because they lack the preferred receptor for Stx1 and Stx2, which is globotriaosylceramide (Gb3). Consequently, they can serve as sources of infection for humans [8, 12].Poor hygienic practices on farms and during milk handling can lead to contamination of milk with cattle feces harboring E. coli O157:H7 [13–17].

Overuse and misuse of antimicrobial medications in animals have been associated with antimicrobial resistance in both humans and animals [18, 19]. Studies conducted in Ethiopia and other regions have reported the presence of antimicrobial resistant E. coli O157:H7 strains originating from cattle, dairy farm environments, humans (from various clinical sources), and food of animal origin, including milk and milk products. These strains have shown resistance to commonly used antimicrobials such as erythromycin, amoxicillin, ampicillin, tetracycline, streptomycin, ceftriaxone, chloramphenicol, and ciprofloxacin [20–24].

Although E. coli O157:H7 poses a significant public health threat, it has been relatively overlooked in public health research, especially within the Wolaita Zone. There is limited or no data regarding its detection rate and antimicrobial resistance patterns in milk throughout the supply chain, the dairy farm environment, and in humans in Wolaita Sodo. Understanding this information is crucial for guiding future efforts in designing and implementing effective prevention and control strategies. Therefore, our study aimed to assess the occurrence and determine the antimicrobial susceptibility profile of E. coli O157:H7 across the milk supply chain, milking environments, and humans in Woliata Sodo Town, Southern Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study area

The research was conducted in Wolaita Sodo, Southern Ethiopia which is located at a latitude and longitude of 6°54′N and 37°45′E, respectively (Fig. 1). Based on data from the Central Statistical Agency [25], Sodo town has an estimated population of approximately 76,780 residents. Furthermore, the Agricultural Zonal Office [26] indicated that the town hosts more than 200 smallholder dairy farms and 26 commercial dairy farms, totaling around 418 registered lactating cows.

Study setting

Samples were collected from dairy farm environment, diarrheic patients, and milk samples along the milk supply chain. Both smallholder (80) and commercial dairy farms (26) with lactating cows were included in the study. Smallholder dairy farms were defined as farms with fewer than 10 cows that are predominantly consisting of local Zebu breeds, while commercial dairy farms were those keeping more than 20 cows, mainly Zebu and Holstein Friesian crossbreeds, managed intensively for milk marketing. A total of 106 dairy farms, which directly supply milk to two or more consumers or serve as milk collectors/retailers, were included in the study [26]. The milk supply chain segment comprises dairy farms, collectors, milk retailers, vendors, and consumers.

Study design, sample size and sampling strategy

A cross sectional study design was employed to collect the necessary data from January 2020 to February 2022.The sample size for selecting lactating cows was calculated using Thursfield’s (2005) formula [27], based on an expected prevalence of 6.9% [28], with a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence level. This calculation yielded a minimum sample size of 99 cows. Proportional random sampling was used to select one lactating cow from each smallholder farm, and two cows from each commercial dairy farm, while three cows were sampled from one commercial farm with more than 30 dairy cows, making a total of 133 cows. The study collected and processed a total of 644 samples, including 339 cow milk samples, 202 environmental samples, and 103 human stool samples. In this study, a total of 441 samples were collected from 106 dairy farms, comprising various sample types: bulk tank milk (n = 106), udder milk (n = 133), teat surface swabs (n = 51), milk storage container swabs (n = 55), farm floor surface swabs (n = 37), and milkers’ hand swabs (n = 59), as detailed in Ayichew et al. (2024) [26]. The minimum sample size for studies involving human participants was determined using Thursfield’s 2005 formula [27], which estimated a required sample of 42 individuals based on an expected prevalence of 2.8% [5]. In this research, stool samples were collected from 103 outpatients presenting with diarrhea who reported consuming raw milk and dairy products at Otona Comprehensive Referral Hospital during the study period. Additionally, milk samples were obtained from 50 milk collecting centers and 50 milk retailers, selected according to the volume of customers served. The study was conducted in three phases. In Phase I, sampling and analysis were performed at dairy farms. During Phase II, milk samples from milk collection centers and retailers were analyzed. In Phase III, stool samples were examined until the required sample size was reached.

Milk sample collection

From each milk source including the udder, farm bulk tank, collection centers, and milk retailers, approximately 25 mL of milk was collected and transferred into a sterile screw-capped universal bottle. The collection of udder milk was conducted following the methodology described by Quinn et al. (2002) [29]. Bulk tank milk samples were collected from farms, collection centers, and retailers using the methods described in Ayichew et al., 2024 [26].

Collection of swab samples

Environmental samples, including swabs from various milking environment such as milkers’ hands, teat surfaces, farm floors, and equipment, were collected using sterile cotton-tipped swabs. The collection process adhered to the microbial surface sampling procedures outlined by [30].These samples were then promptly placed in a sterile screw capped containers containing 10 mL of Tryptone soy broth (TSB) (TSB, CM 0509 Oxoid). All swab samples were collected prior to the beginning of the milking process.

Stool sample collection

Laboratory personnel at the hospital collected stool samples using clean, detergent free, disposable screw cap bottles labeled with the patient’s code number and collection date. Approximately 2 g of freshly voided fecal material were transferred into tubes containing 10 mL of Tryptone Soy Broth (TSB) [31]. All samples were labeled with waterproof ink, stored at 4 °C in an ice box with ice packs, and transported to the Veterinary Microbiology Laboratory at Wolaita Sodo University. Samples were processed within 24 h and maintained at 4 °C until analysis.

Isolation and identification of E. coli O157:H7

The ISO method for detecting E. coli O157:H7 in food and animal feed was employed [31]. Following thorough mixing, 25 mL of the milk samples were added to stomacher bags containing 225 mL of Tryptone Soy Broth (Oxoid, UK). The samples were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h for pre-enrichment. Samples of stool or swabs that contained the Tryptone soy broth (Oxoid, UK) were immediately incubated at 37 °C. The culture growth and turbidity were assessed after 24 h. Eosin Methylene Blue agar (EMB) (Oxoid, UK) was streaked with a loopful of turbid Tryptone soy broth using the streak plating technique, and the plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. E. coli colonies exhibiting a metallic sheen on EMB agar were transferred to sorbitol MacConkey agar supplemented with cefixime and potassium tellurite (CT-SMAC) (Oxoid, UK) and cultured for 24 h at 37 °C to determine whether colorless colonies (indicative of the inability to ferment sorbitol) were present. A presumptive E. coli O157:H7 colony was sub-cultured into nutrient broth and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, biochemical tests including the Voges Proskauer test, citrate utilization, urease production, methyl red, indole, and Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) test were conducted to confirm the bacterial identity [31]. The confirmed isolates were stored at − 20 °C in nutrient broth supplemented with 15% glycerol for future molecular analysis. The E. coli ATCC 25,922 strains was used as a quality control reference throughout the testing process.

DNA extraction

The DNA was extracted using the boiling method [32]. Briefly, from the presumptive E. coli O157:H7 culture glycerol stock, an aliquot was sub-cultured in nutrient broth and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, 1.5 mL of the culture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was removed. After being re suspended in 50 µl of nuclease free water, the bacterial pellet was lysed and boiled for 10 min at 95 °C in a water bath. Following another centrifugation of the lysate, 50 µl of the supernatant was collected in a new collection tube, stored at − 20 °C, and used as a DNA template for PCR [33].

Confirmation of E. coli O157:H7

The confirmation of E. coli O157:H7 was performed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting virulence genes encoding Shiga toxins (stx1 and stx2) and the intimin protein (eae) (Table 1). A 25 µl PCR reaction mixture was prepared containing 8 µl of nuclease free water, 2 µl of each primer (forward and reverse), 12 µl of Gotaq Green Master Mix (Promega, USA), and 3 µl of DNA template. For the stx1 gene, the amplification protocol included an initial denaturation step of 3 min at 95 °C, followed by 35 cycles consisting of 40 s at 95 °C, 40 s at 55 °C, and 30 s at 72 °C, with a final extension of 8 min at 72 °C. For the stx2 gene, the PCR conditions were similar but involved 30 cycles with denaturation at 95 °C for 60 s, annealing at 55 °C for 60 s, and extension at 72 °C for 60 s, followed by an 8 min final extension at 72 °C. To detect the intimin (eae) gene, PCR was performed using a thermal cycler (BIO-RAD T100™ Thermal Cycler, Singapore) with an initial denaturation of 3 min at 95 °C, then 35 cycles of 40 s at 95 °C, 60 s at 55 °C, and 60 s at 72 °C, followed by a 10 min final extension at 72 °C. Agarose gel electrophoresis was used to analyze 5–7 µl of the PCR products, following the method described by El-Baz et al., 2022 [34]. A 100 bp (bp) molecular weight ladder (product no N32315) served as the size marker. The gels were visualized under UV light. The pathogenic genes, eae, stx1, and stx2, were identified based on their characteristic band sizes of approximately 490 bp, 110 bp, and 350 bp, respectively.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test

The antimicrobial susceptibility of PCR confirmed E. coli O157:H7 isolates was assessed using the Kirby Bauer Disk Diffusion method on Mueller Hinton agar (HIMEDIA M173, India). Fourteen antimicrobial agents representing various classes of antibiotics were selected based on their clinical and veterinary relevance [17]. These included ceftriaxone (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), norfloxacin (10 µg), streptomycin (10 µg), vancomycin (30 µg), trimethoprim (5 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), amoxicillin (30 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), clindamycin (2 µg), tetracycline (30 µg), and doxycycline (30 µg) (Oxoid, UK). The isolates’ resistance patterns were analyzed to identify multidrug resistance (MDR), defined as resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes [36]. Results were interpreted following the guidelines of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Testing (EUCAST), categorizing each isolate as either susceptible or resistant. Intermediate susceptibility was considered susceptible for the purpose of analysis [37].

Data management and analysis

The data were entered and stored using Microsoft Office Excel 2010, and all analyses were conducted with STATA version 14 (StataCorp, 4905 Lakeway Drive, College Station, Texas 77845, USA). The detection rate was calculated by dividing the number of isolates detected by the total number of samples tested. The antimicrobial susceptibility testing results of E. coli O157:H7 were summarized using descriptive statistics, and the findings were presented in tabular form.

Results

Among the 644 samples analyzed, 20 samples tested positive for E. coli O157:H7. The overall detection rate was 3.1% (20/644). Milk samples collected at the farm gate showed the lowest detection rate, with bulk milk samples at 1.9% and udder milk samples at 1.5%. No E. coli O157:H7 was detected in teat swab samples. In contrast, the highest detection was observed in milk samples collected from the collection center at 10%, followed by milkers’ hand swabs at 6.8%, and floor swabs at 5.4%. All twenty isolates carried the eae gene, the stx2 gene, or both. Specifically, 5 isolates were positive for stx2 (Fig. 2), 17 isolates were positive for the eae gene (Fig. 3), while no stx1 gene was detected in any of the isolates (Table 2).

Occurrence of E. coli O157:H7 in milk supply chain and diarrheic patients

The antimicrobial susceptibility profile of E. coli O157:H7 isolates (Table 3) revealed high resistance to commonly used antimicrobials. Notably, 100% of the isolates were resistant to clindamycin, doxycycline, erythromycin, tetracycline, and vancomycin, indicating a lack of susceptibility across the lincosamide, tetracycline, macrolide, and glycopeptide classes. At least one isolate showed resistance to all the antimicrobials tested. Similarly, penicillin class drugs exhibited high resistance rates, with 95% resistance to amoxicillin and 90% to ampicillin. Resistance to streptomycin, an aminoglycoside, was also high at 90%, while resistance to ciprofloxacin, a quinolone, and ceftazidime, a cephalosporin, was 85%. In contrast, some antibiotics demonstrated comparatively better efficacy. For instance, ceftriaxone and chloramphenicol showed good activity, with 80% and 85% of the isolates remaining susceptible, respectively. Likewise, norfloxacin, another quinolone, performed relatively well, with 55% of isolates being susceptible.

Discussion

We investigated the occurrence and determined the antimicrobial susceptibility profile of E. coli O157:H7. The microbiological safety and quality of dairy products are critical global health concerns, particularly in developing nations such as Ethiopia. In these regions, milk and milk products are often produced and processed under suboptimal hygienic conditions, with many consumers preferring to consume raw milk. The presence of E. coli O157:H7 in milk and dairy products poses serious public health risks and economic challenges, underscoring the need for improved safety measures and proper handling practices [38–41]. Our study found low overall detection rates of E. coli O157:H7 in Woliata Sodo, Southern Ethiopia; however, a high proportion of these isolates were multidrug resistant. This highlights significant public health risks and need to enforce strict hygienic practices throughout the entire supply chain and promote responsible antimicrobial use to mitigate the spread of resistant strains.

The 1.5% detection rate of E. coli O157:H7 in udder milk was comparable to previous studies that reported detection ranging from 0.2 to 3.1% in milk from lactating cows in Ethiopia [22, 42]. However, it was lower than other findings within the country that reported rates from 4.5 to 10.4% [20, 43–45], and significantly lower than reports from different countries such as Bangladesh [46], Malaysia [47], South Africa [48], and Iran [49], where prevalence ranged from 12 to 43.8%. The 1.9% prevalence of E. coli O157:H7 in Bulk milk was comparable to the 1.74% reported by Gudisa et al., 2022 [50] but lower than the report of 16.7% by Engidaw et al., 2022 [44] in Ethiopia. Variations in detection rates of E. coli O157:H7 in milk from dairy cows and bulk tanks could be attributed to differences in management practices, including the implementation of effective milking and milk handling protocols [51], hygienic practices such as udder hygiene, cleaning and maintenance of milking equipment, and personnel hygiene [52]. Environmental factors like climate and season [53, 54], geographic location [20], animal-related factors such as stage of lactation, age, and parity [24, 55], as well as socioeconomic factors like farm size which influences investment in hygiene and management may also play a role. Proper management techniques, such as maintaining hygiene during milking, can help reduce the detection rate of pathogens [20, 24].

The 10% contamination rate of E. coli O157:H7 in milk at the collection point closely corresponds to the 9.52% reported by Gudisa et al. (2022) [50] in Ethiopia, indicating a consistent level of contamination throughout the milk supply chain. The 2% contamination rate among milk retailers was relatively lower than the 5.0% reported by Disassa et al. (2017) [53] and the 34.62% reported by Dejene et al. (2022) in Ethiopia [24]. This rate is also lower than the contamination level at the collection point. Variations in E. coli O157:H7 contamination levels at milk retailers could be attributed to differences in hygienic handling practices, infrastructure such as milk cooling systems (refrigeration or freezing) during transportation and storage, and the level of awareness regarding hygienic milk handling among handlers [24, 53, 56].

The 1.8% E. coli O157:H7 detected in milk container swabs was lower than the 11.1% reported by Vanitha et al. (2018) [57] and the 19% observed by Msolo et al. (2016) [48] in similar samples. Additionally, the 6.8% contamination rate in swab samples from milkers’ hands aligned with the 7% reported by Msolo et al. (2016) [48] and the 9.23% noted by Dejene et al. (2022) [24] in hand swabs from milkers. The detection of the pathogen in milkers’ hand swabs and farm environments further highlights the role of poor sanitation and handling practices in bacterial transmission. Factors such as hygienic milk handling, proper hand washing, inadequate cleaning of milk utensils and containers, the type of container used for milk storage and transport (e.g., plastic, metal), and personal hygiene may contribute to differences in contamination levels [22, 48, 58].

In humans, the 2.9% prevalence of E. coli O157:H7 detected in patients with diarrhea was comparable to reports of 1.14% by Kargar and Homayoon (2015) [59], 2.8% by Gutema et al. (2021) [5], and 4.61% by Shrivastava et al. (2017) [60]. However, it is lower than findings such as 15.3% by Getaneh et al. (2021) [7], 33.6% by Brandal et al. (2015) [61], 39.3% by Darbandi et al. (2016) [62], and 58.8% by Rivero et al. (2010) [63]. These differences might be attributed to variations in sample size, study population (e.g., age of individuals), levels of personal hygiene, sampling methods, and detection techniques [7]. The presence of the organism in stool samples from diarrheic patients may be linked to close contact between animals and humans and the consumption of raw dairy products in the study area, highlighting its role as a significant diarrheal pathogen [63]. However, our study is too limited to establish exposure or a causal relationship.

Another concerning aspect of this study is the high level of antimicrobial resistance observed among E. coli O157:H7 isolates from various sources. All tested isolates exhibited resistance to tetracycline, consistent with previous reports indicating resistance rates between 81% and 100% [46, 49, 64]. However, our results exceed those documented in other studies, which reported resistance rates ranging from 33 to 59.3% in Ethiopia and other regions [24, 43, 65]. The study also found a 95% resistance rate to amoxicillin, aligning with findings by Ranjbar et al. (2018) [49] and Reuben and Owuna (2014) [64], who reported resistance levels from 84.2 to 100%. Nonetheless, these figures are significantly higher than the 50% resistance reported by Hamid et al. (2018) [66] and the 40% reported by Engidaw et al. (2022) [44]. The 90% resistance against ampicillin observed in the present study is consistent with findings of 100% resistance by Dejene et al. (2022) [24], 100% by Mesele et al. (2023) [20], and 92.8% by Haile et al. (2022) [23], but higher than the 56.25% reported by Hamid et al. (2018) [66]. Additionally, the study revealed a 90% resistance rate against streptomycin, which is comparable to the findings of 85.7% by Bedasa et al. (2018) [67], 81.8% by Disassa et al. (2017) [53], and 70.37% by Dejene et al. (2022) [24]. This resistance is higher than the 52% reported by Msolo et al. (2016) [48] and the 50% reported by Alam et al. (2017) [46].

In the current study, 85% of the tested isolates were susceptible to chloramphenicol, which is comparable to reports of 100% susceptibility by Mesele et al. (2023) [20] and Gudisa et al. (2022) [65]. However, this result is higher than the findings of Msolo et al. (2016) [48], Reuben and Owuna (2014) [64], and Ababu et al. (2020) [43]. In the current study, 85% of the tested isolates were susceptible to ciprofloxacin, which aligns with findings of 100% susceptibility by Dejene et al. [24], Mesele et al. [20], Haile et al. (2022) [23], and Ababu et al. (2020) [43]. Nonetheless, this is higher than the results of 72% susceptibility reported by Engidaw et al. (2022) [44] and 66.67% by Alam et al. (2017) [46]. The 80% susceptibility of the tested isolates to ceftriaxone in this study is comparable to the findings of 100% by Haile et al. (2022) [23] and 96% by Engidaw et al. (2022) [44]. The observed variations in antimicrobial resistance may be linked to differences in the extent of drug use in the respective localities, as well as the improper or indiscriminate application of antibiotics for treatment and prevention purposes [20, 67].

The 100% multidrug resistance observed in the present study is consistent with the findings of 100% resistance reported by Edilu et al. (2023), Dejene et al. (2022), and Mesele et al. (2023) [20, 22, 24]. This finding is also comparable to the 92.5% resistance reported by Bedasa et al. (2018) [67], followed by 75% by Hamid et al. (2018) [66]. However, it is higher than the resistance levels reported by other studies conducted by Haile et al. (2022) [23], Ranjbar et al. (2018) [49], and Hiko et al. (2008) [68]. The differences in the development of multidrug resistance among bacteria may be attributed to factors such as variations in dosage, routes of administration, testing methods, and the indiscriminate use of antimicrobials for treatment and feed additives across different study areas, as well as disparities in awareness levels [24, 45]. The observed universal resistance of isolates to several important antibiotics including doxycycline, erythromycin, tetracycline, clindamycin, and vancomycin and resistance across multiple drug classes is particularly alarming and aligns with findings from several other recent Ethiopian studies. This study is limited by its sample size and study area. Additionally, resource constraints prevented sequencing of isolates to assess their genetic relatedness across different sample sources.

Conclusion

The study revealed a lower detection rate but a higher prevalence of multidrug resistant E. coli O157:H7 in the milk supply chain, milking environments, and in humans with diarrheal illness, suggesting the spread and transmission of the pathogen in the study area. In general, a higher detection rate of E. coli O157:H7 was observed in the milking environment and along the milk supply chain compared to milk drawn directly from the udder and in diarrheic patients. Public education campaigns are essential to raise awareness about the potential risks of consuming raw milk and the importance of strict hygiene measures, such as hand washing and teat cleaning before milking, to reduce milk contamination. More importantly, advocating for the rational use of antimicrobials in both animals and humans is necessary to decrease the risk of exposure to resistant E. coli O157:H7. Future studies should focus on quantifying the risk associated with consuming milk contaminated with E. coli O157:H7, analyzing the genetic relationships among isolates to identify transmission pathways and mediating factors between cattle and humans, and investigating antimicrobial usage practices at one health interfaces to inform future interventions aimed at ensuring milk safety in the study area.
