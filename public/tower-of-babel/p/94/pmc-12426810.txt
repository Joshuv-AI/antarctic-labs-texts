Long‐Term Safety and Clinical Outcomes With Olipudase Alfa Enzyme Replacement Therapy in Children and Adolescents With Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency
Authors: Maurizio Scarpa, George A. Diaz, Roberto Giugliani, Simon A. Jones, Eugen Mengel, Nathalie Guffon, Peter Witters, Jaya Ganesh, Nicole M. Armstrong, Shruti Srivastava, Yong Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcid sphingomyelinase deficiency (ASMD) is a rare lysosomal storage disease characterized by hepatosplenomegaly, pulmonary dysfunction, dyslipidemia, and growth deficits. Olipudase alfa (recombinant‐human ASM) is the only treatment for non‐central‐nervous‐system ASMD manifestations in children and adults. An open‐label long‐term study followed 4 to 8 years of olipudase alfa treatment in 20 children and adolescents with ASMD (baseline age (years) 1.5–17.5). At final assessments, splenomegaly and hepatomegaly were reduced relative to baseline (mean percent decrease in spleen and liver volumes 78% ± 1.2% and 59.8% ± 1.5%, respectively). Baseline splenomegaly was severe (spleen volume > 15 MN [multiples of normal]) or moderate (5–15 MN) (n = 12 and n = 8, respectively) versus mild/absent (< 5 MN) (n = 12) or moderate (n = 8) at final assessment. Baseline hepatomegaly was severe (liver volume > 2.5 MN) (n = 10) or moderate (1.25–2.5 MN) (n = 10) versus mild/absent (< 1.25 MN) (n = 19) or moderate (n = 1) at final assessment. Among nine individuals able to perform assessments, diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) impairment was severe (< 40%) (n = 1), moderate (40%–60%) (n = 4), or mild (60%–80%) (n = 4) at baseline versus absent (n = 4), mild (n = 4) or moderate (n = 1) at final assessment. Mean percent increase in DLCO was 53.7% ± 6.5%. At baseline, 10/20 children had clinical short stature (height Z‐scores ≤ − 2) at baseline versus 0/20 at the final assessment. Atherogenic lipid profiles and liver function tests normalized within 2 years and remained stable. Adverse events were mostly mild or moderate, with 4 individuals experiencing 7 serious adverse events, all recovered/resolved. Interpretation: Enzyme replacement therapy with olipudase alfa in children and adolescents with chronic ASMD was well‐tolerated with clinically meaningful improvements in multiple disease parameters. Trial Registration: NCT02004704.

Introduction

Olipudase alfa (Xenpozyme) is a recombinant human enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) indicated for the non‐central nervous system manifestations of acid sphingomyelinase deficiency (ASMD) in children and adults [1]. It is the first disease‐specific treatment for this rare, inherited lysosomal storage disease. In an international study of the diagnostic odyssey for individuals with ASMD, 61% of children or adolescents (n = 165) first presented to pediatricians with ASMD symptoms [2]. ASMD is an underrecognized rare, progressive disease with delays in diagnosis that impede access to early treatment.

ASMD arises from pathogenic mutations in the SPMPD1 gene with resulting deficient activity of the enzyme acid sphingomyelinase (ASM). Without sufficient functional ASM, the primary substrate sphingomyelin accumulates in a broad range of cells including macrophages, hepatocytes, and, in the most severe cases, neurons [3]. Clinical manifestations reflect the wide number of affected organ systems, and include splenomegaly, interstitial lung disease (ILD), and hepatomegaly accompanied by liver dysfunction with progressive fibrosis, thrombocytopenia, bleeding/bruising, dyslipidemia, growth deficits and delayed puberty, osteoporosis/osteopenia, and, in the most severe subtype, neurodegeneration [4, 5].

The phenotypic spectrum of ASMD is broadly delineated into three subtypes that are based on age of onset, rate of progression, and extent of neurologic involvement. ASMD type A (OMIM257200), also known as infantile neurovisceral ASMD and historically known as Niemann‐Pick disease type A (NPD A), is the most severe subtype and is characterized by rapidly progressive visceral and neurologic disease and death within the first 3 years of life. ASMD type B (chronic visceral ASMD; NPD B; ASMD (OMIM607616)) follows a protracted, multi‐systemic course with onset in infancy and childhood, minimal or absent neurodegeneration, and age at death ranging from childhood to late adulthood. Intermediate ASMD type A/B, (chronic neurovisceral ASMD; NPD A/B) shares similarities with ASMD type B but often with earlier onset and slowly progressing neurological symptoms. ASMD types B and A/B, often referred to jointly as chronic ASMD, are associated with significant morbidity and disease burden, including liver, pulmonary, and skeletal disease that increase over time [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Prospective and retrospective studies in chronic ASMD have identified early mortality [10, 12, 13] primarily due to respiratory or liver failure [10, 12, 13, 14] in a significant proportion of children and adolescents.

ERT with olipudase alfa reduces sphingomyelin accumulation with resulting improvements in lung function, hepatosplenomegaly, lipid profiles, platelet counts, growth in children, and other clinical manifestations in individuals with chronic ASMD [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Olipudase alfa is well‐tolerated in children [15, 16] and adults [17, 18, 19, 20, 21] with the use of a dose escalation regimen designed to gradually debulk tissue sphingomyelin and minimize adverse events arising from rapid production of high concentrations of bioactive catabolites [22, 23]. An open‐label single‐arm study in children ranging in age from infancy to adolescence with chronic ASMD demonstrated improvements across a range of endpoints after 1 year of olipudase alfa that were further improved or maintained during year 2 when children entered a long‐term study [15, 16]. This paper reports on the long‐term safety and clinical outcomes for this pediatric population who received olipudase alfa infusions for at least 4 years.

Methods

Study Design/Participants

The study population consisted of 20 individuals (< 18 years of age at baseline) with confirmed ASMD, spleen volume ≥ 5 multiples of normal (MN), and height Z‐score ≤ −1 who participated in an open‐label, single‐arm trial (ASCEND‐Peds/NCT02292654) [15]. All had either ASMD type B or A/B. After the 52‐week (efficacy)/64‐week (safety) primary analysis periods, participants were eligible to continue treatment in a long‐term study (NCT02004704/EudraCT2013‐000051‐40). Institutional Review Boards at study sites approved the protocol, and written consent was provided by participants/parents.

Olipudase Alfa Infusions

Olipudase alfa infusions were administered every 2 weeks. Dose escalation was conducted over a minimum of 16 weeks in the ASCEND‐Peds trial to reach the target maintenance dose of 3 mg/kg as described previously [15]. Both investigational site and home infusions were permitted for eligible participants. Home infusion was permitted from the first protocol amendment for participants on stable doses of olipudase alfa that were well tolerated. The investigator had to agree that home infusions were appropriate. Home infusion infrastructure, resources, and procedures were to be established and available according to applicable regional regulations. Compliance with treatment was monitored by determining the percentage of scheduled infusions received.

Outcomes

Biomarkers reflecting ASMD disease (plasma chitotriosidase activity [normalized, μmol/L/h] and plasma lyso‐sphingomyelin level [μg/L]) were evaluated as previously described [15]. Plasma chitotriosidase activity was normalized based on CHIT1 genotype. Those homozygous for the null mutation (one participant) were excluded from analysis, and values for heterozygous individuals were doubled (nine participants). Clinical parameters included spleen volume and liver volume in multiples of normal (MN, where normal spleen and liver volumes are considered to be 0.2% and 2.5% of body weight, respectively [24]), the percent predicted diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide [DLCO] adjusted for hemoglobin, volumetric lung function tests, high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT) lung imaging scores, platelet counts, liver function, plasma lipid profiles, and height Z‐scores.

Adverse events were summarized by incidence, seriousness, severity, and relationship to olipudase alfa treatment, including infusion‐associated reactions (IARs).

Analyses

Analyses were performed for the overall population. Summary statistics (means and standard deviations) were used for continuous variables and for concentration‐time data. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percents. Change and percent change from baseline to final assessment were analyzed with the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) method adjusting for baseline value for efficacy outcomes. A linear regression model was used to assess change from baseline for HRCT scores adjusted by baseline HRCT scores. Ninety‐five percent confidence intervals (CI) and p‐values are presented for the least square (LS) mean ± SE change/percent change from baseline to final assessment. p‐values are nominal.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

All 20 participants who completed the primary analysis period of the ASCEND‐Peds trial (first enrolled May 2015‐last enrolled September 2018) enrolled in and completed the long‐term study (last visit September 2023) (Figure 1). Time on olipudase alfa ranged from 4.3 to 8.2 years (mean 6.1 ± 1.3 years) due to a staggered trial enrollment period over 3 years that sequentially enrolled participants in three cohorts beginning with Adolescent (12 ≤ 18 years, n = 4), Child (6 ≤ 12 years, n = 9), and Infant/early child (< 6 years, n = 7) groups [15]. Older participants had the longest olipudase alfa exposures. Because most participants moved from one age category to another during the course of the trial, aggregate data are presented for the full population and not by baseline age group.

Disposition of trial participants.

Baseline characteristics have been previously described [15] and are summarized in Additional File 1, Table S1. Age at symptom onset ranged from 1.9 months to 3.9 years, and presenting symptoms at disease onset included splenomegaly (100%), hepatomegaly (100%), respiratory diseases (35%), thrombocytopenia (25%) and excessive bleeding and bruising (10%). Age at trial enrollment ranged from 1 to 17 years. Males and females were equally represented, and the majority of participants were Caucasian.

Treatment Compliance and Missed Infusions

The median time (min, max) to achieve the target dose of 3 mg/kg during the primary ASCEND‐Peds 64‐week study was 18 weeks [15] and during the second year of treatment, the target dose was maintained with a temporary dose reduction in one individual [16]. The mean compliance with treatment was 96% and ranged from 87% to 100%. Four participants missed two or more consecutive infusions, and five missed four or more infusions within any 12‐month period. Missed infusions occurred due to COVID‐19 disease or restrictions, adverse events, unsuccessful IV attempts, lack of nursing availability, lack of documentation from the home care nurse, and personal issues. COVID‐19 pandemic restrictions, including travel‐related and laboratory closures, interfered with study assessments for 7 (35%) individuals. Treatment compliance and overall olipudase alfa exposure were similar in COVID‐19 impacted and non‐impacted participants. A total of 14 of 20 participants (70%) received home infusions during the study.

Efficacy Outcomes

Disease Biomarkers

Both plasma lyso‐sphingomyelin, the deacylated form of sphingomyelin, and plasma chitotriosidase activity, a biomarker of activated macrophages, were elevated at baseline in all individuals (mean values > 60 and > 6 times the upper limit of normal [ULN], respectively) and decreased within the first 6 months of treatment [15, 16]. Lyso‐sphingomyelin levels stabilized by 9 months (mean ± SD value 86.7 ± 76.2 μg/L; ULN 10 μg/L) (Additional File 1, Figure S1A). Plasma chitotriosidase activity continued to decrease (improve) with treatment and was within the normal range by 33 months (mean ± SD activity 156 ± 92 μmol/L/h; ULN 180 μmol/L/h) (Additional File 1, Figure S1B).

Spleen and Liver Volume and Related Assessments

The mean splenomegaly and hepatomegaly values were classified [24] as severe at baseline and improved in all participants, with the largest magnitude of improvement occurring during the first 6 months of treatment [15] followed by further incremental improvement or stabilization (Figure 2A,C mean ± SD spleen and liver volumes over time, respectively).

Mean ± SD spleen volume improved from 19.0 ± 8.8 MN (severe splenomegaly) at baseline to 4.6 ± 1.8 MN (mild splenomegaly) at final assessment (mean time on olipudase alfa 5.7 ± 1.5 years). Individual spleen volumes over time are shown in Additional File 1, Figure S2.

A similar pattern was observed for improvement in hepatomegaly. Mean ± SD liver volume decreased from 2.7 ± 0.7 MN at baseline (severe hepatomegaly) to 1.0 ± 0.2 MN (no hepatomegaly) at the final assessment (mean time on olipudase alfa 5.7 ± 1.5 years). Individual liver volumes at baseline and final assessments are shown in Figure 2D. Hepatomegaly was absent or mild at the final assessment in all but one individual who had severe hepatomegaly at baseline and moderate hepatomegaly at the final assessment. Individual decreases from baseline at the final assessments ranged from 41.7% to 78.6%, with a LS mean ± SE percent difference of −59.8% ± 1.5% (p < 0.0001; 95% CI: −62.9, −56.7). Individual liver volumes over time are shown in Additional File 1, Figure S2.

Baseline mean ± SD platelet counts indicated mild thrombocytopenia (137.7 ± 62.3 × 109/L) and 15/20 (75%) children had platelet values below the thrombocytopenia threshold of 150 × 109/L. At final assessments, 7/20 (35%) children had platelet values below the thrombocytopenia threshold, and the mean ± SD platelet count was 182.5 ± 70.6 × 109/L. The LS mean ± SE percent change from baseline at final assessment was 38.2% ± 7.0% (p < 0.0001; 95% CI: 23.5, 52.9).

Elevated baseline transaminase levels reflected liver pathology and normalized following olipudase alfa treatment as previously reported [15, 16]. Mean values for AST and ALT that normalized by 6 to 12 months, respectively [15], remained within normal ranges with continued olipudase alfa treatment (Additional File 1, Figure S3).

Pulmonary Disease

Percent predicted DLCO data over time were available for nine individuals with baseline data (10 were not old enough to perform the test at baseline and one adolescent missed the baseline assessment). Mean ± SD percent predicted DLCO improved from 54.8% ± 14.2% at baseline (moderate impairment) to 78.3% ± 14.7% at final assessment (mild impairment; mean time on olipudase alfa 6.2 ± 1.6 years) (Figure 3A). Individual percent predicted DLCO at baseline and final assessment are shown in Figure 3B. At baseline, most individuals had mild (n = 4) or moderate (n = 4) impairment, and one individual had severe impairment. At final assessment, DLCO values were normal in four individuals, mild in four, and one individual had moderate impairment. The percent change from baseline at final assessment ranged from −12% to 122%, and the LS mean ± SE percent change from baseline was 53.7% ± 6.5% (p < 0.0001; 95% CI: 38.2, 69.2). Individual percent predicted DLCO over time for all trial participants, including those without baseline values, is shown in Additional File 1, Figure S2.

Plasma Lipoprotein Profiles

Improvements in plasma lipoprotein profiles reported in the first 2 years of treatment [15, 16] were maintained or further improved through the end of the study. Mean values for atherogenic lipids (total cholesterol, triglycerides, low density lipoprotein cholesterol) normalized by 6 months of initiating olipudase alfa and remained normal with continued treatment (Figure 4A–C). The non‐atherogenic factor high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) was below the LLN (45 mg/dL) in all children, improved through 2 years of treatment as previously reported [15, 16], and normalized with continued olipudase alfa treatment (Figure 4D). Mean ± SD HDL‐C improved from 16.9 ± 6.1 mg/dL at baseline to 45.4 ± 7.4 mg/dL at final assessment, with a LS mean ± SE percent change from baseline of 206% ± 13% (p < 0.0001; 95% CI: 178, 234).

Growth

Individual height Z‐scores over time are shown in Figure 5. Baseline height Z‐scores ranged from −3.8 to −1.0 with a median of −2.0. Ten children (50%) met the criteria for short stature (i.e., height 2 or more standard deviations below the mean for age [26]) with Z‐scores of −2 or less. No child met the criteria for short stature at the final assessment (Figure 5). Mean height Z‐score of −2.14 ± 0.84 at baseline improved to −0.37 ± 0.62 at the final assessment. The LS mean ± SE percent change from baseline was 82.5% ± 8.1% at the final assessment (p < 0.0001; 95% CI: −99.551, −65.493). The baseline mean ± SD difference between bone age and actual age was 24.3 ± 18.4 months. Among 12 individuals < 18 to 19 years at 48 months, this gap decreased to 6.4 ± 19.5 months (Additional File 1, Figure S4). Four out of 5 individuals who became adults during the course of the study reached complete skeletal maturity.

Safety

Olipudase alfa showed a favorable long‐term safety profile and was well tolerated. There were no permanent treatment discontinuations or withdrawals from the study. Treatment‐emergent adverse events occurring during the primary 64‐week safety period and during the second year of treatment have been described in detail previously [15, 16], and no new safety issues arose in the remaining treatment period. Among 1794 treatment‐emergent adverse events occurring from first dose through end of study, 99.4% (1783/1794) were reported as mild (1588/1794, 88%) or moderate (195/1794, 11%), and 0.6% (11/1794) as severe (Table 1).

Considered hypersensitivity IAR.

Among the 1794 treatment‐emergent adverse events, 164 (9%) were considered potentially related to olipudase alfa treatment. As reported during the primary 64‐week safety period [15] the most common related adverse events were pyrexia, urticaria, vomiting, and headache, most of which were mild infusion‐associated reactions (IARs) (Table 1). The number of IARs and the number of individuals with IARs peaked from 3 to 6 months of initiating treatment and subsequently decreased over time, as shown in the inset to Table 1. All IAR events were categorized as mild or moderate in severity, with the exception of an anaphylactic reaction in a participant in the early childhood/infant group during the second month of treatment, as previously reported [15]. This child underwent a tailored desensitization regimen and was able to resume treatment [15]. There were no acute phase reactions or cytokine release syndrome events during the long‐term study.

Overall, four individuals had serious adverse events related to olipudase alfa, all of which occurred in the first (n = 3) or second (n = 1) year of treatment and which have been described previously [15, 16]. All olipudase‐alfa‐related serious adverse events were considered IARs or hypersensitivity IARs and included an individual with two events of increased alanine aminotransferase, an individual with one event each of urticaria and rash, and an individual with an anaphylactic reaction during dose escalation. One individual experienced two hypersensitivity events during the second year of treatment.

Treatment‐induced anti‐drug antibodies (ADA) developed in 14 participants, 12 of whom had persistent responses. The median peak ADA titer was 200, and no individual had a high ADA response (considered as a titer > 12,800 based on general experience with other replacement enzymes in these assays). No participant developed neutralizing antibodies that inhibited cellular uptake. There was no impact of ADA response on efficacy as determined by improvements in spleen volume and platelet counts.

Discussion

ASMD disease burden and mortality risk increase over time in untreated children and adolescents with ASMD type B and A/B [6, 7, 9, 10]. The present findings demonstrate that olipudase alfa substantially improves the disease burden in children with chronic forms of ASMD from infancy through adolescence. Improvements in clinical parameters observed as early as 6 months after initiating treatment were maintained or amplified with long‐term treatment, often resulting in normalization of disease manifestations. The clinical improvements are mirrored by early and sustained decreases in the levels of disease markers lyso‐sphingomyelin and chitotriosidase to normal or near normal values.

Moderate to severe organomegaly [9, 10, 27] occurs early in life in children with chronic ASMD and does not resolve or improve over time [9, 10, 27]. Enlarged abdomens, nausea and vomiting, and abdominal pain are significant symptoms contributing to reduced appetite and sleep, and overall quality of life in children with ASMD [28]. Nutritional and developmental deficits can result from decreased satiety in children due to enlarged livers and spleens [24, 29]. In addition, splenomegaly can require lifestyle modifications to reduce the risk of splenic rupture [30] or increased bleeding [27]. All children in the long‐term study had reductions from baseline in spleen and liver volumes of greater than 50% by 4 years of treatment, and treatment extension resulted in further incremental improvements and normalization of liver volumes in most cases. While significantly improved, normalized spleen volumes may not be expected with baseline moderate and severe splenomegaly due to irreversible fibrosis that may occur at an early age, as is observed in Gaucher disease [24], which emphasizes the need for early treatment in children with ASMD.

Respiratory symptoms persist or worsen over time in untreated children with ASMD [9, 31]. ILD and lung function deterioration are associated with increased risk of respiratory infections and respiratory failure [9, 14] and contribute to decreased quality of life and early mortality [8, 10, 31]. Olipudase alfa treatment improved diffusing capacity of the lung to mild or no impairment in children able to perform the test and this outcome was supported by decreases in mean scores for ground glass appearance and ILD, indicative of no or mild disease.

Dyslipidemia [6, 9] and thrombocytopenia [9, 31] worsen over time in children with chronic ASMD and are of concern due to the potential impact on the risk of cardiac disease and hemorrhage, respectively. Lipid profiles normalized, and platelet counts reflected no thrombocytopenia in the majority of individuals. Liver transaminase elevations indicate ASMD‐associated liver pathology in untreated children [9, 27]. Elevated transaminase levels normalized beginning in the first few weeks of olipudase alfa treatment [15] and remained within normal limits throughout the long‐term study.

Children and adolescents with chronic ASMD frequently have growth delays [9, 32] although normal stature is often observed in association with homozygosity for the SMPD1 variant p.Arg610del [32, 33]. All of the children in this study had growth deficits, as height Z‐score ≤ −1 was a trial entry criterion, and none were homozygous for p.Arg610del [15]. Short stature (height Z‐score ≤ 2) from any cause is associated with poorer quality of life in children and adults [34]. Growth delays are a significant cause of concern for caregivers of children with chronic ASMD [28]. Improvements in Z‐scores occurred in all participants, and no child had short stature at the final assessment. Catch‐up growth was paralleled by catch‐up bone maturation, reflected in a narrowing gap between bone age and actual age.

Olipudase alfa ERT was well tolerated with long‐term treatment. All 20 participants completed the trial without permanent treatment discontinuations. In addition, treatment compliance, even when families were faced with COVID‐related restrictions, remained very high throughout the study, in part due to the availability of olipudase alfa home infusions. Home‐based ERT therapy for several metabolic disorders was found to increase satisfaction with treatment as well as improve treatment adherence [35, 36]. Treatment interruptions and missed assessments occurring due to COVID‐19 travel restrictions, laboratory closures, or other pandemic‐related issues resulted in less data for some endpoints, particularly pulmonary measurements. Most treatment‐related events were mild to moderate IARs that occurred primarily in the first 6 months of treatment and became less frequent over time, which is a common observation with ERTs [37]. The IAR events occurring with olipudase alfa infusions were managed in some cases with temporary infusion interruptions or dose reductions that did not prevent maintaining or re‐establishing the target olipudase alfa dose.

Conclusion

ERT with olipudase alfa remained well‐tolerated with long‐term treatment in children and adolescents with chronic ASMD type B or A/B. Treatment ranging from 4 to 8 years was associated with normalization and/or stabilization of disease manifestations across multiple clinically meaningful endpoints. Thus, olipudase alfa can significantly reduce the disease burden in the pediatric ASMD population.
