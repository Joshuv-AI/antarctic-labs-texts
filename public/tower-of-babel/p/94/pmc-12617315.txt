Prognostic Value of A/T/N Biomarkers
Authors: Sohyun Yim, Yuna Gu, Heekyung Kang, Daeun Shin, Hyemin Jang, Heejin Yoo, Henrik Zetterberg, Kaj Blennow, Fernando Gonzalez-Ortiz, Nicholas J. Ashton, Michael W Weiner, Theresa A. Day, Eun Hye Lee, Duk L. Na, Hee Jin Kim, Sung Hoon Kang, Ko Woon Kim, Yeo Jin Kim, Yeshin Kim, Jaeho Kim, Min Young Chun, Na Yeon Jung, Soo Hyun Cho, Jun Pyo Kim, Jihwan Yun, Sang Won Seo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Alzheimer disease (AD) is characterized by amyloid-β plaques (A), tau tangles (T), and neurodegeneration (N), collectively defining the ATN framework. While imaging biomarkers are well-established, the prognostic value of plasma biomarkers in predicting cognitive decline remains underexplored. This study compares plasma and imaging A/T/N biomarkers in predicting cognitive decline and evaluate the impact of combining biomarkers across modalities.Patients and Methods:We conducted a longitudinal study using K-ROAD cohort participants who underwent at least 2 cognitive assessments. All participants had plasma biomarker testing (Aβ ratio, p-tau181, p-tau231, p-tau217, NfL), and a subset with imaging biomarker assessments (Aβ PET, tau PET, structural MRI) formed an imaging subcohort. Multiple linear regression models identified the most predictive markers within each modality and evaluated the effect of combining A/T/N biomarkers.Results:Among 1,614 plasma cohort and 130 imaging subcohort participants, tau markers demonstrated the strongest predictive value. p-tau217MSD outperforming other plasma biomarkers, and the neo-temporal ROI showing the highest predictive power among imaging biomarkers. In plasma-based model, adding neurodegeneration markers to combination of amyloid and tau biomarkers improved the performance. In imaging-based models, same strategy decreased the performance, suggesting that combinations of amyloid and tau PET captures the most relevant prognostic information.Conclusions:Imaging biomarkers, particularly tau PET, show superior prognostic accuracy compared with plasma biomarkers, whereas plasma biomarkers offer advantages in combination models through neurodegeneration markers. These findings underscore the complementary roles of plasma and imaging biomarkers and emphasize the need for tailored strategies for prognostic modeling in AD.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer disease (AD) is characterized by a progressive neurodegenerative process underpinned by 3 key pathologic features:1,2 amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques (A), tau tangles (T), and neurodegeneration (N), collectively referred to as the ATN framework.3,4 Advances in in vivo biomarker detection have led to the development of imaging markers such as Aβ PET,5–7 tau PET,7–9 and structural MRI,10,11 which allow for detailed visualization of these pathologic changes in the brain. Within this framework, amyloid pathology (A) is assessed through Aβ PET imaging, tau pathology (T) through tau PET imaging, and neurodegeneration (N) through structural MRI measures such as cortical thickness and hippocampal volume that reflect brain atrophy. More recently, plasma biomarkers, including Aβ ratio,12–14 phosphorylated tau (p-tau),15–17 and neurofilament light chain (NfL),18–20 have emerged as minimally invasive tools for detecting these pathologies. These plasma biomarkers offer a minimally invasive alternative to imaging markers by capturing systemic indicators of ATN pathology. Both plasma and imaging biomarkers contribute to biological staging for AD, providing a framework for understanding disease progression and developing targeted interventions.3,21

Plasma biomarkers have shown strong correlations with imaging markers, particularly amyloid and tau PET, and are effective at accurately predicting in vivo ATN pathology.12–14,22–26 However, while plasma biomarkers are well-suited for detecting underlying pathologies, their ability to predict disease progression and cognitive decline remains less well understood. Given that cognitive decline in AD is closely linked to downstream processes such as tau-related neurodegeneration,27,28 markers that directly reflect these changes are likely to be the most predictive.29–31 This gap in knowledge underscores the need to evaluate the prognostic value of plasma biomarkers compared with imaging markers.

Despite the promise of plasma biomarkers, they differ from imaging biomarkers in several critical ways. Imaging biomarkers, such as amyloid and tau PET, directly measure ATN pathology within the brain, offering precise regional and topographical information crucial for understanding disease progression.9,31–35 In contrast, plasma biomarkers reflect systemic measurements that may be influenced by factors such as body mass index (BMI),36–38 kidney function,38–41 and other metabolic or vascular conditions,38 potentially diluting their specificity for brain pathology. Plasma biomarkers also generally exhibit smaller effect sizes compared with imaging biomarkers, which may limit their standalone utility for predicting cognitive decline.42–45 However, combining multiple biomarkers across the ATN framework has shown potential for improving predictive performance, particularly in plasma-based models where the integration of tau with amyloid or neurodegeneration markers may capture complementary aspects of pathology.46–48 By contrast, imaging biomarkers, particularly tau PET, may already encompass key aspects of disease pathology, limiting the incremental value of additional markers.45,49 These differences underscore the importance of directly comparing plasma and imaging biomarkers and exploring how integrating ATN markers influences their utility in predicting cognitive decline.

In the present study, we compared the utility of plasma and imaging markers from the A/T/N categories in predicting prognosis. First, we examined which ATN markers within plasma and imaging modalities are most predictive of cognitive decline. Second, we evaluated the relative prognostic utility of plasma and imaging biomarkers. Third, we assessed how combining ATN markers influences predictive performance across modalities.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Participants Selection

This study included 1614 individuals from the Korea-Registries to Overcome Dementia and Accelerate Dementia Research (K-ROAD) cohort, who had at least 2 Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) follow-ups. K-ROAD is a genotype–phenotype cohort established to accelerate the development of innovative diagnostic and therapeutic techniques for neurodegenerative diseases, primarily AD and related dementia syndromes, in collaboration with 25 nationwide university-affiliated hospitals in South Korea between 2016 and 2024.50 The participants comprised cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals, those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and those with dementia of Alzheimer type (DAT). The detailed diagnostic criteria and processes are described in Supplementary Method 1 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CNM/A576).

The Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (No. 2021-02-135) approved this study. All participants provided informed consent to participate in the study, and the data were collected in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Plasma Collection and Processing

The detailed plasma collection and processing methods are described in Table 1 and Supplementary Method 2 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CNM/A576). Plasma Aβ40, Aβ42, and NfL concentrations were measured using the commercial Neurology 4-Plex E kit (Quanterix). Plasma p-tau181 and p-tau231 concentrations were measured using in-house Simoa (SIngle MOlecule Array) assays developed at the University of Gothenburg, and p-tau217 concentration was measured using a commercial ALZpath p-tau217 assay kit. The measurements were performed by analysts blinded to the clinical data from one round of experiments using one batch of reagents. The intra-assay coefficients of variation of the biomarker assays were <10%. In addition, we obtained another p-tau217 value using immunoassay on a Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) platform by Lilly Research Laboratories.

PET Imaging Acquisition

A total of 1561 participants underwent Aβ PET imaging using either F-18-florbetaben (FBB) or F-18-flutemetamol (FMM), following the manufacturer’s imaging guidelines. The detailed guidelines are described in Table 1 and Supplementary Method 3 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CNM/A576).

A subset of 130 individuals underwent tau PET scanning, using F-18-flortaucipir (FTP) per standard imaging procedures. The detailed methods are described in Table 1 and Supplementary Method 4 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CNM/A576).

Brain MRI Acquisition

Three-dimensional (3D) T1 turbo field echo images were acquired using the 3.0T MRI scanner (Philips 3.0T Achieva; Philips Healthcare)51 with the following imaging parameters: sagittal slice thickness, 1.0 mm with 50% overlap; no gap; repetition time, 9.9 ms; echo time, 4.6 ms; flip angle, 8° and matrix size, 240×240 pixels reconstructed to 480×480 over a field view of 240 mm. All images were centralized at the Samsung Medical Center.

Amyloid PET Analyses

Aβ uptake was measured using Klunk Centiloid (klunk CL) scale, which has been increasingly used in various cohort studies and clinical trials. Aβ PET positivity (A+) was defined using cutoff value of 20.0 for the study analysis. This threshold was selected to maintain methodological consistency with our previous work52–54 and is also supported by prior studies that have adopted 20 CL as a threshold for early amyloid positivity in preclinical and observational cohorts.55 All imaging analyses for the K-ROAD study were conducted at the laboratory of Samsung Medical Center, which served as a core center. Quantitative Analysis Methods for Tau PET

To calculate Tau PET standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs), we employed FreeSurfer without Partial Volume Correction (PVC) approaches to analyzing tau deposition in the brain. FreeSurfer 6.0 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) was used to generate region of interest (ROI) masks in native space, with the inferior cerebellar grey matter as a baseline as the reference region. The method without PVC was included to provide a baseline for comparison, reflecting the variability commonly observed in PET analyses.

SUVR values were calculated for 4 key brain regions: the medial temporal lobe (MTL) ROI, which included the entorhinal cortex and amygdala, the meta-temporal (meta) ROI, which encompasses the entorhinal cortex, amygdala, fusiform gyrus, parahippocampal gyrus, inferior temporal cortex, and lingual gyrus, the neo-temporal (NEO) ROI, which includes the inferior and middle temporal cortices, and the temporoparietal (TP) ROI which included the inferior, middle, and superior temporal cortices, as well as the superior parietal gyrus, inferolateral parietal lobe, and posterior cingulate gyrus. These regions were selected to capture both early and advanced stages of tau pathology, ensuring comprehensive assessment across the AD continuum. Representative images showing negative case without tau deposition and a positive case with tau deposition across neocortex are presented in Figure 1.

Measurement of Cortical Thickness and HV

The images were processed using the CIVET anatomic pipeline (version 2.1.0).56 The native MR images were registered to the Montreal Neurological Institute’s 152 brain template through a linear transformation57 and corrected for intensity nonuniformities using the N3 algorithm.58 The registered and corrected images were separated into white matter, gray matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and background. Furthermore, the Marching-cubes algorithm was used to extract the inner and outer surfaces of the cortex to determine the cortical thickness (Cth) defined as the Euclidean distance between the linked vertices of the inner and outer surfaces.59

The total volume of the voxels within the skull-stripped brain mask was measured to determine the intracranial volume (ICV). The cortical thickness was measured in the native space by applying an inverse transformation matrix to the cortical surface and reconstructing them in native space, as the cortical surface models were extracted from MRI volumes transformed into stereotaxic space.60

Surface-based two-dimensional registration was performed using a sphere-to-sphere warping algorithm. The cortical thickness values were normalized spatially to compare the thickness of the corresponding registration algorithm with that of an unbiased iterative group template with enhanced anatomic detail61 to transform the thickness information for the vertices into an unbiased iterative group template. Each map of cortical thickness was blurred through surface-based diffusion smoothing with a full width at half-maximum of 20 mm, to increase the signal-to-noise ratio and statistical power.60,62

An automated hippocampus segmentation method, which combined a graph cut algorithm with an atlas-based segmentation and morphologic opening, was used to determine HV as described in a previous study.63 The automated segmentation was performed by trained researchers with extensive experience in neuroimaging analysis. Representative images showing a case with markedly reduced cortical thickness and a case with marked hippocampal atrophy are presented in Figure 1.

Longitudinal Follow-up

A total of 1614 participants who underwent at least 2 MMSE assessments were included, with a median follow-up of 2.06 years (IQR: 1.25–3.12 y).64 In the plasma biomarker cohort, both retrospective and prospective MMSE scores were collected at the time of blood sampling. Similarly, 130 participants in the imaging biomarker subcohort who underwent at least 2 MMSE assessments were included in the longitudinal MMSE analysis, with a median follow-up of 3.12 years (IQR: 2.18–4.31 y). In this subcohort, retrospective and prospective MMSE scores were obtained relative to the timing of tau PET scans.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were performed after log transformation due to the non-normality of the data. Multiple linear regression models were constructed with MMSE change as the outcome variable and each plasma or imaging biomarker as the predictor, while controlling for age, sex, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status, and years of education. From these models, the proportion of variance explained by each predictor (R2) was calculated to evaluate its contribution to cognitive decline. All tests were 2-tailed, and P <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.1 (http://www.R-project.org/).

RESULTS

Study Participants

The study included 1614 participants from the plasma biomarker cohort in K-ROAD, with a median age of 73.0 years, 62.8% females, and 44.5% APOE ε4 carriers. In the imaging marker subcohort (N = 130), participants had a median age of 73.5 years, 60.8% were females, and 51.5% were APOE ε4 carriers. Aβ amyloid PET positivity was 61.2% in the plasma cohort and 83.7% in the imaging subcohort, with tau PET meta-temporal positivity at 62.0% in the latter. Model Fit and Performance of A/T/N Biomarker Categories

In the plasma biomarker cohort, multiple regression models demonstrated variable adjusted R2 values (Fig. 4). For amyloid, ln Aβ 42/40 was 0.018. Among tau biomarkers, ln_ptau181, ln_ptau231, ln_ptau217Alzpath, and ln_ptau217MSD yielded adjusted R2 values of 0.064, 0.069, 0.121, and 0.159, respectively, with ln_ptau217MSD demonstrating the strongest performance. The neurodegeneration marker (ln_NfL) exhibited an adjusted R2 of 0.087, while the neuroinflammation marker (ln_GFAP) reached 0.100.

In the imaging markers subcohort, the amyloid measure (klunk CL) demonstrated an adjusted R2 of 0.155. Among the tau markers (MTL, Meta, NEO, TP), the adjusted R2 values ranged from 0.191 to 0.354, with NEO demonstrating the most optimal performance. For neurodegeneration, MTA and cortical thickness (Cth) reached 0.185 and 0.230, respectively, with Cth exhibiting a superior model fit.

Optimizing Predictive Models Using A/T/N Biomarker Combinations

In the plasma biomarker cohort, the top-performing biomarkers from each A/T/N category, based on R2, were ln_Aβ 42/40 (amyloid), ln_ptau217MSD (tau), and ln_NfL (neurodegeneration). Their combinations yielded adjusted R2 values of 0.159 for AT (ln_Aβ 42/40 + ln_ptau217MSD), 0.091 for AN (ln_Aβ 42/40 + ln_NfL), and 0.175 for ATN (all 3).

In the imaging markers subcohort, klunk CL was selected for amyloid, NEO for tau, and cortical thickness (Cth) for neurodegeneration. The adjusted R2 values were 0.361 for AT (klunk CL + NEO), 0.284 for AN (klunk CL + Cth), and 0.357 for ATN (klunk CL + NEO + Cth).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we compared the utility of plasma and imaging markers from the A/T/N categories in predicting prognosis to identify optimal combinations for assessing disease progression. Our findings revealed 3 key points: First, tau markers showed the strongest predictive value in both modalities, with ptau217MSD leading among plasma markers and the uptakes in the neo-temporal ROI among imaging markers. Second, imaging biomarkers outperformed plasma biomarkers in prognostic accuracy across A/T/N categories. Finally, combining A/T/N markers had differing effects: in plasma, adding N markers to combinations of these biomarkers include AT improved the adjusted R2, whereas in imaging, adding N markers to combinations of these biomarkers include AT reduced the adjusted R2. Taken together, our findings suggest that imaging biomarkers, particularly tau imaging, have superior prognostic utility compared with plasma biomarkers across A/T/N categories. While plasma biomarkers benefit from the integration of multiple markers, including A/T markers, imaging biomarkers rely primarily on A/T markers, highlighting the differing dynamics between these modalities and the need for tailored approaches to optimize prognostic models in AD.

Our first major finding was that tau markers showed the strongest predictive value across both plasma and imaging modalities, reflecting their critical role in AD progression. Pathophysiologically, tau pathology emerges later in the disease course than amyloid65–67 and is more directly linked to cognitive impairment.27,28 While amyloid accumulation marks earlier stages,68 tau deposition more accurately reflects neuronal injury and clinical symptoms,69,70 making tau-based biomarkers particularly robust predictors of cognitive decline. Although neurodegeneration markers likely represent an even later stage of pathology,68 tau markers outperform them in predicting prognosis because tau directly drives neurodegeneration through neurofibrillary tangle formation and exerts additional effects through synaptic dysfunction,28,71,72 mitochondrial disruption,72,73 and neuroinflammation.74,75 Furthermore, studies have shown that neurodegeneration markers, such as NfL and cortical thickness, partially mediate the relationship between tau pathology and cognitive decline, suggesting their secondary role in capturing disease progression.20,76,77

We also found that ptau217MSD was the leading plasma marker, likely due to its strong associations with tau pathology. As is known, p-tau epitopes may correlate with amyloid and tau PET uptakes independently, as proposed by the intersection hypothesis, with different epitopes showing varying degrees of association.26,28 In our previous studies, ptau217MSD demonstrated stronger correlations with tau PET uptake than with amyloid PET uptake, highlighting its specificity for tau pathology and its value as a sensitive indicator of pathologic tau changes. On the imaging side, the neo-temporal ROI emerged as the strongest predictor of cognitive decline.78 This region, which include the inferior and middle temporal cortices, is affected in later Braak stages and reflects advanced tau pathology.31,79 These neocortical areas, strongly linked to progressive cognitive impairment,31 make the neo-temporal ROI a particularly relevant choice for tau PET imaging in assessing disease progression and prognostic accuracy.

Our second major finding was that imaging biomarkers outperformed plasma biomarkers in predicting prognosis across the A/T/N categories. This difference likely reflects the distinct nature of these markers: imaging biomarkers, such as tau PET, directly measure brain pathology and capture the regional burden of amyloid and tau linked to cognitive decline.9,31–35 In contrast, plasma biomarkers provide a peripheral snapshot and are influenced by systemic factors such as BMI, kidney function, and other metabolic or vascular conditions, which may dilute their specificity for brain pathology.80 In addition, imaging biomarkers uniquely detect insoluble tau aggregates in specific regions like the neo-temporal ROI, which are strongly associated with disease progression,81 whereas plasma biomarkers primarily detect soluble forms occurring earlier in the disease process.28,82 These differences in temporal dynamics, specificity, and susceptibility to systemic variability likely explain the superior prognostic accuracy of imaging biomarkers.

Our final major finding was that combining A/T/N markers had differing effects: in plasma, adding N markers to combinations of these biomarkers include AT improved adjusted R2, while in imaging, it reduced adjusted R2. In plasma-based models, neurodegeneration markers provided additional explanatory power, capturing aspects of neuronal injury not fully reflected by amyloid and tau. In contrast, imaging-based models showed diminished adjusted R2 with added N markers, likely because amyloid and tau imaging already reflects both tau pathology and neuronal injury, leaving little variance for other markers to explain. These findings align with previous studies showing that plasma neurodegeneration biomarkers offer complementary information when tau alone is insufficient,69,83,84 while imaging biomarkers, particularly tau PET, inherently capture advanced pathologic changes.85 This highlights the importance of modality-specific strategies for integrating A/T/N biomarkers, as their added value depends on the measurement modality.

Our study has several methodological strengths, including a large and diverse patient cohort and the integration of both plasma and imaging markers across the A/T/N categories, allowing for a comprehensive evaluation of their prognostic utility. However, there are some limitations to consider. First, while cognitive changes were assessed longitudinally, plasma and imaging biomarkers were measured cross-sectionally, which limits our ability to establish causal relationships between biomarkers and disease progression. Second, while we analyzed multiple plasma and imaging markers, the relatively small size of the imaging subcohort may have constrained statistical power for certain analyses. Third, systemic factors such as BMI or kidney function, which can influence plasma biomarker levels, were not fully accounted for in our models. Fourth, FDG PET was not incorporated in this study due to concerns regarding participant burden, as amyloid and tau PET imaging had already been conducted. Although FDG PET is sensitive in detecting early metabolic alterations,86 we assessed neurodegeneration using structural MRI. Consequently, this methodological choice may limit direct comparisons with studies employing metabolic imaging modalities. Finally, although a head-to-head comparison of plasma and imaging biomarkers was conducted as a sensitivity analysis, yielding consistent results, the study design prevents full generalizability to broader populations. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into the complementary roles of plasma and imaging biomarkers and highlights the need for modality-specific strategies in prognostic modeling, making it a meaningful contribution to AD research.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, our study highlights the complementary roles of plasma and imaging biomarkers in predicting prognosis for AD, with tau markers demonstrating the strongest predictive value. Imaging biomarkers, particularly tau PET, exhibited superior prognostic accuracy, while plasma biomarkers offered unique advantages when multiple biomarker categories were integrated. These findings emphasize the need for tailored strategies in biomarker integration to improve personalized disease management.
