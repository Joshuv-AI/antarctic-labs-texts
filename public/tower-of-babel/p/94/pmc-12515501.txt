Emerging roles for innate and adaptive immunity in tauopathies
Authors: Alexis M. Johnson, John R. Lukens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYTauopathies encompass a large majority of dementia diagnoses and are characterized by toxic neuronal or glial inclusions of the microtubule-associated protein tau. Tau has a high propensity to induce prion-like spreading throughout the brain via a variety of mechanisms, making tauopathy a rapid and lethal form of neurodegeneration that currently lacks an effective therapy or cure. Tau aggregation and neuronal loss associated with this pathology are accompanied by robust neuroinflammation. Innate immune responses—particularly those involving microglial activation, altered lipid metabolism, and type I interferon signaling—have emerged as key drivers of tau hyperphosphorylation and aggregation. Recent advances also point to a significant role for the adaptive immune system in shaping tauopathy progression. This review examines the current understanding of innate immunity in tauopathies and highlights emerging evidence linking T cell responses to tauopathy progression. Last, we conclude with a discussion of potential ways in which the immune system can be harnessed to treat tauopathy.

INTRODUCTION

Tauopathies are a class of neurodegenerative diseases characterized by the abnormal intracellular accumulation of tau protein. These include Alzheimer’s disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration, chronic traumatic encephalopathy, and others. In these diseases, tau aggregates act in a prion-like fashion, spreading across brain regions and driving neurodegeneration.1 Regarding homeostatic function, tau exists as a microtubule-associated protein that aids in the maintenance of neuronal architecture and intracellular transport. However, under cellular stress, inflammation, or dysregulated signaling conditions, tau undergoes post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation and acetylation. Excessive post-translational modifications of tau result in its misfolding and aggregation into oligomers, filaments, and tangles.2 These aggregated tau species disrupt neuronal function and viability, ultimately contributing to subsequent cognitive decline and brain atrophy.3

While the pathological role of tau has been well studied, the contribution of the immune system to tau-mediated neurodegeneration is gaining traction as a pivotal driver of disease.4 Once thought to be immune privileged, the central nervous system (CNS) is now understood to host dynamic immune activity through resident macrophages, such as microglia, and interactions with peripheral immune cells.5 Unchecked inflammation is well established as detrimental to neuronal health, and a growing body of work has begun to reveal the molecular mechanisms by which immune activation contributes to tau pathology. This review aims to synthesize current findings on how innate and adaptive immune responses shape tau-driven neurodegeneration and highlight emerging therapeutic opportunities to target these immunological mechanisms.

Innate immune responses that influence tauopathies

The innate immune system serves as the body’s first line of defense against infection and cellular damage. Unlike the adaptive immune system, which mounts specific responses tailored to individual antigens, innate immunity relies on evolutionarily conserved receptors to rapidly detect general danger signals, such as microbial components or signs of cellular stress. In the CNS, microglia are the primary innate immune cells and play key roles in maintaining tissue homeostasis. These cells are equipped with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and inflammasomes, which initiate inflammatory signaling cascades.6 While these responses are essential for protecting the brain from acute challenges, persistent or dysregulated innate immune activation can lead to chronic neuroinflammation and contribute to neurodegenerative disease.

In the context of tauopathies, growing evidence implicates several innate immune pathways as key drivers of disease onset and progression. Among the most studied are complement signaling, TLR activation, and inflammasome-mediated responses, all of which have been shown to promote inflammatory environments that exacerbate tau pathology (Figures 1A–1C). Emerging data also suggest that innate immune-based DNA damage-sensing pathways, such as the cGAS-STING axis and its regulation of downstream interferon (IFN) responses, are involved in amplifying neuroinflammation in tau-mediated neurodegeneration (Figure 1D). Additionally, recent findings indicate that disrupted immunometabolism and lipid processing within innate immune cells may further fuel pathogenic inflammatory responses in primary tauopathies (Figure 1E). In the sections that follow, we explore these mechanisms in greater detail and highlight how innate immune dysregulation intersects with tau pathology.

Complement cascade

The complement system is a proteolytic cascade that plays a central role in immune defense by tagging, opsonizing, and lysing cellular threats. All three complement activation pathways—classical, lectin, and alternative—converge at the formation of C3 convertase, ultimately leading to the assembly of the membrane attack complex, which can directly lyse target cells. In addition to its cytolytic function, complement activation also facilitates opsonization and promotes immune cell recruitment via the generation of chemoattractant fragments such as C3a and C5a.7 In the CNS, microglia and astrocytes serve as both key sources and targets of complement proteins. While cerebral complement signaling was once thought to be primarily activated in response to infection and injury, it is now recognized as a dynamic regulator of brain homeostasis as well as sterile pathological processes.8 For instance, during development, complement components such as C1q and C3 facilitate the elimination of excess synapses.9,10 This is a critical pruning mechanism that, when dysregulated, contributes to synaptic loss in neurodegenerative diseases.10,11

In tauopathies, dysregulated complement activity is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of disease pathology. Elevated expression of C1q, a key initiator of the classical complement cascade, has been observed in both symptomatic and presymptomatic individuals with tauopathies as well as in multiple tauopathy mouse models. This suggests that complement activation may precede overt neurodegeneration and contribute to disease progression (Figure 1A).12,13 Genetic deletion of C1q in the PS19 mouse model of tauopathy has been shown to attenuate synapse loss and neurodegeneration, supporting a pathological role for this complement molecule in FTD.14 However, it should be noted that follow-up investigation by the same group added nuance to the notion that C1q inhibition is uniformly beneficial. Here, they showed that, although chronic deletion provided protective effects, acute treatment with C1q-blocking antibodies failed to restore synaptic function or prevent neuronal loss even when administered at early symptomatic stages (Figure 1A).14,15 These conflicting results suggest that timing, disease stage, and therapeutic strategy (i.e., genetic vs. pharmacologic) may critically influence disease outcomes when targeting C1q in tauopathy. Moreover, these findings highlight the nuanced role of complement in tauopathy and the challenges of developing effective therapies targeting this pathway.

In addition to C1q, C3 signaling has also emerged as a central contributor to tau pathology. Genetic deletion of C3 or its receptor C3aR has been shown to reduce neuroinflammation and ameliorate neurodegenerative phenotypes in tauopathy models.16 Furthermore, in vitro studies have identified the complement receptor CR4 on microglia as a receptor for tau fibrils, mediating their internalization and potentially linking complement signaling to tau clearance mechanisms (Figure 1A).17 Despite these insights, direct in vivo evidence for CR4-mediated tau clearance remains absent. Therefore, future studies are needed to evaluate whether CR4-targeting strategies can enhance tau removal and protect neuronal function in tauopathy models.18

Inflammasome signaling

Inflammasomes are multiprotein signaling complexes that coordinate the production of the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18, and also execute a gasdermin D (GSDMD)-driven form of cell death known as pyroptosis. Inflammasomes typically consist of an innate immune sensor molecule such as NLRP3 or AIM2, the adaptor protein ASC, and caspase-1. Formation of this signaling platform positions caspase-1 in the correct orientation to allow for its auto-cleavage and subsequent activation. Canonical inflammasome activation follows a two-step process: a priming signal, typically via nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) activation, which induces the expression of inflammasome components (e.g., NLRP3) and pro-cytokines. The secondary signal, often triggered by pathogen- or damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs or DAMPs, respectively), activates the inflammasome sensor, which instructs complex assembly and cleavage-induced caspase-1 activation.19 Once activated, caspase-1 then proceeds to cleave pro-IL-1β, pro-IL-18, and full-length GSDMD, which elicits their biological activation. In the context of tauopathies, internalization of pathologically phosphorylated tau (p-tau) by microglia has been shown to trigger NLRP3 inflammasome activation.20

Once released, IL-1β and IL-18 amplify inflammatory signaling cascades that can indirectly promote further tau pathology. Specifically, these cytokines activate their respective receptors on glia and neurons, leading to downstream signaling through the MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase) and JNK (c-Jun N-terminal kinase) pathways, both of which are known to hyperphosphorylate tau. Indeed, in vitro studies indicate that IL-1β and IL-18 can upregulate the activity of several tau kinases, including GSK3β, CDK5, and p38 MAPK (Figure 1B).21–24 Furthermore, elevated in vivo expression of IL-1β in tauopathy mouse models has been associated with increased kinase activity and exacerbated tau hyperphosphorylation,25 which suggests a feedforward loop that links inflammasome activation with progressive tau pathology.

Earlier studies using tauopathy mouse models (i.e., PS19 and Thy-Tau22 mice) found that genetic deletion of inflammasome components such as Nlrp3 or Asc reduced tau aggregation and improved cognitive outcomes (Figure 1B).26–28 These findings support a model in which inflammasome activation in microglia contributes to tau pathology, likely through the production of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β). Conversely, more recent work using the Thy1-hTau.P301S model, which exhibits a different regional pattern of tau pathology, did not observe appreciable changes in disease progression or cytokine levels following genetic deletion of Nlrp3 or Gsdmd in microglia (Figure 1B).29 These conflicting results suggest that inflammasome-mediated pathology in tauopathies may be context dependent. Most notably, differences in the discrete tauopathy mouse models employed in these studies could help to explain these divergent results. More specifically, differences in promoter systems (e.g., Prnp in PS19 vs. Thy1 in hTau.P301S), tau isoforms expressed (e.g., hTau mice expressing all six isoforms vs. 0N4R in Thy1-hTau.P301S), and regional vulnerability (hippocampal vs. midbrain) may all influence the overall impact inflammasome deletion has on tauopathy disease progression. Nevertheless, future studies are needed to further reconcile these disparate results regarding the involvement of inflammasome signaling in tauopathies. Further efforts to decipher the optimal targeting of inflammasome signaling in tauopathies will lead to improved understanding of disease etiology and the development of more effective treatment strategies.

TLR signaling

TLRs are PRRs that detect DAMPs and PAMPs, initiating immune responses via NF-κB, MAPK, and IFN signaling.30 In tauopathies, TLR activation promotes neuroinflammation and exacerbates tau pathology by upregulating tau kinases such as CDK5, MAPK, GSK3β, and JNK, ultimately driving tau hyperphosphorylation and aggregation (Figure 1C). Indeed, in vitro studies using SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells demonstrated that TLR3 stimulation leads to enhanced phosphorylation of tau through downstream signaling cascades, implicating TLR3 in the modulation of tau kinases.31 Likewise, TLR9 activation induced by stimulating primary hippocampal neurons under high-glucose conditions was similarly shown to promote hyperphosphorylation of tau.32 Later research extended these findings by showing that extracellular pathogenic tau can directly activate TLR4-dependent inflammatory cascades, further amplifying neuroinflammation and tauopathy progression in vivo (Figure 1C).33 These studies underscore the relevance of distinct TLR pathways in promoting pathological tau modifications through kinase activation.

To add further complexity to the innate immune responses discussed thus far, complement, TLR, and inflammasome pathways do not act in isolation but form an interconnected network that amplifies neuroinflammation and tau pathology. For instance, TLR4 activation by extracellular tau can prime inflammasome components and promote the release of IL-1β and IL-18,33 which, in turn, upregulate tau kinases such as GSK3β and CDK5 in neurons.21–25 In parallel, IL-1β and complement-mediated signaling increase neuronal oxidative stress, leading to neuronal phosphatidylserine exposure and phagoptosis by microglia, another point of convergence between TLR and inflammasome pathways.33–35 Complement receptor CR4 also facilitates microglial uptake of tau fibrils, potentially acting in concert with TLR-driven responses.17 Despite these emerging links, the coordination and hierarchy of these interactions remain poorly understood. Future studies should explore how these pathways intersect over time and across brain regions and whether targeting common regulatory nodes, such as NF-κB signaling or oxidative stress, might yield more effective therapeutic strategies than interventions focused on isolated innate immune pathways.

DNA damage sensing by the innate immune system

Protein aggregates, like tau-based neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), are known to induce damage to both the nucleus and mitochondria, which can lead to the subsequent accumulation of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and genomic DNA damage as tauopathies progress.36 This mislocalization of mtDNA and genomic DNA into the cytosol can then trigger innate immune DNA-sensing pathways such as cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) stimulator of interferon genes (STING) and the AIM2 inflammasome. These pathways have been increasingly implicated in many neurological conditions.37 However, as the tauopathy field stands, cGAS-STING is the predominant DNA damage immune pathway implicated.

cGAS is one of many sensors capable of detecting cytoplasmic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA). The source of this dsDNA can vary, originating from exogenous pathogens, such as viruses or bacteria, or from endogenous sources, like mtDNA or genomic DNA damage.38 Upon activation, cGAS catalyzes the production of the second messenger cyclic guanosine monophosphate-AMP, which then initiates STING activation at the endoplasmic reticulum. STING activation promotes phosphorylation and binding with TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and IFN regulatory factor 3, facilitating their nuclear translocation to IFN response elements and NF-κB target sites. The resulting transcriptional events enhance type I IFN- and NF-κB-mediated cytokine production and can promote cell death in some instances.39

During aging, independent of any major pathology, levels of type I IFNs rise (Figure 2).40 Chronic exposure to type I IFN alone is sufficient to induce cognitive decline and accelerate aging-related phenotypes in mice.41 Within the CNS, microglia are key responders to IFN signaling, and under prolonged IFN stimulation, they adopt an altered, maladaptive activation state.41 A compelling illustration of the impact of dysregulated IFN signaling comes from studies of microglia derived from induced pluripotent stem cells of individuals with Down syndrome, where trisomy of type I IFN receptor genes results in hypersensitivity to IFN. These Down syndrome-derived microglia exhibit excessive synaptic pruning in brain organoids, a phenotype reversed by interferon-α/β receptor (IFNAR) knockdown and absent in wild-type controls.42 Recent work further supports this model, showing that sustained IFN signaling in the aged brain primes microglia toward a chronic inflammatory phenotype that accelerates neurodegeneration, even without classic proteinopathies.43 These findings suggest that type I IFNs disrupt microglial homeostasis and sensitize the CNS environment, lowering the threshold for harmful immune responses to tau and other pathological stimuli.

Both human tauopathy samples and PS19 mouse models exhibit upregulated cGAS expression and activity38 as well as notable increases in type I IFN responses.44 Genetic deletion of cGAS or pharmacological inhibition of STING was further shown to dampen pathological hallmarks of tau-mediated disease, suggesting that cGAS and STING signaling are drivers of tauopathy pathogenesis (Figure 1D).38 It is important to note that recent reports also suggest that polyglutamine binding protein 1 (PQBP1) is another driver of cGAS-STING-mediated neuroinflammation in tauopathies. In this scenario, it has been proposed that intracellular tau inclusions can bind PQBP1 and lead to cGAS-STING activation.45

How dysregulated cGAS-STING activation contributes to tauopathy progression has not yet been conclusively established; however, multiple potential mechanisms have been proposed. As described above, one possibility is that cGAS-STING-mediated induction of type I IFN production is responsible for downstream potentiation of neuroinflammation and tauopathy. Consistent with this idea, genetic ablation of the receptor that coordinates type I IFN signaling, IFNAR, in P301S tauopathy mice has been shown to markedly attenuate neuroinflammation and tau pathology.44

One notable downstream consequence of exaggerated type I IFN responses in tauopathy is the marked suppression of myocyte enhancer factor 2C (MEF2C), a transcription factor that plays critical roles in both microglial and neuronal homeostasis (Figure 2). In microglia, MEF2C supports a homeostatic, anti-inflammatory state by repressing pro-inflammatory gene expression and promoting functions such as synaptic maintenance and debris clearance.38,41 In one study, loss of microglial MEF2C was sufficient to induce inflammatory and aging-associated phenotypes, independent of underlying neuropathology. These MEF2C-deficient microglia were also hyperresponsive to acute inflammatory challenge, such as lipopolysaccharide injection.41 In neurons, MEF2C governs gene expression essential for differentiation, synaptic maturation, and the maintenance of proper network activity.46 Recent evidence shows that activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and heightened type I IFN signaling contribute to MEF2C downregulation in neurons within models of tauopathy (Figure 1D).38 Inhibition or deletion of cGAS or STING restored MEF2C expression, which was associated with reduced synaptic loss and partial rescue of aberrant neuronal firing (Figure 2).38 These findings indicate that chronic cGAS-STING signaling disrupts MEF2C-dependent regulatory programs. This can subsequently lead to exacerbated microglial dysfunction via loss of immune restraint and impaired neuronal circuit function through dysregulated transcription.

Lipid metabolism-based modulation of innate immunity

Aging is accompanied by widespread metabolic and immune changes that contribute to cognitive decline and neurodegenerative diseases. As cellular metabolic flexibility declines, disruptions in immunometabolism, particularly in lipid processing and energy utilization, drive chronic neuroinflammation and neuronal dysfunction.47,48 Microglia and astrocytes are especially sensitive to these shifts. Both cell types exhibit alterations in glycolysis, mitochondrial function, and lipid metabolism with age, leading to lipid droplet accumulation, oxidative stress, and impaired phagocytic clearance of toxic protein aggregates.49 One notable population, lipid droplet-accumulating microglia, has been identified in the aging brain and displays dysfunctional innate immune features, including impaired phagocytosis, elevated ROS production, and a pro-inflammatory transcriptional profile (Figure 1E).49,50 In tauopathy models, microglial lipid droplet accumulation is a hallmark feature that is associated with impaired phagocytosis, elevated oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation.51 However, recent studies suggest that, in tauopathy, a substantial proportion of the accumulated lipid may reside not in classical cytoplasmic lipid droplets but rather within the endolysosomal system.52,53 This distinction is important, as endolysosomal lipid buildup may reflect defects in lipid degradation or trafficking, which could implicate a role for lysosomal dysfunction rather than lipid storage per se. These findings highlight the need to better delineate the subcellular localization and functional consequences of lipid accumulation in disease-relevant microglial states.

Similarly, recent work demonstrates that astrocyte lipid accumulation drives a shift toward a reactive, pro-inflammatory state, mediated by excessive STAT3 signaling. This underscores the metabolic sensitivity of astrocytes and their emerging role as key modulators of innate immune dysfunction in aging and neurodegeneration.54 Together, these observations reinforce the importance of cellular lipid metabolism in regulating neuroimmune health and suggest that both the source and compartmentalization of lipid accumulation may play distinct roles in disease progression.

Systemic metabolic disorders, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2D), have been increasingly linked to heightened risk for neurodegenerative diseases, including AD and tauopathies, in part by amplifying CNS innate immune activation through several converging mechanisms. In T2D, chronic hyperglycemia and systemic insulin resistance result in a pro-inflammatory state characterized by elevated levels of circulating cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor alpha [TNF-α] and IL-6) and free fatty acids. These inflammatory mediators can either cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) directly or act indirectly by activating endothelial cells, perivascular macrophages, and astrocytes, leading to the release of secondary messengers, which can then activate microglia.55 In addition, hyperglycemia itself has been shown to increase oxidative stress and advanced glycation end product (AGE) formation, which can activate PRRs, such as receptor for AGEs, on microglia and further promote inflammatory responses. Insulin resistance within the CNS may also impair insulin signaling in neurons and glia, disrupting energy homeostasis and enhancing susceptibility to neuroinflammation.56 Together, these mechanisms are thought to sensitize innate immune pathways, leading to exaggerated microglial activation, neuronal stress, and increased vulnerability to AD and related tauopathies.57,58

Notably, metabolic interventions, such as metformin-mediated mTORC1 inhibition and pharmacological activation of AMP-activated protein kinase, have been shown to recalibrate innate immune function by shifting microglial phenotypes toward a more homeostatic, anti-inflammatory state.51,59 In a tauopathy model (tau-seeded PS19 mice), metformin treatment attenuated tau pathology, suppressed inflammatory microglial activation, and rescued cognitive deficits without altering tau kinase activation states.59 This suggests that the therapeutic benefit may derive from modulating microglial metabolism and mTORC1 signaling rather than directly interfering with tau phosphorylation pathways.59 These findings underscore the strong intersection between aging, metabolic dysregulation, and innate immunity, highlighting immunometabolism as a promising therapeutic axis for tauopathies.

The well-established genetic link between apolipoprotein E (APOE) and tauopathy progression in humans provides further evidence of the important role aberrant lipid metabolism plays in these disorders. APOE is a lipid transport protein with three major isoforms in humans: APOE2, APOE3, and APOE4. In addition to its role in lipid transport, APOE isoforms can modulate immune responses and cellular metabolism, particularly in microglia. Among the isoforms, APOE4 is the greatest genetic risk factor for late-onset AD, where it is associated with increased β-amyloid (Aβ) deposition, tau pathology, and neuroinflammation. In patients with FTD, APOE4 carriers exhibit greater neuronal atrophy compared to non-carriers, suggesting a more severe disease course even in primary tauopathies.60 Understanding how APOE variants, especially APOE4, shape microglial biology and innate immunity is critical for elucidating their contributions to tau-mediated neurodegeneration.61

A growing body of research highlights how systemic APOE4 expression drives neuroinflammation and exacerbates tau pathology through disruptions in lipid metabolism and immune signaling (Figure 1E). In both PS19/APOE4 mice and human APOE4 carriers, impaired lipid handling contributes to tau hyperphosphorylation, synaptic dysfunction, and neurodegeneration.52,62 One key mechanism involves dysregulated cholesterol synthesis and trafficking, leading to the accumulation of lipid droplets in brain-resident immune cells.63 This lipid sequestration, though initially protective, ultimately impairs autophagy and lysosomal clearance pathways. Notably, reducing cholesterol biosynthesis in APOE4-expressing microglia restores autophagic flux, limits lipid droplet buildup, and mitigates tau-associated neurodegeneration and aberrant pro-inflammatory cytokine release.64,65 Liver X receptors (LXRs), a family of nuclear receptors that act as lipid-sensing transcription factors, play a critical role in maintaining cholesterol homeostasis and modulating inflammation by regulating the expression of genes involved in cholesterol efflux and immune signaling, particularly in microglia and astrocytes. Therapeutic strategies aimed at rebalancing lipid metabolism, such as LXR agonists, have shown promise in restoring cholesterol balance, dampening inflammatory gene expression, and ameliorating tauopathy progression in PS19/APOE4 mice.52

More recently, studies have begun to dissect how cell-type-specific expression of APOE4 shapes disease progression, particularly through microglial dysfunction. Microglia isolated from young APOE4 homozygous mice exhibit heightened glycolysis, increased HIF1α signaling, and dysregulated glycerolipid metabolism compared to APOE3-expressing counterparts.66 These effects intensify with age or disease triggers, driving a disease-associated microglia-like phenotype marked by increased APOE expression and pro-inflammatory responses.66 Beyond metabolic reprogramming, APOE4 also interferes with key microglial checkpoint mechanisms. In the P301S tauopathy model, microglial APOE4 expression has been shown to impair the induction of protective microglial neurodegenerative (MGnD) programs via ITGB8-mediated transforming growth factor β signaling. Deletion of APOE4 in microglia restored neuroprotective MGnD gene expression and attenuated tau-related pathology, suggesting that APOE4-mediated checkpoint signaling may suppress beneficial microglial responses in tauopathy.67

Adding to the complexity, APOE4’s impact on microglial metabolism is further influenced by systemic immune alterations, including changes in the gut microbiota and modulation of TRPV1 signaling, both of which have been shown to affect neuroinflammation and microglial activation.68 These collective findings underscore the importance of microglial metabolism and APOE4 modulation in the pathophysiology of tauopathies, providing insights into potential therapeutic strategies targeting metabolic pathways to mitigate neurodegeneration.

Role for adaptive immunity in tauopathy

Adaptive immunity provides highly specific and long-lasting protection through the actions of lymphocytes, primarily B cells and T cells. T cells develop in the thymus and are broadly classified into CD4+ and CD8+ subsets, each with distinct functions. CD4+ T cells, often referred to as T helper cells, coordinate immune responses by secreting cytokines that activate other immune cells, including macrophages, B cells, and CD8+ T cells. CD8+ T cells, or “cytotoxic” T cells, directly kill infected, damaged, or abnormal cells. In the periphery, T cells are essential for pathogen clearance and immune regulation.6 Although the CNS was once considered largely immune privileged, it is now clear that T cells can access the CNS under both physiological and pathological conditions, where they may participate in immune surveillance or, conversely, contribute to neuroinflammatory damage.69

In recent years, increasing evidence has suggested a deleterious role for T cell responses in driving tau-mediated neurodegeneration. Numerous studies have confirmed T cell infiltration in human tauopathy postmortem brain samples and tauopathy mouse models, hinting at a role for these immune cells in disease progression.70 Single-nucleus RNA sequencing of Braak-staged human brain tissue has further revealed amplified T cell responses in the advanced stages of tauopathy, marked by increased intercellular communication with brain-resident cells.71 Postmortem analysis of FTD brains has shown infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the parenchyma near tau inclusions, which is distinct from healthy controls, where CD8+ cells remain confined to perivascular spaces.72 In people with FTD, the abundance of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in affected brain regions was shown to correlate with the density of p-tau, suggesting a potential role for adaptive immune responses in driving or exacerbating tau pathology.70

Experimental data from tauopathy murine models further confirm robust T cell infiltration in regions marked by neuronal loss, microgliosis, and tau aggregation. Hippocampal T cell accumulation positively correlated with heightened neuroinflammation and cognitive decline. These effects were mitigated by blocking T cell entry in the Thy-Tau22 tauopathy mouse model.70 Likewise, antibody-mediated depletion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in PS19-APOE4 mice was also found to significantly reduce brain atrophy and improve cognitive outcomes.73 In the PS19-APOE4 mouse model, it was found that microglia play an especially critical role in supporting cerebral T cell responses during tauopathy. In these studies, depletion of microglia with colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor inhibitor treatment effectively blunted T cell accumulation in the brain and limited neuropathology in PS19-APOE4 mice.73

Most studies to date exploring the roles of T cells in tauopathies have predominantly relied on global T cell depletion,70,73 and this has led to the emerging paradigm that T cells as a whole promote exacerbated tauopathy progression. However, the potential exists that certain subsets of T cells exhibiting immunoregulatory and suppressive properties, such as regulatory T (Treg) cells, may also be acting side by side in a neuroprotective fashion to counterbalance tauopathy-promoting inflammatory T cell responses. Interestingly, recent work has uncovered a potentially protective subset of CD8+ T cells in Thy1-hTau. P301S tauopathy mice that express granzyme K (GzmK) but not traditional effector molecules such as IFNγ or TNF. In these studies, they found that genetic ablation of CD8+ T cells in P301S+/+ mice leads to increased numbers of highly activated microglia and accelerated tau pathology spread. These findings led the authors of this study to conclude that these GzmK+ CD8+ T cells are targeting highly activated and pathogenic microglia for clearance while sparing neurons.74 However, additional studies that involve the specific deletion of GzmK+ CD8+ T cells are needed to conclusively test this. Taken together, these findings highlight the multifaceted and context-dependent roles of T cells in tauopathies. Future studies are warranted to further explore the roles of individual T cell subsets (e.g., Treg cells) and effector molecules (e.g., perforin and other cytolytic molecules) in disease progression.

T cell education, CNS homing, and local restimulation

Upon activation, T cells upregulate homing receptors that guide them to specific tissues, including the CNS, where they may establish residency. This migration is tightly regulated by chemokine-chemokine receptor interactions, which direct T cell recruitment to sites of inflammation. Once in the CNS, T cells respond to local cytokine and chemokine cues that help maintain their presence and potentially influence their functional phenotype. As we will discuss, specific cytokine and chemokine signatures in the brain may contribute to the maintenance of T cell-mediated inflammatory responses.

Several chemokine axes have been implicated in T cell recruitment to the CNS and may be relevant in tauopathies. Aging and AD are associated with an expansion of clonally persistent effector memory CD8+ T cells in the brain, many of which express CXCR6, facilitating their directed migration toward CXCL16-expressing microglia near sites of tau and amyloid pathology. This interaction supports long-term CD8+ T cell residency and has been shown to promote neuroinflammation and cognitive decline in amyloidosis mouse models and human AD brains (Figure 3).75,76 In parallel, the CXCR3 receptor, expressed primarily on Th1 cells, binds the IFN-inducible chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, which are produced by activated astrocytes and microglia. Thus, CXCR3 signaling facilitates the recruitment of pro-inflammatory T cells into the IFNγ-rich CNS environment and has been linked to immunopathology in models of neuroinfection and neuroinflammation.77 Other receptors, including CCR1, CCR2, CCR3, and CCR5, respond to ligands such as CCL2, CCL3, and CCL5, which are secreted by astrocytes, microglia, and endothelial cells under inflammatory conditions. CCR2 is critical for both monocyte and T cell infiltration in CNS diseases and contributes to hippocampal CD8+ T cell accumulation and memory impairment following viral encephalitis.78–80 Similarly, CCR3 has been implicated in age-related T cell infiltration and cognitive decline in murine models,81 and CCR1 signaling has been shown to modulate Th9/Th22 cell recruitment and activation in neuroinflammatory contexts.82 CCR5 has been shown to be upregulated in AD brains and promotes microglial activation and T cell trafficking.83–85 Together, these chemokine-receptor interactions modulate spatial and temporal dynamics of immune cell infiltration, effector polarization, and persistence within the CNS. While the role of CXCR6-CXCL16 has been characterized in AD models, the functional relevance of these other chemotactic cues in tauopathies remains underexplored and represents an important avenue for future research.

Potential for tau to be a cerebral antigen

Antigen processing and presentation are essential for T cell activation and the development of immune specificity. The proteasome degrades endogenous proteins within nucleated cells, and the resulting peptides are transported into the endoplasmic reticulum by the transporter associated with antigen processing 1 (TAP1). There, peptides are loaded onto major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules, stabilized by β2-microglobulin (B2m), and presented on the cell surface for CD8+ cytotoxic T cell recognition. In parallel, professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs), including dendritic cells, macrophages, and microglia, internalize extracellular proteins, process them in endolysosomal compartments, and present peptides on MHC class II molecules for CD4+ T cell recognition. These dual pathways allow the immune system to detect both intracellular and extracellular perturbations.86

MHC class I signaling appears to have detrimental effects on brain function in aging and neurodegeneration. For instance, B2m is elevated in the aging brain, including in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and hippocampal lysates, and is associated with impaired cognition and reduced adult neurogenesis.87 Direct hippocampal injection of B2m leads to decreased markers of neural progenitor cell proliferation, further supporting its negative role in neuroplasticity. Notably, these impairments are absent in Tap1 or B2m knockout mice, suggesting that MHC class I surface expression is necessary for the observed cognitive and neurogenic deficits.87 In contrast, certain MHC class II alleles are linked to decreased NFT burden, lower CSF tau levels, and later onset of AD.88 These variants show stronger predicted binding to acetylated tau peptides, suggesting enhanced immune recognition and potential clearance of these toxic tau species.88 It is also possible that such immune recognition could influence the balance of T cell responses, potentially favoring the induction of Treg cells, though this remains speculative and warrants further investigation. These data suggest that MHC class I may contribute to age-related cognitive decline, while certain MHC class II variants could provide resilience in tauopathies. These findings raise the possibility that age-related shifts in MHC class I and II expression may tip the balance between harmful neuroinflammation and protective immune surveillance. However, as we will discuss, the T cell immune responses in neurodegeneration are complex and will require further research to fully understand their role.

Several studies have utilized peptide restimulation assays with peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients and healthy donors to better understand the antigenic potential of tau in primary tauopathies. Interestingly, tau peptides elicit robust T cell responses in patients with Parkinson’s disease, aged individuals, and even young healthy donors.89 These responses were significantly greater than those to other CNS-derived antigens, such as amyloid precursor protein, Aβ, and α-synuclein.89,90 Clinical correlations between cerebral T cell infiltration and tau and amyloid burdens support the idea that it is p-tau accumulation rather than Aβ plaque pathology that drives CNS T cell residency in secondary tauopathies such as AD. This lack of correlation with Aβ further highlights the potential importance of tau-specific T cell responses in broader neurodegenerative disease contexts. This suggests that tau may possess unique antigenic features that allow it to evade central or peripheral tolerance mechanisms and provoke immune responses, even in the absence of overt pathology. However, future studies are needed to ascertain whether tau is an immunodominant antigen that drives disease-modifying T cell responses in tauopathies.

BBB breakdown and leakage of tau

One potential mechanism by which tau antigens reach the periphery is through altered CSF dynamics and compromised BBB integrity. Supporting this, PS19 tauopathy mice exhibit reduced CSF flow and increased BBB disruption, which may facilitate the peripheral leakage of tau (Figure 3).70 Tau aggregates can accumulate in brain endothelial cells via a heparin-sensitive mechanism, leading to microtubule destabilization and endothelial cell senescence, a state marked by stable cell-cycle arrest, pro-inflammatory secretory activity, and impaired vascular function.91 These senescent endothelial cells contribute to the age-related decline of vasodilation observed in PS19 mice, a phenotype that can be reversed by treatment with tau oligomer-specific monoclonal antibodies.91

Additional evidence of BBB dysfunction comes from various tau-overexpressing mouse models, which show abnormal vascular morphology, reduced vessel diameter, increased cortical vessel density, and upregulation of vasculature-associated genes such as VegfA, Serpine1, and Plau.92 These vascular changes are further supported by studies demonstrating cerebrovascular dysfunction in tauopathy models, including chronic cerebrovascular inflammation, pericyte degeneration, and compromised endothelial integrity.93 In these studies, BBB breakdown coincided with the appearance of tau inclusions within the vasculature. Excitingly, suppression of tau expression has been reported to recuperate BBB integrity, suggesting that stabilizing the BBB is achievable in tauopathy models by reducing tau levels.94 Collectively, these findings reinforce the view that BBB impairment is a central pathological feature in tauopathies.

The dual role of the BBB in tauopathy presents a therapeutic paradox. Chronic type I IFN signaling has been implicated in BBB disruption by downregulating tight junction proteins and promoting vascular leakiness.95 Therefore, targeting the heightened type I IFN signaling observed in tauopathies may offer a route to restore BBB integrity. Stabilizing the BBB could reduce neuroinflammation and peripheral immune cell infiltration, both of which contribute to disease progression. Conversely, strategically enhancing BBB permeability may facilitate the clearance of pathological tau or improve drug delivery. Peripheral clearance techniques, such as blood filtration and heterochronic parabiosis, have been shown to increase tau efflux from the CNS.96 Furthermore, scanning ultrasound has been successfully used in preclinical tauopathy models to transiently open the BBB and enhance the delivery of anti-tau therapeutics.97 These approaches underscore the importance of temporally and spatially controlled BBB modulation to achieve favorable therapeutic outcomes in tauopathies.

Possible antigen-presenting CNS cell populations

Neurons, like all nucleated cells, possess the machinery to present antigens on MHC class I to CD8+ T cells. In the context of tauopathies, neurons harbor the majority of intracellular pathogenic tau inclusions, raising the possibility that neuronal MHC class I presentation could contribute to tau-specific CD8+ T cell responses. Although direct evidence of neuronal MHC class I presentation of tau peptides is currently lacking, parallels can be drawn from other neurological diseases. For example, neuronal MHC class I upregulation has been observed in conditions such as tuberous sclerosis complex and ganglioglioma, suggesting disease-specific regulation of this pathway.98 Despite this potential, several lines of evidence indicate that neurons may not be the primary presenters of antigen to CD8+ T cells in vivo. In viral encephalitis models, such as vesicular stomatitis virus, infected neurons do not directly initiate MHC class I-restricted immune responses. Instead, microglia acquire viral antigens from the infected neurons and cross-present them to CD8+ T cells. Cross-presentation is a process by which professional APCs display exogenous antigens on MHC class I molecules, normally reserved for endogenous peptides. This antigen handoff mechanism could similarly apply to aggregated tau in tauopathies (Figure 3).99 Additionally, tauopathy models demonstrate dysregulation of immune signaling pathways, such as type I IFN signaling, which could influence neuronal immune function by modulating MHC class I expression and increasing neuronal immunological visibility.44 The role of MHC class I presentation by neurons in tauopathy remains a highly debated area in the field that warrants further investigation in future studies.

Microglia express both MHC class I and II, allowing them to present antigens to CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in the brain.99–101 However, whether they function as bona fide APCs is still debated. Unlike dendritic cells, which consistently express key costimulatory molecules like CD80 and CD86, microglia typically exhibit low expression of these molecules under normal conditions.102 While they can upregulate CD80 and CD86 in vitro or during inflammation,103 it remains unclear whether they do so effectively in vivo during tauopathy. This is an important point, given that full T cell activation requires both antigen presentation and costimulatory signals from an APC. In tauopathy models, subsets of microglia with high MHC class II expression, such as CD11c+ cells, have been linked to T cell expansion and disease progression, though direct evidence of microglia driving pathology through antigen presentation is still limited (Figure 3).72,104 In the P301S model of tauopathy, this CD11c+ MHC class II+ population appeared to be a deleterious state of microglial activation, as pharmacological depletion of microglia at symptom onset improved disease outcomes. Likewise, clearance of hyperactivated microglia by GzmK+ CD8+ T cells has similarly been proposed to limit tauopathy severity, suggesting that both intervention strategies may mitigate harmful microglial activity.74

Parenchymal border macrophages (PBMs), which line the brain-CSF interface, play a vital role in antigen sampling and presentation via MHC class II as well as solute clearance through the glymphatic system. In aging, PBMs exhibit reduced arterial motion and contribute to extracellular matrix and collagen accumulation. These changes impair brain perfusion and hinder the clearance of waste products from the CSF.105 Moreover, in tauopathy mouse models, PBMs upregulate MHC class II expression compared to wild-type controls, indicating a shift toward an inflammatory and antigen-presenting phenotype (Figure 3).106 Following the administration of clodronate liposomes into the CSF, PBMs are selectively depleted while the microglial compartment is preserved.105,106 Intriguingly, this selective depletion of PBMS with clodronate liposome treatment in PS19 mice exacerbated tau pathology and neurodegeneration.106 The worsening of tauopathy may reflect a failure of antigen or debris clearance from the parenchymal spaces rather than a loss of pathogenic antigen presentation, as PBMs may not be the sole neurotoxic APC population responsible for restimulating infiltrative T cells. Further studies are required to fully decipher the role that PBM antigen presentation plays independent of antigen clearance through the glymphatic system. Taken together, these findings may suggest that PBMs serve a protective, homeostatic role in tauopathy—one that may be more closely tied to maintaining waste clearance rather than driving maladaptive immune activation.

Aside from PBMs, peripheral immune cells, including infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages, are also increasingly considered as potential contributors to tauopathy-related neuroinflammation. These cells are robustly recruited to the brain in various neurological diseases.107 In tauopathy models, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated overexpression of the monocyte-recruiting chemokine CCL2 was shown to exacerbate disease pathology and enhance the accumulation of peripheral-derived CD45+ immune cell numbers in the brain.108 Once within the CNS parenchyma, infiltrating monocytes can differentiate into macrophages capable of presenting antigens and activating T cells.109 However, the extent to which monocyte-derived macrophages act as APCs in tauopathies has not been extensively explored to date and will be an important area of future investigation in the field. In total, these findings highlight a potentially vital role for peripheral-derived innate immune cells in shaping adaptive neuroimmune responses in primary tauopathies.

Other CNS-resident cell types, such as oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) and endothelial cells, are also increasingly recognized as APCs. In fact, recent studies have shown that OPCs can express MHCII and present antigen to CD4+ T cells.110,111 Similarly, brain endothelial cells are capable of upregulating MHCI and costimulatory molecules and can present antigen to CD8+ T cells in an MHCI–restricted manner.112 Although their specific roles in tauopathies remain to be fully elucidated, these findings broaden the spectrum of potential APCs within the CNS and underscore the importance of investigating how diverse cellular populations contribute to adaptive immune responses in neurodegenerative disease.

Taken together, these findings position T cells as multifaceted contributors to tauopathy pathogenesis. T cells are capable of interacting with dysfunctional microglia, responding to tau antigens, and amplifying local neuroinflammation through complex antigen presentation dynamics. The involvement of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, their parenchymal infiltration, and their apparent responses to tau-derived peptides suggest a breakdown in tolerance mechanisms and a pathological loop of immune activation within the CNS. However, much is still left unknown about the nature of the antigens being recognized, how these T cells are educated and recruited, and what sustains their presence and reactivation within tau-burdened regions of the brain. Expanding our understanding of these dynamics will be crucial for designing immunomodulatory interventions.

B cells–the next frontier in adaptive immunity tauopathy research

While T cells have received the bulk of attention in neurodegenerative contexts, B cells and their derived immunoglobulins are also gaining recognition as key players in CNS immune responses.113 B cells can contribute to neurodegeneration through multiple mechanisms: producing pathogenic autoantibodies, presenting antigens to T cells, and secreting pro- or anti-inflammatory cytokines.6 In AD, B cells have been detected in the meninges, choroid plexus, and perivascular spaces.114 B cells are a heterogeneous population of infiltrating cells that have been implicated in both protective and pathogenic roles in AD. These effects are mediated through a variety of mechanisms, including both antibody-dependent and antibody-independent pathways.114,115 Studies have also detected intrathecal antibody production and clonal B cell expansion in other neurodegenerative disorders, including AD and Parkinson’s disease, suggesting that B cell responses may not be disease-specific but instead reflect broader CNS immune dysregulation.116 Moreover, recent reviews have highlighted the diverse roles of immunoglobulins in modulating microglial activity, complement activation, and neuroinflammation, positioning B cells as both potential contributors to disease and candidates for therapeutic targeting.117

Despite these emerging insights, the functional roles of B cells and their antibody repertoires in tauopathies remain poorly defined, representing an important gap in the field. Future work is needed to delineate whether B cells act primarily through antibody-mediated mechanisms or also contribute via antigen presentation and cytokine production, as seen in other CNS diseases.116 Addressing these questions will be crucial for developing a comprehensive picture of adaptive immunity in neurodegeneration and identifying novel intervention points beyond T cells alone.

Cell-mediated therapeutic interventions

As our understanding of neurodegeneration evolves, it is increasingly evident that both tauopathies and other proteinopathies share common themes of immune dysregulation, particularly involving maladaptive T cell responses and innate immune dysfunction. By and large, the immune system does not merely respond to damage associated with tauopathies; but actively shapes disease progression, often exacerbating tau aggregates through chronic inflammation and failure of immune resolution. These insights highlight a growing need for therapeutic strategies to suppress excessive immune activation and potentially halt disease progression.

Recent advances in synthetic biology and immune-engineering have laid the foundation for a new generation of cell-based therapies with the potential to modulate neuroimmune interactions with unprecedented precision.118 Engineered T cells, in particular, have emerged as a leading clinical modality in oncology, with several FDA-approved therapies demonstrating the feasibility of modifying T cells to recognize and eliminate specific targets. These technologies are now being adapted for chronic inflammatory and neurodegenerative conditions.119 In the context of tau-driven pathology, the presence of clonally expanded T cells within the brain suggests that immunotherapies could be engineered to selectively suppress or redirect these pathogenic populations.120 For example, Tregs could be engineered to resist activation by tau-derived peptides or inflammatory cytokines such as IFNγ and CCL3, enabling them to deliver localized immunosuppressive signals and mitigate T cell–driven neurotoxicity.

Conversely, efforts to engineer microglia remain largely confined to preclinical models, as technical and biological challenges continue to limit the ability to modify these CNS-resident cells, despite their clear therapeutic potential. Nevertheless, experimental approaches using CRISPR-based tools or AAV vectors have demonstrated proof-of-concept evidence of impacting microglial-dependent control of neuroinflammation and pathology in other models of neurodegenerative disease.121–124 For example, microglia could be engineered to overexpress the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, which has been shown to suppress tau phosphorylation, reduce neuroinflammation, and improve cognitive outcomes in tauopathy models.125 This suggests a promising strategy for dampening the chronic inflammatory milieu that accelerates tau pathology. Another potential approach involves enhancing expression of CR4, which may mediate beneficial phagocytic responses and protect against synaptic loss in tauopathies.17 These early-stage studies highlight the therapeutic promise of engineered microglia, though considerable translational hurdles remain.

In summary, these parallel efforts in adaptive and innate immune modulation underscore the broader potential of cell-based and genetic interventions to rebalance immune function in the aging and degenerating brain. While engineered T cells are closer to clinical application, continued investment in microglial engineering could eventually open new frontiers for treating tauopathies and related proteinopathies with greater precision and durability.

Concluding remarks

The immune system plays a central role in tauopathy progression, with both innate and adaptive branches contributing to neurodegeneration. Innate immune dysregulation, largely through inflammasome activation, complement signaling, DNA damage-induced innate immune responses, and altered metabolism, creates a pro-inflammatory CNS environment that accelerates tau pathology. Concurrently, growing evidence indicates that increased infiltration of T cells into the brain parenchyma is a hallmark of tauopathy progression, with T cells being modulators of neuroinflammation, tau spread, and neuronal loss in tauopathy mouse models.

While early contributions of the adaptive immune system have been identified in tauopathy, major questions remain. For instance, the specific antigens driving T cell responses, the identity of CNS APCs, and the balance between protective and harmful immune activity still require further investigation. Conflicting findings, especially surrounding complement and inflammasome signaling in tauopathy, as well as potentially divergent roles for CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, underscore the complexity of these pathways and the importance of disease context.

Emerging areas within neuroimmunology, including meningeal immunity, border-associated macrophages, and systemic metabolic influences, are broadening our understanding of neuroimmune crosstalk. The meninges in particular are increasingly recognized as a dynamic neuroimmune interface containing both innate (macrophages, mast cells, innate lymphoid cells, and natural killer cells) and adaptive immune populations.126 Though currently underexplored in tauopathies, the meninges may serve as a gateway through which peripherally derived immune cells sense and respond to CNS inflammation.127 Future work should examine whether immune dysregulation in the meninges contributes to T cell priming, cytokine release, or antigen trafficking in tauopathy pathogenesis.

In addition, the interplay between innate and adaptive immunity remains a critical but understudied axis in tau-mediated neurodegeneration. Microglia, PBMs, and monocyte-derived macrophages likely influence T cell recruitment, activation, and function through cytokine signaling, antigen presentation, and metabolic cues. Conversely, T cells may shape the behavior of innate immune cells, either exacerbating or restraining neuroinflammatory states depending on the context. Beyond microglia, astrocytes and OPCs have also been shown to perform immunological functions in the context of other neurological diseases, such as multiple sclerosis.128,129 For instance, recent studies demonstrated that reactive astrocytes can express MHC molecules, secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, and modulate T cell activity under neurodegenerative conditions.129 Similarly, OPCs have been shown to upregulate IFN-responsive genes and engage in immune signaling during CNS stress,128 suggesting a broader landscape of glial contribution to neuroimmune crosstalk. A more complete understanding of these reciprocal interactions, particularly across glial subtypes, will be essential for identifying effective strategies to treat tauopathies.

Looking forward, future therapies will need to modulate immune responses with precision. Advances in immune engineering, including adoptive cell therapies and cytokine pathway modulation, offer exciting opportunities to reprogram immune cells or restore homeostasis. However, their success will depend on a deeper understanding of the complex and context-dependent role of the immune system in tauopathies.
