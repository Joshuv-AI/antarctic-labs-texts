Triggering of Koebner Phenomenon by Picosecond Laser in a Psoriatic Patient: Clinical and Immunopathologic Insights
Authors: Teppei Sakai, Koya Sonoda, Jun Omatsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo report the first case of Koebner phenomenon induced by picosecond laser treatment in a patient with psoriasis. We analyzed the clinical and histopathological features and discussed the possible mechanism by which the laser's photomechanical effect triggered the reaction.MethodsA 58‐year‐old woman with well‐controlled plaque psoriasis underwent 755‐nm picosecond alexandrite laser treatment (pulse duration: 750 ps; fluence: 3.77 J/cm2; spot size: 3 mm) for seborrheic keratoses on the back and lower waist. Clinical, dermoscopic, and histopathological assessments were performed.ResultsOne month after treatment, erythematous, scaly plaques emerged at the laser‐irradiated sites. Clinical and dermoscopic findings were consistent with psoriasis. Histology confirmed psoriasiform changes, and immunohistochemistry demonstrated a T‐cell–predominant infiltrate (CD3+) with dermal CD4+ predominance and epidermal CD8+ enrichment. Notably, no Koebner phenomenon was seen in coexisting melanocytic nevi. Lesions resolved after 4 weeks of topical corticosteroids, with no recurrence up to 4 months.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is the first report of the Koebner phenomenon induced by picosecond laser therapy in a patient with psoriasis. Although picosecond lasers are known for minimal thermal damage, their photomechanical effects may activate immune responses and trigger new psoriatic lesions. Therefore, caution and careful monitoring are advised when using picosecond lasers in patients with psoriasis.Keywords: Koebner phenomenon, picosecond laser, psoriasis, seborrheic keratosis

Introduction

Picosecond lasers have recently become widely used in dermatology, not only for tattoo removal but also primarily for the treatment of pigmentary lesions, including benign pigmented lesions such as solar lentigines and nevus of Ota [1, 2]. Owing to their ultrashort pulse duration, picosecond lasers enable highly precise pigment removal with minimal collateral damage. Seborrheic keratosis (SK) is a common benign epidermal tumor and is often treated for cosmetic reasons.

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder characterized by keratinocyte hyperproliferation and immune dysregulation with a complex, multifactorial pathogenesis [3]. Patients with psoriasis also frequently request elective laser procedures for cosmetic purposes. Previous reports on cosmetic/ablative laser procedures suggest that the risk of inducing new psoriatic lesions may be low when active psoriatic plaques are avoided, although sporadic cases of laser‐associated psoriasis have been reported. However, reports of picosecond laser procedures in patients with psoriasis remain limited, and the true frequency of new psoriatic lesion development after treatment has not been established.

The Koebner phenomenon (KP) refers to the appearance of new psoriatic lesions at sites of cutaneous trauma and is a well‐recognized feature of psoriasis, reported to occur in approximately one‐third of patients [4]. Mechanical stress is a known trigger, and recent work has increasingly clarified the underlying immunologic pathways [5].

Here, we present a case of KP induced by 755‐nm picosecond alexandrite laser treatment for SK in a patient with psoriasis, and we discuss the underlying pathophysiology and safety implications.

Case Report

A 58‐year‐old woman with a history of plaque psoriasis presented with multiple SKs on the back and lower waist. Her psoriasis had been well‐controlled with topical therapies for several years. On the day of treatment, her psoriasis was mild and well controlled (PASI 3.6) on topical calcipotriol/betamethasone dipropionate (Dovobet), with only limited lesions on the scalp and small plaques in the antecubital and popliteal fossae. The patient sought treatment for cosmetic improvement of her SKs (Figure 1A).

Laser treatment was performed using a 755‐nm picosecond alexandrite laser (PicoSure, CYNOSURE LUTRONIC, Westford, MA, USA) with the following settings: pulse duration of 750 ps, fluence of 3.77 J/cm2, and spot size of 3 mm. Five passes were applied uniformly, and immediate whitening was observed. In addition, adjacent melanocytic nevus was treated with a carbon dioxide (CO2) laser. Post‐treatment care included emollients and UV avoidance. She reported no immediate adverse effects. Post‐treatment care included application of emollients and strict avoidance of ultraviolet exposure. The patient reported no immediate adverse effects and returned home without complaint.

At the 1‐week follow‐up, no notable erythema or adverse reaction was observed (Figure 1B). However, at 1 month post‐treatment, erythematous papules and plaques with overlying scales appeared at the exact laser‐irradiated sites (Figure 1C). The rest of the patient's psoriasis remained stable without any change in topical therapy, and no new psoriatic lesions developed at other sites. She initially declined further evaluation or treatment. At 2 months, the plaques had progressed (Figure 1D). Dermoscopy showed pinpoint bleeding and uniform scaling. Histopathology revealed elongated rete ridges, parakeratosis, and dermal lymphocytic/neutrophilic infiltration (Figure 2A), consistent with psoriasis. A separate biopsy from a typical SK lesion on the same patient confirmed features characteristic of seborrheic keratosis (Figure 2B). Immunohistochemistry revealed CD4+ T cells predominating in the dermis and CD8+ T cells more frequent in the epidermis at the laser‑exposed plaques (Figure 2C,D). These findings support psoriatic inflammation at the treated sites. Based on the clinical, dermoscopic, and histopathological findings, a diagnosis of Koebner phenomenon induced by picosecond laser was established. Interestingly, no Koebner phenomenon was observed in coexisting melanocytic nevi within the CO2 laser‐treated areas (Figure 1D). Topical corticosteroid therapy was initiated, and the psoriatic plaques resolved completely within 4 weeks. No recurrence was observed over a subsequent 4‐month follow‐up period.

(A) Biopsy from a picosecond laser–treated erythematous scaly plaque (H&E, original magnification ×100). (B) A separate biopsy specimen from a typical SK lesion on the same patient (H&E, ×100). (C) CD4 immunostaining reveals predominance of CD4+ T cells in the dermis. (D) CD8 immunostaining shows relatively increased CD8+ T cells within the epidermis, highlighting a characteristic psoriatic distribution pattern (C, D: immunohistochemistry, ×100).

Discussion

The Koebner phenomenon (KP), or isomorphic response, refers to the development of disease‐specific lesions at sites of skin trauma in predisposed individuals. First described by Heinrich Koebner in 1876, it is most commonly seen in psoriasis, vitiligo, and lichen planus. KP is classified into four types: true KP, involving identical lesions at trauma sites; pseudo‐KP, caused by trauma‐induced spread of infections like warts; occasional KP, seen in conditions such as Darier or Behçet's disease; and questionable KP, where the trauma–disease link remains uncertain [6]. Approximately one‐third of psoriasis patients experience KP following trauma or experimental provocation, such as acupuncture or tape stripping [4]. The latency between trauma and lesion onset typically ranges from 10 to 20 days but may vary from 3 days to as long as 2 years [7].

In our case, new psoriatic plaques arose strictly within the picosecond‐laser–treated fields, indicating a true KP. Although picosecond lasers are generally considered safe because they minimize thermal diffusion, their ultrashort pulses rely predominantly on photomechanical (photoacoustic) effects. These lasers have therefore become favorable for the treatment of tattoos, lentigines, and nevus of Ota [1, 2]. The acoustic microtrauma generated by these pulses may be particularly relevant in the context of KP, since mechanical stress is a well‐recognized trigger of psoriatic inflammation [5, 8, 9, 10, 11]. It is also noteworthy that no Koebner response was observed at the site treated with CO2 laser, whereas new lesions developed after picosecond laser exposure. However, differences in treated surface area, injury depth, and wound‐healing dynamics limit direct comparison, and this observation should be considered hypothesis‐generating. Nevertheless, because CO2 lasers act predominantly via photothermal effects (vaporization and coagulation), this pattern may suggest that photomechanical injury could be a relevant trigger for KP in susceptible patients. Histopathology showed psoriasiform changes, and immunohistochemistry demonstrated dermal CD4+ and epidermal CD8+ T‐cell predominance, a distribution typical of psoriasis. This pattern supports the interpretation of a trauma‐driven, T‐cell–mediated mechanism of KP after picosecond laser injury.

Reports of laser‐induced KP in psoriasis are rare [12]. Cases have been described after diode laser hair removal and pulsed‐dye laser therapy [13, 14], whereas other series using ablative or non‐ablative devices did not observe KP [15]. Compared with primarily photothermal modalities, picosecond lasers depend on photomechanical effects, which may represent a distinct risk factor for inducing KP. Clinically, this case underscores that even minimally invasive procedures can provoke KP in psoriasis. Careful patient selection, pre‐treatment counseling, and vigilant follow‐up are warranted when considering picosecond‐laser treatment in patients with a history of psoriasis. Practical considerations include documenting baseline disease activity, avoiding treatment of active psoriatic lesions, counseling on the rare risk of new plaques after laser procedures, and considering a test spot with early follow‐up.

This report is limited by its single‐case nature, which restricts generalizability. In addition, follow‐up was limited to 4 months. Further case accumulation with longer follow‐up is warranted.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first report of the Koebner phenomenon induced by picosecond laser therapy in a patient with psoriasis. Although picosecond lasers are known for minimal thermal damage, their photomechanical effects may activate immune responses and trigger new psoriatic lesions. Therefore, caution and careful monitoring are advised when using picosecond lasers in patients with psoriasis.
