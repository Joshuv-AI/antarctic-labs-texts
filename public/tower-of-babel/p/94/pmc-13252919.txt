Audiological Outcomes of Salvage Intratympanic Corticosteroid Therapy in Idiopathic Sudden Sensorineural Hearing Loss
Authors: Carina Götting, Claudia Lädrach, Sara-Lynn Hool, Samuel Tschopp, Marco Caversaccio, Urs Borner, Lukas Anschuetz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntratympanic corticosteroids (ITCs) are used to treat patients with idiopathic sudden sensorineural hearing loss (ISSNHL) when systemic therapy has been unsuccessful. However, the efficacy of this treatment remains uncertain.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed ISSNHL patients at a tertiary reference centre, who received ITC injections following unsuccessful systemic corticosteroid therapy administered either orally or intravenously. Patients were eligible for ITC if they showed insufficient improvement after systemic treatment, defined as a remaining hearing loss of ≥30 dB HL in at least three frequencies. They received three intratympanic injections of 0.3–0.5 mL dexamethasone (4 mg/mL) within 10–14 days from first to last injection. Changes in pure-tone audiometry were assessed.ResultsA total of 34 patients met the inclusion criteria for the study. The mean pure tone average (PTA) improvement was 10.62 dB HL. A total of 18 (53%) patients showed hearing improvement of ≥10 dB HL (of these: n = 11 (32%) ≥20 dB HL, n = 2 (6%) ≥30 dB HL). Sixteen (47%) patients had an improvement of less than 10 dB HL. In addition to the PTA analysis, individual frequencies at 0.5 kHz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz, and 4 kHz were examined and showed significant improvements, with p values ranging from 0.007 to 0.037. Regarding the World Health Organization grading of hearing impairment, 13 (38.2%) patients improved by at least one category. Concomitant vestibular syndrome (n = 5) showed a marginally non-significant trend (p = 0.066) towards less improvement. There was no significant correlation between age and degree of hearing loss (p = 0.123). Complications of ITC were minimal, with only 1 patient reporting a temporary headache following injection.ConclusionIn patients with persistent ISSNHL after systemic corticosteroid therapy, ITC injections resulted in limited hearing improvement. Despite the low risks associated with this therapy, its routine use should be considered on a case-by-case evaluation.

Introduction

Idiopathic sudden sensorineural hearing loss (ISSNHL) is defined as a rapid, unexplained decrease in sensorineural hearing sensitivity, typically affecting one ear. Its severity can range from mild hearing loss to complete deafness and is often associated with tinnitus and vertigo, significantly reducing the quality of life [1, 2]. ISSNHL is most commonly defined as a loss of at least 30 dB HL in three consecutive frequencies over a period of 72 h or less compared to the contralateral hearing threshold [3, 4]. The incidence is estimated to be 5–20 cases per 100,000 people annually in developed countries [5, 6]. However, a study from Germany reports a significantly higher incidence rate, with estimates of 160 per 100,000 people per year [7]. The average age of those affected individuals is 47–56 years, with no gender preference [8, 9]. Various hypotheses regarding its aetiology have been proposed, including infectious causes such as viral and bacterial, as well as inflammatory, vascular, autoimmune and genetic predisposition, but none have been conclusively proven [10, 11]. Approximately 32–65% of patients with ISSNHL experience spontaneous recovery [12]. Systemic glucocorticoids (GCs) are commonly used worldwide as primary therapy, with intratympanic administration recommended as a secondary option [3, 13–15].

The aim of intratympanic corticosteroid (ITC) injections was to bypass the blood-labyrinthine barrier to achieve higher concentrations in the inner ear while avoiding the adverse effects of systemic steroids [16, 17]. This therapy is well tolerated, can be administrated in an in-office setting, and targets the affected ear. The rare reported side effects include a persisting perforation of the tympanic membrane, localised pain, otorrhoea, acute otitis media, vertigo, and light-headedness. Dexamethasone and methylprednisolone are commonly used for intratympanic injections to reduce intracochlear oxidative stress [18]. A Cochrane review by Plontke et al. [13] (2022) concluded that ITCs as primary therapy provide little or no improvement in hearing thresholds compared to systemic corticosteroids. Combined therapy shows only a small additional benefit over systemic corticosteroids alone. In addition, salvage therapy using ITC injections has been shown to have a small effect on hearing recovery, with a median PTA improvement of 9.07 dB HL. Given the limited evidence available and the ongoing debate, we aim to evaluate the effectiveness of ITC injections in improving hearing after unsuccessful systemic corticosteroid therapy. We hypothesise that this treatment has no clinically relevant benefit regarding post-interventional hearing levels. Furthermore, by providing confirmatory data from a tertiary reference centre, this study supports and reinforces the existing evidence base on the limited efficacy of salvage ITC therapy following failed systemic treatment.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We performed a retrospective analysis of patients who received ITC injections at the Inselspital Bern between May 2018 and March 2023, of whom 34 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The Local Ethics Committee reviewed and approved this study (Kantonale Ethikkommission Bern, Nr. KEK-BE 2019-00555).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The main inclusion criteria were patients aged 18 years and older with a diagnosis of ISSNHL, defined as a hearing loss of ≥30 dB HL at three consecutive frequencies, who had insufficient improvement in hearing loss after prior oral or intravenous corticosteroid therapy i.e. a persisting hearing loss of ≥30 dB HL at three consecutive frequencies. Patients with a known cause of hearing loss were excluded from the study. Of the 107 patients who received ITC injections during the study period, 73 were excluded as hearing loss was not idiopathic, as summarised in Table 1. Vestibular syndrome was defined as an acute vestibular syndrome diagnosed clinically by an otorhinolaryngologist, characterised by the acute onset of continuous vertigo lasting at least 24 h, accompanied by spontaneous nystagmus, postural instability and a pathological head impulse test [19].

Procedure

All patients gave informed consent before the procedure. The local protocol for intratympanic injection was as follows: dexamethasone (4 mg/mL, warmed to body temperature) was used for all injections. Dexamethasone was chosen because of its favourable diffusion characteristics across the round window membrane and its well-established safety profile in intratympanic use [3, 20]. The treatment regimen applied in this study is aligned with protocols commonly employed in published clinical investigations [3, 21–23]. Topical local anaesthesia of the external auditory canal (EMLA cream or 10% xylocaine spray) was applied for 30 min before injection. Subsequently, 0.3–0.5 mL dexamethasone was injected through the posterior-inferior quadrant of the tympanic membrane into the middle ear. After the intratympanic injection, patients were instructed to lie on their side with the affected ear facing upwards for 30 min. They were also advised to avoid swallowing and to spit out any saliva to prevent drainage through the Eustachian tube. This procedure enables a high concentration of corticosteroids to be achieved at the round window membrane and, consequently, in the perilymph. Three injections were performed within 10–14 days from first to last injection.

Outcome Measures

Pure-tone audiograms were obtained and compared at three time points: at the time of diagnosis, after oral or intravenous corticosteroid therapy and one after ITC therapy. Between the first audiogram, marking the diagnosis of ISSNHL, and the audiogram following systemic corticosteroid therapy, the median time was 16 days (interquartile range [IQR] 14–20). The interval between the audiogram after systemic therapy and the audiogram following ITC therapy was a median of 34 days (IQR 25–42). Finally, the time from the last injection to the follow-up audiogram was a median of 86 days (IQR 33–130). Audiograms were performed by trained audiometrists or resident physicians.

All audiometric measurements were performed using a calibrated clinical audiometer (Interacoustics Equinox, Diatec) in a soundproof booth. Calibration was conducted in accordance with ISO 389-1:2017 (reference standard), IEC 60645-1:2017 (tolerance standard), and IEC 60318-1:2009 (electroacoustic simulator standard) to ensure the accuracy and reliability of all measurements. Speech audiometry and word recognition tests were not performed, as only pure-tone audiometry data were available for this retrospective study.

As there is no internationally standardised definition of hearing recovery and major guidelines apply different PTA-based criteria, we aligned our outcome measures with the principles used in these recommendations. The Spanish guideline distinguishes between no recovery, partial recovery and complete recovery based on PTA changes of <10 dB HL, >10 dB HL and a final PTA of around 10 dB HL, respectively. The Chinese guideline similarly differentiates between no recovery, partial recovery and marked recovery using improvements of <15 dB HL, 15 to <30 dB HL, and ≥30 dB HL, or a final PTA within the normal range. In contrast, the American guideline does not specify fixed PTA thresholds for defining recovery [3, 24, 25]. Based on these differences, we defined our primary outcomes using graded PTA improvement categories. The primary outcome was defined as improvements of 10, 20, and 30 dB HL in PTA (0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 kHz) 3 months after ITC injections. In addition, pure-tone audiometric thresholds were analysed at the frequencies 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 kHz, while 3.0 kHz was excluded due to a high number of missing values. Secondary outcomes included changes in the distribution of patients in different WHO grades of hearing impairment following ITC therapy.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics, including mean and standard deviation or range, median, and IQR, depending on the data distribution. Non-parametric statistical tests were applied due to the small sample size. Paired comparisons of pure-tone audiometric thresholds at 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 and 4.0 kHz, as well as PTA, before and after ITC injection were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A binomial test was used to determine whether one side was more affected than the other. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to analyse differences in improvement after ITC injection between patients with and without vestibular symptoms, as well as differences in outcome between sexes. Correlations between the magnitude of hearing loss and age were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. For frequency-specific analyses, a Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons.

Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Graphical representations of the data were made using box plots supplemented with means and individual measurements, Tukey plots, and scatter plots. For Tukey plots, a family-wise confidence level was applied to control for family-wise error rate in all pairwise comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2, The R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

After applying the inclusion criteria, n = 34 patients were included in the final analysis; all subsequent analyses refer to this cohort unless otherwise stated. Of these, 23 patients (68%) were male and 11 (32%) were female, with a mean age of 58.1 years (SD 20.0). The affected ear was left-sided in 20 (59%) patients and right-sided in 14 (41%). A binomial test showed no significant preference for the affected side (p = 0.392) or gender (p = 0.636). Five (15%) patients had a concomitant vestibular syndrome, with no significant difference in severity of hearing loss between those with and without vestibular syndrome (z = −1.512, p = 0.138).

The mean PTA improvement after ITC injections was 10.76 dB HL, with a mean of 80.17 dB HL (SD 22.49) before treatment and 69.41 dB HL (SD 28.13) after treatment (Bonferroni-corrected p = 0.008). A total of 18 patients (53%) showed an improvement of ≥10 dB HL, 11 (32%) patients an improvement of ≥20 dB HL and 2 (6%) patients an improvement of ≥30 dB HL. As these thresholds are cumulative, patients with ≥20 dB HL and ≥30 dB HL improvement are also included in the ≥10 dB HL category. Sixteen (47%) patients had an improvement of less than 10 dB HL. Patients with vestibular syndrome showed a significantly less improvement in hearing loss compared to patients without vestibular syndrome (Wilcoxon signed-rank test: z = 2.021, p = 0.042). In addition to the PTA analysis, individual frequencies at 0.5 kHz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz, and 4 kHz were examined. All frequencies showed significant improvements after Bonferroni correction, as detailed in Table 2 and graphically illustrated in Figure 1.

Patients were divided into three age groups: <40 years (8 patients, 23.5%), 40–60 years (8 patients, 23.5%) and >60 years (18 patients, 53%). The group aged >60 years had the most severe hearing loss, with a mean PTA of 95.76 dB HL, compared to 88.80 dB HL in the youngest group and 76.88 dB HL in the 40–60 year group. There was no significant correlation between age and degree of hearing loss (Spearman’s ρ = 0.303, p = 0.082). After ITC therapy, the group of patients aged under 40 years showed a mean PTA improvement of 9.06 dB HL. Patients aged 40–60 years showed a mean improvement of 4.84 dB HL, while patients aged over 60 years showed a mean improvement of 14.14 dB HL. An analysis of improvement between age groups showed no significant difference (p = 0.410).

Before the ITC injection and after oral corticosteroid treatment, patients were classified according to the WHO grading of hearing impairment. One patient (2.9%) had slight impairment, 6 (17.6%) had moderate impairment, 7 (20.6%) had severe impairment, and 20 (58.9%) patients were classified as having profound impairment (including deafness). Following ITC injection, only 1 (3%) patient developed a complication of temporary headache. At the final follow-up, 8 (33%) patients were fitted with a unilateral hearing aid, 5 (21%) with a CROS/Bi-CROS, and 4 (17%) with a cochlear implant. Seven (29%) patients required no further intervention, and 10 (29%) patients had no data available on post-treatment management.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study on salvage ITC injections after unsuccessful oral steroid therapy, we observed a small mean effect on hearing recovery of 10.76 dB HL. A bit more than half of the patients experienced a hearing improvement of ≥10 dB HL, of these 32% improved by ≥20 dB HL, and only 2 (6%) patients showed an improvement of ≥30 dB HL. The other half of the cohort (47%) had an improvement of less than 10 dB HL. Regarding changes in hearing impairment gradings according to the WHO classification, only 38.2% improved by at least one category. No significant correlation was found between age and the degree of hearing loss at diagnosis, nor was there a significant difference in hearing improvement among the different age groups following salvage intratympanic treatment.

A critical aspect of evaluating therapy outcomes for ISSNHL is the variability in defining recovery and determining the appropriate time point for assessment. While most studies report hearing recovery within the first month after treatment, some describe delayed improvements occurring up to 8 months [26]. These differences make it difficult to standardise outcome measures across trials. Moreover, there is also no universally accepted definition of hearing recovery, which limits the direct comparability of results across studies. Many studies consider a minimum improvement of 10 dB in PTA or 5–10% in word recognition score (WRS) to indicate recovery. However, the Clinical Practice Guideline For Sudden Hearing Loss [3] suggests that an improvement of less than 10 dB HL should be classified as no recovery. According to this definition, the mean improvement of 10.76 dB in our cohort is close to no effect.

These findings are consistent with evidence from larger systematic reviews and meta-analyses. A Cochrane review found that salvage ITC therapy resulted in only minor audiometric improvements, with a median pure-tone average improvement of 9.07 dB HL [13]. Similarly, a meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials demonstrated a small but statistically significant improvement in hearing thresholds compared with no further treatment, with a standardised mean difference corresponding to an effect size comparable to that observed in the present study [20]. Importantly, such modest improvements in pure-tone average do not necessarily translate into meaningful functional hearing benefit, which is why current guidelines recommend categorising recovery according to whether the affected ear is serviceable or non-serviceable rather than relying solely on audiometric threshold changes.

These observations are further supported by recent randomised trials. The HODOCORT trial by Plontke et al. [13] has shown that high-dose GCs per os do not offer superior efficacy in hearing recovery compared to lower doses of prednisone in the primary treatment of ISSNHL. However, higher doses are associated with an increased risk of adverse events and serious adverse events. These observations highlight that while GCs may address certain treatable aspects of the pathophysiology, they are not universally effective in treating ISSNHL or other acute inner ear disorders since the underlying mechanisms remain unclear [10, 11]. This lack of clarity regarding the aetiology poses difficulty to develop targeted and effective treatments for ISSNHL. Given the profound impact of hearing loss on individuals, including an increased risk of cognitive impairment and dementia [26, 27], further research into the aetiology is essential to understand better the mechanisms involved and pave the way for the development of medical, biological, or genetic therapies for sensorineural hearing loss.

GCs are primarily recognised for their anti-inflammatory properties, but their effects on other critical cochlear pathophysiological mechanisms – such as oxidative stress, apoptosis, synaptopathy, and neuronal degeneration – remain uncertain. While some studies suggest that GCs may have additional protective effects, possibly through cellular stress response pathways, the extent and clinical relevance of these mechanisms are not well understood [28].

Achieving high drug concentrations in the inner ear may be essential for certain effects, particularly to maximise anti-apoptotic and antioxidant properties [29]. Due to the blood labyrinth barrier, systemically administered corticosteroids cannot reach inner ear concentrations above plasma levels. In contrast, intratympanic injection allows short-term, high drug concentrations in the middle ear, from where corticosteroids diffuse into the inner ear through the round and oval windows [22, 30]. This localised delivery method allows for higher corticosteroid levels in the cochlea, which may be critical for the effective treatment of ISSNHL [17].

However, given the variable response to corticosteroid therapy, it is possible that additional non-inflammatory mechanisms are also involved. The vascular hypothesis posits that transient cochlear ischaemia or microvascular dysfunction may contribute to inner-ear injury, which could explain the beneficial effects of adjunctive hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBO). A recent meta-analysis further supports this, demonstrating that the addition of HBO to systemic or ITC therapy results in an additional mean hearing gain of approximately 10 dB compared with corticosteroid therapy alone, particularly when initiated early after onset [31]. These findings suggest that different pathophysiological mechanisms, including vascular compromise, inflammation and oxidative stress, may contribute to ISSNHL, either individually or in combination [3].

In animal models, GCs regulate cochlear function beyond their anti-inflammatory properties. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is essential for synaptic stability and neuronal survival in the auditory system. Studies in mouse models suggest that short-term exposure to GCs may enhance BDNF expression, whereas prolonged exposure may suppress BDNF levels, potentially leading to synaptic degeneration and hearing loss [32]. In addition, GCs interact with mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways that regulate cell survival and regeneration after acoustic trauma. While GC-mediated MAPK activation supports cochlear recovery, long-term exposure may disrupt neurotrophic signalling and reduce its protective effects [32, 33]. These findings highlight the need for a balanced therapeutic approach, as the optimal dose and duration of GC treatment for ISSNHL remains to be determined to maximise benefits while minimising potential risks.

While our study does not present new therapeutic effects, it provides valuable confirmatory evidence supporting the current understanding of salvage ITC therapy for ISSNHL. Reproducibility and consistency of results across independent cohorts are essential for strengthening clinical recommendations. By demonstrating comparable audiometric outcomes to those reported in previous trials and systematic reviews, our data reinforce the robustness of existing evidence and provide a more realistic perspective on the limited efficacy of ITC in this context. Publishing studies that confirm or refine earlier findings is crucial to prevent publication bias and to ensure that clinical decision-making is based on balanced and comprehensive evidence rather than selectively positive results.

As part of a final data review, all patient records were re-evaluated to determine if any patients had returned for a follow-up visit after their last post-injection pure tone audiogram. This review aimed to identify any additional events related to hearing loss that may have occurred or the type of hearing rehabilitation. Notably, 2 patients were subsequently diagnosed with Meniere’s disease, resulting in their exclusion from the study cohort, as illustrated in Table 2. This finding highlights an important observation: what initially presents as ISSNHL may, in some cases, later prove to be attributed to underlying causes, estimated to account for approximately 30% of cases [34]. This highlights the importance of careful, standardised long-term follow-up, not only to detect potential aetiologies that may manifest later after the initial episode of hearing loss but also to identify patients who may benefit from rehabilitation strategies for residual hearing loss or tinnitus. In line with this, the American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery Foundation (AAO-HNSF) recommends follow-up pure tone audiograms at 6 months [3].

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the results. Firstly, its retrospective nature limits the analysis to associations rather than causal inferences. In particular, the lack of a control group limits the ability to definitively attribute the observed hearing improvements to ITC therapy, as spontaneous recovery or other confounding factors cannot be ruled out. The relatively small sample size (n = 34) reduces statistical power and limits the generalisability of the findings. In addition, heterogeneous follow-up intervals inherent to the retrospective design may have introduced both selection and measurement bias. In addition, the absence of uniform imaging or ancillary testing (e.g., MRI to rule out retrocochlear pathology or endolymphatic hydrops) may further have affected diagnostic consistency.

The absence of speech audiometry and patient-reported outcome measures restricts the evaluation of functional hearing improvement. While speech audiometry is widely used in ISSNHL studies and provides valuable information on speech perception, its diagnostic accuracy may be limited in increasingly multilingual patient populations, as test performance depends on proficiency in the test language and may not accurately reflect functional hearing ability. In contrast, validated hearing-related questionnaires can be administered in multiple languages and represent a more adaptable, language-independent tool for evaluating functional outcomes. Such measures should be incorporated into future studies to allow a more comprehensive assessment of functional hearing improvement. Finally, conducting such studies is inherently challenging due to the lack of internationally accepted standards for the diagnosis and treatment of ISSNHL, making it difficult to compare results across studies and settings.

Conclusion

Salvage ITC therapy has a small effect on improving PTA in patients who have failed to respond to systemic corticosteroids. The absence of vestibular symptoms may be related to a better prognosis. Complications associated to the injections are minimal. Further studies are needed to determine which patients are most likely to benefit from this therapy and achieve meaningful hearing improvement.
