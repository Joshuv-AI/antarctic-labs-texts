Marital Failure and Subsequent Heart Failure: A Prospective Study
Authors: Ruiqi Xia, Liming Lin, Yaqi Li, Zhicheng Zhang, Yinshun Peng, Wenchang Yang, Yilun Huang, Shuohua Chen, Shouling Wu, Xiang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoss of a stable spousal relationship has been associated with a high risk of morbidity and premature death, yet there is limited evidence on the association between marital failure and the risk of subsequent heart failure (HF). We examined the association between marital failure and the risk of subsequent HF in large prospective studies.MethodsThis cohort study utilized data from Kailuan Study I and Kailuan Study II. Marital status was assessed through multiple self‐reports. HF cases were identified via biennial interviews, hospital records, social insurance, and death registries, confirmed by cardiologists. Associations were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsAmong the 125 042 participants (mean age, 55.3 years; 83.1% men), 6042 (4.83%) experienced marital failure. During a median follow‐up of 13.5 years (interquartile range, 9.00–14.1 years), 3779 subsequent HF cases were documented. Compared with marital stability, the hazard ratio for HF was 1.30 (95% CI, 1.14–1.49) for marital failure, adjusting for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, biochemical parameters, and medical history. The association was stronger in individuals with higher educational levels and those with poorer lifestyle score, as assessed by smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, salt diet, and body mass index, relative to their counterparts (P<0.05 for interaction for both).ConclusionsIn this large prospective study, we found that marital failure was associated with a higher risk of subsequent HF compared with marital stability, particularly among individuals with high educational levels and poor lifestyle habits. Marital failure may be a noteworthy socioeconomic factor that assists in identifying at‐risk populations and developing supportive strategies for better health outcomes accordingly.

Heart failure (HF) is a life‐threatening syndrome with a growing public health burden. An estimated 64.3 million people worldwide were diagnosed with HF in 2017. 1 In China, 1.3% of adults older than 35 years (an estimated 13.7 million) experienced HF, with its prevalence continuing to rise after 2012. 2 HF has important clinical and public health implications, as it is associated with significant socioeconomic burden and is a key cause of hospital admissions and revisits, leading to loss of work capacity and productivity. 3

Spousal relationships are one of the most intimate connections in human life. Previous studies have found that maintaining stable and harmonious relationships with spouses, is associated with a low risk of mortality. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 The loss of intimate relationships, such as dissolution of marriage, is an adverse life event that may contribute to the development of cardiovascular diseases. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Recently, a prospective study revealed an association between unstable marital status and high risk of cardiovascular diseases, including ischemic heart disease and stroke 17 ; however, HF was not examined in this study.

Although some previous research has indicated an association between unstable marital status and worse prognosis among patients with HF, 18 , 19 , 20 it remains unknown whether marital status is associated with altered risk of developing HF. We thus prospectively analyzed data from 2 large‐scale community‐based cohorts, Kailuan Study I and Kailuan Study II, with marital status assessed by multiple follow‐ups, to evaluate the association between marital failure and future risk of HF in the Chinese population.

METHODS

The current study was performed according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of Kailuan General Hospital (approval number: 2006‐05). All participants agreed to take part in the study and provided informed written consent.

All data collected in the current study are available with permission from the Kailuan institutional review board. Written proposals can be addressed to the corresponding authors for appropriateness of use.

Population and Study Design

Participants were from 2 ongoing prospective cohorts, Kailuan Study I and Kailuan Study II, in Tangshan, China, which were established in 2006 and 2008 to 2010, and have been followed biennially. Details of the study design can be found in previous studies. 21 , 22 Briefly, participants underwent questionnaires, physical and clinical examinations, and laboratory tests at each visit. The study design followed the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines. Ethic reports were confirmed by the ethics committee from Kailuan General Hospital, China, on January 5, 2006. All participants provided written informed consent.

For the current study, 166 042 participants with their marital status recorded at least once between 2006 and 2016 were initially included. After excluding individuals younger than 40 years (n=40 175), with a history of cancer (n=504), or HF (n=321), the final analysis was based on 125 042 participants (Figure 1).

Assessment of Marital Status

Information on marital status was collected via a questionnaire and updated at each visit. We categorized participants into 3 groups (“marital stability,” “marital failure,” and “other marital status”) based on their marital status at baseline and during follow‐up. Specifically, the marital stability group included participants who reported being married with no record of divorce, widowed, or remarriage, and those who transitioned from single to married. The marital failure group included participants who transitioned from married to single, divorced, or widowed. Other participants (eg, remained unmarried) were considered the other marital status group.

Assessment of Covariates

In this study, the selection of covariates was based on prior knowledge of potential confounding factors associated with HF. 14 , 23 Information on sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, including birth date, age (years), sex (men; women), income (<3000 ¥/month; ≥3000 ¥/month, a threshold for low and high income that has been commonly used in prior studies 24 ), education (primary and below; high school; college and above), smoking status (never; past; current), drinking status (never; past; current), physical activity (never; seldom; always), and salt intake (low‐salt; moderate; high‐salt), was obtained using standardized questionnaires and medical records, as previously detailed. 22 Smoking status was defined based on the average number of cigarettes smoked per day in a recent year, and drinking status was determined by the average alcohol consumption in the past year, as detailed elsewhere. 25

Every 2 years, participants in our study were surveyed about their daily salt intake, which was classified as low, moderate, or high. Intake <6 g/d was considered low (<2400 mg/d sodium intake), 6 to 10 g/d as moderate (2400 to 4000 mg/d sodium intake), and >10 g/d as high (>4000 mg/d sodium intake). These categories followed the standards cited in previous research. 26 Despite the low category exceeding the World Health Organization's recommended maximum of <5 g/d, it was consistent with typical dietary habits in China. All measurements adhered to the standard protocol described earlier by our group. 27 Anthropometric measurements included height, weight, and body mass index, which was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure were both measured twice using a mercury sphygmomanometer with the participant seated, and the average of the 2 measurements was used for analysis. Blood samples were collected from the anterior tibial vein in the early morning after overnight fasting and analyzed within 4 hours using a Hitachi 7600 autoanalyzer in the central laboratory of Kailuan General Hospital, with fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, C‐reactive protein, and serum creatinine concentrations determined. The estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated using the creatinine equation of the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration. 28 Hypertension was defined with a measured systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, use of antihypertensive medication, or self‐reported or physician‐diagnosed history of hypertension. 29 Diabetes was defined as a fasting blood glucose level ≥7.0 mmol/L, taking antihyperglycemic medication, or a self‐reported or diagnosed history of diabetes. History of myocardial infarction, stroke, atrial fibrillation, and cancer was confirmed by medical record review.

Lifestyle score was calculated by assigning 1 point for each of the following criteria: never smoking, never drinking, low or moderate salt intake (where salt intake serves as a surrogate of diet quality, as previously validated 22 ), a body mass index <24, and always engaging in physical activity; otherwise, 0 points were assigned. 30 , 31 The total lifestyle score ranged from 0 (least healthy) to 5 (healthiest).

Ascertainment of HF

The outcome of the study was the first occurrence of incident HF.

Potential HF cases were annually reviewed and cross‐validated through 4 complementary sources as previously described 32 : the biennial face‐to‐face interviews, death certificates from provincial vital statistics offices, discharge records from 11 local hospitals, and medical data from Municipal Social Insurance Institutions, which covered all participants in the Kailuan studies, using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), code I50.x.

Following the guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology, 33 and the criteria outlined in the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Heart Failure 2018, 34 these potential cases were confirmed by 2 experienced cardiologists, blinded to the current study protocol, who agreed on the first occurrence of HF by reviewing medical records. If there was disagreement, a third cardiologist provided final adjudication. HF was diagnosed using the following comprehensive evaluation: (1) individual clinical symptoms and signs, including dyspnea, fatigue and fluid retention, and cardiac function in discharge diagnoses classified according to New York Heart Association functional grades II, III, or IV, or Killip grades II, III, or IV; (2) echocardiographic evidence, such as left ventricular ejection fraction and E/e′ ratio, as measured using 2‐dimensional and Doppler echocardiography; and (3) biomarkers of cardiac injury, mainly elevated plasma N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide levels.

Statistical Analysis

Follow‐up time was calculated from baseline (the date of initial married record for the marital stability group, the date of first marital status record for the other marital status group, and the date of latest marital breakdown for the marital failure group) until the first occurrence of HF, death, or the end of follow‐up (December 31, 2020), whichever came first. The missing covariates, the proportion of which were all within 10%, were filled in using medians for continuous variables and plurality for categorical variables. Detailed information on missing covariates are shown in Table S1. Baseline characteristics of participants were described and compared according to marital status, with ANOVA used for quantitative data and χ2 tests for categorical data. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to depict the cumulative risk according to marital status, and log‐rank test was used to test group difference. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to compute hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs assessing the association between marital status and subsequent HF risk. We tested the proportional hazards assumption by creating a product term of follow‐up time and marital status and found no violation of the assumption. Model 1 was adjusted for age (years) and sex (men; women). Model 2 was further adjusted for education (primary and below; high school; college and above), income (<3000; ≥3000 ¥/month), smoking status (never; past; current), drinking status (never; past; current), salt diet (low‐salt; moderate; high‐salt), physical activity (never; seldom; always), and body mass index. Model 3 was adjusted for triglycerides (mmol/L), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (mmol/L), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (mmol/L), C‐reactive protein (mg/L), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (<30; 30–59; 60–89; ≥90 mL/min·per 1.73 m2]). Model 4 was additionally adjusted for hypertension (no; yes), diabetes (no; yes), history of myocardial infarction (no; yes), history of stroke (no; yes), and history of atrial fibrillation (no; yes).

We tested interactions between marital status and age (<60 years; ≥60 years), sex (men; women), education (primary and blow; high school; college and above), income (<3000 ¥/month; ≥3000 ¥/month), and lifestyle score (0–2 [low]; 3–4 [moderate]; 5 [high], according to the distribution of participants). Multiplicative interactions were assessed by likelihood ratio tests. 35

To examine the potential short‐ to long‐term impact of marital failure on subsequent HF risk, we conducted 1‐, 2‐, and 5‐year lag analyses by excluding subsequent HF cases that occurred within 1, 2, and 5 years of follow‐up. Sex‐stratified analyses were also performed.

To assess the potential impact of the evolution of lifestyle factors before and after marital failure on HF risk, we conducted an analysis by dividing participants in the marital failure group with complete premarital and postmarital failure lifestyle data (n=5377) into a “decreased lifestyle score after marital failure” group (n=920) and a “stable or improved lifestyle score” group (n=4457). The interaction between marital status and change in lifestyle score in relation to HF risk was also examined. The proportion of the association between marital failure and HF risk mediated by a decrease in lifestyle score among participants with complete prelifestyle and postlifestyle data (n=83 456) was assessed by mediation analysis using a marginal structural model. 36 , 37 Briefly, the binary mediator (decrease in lifestyle score [yes versus no]) and all baseline covariates except 5 lifestyle factors were included in this model. Mediation analysis proceeded in 4 primary steps: (1) estimating exposure inverse probability weights, (2) estimating mediator inverse probability weights, (3) obtaining the final weight by calculating the product of the 2 weights, and (4) estimating controlled direct effect and total effect using weighted regression models.

We performed a subcategory analysis of marital failure, classifying participants into “divorced,” “widowed,” and “unspecified marital failure” (eg, married to single, considering that some divorced or widowed participants were reluctant to report their real marital status) groups, to examine their associations with HF risk.

We also performed several sensitivity analyses. First, we excluded participants with a history of myocardial infarction, stroke, or atrial fibrillation. Second, a competing risk model was utilized to account for the competing risks of mortality and subsequent HF. 38 Third, we employed multiple imputation via R package mice for missing variables (predictive mean matching for continuous variables, logistic regression for binary variables, and polytomous regression for multicategorical variables) in the analyses, 39 and a single imputed data set was generated after 5 iterations, given computational constraints. Fourth, we excluded participants with missing covariates. Fifth, we excluded participants with only one record on marital status. Last, we conducted a Cox model with time‐varying covariates, 40 incorporating the latest‐available covariate data before the first occurrence of HF, death, or the end of the follow‐up (December 31, 2020).

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). 41

RESULTS

Among the 125 042 participants (mean age, 55.3 years; 83.1% men), 116 700 (93.3%) had stable marriages and 6042 (4.83%) experienced marital failure (Table 1). Those with marital failure tended to be women, less educated, and with healthier behaviors, such as smoking and drinking less, consuming less salt, engaging in more physical activity, and having a lower body mass index, which contributed to higher lifestyle scores, compared with those with stable marriages. In addition, higher triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol and C‐reactive protein concentrations; lower high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol concentrations and estimated glomerular filtration rate; and a higher proportion of hypertension, diabetes, history of myocardial infarction, stroke, and atrial fibrillation were observed among participants with marital failure, compared with those with stable marriages (Table 1).

Significant interaction between lifestyle and marital failure in relation to HF risk was observed (P=0.01 for interaction) and the adjusted HR was 1.59 (95% CI, 1.24–2.04), 1.28 (95% CI, 1.07–1.53), and 0.98 (95% CI, 0.65–1.48) for the low, moderate, and high lifestyle score groups, respectively (Figure 3). Although the HR point estimate for the decreased lifestyle score after marital failure group (HR, 1.50) was higher than that for the stable or improved lifestyle score group (HR, 1.27), the interaction (P=0.86 for interaction) between marital status and change in lifestyle score in relation to HF risk was not significant (Figure S5). In addition, a decrease in lifestyle score was not found to significantly mediate this association (proportion of mediation effect: 1.63% [95% CI, −4.69% to 9.63%]; Figure S6). Interestingly, the association between marital failure and HF risk was more pronounced among participants with high educational levels (college and above: 1.69 [95% CI, 0.87–3.28]; high school: 1.54 [95% CI, 1.30–1.82]), relative to those with low educational levels (primary school or below: 0.95 [95% CI, 0.73–1.22]; P=0.001 for interaction). We did not find a significant interaction between marital failure and other factors, including age, sex, and income (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

In this large Chinese prospective cohort, after adjusting for sociodemographic, lifestyle factors, medical history, and other covariates, marital failure was associated with higher HF risk, compared with marital stability. The potential impact of marriage status was stronger for short‐term HF risk, relative to long‐term HF risk. Stronger associations were observed among participants with unhealthier lifestyles and those with higher educational levels, relative to their counterparts.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between marital factors and subsequent HF. Our findings align with prior research, indicating that stable marital status was associated with lower disease incidence, compared with unstable marital status. Previous studies predominantly associated a stable baseline marital status with low rates of all‐cause mortality, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 cancer, 7 , 42 and cardiovascular disease, 12 , 13 , 43 yet none specifically explored the relationship between marital status and HF. Departing from the common practice of categorizing populations by their baseline marital status, our study leveraged multiple follow‐up records. This method facilitated a finer differentiation between individuals with varying marital statuses, enabling a more detailed distinction between stable and unstable marital situations. The main strengths of this study also include its large sample size, comprehensive collection of baseline information at each phase, and a follow‐up period of >10 years.

Our research findings suggest that the association between marital failure and HF risk became weaker over time. This observation aligns with previous research, emphasizing the potentially pivotal role of social bonds in cardiovascular health, particularly during interactions with partners and spouses. 44 The reduction in partner‐associated stress following extended separation might reflect a similar adaptation process in humans. Although the initial period following marital separation might be associated with increased stress and HF risk due to disrupted social bonds, individuals might gradually adapt over time, thereby lessening the subsequent HF risk.

Intriguingly, healthy lifestyle might alleviate the harmful effects of marital failure on HF risk. A wide range of mechanisms have been proposed to explain how a healthy lifestyle can reduce the risk of HF, including reducing oxidative damage, and enhancing anticoagulation and anti‐inflammatory effects. 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 The compounding effects of poor dietary and behavioral patterns that trigger stress, along with the physiological toll of emotional distress on cardiovascular health, 49 may amplify the risk of HF. There is a need for comprehensive strategies, including measures to improve dietary patterns and lifestyles, especially in the context of major life transitions such as marital failure.

Furthermore, our study found that participants with higher educational levels might have increased susceptibility to marital instability associated with HF risk, which was also indicated by prior research. 13 , 50 , 51 This pattern may stem from the importance that individuals with advanced education attach to marital stability and emotional intimacy. These individuals often aspire to derive more profound emotional and substantial support from their partners. As such, marital failure could inflict more far‐reaching psychological and emotional distress on this particular demographic. 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 However, this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the low number of HF events and nonsignificant effect size in the college and above group.

Our study found no significant sex difference in the association between marital failure and subsequent HF, which contrasts with findings from Italian studies reporting stronger cardiovascular impacts of unstable or nonmarital status in women than in men. 52 , 53 Conversely, a recent Chinese cohort demonstrated that cardiovascular protection associated with stable marital status was confined to men rather than women. 54 These discrepancies may stem from sociocultural differences in the studied populations. In addition, ethnic and regional differences in modifiable risk factors such as lifestyle and genetic predispositions contribute to sex differences in this association. 55 Caution is warranted when interpreting these nonsignificant sex differences due to limited representation of women, which reduced statistical power to detect significance. Future cross‐cultural studies are needed to elucidate these mechanisms.

Multiple interrelated mechanisms may underlie the observed association between marital failure and subsequent HF risk. Marital failure is associated with low social support and high social isolation risk, 56 exacerbating cardiovascular vulnerability through behavioral and biological pathways: sustained psychosocial stress from marital failure activates sympathetic nervous system signaling, elevates cortisol, and increases proinflammatory cytokines, all of which adversely affect cardiovascular health. 57 In addition, the loss of spousal support networks amplifies stress‐related physiological disruptions. 58 Those who have experienced marital failure often lose access to care and financial support previously provided by their spouses, restricting their health care utilization. 59 This delays early intervention for modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension and diabetes, and reduces diagnostic monitoring of subclinical cardiac dysfunction.

There are some limitations of this study. First, the marital status classification based on questionnaires could not distinguish stable nonmarital cohabitation from singlehood, which could introduce exposure misclassification bias. However, the low prevalence of cohabitation arrangements in China likely attenuated the effect of this bias. 60 Second, the incidence of HF might be underestimated, as individuals with mild and asymptomatic HF not seeking medical help were not recorded. Moreover, the inability to identify subtypes of HF (eg, HF with reduced ejection fraction versus preserved ejection fraction) in this study limited more detailed analysis. Third, previous research indicates that psychological and emotional issues, such as social isolation and depression, 61 , 62 were more likely to be associated with high HF risk following marital transitions. Regrettably, the Kailuan Study did not collect data on psychological or emotional well‐being before 2016, limiting our capability to examine the psychological factors underlying the observed association. Fourth, combining self‐reported data into defining hypertension and diabetes could introduce bias. Although we adjusted for multiple covariates, we cannot exclude the possibility of residual confounders given the observational study design. In addition, although the interaction between sex and marital status in relation to HF was not significant, there was limited statistical power to assess sex‐specific associations due to the small sample size of female participants. The results should be interpreted with caution. Further, the generalizability of our findings might be limited because the Kailuan Study is not a nationally representative cohort.

CONCLUSIONS

In this large cohort study, we found that marital failure was associated with a higher risk of HF compared with marital stability, and the association appeared to be attenuated over time. Importantly, a healthy lifestyle might alleviate the potential harmful effects of marital failure on HF risk. Our findings underscore the critical importance of stable marital relationships in adult health, as those experiencing loss of a stable spousal relationship may face greater health challenges.
