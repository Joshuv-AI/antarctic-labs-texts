Role of aspartic acid and glutamic acid in dentin remineralization: A systematic review of emerging therapeutic approaches
Authors: Aruna Krishnan, Sandhya Raghu, Jayalakshmi Somasundaram, G. Manobharathi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The aim of the systematic review was to evaluate the role of aspartic acid and glutamic acid in dentin remineralization, focusing on biomimetic strategies that use noncollagenous protein analogs. Registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024547624), this review searched PubMed/Medline, Web of Science, and Scopus up to February 2025 using PICO-based terms. In vitro studies investigating aspartic acid and glutamic acid in dentin remineralization were included. Study quality was assessed with the QUIN tool. Screening and selection were conducted independently by two reviewers followed by the data extraction and data synthesis. The systematic review was reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses 2020 guidelines. Out of 430 articles identified, 10 met the inclusion criteria. Quality assessment revealed 4 studies with low risk of bias, 5 with medium, and 1 with high risk. Aspartic acid and glutamic acid were identified to enhance hydroxyapatite crystallization and mechanical properties of dentin. The polymer-induced liquid-precursor process using poly-L-aspartic acid restored up to 91% of the elastic modulus in demineralized dentin. Glutamic acid improved both intrafibrillar and extrafibrillar mineralization, and its combination with tricalcium phosphate notably enhanced mineral deposition. Low-risk studies confirm that aspartic and glutamic acids effectively promote dentin remineralization by mimicking natural biomineralization processes, showing potential for clinical applications. Future research should aim to standardize these treatments through clinical trials to confirm their long-term efficacy and safety.

INTRODUCTION

Dental caries is a noncommunicable, multifactorial disease caused by the interaction of dietary sugars, biofilm activity, and host factors, resulting in progressive demineralization of dental hard tissues.[1] Teeth undergo continuous cycles of mineral loss and gain; however, when demineralization exceeds remineralization, structural breakdown occurs. Dentin, composed of 70% hydroxyapatite (HA), 20% organic matrix (mainly type I collagen), and 10% water, becomes increasingly susceptible. In early stages, mineral loss occurs with intact collagen, but prolonged demineralization leads to enzymatic breakdown of collagen fibrils, compromising mechanical integrity.[2]

Remineralization is the natural process of restoring minerals lost during demineralization. Conventional approaches – such as fluoride, bioactive glass (BAG), and peptides – primarily promote surface-level repair and have limited penetration into the dentin matrix.[34] As dentin is more porous and collagen-rich compared to enamel, successful repair requires deeper, intrafibrillar mineral deposition.

Two main theories explain this process. The classical (ion-based) theory relies on preexisting HA crystals as templates, whereas the nonclassical pathway emphasizes amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) nano-precursors infiltrating collagen fibrils to enable intrafibrillar remineralization without seed crystals.[45] This shift has driven interest in biomimetic agents that mimic noncollagenous proteins (NCPs), which naturally regulate mineral nucleation and growth through acidic functional groups.[56]

Proteins like dentin matrix protein 1 and dentin phosphophoryn are rich in phosphorylated serine and threonine residues that guide HA crystal formation.[7] Synthetic analogs – such as polyacrylic acid and poly-L-aspartic acid – replicate these effects by stabilizing ACP precursors and guiding their infiltration into collagen. Functional groups such as phosphate, carboxyl, amido, and hydroxyl exhibit calcium-binding affinity and enable mineral deposition within demineralized dentin.[89]

Amino acids such as aspartic acid and glutamic acid abundant in NCPs – have emerged as promising biomimetic tools due to their ability to modulate ACP transformation into organized HA crystals and promote mechanical recovery.[101112]

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review was to evaluate the role of aspartic acid and glutamic acid in dentin remineralization, focusing on biomimetic strategies that use NCP analogs.

The effectiveness of the two amino acid therapies was examined in in vitro studies. The search was conducted across several reputable databases, including PubMed/Medline, Web of Science, and Scopus, with a focus on identifying articles published up to February 2025. Screening and selection

The search and screening process was conducted independently by two reviewers, achieving a strong level of agreement (κ = 0.81), which reflects near-perfect consensus. Both reviewers conducted title, abstract, and full-text screening independently. Inter-rater reliability was quantified using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ = 0.81). Disagreements were resolved through discussion or involvement of a third reviewer. The screening followed a systematic four-stage removal process, beginning with the evaluation of titles and abstracts obtained from the electronic search. This process was guided by the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure the selection of relevant articles.

In Stage 1, citations that were irrelevant to the study were immediately excluded. During Stage 2, two independent reviewers thoroughly assessed all titles and abstracts against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were discarded, while those that required further evaluation were retrieved for full-text assessment. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consulting a third reviewer to ensure accuracy and minimize selection bias. This process ensured methodological rigor and alignment with best practices for systematic reviews.

The articles shortlisted from Stage 1 were reviewed by two independent reviewers to verify their eligibility before proceeding to Stage 3. At this stage, studies with inadequate references, flawed study designs, or missing critical data were excluded from further analysis.

In Stage 4, the refined articles underwent a thorough review, and relevant data were extracted from each study. The clinical methodologies of the included studies were critically analyzed, considering the various outcome measures and interventions employed. This multistage selection process ensured a detailed and comprehensive evaluation, enhancing the methodological rigor and overall quality of the systematic review.

Data extraction

Both reviewers were involved in a meticulous data extraction process. Initially, the first examiner extracted the data from the selected articles, followed by cross-verification by the second examiner to ensure accuracy and completeness. All the articles meeting the inclusion criteria underwent this standardized extraction process. The data were systematically organized using Microsoft Excel 2013, ensuring uniformity and facilitating ease of analysis. Each data point was meticulously recorded under specific headings, capturing essential information such as the author, year of publication, amino acid used, mode of application, group, assessment of remineralization, and results of assessment of remineralization. This structured approach ensured that all critical details were systematically organized for analysis.

Assessment of risk of bias

The QUIN tool was employed to evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies [Tables 2 and 3]. Each study was assessed against 12 criteria, with scores assigned as follows: Adequately specified = 2 points, inadequately specified = 1 point, not specified = 0 points. Based on total scores, studies were categorized as low risk (>70%), medium risk (50-74%), or high risk (<50%). Two reviewers independently scored the studies. In cases of scoring discrepancies, consensus was reached through discussion. A third reviewer was consulted when agreement could not be reached.

Thula-Mata et al. evaluated the effect of poly-L-aspartic acid within the context of the PILP process using human dentin discs. Their study demonstrated that 14-day treatment resulted in approximately 20% greater mineral occlusion of dentinal tubules compared to the 7-day protocol.[10]

Burwell et al. used dentin blocks from extracted human teeth and applied poly-L-aspartic acid through the PILP approach over multiple time intervals. Nanoindentation and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses showed a progressive increase in mechanical properties, with a 91% recovery of elastic modulus after 28 days. The study confirmed both intrafibrillar and extrafibrillar mineralization.[11]

Sun et al. studied human dentin disks treated with a solution containing polyacrylic acid and L-glutamic acid. The combination enhanced HA formation and mechanical strength, with TEM and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) confirming improved intrafibrillar mineralization compared to controls.[12]

Zhang et al. employed Raman spectroscopy to assess the remineralization effects of DL-aspartic acid on demineralized human dentin disks. The group treated with a combination of aspartic acid and BAG showed significantly increased Raman intensity, indicating greater mineral deposition.[14]

Bacino et al. tested poly-L-aspartic acid both in immersion form and modified dental cement on artificially created lesion in extracted human dentin disks. Nanoindentation and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses revealed increased elastic modulus (up to 6 GPa), reduced lesion shrinkage, and formation of a reaction layer at the dentin-cement interface.[15]

Ahdi et al. assessed the remineralization process in human dentin blocks using polyaspartic acid over 3, 7, and 14 days. X-ray diffraction confirmed consistent HA crystal size (~20–30 nm), and mineral infiltration was observed to increase over time.[16]

Babaie et al. conducted a comparative evaluation on shallow and deep artificial dentin lesions blocks using PILP-based treatments containing polyaspartic acid. They reported up to 27% increase in mineral density and a 21% reduction in lesion volume. The E-modulus improved to 6.3 GPa over 3 months.[17]

Su et al. studied bovine dentin treated with poly (γ-glutamic acid) and tricalcium phosphate composites. The group exhibited significantly higher calcium and phosphorus content, and microhardness compared to other groups, as well as reduced mineral loss.[18]

Zhao et al. examined human dentin treated with DL-aspartic acid and calcium phosphate. The study observed accelerated transformation of ACP to HA, reducing treatment duration from 7 to 2 days. Collagen stability improved with reduced enzymatic degradation.[19]

Zhang et al. examined human dentin disks treated with poly L-aspartic acid calcium complex (PCCP group) or poly L-aspartic acid stabilized ACP (PILP group). On examining at the end of 15 days, heavy remineralization to a depth of 120 μm has been demonstrated with PCCP group compared to PILP group showing inadequate remineralization.[20]

Collectively, the reviewed studies suggest that aspartic and glutamic acid derivatives enhance dentin remineralization through improved mineral nucleation, infiltration into collagen fibrils, and increased mechanical recovery. While findings consistently support the potential of amino acid-based therapies, variability in dentin models, treatment protocols, and outcome measures was observed.

Assessment of risk of bias

According to the QUIN tool, four articles had low risk of bias, five articles exhibited a medium risk of bias, and one article showed a high risk of bias [Table 3]. The studies with medium risk primarily lacked comprehensive blinding and randomization procedures, which compromised the validity of their results. In addition, these studies often failed to specify their sample size calculations and sampling methods adequately, diminishing the robustness of their conclusions. In contrast, the study categorized with a high risk of bias demonstrated significant deficiencies in detailing operator and outcome assessor information, which compromised the objectivity of the findings. The absence of effective blinding and randomization further increased the potential for selection and measurement biases, critically undermining the study’s reliability. Due to the limited number of studies included, along with variability in research design, the specific amino acids used, and the outcomes evaluated, a meta-analysis was not feasible. This heterogeneity arose from several factors across the included studies. First, each study assessed different amino acids, such as poly-L-aspartic acid, DL-aspartic acid, and poly-γ-glutamic acid. Second, the studies employed differing application methods (e.g., immersion in PILP solution and modification of restorative materials) and durations of treatment, ranging from 3 days to 28 days. Third, the outcome measures varied, including microhardness, elastic modulus, mineral content analysis, and imaging techniques such as SEM, TEM, and Raman spectroscopy. In addition, demineralization protocols and control groups differed between studies, with some employing fluoride or BAG controls, while others used artificial saliva or no treatment controls. These variations collectively contributed to clinical and methodological heterogeneity, precluding meaningful statistical pooling of data through meta-analysis. The lack of standardized methodologies and the small number of samples available further restricted our ability to perform a comprehensive meta-analysis.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review provides a comprehensive evaluation of the impact of aspartic and glutamic acids on the biomimetic remineralization of demineralized dentin, incorporating data from nine studies. These amino acids are the key components of NCPs involved in natural biomineralization processes and have been shown to influence mineral phase formation and distribution within the dentin matrix.

The aggregated findings across the studies demonstrate a consistent effect of aspartic and glutamic acids in facilitating the transformation of ACP into HA, the essential mineral phase associated with dentin remineralization. Tavafoghi and Cerruti emphasize the role of these negatively charged amino acids in modulating the nucleation and growth of HA, reflecting their abundance in NCPs responsible for initiating crystallization within the collagen matrix.[21] However, the studies reviewed do not delve into detailed biochemical pathways, leaving a knowledge gap in the mechanistic understanding of these processes. Similarly, Parati et al. demonstrated the ability of poly-γ-glutamic acid to inhibit enamel demineralization through calcium chelation, suggesting possible extrapolation to dentin, although this requires direct investigation.[22]

The methodological quality of the included studies, assessed using the QUIN tool, revealed that four studies had a low risk of bias, five had medium risk, and one had high risk. Strengths included clearly stated aims, detailed experimental protocols, and well-defined outcomes. However, limitations were noted in sample size justification, lack of randomization and blinding, and insufficient reporting of operator or outcome assessor involvement. Studies with lower risk, such as Bacino et al.[15] and Zhao et al.,[19] applied rigorous methodologies and detailed reporting. In contrast, Thula-Mata et al. scored less due to insufficient transparency.[11] Future studies should aim to standardize protocols, report methodological parameters clearly, and incorporate the long-term evaluations to support translational potential.

The reviewed studies suggest that aspartic acid or glutamic acid can improve the mineral content of dentin and may contribute to mechanical reinforcement. He et al. provide evidence that NCPs influenced by acidic amino acids can enhance the structural integrity of remineralized dentin.[5] However, comprehensive mechanical testing remains limited, and the correlation between biochemical modulation and mechanical properties has not been robustly established. Further work is needed to substantiate these relationships.

Aspartic and glutamic acids appear to facilitate the stabilization of mineral precursors and influence mineral deposition during remineralization. George et al. describe the role of phosphorylated acidic proteins in directing apatite formation, supporting the rationale for exploring amino acid-based analogs.[23] Nonetheless, these findings are largely limited to in vitro models, and further investigation is needed to determine their clinical relevance and biocompatibility in vivo.

This synthesis aligns with the existing literature underscoring the involvement of acidic amino acid domains in mineralization processes. For instance, Fujisawa et al. reported that osteonectin, rich in glutamic acid, binds HA and regulates mineral nucleation.[24] These findings support the potential utility of amino acid-based compounds in enhancing dentin biomineralization but require further validation.

Studies demonstrate the synergistic effects of Casein Phosphopeptide-ACP (CPP-ACP) with fluoride on enamel suggest a promising approach that could be applicable to dentin.[25] Given dentin’s greater organic content and porosity, there is a critical need for strategies that penetrate deeply and stabilize at the collagen matrix level. Therefore, the potential of CPP-ACP to facilitate deep calcium and phosphate ion deposition within the dentin structure merits detailed investigation.

The use of self-assembling peptides like P11-4, which promote enamel remineralization through a biomimetic approach, highlights the need for similar strategies in dentin.[262728] These peptides scaffold HA formation, a process potentially beneficial for the complex microstructure of dentin. This underscores the importance of researching biomimetic compounds tailored specifically to target dentin’s unique properties. The exploration of strontium-doped nanohydroxyapatite, has shown efficacy in enamel and dentin studies.[29] It has similar mineral composition and ability to integrate seamlessly with dentin structures and has reduced cytotoxic effects compared to traditional fluoride treatments. However, current evidence is insufficient to confirm its efficacy in this context.

CPP – ACP fluoride has been reported to have lesser remineralization potential.[30] However investigation on the synergistic effect of CPP-ACP and plant-derived agents in eroded dentin and observed significant microhardness recovery and mineral deposition.[31] The potential of combining organic biomolecules with inorganic carriers to enhance nucleation and remineralization efficiency in dentin substrates needs exploration. Hemalatha et al. found that bioactive materials such as CPP-ACP and calcium sodium phosphosilicate produced greater remineralization in dentin than enamel. This was attributed to the higher organic content and porosity of dentin, which favor deeper mineral penetration and underline the importance of substrate-specific biomimetic design.[32]

This review focused on amino acid-based approaches, few of the included studies directly compared them with established remineralization agents such as fluoride or BAG. For example, Zhang et al. reported enhanced mineralization with a combination of aspartic acid and BAG compared to BAG alone.[14] Recently, the Polyelectrolyte° Calcium Complex Precursor (PCCP) has been assessed as an alternative to conventional PILP-based remineralization. Incorporation of poly-L-aspartic acid in PCCP and PILP demonstrated that the PCCP process achieved deeper and more uniform remineralization of dentin artificial caries lesions (ACLs) up to 120 μm in 15 days. Unlike the PILP process, which showed limited remineralization in the superficial layer, the PCCP-treated group exhibited dense HA deposition, superior mechanical recovery, and enhanced mineral-to-matrix ratios, as confirmed by SEM-Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy, TEM, FTIR, and nanoindentation. These findings underscore the potential of ultrahigh-concentration PAsp-Ca complexes in overcoming diffusion barriers and accelerating functional dentin restoration.[20]

Limitations

This systematic review has few limitations. The small number of included studies limits the generalizability of the findings. All included studies were in vitro experiments, which restricts the translation of these results to clinical practice. Moreover, there is a potential risk of publication bias, as studies with negative or inconclusive findings may not have been published or indexed. Furthermore, methodological limitations were observed in several studies, including inadequate blinding, lack of randomization, and insufficient reporting of operator details, which may have introduced bias. Finally, the heterogeneity in study designs, interventions, and outcome measures precluded a quantitative synthesis through meta-analysis. Future research should aim to address these limitations by conducting well-designed in vivo and clinical studies with standardized protocols to validate the findings of amino acid-based dentin remineralization therapies.

Future research directions

Although current evidence highlights the potential of aspartic acid and glutamic acid–based strategies for dentin remineralization, further investigations are warranted to translate these findings into predictable clinical outcomes. Future studies should standardize the experimental protocols, establish standard concentrations, application times, and assessment methods for comparison across studies. Validation of long-term outcome is important, long-term assessment of stability of intrafibrillar mineralization, enzymatic degradation analysis, and thermocycling assessment. Well-designed animal studies and randomized clinical trials to confirm the efficacy and safety of Asp and Glu modified scaffolds, cements, and biomimetic systems in the clinical settings must be conducted. Combination of Asp or Glu with other biomimetic remineralizing agents such as BAG,[33] CPP-ACP,[32] and peptide analogs[26] to enhance nucleation and crystal growth. Further, interaction with adhesive and restorative systems to improve bond stability and prevent secondary caries must be assessed. Collectively, these directions may bridge the gap between laboratory findings and clinical translation, ensuring that Asp- and Glu-based biomimetic remineralization approaches can be effectively incorporated into restorative dentistry.

CONCLUSION

The present systematic review highlights the potential of aspartic and glutamic acids in promoting biomimetic remineralization of demineralized dentin through the modulation of mineral precursor stabilization and HA formation. The included studies consistently demonstrate that these amino acids enhance mineral deposition, influence mechanical recovery, and mimic the role of NCPs in dentin. However, most findings are derived from in vitro models, and direct comparative analyses with conventional agents such as fluoride and BAG remain limited.

While these results are promising, further research involving standardized protocols, long-term in vivo evaluation, and translational studies is essential to substantiate the clinical utility of amino acid-based remineralization therapies. Until such data are available, these approaches should be regarded as emerging adjuncts with potential application in future dentin regeneration strategies.
