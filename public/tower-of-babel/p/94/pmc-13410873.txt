Association between hemoglobin-to-red blood cell distribution width ratio trajectory patterns and in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with cirrhosis: a retrospective cohort study based on MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD
Authors: Zhikai Zhang, Guangren Peng, Meihua Zhou, Xiaomin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCritically ill patients with cirrhosis face an elevated risk of mortality. The hemoglobin-to-red blood cell distribution width ratio (HRR) has emerged as a potential biomarker in various critical illnesses. However, the prognostic significance of longitudinal HRR changes in patients with cirrhosis remains underexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between HRR trajectory patterns and in-hospital mortality in this population.MethodsData were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV, version 3.1 (MIMIC-IV v3.1), and the eICU-Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD). Group-based trajectory modeling was applied to identify distinct HRR trajectory patterns. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to estimate survival rates, and multivariate Cox regression was employed to evaluate associations. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the findings.ResultsA total of 1,826 patients from MIMIC-IV and 669 patients from eICU-CRD were included. Background

Cirrhosis, characterized by irreversible fibrosis of hepatic tissue, represents a complex interplay of hepatic dysfunction, portal hypertension, and increased susceptibility to infections [1]. As the end stage of chronic liver disease, cirrhosis poses a substantial global health challenge [2, 3]. Patients who progress to decompensated cirrhosis frequently develop life-threatening complications such as ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and esophageal variceal bleeding [4]. Complications related to cirrhosis constitute the primary cause of hospitalization, with in-hospital mortality rates reaching approximately 26% [5]. The clinical course of critically ill patients with cirrhosis is notably heterogeneous; while some patients remain relatively stable, others experience rapid deterioration leading to liver failure [6]. Despite recent advances in critical care [7], there remains an urgent need for reliable prognostic tools to support clinical decision-making and optimize resource allocation.

The hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio (HRR) is an emerging clinical biomarker that has demonstrated prognostic value in patients with sepsis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and cardiovascular disease [8–10]. During inflammatory states, proinflammatory cytokines (PICs) disrupt iron metabolism and impair erythropoiesis, resulting in anemia [11]. Anemia secondary to liver dysfunction and systemic inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of cirrhosis [12, 13]. Although HRR provides an integrated measure reflecting both anemia and systemic inflammation, its relationship with clinical outcomes in patients with cirrhosis remains insufficiently elucidated.

Most existing studies on HRR have relied on measurements taken at specific time points, which fail to capture the dynamic evolution of HRR or reflect the complex pathophysiological changes underlying liver cirrhosis. To overcome this limitation, group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) provides a sophisticated analytical approach [14, 15] for identifying distinct clusters based on repeated-measures data. Furthermore, longitudinal trajectory analysis offers a more detailed understanding of the mechanisms underlying cirrhosis progression.

Therefore, this study aimed to use GBTM to categorize distinct HRR trajectory patterns. Clinical data of patients with cirrhosis were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) to explore the association between HRR trajectories and prognosis among critically ill patients with cirrhosis admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). This work may enhance current risk stratification models and guide more individualized management strategies for this high-risk population.

Methods and materials

Study design and population

This retrospective study utilized data from the MIMIC-IV version 3.1 and the eICU-CRD [16–18]. The MIMIC-IV cohort includes all patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022. For each patient, detailed hospitalization data—including vital signs, laboratory test results, treatment records, and other relevant clinical information—were collected. All patient data were de-identified through random encoding to ensure privacy protection; therefore, no additional ethical approval was required for this study. Database access was granted to the first author, Zhikai Zhang (ID: 14905962), after registration and completion of the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative program. The eICU-CRD contains information on 200,859 ICU admissions involving 139,367 patients between 2014 and 2015 across 208 hospitals in the United States. The MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD cohorts were mutually exclusive.

This study included 3,388 and 1,654 patients with cirrhosis from first-time ICU admissions in the two databases, respectively. Diagnoses were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (Supplementary Table 1–2). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) ICU stay < 48 hours; (2) fewer than five recorded hemoglobin or RDW measurements; (3) missing hemoglobin or RDW data; (4) age < 18 years; and (5) missing in-hospital mortality data. Measurement of HRR and endpoints

The HRR was calculated using hemoglobin and red blood cell distribution width values, according to the formula: HRR [19]=hemoglobin (g/L) / red blood cell distribution width (RDW, %). While both hemoglobin and RDW are individually associated with outcomes in critically ill patients, we chose to use the HRR as the primary longitudinal indicator for several reasons. First, hemoglobin and RDW represent distinct but interrelated pathophysiological processes. Their ratio may reflect a more holistic clinical state and provide more information than a single variable [10]. Second, although emerging evidence suggests that HRR is a robust predictor of mortality across various critical illnesses, its prognostic utility remains uncertain in cirrhosis patients. Building on these, our study aimed to fill this knowledge gap by examining the association between the dynamic trajectory patterns of this novel index and clinical outcomes. The primary endpoint of this study was in-hospital mortality, defined as death from any cause occurring during the corresponding hospital stay.

Variable extraction

Variables considered as potential covariates were extracted from both databases using structured query language queries within the first 24 hours after ICU admission. Demographic characteristics included sex, age. Vital signs and physiological parameters included heart rate, respiratory rate, mean blood pressure, and body temperature. Comorbidities included myocardial infarction (MI), chronic heart failure (CHF), cerebrovascular disease, diabetes mellitus (DM), renal disease, and malignancy. Clinical severity scores comprised the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II). Laboratory parameters included hematocrit, white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, and potassium levels. Treatment variables included the use of vasopressors and mechanical ventilation. For HRR calculations, the first hemoglobin and RDW values recorded within each 24-hour interval during the first seven days after ICU admission were extracted to establish the GBTM.

GBTM

GBTM was employed to classify longitudinal HRR measurements into distinct subgroups, with each subgroup representing a similar developmental trajectory over time. The analysis was performed using the “lcmm” package in R, which identifies heterogeneous longitudinal patterns through maximum likelihood estimation combined with the expectation–maximization algorithm. The trajectories were modeled using polynomial functions that incorporated both linear and quadratic time terms. The optimal number of trajectory groups was determined based on several model fit indicators, including lower absolute values of the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and log-likelihood (LL), as well as practical thresholds such as subgroup sample sizes exceeding 5% and an average posterior probability greater than 0.7 [20, 21]. In addition, the clinical interpretability and plausibility of the model were comprehensively evaluated to support final model selection [22].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distributions were expressed as means ± standard deviation, whereas skewed continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. To compare HRR trajectory groups, the chi-square test was used for categorical variables, one-way analysis of variance for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis H test for non-normally distributed variables. Individuals with missing HRR or mortality data were excluded from the analysis. Remaining missing covariates, representing less than 30% of the dataset, were imputed using multiple imputation [23].

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed to estimate in-hospital survival probabilities among patients with cirrhosis across HRR trajectory groups, and differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. In the eICU-CRD external validation cohort, a landmark analysis was prespecified at 21 days post-admission to address potential immortal time bias inherent in trajectory analyses. Because assignment to HRR trajectory groups requires sufficient longitudinal data to characterize a patient’s hemodynamic course, standard survival analysis from admission may inadvertently condition on a period of guaranteed survival. The 21-day threshold was selected based on established clinical evidence, as this interval has been previously employed as a short-term prognostic landmark in hepatology critical care research [24–26].

The Cox proportional hazards model was applied to assess the association between HRR trajectories and the risk of in-hospital mortality. The proportional hazards assumption was verified through inspection of log–log survival plots and Schoenfeld residual tests. Three multivariate Cox regression models were developed: Model 1 was adjusted for sex, age, heart rate, respiratory rate, mean blood pressure, and body temperature. Model 2 included additional adjustments for MI, CHF, cerebrovascular disease, DM, renal disease, malignancy, SOFA score, and SAPS II. Model 3 was further adjusted for hematocrit, WBC count, platelet count, BUN, creatinine, potassium, mechanical ventilation, and vasopressor use. Covariate selection was guided by prior literature, established associations with the outcome, or a change exceeding 10% in the effect estimate. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF); variables with VIF values ≥ 5, indicating multicollinearity, were excluded from the final model.

To explore potential effect modifications, subgroup analyses were performed to examine whether the association between HRR trajectories and in-hospital mortality varied across different participant characteristics. Subgroups were defined according to sex (female vs. male), age (<65 vs. ≥65 years), MI (no vs. yes), CHF (no vs. yes), DM (no vs. yes), and etiologies (alcoholic cirrhosis vs. other/unspecified etiologies). In addition, two sensitivity analyses were performed, one excluding patients with missing covariates and the other excluding patients with malignancies, followed by repeated multivariable Cox regression.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; http://www.R-project.org), and Free Statistics software, version 2.3 (Beijing FreeClinical Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). A two-tailed test was applied, and a P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Kaplan–Meier analysis

In the MIMIC-IV cohort, Kaplan–Meier survival curves for in-hospital mortality demonstrated statistically significant differences among HRR trajectory groups (P < 0.001). At each major assessment point, patients in trajectory 1 exhibited significantly lower survival rates than those in the other groups (Fig. 3A). These results indicate that persistently low HRR levels are associated with an increased risk of mortality. In the eICU-CRD external validation cohort, a landmark analysis at 21 days was performed to address immortal time bias (Fig. 3B). Among patients surviving beyond 21 days, survival probability for the trajectory 1 group was lower than the other two groups (P = 0.025). Multivariate Cox regression analysis of HRR trajectories and mortality in patients with cirrhosis

To assess the independent association between HRR trajectories and in-hospital mortality, three multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed (Table 3). The trajectory 1 group served as the reference category for comparison. In the MIMIC-IV cohort, after adjustment for all potential confounders (Model 3), both trajectory 2 (HR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.42–0.76) and trajectory 3 (HR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.51–0.77) were associated with a reduced risk of in-hospital mortality. To evaluate the external validity and transportability of the findings, the analysis was replicated in the eICU-CRD cohort. In the fully adjusted model, compared with the trajectory 1 reference group, patients in trajectory 3 were significantly associated with reduced mortality (HR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.40–0.92). While those in trajectory 2 showed a similar directional trend that did not reach statistical significance (HR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.35–1.13) (Table 4). Subgroup analysis

To assess the robustness of the association between HRR trajectories and mortality across different demographic and clinical subgroups, subgroup analyses were performed (Fig. 4). Consistent with the primary findings, no significant interactions were observed between HRR trajectory and mortality when stratified by sex, CHF, MI, DM, or etiologies (all P for interaction > 0.05). Although the P for interaction by age was less than 0.05, this finding likely lacks clinical significance due to multiple testing and the consistent direction of association across age groups. Similarly, in the eICU-CRD cohort, the results were consistent (P for interaction > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating that the prognostic value of HRR trajectories is robust.Fig. 4Forest plot for subgroup analysis of the association between HRR and in-hospital mortality. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by separately excluding patients with missing covariates and patients with malignancies, after which multivariable Cox regression analyses were repeated (Supplementary Tables 4–5). Consistent with the primary results, HRR trajectories exhibited similar associations with in-hospital mortality, maintaining the same direction and magnitude of effect as in the main analysis. These findings confirm that the results are robust.

Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort study, GBTM was employed to examine the association between longitudinal HRR trajectories and mortality risk among critically ill patients with cirrhosis. Using GBTM, patients were classified into three distinct HRR trajectory groups: trajectory 1, characterized by persistently low HRR levels; trajectory 2, characterized by a descending pattern from a relatively high HRR level; and trajectory 3, characterized by a descending pattern from a moderate HRR level. The trajectory patterns observed in both primary databases were consistent, supporting the robustness and external validity of the findings. The results demonstrated that patients in trajectory 2 and trajectory 3 had significantly lower risks of in-hospital mortality compared with those in trajectory 1. Additionally, subgroup analyses revealed no significant interactions across demographic and clinical subgroups, indicating the stability of these associations. Overall, our findings highlight the potential importance of continuous HRR monitoring in patients with cirrhosis and suggest that HRR trajectory assessment may serve as a useful tool for early risk stratification and the development of individualized therapeutic approaches in critical care settings.

Growing evidence has recognized the HRR as a valuable prognostic indicator in severe diseases of the digestive system. For example, a retrospective cohort study by Xiao et al. reported that higher HRR levels were independently associated with reduced long-term mortality in critically ill patients with gastrointestinal bleeding [27]. Similarly, Xiong et al. found that declining HRR levels were associated with increased 30-day mortality in patients with acute pancreatitis [28]. Moreover, Yu et al. demonstrated that incorporating HRR significantly improved the prognostic accuracy of the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease score for predicting mortality in patients with hepatitis B virus-related decompensated cirrhosis [29]. These findings align with the results of our study. Recent research has also highlighted the importance of longitudinal biomarker trajectories in predicting clinical outcomes. Continuous monitoring of anemia is critical in intensive care, as it represents a common complication and a significant predictor of mortality. Hemoglobin levels provide a direct measure of anemia severity. Studies have shown that in patients with hemorrhagic brain injury without sepsis, persistently low hemoglobin levels predict poor outcomes, whereas higher levels are associated with lower mortality, even in the presence of a mild declining trend [30]. RDW has been recognized as a potential biomarker for assessing inflammatory responses. In patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury, a rapid increase in RDW has been correlated with the highest mortality, whereas a gradual rise was linked to better inflammation control [31]. HRR is defined as hemoglobin divided by RDW. As a composite ratio, HRR reflects the degree of anemia and systemic inflammatory response [32]. This combined approach supports a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying pathophysiology. HRR is derived from routine complete blood count, making it readily available at the bedside, cost‑effective, and efficient for monitoring. However, unlike conventional laboratory parameters with standardized reference ranges, HRR currently lacks universally accepted criteria for defining normal or abnormal values. Despite these limitations, it remains a useful bedside tool for risk stratification.

The dynamic changes in HRR levels are driven by multiple interrelated mechanisms. On one hand, in cirrhosis, inflammation and oxidative stress act in a mutually reinforcing manner, leading to elevated levels of PICs and reactive oxygen species [33]. Together, these factors suppress erythropoietin production and accelerate membrane lipid peroxidation, which increases erythrocyte fragility and rupture [34, 35]. Consequently, red blood cell production is impaired, while immature erythrocytes are prematurely released into the peripheral circulation, resulting in a concurrent decline in hemoglobin and an increase in RDW. On the other hand, malnutrition, hypoproteinemia, and micronutrient deficiencies—arising from impaired hepatic synthetic function and metabolic dysregulation in decompensated cirrhosis—may also contribute to variations in hemoglobin and RDW levels [36, 37]. Moreover, metabolic abnormalities interact synergistically with inflammation and oxidative stress to induce myelosuppression, erythropoietin hyporesponsiveness, and functional iron deficiency, effects that persist throughout the processes of erythropoiesis and erythrocyte degradation [38, 39]. Hemoglobin and RDW, as pivotal hematological parameters in complete blood count analysis, exhibit interconnected pathophysiological dynamics under inflammatory conditions. Rather than a simple isolated decrease in hemoglobin or increase in RDW, the aforementioned mechanisms jointly lead to decreased hemoglobin levels and increased erythrocyte heterogeneity, thereby elevating RDW. This synergistic dysregulation collectively contributes to a low HRR [40], suggesting anemia and inflammation are often mechanistically intertwined in critically ill populations [41]. Collectively, these mechanisms support our finding that patients with persistently low HRR trajectories experience the poorest outcomes in both cohorts.

Patients in trajectory 1 presented with the lowest HRR levels at ICU admission, which remained persistently low throughout the observation period. This pattern may reflect impaired compensatory mechanisms, systemic inflammation, or resistance to therapeutic interventions. Chronic inflammation in cirrhosis upregulates hepcidin, leading to functional iron deficiency and impaired hemoglobin synthesis [42]. Concurrently, erythropoietin resistance induced by inflammatory mediators impairs erythropoiesis and increases erythrocyte size heterogeneity, thereby elevating RDW levels [43]. Furthermore, supportive ICU treatments, including massive packed red blood cell transfusion, may precipitate dilutional coagulopathy, which in turn exacerbates the persistent low HRR state [44]. In contrast, patients in trajectories 2 and 3 demonstrated higher baseline HRR levels followed by moderate declines. These patterns likely reflect relatively preserved bone marrow reserve and responsiveness to erythropoietic stimuli. The gradual HRR decline over time may represent an effective adaptive response to oxidative stress, as evidenced by stabilized hemoglobin concentrations accompanied by slightly elevated RDW values [45]. Although HRR values declined in both trajectories, the overall levels remained higher than those observed in trajectory 1, which may explain the lower mortality rates in trajectories 2 and 3. Interestingly, variations in HRR trajectory patterns between the two cohorts warrant attention. Specifically, patients in the trajectory 3 group of the eICU-CRD cohort exhibited a more rapid decline in HRR. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in patient demographics and clinical management practices across participating centers within the eICU-CRD network [46], including variations in blood sampling frequency and laboratory calibration standards. Likewise, the protective association of trajectory 2 with mortality did not reach statistical significance in the eICU-CRD cohort (HR = 0.63, P = 0.120), likely due to the smaller number of events in this trajectory and between-cohort heterogeneity. These findings emphasize the necessity of context-specific interpretation when applying trajectory-based models across diverse critical care populations.

Furthermore, cirrhosis etiology may differentially shape HRR trajectory patterns via distinct pathophysiological drivers. Chronic alcohol consumption in alcoholic cirrhosis patients induces direct myelotoxicity and nutritional deficiencies, thereby compromising hematopoietic function [47]. Notably, macrocytosis characterized by a marked elevation in RDW has been reported to be more common in alcoholic cirrhosis than in other etiologies [48]. Conversely, viral hepatitis-related cirrhosis typically follows a more indolent inflammatory course, whereas metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)-related cirrhosis involves chronic low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance. Both conditions may retain comparatively better bone marrow reserves, leading to higher initial HRR measurements that frequently align with trajectories 2 or 3. Critically, our etiology-stratified sensitivity analysis revealed no significant interaction effects, despite these potential distributional variations. This consistency across etiological subgroups confirms that the HRR trajectory-mortality relationship remains robust regardless of cirrhosis etiology.

HRR trajectories provide a dynamic framework for clinical risk stratification. Patients exhibiting the trajectory 1 pattern may benefit from early intensive management. Clinicians should simultaneously monitor nutritional status and oxidative stress while optimizing transfusion practices and correcting microcirculatory perfusion impairments. Persistent reductions in HRR warrant proactive investigation into underlying causes. For patients with trajectory 2 or 3 patterns, continued monitoring remains essential. When fluctuations or rates of decline in HRR exceed established thresholds, prompt evaluation for acute clinical events—such as gastrointestinal variceal bleeding or major cardiovascular complications—is recommended [49, 50], requiring immediate diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.

Compared with traditional static analytical models, GBTM enables the identification of temporal patterns that may reflect clinical improvement or deterioration. However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as with all studies using electronic health record data, residual confounding by indication may exist, particularly regarding time-sensitive interventions such as antibiotic escalation or diuretic use. Second, the inability to distinguish among cirrhosis etiologies due to limitations in data granularity may have affected prognostic estimates, given that different etiologies entail distinct management approaches and outcomes. Studies with detailed etiological information are warranted to overcome this limitation. Third, the determination of HRR trajectory clusters was primarily based on statistical rather than biological considerations, which may have obscured underlying pathophysiological differences. Fourth, this study could not definitively differentiate whether the stable low HRR trajectory primarily reflects persistent anemia or severe inflammation. Our analyses suggest that the elevated mortality risk associated with this trajectory likely arises from the convergence of both pathological processes. Future studies incorporating serial inflammatory and iron biomarkers are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms. Fifth, because both databases were derived from U.S. populations, the generalizability of these findings to non-American populations may be limited. Finally, the observational design of this study precludes causal inference. Although the association between HRR trajectories and mortality appears robust and biologically plausible, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate these conclusions.

Conclusion

In this study, GBTM was used to identify three distinct HRR trajectories across two large cohorts. The findings indicate that patients with persistently low HRR levels were at an increased risk of in-hospital mortality. In contrast, higher baseline HRR levels were associated with a lower risk of mortality, even when modest declines occurred after ICU admission. Overall, HRR trajectory patterns may serve as an accessible and cost-effective approach for early risk stratification in critically ill patients with cirrhosis.
