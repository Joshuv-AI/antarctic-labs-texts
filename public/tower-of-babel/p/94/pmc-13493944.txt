Scale-bridging interface design enables high-performance sustainable thermoelectrics
Authors: Gang Wu, Airan Li, Xinzhi Wu, Takao Mori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Improving energy efficiency and reducing resource waste motivate sustainable thermoelectric technologies for harvesting low-grade heat. Yet achieving high-performance and durable systems remains challenging due to coupled transport and multiscale optimization. Here, we establish a scale-bridging interface-centric design strategy that links macroscopic junction engineering and microscopic interface modulation. Based on n-type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 as the representative system and guided by comparative evaluation of device-level contact interfaces, we translate Mg2Ni into a scale-bridging interfacial phase in n-type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2: it forms low-resistance and thermally stable external junctions while, as an internal interfacial modifier, alleviating grain-boundary carrier scattering and strengthening phonon scattering. This scale-bridged interface design yields an average zT of 1.5 over 300 to 723 K with high thermal stability. By applying Mg2Ni as both an internal modifier and an external barrier layer, a two-pair module delivers a stable conversion efficiency of 8.3% when coupled with p-type MgAgSb, comparing favorably with representative Bi2Te3-based thermoelectric modules in terms of conversion efficiency under comparable temperature differences. This work establishes a bridging interface design framework that links device contact engineering with internal interface modulation, providing a practical route toward efficient and durable thermoelectric energy conversion.

Introduction

Sustainable societal development depends critically on reducing resource waste and improving energy efficiency. Thermoelectric technologies, which directly convert heat into electricity via the Seebeck effect1–7, offer a compelling pathway for harvesting low-grade waste heat and enhancing overall energy utilization. However, achieving high-performance and sustainable TE systems remains challenging, as transport properties are intrinsically coupled and optimization targets span multiple length scales, from atomic-level defects to device-level junctions8–12. Interfaces are among the key factors in this challenge. They are ubiquitous across TE systems, from grain boundaries and second-phase interfaces within materials to material-barrier layer junctions in devices. These interfaces simultaneously govern charge and phonon transport and dictate long-term stability through interfacial reactions, diffusion and mechanical degradation13,14. Despite their shared functional roles, interface engineering has largely proceeded along two disconnected directions: device-level optimization focusing on barrier layers for low contact resistance and robust bonding15–17, and materials-level design targeting internal interfaces to modulate carrier and phonon transport18–20.

This separation has hindered progress toward unified high-performance sustainable TE systems. In principle, the physical principles governing macroscopic junction interfaces, such as minimizing interfacial resistance and suppressing chemical instability, should also apply to microscopic interfaces. Establishing such interface-centric design offers a promising opportunity to simultaneously optimize performance and stability across different length scales, shown in Fig. 1a. In this work, we focus on n-type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2-based thermoelectric materials, which are widely regarded as promising candidates for practical low-grade heat recovery owing to their high performance in the near-room-temperature to medium-temperature range21–27. Moreover, their TE properties are highly sensitive to interfaces, including both grain boundaries within the material and junctions between the material and barrier layers. Owing to the volatility of Mg, grain boundaries in Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 exhibit high energy barriers that significantly suppress carrier mobility, particularly near room temperature28–33. In addition, material-barrier-layer interfaces degrade during aging, primarily due to Mg evaporation and migration. Recent studies, like Chen et al. 34 that have demonstrated that Mg2Ni can provide a low-resistance and thermally stable external contact interface for Mg-based thermoelectric devices, propose active diffusion control strategies to improve stability. However, a unified approach that links junction interfaces to internal interface engineering, aiming to simultaneously enhance performance and ensure long-term stability, remains lacking.

Here, we first screen different candidate material-barrier layer combinations under identical conditions to identify an interface phase that simultaneously satisfies the requirements of low contact resistance and high thermal stability. From this comparative evaluation, Mg2Ni is selected as the most suitable interface component. We then translate this device-level interface selection to the microscale by incorporating Mg2Ni as an internal interfacial modifier within n-type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, thereby alleviating grain-boundary carrier barriers and strengthening phonon scattering through Mg2Ni-derived internal interfaces. This scale-bridging interface design enables simultaneous improvements in efficiency and durability. The resulting materials achieve an average zT of 1.5 across 300-723 K together with enhanced thermal stability, shown in Fig. 1b–d. When implemented in a device configuration using Mg2Ni as both interfacial modifier and external barrier layer, a two-pair module delivers a stable conversion efficiency of 8.3% when coupled with p-type MgAgSb, comparing favorably with representative Bi2Te3-based modules evaluated under comparable temperature differences35–37. This work establishes a general framework for scale-bridging interface design, offering a pathway toward high-performance and sustainable TE energy conversion.

Results

Screening barrier layers

To implement the scale-bridging interface design, we first screen candidate interface materials at the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 contact junction under identical conditions. These candidates are assessed according to two key requirements: electrical transparency, reflected by low contact resistivity and interfacial stability, reflected by minimal resistance increase and stable microstructures after thermal aging. Therefore, the selected interface phase should not only serve as a stable external contact layer but also provide a rational basis for subsequent modulation of the internal microstructural interface. Based on the scale-bridging interface design shown in Fig. 2a, we first seek an interfacial phase that can satisfy device-level contact requirements and subsequently serve as the basis for internal interface engineering. We therefore screen seven candidate barrier layers, including Fe, 304 stainless steel (304 SS), Nb, Ti, Ni, Mg2Cu and Mg2Ni at the device contact interface in Fig. 2b–f38–43, where contact resistivity, aging stability and interfacial reactions can be directly evaluated. We perform a screening of these seven candidate barrier layers using the same Mg3(Sb, Bi)2-based matrix and the same aging condition. Contact-resistivity measurements before and after ageing at 623 K for 6 days are combined with cross-sectional SEM and elemental mapping to identify interfacial microstructures without obvious degradation features such as thick reaction layers, pores, cracks or abnormal elemental interdiffusion that simultaneously satisfy both transport and stability criteria, shown in Fig. 2 and Figs. S1–4.

Initially, seven barrier-layer candidates show clear differences in the as-sintered state. Contact-resistance measurements reveal that Mg2Ni and Mg2Cu exhibit the lowest contact resistivity among all candidates, whereas Fe, 304 SS, Nb, Ti and Ni all give substantially higher values, shown in Fig. 2e and Figs. S3, 4. However, low initial contact resistance alone is insufficient for practical thermoelectric devices. The cross-sectional SEM observations are further used to verify the chemical and mechanical reliability of the interfacial phase. Here, chemical reliability is assessed by the absence of pronounced interfacial reactions, elemental interdiffusion or thick reaction layers, which would otherwise increase contact resistance and accelerate aging-induced degradation. For instance, Ni shows evident interfacial reaction with the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 matrix displayed in Fig. S1c even in the as-sintered state, indicating limited chemical reliability. Ti exhibits poor interfacial bonding and cracking, shown in Fig. S2c, suggesting that the physical integrity of the contact is intrinsically fragile.

Thermal ageing further separates the candidate materials. After aging at 623 K for 6 days, Mg2Ni maintains a low contact resistivity and a relatively clean interfacial region, whereas the other barrier layers show different forms of degradation, displayed in Fig. 2b–d and Figs. S1, 2. Specifically, the aged Mg2Cu/Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 interface exhibits compositional redistribution, including Mg depletion and partial Cu depletion near the interface. This thermally activated redistribution may be related to the relatively low melting or eutectic temperatures of Mg-Cu alloys containing the Mg2Cu phase. In addition, Fe, 304 stainless steel and Ni exhibit interfacial reactions and elemental interdiffusion, whereas Nb leads to pore formation. Ti fails to preserve an effective contact after aging owing to interfacial fracture. These results demonstrate that Mg2Ni provides the best balance of low contact resistance and thermal stability among the examined candidates under identical conditions. We therefore select Mg2Ni as the interface component for the subsequent scale-bridging design, in which the same phase is further introduced into Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 to regulate internal grain-boundary and second-phase interfaces.

Modifying microstructural interfaces

Having selected Mg2Ni through the device-level interface screening, we then translate this device-level insight into internal microstructural engineering of n-type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, shown in Fig. 3a. The key question is whether an interface phase selected for low contact resistance and thermal stability at external junctions can also construct internal interfaces that facilitate electronically transparent and structurally stable internal interfaces. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, pristine grain boundaries in Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 tend to hinder carrier transport through grain boundary barriers and can also serve as pathways for Mg migration. By contrast, introducing Mg2Ni at near-grain boundaries is expected to lower the interfacial carrier barrier and improve local interfacial stability. TEM characterization of the Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.492Te0.008 + 1 wt% Mg2Ni sample reveals abundant nanoscale second phases distributed in the vicinity of grain boundaries in Fig. 3b and Figs. S5, 6. The elemental maps in Fig. 3b, elemental mapping of Area 1, show Ni enrichment associated with these grain-boundary nanoscale phases, together with signals from the surrounding Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 matrix. Local phase identification is further provided by HRTEM analysis of a representative grain-boundary-associated second phase: the corresponding FFT pattern and measured lattice spacing can be indexed to crystalline Mg2Ni in Figure S5d. These observations indicate the formation of Mg2Ni-derived nanoscale internal interfaces near grain boundaries, providing the microstructural basis for the modified carrier and phonon transport discussed below. The incorporation and reliability of Mg2Ni are further supported by the sintering behavior. Mg2Ni addition systematically increases SPS displacement and post-sintering extrusion in Fig. S7. XRD of the expelled phase identifies mainly Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 and Mg2Ni in Figure S8, and the calculated Mg-Bi-Sb-Ni phase diagram suggests their stable coexistence shown in Fig. S9. At a larger length scale, EPMA elemental mapping in Figs. S10, 12 shows that Mg, Sb, Bi and Te remain relatively homogeneous in the matrix, whereas Mg2Ni second phases are distributed broadly and rather uniformly throughout the sample after addition. These results support that Mg2Ni remains chemically reliable with the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 matrix and is incorporated as a dispersed interfacial phase. The electrical transport results show that this internal interfacial modifier effectively reduces grain-boundary carrier blocking in Fig. 4 and Fig. S13. Electrical conductivity is markedly enhanced over the entire measured temperature range after Mg2Ni addition in Fig. 4a. More importantly, the temperature dependence of conductivity evolves from grain boundary scattering behavior in the pristine sample to a trend approaching acoustic phonon-dominated transport in the Mg2Ni-modified samples. Quantificationally, Seto analysis based on the ln(σT1/2)-1/kBT relationship gives a grain-boundary barrier height of about 23 meV for the pristine sample, consistent with reported values23,44, whereas the barrier becomes nearly negligible after Mg2Ni modification in Fig. S14. Notably, EBSD analysis shows that the average grain size slightly decreases after Mg2Ni addition in Fig. 3c, d, excluding the reasons for grain coarsening. Hall measurements further reveal simultaneous increases in carrier concentration and mobility in Fig. S13b, suggesting that Mg2Ni-modified interfaces not only alleviate carrier blocking but may also influence local carrier balance. The increased carrier concentration may be partly related to interfacial charge transfer arising from the work-function difference between Mg2Ni and n-type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 in Fig. S1545,46. Accordingly, Mg2Ni (φMg2Ni = 3.58 ~ 4.28 eV, depending on surface termination) exhibits lower work-function values than the n-type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 (φMg3(Sb, Bi)2 = 5.38 eV) matrix. Before contact, the Fermi level of Mg2Ni is therefore higher than that of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 relative to the vacuum level. After interfacial contact, electrons tend to transfer from Mg2Ni to the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 matrix until Fermi-level alignment is reached, leading to downward band bending and electron accumulation near the semiconductor side of the interface. This anti-barrier contact can help increase the local electron concentration and improve carrier transport across Mg2Ni-derived internal interfaces. Possible suppression of compensating Mg vacancies near Mg2Ni-rich regions may also contribute to the increased carrier concentration. The mobility reaches a highly competitive level compared with reported Mg3(Sb, Bi)2-based materials at similar carrier concentrations in Fig. 4b30–33,44,47–50, and the weighted mobility is enhanced over the whole temperature range in Fig. 4c. Beyond the optimization of electronic transport, Mg2Ni modification also reshapes heat transport through internal interfaces. Although the interfacial phase is selected for electrical transparency and interfacial stability, its presence within the material also introduces additional phonon scattering while maintaining a relatively transparent pathway for carriers. After Mg2Ni addition, the κtot decreases in Fig. S16b, despite the increased κele contribution arising from the higher electrical conductivity in Fig. S16c. This reduction, therefore, mainly originates from the lattice component, which is strongly suppressed in the Mg2Ni-modified samples shown in Fig. 4d. Since the measured sound velocities remain nearly unchanged in Fig. S16a, the lower κlat is more reasonably attributed to shortened phonon relaxation time51. Microstructurally, the reduced grain size increases the density of grain boundary scattering, while Mg2Ni-derived second phases with sizes from tens to hundreds of nanometers decorate grain-boundary regions, further interrupting phonon transport.

We use the Debye-Callaway model to quantify the contribution of different phonon-scattering mechanisms. As shown in Fig. S17, the lattice thermal conductivity of the pristine sample is well reproduced by considering U, PD, GB, DC and DS scattering. For the Mg2Ni-modified sample, even after updating the corresponding parameters to reflect the modified state, the κlat values are still higher than the experimental data. After introducing the additional nanoprecipitate-scattering term, the experimental κlat of the Mg2Ni-modified sample can be well reproduced. These results indicate that the Mg2Ni-induced changes in grain size and dislocation-related scattering contribute to the decrease in lattice thermal conductivity, while Mg2Ni-derived nanoprecipitates provide the major additional phonon-scattering contribution responsible for the further suppression of κlat. Consistently, the calculated phonon mean free path decreases markedly after Mg2Ni addition in Fig. S16d, confirming that grain boundary-associated Mg2Ni phases convert internal interfaces into effective phonon-scattering regions. Importantly, this enhanced phonon scattering does not compromise electronic transport. Instead, the carrier mean free path increases after Mg2Ni modification in Fig. S18a. The results also reflect in μw/κlat30,31,47,50,52–54, and the favorable position in the μw versus 1/κlat plot demonstrates an effective decoupling between electron and phonon transport in Fig. 4e and Fig. S18b. Benefiting from this scale-bridging interface design, the Mg2Ni-modified samples achieve simultaneously improved electrical and thermal transport. The optimized composition combines a high power factor with suppressed lattice thermal conductivity, yielding a peak zT of about 1.9 and maximum B of 1 in Fig. 4f and Fig. S18c. In addition, these results further distinguish Mg2Ni modification from previously reported additive-based interface-engineering strategies for Mg3(Sb, Bi)232,55. For instance, Mg2Cu nano-sintering primarily improves electrical transport through liquid-phase sintering-assisted grain growth, whereas nanoscale transition-metal additions have been associated with reaction/diffusion-mediated defect regulation. In contrast, Mg2Ni markedly suppresses the grain-boundary potential barrier without relying on grain coarsening, while Mg2Ni-derived nanoscale internal interfaces provide additional phonon-scattering sites. As shown in Fig. 1b, the Mg2Ni-modified sample is slightly higher than that of the reported Mg2Cu-modified material. Beyond internal transport regulation, Mg2Ni can also be implemented at external device junctions, providing a basis for scale-bridging interface design. Thermal cycling in Fig. 4g-h further shows that the Mg2Ni-modified materials maintain stable transport properties, consistent with the chemical reliability and interface stability suggested by the sintering, phase-equilibrium and microscopy results. Therefore, the role of Mg2Ni is not limited to that of a conventional second-phase additive. Rather, Mg2Ni acts as a bridging interfacial phase: it is selected at the device-contact level for electrical transparency and stability, and then translated to internal grain-boundary and second-phase interfaces to suppress carrier barriers, scatter phonons, and improve material stability. These results validate the proposed scale-bridging interface design and provide the material-level basis for the device performance discussed below.

Unified interface design for efficient and durable TE devices

The Mg2Ni-enabled scale-bridging interface design is evaluated at the device level by integrating the Mg2Ni-modified n-type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 legs with Mg2Ni barrier-layer interfaces in Fig. 5a. Owing to the optimization of internal grain boundary interfaces, the optimized n-type material achieves a high average zT of 1.5 from 300 to 723 K, comparing favorably with representative Mg3(Sb, Bi)2-based systems in Fig. 5b25,31,33,46,47,56,57. A two-pair module is fabricated by coupling the Mg2Ni-modified n-type legs with p-type MgAgSb legs14,58, and the optimized module geometry is shown in Fig. S19. Benefiting from the low contact resistance from junctions and high-performance of materials, the measured I-V and I-P curves show typical TE power-generation behavior under different temperature differences, and the module delivers a maximum conversion efficiency of 8.3% together with a maximum power density of 0.36 W cm-2 in Fig. 5c, d and Fig. S20. Single-leg device measurements are also performed in Fig. S21. The device reaches a conversion efficiency of 11.5% at a temperature difference of 400 K, demonstrating competitive power-generation capability under comparable operating conditions. The conversion efficiency and output power density of the present module are compared with representative reported thermoelectric devices in Fig. 1c and Fig. 5e, respectively8,16,38,52,59–67. Under a temperature difference of 300 K, the two-pair Mg3(Sb, Bi)2/MgAgSb module delivers a stable conversion efficiency. Benefiting from both the stability of materials and junctions, during repeated thermal cycling, the open-circuit voltage, internal resistance and heat flow remain stable in Fig. 5f–h, indicating stable electrical output, robust contact interfaces and reliable heat transport, further confirming the sustainability relevance of this unified interface design. Discussion

In summary, we establish a scale-bridging interface design framework by linking device contact engineering with internal microstructural interface modulation. This strategy is built on two shared interfacial requirements, electrical transparency and interfacial stability, that are essential for translating high material performance into durable device operation. Through barrier-layer screening, Mg2Ni is identified as a low-resistance and thermally stable internal interfacial modifier in n-type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2. The scale-bridging interface design suppresses grain-boundary barrier, strengthens interfacial phonon scattering and improves thermal stability, yielding an average zT of 1.5 over 300–723 K. When implemented simultaneously as an internal modifier and an external barrier layer, Mg2Ni enables a two-pair Mg3(Sb, Bi)2/MgAgSb module with a stable conversion efficiency of 8.3% at ΔT = 300 K and a maximum power density of 0.36 W cm-2. This work offers an interface-centric and broadly transferable route toward efficient, durable and more sustainable thermoelectric energy conversion for low-grade heat recovery.

Methods

Sample synthesis

Polycrystalline Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.492Te0.008 + x wt% Mg2Ni (x = 0, 1, 2) samples were synthesized from high-purity Mg, Sb, Bi, Te and Mg2Ni powders (all 99.99%). The starting materials were ball-milled for 5 h in a SPEX 8000 mixer mill. The milled powders were then loaded into a 10 mm graphite die in an Ar-filled glovebox and consolidated by spark plasma sintering (SPS-1080, SPS SYNTEX INC) at 993 K under 60 MPa for 20 min.

Measurement and characterization

Electrical conductivity (σ) and Seebeck coefficient (S) were measured in Helium atmosphere using a ZEM-3 instrument (ULVAC-RIKO). Total thermal conductivity was obtained from κtot = DρCp, where D was measured by laser flash analysis (LFA 467, Netzsch), ρ by the Archimedes method, and Cp was estimated from Cp = 3NR (1 + 1.3 × 10−4 T − 4 × 103 T −2)/M68. Carrier concentration (n) and mobility (μ) at room temperature were determined by Hall measurements in a PPMS (Quantum Design). Structural and microstructural analyses were performed by X-ray diffraction (XRD), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), electron probe microanalysis (EPMA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Sound velocity at 300 K was measured using an ultrasonic system (UVM-2, Ultrasonic Engineering Co., Ltd.).

TE module fabrication

Seven representative barrier-layer materials were screened in this work. For Ti, Ni, Nb, Fe and 304 stainless steel, the barrier layer and the Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.492Te0.008 matrix were joined directly by one-step sintering. Owing to the lower melting points relative to the sintering temperature of the matrix, Mg2Cu and Mg2Ni were processed using a two-step route. The matrix material was first consolidated into an ingot, and Mg2Cu or Mg2Ni powder was then placed on both sides of the ingot, followed by a second sintering step at 773 K under 60 MPa for 10 min.

For module fabrication, two pairs of n-type Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.492Te0.008 + 1 wt% Mg2Ni legs (3.5 × 3.5 × 6.5 mm3) and p-type MgAgSb legs (3.4 × 3.4 × 6.5 mm3) were assembled into a thermoelectric module. Specifically, the p-type MgAgSb material was prepared with a nominal composition of MgAg0.97Sb0.99 (referred to as MgAgSb) using Mg turnings, Ag powder and Sb shots, together with 0.75 wt% stearic acid C18H36O2 as an additive. The starting materials were loaded into a ball-milling jar under an Ar atmosphere and mechanically alloyed for 5 h using a SPEX-8000D mill. For fabrication of the p-type legs used in the module, the as-milled MgAgSb powder and the Sb barrier layer were consolidated together through a one-step spark plasma sintering process at 573 K under 60 MPa for 5 min. Device conversion efficiency was determined using a Mini-PEM system (ADVANCE RIKO, Japan). The transport properties of the p-type MgAgSb counterpart can be seen in previous report62.
