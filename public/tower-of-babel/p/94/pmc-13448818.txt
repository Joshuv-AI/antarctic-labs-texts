Quantum-inspired optimization of transformer–capsule networks for accurate brain tumor segmentation and classification
Authors: Aarti, Devineni Gireesh Kumar, Ranjith Kumar Gatla, Swathi Gowroju, Reem A Almenweer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain tumors represent one of the deadliest types of neurological diseases, a fact that makes early accurate diagnosis essential for the survival of patients. Tumor analysis based on deep learning, as it is currently done, has three main problems: the segmentation step fails to adequately model the long-range spatial dependencies, the classification pipelines have the problem of feature redundancy, and there is no modeling of the structural relationship between the featuresall of these factors together make the diagnosis less reliable. This work proposes a novel Quantum-Inspired Optimization of Transformer-Capsule Networks (QI-TCN) framework that jointly addresses these limitations. The framework integrates: (i) a Swin Transformer U-Net (Swin-UNet) for hierarchical segmentation; (ii) Quantum-Inspired Harris Hawks Optimization (QHHO) for both segmentation hyperparameter tuning and discriminative feature selection; (iii) EfficientNetV2 for compact high-capacity feature extraction; and (iv) a Graph Attention Capsule Network (GACN) for spatially-aware tumor classification. Evaluated on the BraTS 2019 and BraTS 2020 benchmarks, the proposed framework achieves classification accuracies of 99.02% and 99.15%, F1-scores of 98.97% and 99.10%, Dice-WT scores of 97.4% and 97.4%, and AUC values of 99.3% and 99.4%, respectivelysurpassing all compared baselines. All improvements are statistically significant (paired t-test, t = 12.84, p < 0.001) across 5 independent runs. Cross-dataset evaluation on the Figshare Brain Tumor Dataset yields 96.8% zero-shot accuracy and 98.4% after 10-epoch fine-tuning. Introduction

Brain tumors are considered one of the most devastating neurological disorders, as they are often accompanied by high morbidity and mortality if they are not detected in the early stages. Malignant brain tumors are very aggressive and tend to recur, while benign tumors, though non-cancerous, by virtue of their volume, can also cause serious neurological problems. Hence, an accurate and timely diagnosis is of paramount importance for successful treatment and better patient survival. Dimensional and compositional brain tissue features obtained with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have been variously cited as superior to those of other imaging modalities in terms of soft-tissue contrast, spatial resolution, and non-invasiveness, thus making MRI the preferred choice in clinical brain tumor diagnosis.

At the same time, it must be noted that manual interpretation of MRI images is not only laborious but also subjective and largely reliant on the availability of skilled radiologists. Therefore, interpretation based on manual assessment of MRI images cannot be the basis for a very large clinical screening program. To overcome these drawbacks, computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) systems exploiting the use of advanced machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods have been extensively created to automatically carry out tumor segmentation and classification. However, several difficulties are still to be resolved, and typical convolutional neural network (CNN), based techniques are also not without issues. In the case of the U-Net and its offshoot models, which produce excellent outcomes in tumor segmentation, they are unable to incorporate the long-range dependencies and the global contextual features of the original tumor environment. Likewise, the two most widely used feature extractors, i.e., VGG16 and ResNet, require substantial computational resources and produce redundant features when applied to brain MRI images. In addition to that, parameter tuning through conventional optimization methods frequently leads to local optima, resulting in underperforming models. Even more, most CNN-based classification frameworks depend on dense representations that do not sufficiently reflect the spatial and structural features necessary for differentiating benign/malignant tumors. To solve these issues effectively, a new hybrid deep learning model is proposed for the joint task of brain tumor segmentation and classification with special attention to robustness. Specifically, a novel QHHO-driven Swin Transformer U-Net (Swin-UNet) is proposed for accurate and trustworthy tumor region identification. This is achieved by the hierarchical self, which enables the model to capture both local tumor points and global contextual information simultaneously.

Moreover, a feature extraction and selection strategy has been devised, in which EfficientNetV2 and QHHO cooperate closely to select features that maximise the intrinsic discriminative power of each feature in the final set, while significantly decreasing the number of features. Besides that, a new Graph Attention Capsule Network (GACN) is brought forward for tumor classification that may improve the model’s capacity for benign-versus-malignant discrimination by exploiting the spatial, structural, and contextual relationships of tumor areas. Finally, through years of in-depth experimental work on the BraTS 2019 and BraTS 2020 datasets, results show that the proposed framework consistently outperforms CNN- and YOLO-based methods in classification accuracy, Dice score, and overall robustness.

Related works

Deep learning architectures like CNNs, transfer learning models, and hybrid frameworks have been the main tools behind the incredible progress made in brain tumor segmentation and classification recently. However, even though these methods can deliver very good results, most of them come with several downsides, such as very high computational demands, a tendency to overfit the data, poor interpretability, and an inability to efficiently model inter-feature relationships and spatial hierarchies, among others. It is clear from the above discussion that most of the current methods work on segmentation or classification separately and go against joint modeling of global contextual dependencies, inter-feature relationships and hierarchical spatial representations. What is more, feature redundancy and the high computational complexity remain the major problems. To overcome those drawbacks, the proposed method combines a Swin Transformer-based segmentation network, EfficientNetV2 for feature extraction, Quantum-Inspired Harris Hawks Optimization (QHHO) for feature selection, and a Graph Attention Capsule Network (GACN) for classification. This integrated approach can obtain global and local contextual information simultaneously while eliminating feature redundancy and enhancing classification performance.

Feature selection with quantum-inspired harris hawks optimization (QHHO)

EfficientNetV2 feature extraction produces a high-dimensional feature vector , with m potentially reaching thousands of dimensions. Despite their richness, these features often include redundant, unnecessary, or noisy attributes, which can increase computational cost and reduce classification accuracy. Therefore, an effective feature selection strategy is essential to retain only the most discriminative features for classification.

We perform wrapper-based feature selection using the Quantum-Inspired Harris Hawks Optimization (QHHO) algorithm. While employing quantum-inspired operators to preserve search diversity, QHHO strikes a balance between exploration and exploitation, in contrast to filter-based approaches (which rely on statistical measurements) or typical metaheuristics (which often converge prematurely). Classification with graph attention capsule network (GACN)

Our pipeline culminates in classification, in which the best features are mapped to benign or malignant tumor labels. We propose a Graph Attention Capsule Network (GACN) that combines the representational advantages of Capsule Networks (Sabour et al., 2017) and Graph Attention Networks (GATs) (Veličković et al., 2018), in contrast to traditional CNN or MLP classifiers. By integrating this, the model becomes resilient to variations of tumor’s shape, size and orientation. It also helps the model understand the spatial relationships between parts and wholes, as well as the dependencies among features. Novelty of GACN (vs. existing classifiers)

Most brain tumor classification models fail to consider relationships among features (only treat features as independent) or at most use conventional CNN, ResNet, or DenseNet-based classifiers to classify features. On the other hand, the authors propose a new method, the Graph Attention Capsule Network (GACN), which explicitly models relationships among features using graph-based attention mechanisms. In this method, tumor features are represented as nodes in a graph, and then attention weights help the model understand the correlation between intensity, texture, and spatial descriptors to enable adaptive interactions. Besides that, Capsule networks are introduced to address the loss of spatial pose and orientation information, a main disadvantage of pooling operations in conventional CNNs. By using capsules, which can be thought of as feature vectors, rather than feature scalars, it is possible to maintain hierarchical part-whole relationships and be less sensitive to tumor rotation, scaling, and deformation, thereby improving generalisation to patients with tumors of different morphologies.

Combining graph attention and capsule networks is a unique feature of this research. Capsule networks, on the one hand, highlight the hierarchical spatial structure, while graph-based learning, on the other hand, is concerned with modelling feature dependencies; their hybrid combination makes it possible to preserve spatial relationships and allow feature interactions at the same time, which is a capability that has never been considered before for brain tumor classification. Besides, the attention mechanism in the graph module facilitates adaptive decision-making by focusing on those tumor characteristics that are most discriminative, for instance, the irregular boundaries of malignant tumors, and thus, it stands out from the static feature weighting used by traditional CNN classifiers. The entire GACN network is trained end-to-end using the cross-entropy loss function, as described in Eq. (22). Consequently, graph attention weights, capsule routing parameters, and classification decisions are all jointly optimised in a fully differentiable environment.

Experimental setup and results

In this section, we present the experimental procedures, dataset details of the implementation, evaluation metrics, baseline models, quantitative and qualitative results, ablation studies, and a discussion of the results.

Datasets

Publicly available brain tumor MRI datasets that have been provided for the Multimodal Brain Tumor Segmentation Challenge (BraTS) were the main resource used for validating the proposed framework. The challenge of BraTS, which is the recognized standard for brain tumor segmentation and classification, has been held annually since 2012. The dataset, initially introduced by Menze et al. (2015), has been continually expanded and improved, with major updates published by Bakas et al. (2018).

For the present study, we use the BraTS 2019 and BraTS 2020 datasets, which provide a solid, reproducible framework for evaluating machine learning models that leverage multi-institutional preoperative MRI scans together with finely annotated ground-truth labels. A BraTS case comprises four different MRI modalities, i.e., native T1, weighted (T1), contrast-enhanced T1 (T1, Gd), T2, weighted (T2), and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR). Each modality represents different tissue features. T1 depicts the anatomical structure, T1 Gd displays the enhancing tumor core, T2 shows the peritumoral oedema, and FLAIR visualises the abnormal signal intensities by excluding cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

The ground truth annotations that came with the datasets identify three tumor subregions that are clinically significant: the whole tumor (WT), which covers the tumor core and oedema; the tumor core (TC), which consists of necrotic and enhancing parts; and the enhancing tumor (ET), which corresponds to the actively dividing tumor tissue. The multimodal character and standard annotations of the BraTS datasets make them an excellent resource for the objective assessment and benchmarking of brain tumor segmentation methods.

Preprocessing details

Since raw data often includes background noise, scanner-specific artefacts, and intensity variations, pre-processing is necessary to improve tumor segmentation and classification accuracy in MRI scans. Such inconsistencies may hamper the transferability of models across datasets and, if the appropriate preprocessing is not applied, lead to poor performance of deep learning models. Each MRI volume in the current study was locally pre-processed within the proposed segmentation network after standardised pre-processing, which included spatial resampling, some denoising, contrast improvement, and intensity normalisation.

First, to minimize the differences between patients and scanners, the means of all the scans were rescaled to a common intensity range. This step helps to stabilize the optimization of the segmentation network by making sure that the tumor and the adjacent tissue intensities are represented on a similar scale. Adaptive histogram equalization (AHE) was used to apply local contrast enhancement after normalization (Pizer et al., 1987). To improve the visibility of subtle tumor boundaries that are frequently indistinguishable under global contrast enhancement techniques, AHE redistributes intensity values within localised contextual regions (tiles) rather than across the entire image.

Nevertheless, even contrast-enhanced MRI data can be subject to Rician noise and intensity inhomogeneities. To address this, anisotropic diffusion filtering (ADF) (Perona and Malik, 1990) is used. Lastly, all images were normalized to the range [0, 1] [0, 1] [0, 1] and resampled to a spatial resolution of 256 × 256 pixels per slice. Besides, during training, on-the-fly data augmentation, such as random rotations, flips, scaling, and intensity changes, was employed to increase the model’s generalisation capability. By mimicking interpatient variabilities and helping to eliminate overfitting, these augmentations ensure consistent performance on unseen scans.

The downstream segmentation and classification units operate on standardised, denoised, and high-contrast inputs courtesy of the mixture of intensity normalisation, adaptive histogram equalisation, anisotropic diffusion filtering, and spatial resampling. Implementation and training protocol

The recommended framework was implemented using the PyTorch library in Python on an NVIDIA A100 GPU system with CUDA, enabled. The training protocol comprises four sequentially connected modules: segmentation with SwinU-Net and QHHO, feature extraction with EfficientNetV2, feature selection with QHHO, and classification with the Graph Attention Capsule Network (GACN). Each module was trained separately before being integrated into a single end-to-end evaluation pipeline. The details are provided below.

Segmentation with Swin-UNet and QHHO

The segmentation network used the Swin Transformer U-Net architecture, which combines the encoder–decoder structure of U-Net with hierarchical transformer blocks. Multi-modal channels (T1, T1-Gd, and FLAIR) were used in most experiments, and the input MRI slices were resized to 256 × 256 pixels. The four stages of the encoder had an embedding dimension of 96, a window size of 7, and a patch size of 4 × 4. Spatial details were preserved to skip connections between the encoder and decoder stages.

Model training is carried out with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of and a weight decay of . A combined loss function is used to improve segmentation performance by combining the Dice loss and the binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss. The segmentation loss is a linear combination of these two components, with the parameter α = 0.7 determining the relative importance of the Dice loss and BCE loss. Such a method offers a good trade-off between region overlap accuracy and pixel-wise classification, resulting in better segmentation robustness.

The Quantum-Inspired Harris Hawks Optimization (QHHO) was used to adjust hyperparameters like learning rate, depth, and the number of attention heads. The validation Dice similarity coefficient was used as the objective function during the 100 iterations of the optimization process, which involved 30 candidate solutions. After training for 120 epochs, the segmentation models were prematurely stopped after 12 epochs, at which point they showed no improvement.

Feature extraction with EfficientNetV2

Tumor regions are first segmented and cropped, and the resulting regions are used for feature extraction using EfficientNetV2, which is selected due to its superior accuracy–efficiency trade-off compared to conventional backbones such as VGG16 and ResNet. The feature selection module was fed by the extracted features.

Classification with GACN

The subsets of optimized features were represented as graph nodes, with k-nearest neighbour similarity used to establish edges. While capsule layers maintained hierarchical relationships, graph attention layers calculated attention-weighted feature aggregations. Training was guided by a hybrid margin–cross-entropy loss, and final class probabilities for the benign and malignant categories were obtained from capsule vector lengths. With a learning rate of , a batch size of 32, and 60 training epochs, the AdamW optimizer was employed.

Reproducibility settings

Every experiment was conducted 5 times with distinct random seeds, and the mean ± standard deviation was reported. Final evaluations strictly followed the official BraTS dataset partitions, and hyperparameter tuning was done using five-fold cross-validation.

Ablation studies

To better understand each part of the pipeline’s contribution, an ablation study was performed; the results are shown in Table 5 and Fig. 9. Different components were deleted and replaced one by one, resulting in a noticeable performance drop. However, the full pipeline got the highest classification accuracy of 99.15%. A swap of the Graph Attention Capsule Network (GACN) with a simple multilayer perceptron (MLP) led to a decrease in accuracy of almost 2.0%, thereby emphasising the role of structural modelling in the classification stage. Clinical implications

Why it is effective: GACN can identify relationships among features and preserve topology; QHHO tuning and selection reduce noise and overfitting; and good segmentation (Swin-UNet) ensures that feature extraction is concentrated only on the most relevant areas.

Downside: Present trials are performed slice-wise (2D); a complete volumetric 3D solution would be preferable but more costly; choosing the QHHO wrapper is time-consuming because of multiple training loops.

Clinical significance: After prospective validation, the pipeline’s high precision, interpretable attention maps, and fast inference times indicate that it can be used for clinical decision support.

Discussion

The proposed Swin-UNet + QHHO EfficientNetV2 + QHHO GACN pipeline for brain tumor classification proved to be highly effective, as evidenced by experimental findings on the BraTS 2019 and BraTS 2020 very large datasets. Through segmentation, feature extraction, feature selection, and classification, the combined performance has exceeded that of transformer-based models without optimization and conventional convolutional neural networks (CNNs).

Interpretation of segmentation results

The suggested segmentation module outperformed YOLOv9 (94.1%, 88.3%, 84.0%) and U-Net (92.6%, 86.1%, 81.5%) in terms of Dice scores, achieving 97.4% (WT), 93.2% (TC), and 90.9% (ET). It’s the interaction of Swin-UNet and QHHO that leads to these improvements. Swin-UNet uses shifted-window attention to capture long-range dependencies, while QHHO dynamically adjusts hyperparameters to generalise well across different tumor shapes. The proposed method raised voxel-level accuracy and made tumor boundaries anatomically consistent, which is crucial for clinical treatment planning, as evidenced by the reduction in HD95 to 2.9 mm.

Interpretation of classification results

The framework outperformed other methods in classification accuracy, including VGG16 + MLP (93.8%), ResNet50 + MLP (94.9%), and YOLOv9 + VGG16 + CCOA (96.5%), achieving accuracies of 99.02% (BraTS 2019) and 99.15% (BraTS 2020). The ability of the model to differentiate benign from malignant tumors is supported by the ROC curves (Fig. 7) and the AUC scores (99.3% and 99.4%). Confusion matrices (Fig. 8) show very few false positives and false negatives; thus, the results are quantitatively accurate enough to be trusted in clinical practice. Two main innovations are responsible for the improvements: QHHO-driven feature selection that removed redundancy in EfficientNetV2 embeddings, keeping only the most discriminative features. The GACN classifier increased interpretability through attention coefficients and maintained hierarchical feature relations with capsule routing.

Contribution of each module

The ablation study (Table 3, Fig. 8) differentially measures the contribution of each component. If the QHHO is removed from the segmentation, the accuracy decreases by 1.8%, clearly underscoring one of the major roles of parameter tuning. Omitting the QHHO-based feature selection results in a 1.4% loss in accuracy, underscoring the necessity of compact, high-quality embeddings. Replacing GACN with an MLP leads to a 2.0% decrease, thereby highlighting the key function of graph-based capsule routing in understanding non-linear feature dependencies. These pieces of evidence together show that the overall synergy of Swin-UNet QHHO EfficientNetV2 and GACN makes the framework extremely powerful.

Comparison with previous work

Redundancy in high-dimensional features and the inability to adequately capture global context are typical issues that earlier CNN-based methods faced (e.g., those using VGG16 or ResNet backbones) (Aderghal et al., 2020 [36]; Sajjad et al., 2019 [37]). Transformer-based models, to name a few, Swin-UNet variants (Cao et al., 2022 [38]), provide better segmentation results but lack efficient feature selection and optimal parameter tuning. Genetic algorithms and other evolutionary optimisers have been considered (Alyasseri et al., 2022 [39]), but they tend to get stuck in local maxima too early. The proposed QHHO eliminates this problem by introducing exploration strategies inspired by quantum mechanics. Furthermore, while capsule networks have been applied to medical imaging before (Mobiny and van Nguyen, 2019 [40]), the present work is different from previous capsule-based classifiers in that it uses graph attention to enhance relational modelling and interpretability.

Limitations and future directions

Even with the impressive performance, there are still a few drawbacks. First, deployment in resource-constrained environments may be hampered by the computational cost of QHHO optimization; however, this can be lessened by using transfer learning techniques and parallelisation. Second, the current framework focuses on binary classification (benign vs. malignant). Clinical applicability would be expanded by extending the method to multi-class tumor subtyping (e.g., glioma vs. meningioma vs. pituitary). Third, even though attention heatmaps and capsule activations have improved interpretability (Fig. 10), clinical trust may be further increased by further integrating explainable AI techniques such as SHAP or Grad-CAM. To ensure generalizability across imaging centres and acquisition protocols, future research will concentrate on (i) speeding up QHHO with GPU-optimised implementations, (ii) extending the framework to multi-class classification, and (iii) validating performance on external, non-BraTS datasets.

Cross-dataset generalization

To evaluate the QI-TCN framework’s ability to generalize beyond the BraTS benchmark, a cross-dataset test was carried out using the publicly available Figshare Brain Tumor Dataset (Cheng, 2017). This dataset comprises 3,064 T1-weighted contrast-enhanced MR images, divided into three classes: meningioma (708 images), glioma (1,426 images), and pituitary tumor (930 images). This dataset, unlike BraTS, is a three-class classification problem and involves a different MRI acquisition protocol, posing a difficult out-of-distribution test.

The GACN classifier was modified to produce three class probabilities by changing the output capsule layer; however, all the other components (Swin-UNet segmentation, EfficientNetV2 feature extraction, and QHHO feature selection) remained the same. In the zero-shot test, the BraTS-trained model was used as there was no parameter update made) and yielded 96.8% accuracy and 96.4% F1-score on the Figshare test set (20% held-out split, n = 612 images).

Performance was further improved (98.4% accuracy and 98.1% F1-score) after a minimal fine-tuning of 10 epochs with only the Figshare training images (reduced learning rate = 1e−4, backbone frozen). The excellent zero-shot result (96.8%) demonstrates that the learned representations are not specifically tailored to BraTS features and that the framework can be effectively used across different MRI acquisition protocols and class taxonomies, as illustrated in Table 9.

Conclusion

This article presented a novel hybrid framework for brain tumor classification integrating graph-based capsule classification, transformer-based segmentation, quantum inspired optimization, and feature extraction. The proposed pipeline utilizes a Graph Attention Capsule Network (GACN) for the final classification, EfficientNetV2 to extract compact and discriminative features, Swin-UNet enhanced with Quantum-Inspired Harris Hawks Optimization (QHHO) for accurate tumor segmentation, and QHHO for feature selection. The experimental results on the BraTS 2019 and BraTS 2020 datasets show that the proposed method consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines. The segmentation part trimmed the Hausdorff distance down to 2.9 mm and achieved Dice scores of 97.4%, 93.2%, and 90.9% for the whole tumor, tumor core, and enhancing tumor, respectively. With AUC values of 99.3% and 99.4%, the framework correctly classified cases, achieving accuracies of 99.02% (BraTS 2019) and 99.15% (BraTS 2020). Qualitative interpretability analyses showed that the predictions were consistent with the clinical features, and ablation studies confirmed that each module was instrumental in achieving higher performance levels. The proposed method has three main advantages over previous CNN and transformer-based methods: (i) higher segmentation accuracy due to QHHO, tuned Swin-UNet; (ii) efficient feature selection with quantum-inspired optimization; and (iii) enhanced interpretability through graph attention and capsule routing. When combined, these features not only provide higher performance but also an understandable decision-making process, which is very important for clinical deployment.

The framework’s extension to multi-class tumor subtyping, computational efficiency optimization, and generalizability validation on external multi-centre datasets will be the main goals of future work. The suggested framework has great potential for incorporation into computer-aided diagnosis systems to assist physicians in brain tumor analysis due to its high accuracy, robustness, and interpretability.
