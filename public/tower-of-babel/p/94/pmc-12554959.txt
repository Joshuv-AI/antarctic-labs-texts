Defying the Clock: Restoring Renal Function After 72 Hours of Subacute Renal Thrombosis
Authors: Roberto Cunha, Nuno Coelho, Alexandra Canedo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute renal artery occlusion, especially of thrombotic origin, is rare and often challenging to diagnose. Although early revascularisation is generally recommended, the optimal timing of intervention, especially beyond six hours, remains uncertain.ReportA 51 year old man, an active smoker, with a history of ST elevation myocardial infarction and previous triple coronary artery bypass grafting, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia, developed subacute renal artery thrombosis. He presented to the emergency department, with non-specific symptoms, >72 hours after symptom onset. Computed tomography angiography confirmed right renal artery occlusion with preserved distal arterial patency and contrast enhancement of the renal parenchyma, except in the lower third of the kidney. Endovascular revascularisation was performed with primary placement of a covered stent (Advanta 5×22 mm), resulting in improved estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) from 30 to 77 mL/min/1.73 m2. At the six month follow up, there were no recurrent symptoms, and laboratory results showed eGFR >90 mL/min/1.73 m2.DiscussionA thrombotic aetiology was presumed, based on the patient's cardiovascular history, smoking status, atherosclerotic changes in the renal arteries, and absence of dysrhythmias. While standard practice favours revascularisation within six hours, this case supports that delayed intervention may still be effective in thrombotic occlusions. This aligns with emerging evidence from fenestrated endovascular aortic repair related renal artery thrombosis, which shows favourable outcomes despite delayed treatment. Clinical decisions should therefore consider factors such as preserved renal parenchyma perfusion and distal arterial patency. Timing of revascularisation should be individualised, rather than strictly time dependent.Keywords: Acute renal occlusion, Kidney disease, Late renal revascularisation, Renal artery thrombosis, Renal stenting

INTRODUCTION

Acute renal artery occlusion is a rare condition, and even rarer when caused by in situ thrombosis rather than embolic aetiology.1 The estimated prevalence of renal artery thrombosis is two/1 000 000.2 Embolic events are usually associated with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions, such as dysrhythmias, valvular disease, or cardiomyopathy. In contrast, thrombotic occlusion is often related to the progression of atherosclerotic disease, possibly involving plaque rupture and subsequent in situ thrombosis. Renal artery thrombosis is also a recognised complication following stent placement, particularly in the context of complex aortic interventions such as fenestrated or branched endovascular aortic repair (FBEVAR), where altered flow dynamics or stent kinking may contribute to thrombotic events.

Renal artery occlusions can be classified as acute (presenting within 24 hours), subacute (presenting between 24 hours and two weeks), or chronic (presenting after more than two weeks).3 No standardised guidelines currently exist for the management of acute renal artery occlusion. Given the non-specific nature of the clinical presentation, a high index of suspicion is essential for timely diagnosis. Symptoms such as flank pain, haematuria, acute renal failure with reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), new onset hypertension, fever, or nausea may suggest this condition.1

Prompt revascularisation is generally recommended within six hours of occlusion. However, the benefits of delayed intervention remain controversial and probably depend on the degree of ischaemic tolerance in the renal parenchyma.3

This case report details a subacute renal thrombosis, with symptom progression for >72 hours, that was successfully revascularised, resulting in recovery of renal function. Informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report.

REPORT

A 51 year old man, an active smoker, with a history of ST elevation myocardial infarction and previous triple coronary artery bypass grafting, known hypertension, and dyslipidaemia, presented with epigastric pain radiating to the right lumbar region. He had attended the emergency department on two previous occasions, five and two days before admission, with intermittent right flank and upper abdominal pain, accompanied by nausea and general malaise. Routine laboratory investigations were unremarkable, and the symptoms were attributed to a presumed self limiting viral illness. Symptomatic treatment was initiated both times, but no clinical improvement was observed.

On this third visit, the patient denied nausea and fever. On examination, he was hypertensive (161/97 mmHg), tachycardic (102 bpm), in sinus rhythm with no evidence of dysrhythmias, and presented with right flank tenderness. His pain was difficult to control, requiring opioid analgesia. Laboratory tests revealed leucocytosis (12.72×103/μL), elevated creatinine (2.4 mg/dL), urea (55 mg/dL), an eGFR of 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, and lactate of 3.7 mmol/L. Urinalysis demonstrated microscopic haematuria (6.5 per field), leukocyturia (12 per field), and proteinuria (0.65 g/dL).

Abdominal ultrasound excluded acute appendicitis and cholecystitis, and renal ultrasound revealed no signs of hydronephrosis. Given the persistence of symptoms, computed tomography angiography (CTA) was performed, which revealed renal asymmetry (right kidney measuring 10 cm vs. left kidney 12 cm), infarction of the lower third of the right kidney, and occlusion at the origin of the right renal artery. No embolic phenomena were observed in other arterial territories. Importantly, despite the occlusion, the distal renal artery remained partially patent, allowing residual perfusion, which suggested preserved tissue viability (Figure 1, Figure 2). A diagnosis of subacute renal artery thrombosis was made, probably evolving since the patient's first emergency department visit five days earlier.

Angiographic images in the A early arterial phase and B late arterial phase, where the arrow indicates the perfusion of the renal artery distal to the occlusion.

The patient was immediately anticoagulated with 5 000 IU of unfractionated heparin (UFH). Endovascular revascularisation was performed via left femoral access, with primary implantation of a 5 × 22 mm Advanta V12 covered stent graft (Getinge, Göteborg, Sweden) in the right renal artery, without thrombolysis or aspiration techniques. The procedure resulted in an excellent outcome, evidenced by immediate and homogeneous contrast opacification of the renal artery and parenchyma on the final angiogram, except for the infarcted lower third of the kidney (Fig. 3).

Final angiographic result after primary renal artery stenting, showing renal perfusion, except for the lower third of the kidney, which was infarcted.

The patient was discharged on the fourth post-operative day with improved renal function: creatinine 1.1 mg/dL, urea 51 mg/dL, and eGFR 77 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Fig. 4). At discharge, his blood pressure was normal (108/68 mmHg) without the need for antihypertensive medication. He was prescribed dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel) and advised to continue his pre-existing high intensity statin therapy. At the six month follow up, he remained asymptomatic, with an eGFR >90 mL/min/1.73 m2 and normal blood pressure, still without the need for medication.

DISCUSSION

Spontaneous renal artery thrombosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of renal colic and elevated LDH levels. In addition to atherosclerotic thrombosis, other causes such as trauma, arterial manipulation, vasculitis, and antiphospholipid syndrome may be implicated in renal artery thrombosis.4

Given the rarity of the disease and absence of specific clinical features, diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion. It can be established using CTA, gadolinium enhanced magnetic resonance angiography, or renal angiography.

Regarding treatment, conventional surgical approaches, such as thrombectomy or aortorenal bypass, are now less favoured due to their high morbidity and mortality rates. Moreover, their limited ability to restore renal function, especially when revascularisation is delayed, challenges their role as a first line strategy.5 Percutaneous options include percutaneous thrombectomy, catheter directed thrombolysis, angioplasty, and stenting.

Endovascular revascularisation, although less invasive, may carry risks such as athero-embolism, renal or aortic dissection, arterial rupture, intrarenal haemorrhage, and contrast induced nephropathy. When comparing mechanical thrombectomy and thrombolysis, the former offers immediate thrombus removal and prompt reperfusion, while the latter may be less effective in embolic occlusions and can delay revascularisation.6,7 Aspiration systems such as Penumbra or AngioJet are particularly effective in high thrombus burden cases but increase the risk of vessel wall injury, distal embolisation, and haemolysis related complications. In atherosclerotic plaque cases, adjunctive stenting is often required. Conversely, primary stenting alone enables mechanical revascularisation with limited thrombus manipulation and may be appropriate for localised thrombi.7 Nonetheless, the long term risk of in stent thrombosis must be acknowledged.

Although revascularisation within the first six hours is typically associated with better outcomes, its benefit beyond this window remains controversial. Concerns include haemorrhagic complications after reperfusion of irreversibly damaged parenchyma.2 The study by Ouriel et al. showed that in embolic or traumatic acute renal occlusion, even early revascularisation (less than six hours) did not consistently restore function. Conversely, in thrombotic aetiologies, renal function recovery was more frequent, regardless of the time to intervention, probably due to collateral circulation.5

Recent evidence from patients undergoing FBEVAR further supports the feasibility of delayed revascularisation in thrombotic occlusion. Endovascular strategies such as aspiration thrombectomy, catheter directed lysis, and stent graft relining have shown favourable outcomes in restoring perfusion after prolonged renal ischaemia.8 Additionally, revascularisation of occluded renal stents following complex aortic repair has proven safe and effective, even after >24 hours of ischaemia, provided that some parenchymal perfusion remains. Long term patency data also support an aggressive revascularisation strategy in these cases.9

In the present case, thrombotic aetiology was presumed based on active smoking, previous cardiovascular disease, visible renal artery atherosclerosis, and the absence of dysrhythmias. The patient's initial emergency department visits probably reflected early, non-specific symptoms of renal artery thrombosis. Despite >72 hours of ischaemia, revascularisation was deemed viable due to signs of preserved parenchymal perfusion, namely, contrast uptake in the viable regions of the kidney and patency of the distal artery. Additionally, infarction was limited to the lower third of the right kidney, and both kidneys exceeded 8 cm in length, supporting overall preserved renal function.10 Regarding treatment strategy, although both catheter directed thrombolysis and aspiration thrombectomy were considered, the former was avoided due to the potential for prolonging renal ischaemia time, and the latter was excluded given the increased risk of distal embolisation. For these reasons, primary stenting was selected as the most appropriate and effective approach in this case. The authors believe that the sudden deterioration in renal function was due to the acute occlusion of a renal artery, which did not allow sufficient time for the contralateral kidney to compensate for the loss of function. Although differential renal function assessment using radionuclide scans was considered, it was not performed, as the progressive improvement in renal function observed during follow up suggested adequate global renal recovery, making additional functional imaging unnecessary.

In conclusion, in acute renal artery occlusion of thrombotic origin cases, revascularisation should not be restricted to a strict six hour window. Instead, decision making should be individualised, incorporating parameters such as contrast uptake in the parenchyma and patency distal to the occlusion. Given its effectiveness and minimally invasive nature, endovascular revascularisation should be regarded as the first line treatment. While recent data from FBEVAR procedures support delayed intervention, this case underscores that similar principles are applicable to spontaneous, non-iatrogenic renal artery thrombosis. This highlights the broader relevance of physiology based decision making over rigid time thresholds in renal revascularisation.
