Background

Vaping can result in acute and severe respiratory distress, a condition known as e-cigarette or vaping-associated lung injury (EVALI). EVALI is characterized by a range of clinical and pathological findings, including pulmonary infiltrates, diffuse alveolar damage, but mainly ground-glass opacities [1]. While the long-term health effects of vaping are still under investigation, emerging evidence suggests a strong association with respiratory complications, particularly among individuals with pre-existing asthma [2].

According to the Center for Disease Control and Prevention criteria, EVALI is a clinical diagnosis that requires the use of an e-cigarette in the 90 days preceding the appearance of initial symptoms: pulmonary infiltrates on a plain chest radiograph or chest CT, and the absence of any other possible etiology, such as infection [1].

This case report presents a case of severe lung injury as a result of vaping, but not with the standard presentation or clinical findings.

Written informed consent for publication of this case report and any accompanying images was obtained from the patient and her legal guardians, as the patient was a minor at the time of presentation.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old Dutch woman, diagnosed with asthma since primary school, presented to the emergency department with respiratory distress. Her mother was also diagnosed with asthma. The evening prior to admission, the patient experienced three episodes of acute breathlessness accompanied by wheezing and coughing, preceded by 4–5 days of headache and cold symptoms. Aware of her mother's severe asthma, she self-administered inhalation beta2-agonists, providing partial relief.

Upon admission to a non-academic hospital, she exhibited severe dyspnea and tachypnea. Though there was initial improvement, her condition rapidly deteriorated with silent chest, tachypnea at 70/min, increased respiratory effort with nasal flaring, and muscle retractions. Despite intravenous administration of salbutamol and magnesium, her condition worsened, necessitating transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU) and subsequent intubation. Laboratory results showed a C-reactive protein of 200 mg/L and serum leukocytosis of 20 × 109/L.

Despite receiving mechanical ventilation and the treatment for a status asthmaticus her condition continued to deteriorate. Additional hetero anamnesis stated that the days prior to presentation, the patient vaped up to 600 inhalations daily for several days. Due to the severeness of the respiratory distress, she was transferred to the Erasmus Medical Center (EMC) for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Imaging studies at the EMC, including chest computed tomography (CT), revealed severe subcutaneous emphysema and pneumomediastinum without a pneumothorax (see Fig. 1), as well as severe central and peripheral mucus plugs (see Fig. 2). There were no ground- glass opacities, consolidations or crazy-paving pattern. Bronchoscopy confirmed severe bronchial obstruction with epithelial shedding with easy bleeding epithelial. Due to progressive respiratory failure refractory to maximal conventional mechanical ventilation and Plateau pressures over >30 mmHg, preventing her to be ventilated with lung protective mode, veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) was initiated. Prior to ECMO initiation, arterial blood gas analysis showed severe hypercapnic acidosis with a pH of 7.10 and a PaCO2 of 9.2 kPa and a P/F ratio below 50. After ECMO initiation the patient was ventilated with tidal volumes of 200 ml and low PEEP, with a blood flow of 3.5 L with 2650 rounds per minute and a sweep gasflow of 5.5 L/min, resulting in adequate oxygenation and a correction of the hypercapnia.

In addition, microbiological tests remained negative, including standard cultures, sputum cultures and bronchoalveolar lavage cultures, reducing the likelihood of an underlying infectious etiology.

She received high dose methylprednisolone (1000 mg/day) for 3 days. After 17 days of ECMO support, the patient showed signs of improvement and was successfully weaned off ECMO. However, she experienced muscle weakness, necessitating prolonged mechanical ventilation and tracheostomy placement. Conclusions and discussion

In the absence of pulmonary infiltrates and ground-glass opacities, a diagnosis of EVALI could not be stated for our patient. Notably, there was a significant amount of epithelial shedding and numerous mucus plugs present causing the respiratory distress in this patient. Notably, bronchoscopy revealed both extensive epithelial shedding and the presence of soot within the airways, supporting the hypothesis of direct thermal or combustion-related airway injury. Unfortunately, no toxicological screening for substances such as vitamin E acetate was performed.

The markedly elevated CRP (200 mg/L) and leukocytosis may suggest infection; however, similar elevations have been reported in non-infectious inflammatory lung injury, including ARDS and inhalation injury. These elevations are thought to reflect a systemic inflammatory response triggered by epithelial damage and cytokine release, rather than infection [3–5].

As the patient was known to have asthma, and presented with subcutaneous emphysema and a pneumomediastinum a near fatal asthmatic attack was also a possible explanation. While several case reports have described patients with severe asthma exacerbations leading to pneumomediastinum or subcutaneous emphysema, these cases typically showed significant improvement within a few days and often involved high levels of pulmonary edema [6]. Although the clinical presentation and bronchoscopic findings (including soot and epithelial shedding) are suggestive of inhalation injury, the diagnosis cannot be definitively established. Alternative diagnoses such as EVALI, infectious pneumonia, or a severe asthma exacerbation cannot be fully excluded, as no bronchoalveolar lavage cytology, comprehensive microbiological panel, or toxicological analysis was performed. Therefore, this case should be interpreted as a probable inhalation injury rather than a confirmed diagnosis. In addition, it is unclear whether her history of asthma worsened her condition during the entire intensive care admission.

Inhalation injury is a broad term encompassing pulmonary exposure to a variety of heat and chemicals in forms, such as smoke, gases, vapors, or fumes. These substances can cause damage to the respiratory tract, leading to both upper and lower airway injuries [7]. The inhalation temperature of e-cigarettes can reach up to 315–40 °C (600–750 °F) [8]. However, extremely high temperatures, approaching 1000 °C, may occur if a device is misused or if the coil overheats due to insufficient liquid, causing inhalation injury. In addition, inhaling chemicals may cause inhalation injury itself [8]. As with the presented case, the clinical presentation of inhalation injury includes severe respiratory distress with wheezing and coughing, often presented with (severe) headache.

Airway tissue damage cause by heat and chemicals can lead to increased mucus production, edema, epithelial denudation, mucosal ulceration, and potential hemorrhage. This damage often results in airflow obstruction, as tissue edema narrows the airways, while mucus, blood, and fluid further impede airflow [9].

To our knowledge, reports describing vaping-associated lung injury with predominant features of inhalation injury without classical EVALI imaging findings are limited [10].

Although EVALI, asthma exacerbation, and inhalation injury can all present with severe respiratory distress, their treatment approaches differ. All three conditions may require respiratory support, including mechanical ventilation, and in severe cases, ECMO. Asthma is characterized by bronchial hyperresponsiveness, airway inflammation, mucus plugging, and air trapping rather than pulmonary edema. Inhalation injury, in contrast, is associated with direct epithelial damage, mucosal sloughing and airway obstruction by debris. Both asthma and inhalationinjury may, therefore, respond to bronchodilator therapy aimed at reducing airway obstruction, whereas patients with EVALI are often treated with bronchodilators and systemic corticosteroids, with variable response [11].

The long-term prognosis between the diagnosis varies. According to prior studies and clinical findings, patients with smoke inhalation injury (not caused by vaping) are at high risk for complications, such as pneumoniae. Severe injuries may result in long-term consequences, including bronchiectasis, chronic bronchiolitis obliterans, and the potential need for an artificial airway. However, many individuals experience no lasting effects following a single episode of smoke inhalation injury [12]. In contrast, patients with EVALI, despite their youth, commonly have significant long-term respiratory disability; cognitive impairment; symptoms of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress; and persistent vaping [3].

The patient reported that after hospitalization she was initially very distressed by the severity of her illness and the need for ICU admission. She expressed relief after her recovery. She is grateful to have survived this life-threatening event.

This case suggests a severe vaping-associated lung injury with features of inhalation injury, rather than classical EVALI. Adequate and critical evaluation of patients with respiratory distress after vaping should be performed.
