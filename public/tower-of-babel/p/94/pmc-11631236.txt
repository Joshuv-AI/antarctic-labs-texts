Association of Fibrate use with clinical expression of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Chan Soon Park, Bongseong Kim, Jin‐Hyung Jung, Tae‐Min Rhee, Hyun Jung Lee, Hee‐Sun Lee, Jun‐Bean Park, Yong‐Jin Kim, Kyungdo Han, Hyung‐Kwan Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAn association between obesity, metabolic abnormalities and clinical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) expression has been reported. We investigated whether managing dyslipidaemia with fibrates could affect the clinical expression of HCM.MethodsWe screened patients who used fibrates between 2010 and 2017 from a nationwide database. After excluding patients with a history of HCM, we identified fibrate‐user group (n = 412 823). We then constructed a 1:1 matched cohort of fibrate‐naïve participants (n = 412 823). After a 1 year lag period, we identified the incident HCM cases for the following 5 years.ResultsDuring a median follow‐up period of 3.96 years, we identified 454 incident clinical HCM cases. After adjusting for covariates, fibrate use was associated with a lower risk of clinical HCM expression [hazard ratio (HR) 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.763 (0.630–0.924)]. In subgroup analyses, fibrate use was associated with a reduced risk of clinical HCM expression in patients with a body mass index ≥25 kg/m2 and those with abdominal obesity [HR (95% CI): 0.719 (0.553–0.934) and 0.655 (0.492–0.872)], but not in those without obesity. Fibrate use was also associated with lower risks of incident clinical HCM in patients with triglyceride levels ≥150 mg/dL and those with metabolic syndrome [HR (95% CI): 0.741 (0.591–0.929) and 0.750 (0.609–0.923)], but not in their counterparts. Regarding lifestyle behaviours, fibrate use appeared to provide more prognostic benefits in patients who currently smoked, consumed alcohol or did not engage in regular physical activities.ConclusionThe use of fibrates is associated with a lower incidence of clinical HCM expression. This association was also more prominent in those with obesity, unhealthy metabolic profiles and poor lifestyle behaviours.Keywords: cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic, fibric acids, obesity, metabolic syndrome

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common inherited cardiomyopathy, with an estimated prevalence of 0.2%–0.5% in the general population. 1 It is defined as an increase in left ventricular (LV) wall thickness that cannot be properly explained by abnormally increased loading conditions. 2 The natural history of HCM varies among patients. 1 A notable portion of patients with HCM do not suffer from serious cardiovascular symptoms over their lifetimes. Moreover, the point at which HCM becomes clinically evident varies. 3 To elucidate the disparities in the onset of clinical HCM expression, 3 , 4 several attempts have been made to identify potential risk factors contributing to its development. 5 , 6 Recently, several studies reported an association between obesity and an unhealthy metabolic status, including dyslipidaemia, with the clinical expression of HCM. These findings suggest a potential role of metabolic abnormalities in the clinical expression of HCM. 5 , 7 As supportive evidence, we have recently reported that metabolic dyslipidaemia, especially high triglyceride, is associated with the clinical expression of HCM. 8 However, there is a lack of data on whether managing metabolic dyslipidaemia is associated with a lower incidence of the clinical expression of HCM.

Fibrates, also known as fibric acid derivatives, function by activating the peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptors‐α (PPAR‐α) in the liver. This activation leads to changes in gene expression, reducing production of triglycerides and increasing the clearance of triglycerides from the bloodstream. Concurrently, there is a concomitant rise in high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels. 9 Although fibrate use showed neutral effects on atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases in randomized controlled trials, 10 , 11 several studies have suggested an association between fibrate use and a decrease in cardiovascular events, especially among patients with metabolic syndrome. 12 , 13 Given the observation on the association between metabolic abnormalities and the clinical expression of HCM, 5 , 7 we hypothesized that fibrate use might be associated with the incidence of clinical HCM expression.

To test this hypothesis, we analysed the association between fibrate use and the incidence of the clinical diagnosis of HCM using a large‐scale nationwide database.

Methods

Ethical statement and data availability

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. E‐2208‐110‐1351). The requirement for informed consent was waived because anonymized data were analysed. For reasonable request, data are available under the approval and oversight of the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS).

Data source and study population

This nationwide cohort study used data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database. Detailed information about the NHIS database has been published elsewhere. 14 , 15 , 16 In brief, the NHIS is a single public insurer that covers the entire Korean population and encourages eligible Korean adults to undergo regular general health check‐ups. The NHIS database includes demographic information, results of general health check‐ups, and medical histories coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM). Additionally, the NHIS database incorporates a registration code for rare intractable diseases (RIDs) (please see below for details). 5 , 17

The flowchart of this study is illustrated in Figure 1. Initially, we screened 1,465,824 patients who used fibrates between 2010 and 2017. From this pool, patients aged <30 years and those who took statins before fibrate use were excluded. We also excluded patients who had not undergone a general health check‐up provided by the NHIS within a two‐year interval before starting fibrates. Furthermore, we excluded patients who had previously been diagnosed with HCM, those with a body mass index (BMI) of <18.5 kg/m2, and those with missing data during the general health check‐up. The index date was defined as the first date of fibrates prescription.

Study population. Selection of the study population is schematically presented.

After excluding patients diagnosed with HCM during the 1 year lag period to minimize the reverse causality bias, we identified a 1:1 matched cohort of fibrate‐naïve participants. These participants were matched based on the year of the index date, age, sex and proportion of patients with triglyceride levels ≥150 mg/dL; we ensured that the index year between the fibrate‐naïve group and the fibrate‐user group was matched to compare the incidence of newly diagnosed clinical HCM cases. The same exclusion criteria were applied to fibrate‐naïve participants, and all participants were followed up to 5 years after 1 year lag period.

Identification of HCM

The study endpoint was a new diagnosis of clinical HCM, defined using ICD‐10‐CM codes (I42.1 or I42.2), and registration in the RID programme. 5 , 18 In South Korea, HCM has been classified under the RID category since 2006, with the aim of reducing medical expenses for patients with these conditions. As the Korean government covers 90% of the medical expenses for patients registered in the RID programme, 5 , 19 this disease category is closely regulated, requiring physicians to complete an RID registration application form for patients diagnosed with HCM. The application includes comprehensive information such as the ICD‐10 code, date of diagnosis, diagnostic methods used (echocardiography or cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging) and the name and licence number of the physicians confirming the diagnosis. After a thorough review and monitoring of the application by independent health professionals, the NHIS assigns an RID code (V127) to validate HCM diagnosis. The validity of the RID code for HCM was independently assessed and confirmed by examining the medical records and images of a random sample of 1100 individuals, with findings indicating a sensitivity of 91.5%, specificity of 100% and accuracy of 92.5%. 18

Fibrate use and other variables

The fibrates considered in this study were fenofibrate, bezafibrate, ciprofibrate, etofibrate and gemfibrozil. The assessed covariates included a history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome, chronic kidney disease, myocardial infarction, ischaemic stroke, atrial fibrillation and concomitant statin use (see detailed definitions in Table S1). Obesity was categorized into three groups according to the BMI: 18.5 to <23.0 kg/m2, 23.0 to <25.0 kg/m2 and ≥25.0 kg/m2, respectively. 20 Abdominal obesity was defined as a waist circumference (WC) of ≥90 cm in men and ≥85 cm in women. 20 Additionally, we conducted analyses using decile categories of BMI and WC, with cut‐off points summarized in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with inter‐quartile range (IQR) while categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. Appropriate statistical tests were applied, including the χ 2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, and the unpaired Student's t test for continuous variables. The chronological trends of incident clinical HCM were depicted using Kaplan–Meier survival curves according to fibrate use and statistical differences were assessed using the log‐rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression models were constructed to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to assess the association between fibrate use and the incidence of clinical HCM. All these findings are observed after 1 year lag period of data collection after enrolling the study participants to exclude pre‐existing HCM. Only new‐onset HCM diagnosed after a 1 year lag period following the index date was included in the outcome. A two‐sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant and all statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

In total, 825,646 participants (mean age, 57.8 ± 10.6 years; male, 445,492 [54.0%]) were analysed. Hypertension was present in 464,461 (56.3%), diabetes mellitus in 230,724 (27.9%), and metabolic syndrome in 595,085 (72.1%) patients. Statins were prescribed to 629,921 (76.3%) patients. The median level of triglyceride was 189.1 mg/dL (IQR 188.9–189.4 mg/dL).

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of patients according to fibrate use (fibrate‐naïve vs. fibrate‐user group). Although both groups were of similar age, fibrate users exhibited a higher prior history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus and metabolic syndrome and were more frequently prescribed statins. Fibrate users also had a higher proportion of current smokers and alcohol consumers than fibrate‐naïve group. Regular physical activity was more frequently reported in the fibrate‐naïve group than in the fibrate‐user group, although the difference was marginal (20.4% vs. 20.0%, P < 0.001). Although the proportion of patients with triglyceride levels ≥150 mg/dL was well‐matched between the two groups, fibrate users had significantly higher triglyceride levels than the fibrate‐naïve group.

Association of fibrate use with incident clinical HCM

During a median follow‐up of 3.96 years (IQR, 2.26–5.00 years), 454 patients (0.05% of the total study population) were newly diagnosed with HCM, with an aIR of 0.153 per 1000 PY. The aIRs of fibrate‐naïve and fibrate‐user groups were 0.166 and 0.141 per 1000 PY, respectively. Kaplan–Meier curves for newly diagnosed HCM according to fibrate use (Figure 2) showed statistically marginal differences between fibrate‐naïve and fibrate‐user groups (log‐rank P = 0.087). However, after adjusting for covariates using Cox regression analysis, fibrate use was significantly associated with a decreased risk of newly diagnosed clinical HCM (HR 0.763, 95% CI, 0.630–0.924; P = 0.006) (Table 2).

Association between fibrate use and incident clinical HCM according to obesity and metabolic profiles

We performed subgroup analyses to investigate whether obesity profiles, including BMI and abdominal obesity, as well as the presence of metabolic syndrome or higher triglyceride levels, influenced the association between fibrate use and incident clinical HCM.

As demonstrated in Table 3, fibrate use was associated with a reduced relative risk of incident clinical HCM in patients with a BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m2 (HR 0.719, 95% CI 0.553–0.934). However, this association was not observed in those with a BMI 18.5 to <23.0 kg/m2 (HR 0.897, 95% CI 0.613–1.311) and those with 23.0 to <25.0 kg/m2 (HR 0.741, 95% CI 0.508–1.081). When stratified by abdominal obesity, fibrate use was associated with a decreased risk of incident clinical HCM in patients with abdominal obesity (HR 0.655, 95% CI 0.492–0.872), but not in those without (HR 0.851, 95% CI 0.663–1.093). Furthermore, when patients were categorized into quintiles according to BMI or WC, those in the higher quintiles derived greater benefits from fibrate use (Figure 3).

When triglyceride levels were used to stratify patients, fibrate use was associated with a decreased risk of incident clinical HCM in those with triglyceride levels ≥150 mg/dL (HR 0.571, 95% CI 0.387–0.844), but not in those with triglyceride levels <150 mg/dL (HR 0.834, 95% CI 0.671–1.036). Association between fibrate use and incident clinical HCM according to lifestyle behaviours

Table 4 summarizes the association between fibrate use and the incidence of clinical HCM according to lifestyle behaviours. Among current smokers, fibrate use was associated with a lower risk of incident clinical HCM (HR 0.644, 95% CI, 0.450–0.921); however, no association was observed in nonsmokers (HR 0.813, 95% CI, 0.651–1.015). Likewise, fibrate use was associated with decreased risks of incident clinical HCM in patients who consumed alcohol (HR 0.702, 95% CI 0.513–0.959), but not in those who did not (HR 0.800, 95% CI 0.632–1.013). Regarding physical activity, fibrate use was associated with a lower incidence of clinical HCM in patients who did not engage in regular physical activity (HR 0.706, 95% CI, 0.572–0.807); however, no such association was found in those who engaged in regular physical activity (HR 1.121, 95% CI, 0.711–1.766).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the association between fibrate use and incident clinical HCM using a large nationwide population cohort database. The key findings of our study are as follows. First, we found patients who had been newly diagnosed with clinical HCM, with an aIR of 0.153 per 1000 person‐years during the follow‐up period. The fibrate‐user group was associated with a lower risk of incident clinical HCM than the fibrate‐naïve group after adjusting for covariates. Second, subgroup analyses showed that fibrate use was associated with a reduced risk of incident clinical HCM in patients with obesity, metabolic abnormalities and unhealthy lifestyle behaviours, including current smokers or alcohol drinkers. Most patients with HCM do not experience serious cardiovascular symptoms during their lifetime, but some may encounter symptoms such as dyspnoea, chest pain and even sudden cardiac death, which necessitate medical attention. 1 Efforts have been made to improve clinical outcomes in patients who manifest clinical signs of HCM by identifying prognosticators 21 , 22 and developing novel therapeutic agents. 23 , 24 As part of these endeavours, attempts have been made to identify factors that may modify the clinical expression of HCM. This is particularly crucial because predisposing genetic factors alone cannot always explain its clinical manifestations. In fact, it is noteworthy that only 50%–70% of patients with HCM carrying genetic mutations are reported to exhibit clinical manifestation. 25 , 26 Representatively, one study showed that BMI is independently associated with LV mass index in patients with HCM. 7 We previously showed that individuals with obesity or those with metabolically unhealthy status have a higher risk of developing clinical HCM than their counterparts. 5 , 8 In this respect, obesity and metabolically unhealthy status have been proposed as modifiable risk factors for mitigating the clinical expression of HCM. However, whether body weight reduction and/or improved lipid profiles can attenuate clinical HCM expression remains to be determined.

Fibrates are lipid‐modifying agents that reduce triglycerides and increase HDL cholesterol levels. 9 , 27 Based on studies showing the possibility that fibrates may reduce major cardiovascular events in select patient groups, such as atherogenic dyslipidaemia, 12 , 13 , 28 efforts have been made to identify patients who benefit from fibrate use. Considering the potential beneficial effects of fibrates in addressing insulin resistance, a key feature of metabolic syndrome, 29 , 30 it has been suggested that the use of fibrates may offer more positive outcomes for patients with metabolic syndrome. 9 , 28 , 31 This notion is in line with reports indicating that fibrates could potentially counteract insulin resistance, a prominent aspect of metabolic syndrome. 32 , 33 Intriguingly, one mechanism explaining the association of obesity and metabolic health status with the incidence of clinical HCM is insulin resistance, as well. Specifically, overexpression of insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1) and transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β), well‐known trophic and mitotic factors, was suggested to be major contributors to the pathological manifestations of HCM. 34 Given that high levels of IGF‐1 and TGF‐β are related to insulin resistance, 35 , 36 clinical expression of HCM can be promoted by a variety of conditions associated with insulin resistance, such as obesity or metabolic syndrome. 5 We here extended the potential benefits of fibrates beyond their established preventive effects on conventional atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases to attenuate the clinical manifestation of HCM. Although fibrate users showed a higher prevalence of obesity and unhealthy metabolic status, fibrate use was associated with decreased risks of incident HCM. We also observed that fibrate use was associated with a reduced risk of incident clinical HCM, especially in patients with obesity, metabolic abnormalities and unhealthy lifestyle behaviours. More importantly, the use of fibrates appeared to offer greater prognostic benefits in patients who currently smoke, consume alcohol, and do not engage in regular physical activity. The association between these unhealthy lifestyle behaviours and benefits of fibrates use is not well established but can be expected. 28 In fact, current smokers were reported to be associated with a higher risk of developing metabolic syndrome and have higher triglyceride and lower HDL cholesterol levels. 37 Also, excessive alcohol consumption and absence of regular physical activity were reported to be associated with a greater risk of metabolic syndrome, despite controversies on the metabolic effects of mild alcohol consumption. 38 , 39 , 40 Therefore, the authors cautiously propose that it is not counterintuitive for patients with unhealthy lifestyle behaviours to potentially be more prone to receive benefits from the use of fibrates. The same concept was applied to obese patients and/or those presenting with metabolic abnormalities.

Our study provides a novel perspective on a preventive strategy for the clinical expression of HCM with pharmacological intervention. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to suggest pharmacological interventions and some potential benefits by modifying risk factors for the clinical expression of HCM. However, the cost‐effectiveness offered by fibrate use does not appear to be significant because the difference between fibrate‐user and the fibrate‐naïve groups was small. However, as there is no proven strategy to prevent or delay the clinical manifestation of HCM, the observations presented herein could provide new perspectives. In particular, in subgroup analyses, fibrates were significantly associated with a decreased risk of the clinical expression of HCM in patients with obesity and those with an unhealthy metabolic status, both of which are the main components of metabolic syndrome and were previously suggested as acquired risk factors for the clinical expression of HCM. 5 , 7 Fibrates are not an expensive class of drugs with fewer adverse effects. Further clinical studies are required to verify the positive effects of fibrate in attenuating the clinical expression of HCM.

This study has several limitations. First, although we analysed a large, unselected, and well‐controlled cohort, interference from unmeasured confounding factors is possible, which is an inherent limitation of the observational nature of the study. In addition, the medication status and metabolic profiles could have been changed during follow‐up. This information, however, is not available. Finally, it is important to note that the enrolled patients were of Korean ethnicity; therefore, extrapolation to other ethnicities requires further investigation.

In conclusion, the use of fibrates is associated with a reduced risk of clinical HCM expression. This association was more prominent in patients with obesity, unhealthy metabolic profiles and unhealthy lifestyle behaviours.
