Content matters, context matters: unraveling behavior dynamics in an online health community for tobacco cessation
Authors: Tavleen Singh, Runzhi Zhou, Kayo Fujimoto, Sahiti Myneni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objective of this research was to examine the content and context-specific information diffusion patterns underlying communication pertaining to tobacco use from online health communities (OHCs).Materials and MethodsWe utilized a mixed-methods approach comprising multidimensional qualitative coding to identify themes and communication attributes, automated text analysis leveraging advances in large language models (LLMs) to classify message content and context, and social network analysis to examine the dynamics of peer interactions in this study. Using QuitNet, an online tobacco cessation forum (n = 64 632 members, n = 2.39 million forum messages spanning 2000-2015), we extracted message-level features (eg, topic, theory) and context-related communication attributes underlying tobacco use behaviors as manifested in peer interactions. We then utilized stochastic actor-oriented models (SAOMs) to examine how communication content and context impact social network topologies and behavior dynamics [n = 3055 members (Wave 1), 2475 members (Wave 2), and 2289 members (Wave 3)].ResultsOHC members expressed themselves using a variety of content and context categories such as social support (communication themes), feedback and monitoring (behavior change techniques), and emotion (pragmatic context). For the classification of communication attributes, LLMs trained on domain datasets outperformed other deep learning models [average F1-score = 0.91 (communication themes), 0.81 (behavior change techniques), and 0.79 (pragmatic context)]. Content-specific SAOMs revealed that engaging in dense triads with specific content-context patterns [comparison of behavior (content) with questions (context)] significantly affected the abstinence status of community members (P < .05).DiscussionThese findings indicate that specific combinations of communication content and interaction context are associated with peer influence and abstinence outcomes in online health communities.ConclusionsNovel behavior modeling approaches can identify latent peer interaction patterns in OHCs and advance the science of just-in-time digital behavioral interventions. Theory-enriched large language models combined with network analysis provide scalable and actionable insights for individual- and network-level strategies to support risky behavior modification such as tobacco cessation.Keywords: tobacco cessation, online health communities, large language models, stochastic actor-oriented models, social network interventions

Introduction

Tobacco use is one of the largest public health problems across the globe1; in the United States, 28.8 million individuals (11.6%) consumed cigarettes in 2022.2 Tobacco use leads to one of every five deaths in the United States (US) each year3 and costs about $240 billion for healthcare expenditures in 2018.4 It is a significant risk factor for the development of cardiovascular diseases, lung problems, and different types of cancers, leading to total economic costs of about 1.4 trillion US dollars annually worldwide.1 Although tobacco use rates have plateaued, this dangerous behavior is morphing into new forms such as electronic and heated tobacco products, that introduce new risk factors and exposure pathways.5

Social media has emerged as a viable platform for studying risky health behaviors and factors that promote positive behavior change.6–9 Online Health Communities (OHCs) such as BecomeAnEx.org,10 QuitNet,11 etc. which are digital peer-support platforms have gained popularity among individuals to share experiences, seek support, and exchange health-related information to facilitate behavior change. Studies on social diffusion research in social media underscores the role that online social relationships play in the adoption and spread of behaviors.12 Prior studies have taken siloed approaches when it comes to analyzing online communication’s content and its underlying theoretical constructs (what information is exchanged)13–16 or structure (who is exchanging information with whom)17,18 or context (how is the exchanged information expressed).19,20 However, it is important to study these aspects of human communication together to: (1) understand the individualistic and environmental factors underlying behavior change, (2) expand theoretical frameworks underlying health-related behavior change in the digital era, and (3) develop effective behavior change interventions (BCIs).21–25 Examining peer interactions through multiple lenses of social, behavioral, and cognitive theories is imperative to model human health behaviors since these multidimensional facets together influence the cognitive state of individuals interacting in the social environment.26–28 While manually combining these concepts is a labor-intensive process due to the volume, velocity, and ambiguity of social media data, recent advances in natural language processing (NLP) with large language models (LLMs) allow us to bridge these siloed concepts with social network dynamics at a scalable and a replicable level. In this study, our objectives are as follows: (1) derive message-level communication attributes embedded within online peer interactions along with member-level abstinence behaviors, to understand the granular elements of communication in OHCs; (2) adapt NLP techniques to scale the extraction of such attributes to the entire datasets, enabling comprehensive analysis of large-scale social media data; and, (3) utilize social network models to determine social influence dynamics and model the co-evolution of network and behavior dynamics as manifested in peer interactions in an online health community for tobacco cessation, thereby uncovering how social structures and communication patterns interact to influence health behaviors. Despite extensive research on social influence and tobacco cessation, our understanding of the social processes and behavioral mechanisms have been limited due to siloed interpretation of these deeply intertwined constructs in daily lives of individuals trying to quit tobacco use. This study addresses this key gap by integrating message-level peer communication mapping with network-based behavioral modeling to reveal how peer interactions in online health communities influence tobacco cessation, thereby advancing integrative, scalable, and just-in-time adaptive socio-behavioral interventions.

Methods

In this study, we used a de-identified version of the dataset obtained from QuitNet, one of the most popular OHCs that promotes tobacco cessation.11 This dataset spans from 2000 to 2015 and consists of approximately 2.39 million forum messages from 280 476 unique threads posted by 64 632 unique community members was used in this research. In this work, we complement qualitative analysis techniques with automated text analysis methods to gain useful insights into the conversations exchanged between QuitNet members and also facilitate the application of stochastic actor-oriented models to understand the content-sensitive and OHC member context-specific attributes of network diffusion on an individual’s abstinence behaviors. The study employed a sequential mixed-methods framework in which qualitative coding outputs served as labeled data for automated text classification, and the resulting model-generated annotations were subsequently used to construct actor-affiliation matrices that informed the SAOM analysis of behavioral dynamics. Qualitative analysis

Firstly, we extracted the following three communication attributes from the online peer interactions using inductive and deductive analyses: communication themes (CTs) (capture the conversational content), behavior change techniques (BCTs) (capture the behavior-theoretical underpinnings), and speech acts (SAs) (capture the pragmatic context). We began with an inductive, open-coding approach to identify salient patterns and categories emerging directly from the data. These preliminary themes were then refined using a deductive framework informed by behavioral theory and prior literature, which ensured that the coding schema was both data-driven and theoretically grounded. A total of 2005 messages from the QuitNet community were manually annotated along the three attributes using the detailed qualitative coding schema outlined in Table S1. Table 1 presents illustrative examples of CTs, BCTs, and SAs. Examples are adapted to reflect typical online tobacco cessation discussions and are not verbatim user posts. Each message may be assigned multiple coding dimensions simultaneously. Two coders with graduate-level training in behavioral informatics and qualitative research methods independently performed the coding. Each coder used the qualitative coding schema to independently assign CTs, BCTs, and SAs categories to a subset of messages by performing line-by-line analysis of every message using directed content analysis techniques.29 The inter-rater reliability in the grounded theory analysis between the two coders was 81.6% for CTs,11,30 74% for BCTs,30 and 76% for SA’s.31 Discrepancies in coding were resolved through consensus discussions until mutual agreement was reached, ensuring a shared interpretation of the coding schema in the final gold-standard dataset.

Automated text analysis

Secondly, we developed a semi-automated text analysis pipeline to scale the qualitative analysis of OHCs to large datasets by adapting the existing deep-learning approaches. We used the annotated dataset to train the model on the text classification task, i.e, the model labeled each message with different categories of CTs, BCTs, and SAs. We applied the Bidirectional Encoder Representations from the Transformers (BERT) model32 and further trained on the QuitNet corpus to derive the three communication attributes from the entire QuitNet conversational dataset. The use of such automated methods facilitates scaling qualitative analysis to large-scale social media data sets. This, in turn, allows for the structuring of a network based on different content types (i.e, by CTs or BCTs) and for visualizing and understanding the expression of members (based on SAs) within different types of networks. The detailed description of this methodology is beyond the scope of this work but is described in detail elsewhere.33 We used the held-out test set to evaluate the performance of the models using evaluation metrics: precision, recall, and F1-score. To contextualize BERT’s performance, we evaluated a general-purpose GenAI baseline (Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct) on a stratified subset of 500 messages using the same schema and metrics. The model achieved micro-F1 scores of 0.52 (CT), 0.38 (BCT), and 0.40 (SA), substantially below our domain-trained BERT classifier. This comparison highlights that GenAI models without task-specific supervision or domain adaptation underperform on nuanced multi-label behavioral annotation.

Stochastic actor-oriented models

Lastly, we characterized the social networks of QuitNet using content-sensitive member-context affiliation networks and stochastic actor-oriented models (SAOMs). The study of social network dynamics can provide insights into these theoretical micro-level mechanisms that eventually bring about changes in social network structures at the macro-level.34 The main approach for modeling the dynamics of a social network is an actor-oriented model in which it is assumed that social actors (individual community members) in the network play a crucial role in changing their ties with other individuals as well as changing their communication attributes (what individuals talk about on their platforms and how do they express themselves). These networks consisted of two modes- the first one being the community members and the second one being the different SA categories. The ties between them recorded the affiliation of each member with each speech act in a given CT or BCT. The community members were assigned to a certain CT or BCT if they had at least exchanged one message belonging to the respective CT or BCT (Table 2). In obstacles CT-based social network, the first community member is affiliated to assertion, the second community member is affiliated to commissive, and the third community member is affiliated to both SAs given that these members expressed themselves using these categories of SAs in the given CT (Table 2). We constructed visual representations of various CT and BCT-based affiliation networks between community members and SAs. Networks were visualized in Gephi35 using the ForceAtlas2 layout algorithm, which places more strongly connected nodes closer in space. Node size reflects degree centrality, and both degree and betweenness were computed to quantify member prominence. Because the affiliation networks were highly dense, Louvain modularity detection was applied to reveal coherent communities and reduce visual clutter; node colors in the figures therefore represent modularity classes. The illustrative Comparison-of-Behavior BCT network includes only power-engaged users and displays all member-SA interactions from 2000 to 2015.

For the implementation of SAOMs, we used a de-identified version of the QuitNet dataset consisting of a total of three years from 2012 to 2014. The dataset characteristics across years are presented in the Table S2. For modeling the network/behavioral dynamics, we selected the peer interactions from three years - 2012 (Wave 1), 2013 (Wave 2), and 2014 (Wave 3).

For a given year, the total count of the number of times a specific SA was expressed by a certain member was calculated to create a valued affiliation matrix (member-by-SA). This network was dichotomized based on whether a specific SA category was expressed by that member in a given year. The eight SAs were also categorized in a similar manner based on community members’ communication styles. The two broad communication styles (CS) were defined as follows: (1) the sender of the message has an intention to “push-in” information to the receiver (using SAs - assertion, commissive, declarative, directive, statement), (2) “interactive turn-taking” where the sender might try to engage their peers by pulling out and pushing in information in the form of question, expressive, or emotion. Member engagement was defined using message count percentiles (power, sustained, infrequent), with details provided in the Supplementary Materials. Abstinence status was inferred from self-reported abstinence signatures in posts (eg, “Day 72”), using the most recent signature to classify members as abstinent or non-abstinent, and further categorized by time since quitting; detailed definitions are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Multiple theme-based networks were created, and the parameters were estimated using the method of moments (MOM). All the models achieved excellent convergence, with an overall maximum convergence ratio of less than 0.25 and the convergence ratio for each parameter, which is set to less than 0.1. All model effects were above the customary P < .05 threshold, indicating acceptable model fit. For the entire analysis, we used RSiena Version 1.3.14.1,36 a package of the statistical system R v.4.2.2. The mathematical representation and description of effects included in the models are provided in Table S3.

Extraction of social network interventional insights

We used a taxonomy from Robins et al,37 for proposing insights to design social network interventions promoting behavior change. This taxonomy includes three types of intervention actions: (1) identification which includes identifying key actors or individuals holding prominent positions in a social network, (2) diffusion which includes diffusion of information via actors of a network, and (3) structural change which includes making structural changes to the network by adding or deleting certain actors to improve efficiency of behavior change. Within the category of structural change, the network intervention action is directed at changing the network, whereas in the case of identification and diffusion are directed at using the existing network structure. Using the results generated from SAOMs, we assigned members with different characteristics (engagement) and expressions (content and context) to different interventional actions for propagating behavior change.

Results

Qualitative analysis

The most commonly identified CTs included social support, which highlighted the presence of community support among the peers, followed by progress in which the members shared their cessation progress in terms of days since they last smoked. QuitNet members followed community-specific traditions to monitor their progress or goals with other community members, expressed in rituals such as virtual bonfires and pledges, and shared quit statistics or milestones achieved. Community members also posted messages in which they sought help to fight cravings or, were faced with obstacles to quitting tobacco use or confessed to others their relapse.

In terms of BCTs, the community members provided feedback to one another regarding their changed behavior or its related outcomes (feedback and monitoring). Members also provided support to one another in the form of emotional support or practical guidance (social support). Members demonstrated their behaviors to peers by stating the number of days that they had been smoke-free (comparison of behavior). For SAs, emotion was the most seen pragmatic context embedded in peer interactions, which captures the nature of such communities, wherein members provide emotional support to each other. Assertion SA describes the beliefs of community members as they describe their current behaviors or their outcomes. Directive SA denotes the guidance given by community members to one another. In contrast, declarative SA captures objective behavioral information, such as quit dates, stated by community members.

Automated text analysis

For the classification of CTs, BERT (QuitNet-trained) outperformed other models with an average F1-score of 0.91 for various categories of CTs. The highest F1-score (0.95) was obtained for social support and traditions CTs, whereas the obstacles CT obtained the lowest F1-score (0.76). For the classification of BCTs, the average F1-score was 0.81 for various categories of BCTs. The highest F1-score was obtained for reward and threat (0.90) and natural consequences (0.89) BCTs, whereas the self-belief BCT obtained the lowest F1-score (0.74). For the classification of SAs, the average F1-score was 0.79 for various categories of SAs. The highest F1-score was obtained for statement (0.89) SA, whereas the directive SA obtained the lowest F1-score (0.71).

Stochastic actor-oriented models

For the illustrative Comparison of Behavior BCT network (Figure 2), the modularity detection identified three communities (Modularity Classes 0-2). Analysis of the centrality metrics showed that Mod Class 2 contained the highest-degree nodes (up to degree 8), compared with 4-5 in Class 0 and 3-4 in Class 1, indicating greater structural prominence. Betweenness values followed the same pattern, with Class 2 containing the majority of bridging nodes in the network. These quantitative differences suggested that Mod Class 2 plays a disproportionately central role in organizing Comparison of Behavior -related member-speech-act interactions, even though all communities appear visually close in the ForceAtlas2 layout due to overall network density.

1) Impact of content and context on network dynamics:

1a) In both CT-and BCT-based communication networks, the network dynamic model reveals that the network structure becomes less dense over time for all communication styles, ie, the overall tendency to exchange messages decreases over time (degree density) except for interactive turn-taking communication style SAs in obstacles and readiness regulators CTs. This could be due to gradual decline of conversations in the community as the members get accustomed to online interactions.

1b) In progress, social support and traditions CT-based communication networks, when members with power engagement status express interactive turn-taking communication style SAs, they attract more communication ties (engage ego) (see Tables S6 and S7).

2) Impact of content and context on behavior dynamics:

2a) For the CT-based communication networks, the results of the behavioral dynamics model showed that in progress and social-support CT-based networks, engaging in dense triads, that is, tightly interconnected groups of three members who frequently interact, via any communication-style SAs had a marginally significant effect on individuals’ abstinence status, suggesting that such cohesive interactions may influence the early stages of quitting (P < .1). However, the model did not show an effect of broader peer influence, as captured by the total alter effect, which reflects how strongly an individual’s behavior aligns with the behaviors of their connected peers. For detailed definitions and illustrative examples of these SAOM parameters, please refer to the Table S3.

2b) In readiness regulators CT-based networks, power engagement members that express themselves using interactive turn-taking communication style SAs members improve on their abstinence status over time compared to infrequent or sustained engagement members (Abstinence: effect from Engage) (see Table S6).

2c) In self-belief BCT-based communication networks, when members with higher abstinence status express push-in communication style SAs, they attract fewer communication ties or vice-versa (abstinence ego).

2d) In the comparison of behavior, feedback and monitoring, and self-belief in BCT-based communication networks, when members express a high number of interactive turn-taking communication style SAs, they attract fewer communication ties (SA Push-in/Interactive ego). However, in the comparison of behavior BCT-based communication networks, when members express a high number of push-in communication style SAs, they attract more communication ties (SA Push-in/Interactive ego) (see Table S7).

2e) For the BCT-based communication networks, the results of the behavior dynamic model showed that engaging in dense triads via expressing comparison of behavior BCT with interactive turn-taking communication style SAs and feedback and monitoring BCT with push-in communication style SAs significantly affected the abstinence status of individuals in a way that triads influence the beginning stages of the quit (P < .05). However, it did not show an influence of the network dynamics on the abstinence status of the QuitNet community members as captured by the total alter effect.

2f) In feedback and monitoring and self-belief BCT-based networks, power engagement members that express themselves using interactive turn-taking communication style SAs improve their abstinence status over time compared to infrequent or sustained engagement members (Abstinence: effect from Engage) (see Table S7).

Social network interventional targets and insights

Our analysis indicated that highly engaged (“power”) members were more likely to maintain abstinence when actively participating in conversations. They tended to ask questions, express emotions (eg, pride or frustration), and share progress, incorporating communication themes such as readiness to quit and behavior change techniques such as feedback and monitoring and self-belief. Power members also engaged newer or less active members, suggesting an important role in sustaining community engagement. We therefore hypothesize that enabling these members to use effective communication patterns may improve engagement and abstinence outcomes. For example, effective messages may combine a readiness-related theme (CT), feedback and monitoring (BCT), and an expressive or question-based communication style (SA). Accordingly, our first interventional target is to support power members in crafting effective messages by identifying influential members and providing message authoring assistance to diffuse context-specific content across the network (Table 3).

Participation in tightly connected groups (dense triads) was associated with improved abstinence outcomes, particularly when members exchanged context-specific content, suggesting that both network structure and communication patterns influence behavior change. We therefore hypothesize that modifying network structure and guiding communication may improve abstinence. Our second interventional target is an AI-based moderator that supports both network formation and communication by recommending connections to form dense triads and guiding effective messaging (Table 3). For example, the AI moderator could encourage members to compare current behavior with past progress, engage in interactive conversations, and track and share progress using directive or supportive communication styles. Similar patterns were observed in networks centered around progress and social support themes, where both directive and interactive communication styles were associated with improved outcomes.

Discussion

This study takes an empirically grounded approach to derive communication content and context-driven network patterns of behavior change that can be translated into the design of adaptive BCIs. Our qualitative analysis revealed a rich gradient of expressions using BCTs and themes with a distinct variation in emotional and informational needs depending on the behavior management profiles of OHC members. Another study also used the qualitative lens of interpersonal pragmatics to understand how information is shared between help-seekers and advice-givers in a tobacco cessation forum.38 This study reported how advice-givers focus more on the interpersonal side of interaction to provide motivation and support to initiators to become successful quitters.38 Similarly, in our study, we found that community members expressed themselves in the form of directives and declaratives for motivation to stay on track in their cessation journey.

Our automated methods enabled the extraction and integration of pragmatic, behavioral, and social dimensions from large-scale social media datasets, offering a comprehensive view of how communication processes shape health behaviors. Our results also showed that general instruction-tuned LLMs, despite strong fluency, lack grounding in behavioral-science constructs and therefore struggle with the specialized cues required for CT, BCT, and SA annotation. This underscores the need for domain-adapted models, positioning our fine-tuned BERT classifier as a more reliable approach for large-scale behavioral-informatics annotation. This approach allowed us to uncover multilevel mechanisms of social influence that influence individuals’ behavioral trajectories and transient needs during different stages of cessation. The findings demonstrate that specific combinations of content (CTs and BCTs) and interactional context (SAs) can meaningfully predict abstinence outcomes. Notably, the SAOM results indicate that engagement in dense triads characterized by supportive communication patterns exerts positive social influence during the early stages of quitting when individuals are most vulnerable to relapse. Previous studies have applied this model to examine how online friendship networks and behavior adoption co-evolve among peer leaders, showing that individuals who are centrally connected within cohesive peer structures are more likely to adopt preventive health behaviors such as PrEP use and to facilitate diffusion of these behaviors across the network.39 Another study utilized SAOMs to identify the coalition dynamics influencing childhood obesity behaviors across communities.40 From an intervention standpoint, the findings from our study highlight that peer communication structures can be critical leverage points for sustaining engagement and preventing relapse during early stages of a quit attempt. Particularly, multicomponent digital cessation programs could be enhanced by embedding adaptive, peer-driven support mechanisms that dynamically respond to users’ evolving behavioral states. We propose two intervention strategies: (1) message-authoring assistance to generate context-specific supportive content, and (2) an AI Bot Moderator that can restructure community interactions by recommending new peer connections, facilitate supportive communications or prompt interactions that strengthen social cohesion and promote abstinence-supportive behaviors through targeted just-in-time messaging through identifying common affiliations.

This study has several limitations. Our qualitative analysis focused on a specific set of behavioral theories and a subset of 2005 forum messages, which may not capture all discussion patterns. The analysis was limited to text-based interactions, excluding engagement behaviors such as likes or shares that could offer additional insights. Abstinence status was inferred from self-reported signatures in posts rather than survey-based or biochemically verified measures and may be subject to misclassification due to selective reporting and ambiguity in member-generated text. The dataset spans 2000-2015; although core social dynamics remain consistent, future work will incorporate newer data. Automated analyses also combined certain categories to enhance interpretability, which may have influenced results. Lastly, the longitudinal network analysis was restricted to dyads with repeated interactions. Despite these constraints, the study offers a scalable framework for examining social influence and communication dynamics in online health communities.

Conclusions

In this paper, we describe a novel approach that integrates social network modeling and natural language processing to incorporate communication-level attributes (content and context) alongside individual-level factors. This approach provides deeper insight into the social influence mechanisms underlying abstinence behaviors in online health communities. Our findings highlight the importance of dynamic messaging to address evolving individual needs during behavior change, such as tobacco cessation. Identifying both population- and individual-level factors is critical for contextualizing digital health experiences. This framework can inform the design of personalized digital interventions to promote positive health outcomes.
