Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair With Superior Fulcrum Reconstruction Using Autologous Ipsilateral Peroneus Longus Tendon for Irreparable Massive Rotator Cuff Tears
Authors: Hongyan Li, Guang Chen, Run Fang, Daobin Zhou, Kehao Wang, Chen Peng, Rende Ning
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Rotator cuff tears are still among the serious challenges encountered by sports medicine surgeons. With the development of arthroscopic technology, the treatment options for irreparable massive rotator cuff tears (IMRCTs) have gradually increased, but postoperatively, these options are prone to graft retears, reoperations, infections, revision surgical procedures, and so on. On the basis of studies on the balance of the force couples of the rotator cuff, more scholars believe that when addressing IMRCTs, attention should be paid to the reconstruction of the force couples of the rotator cuff; hence, we developed the superior fulcrum reconstruction technique. This article describes an alternative approach to IMRCT repair with superior fulcrum reconstruction using autologous ipsilateral peroneus longus tendon.

Rotator cuff tears are the leading cause of shoulder pain and dysfunction, and the incidence is over 50% in individuals older than 80 years.1 On the basis of studies of irreparable massive rotator cuff tears (IMRCTs), the balance of the force couples of the rotator cuff has gradually received the attention of scholars.2 Clinically, we have been skilled in performing anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction of the knee to restore knee stability. On the basis of this, we developed the superior fulcrum reconstruction technique using autologous ipsilateral peroneus longus tendon. Despite the promising outcomes of this procedure, its technical aspects are daunting and require not only physician patience but also multiple surgical assistants. We consider performing the described technique in patients with IMRCTs (>5 cm). A routine examination is performed preoperatively to determine whether this technique is the best treatment option for the patient. The final operative method should be determined according to the specific situation during the operation. A standard preoperative physical examination and magnetic resonance imaging are also required to judge the size of the tear, amount of tendon retraction, and degree of fatty infiltration. Preoperatively, a physical examination is performed and the shoulder history and imaging data are routinely collected to exclude other conditions that cannot tolerate surgery. The technique is not suitable in patients with small to large rotator cuff tears, patients with glenohumeral arthritis, patients with poor-quality peroneus longus tendon, and older patients. In this technique, we perform autologous ipsilateral peroneus longus tendon transplantation in the shoulder to achieve superior fulcrum reconstruction. This article details the surgical procedures for surgical preparation, arthroscopic portal placement, tendon acquisition, establishment of bone tunnel locations, tendon traction and fixation, and so on.

Position and Preparation

The patient is placed in the beach-chair position under general anesthesia. After patient positioning, the bony landmarks (clavicle, coracoid, humeral head, and acromion) are drawn with a marker pen. After application of a routine sterile drape, the arthroscopic device is connected.

Surgical Technique

We first establish a standard posterior viewing portal and anteroinferior portal near the coracoid for arthroscopic diagnosis and intra-articular exploration. According to the intra-articular condition, we determine whether subscapularis repair should be performed, and the tendon long head is severed or fixed depending on the patient’s age and the condition of the long head of the biceps tendon. The arthroscope is then transferred into the subacromial space; the lateral portal is established with a spinal needle, which is located on the extension line of the clavicle and 3 to 4 cm from the lateral edge of the acromion; and the anterolateral portal is established on the horizontal line of the lateral approach. The arthroscope (ConMed) enters the subacromial space with the lateral portal as the viewing portal and the anterolateral portal as the operational portal, a thorough bursectomy is performed for exposure of a sufficient surgical view, and subacromial decompression is routinely performed (Video 1). Then, the surgeon resets the torn rotator cuff tissue using a grasper (Smith & Nephew), judges the size and type of rotator cuff tear, and decides whether to perform superior fulcrum reconstruction (Fig 2A).

Next, attention turns to the autograft. A 1-cm incision is first made 3 cm above the external malleolus ipsilaterally,3 and the peroneus longus tendon is identified and separated. One-half of the peroneus longus tendon is detached from the incision to the proximal end using a tendon extractor (Smith & Nephew). The tendons are equally divided into 2 bundles on the operating table by an assistant, and these tendons are braided with the nonabsorbable polyester suture (Ethicon). The braided threads at the 2 ends of each tendon have different colors to distinguish them. The braided tendons are 13 to 15 cm in length and 2 to 3 mm in diameter and are stored in saline solution–soaked gauze.

Attention turns back to the subacromial space (Video 1). A 4.5-mm-diameter bone tunnel is established on the glenoid side through the anteroinferior portal using a glenoid tendon tunnel localizer invented by our team (Fig 1A). The glenoid tendon tunnel localizer is used to ensure that the bone tunnel entrance and exit are in the direction of the 10:30 and 1:30 clock-face positions of the glenoid (Fig 2 B and C), with care taken to protect the superior and lateral bone cortex of the tunnel. Two 4.5-mm bone tunnels are established in the humeral head with an anterior cruciate ligament localizer (Smith & Nephew) (Fig 1B). The anterior cruciate ligament localizer is placed through the lateral portal and is used to locate the junction between the bone and cartilage on the greater tuberosity of the humerus. The tunnels run from the intra-articular humeral head forward and down to the junction between the bone and cartilage of the humerus. We ensure that the entrances of 2 bone tunnels intra-articular are 2 cm apart and the exits of 2 bone tunnels in the greater tuberosity of the humerus are 0 cm apart (Fig 2 D and E). On the glenoid side, the Bi type needle pulls the PDS line (Ethicon) through the bone tunnel, and on the humeral head side, a grasper pulls the tail of the PDS line from the 2 bone tunnels. The 2 braided tendons are merged and pulled by the PDS line through the humeral head–side tunnel, the glenoid-side tunnel, and the humeral head–side tunnel in turn (Fig 2 F-I). The tail ends of the 2 tendons are then pulled out of the bone tunnels on the humeral head side so that all 4 braided tail ends are in the intra-articular space. The 2 tendons are identified and separated, and a grasper is used to pull the 2 tail ends of 1 of the tendons through 1 of the bone tunnels on the humeral head side. The 2 tail ends of the other tendon are pulled through the other bone tunnel on the humeral head side in the same way. In this way, the 2 tendons form a structure similar to a suture bridge intra-articularly, and the shoulder joint is more stable. Finally, the tail ends of all braided threads are tied at the exit of the 2 bone tunnels on the humeral head side (Fig 2J). Postoperative Rehabilitation Protocol

A standard postoperative rehabilitation protocol is needed. After superior fulcrum reconstruction, the patient is placed in a sling for 6 weeks. Passive activities and active activities of the wrist and elbow are performed within 2 weeks postoperatively, and active-assisted activities begin at 6 weeks postoperatively, at which point the shoulder outreach pillow is removed. Isometric contraction training of the deltoid is performed at 4 weeks. Complete passive activity is performed at 6 weeks according to the patient’s tolerance level; endurance activities are allowed at 12 weeks. Pearls and Pitfalls

Discussion

Treatment options for IMRCTs include partial repair, repair with patch augmentation, subacromial balloon spacer placement, tendon transfer, superior capsular reconstruction (SCR), and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA).5 Although many surgical methods exist for the treatment of IMRCTs, their treatment is prone to various postoperative complications and has a high failure rate.6 Partial repair can result in high retear rates (often >50%) when used to treat IMRCTs,7, 8, 9 and young age (<60 years) is an independent risk factor for poor functional improvement10 and increased complications after RTSA.11 Recently, the use of subacromial balloon spacers has been popular among scholars, and current short-term follow-up studies have shown that it can help improve shoulder function12,13; however, some authors have shown that the theoretical benefits of subacromial balloon spacer placement for delaying superior humeral head translation and arthropathic changes could not be observed in their studies.14 Furthermore, its functional results and satisfaction rates are low.14 As for tendon transfer, it is reported that this technique is only suitable for young patients without glenohumeral arthritis and with high postoperative functional requirements.2 Moreover, it has been reported that this technique frequently results in subscapularis tears, fatty infiltration of the teres minor, pseudoparalysis, and passive flexion or abduction less than 80°.15, 16, 17 SCR was proposed by Mihata et al. in 2013 as a joint-preserving treatment option for patients with IMRCTs, showing promising postoperative clinical outcomes. Subsequent studies18 have reported highly variable rates of complications including graft retear, reoperations, infections, and revision surgical procedures.19 RTSA has yielded improved clinical outcomes in elderly patients with severe glenohumeral arthritis. Similarly, however, some scholars have pointed out various complications that occur after RTSA. A follow-up observation study of 854 consecutively implanted RTSAs in 797 patients over a 13-year period showed a complication rate of 22%, including further surgical procedures, acromial fractures, and glenoid complications.20 In addition, RTSA yields more trauma in patients and costs more. Therefore, a safe and effective treatment option is urgently needed for sports medicine surgeons to achieve better postoperative efficacy.21

Since Gerber et al.2 proposed latissimus dorsi transfer for massive rotator cuff tears in 1988, the balance of the force couples of the rotator cuff has gradually gained attention. Partial repair restores the balance of the force couples of the rotator cuff through a “suspension bridge” structure, and the short-term clinical outcomes improve significantly postoperatively.22 However, about half of the patients in one study were not satisfied with their outcomes, which deteriorated over time.23 SCR, repair with patch augmentation, and repair with subacromial balloon spacers are all static reconstruction for the force of the rotator cuff, and their biomechanical mechanisms, surgical indications, and long-term clinical efficacy still need to be further investigated.24,25 At present, dynamic reconstruction mainly refers to tendon transfer, which replaces the function of the rotator cuff muscle by changing the starting and ending positions of the normal muscle and tendon around the glenohumeral joint, restores the balance of the force couples of the rotator cuff, effectively improves the range of motion of the shoulder joint, and greatly improves the quality of life of young patients with a high demand for sports participation.26 However, tendon transfer disrupts the anatomic structure, and the expected strength of the transferred tendon unit is always weaker when compared with native function27; in addition, because scapulothoracic motion is a significant component of shoulder joint movement and because some of the transferred muscle-tendon units originate from the thorax, the lines of action, muscle lengths, and muscle forces are all dependent on scapulothoracic dynamics.28 Our technique is also a dynamic reconstruction, where the expected strength of the transferred tendon is less than the native strength of the tendon and no longer dependent on scapulothoracic dynamics.

In our technique, we fix the graft to the humeral head tunnels; we do not perform onto-surface fixation or repair because we consider onto-surface repair ineffective in terms of tendon-bone healing. We prefer humeral bone tunnel–based repair, as in anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction.29 An animal experiment using histologic and biomechanical evaluations has reported that humeral bone tunnel–based repair is advantageous for tendon-bone healing.30 Furthermore, in our technique, the 2 tendons form a suture bridge structure in the shoulder joint, which increases the downward pressure of the humeral head and the acromial–humeral head distance, therefore reducing acromial impingement.31

Compared with other surgical options for IMRCTs, such as SCR, tendon transfer, and RTSA, our technique has some advantages, including increasing the tendon-bone contact area, promoting tendon-bone healing with the growth factor of the bone tunnel, enhancing initial biomechanical strength with the suture bridge structure, and reducing operative costs without the need for medical implants. However, our technique also has shortcomings: We focus on restoring the balance of the force couples of the rotator cuff without anatomically reconstructing the rotator cuff. Therefore, our technique is not suitable for young patients with demanding high postoperative shoulder function. Although our technique has worked well overall, further clinical investigations are needed to confirm that it is, indeed, an alternative treatment option. In conclusion, superior fulcrum reconstruction with autologous ipsilateral peroneus longus tendon can be considered an alternative treatment option for IMRCT patients.
