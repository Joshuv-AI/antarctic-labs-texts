Physical Activity and Psychological Health in Vitiligo Patients: A Cross‐Sectional Study on Depression, Anxiety, and Stress
Authors: Zeinab Aryanian, Elahe Asgari, Azar Shirzadian, Hemmat Gholinia Ahangar, Sama Heidari, Armon Massoodi, Hanie Babaie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsVitiligo is a long‐lasting depigmenting disorder that has a significant impact on people's emotional health but does not cause physical impairment. They deal with high levels of anxiety, depression, and stress. Physical activity (PA) is one of the potentially protective factors against psychological disorders, but its role in patients with vitiligo has not been evaluated before.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted on 85 vitiligo patients aged ≥ 15. Participants with psychiatric comorbidities or physical limitations were excluded. The Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS‐21) was used to measure psychological symptoms, and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to assess PA levels. Disease severity was assessed using the Vitiligo Area Scoring Index (VASI). The relationships between clinical/demographic characteristics, psychological symptoms, and PA were investigated.ResultsPsychologic distress was common, with 58% reporting stress, 55% depression, and 49% anxiety. The proportion of low, moderate, and high PA levels were 26%, 43%, and 31% respectively. No statistically significant association was found between PA levels and stress, anxiety, or depression. Anxiety scores was positively correlated with disease severity scores (r = 1.228, p = 0.04), and longer disease duration was associated with higher depression levels. PA levels were significantly higher among males (p = 0.02), but unrelated to most other demographic or clinical variables.ConclusionAlthough vitiligo cases experience considerable psychological distress, no protective effect of PA was found in this study for any of the mental health symptoms. The results indicate that psychological disorders in this group may need interventions more than general PA or life style changes and targeted psychological care or medications may be needed for this population. Larger and long‐term studies are recommended to better assess PA's role in the psychological management of vitiligo.Keywords: dermatology, mental health, physical activity, psychology, vitiligo

Introduction

Vitiligo is a persistent skin disease characterized by progressive melanocyte destruction and depigmented macules, affecting approximately 0.5% to 2% of the global population [1]. This condition is believed to be caused by a complex interplay that includes immune system dysregulation, oxidative stress, environmental factors, and genetics and is frequently accompanied by other autoimmune conditions, especially autoimmune thyroid disorders. Alopecia areata, Addison's disease, autoimmune gastritis, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), pernicious anemia, psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and type 1 diabetes are among other commonly linked autoimmune disorders, though their prevalence differ by population [2].

Although vitiligo does not cause physical pain and restrictions and does not shorten life expectancy, it causes a considerable psychological burden because of its influence on appearance [3]. Vitiligo is one of the most psychologically disturbing conditions in dermatology that can impact social life and mental health. Low self‐confidence, low self‐esteem, anxiety, stress, and depression can all result from it. Vitiligo patients suffer from social stigma, discrimination, and lower quality of life [4]. These problems can get more severe, causing a substance or alcohol addiction, suicidal thoughts, or even suicide attempts [5]. The most two common psychological disorders among vitiligo patients are depression (0.1%–62.3%) and anxiety (1.9%–67.9%) [6]. In addition, the association between mental health and vitiligo is bidirectional since stress, anxiety and depression can initiate the course of disease [7].

There are numerous vitiligo treatment options available currently, ranging from topical treatments (e.g., corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, topical vitamin D3 analogs, 5‐gluorouracil), phototherapy, systemic immunosuppressants, depigmentation treatments, to surgical procedures such as melanocyte transplantation for stable, localized cases of refractory disease. More recent immune modulating therapies, like Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, seem to be promising in repigmentation. The treatment is selected according to disease activity, disease severity, and previous response to therapy. However, treating vitiligo is still challenging with inconsistent results [8, 9].

Some studies have shown a relationship between various demographic and clinical variables and psychological symptoms. For example, patients who have more skin involvement are more likely to have higher levels of depression and anxiety, and women are more likely to experience higher levels of anxiety and hopelessness than men [10, 11].

Despite the prominent psychological features of vitiligo, dermatologists seem to prioritize the treatment of cutaneous lesions over addressing psychiatric disturbances. Furthermore, many patients do not comply with psychiatric referrals because of the stigma, skepticism about psychiatric drugs, concerns over side effects, and fear of addiction [1, 12]. Thus, exploring natural ways in which patients can cope with stress, anxiety, and depression can be very useful for the holistic management of vitiligo. New research indicates that physical activity (PA) may be protective against mental health disorders and that the odds of depression and anxiety are lowered with regular PA [13, 14, 15]. However, the relationship between PA and psychological well‐being in vitiligo patients has not yet been explored.

The purpose of this study is to establish the relationship between PA and psychological symptoms in vitiligo patients. Using standardized tools to measure stress, anxiety, depression, and PA to clarify the possible protective effect of PA on the psychological distress seen in vitiligo patients. We also explored the correlations between the psychological symptoms and PA and the clinical and demographic characteristics of vitiligo patients. Exploring these links might also lead to more holistic treatment plans, addressing both mental and skin health.

Methods

A cross‐sectional study was performed on 85 vitiligo patients referring to Yahya Nejad Hospital, Babol, Iran between April 2023 to March 2024.

Inclusion criteria comprised patients who diagnosed as vitiligo by a board‐certified‐dermatologists and who were older than 15 years old. The inclusion of mid‐adolescents (15–17 years) was based on two key reasons. First, prior research indicates that this age group is especially prone to the psychosocial effects of vitiligo, as reported by Silverberg et al. [16] teenagers between the ages of 15 and 17 had the greatest self‐consciousness, and their quality of life was the most significantly affected. Second, a notable percentage of patients referred to our dermatological clinic are in this age range; therefore, excluding them would have decreased sample representativeness and missed a population that is obviously impacted by the illness. This wider age range allowed us to reflect the real‐world patient population and a more comprehensive understanding of the psychological impact of vitiligo across different life stages.

Patients with the history of psychological disorders or taking psychological medications and patients with conditions that limit PA, such as respiratory or heart diseases, were excluded. The purpose of the study was explained to the patients and their written informed consent were taken before study. This study was carried out in accordance with the latest Helsinki Declaration. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Babol University of Medical Sciences. The ethics number is IR.MUBABOL.HRI.REC.1401.283.

At first their demographic and clinical data was recorded in their medical documents including their age, sex, marital status, place of residence, body mass index (BMI), having a job, education, smoking status, duration of disease, location of lesions, severity of disease. To estimate the severity of disease Vitiligo Area Scoring Index (VASI) was used. The body was categorized into five distinct and nonoverlapping regions: hands, upper limbs (excluding the hands), torso, lower limbs (excluding the feet), and feet. The axillary region was considered to be part of the upper limbs, while the inguinal region was grouped with the lower limbs. Also, the buttocks were assigned to the lower limbs. Treatment and evaluation of vitiligo on the face and neck were optional and only conducted at the patient's request; however, these areas were not considered in the overall assessment.

To assess the extent of vitiligo involvement, a “hand unit” was used as a standard reference. This unit included the palm along with the inner surfaces of all fingers and was estimated to represent about 1% of the total body surface area [17].

Depigmentation can range from 0% to 100%. Complete depigmentation (100%) means no pigment remains, whereas at 90%, faint traces of pigment are still visible. At 75% depigmentation, the number of areas with no pigment is greater than the number with pigment. At 50% the depigmented and pigmented areas are approximately equal. Level 25% depigmentation means pigmented areas are still dominant, while at 10%, only small areas of pigment loss are visible [18]. The overall body VASI score is computed by the following formula, in which contributions from different body regions are considered; the final score ranges from 0 to 100.

The patients then were asked to fill up the validated Persian version of the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale ‐ 21 Items (DASS‐21) questionnaire [19] and the validated Persian version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [20]. The DASS‐21 scale assesses symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress over the past week. It includes items rated on a four‐point Likert scale, where each response varies from 0 (“does not apply to me at all”) to 3 (“applies to me most of the time”). Depression, anxiety, and stress subscale scores are calculated by the sum of answers in each subscale multiplied by 2, in accordance with DASS‐42 scoring.

On the basis of the DASS guideline cut‐off scores by Lovibond & Lovibond, these subscale scores are further classified into different severity levels, including normal, mild, moderate, severe, and extremely severe [21]. In relation to psychometric properties, the DASS‐21 has demonstrated good validity and reliability among Iranian samples. Sahebi A et al. [22] found good internal consistency (α = 0.77–0.79) as well as significant correlations with the clinical scales (e.g., BDI: r = 0.74; Zung: r = 0.67). Kakemam E et al. [19] also supported these findings in an Iranian clinical sample of nurses, with high reliability (α = 0.88–0.93) and test‐retest ICCs between 0.75 and 0.86.

A previous study by Vahedian‐Azimi A et al. [23] found that the DASS‐21 showed a sensitivity of 75% and specificity of 89%, compared to the structured psychiatric interviews in an Iranian context. These are not based on their own data, but they imply supportive evidence from earlier validation studies.

The IPAQ Short Form includes seven questions designed to evaluate PA levels over the past 7 days. This is a well‐known, worldwide tool, which has been validated in Persian and other languages and populations [24, 25, 26]. These questions assess the frequency, duration, and intensity of PA (vigorous activity, moderate activity, walking, and sitting time).

Metabolic equivalent of task (MET) minutes/week were used to categorize physical activity levels. Walking has been calculated and expressed as 3.3 METs × minutes × days, moderate activity as 4.0 METs × minutes × days and vigorous activity as 8.0 METs × minutes × days. This value is the total MET‐minutes per week which is the sum of all activity MET values.

Low activity can refer to either no reported activity or an amount too low to meet the criteria of the moderate or high activity categories. Moderate activity was defined as accumulating 600 MET‐min/week, either by performing intensive activities for at least 20 min on 3 or more days or by performing moderate activities or walking for at least 30 min on 5 or more days or by 1 of all combinations reaching 600 MET‐min/week. Being highly active is defined as performing vigorous activity on 3 or more days with total activity ≥ 1500 MET‐min/week or any combination of activities on 7 or more days with total activity ≥ 3000 MET‐min/week [27].

The data were analyzed statistically with SPSS 22, p < 0.05 was considered significant. Quantitative data was represented in mean and standard deviation, whereas qualitative data was reported as frequency and percentage. Chi‐square test was used to study the correlation of categorical variables and the results were reported with the p‐values and the corresponding degrees of freedom (df). Where appropriate, Cramér's V was mentioned to evaluate the effect size, or strength of associations.

For continuous variables, the normality distribution was assessed by Shapiro–Wilk test. When assumption of normality was met, Pearson correlation coefficient was used, otherwise Spearman rank correlation was used.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Data

Of the 85 vitiligo cases studied, 53 (62%) were women and 32 (38%) were men, with a mean age of 33.89 ± 13.14 years. 51 (60%) were married. In terms of educational level, 23 patients (27%) had lower than diploma‐level education, and 62 patients (73%) had diploma‐level education or higher. Of these, 41 (48%) were in employment and 44 (52%) were not.

7 (8%) of cases were smokers while 78 (92%) were non‐smokers. 59 (69%) patients lived in urban areas, and 26 (31%) patients resided in rural regions. 47 (55%) of the patients had a BMI less than 25, whereas 38 (45%) had a BMI of 25 or more. The average duration of the disease was 9.15 ± 9.67 years, with a median duration of 5 years. Patient details, including lesion location and disease severity measured by VASI, are detailed in Table 1. About 88% of patients had lesions on visible sites of the body. The upper limbs and hands (88%) and the face and neck (78%) were the most common sites. The VASI score was below 5% in 49% of cases and above 10% in 25%.

Assessing Correlation Between Stress, Anxiety, Depression, PA and Demographic and Clinical Features

No significant relationship was found between stress, anxiety, or depression levels and factors such as age (35 years or younger vs. older than 35), sex, marital status (single or married), place of residence (rural or urban), educational background (lower than diploma or higher than diploma), employment status (employed or unemployed), smoking, BMI (under 25 or above 25), or VASI levels (under 5%, between 5% and 10%, above 10%). Similarly, there was no significant link between the location of lesions, whether they were on exposed areas or not and levels of stress, anxiety, or depression (all p > 0.05).

However, a significant relationship was observed between disease duration (≤ 5 years vs. > 5 years) and depression levels (χ² = 10.63, df = 4, p = 0.03); specifically, all three patients with severe depression had been living with vitiligo for more than 5 years.

We also examined correlations between VASI scores and stress, anxiety, and depression scores using Pearson's correlation coefficient. While stress and depression showed no strong associations (r = 0.088, p = 0.42 and r = 0.101, p = 0.36, respectively) there was a weak but significant positive correlation between VASI scores and anxiety, indicating that anxiety scores tend to increase as disease severity worsens (r = 0.228, p = 0.04) (Figure 2).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study looking into the relationship between PA and psychological problems: stress, anxiety, and depression among vitiligo subjects. Although there is no physical disability, vitiligo has a severe psychological impact, and despite the fact that it is not contagious, it leads to social withdrawal [10]. Our findings indicate a considerable prevalence of psychological distress among vitiligo patients, manifested by 58% having stress, 55% depression, and 49% anxiety, which is consistent with existing literature showing that vitiligo has a major effect on psychological health [5, 6].

The possible role of PA in reducing psychological distress has been increasingly examined in recent years. A meta‐analysis by Felipe B et al. [13] showed that engaging in PA reduces the likelihood of depression developing across different age groups: in young individuals (adjusted odds ratio = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.83–0.98), in adults (adjusted odds ratio = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.70–0.87), and in older adults (adjusted odds ratio = 0.79, 95% CI = 0.72–0.86). This suggests that, regardless of age or geographic location, PA could help to prevent depression. Moreover, a systematic review and meta‐analysis showed that PA lowers anxiety [15]. Another systematic review aiming to understand the effects of PA on depression and anxiety during the COVID‐19 pandemic showed that people who participated in moderate‐to‐high intensity PA were 12%–32% less likely to have depressive symptoms and 15%–34% less likely to have anxiety symptoms [14]. However, the effect of PA in diminishing psychological distress among dermatology patients is less explored. Rajoo et al. looked for the relationship between PA levels and depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms in alopecia areata (AA) patients. They noticed that those with over 50% scalp hair loss and low PA levels were more likely to experience severe depression (p = 0.003), moderate anxiety (p = 0.04), and mild stress (p = 0.003). They concluded that increased PA in AA individuals with significant hair loss may improve mental health [28].

A newly published paper introduced the positive effects of a structured PA intervention on patients suffering from chronic plaque psoriasis. The 10‐week intervention consisted of 60‐min, twice‐weekly walks led by a sports and exercise scientist, followed by 10 weeks of independent activity. The findings confirm a solid relationship among increased PA and improved psoriasis severity, cardiovascular health, psychological health, and functional capacity. Both anxiety and depression improved at week 20, but only the reduction in anxiety was statistically significant [29].

Another study examining the PA and mental health relationship among atopic dermatitis or chronic spontaneous urticaria patients revealed that in atopic dermatitis, any PA was linked to reduced depression (p < 0.001), anxiety (p = 0.043), and insomnia (p < 0.001). In chronic spontaneous urticaria, moderate to vigorous exercise had no impact on mental health or quality of life [30].

Evaluating the connections between stress and PA in Korean atopic dermatitis cases likewise revealed that the level of stress was significantly lower in the group of patients who had regular PA [31].

In a recent paper by Elias de França and colleagues [32], they pointed out the lack of studies on the role of environmental variables like diet, physical exercise, or the level of habitual PA in vitiligo patients. They discuss the therapeutic potential of chronic physical exercise for treating and preventing vitiligo progression and suggest that physical exercise may modulate the inflammatory balance, as well as activate the endogenous antioxidant system (EAS) and restore mitochondrial function, thus stabilizing vitiligo and preventing its spreading while promoting re‐pigmentation.

In this study, we did not find a significant relationship between PA levels and the severity of stress, anxiety, or depression (p = 0.13, > 0.99, 0.74, respectively). This may indicate that vitiligo has a unique relationship with PA and mental health that differs from other dermatologic disorders. The psychological burden of vitiligo might be mostly because of its overt nature and the existing stigma and works through the ways that are not affected by the general mood‐improving effects of PA. The social and cosmetic consequences of vitiligo, including stigma, shame, avoidance, and social impairment, may outcompete the benefits of PA [33, 34]. Many people experience anxiety when exercising in public or in groups, which leads to avoidance behaviors that lower the psychological benefits of PA.

Additionally, vitiligo is an autoimmune condition with underlying immunological dysregulation and chronic inflammation [35], both of which are connected to mood disorders [36]. Although having a regular exercise routine can alter immunological function and reduce systemic inflammation [37], it is still unclear how much of a psychological benefit it has; intensive or prolonged interventions may be required to see noticeable improvements.

Oxidative stress also plays a role in vitiligo development. Patients with vitiligo frequently have mitochondrial dysfunction and reduced antioxidant capacity [35]. Exercise strengthens antioxidant defense systems, which may improve skin and mental health [32]. However, the psychological benefits of PA may not be immediately felt by patients who already have an oxidative imbalance.

Our demographic and clinical analyzes also provide further information. Gender was found to be an important factor for PA levels, where men had higher levels of PA than women (47% high PA) as compared to women (21% high PA) (p = 0.02). This is in concordance with broader trends of gender differences in PA participation [38].

We also noticed that smokers had greater levels of PA than non‐smokers. However, this result must be taken with caution since the number of smokers in our sample was quite small (n = 7), and thus the probabilities of statistical fluctuations are high, which limits the generalization of this result. Moreover, all of the smoker patients in our study were young (35 years old or younger), and 6 of them were men, which may also influence the association of smoking and PA, as younger and male individuals are physically more active in general [39]. In addition, given the small sample size, the impact of the random variation cannot be ignored.

Disease duration and disease severity also showed notable relationships with psychological outcomes. Depression showed a significant relationship with disease duration. In addition, we found a weak but statistically significant positive relationship between VASI scores and anxiety scores (r = 0.23, p = 0.04), indicating that more severe disease increases anxiety.

We did not find significant connections between stress, anxiety, or depression with other demographic and clinical factors such as age, sex, marital status, place of residence, education level, employment status, smoking, BMI, and lesions' location.

Original studies conducted in Middle Eastern countries provide important cultural context. In a study conducted in Saudi Arabia by Molla et al. [3], 58.8% of participants had major depressive disorders. A higher prevalence of depression was found among women, individuals aged 18–25, divorced or single individuals, and those with lower incomes. Another study from Egypt reported that more than two‐thirds of patients with vitiligo suffer from severe depression, anxiety and stress and a significant association between illness duration and lesion site with total depression, anxiety, and stress scores exists [4].

In a study done by Hamidizadeh et al., they claimed a positive and significant relationship between hopelessness, anxiety, depression, and disease duration and a negative relationship between age and hopelessness, anxiety, and depression among Iranian population, highlighting how social and cultural elements affect mental health. Like us, they found no significant distinction between married and single vitiligo patients' degrees of anxiety, stress, and depression [10].

A 2021 Egyptian study published in Middle East Current Psychiatry [40] closely aligned with our findings. The study evaluated 50 vitiligo patients, reporting a prevalence of stress (76%, n = 38), anxiety (78%, n = 39), and depression (80%, n = 40). No statistically significant correlation was found between age, marital status, occupation, vitiligo type, lesion site, or therapy type with stress, anxiety, or depression (p > 0.05). However, a significant positive correlation was observed between female gender and anxiety (p < 0.05), as well as between moderate‐to‐severe vitiligo and both anxiety and depression. A relationship between longer disease duration and higher depression scores was reported by a recent study seeking the stigmatization, impairment in quality of life, and psychosocial comorbidities in vitiligo patients [41].

According to a study evaluating the psychological effects of vitiligo in Iraqi patients, 40% of them had significant psychological distress, as evidenced by high scores on the Beck Depression Inventory‐II (BDI‐II). Their results also showed that female patients and the ones with higher BMI were more likely to experience negative psychological effects, in contrast our findings showed no significant association between BMI and psychological disorders, including depression [42].

These region‐specific findings confirm the psychological distresses identified in our sample.

While PA did not show a statistically significant protective effect against psychological symptoms in this study, the well‐known benefits PA has on general health further support that it should remain a fundamental piece of holistic vitiligo treatment. In addition, beneficial effects of PA on vitiligo‐related comorbidities [32], including diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome [43], psoriasis [29], AA [28], and atopic dermatitis [30], have also been reported. These findings imply that the psychosocial burden of vitiligo needs a more targeted approach, such as counseling, medication, or cognitive‐behavioral therapy. Dermatologists must be alert about the psychological burden of vitiligo and its significant impact on patients' personal and social lives. Since anxiety and depression can reduce the treatment efficacy [7], mental health support must be included in vitiligo management.

There are some limitations to this study. The small size of the sample does not have sufficient power to detect small associations between PA and psychological symptoms. Moreover, DASS‐21 and IPAQ are self‐report‐based measures, which can be biased due to recall, and the perception of stigma was not examined in context with other cultural factors. Although the employment status of participants was collected, we did not obtain detailed information on the type of occupations of the participants (i.e., manual or sedentary work). Therefore, it was not possible to properly assess the potential impact of occupational physical demands on PA levels. Lastly, the cross‐sectional design restricts the capacity to make conclusions. Interventional trials are essential to establish a strong conclusion about PA influence on psychological symptoms of vitiligo.

Conclusion

Psychological distress was common among Iranian vitiligo patients in the current study, with 49% reporting anxiety, 58% reporting stress, and 55% reporting depression, indicating a significant psychological burden of vitiligo.

We did not find a significant relationship between existing PA levels and psychological symptoms among the patients, even though we know about the benefits of PA for the mental health of the general population and other dermatologic disorders, including atopic dermatitis. It can be assumed that the psychosocial impact of vitiligo is mostly attributed to the social and cosmetic repercussions of the disease or the role of immune dysregulation and oxidative stress in vitiligo pathogenesis rather than modifiable lifestyle behaviors as PA.

Our findings also showed that anxiety is weakly related to disease severity, while more extended disease duration is correlated with increasingly greater depression, underscoring the importance of psychosocial screening in chronic cases. The absence of a protective effect of PA in this cohort may also demonstrate the importance of integrated mental health care in the management of vitiligo. Regular psychological evaluation, along with counseling or psychiatric assistance, should be offered as an integral part of the care.
