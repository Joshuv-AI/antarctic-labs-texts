Patients With Nonfast Peritoneal Transfer Rate are More Suitable for Incremental Peritoneal Dialysis
Authors: Jing Guo, Yangyang Xin, Ting Zhou, Shiyan Tu, Xilong Mo, Haishan Wu, Chunyan Yi, Jianxiong Lin, Hongjian Ye, Xiao Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPrevious studies have shown that incremental peritoneal dialysis (iPD) improves the prognosis of patients on PD with tolerable residual kidney function (RKF). The peritoneal solute transfer rate (PSTR) characterizes peritoneal ultrafiltration and solute transfer. However, the most appropriate recipients of iPD have not been identified. Therefore, we hypothesized that patients with different PSTRs would experience different clinical benefits of iPD therapy.MethodsWe studied patients with RKF who initiated continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD) at our center. The enrolled patients were allocated to fast and nonfast PSTR groups, and then to iPD or full-dose PD. To mitigate the influence of confounding factors, propensity score matching was employed. The clinical outcomes associated with iPD and full-dose PD in the fast and nonfast PSTR groups were compared.ResultsA total of 974 patients on CAPD were enrolled. The median duration of maintenance using iPD for the nonfast PSTR group was longer than that for the fast PSTR group (21.0 vs. 15.6 months, P=0.011). Patients in nonfast PSTR group who underwent iPD had lower risks of mortality [hazard ratio (HR) 0.25, P=0.002], anuria (HR 0.53, P=0.003), and cardiovascular (CV) mortality (HR 0.36, P=0.047) than those who underwent full-dose PD after matching. However, these clinical benefits of iPD therapy were not present in the fast PSTR group.ConclusionPatients who show nonfast PSTR maintain iPD longer and experience superior clinical benefits compared with those who undergo fast PSTR. Therefore, patients who experience nonfast PSTR may be more suitable for iPD therapy than those who experience fast PSTR.

PD permits greater autonomy and is associated with a lower burden than hemodialysis for patients with end-stage renal disease.1 However, there is still substantial scope to improve PD therapy and treatment satisfaction. Recently, the International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis recommended a “person-centered” approach to PD by physicians.2 iPD is a specific and direct approach to the implementation of this recommendation for patients with tolerable RKF.3 Observational studies have demonstrated advantages of iPD, in terms of cost, the burdens placed on caregiver and patients, and the quality of life and prognosis of patients.4,5 Previous studies of the effects of iPD on prognosis have yielded inconsistent results.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Given the diversity of patients who undergo PD, it is evident that not all are suitable for iPD therapy. We hypothesize that the inclusion of patients who are not appropriate for iPD therapy masks the benefits of iPD therapy and thereby has contributed to the inconsistent findings of previous studies. We contend that this, plus the limited size of the studies performed to date, means that the most appropriate candidates for iPD have not been identified, and this issue warrants further investigation.

The PSTR is a key characteristic of peritoneal ultrafiltration and solute transfer in patients undergoing PD and is important for the optimization and individualization of PD prescriptions.11 The main challenges facing iPD therapy are volume management and solute removal.5 In addition, numerous studies have shown that adverse clinical outcomes are associated with fast PSTR.12, 13, 14, 15 Therefore, we wondered whether patients undergoing PD who have different PSTRs would obtain different clinical benefits from iPD therapy. To evaluate this possibility, we retrospectively studied patients who underwent CAPD and had tolerable RKF (estimated glomerular filtration rate [GFR] ≥ 3 ml/min per 1.73 m2), comparing the clinical benefits of iPD in those with different PSTRs, to identify the most appropriate candidates for iPD therapy.

Methods

Data Sources and Participants

Eligible patients were initially identified in the PD registry system of our center. We screened the patients who commenced PD therapy between 2008 and 2020, and individuals meeting the following criteria were excluded: (id) age < 18 years, (ii) transfer from permanent hemodialysis or failed renal transplantation, (iii) missing peritoneal equilibration test data or follow-up data, (iv) baseline estimated GFR < 3 ml/min per 1.73 m2, and (v) withdrawal from PD within 6 months of initiation. All the participants were followed-up with until December 31, 2023.

The participants were divided into fast and nonfast PSTR groups according to their baseline 4-hour dialysate-to-plasma creatinine concentration ratio (D/Pcr). These patients were then assigned to iPD (≤ 3 exchange/d) or full-dose PD (>3 exchanges/d) according to their initial PD prescription. The project was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University and was performed according to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data Collection

According to the follow-up protocol in use at our center, the patients routinely underwent peritoneal equilibration test examination and an assessment of dialysis adequacy every 6 months, as well as an assessment of clinical symptoms and laboratory examinations every 3 months, during the follow-up period. Based on our experience, the clinical index of patients with PD stabilized gradually within the first month of PD initiation. In addition, in previous studies, baseline data have been collected at the start of the first month of PD. Therefore, we set and recorded the peritoneal equilibration test data within the first 1 to 6 months of PD as the baseline data, and these were used to assess the PSTR status of the patients.

We collected and recorded their baseline demographic and clinical characteristics and laboratory data, which are summarized in Table 1. The laboratory data for patients who underwent iPD were also recorded when they transferred to full-dose PD, as were the reasons for this transfer. Definitions

iPD was defined as an initial level of PD that was less than a conventional “full dose,” but sufficient for the patient, given their RKF. The level of dialysis was gradually increased as the RKF declined.17 According to this definition, we assigned patients with an initial PD level ≤ 3 exchanges/d to an iPD group and those with an initial PD level > 3 exchanges/d as a full-dose PD group. According to the recommendation of the International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis, the cut-off value used to differentiate fast and nonfast PSTRs was the mean 4-hour D/Pcr, which varies in different regions and centers.18 In our previous study, we found that the mean 4-hour D/Pcr was 0.71 in our center.12 Therefore, a fast PSTR was defined as a 4-hour D/Pcr ≥ 0.71 at the initiation of PD, and a nonfast PSTR was defined as a 4-hour D/Pcr < 0.71 at this time.

Implementation of iPD Therapy

In our center, iPD therapy was considered if the patients had relatively good RKF, a good diet, obtained good sleep, had an acceptable mental state, and achieved their solute clearance target and fluid balance at the initiation of PD. iPD therapy was initiated based on shared decision making by the physicians and patients. All the patients were closely monitored, and their dose of PD was gradually increased to a full dose as the RKF declined, determined using a comprehensive evaluation.17 Specifically, the dose of PD in the patients undergoing iPD therapy was initially ≤ 3 exchanges/d and was gradually increased in the present study. Other details, such as the glucose concentration of the dialysate and whether to continue PD overnight, were determined by the physicians after a comprehensive evaluation.

Study Outcome

The risk of mortality and the preservation of RKF were focuses for the nephrologists. Therefore, we defined all-cause mortality and anuria as the primary outcomes and CV-related mortality as the secondary outcome.

Statistical Analysis

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics and laboratory data of the participants are summarized using mean ± SD or median with interquartile range for continuous datasets, and frequency for categorical datasets. In addition, we calculated the changes in the laboratory parameters during maintenance iPD therapy using the following formula:

Δvariable = (variable value when converting to full-dose PD – baseline variable value) / maintenance time of iPD status.

Datasets were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon test, chi-square test, or t test, as appropriate.

To mitigate the influence of confounding factors, propensity score matching was employed. Propensity scores were generated using logistic regression models and 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching was performed. A caliper of 0.02 × the pooled SD for the full-dose PD and iPD groups was used for this matching. The covariates chosen for matching were age, diabetes, Charlson comorbidity index score, serum phosphorus, hemoglobin, serum creatinine (SCr), GFR, 24-hour urine volume, and total urea clearance normalized to total body water (Kt/V). To assess the similarity of the 2 groups, we compared the baseline characteristics of patients with fast or nonfast PSTR after matching.

We constructed cumulative hazard curves to estimate the mortality, CV-related mortality, and risk of anuria for the full-dose PD and iPD groups and patients with fast and nonfast PSTRs. Because the estimated probability of survival and the associated confidence interval (CI) may be inaccurate when the risk is low, we truncated the curves at 8 years, when the risk fell below 10% for the entire cohort. The risks of mortality and anuria were calculated on an intention-to-treat basis from the time of initiation of PD for patients in the iPD and full-dose PD groups. Unadjusted and adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to generate HRs and 95% CIs for the all-cause mortality, CV-related mortality, and anuria associated with iPD therapy. Variables considered to be potential confounders and those with P < 0.1 in the univariate regression analysis (age, body mass index, diabetes, Charlson comorbidity index score, SCr, total Kt/V, 24-hour urine volume, GFR, hemoglobin, serum albumin, serum phosphorus, and serum total cholesterol) were used to adjust the multivariate Cox models.

Analyses were conducted using SPSS software for Windows (ver. 25; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and R (R Core Team, 2024). Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Sensitivity Analyses

To assess the robustness of the associations between iPD and clinical outcomes, sensitivity analyses were performed. First, we used inverse probability weighting (IPW) regression to reanalyze the primary outcomes in the IPW R package. Furthermore, given that >90% of patients with iPD transferred to full-dose PD within 6 years, we then limited the follow-up period to 6 years and repeated the analysis.

Results

Baseline Characteristic of the Participants

A total of 2778 patients commenced PD therapy in our PD center between 2008 and 2020, of whom 1804 were excluded after screening according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, meaning that 974 were studied (Figure 1). For these patients, the median duration of follow-up was 50.2 (28.3–81.3) months, their mean age was 46.1 years, 34.8% were women, their mean body mass index was 22.2 kg/m2, 26.9% had diabetes, and 31.8% had a history of CV disease. Of these, 451 patients underwent iPD therapy (211 had a fast PSTR and 240 had a nonfast PSTR) and 523 underwent full-dose PD (286 had a fast PSTR and 237 had a nonfast PSTR). Changes in the PD Prescription and Clinical Characteristics During Incremental Dialysis Status

A total of 451 patients commenced iPD therapy in our PD center, of whom 211 (46.8%) had a fast PSTR and 240 (53.2%) had a nonfast PSTR. During the follow-up period, 376 patients on iPD transferred to full-dose PD, and nearly half transferred to full-dose PD within the first year (49% of the fast PSTR group and 35% of the nonfast PSTR group) (Figure 2a). For the 376 patients who transferred to full-dose PD, the most common reason was fluid overload (155, 41.2%), followed by inadequate solute clearance (147, 39.1%), unknown (40, 10.6%), a personal decision (20, 5.3%), and another reason (14, 3.7%) (Supplementary Figure S1). The median duration of iPD was 17.8 months, and the patients with a nonfast PSTR continued this for longer than those with a fast PSTR (21.0 vs. 15.6 months). Risk of Anuria

During the follow-up period, anuria occurred in 105 patients (36.4%) with a nonfast PSTR and 138 patients (50.0%) with a fast PSTR in the matched cohort. In Table 1, we show the relationship between PD prescription and anuria. The patients undergoing iPD in the nonfast PSTR group had a lower risk of anuria than those in the fast PSTR group (HR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.35–0.80, P = 0.003); however, there was no difference in the risk of anuria for patients undergoing each type of PD who had a fast PSTR (HR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.41–1.05, P = 0.079). Sensitivity Analysis

As shown in Supplementary Table S4, the IPW models yielded the same results as the Cox proportional hazards regression models in the matched cohort. After adjustment for confounders in the IPW models, the patients in the nonfast PSTR group who underwent iPD had lower risks of all-cause mortality (HR: 0.34, 95% CI: 0.18–0.64, P < 0.001), CV-related mortality (HR: 0.37, 95% CI: 0.17–0.81, P = 0.014), and anuria (HR: 0.56, 95% CI: 0.39–0.80, P = 0.002) than those who underwent full-dose PD. However, the clinical benefits of iPD therapy were not present in the fast PSTR group. In addition, when the duration of follow-up period was shortened to 6 years, we found that the advantage of iPD therapy remained (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

In the present cohort study, we found that patients with a nonfast PSTR can maintain iPD longer than those with a fast PSTR. In addition, iPD therapy was associated with lower risks of mortality and anuria than full-dose PD therapy in patients with a nonfast PSTR; however, these benefits were not present in patients with a fast PSTR. Thus, patients with a nonfast PSTR appear to be better suited to iPD therapy than those with a fast PSTR.

Previous research regarding iPD therapy has revealed its beneficial effects on prognosis. Studies by Lee et al.,8 Sandrini et al.,9 and Sans et al.,19 and have shown that iPD preserves RKF more effectively than full-dose PD therapy. Furthermore, our previous studies corroborated these findings, with patients showing longer survival within the first 6 years of PD, superior preservation of RKF, and a lower incidence of peritonitis when undergoing iPD therapy.10 In the present cohort, we identified a positive effect of iPD therapy on RKF and survival in the nonfast PSTR groups. The benefits of iPD therapy may be attributed to a lower level of glucose exposure and lower concentrations of glucose degradation products.20 In addition, owing to the fewer exchanges, iPD therapy is associated with a lower incidence of peritonitis. These factors result in a superior prognosis for patients undergoing PD.7,21,22

Despite these findings, some previous studies have shown that iPD and full-dose PD are associated with comparable survival outcomes and loss of RKF.7,9 Notably, the only randomized controlled trial of iPD therapy showed that patients undergoing 3 exchanges/d during CAPD had similar overall survival, technical survival, and anuria-free survival rates to those undergoing 4 exchanges/d.7 We hypothesized that the inclusion of patients who were less suitable for iPD therapy in previous cohorts had masked its clinical benefit, and we tested this hypothesis in the present study. In this study, we identified differing risks of mortality and anuria associated with iPD therapy for the fast and nonfast PSTR groups. Our previous studies and others have shown that patients with a fast PSTR have a higher risk of ultrafiltration insufficiency than those with a nonfast PSTR.12,15 The principal reason for patients to transfer to full-dose PD in the present study was fluid overload. For patients with iPD therapy, dwell time of every exchange is longer, which causes lower ultrafiltration volume in patients with fast PSTR than in those with nonfast PSTR. Therefore, as expected, iPD therapy can be maintained for a longer period in patients with a nonfast PSTR than in those with a fast PSTR. We speculated that the short duration of maintenance of the incremental status was one reason for this. As mentioned above, the benefits of iPD therapy for prognosis may be explained by a lower level of glucose exposure and a lower concentration of glucose degradation products. Therefore, the patients with a fast PSTR did not significantly benefit from iPD therapy for the short duration of maintenance of the incremental status caused by lower ultrafiltration volume. It is well-established that the baseline RKF has a substantial impact on the clinical outcomes of patients with PD. We found that patients in nonfast PSTR groups who underwent iPD had superior baseline RKFs compared with those who underwent full-dose PD.23 However, because this was a retrospective study, we must acknowledge the potential for bias owing to confounding factors, particularly with respect to the baseline RKF. Therefore, we employed propensity score matching and IPW models to improve the reliability of the results. However, although we adjusted for the positive effect of superior baseline RKF on the survival of patients with iPD, there was no clinical benefit of iPD for the fast PSTR group. Furthermore, patients in the nonfast PSTR group who underwent iPD therapy showed lower blood urea nitrogen and SCr concentrations, higher residual CCr, and more rapid declines in Kt/v than those in the fast PSTR group during the incremental status period. This implies that iPD therapy is associated with a slower decline and superior preservation of RKF in patients with a nonfast PSTR than in those with a fast PSTR.

Although the iPD therapy in fast PSTR did not demonstrate equivalent prognostic benefits to that observed in nonfast PSTR patients, it was preferable in terms of cost. Nardelli et al.4 reported that iPD therapy can reduce the time spent on dialysis, economic costs, water consumption, and plastic consumption, while reserving more free lifetime. Because of the short duration of maintenance of the incremental status caused by poor ultrafiltration, patients with fast PSTR cannot obtain clinical benefits from iPD therapy. However, the defect might be solved by using automated PD (APD) and icodextrin dialysate. Numerous studies show that APD and icodextrin dialysate can improve ultrafiltration volume for patients undergoing PD.24, 25, 26 Unfortunately, APD and icodextrin dialysate have not been widely used in developing countries. CAPD remains the main method for patients with PD in these countries. In China, most patients on PD choose CAPD for economic reasons. Because the use of icodextrin dialysate had not been approved in mainland China during the enrollment period, it was not used in any of the patients in the present study. However, we are currently conducting a randomized controlled trial to clarify the advantages of using icodextrin in iPD therapy.

In addition, we must emphasize that the iPD prescription should be personalized for patients with tolerable RKF; however, this does not imply that PD should be initiated early. Furthermore, patients with an iPD prescription must be closely monitored and followed-up to promptly recognize any decline in RKF and adjust the PD prescription.

The strengths of the present study include the long-term regular follow-up and the comprehensive tracking of outcomes. This is the first study to identify the most appropriate patients for iPD therapy, based on their peritoneal transfer status. However, certain limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. First, we only enrolled patients who underwent CAPD using a glucose-based dialysate. However, iPD therapy can be performed in numerous ways, including by using icodextrin, APD, by reducing fill volume, and with the incorporation of dry periods and days off. Unfortunately, patients with APD and icodextrin dialysate were not enrolled in the present study for the reasons mentioned above. Second, because it was a single-center observational study, some bias was unavoidable, and we are unable to draw conclusions regarding potential causal relationships between the PD regimen and the survival outcomes. Although we conducted propensity score matching and IPW analyses to improve the robustness of the findings and obtained consistent results, they are insufficient to provide robust evidence. Therefore, in the future, prospective studies and randomized controlled trials aimed at identifying the most appropriate candidates for iPD therapy are necessary. In addition, the details of the PD prescriptions for the patients who underwent iPD could not be presented, because of the complexity and frequency of the changes made. However, all the modifications were made by experienced physicians and documented by nurses, especially those involving the transfer of patients from iPD to full-dose PD.

In summary, patients on CAPD with a nonfast PSTR can maintain iPD for longer and experience superior clinical outcomes compared with patients with a fast PSTR. Therefore, patients with a nonfast PSTR are more suitable for iPD therapy than those with a fast PSTR. Although iPD therapy was not associated with superior clinical outcomes compared with full-dose therapy in patients with a fast PSTR, it had the advantage of being more cost-effective.
