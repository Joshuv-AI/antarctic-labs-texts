Histopathology-centered computational evolution of spatial omics: integration, mapping, and foundation models
Authors: Ninghui Hao, Xinxing Yang, Boshen Yan, Dong Li, Junzhou Huang, Xintao Wu, Emily S Ruiz, Arlene Ruiz de Luzuriaga, Chen Zhao, Guihong Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpatial omics (SO) enables spatially resolved molecular profiling, while hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) imaging remains the gold standard for morphological assessment in clinical pathology. Recent computational advances increasingly center H&E images in SO analysis and push resolution toward the single-cell level. We systematically review the computational evolution of SO from a histopathology-centered perspective, organizing methods into three paradigms: integration (jointly modeling of paired multimodal data), mapping (inferring molecular profiles from H&E images), and foundation models (learning generalizable representations from large-scale datasets). We summarize actionable modeling directions and persistent gaps, providing a roadmap for developing, and applying computational frameworks in SO.Keywords: histopathology, H&E image, spatial omics, computational trends, multimodal data integration, foundation models

Introduction

Spatially resolved transcriptomics (ST) and spatial proteomics (SP) were respectively named Method of the Year in 2020 [1] and 2024 [2]. These spatial omics (SO) technologies extend bulk and single-cell profiling into a spatial setting, enabling comprehensive analysis of tissue architectures, cellular interactions, and microenvironmental features in development and disease. In typical workflows, SO technologies provide (i) molecular measurements (e.g. gene expression or protein abundance matrices) and (ii) spatial coordinates of measured regions, often accompanied by matched hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) whole-slide images (WSIs) that provide a common morphological backdrop for interpretation and alignment.

H&E-stained histological images remain the gold standard in clinical pathology, providing visualization of tissue architecture and cellular morphology [3]. Beyond routine use, H&E imaging preserves rich context from cellular to tissue-level organization, making them increasingly function as a shared morphological anchor to connect heterogeneous SO measurements across platforms, disease contexts, and studies. Importantly, the role of H&E images in SO computation evolves. Early computational approaches prioritized molecular matrices, where H&E images served as spatial backgrounds for coordinates or rough tissue masks [4]. Recent work elevated H&E images to a first-class modality for representation learning, cross-modal alignment, and pretraining objectives that model morphology-omics correspondence [5]. Taken together, progress in SO computation is increasingly defined by how methods operationalize morphological information from H&E images.

In this survey, we categorize H&E-centered SO computational methods into three classes (Fig. 1). First, integration methods combine multimodal SO measurements (with H&E images), where H&E typically provides morphological context and spatial coordinates. Second, mapping methods emphasize cross-modal inference, particularly H&E-to-X, where H&E is treated as the primary observable and X denotes expensive, noisy, or missing SO modalities (ST, SP, etc.). Third, foundation models aim to learn general-purpose representations, which operationalize H&E usage at scale by pretraining on large-scale paired or weakly paired image-omics data to support diverse downstream tasks.

From the H&E perspective, these three paradigms represent a naturally chronological progression: integration enhances spatial molecular analysis utilizing H&E morphology when paired data exist; mapping infers unobserved profiles from H&E images when modalities are missing or costly; and foundation models leverage ubiquitous H&E data to learn general-purpose representations with improved robustness and generalization. This evolution is better characterized as a spiral rather than a strictly linear progression. Accordingly, foundation models should be viewed as an enabling layer that can be plugged into integration and mapping pipelines, rather than as a competing class. We also provide a roadmap of this evolution toward H&E-anchored, single-cell-aware multimodal analysis (Fig. 2).

A major driver of this field is the transition from spot-level readouts, such as original Visium [6], to cellular and subcellular resolution (e.g. VisiumHD [7]). While early ST techniques aggregate expressions from multiple cells and obscure biological variation, single-cell resolution is now essential. However, since comprehensive single-cell SO datasets remain constrained by cost and complexity, the computational challenge shifts to recovering cell-level characteristics and linking morphologies to molecular programs [8]. Fully leveraging cellular morphology from H&E images is a particularly promising strategy for enabling robust, generalizable representations through H&E-centered multimodal learning.

Although existing surveys have summarized SO literature, they often lack in-depth insights and comprehensive synthesis across integration, mapping, and foundation models (Table 1). Prior surveys have focused on integration strategies of modality embeddings [9], specific tasks (e.g. deconvolution [10]), or clinical applications [11]. By contrast, our survey treats H&E images as an anchor to connect methodological threads across paradigms and across spatial resolutions. We also provide a concise overview of the three paradigms regarding input requirements, output types, typical supervision, primary failure modes, and best-fit use cases in Table 2.

Spatial omics technologies and datasets

Technology background: resolution and data quality

ST is broadly categorized into sequencing-based and imaging-based approaches (Fig. 1a). Sequencing-based ST (e.g. Visium [6]) captures transcriptome-wide profiles (15–30k genes) via barcoded “spots” (55 m). While advancements like Visium HD [7] (2 m) approach subcellular resolution, measurements remain “pseudo” single-cell as spots may capture partial cells or intercellular gaps. Another constraint is the restricted capture area (6.5–11 mm), measuring selected fields of view (FOVs). Importantly, H&E staining in these workflows is performed before RNA capture, preserving diagnostic-grade image quality. Conversely, imaging-based ST (e.g. MERSCOPE [18], CosMx [19], and Xenium [20]) provides true single-cell resolution through cyclic probe hybridization and DAPI-guided segmentation. However, gene coverage is limited by probe design, and H&E quality is typically degraded by the preceding cyclic imaging steps. Illustration of resolution is in Additional file 2: Supplementary Fig. S2.

For computational analysis, H&E images are processed at multiple scales. Tissue blocks are sectioned (5 m) and digitized into gigabyte-scale WSIs. To facilitate modeling, WSIs are typically subdivided into smaller patches or tiles using sliding-window strategies. At the highest resolution, individual pixels representing raw intensity values serve as the fundamental input for downstream spatial algorithms.

SP encompasses imaging-based and mass spectrometry (MS)-based technologies. While immunohistochemistry (IHC) remains the gold standard for assessing protein expression in clinical settings, it offers limited multiplexing. Modern multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) has evolved into cyclic methods like CyCIF [21] and CODEX [22]. CyCIF enables dozens of markers but often requires H&E from adjacent sections due to chemical bleaching-caused tissue degradation. CODEX employs DNA-barcoding for higher multiplexing (40–100 markers) but yields suboptimal H&E quality as staining occurs post-imaging. In contrast, Orion [23] uses single-pass mIF (20 markers) to better preserve morphology for high-quality H&E images. MS-based techniques like imaging mass cytometry (IMC) [24] circumvent spectral overlap using metal-tagged antibodies, while deep visual proteomics (DVP) [25] integrates laser microdissection with MS analysis for antibody-independent proteome coverage.

Throughout this review, spatial granularity is categorized into directly measured at native experimental resolution (spot-level, pseudo single-cell, or true single-cell from imaging-based platforms), computationally deconvolved from coarser measurements using external references, and inferred or predicted by learned models from nonmolecular inputs (at bulk, spot, super-resolution, or true cellular granularity). Per-method classifications under this scheme are listed in Additional file 1: Table S2.

In summary, a fundamental trade-off exists: sequencing-based ST and single-pass SP techniques (Orion) prioritize transcriptome/morphological integrity, while imaging-based ST and cyclic SP (CODEX) offer true cellular spatial resolution at the cost of coverage or H&E image quality.

Canonical human benchmarks include the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) [26] and HER2-positive breast cancer (HER2ST) [27] datasets. Mouse benchmarks across various brain regions, embryos, and organs (e.g. prostate, heart, and pancreas) are equally prevalent. Evolutionary trends of computational methods

Since the early 2020s, SO has rapidly evolved in experimental platforms and computational analysis. Initial tools were primarily workflow-embedded, analyzing sequencing outputs alongside matched H&E images. Subsequent integration methods expanded this by jointly modeling paired cross-platform and multimodal data. To address data scarcity and the need for single-cell deconvolution, mapping methods emerged to learn explicit cross-modal functions for inference when paired measurements are incomplete. While these paradigms have co-evolved, their task-specificity often limits generalizability. More recently, the proliferation of large-scale datasets has enabled foundation models to provide robust representations that can be reciprocally utilized within integration and mapping frameworks. Taken together, we organize the evolution of computational methods for SO into three categories: integration, mapping, and foundation models (Fig. 3). Crucially, the role of H&E images has shifted from a spatial coordinate background to a predictive anchor for cross-modal inference and ultimately to a morphological representation backbone. Integration methods

Integrative methods aim to synthesize molecular profiles from different modalities to reveal intricate biological mechanisms via different integration strategies [9]. This field has shifted from geometry-only representations, which rely on spatial coordinates (e.g. [28]), toward morphology-informed integration, where explicitly encoding H&E patches to capture cellular morphology and local spatial interactions for richer spatial representations (e.g. [29, 30]).

Early integration efforts primarily utilized H&E images as spatial references or validation for cell boundary alignment for cross-platform ST datasets or different SO modalities. SpatialScope [31] leverages paired single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) as a reference to achieve cellular resolution via probabilistic deconvolution of spot-level ST, combining nucleus segmentation on H&E images with gene expression decomposition. Similar graph-based methods employ specific cell-to-cell mapping algorithms for deconvolution (e.g. [4]). Subsequently, sophisticated architectures were adopted to handle complex integrations, including variational autoencoders (VAE) (spaVAE [32]), attention mechanisms (SpatialGlue [33]), contrastive discriminators (COSMOS [34]), and conditional diffusion models (CANDIES [35]). While SIMO [36] extended to integrate single-cell multi-omics, these methods largely utilized H&E images as spatial structure referencing or result validation rather than a rich source of morphological information.

A conceptual shift emerged as methods incorporated H&E patches as morphological embeddings. SpaGCN [37] pioneered graph convolutional frameworks to integrate ST with H&E patches for identifying spatially variable genes. GIST [29] continued to leverage H&E patches for spatial profiling through contrastive learning and knowledge distillation enhanced feature extraction, while StereoMM [30] utilized graph autoencoders to align transcriptomic data with morphology and spatial locations. A representative algorithm is MISO [38], designed to integrate H&E images, ST, and multiple SO data using graph adjacency matrices and separate autoencoders for each input modality. By focusing on inter-modality interaction features, MISO preserves the unique contributions of each data type while filtering out low-quality modalities to prevent noise propagation. This trend has further evolved with spEMO [39], which incorporates embeddings from PFMs [5, 40], large language models (LLMs), and spatialMETA [41], enabling the vertical and horizontal integration of H&E images, ST, and SM across diverse samples. Pushing the H&E-anchored paradigm further, SpatialEx [42] couples a pretrained H&E foundation model with hypergraph and contrastive learning to predict single-cell omics profiles from histology and to diagonally integrate complementary ST, SP, and SM panels measured on serial sections.

Beyond methodological innovations, standardized infrastructures and platforms (e.g. SpatialData [43] and Thor [44]) have emerged to mitigate technical variation and cross-dataset reuse limitations. SpatialData [43] defines a unified storage format optimized for large-scale multimodal datasets, supporting interactive analysis, including visualization, annotation, and landmark-based registration of datasets, and integrates with the scverse ecosystem [45] for downstream spatial tasks [46, 47].

In summary, integration methods evolve from bulk to cellular resolution across multiple SO modalities, advancing architectures to tackle batch effects, noise, and resolution limitations. However, these methods fundamentally depend on paired modalities, and H&E often serves only as spatial context rather than a fully exploited morphological signal. As paired data become the limiting factor, mapping approaches emerge to extend these frameworks to infer missing modalities or refine resolution from partially observed data.

Mapping methods

Beyond paired integration, mapping methods relax the requirement for co-measured modalities by learning cross-modal inference functions, primarily using H&E images as a morphological anchor to predict molecular profiles (H&E-to-X), where X can include (spatial) transcriptomics, proteomics, and other SO modalities.

Early mapping methods focused on transcriptomic prediction from H&E images, spanning bulk expression (e.g. HE2RNA [48]) and spot-level ST (e.g. [49–56]). Many of these approaches leverage convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [51, 57], transformers [49, 50], and contrastive learning [52, 53]. BLEEP [52] is the first to introduce a CLIP-based [58] framework for H&E-to-ST prediction. More recently, generative and foundation model-based designs have been introduced [54, 56]. STPath [56] is a generative model that bridges H&E and ST through a PFM backbone [59], jointly encoding histological images, gene expression, organ type, and sequencing technology via a geometry-aware transformer to predict transcriptome-wide expression from H&E images, pretrained on a curated dataset of paired H&E images and ST annotations from HEST-1K [60] and STImage-1k4m [61]. Together, bulk- and spot-level mapping methods establish a foundation for morphology-driven transcriptomic inference, which naturally motivates subsequent extensions toward cell-level resolution.

Because individual cells are directly observable on H&E images, subsequent work refined mapping toward cellular resolution [62–66]. RedeHist [64] integrates scRNA-seq references and pixel-level features extracted by U-net to mitigate the instability of patch-based representations from irregular cell morphology. Similarly, SPiRiT [66] infers single-cell spatial gene expression from H&E images, with an emphasis on interpretable and trustworthy prediction. It adopts a vision transformer (ViT) and introduces Training and Validation Attention Consistency (TAVAC), a training metric that guides the model to select consistent high-attention regions, which ensures interpretable and robust predictions of disease-relevant biomarkers concordant with expert assessment.

In parallel, super-resolution (SR) models aim to computationally deconvolve or impute expression signals beyond physical sequencing limits. iStar [67] is a representative SR gene expression prediction model that employs a hierarchical ViT to reconstruct unobserved high-resolution expression from paired H&E patches, and it has become a common benchmark for subsequent SR approaches. iSCALE [68] uses scRNA-seq as a reference, combining a hierarchical ViT feature extractor and feedforward MLP to infer cellular ST from H&E images. DeepSpot2Cell [69] adopts a DeepSet-based architecture and leverages pretrained PFMs, whose choice could be tailored to specific tasks, such as UNI [70] or H-Optimus-0 [71]. Nevertheless, benchmarks suggest that SR models (e.g. iStar [67] and scstGCN [72]) exhibit limited generalization to unseen samples beyond the training distribution.

Despite these advances, open challenges in mapping are establishing reliable cross-modal correspondence and generalization to unseen tissues, disease contexts, and technical platforms. Contrastive learning (CL) has been introduced to strengthen alignment by pulling matched (“positive”) pairs together while pushing unmatched (“negative”) pairs apart in a shared embedding space. DANet [53] combines dynamic alignment with CL for bulk gene expression prediction, while HAGE [54] leverages UNI [70] embeddings and hierarchical clustering to align gene-guided image patches with expression embeddings locally and globally using CL. Collectively, these approaches indicate that explicitly enforcing correspondence between paired representations can substantially improve mapping fidelity. To enhance generalization, FmH2ST [55] enhances generalization by modeling both inter-slice heterogeneity and intra-slice complexity with dual feature branches fused via adaptive attention. The foundation model branch incorporates images and dual graphs encoding spatial adjacency, using a graph attention network initialized from a PFM [73]. The spot-specific branch extracts local morphological information from H&E images. The effectiveness of such paradigms naturally depends on the quality and domain coverage of the underlying foundation models.

Mapping has also been extended beyond transcriptomics to SP, linking morphological patterns to protein-level information. VirtualMultiplexer [74] is an early groundwork as a generative toolkit to synthesize multiplexed IHC images for several antibody markers (e.g. AR, p53, etc.) from H&E images. ROSIE [75] predicts mIF protein signals from H&E images using paired H&E and CODEX [22] images from the same specimens, aligned via scale-invariant feature transform and random sample consensus (RANSAC)-based [76] affine alignment. While ROSIE demonstrates the feasibility of inferring proteomic patterns from morphology, it highlights practical limitations in cross-site and cross-technology generalization. GigaTIME [77] advances this via a multimodal NestedUNet-based cross-translator, generating virtual mIF images across 21 tumor immune microenvironment markers from H&E patches. Trained on paired H&E–mIF data comprising tens of millions of cells, GigaTIME learns channel-wise activation maps that are stitched into slide-level virtual mIF images, enabling population-level analyses of protein activation patterns and their associations with genomic biomarkers, staging, and survival. HEX [78] leverages pretrained PFMs to predicts the spatial expression of 40 protein markers from histopathology slides. Extensive validation highlights its potential for clinical translation to advance precision medicine applications. Collectively, these methods map H&E-to-SP, reinforcing histology as a unifying anchor for cross-modal spatial inference.

A fundamental but often-overlooked question is what is and is not predictable from morphology alone. Recent studies show that predictability varies systematically by gene category across the transcriptome [48, 75, 79, 80]. Genes whose expression is tightly coupled to histologically visible features tend to be predicted well, including cell-type lineage markers (e.g. CD3, CD20 for lymphocytes; EpCAM, PanCK for epithelium) [48, 75], proliferation and cell-cycle programs (e.g. MKI67) whose signals manifest as nuclear morphology and architectural disorganization [57, 79, 81], structural and extracellular matrix genes (e.g. collagensand vimentin) whose products are directly stained by eosin [80], and clinically actionable lineage-defining markers, such as ERBB2 and KLK3 [80]. Pathway-level analyses consistently identify tumor-promoting inflammation, sustaining proliferative signaling, and activating invasion and metastasis as the most predictable hallmarks [48, 79].

Conversely, several gene classes are systematically poorly recovered. Housekeeping genes carry little spatial variance and offer few morphological cues, which is why benchmarks report higher correlations on highly variable and spatially variable genes than on the full transcriptome [52, 82]. Membrane and lipid-associated proteins, including the clinically critical PD-L1, are intrinsically difficult to infer because hematoxylin and eosin poorly stain hydrophobic membrane structures [80]. Metabolic states such as fatty acid metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation lack distinctive H&E correlates [81], and genome-maintenance genes such as BRCA2, whose phenotypic effects manifest only after mutation events, are similarly weakly coupled to morphology [80]. When the mutual information between local morphology and a gene’s expression is low, no model architecture can recover it [80]. These observations suggest that future mapping work should explicitly stratify performance by gene category rather than aggregated metrics, flag low-confidence targets such as PD-L1 with calibrated per-gene uncertainty [66, 83], and couple histology with auxiliary modalities (e.g. IHCand scRNA-seq references) for gene classes where morphology alone is insufficient.

Mapping methods successfully address the problem of lacking paired modalities to infer molecular profiles from H&E images, which is promising for future clinical deployments for cost deduction. However, challenges in generalization capabilities across batches, techniques, and organs still limit inference performance.

Foundation models

Compared with paired integration and cross-model mapping models trained on cohort-specific datasets, foundation models aim to learn generalizable representations for diverse downstream tasks (e.g. [84–90]). It is worth emphasizing that foundation models can function as an enabling layer whose pretrained representations are readily plugged into integration and mapping frameworks to reciprocally strengthen both paradigms. Recent advances have focused on ST, where data volume and consistency are more attainable and have evolved from spot-level toward cellular resolution.

Because ST technologies jointly produce H&E images and sequencing readouts, they naturally lend themselves to the development of foundation models at the spot level. ST-Align [84] is an early image-gene foundation model, which designs a spot-level and niche-level learning strategy within spatial context. OmiCLIP [85] leverages CLIP-style architecture [58] with dual image and text encoders, jointly trained on 2.2 million image-transcriptomic pairs from 32 organs using a contrastive loss. Built on OmiCLIP, the Loki platform operationalizes the learned multimodal space for end-to-end ST analysis, including alignment, annotation, and retrieval. Conversely, SpaFoundation [86] demonstrates scalable image-only pretraining, using H&E images as a universal anchor for ST prediction without requiring paired genomic data during scaling. Together, ST-Align, OmiCLIP, and SpaFoundation outline a trajectory from multimodal contrastive alignment to scalable image-only pretraining.

A parallel thread of work aims to learn representations that approximate single-cell molecular profiles. The scGPT-spatial [87] extends scGPT [91], a single-cell multi-omics foundation model, to the spatial domain by continual pretraining on a curated ST dataset with 30 million cells or spots from 821 individual slides using Mixture of Experts decoder to capture modality-specific features. PAST [88] bridges morphology and transcriptomics at cellular resolution using a CLIP-style dual-encoder framework [58] trained on 20 million pairs across 15 cancer types, notably supporting patient-level risk stratification. Nicheformer [89] is a transformer-based ST foundation model pretrained on single-cell transcriptomics and ST datasets covering over 110 million human and mouse cells across 73 different tissues. Gene expression profiles and metadata are tokenized using a similar strategy to Geneformer [92] to predict masked tokens to yield unified cross-tissue and cross-species embeddings. Novae [90] is a self-supervised graph attention network that encodes local environments into spatial representations pretrained on 30 million cells across 18 tissues. It focuses on addressing three limitations: (i) methods’ reliance on predefined gene panels; (ii) sensitivity to batch effects; and (iii) dependence on external tools for batch-effect correction. Together, scGPT-spatial, PAST, Nicheformer, and Novae illustrate a complementary line of foundation models operating on expression profiles while encoding spatial neighborhood information.

Beyond ST, recent efforts have extended foundation models to SP. KRONOS [93] and Eva [94] are two examples. KRONOS is pretrained in a self-supervised manner on 47 million SP image patches covering 16 tissue types and 8 fluorescence-based imaging platforms. Built on a ViT backbone, it introduces a third marker-encoding vector to distinguish protein markers across patches. Eva [94] is also ViT-based but adopts a masked autoencoder with a hierarchical framework to capture inter-channel and spatial relationships in mIF images. It is pretrained on paired histopathology-SP data from over 4000 tissue regions, 64 million cells, and around 200 protein biomarkers. Both KRONOS and Eva support general-purpose downstream tasks such as image retrieval and patient stratification for protein-related spatial analysis. These advances in SP foundation models motivate the next extension toward SM.

MetaboFM [17] is built for SM on mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) data from around 4000 MSI datasets in the METASPACE [95] repository. MetaboFM employs pretrained ViT encoders to learn transferable embeddings of MSI tiles, providing a general representation space that can be reused across downstream tasks such as metadata prediction and interactive visual exploration.

Despite their promise, current spatial FMs inherit unresolved limitations from their parent paradigms, including single-cell FMs (scFMs) and PFMs, that warrant a critical assessment of whether large-scale pretraining genuinely improves biology, robustness, and interpretability beyond benchmark gains.

Biological understanding beyond benchmark performance. Recent zero-shot evaluations show that scFMs such as scGPT and Geneformer can be matched or surpassed by simple baselines (e.g. linear models) in clustering and perturbation prediction [96, 97], suggesting that current pretraining objectives capture co-expression statistics rather than mechanistic gene–gene relationships. Spatial FMs that inherit similar masked-token or contrastive objectives [85, 87, 89] are likely to share this limitation, which is consistent with the modest accuracy of H&E-to-ST predictions reported in the field (GHIST reports per-gene Pearson correlations typically <0.2 [65]). These results indicate that the actual biological information transferred remains limited regardless of model size. Future spatial FMs should therefore be evaluated against non-FM baselines and on biologically grounded tasks (e.g. recovery of pathologist-annotated domains, spatially variable-gene rediscovery, and marker-gene faithfulness) rather than per-gene correlation alone.

Robustness beyond pretraining at scale. PFMs used as backbones in many spatial mapping and FM methods [55, 56, 69] have been shown to retain medical-center, scanner, and staining signatures that are recoverable by linear probes and persist even after stain normalization [98, 99]. Because spatial datasets aggregate slides from a limited number of providers [60, 61], spatial FMs are likely to inherit and may amplify such confounders, with reported gains partly reflecting dataset memorization rather than transferable morphology-omics correspondence. Approaches that explicitly disentangle biological from technical signals, including robustness indices, site-preserved cross-validation, and conditioning on assay- or site-specific tokens, should become routine components of spatial FM evaluation.

Robustness beyond pretraining at scale. PFMs used as backbones in many spatial mapping and FM methods [55, 56, 69] have been shown to retain medical-center, scanner, and staining signatures that are recoverable by linear probes and persist even after stain normalization [98, 99]. Because spatial datasets aggregate slides from a limited number of providers [60, 61], spatial FMs are likely to inherit and may amplify such confounders, with reported gains partly reflecting dataset memorization rather than transferable morphology-omics correspondence. Approaches that explicitly disentangle biological from technical signals, including robustness indices, site-preserved cross-validation, and conditioning on assay- or site-specific tokens, should become routine components of spatial FM evaluation.

Interpretability beyond attention. A systematic audit of scFM interpretability has recently shown that layer-organized attention (i.e. protein–protein interaction like patterns in early layers and regulation-like patterns in late layers) provides no incremental value for regulatory-level prediction over trivial gene-level baselines, and that ablating “regulatory” attention heads does not degrade performance [100]. This indicates that attention captures co-expression rather than unique regulatory signals. Pairing spatial FM embeddings with explicit interpretability constraints, such as the attention-consistency metric TAVAC [66] or the probabilistic attribution framework introduced in STimage [83], offers a promising path toward biological interpretability, although this remains an open problem.

Taken together, these observations suggest that the principal value of current spatial FMs lies in providing reusable feature extractors that lower the data requirements of integration and mapping methods, rather than in enabling zero-shot biological discovery. Closing this gap will require benchmarking against non-FM baselines, the use of biologically grounded evaluation metrics, and interpretability tooling that explicitly rewards biological insight over benchmark performance.

In summary, we discuss the evolution trend of integration, mapping, and foundation models from coarse to cellular resolution and from ST to other SO modalities with the anchor of H&E images. Mapping address paired-modality constraints and deconvolution needs, while FMs leverage massive datasets to provide robust feature extractors that reciprocally benefit the integration and mapping paradigms. Throughout this evolution, the role of H&E images has fundamentally changed from spatial backgrounds providing coordinates to morphological input modalities as representations. Together, future directions of SO computational methods are maximizing dataset utility and designing architectures addressing current limitations to improve performance.

Downstream tasks and actionable modeling improvements

Downstream tasks for H&E-centered spatial analysis

Figure 4 summarizes the main downstream biological and clinical tasks enabled by SO. We categorize these tasks into five major classes: spatial architecture identification, functional spatial pattern recognition, molecular profile imputation, cell-cell network inference, and biomedical endpoint prediction.

Downstream tasks of computational methods. We categorize downstream tasks into five major classes, encompassing spatial architecture identification, functional spatial pattern recognition, molecular profile imputation, cell–cell network inference, and biomedical endpoint prediction.

Spatial Architecture Identification constitutes the foundational layer of downstream analysis to recover the structural organization of tissues. Typical tasks include cell deconvolution from spot-level data, spatial cell localization, region or layer annotation, tissue reconstruction, and denoising. These analyses establish the spatial scaffold for higher-level functional and molecular interpretations.

Spatial Pattern Recognition builds on reconstructed architecture to identify functionally coherent spatial domains or molecular patterns beyond structural organization, which includes identifying spatially differentially expressed genes, functional regions, and cellular niches. By linking molecular variation to spatial context, it reveals emergent tissue functions that are not observable from non-spatial data.

Molecular Profile Imputation infers missing or unmeasured molecular signals, such as gene or protein expression, from available modalities. This task addresses sparsity, noise, and cost constraints of SO technologies. H&E-to-X mapping methods serve as specialized imputation frameworks that leverage morphology to recover high-resolution molecular profiles.

Cell–Cell Network Inference becomes feasible as spatial resolution approaches the cellular or subcellular level. These analyses reconstruct cell–cell communication networks, molecular interaction pathways, and regulatory circuits in situ, often extending to spatiotemporal dynamics [34]. By embedding cellular interactions within their native spatial context, such models provide mechanistic insights into tissue development and disease progression.

Biomedical Endpoint Prediction represents the translational capability of SO analysis, connecting spatial molecular patterns to clinical outcomes. Anchored by H&E images as the clinical gold standard, these tasks highlight the growing potential of SO to inform decision-making in precision medicine.

Beyond methods, comprehensive platforms built on spatial foundation models support diverse downstream tasks. For example, the Loki platform (based on OmiCLIP) enables multimodal downstream analysis like annotations, cross-modality imputations, etc. KRONOS provides embeddings for classification, cross-level phenotyping, and biomedical endpoints, including biomarker discovery and patient stratification.

Common failures and actionable directions

While the downstream tasks demonstrate the vast potential of SO, their success is often contingent upon the quality and scale of the underlying data. While architectural innovations address inherent technical limitations like coarse resolution and batch effects, current frameworks face several failures. In this section, we categorize “Common Failures” with actionable directions as follows: (i) resolution-related failures, (ii) generalization-related failures, and (iii) interpretation- and evaluation-related failures.

Resolution-related failures arise from a fundamental mismatch between the measurement granularity of SO technologies and the cellular (or subcellular) resolution required by downstream biological questions. In practice, many datasets remain spot-level or only “pseudo” cell-level, capturing mixed signals that reduce downstream fidelity. SR for ST [31, 55, 69] leverages abundant scRNA-seq references and PFM embeddings to infer virtual single-cell expression from H&E images. However, non-transcriptomic SR remains limited despite H&E-to-SP feasibility shown by ROSIE [75], GigaTIME [77], HEX [78], and Eva [94], largely due to scarce paired multimodal datasets. Furthermore, predictive accuracy for current cellular-scale reconstructions remains modest (e.g. GHIST reports Pearson correlation coefficients <0.2 between predicted and measured gene expression [65]). These failures are driven by compounding technical and algorithmic factors. Suboptimal quality of H&E images resulting from cyclic staining and bleaching yields tissue distortion and ambiguous cell boundaries, making accurate cell segmentation particularly challenging for weak boundaries and complex tissue structures (e.g. axons or irregular cell morphologies). Since these inaccuracies are propagated and amplified during representation learning, especially at the cellular scale, developing robust segmentation frameworks for vague margins and irregular shapes is a critical architectural priority.

Generalization-related failures occur when methods cannot maintain reliable performance on unseen samples beyond the training distribution. We categorize them as the following levels:

Scale generalization (intra-slide and inter-slide): Standard H&E WSIs (25 mm 55 mm) significantly exceed the capture area of current SO techniques. Only selected FOVs have spatial profiles, leading to sampling bias and missing key biological regions. Therefore, models should be capable of extrapolating molecular profiles from restricted FOVs to WSIs. For instance, iSCALE [68] expands coverage from FOVs to the entire section but remains sensitive to initial FOV selection. Beyond intra-slide expansion, methods should also consider identifying inter-slide domains for common biological features and biomarkers across samples.

Current frameworks address these challenges through several strategies [32, 35, 43, 90]: (i) explicit noise modeling to decouple biological signals from technical artifacts [32, 35]; (ii) self-supervised alignment strategies, such as Novae’s [90] self-supervised graph attention network to align cells with similar biological features; (iii) conditioning the model on explicit metadata, including tissue origin, technology platform, and sequencing protocols [87, 89, 101]; (iv) scGPT-spatial [87] avoids explicitly encoding spatial coordinates to ensure cross-slide generalizability. Interpretation and evaluation-related failures involve black-box predictions with limited biological rationales and assessment under benchmarks that fail to reflect biological reality. Clinical deployment requires models to provide outputs with supported mechanistic or histological explanations. SPiRiT [66] integrates the TAVAC metric to guide attention toward informative regions and report the areas and attention details used for marker gene prediction. Robust clinical adoption further requires rigorous multi-site validation following standardized guidelines for analytical reporting [11]. Current studies often use restricted datasets with inconsistent experimental settings, and the chosen metrics poorly match the biological tasks, leading to potentially inflated performance estimates. These call for unified, large-scale benchmarks spanning diverse cohorts and platforms, with biologically informed evaluation metrics and standardized reporting practices, to ensure methodological advances translate into actionable insights. In summary, these limitations reflect practical constraints, including insufficient (and weakly paired) data, restrictive modeling assumptions, and evaluation protocols misaligned with underlying biological questions, rather than the feasibility of H&E-centered modeling. These gaps motivate actionable directions in method development while distinguishing correctable gaps from intrinsic limitations discussed next.

Discussion and conclusion

We have discussed the computational trajectory of SO, evolving from multimodal integration to cross-modal mapping and foundation models, coinciding with a shift from mechanistic exploration toward clinically oriented endpoints. Central to this evolution is the utilization of H&E images as a morphological anchor to unify heterogeneous modalities and push toward cellular resolution. While architectural innovations have addressed many computational hurdles, several fundamental bottlenecks rooted in biological complexity and data acquisition constraints remain unresolved, as outlined below:

Representation gap. A fundamental bottleneck is the “representation gap” between measured data and biological reality. Most methods implicitly assume that selected FOVs are representative, reflecting underlying pathological and physiological states. However, typical tissue architectures substantially exceed the sampled regions and resolution of H&E and SO measurements, capturing only a fraction of morphological heterogeneity. Thus, key expression patterns or rare biomarkers may remain undetected due to sparse sampling and resolution limits [68]. Addressing this gap requires multi-region, multi-scale modeling, and strategic region-of-interest selection to improve coverage of critical biological signals [104].

Dimensionality gap. Most existing data remain 2D snapshots of 3D biological systems. Sample preparation constraints limit measurements along orthogonal axes, where the distance between adjacent sections often exceeds single-cell dimensions. Together with only selected FOVs being profiled, these limitations complicate the reconstruction of coherent 3D tissue volumes, potentially obscuring long-range cellular interactions and architectural continuity [105]. Bridging this gap requires methodological advances to align adjacent sections and interpolate “vertical” biological information. Emerging frameworks such as STAIG [106] and VORTEX [105] have begun to address these challenges. As sequencing costs decrease and alignment algorithms improve, this field may move toward 3D and even 4D longitudinal (space and time) representations of dynamic processes like tumor evolution in native volumetric context.

Technology gap. Capturing multiple modalities from the same tissue section remains difficult. Despite some attempts for cross-section integration, a “gold standard” for perfectly multimodal alignment is lacking. Furthermore, H&E image quality remains an often overlooked challenge. H&E images from SO experiments are frequently suboptimal due to chemical damage incurred during cyclic staining and bleaching, resulting in lower quality than diagnostic histology. Such degradation poses a significant hurdle for computational architectures and clinical deployment. Furthermore, SP technologies like mIF imaging are limited by the number of antibody markers, restricting protein coverage relative to the transcriptome. Imaging-based ST methods likewise profile far fewer genes than whole-transcriptome sequencing-based approaches.

Modality scope and emerging frontiers. This survey is anchored on H&E images as a unifying morphological backbone, and we therefore concentrate on ST and SP, for which paired H&E data are most abundant and the methodological trajectory across integration, mapping, and foundation models is most clearly established. Representative SM studies that leverage H&E or jointly model SM with H&E-paired modalities are nonetheless included [17, 39, 41]. Several other recent SM methods are conceptually parallel to the mapping paradigms we discuss but use light microscopy or IMC rather than H&E images and are therefore outside our scope. For instance, MetaLens [107] predicts super-resolved metabolite distributions from light microscopy, and GSR [108] super-resolves single-cell SM from multimodal MSI guided by IMC. SE is in an even earlier stage with respect to H&E integration. While dedicated H&E-to-SE methods remain rare, several integration frameworks reviewed in this paper [33–35, 42] natively support ST+ATAC alongside their primary applications, indicating that the integration paradigm has begun to extend toward chromatin accessibility. As SM and SE datasets continue to grow, we anticipate that the integration–mapping–foundation model trajectory documented here will be paralleled in these emerging modalities, ultimately moving the field toward genuinely pan-modality analysis.

In summary, this paper provides a comprehensive and structured overview of the computational evolution in SO anchored by histopathology. By delineating current architectural advancements from systemic bottlenecks, we provide a framework to distinguish what is currently achievable through model design from what remains constrained by technological limits. This paper serves as a roadmap of SO computational methods for both computational biologists developing models and translational researchers seeking to deploy these methods in clinical settings.
