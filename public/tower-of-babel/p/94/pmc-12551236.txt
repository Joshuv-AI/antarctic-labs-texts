Echoes from Macondo: managing disseminated ascariasis with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation therapy. A case report
Authors: Andres Felipe Herazo-Cubillos, Daniela Umaña-Gómez, Juan Sebastian Montoya-Beltran, Laura Ramirez-Herrrera, Andres Duque-Estevez, Carlos Miguel Santacruz-Escudero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ascaris lumbricoides is one of the most prevalent helminthic infections worldwide, yet disseminated disease is rare. In resource-limited settings, delayed diagnosis can lead to severe complications, including multiorgan dysfunction and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). There are only sporadic case reports describing the management of this entity with mechanical circulatory support such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). We report the case of a previously healthy 2-year-old girl from rural Colombia with a one-month history of abdominal pain and diarrhea. Stool examination confirmed A. lumbricoides infection, and abdominal ultrasound revealed intestinal pseudo-obstruction. Initial management included anti-parasitic therapy and surgery. Patient’s condition deteriorated to refractory hypoxemia, biventricular dysfunction, and two cardiac arrests. Peripheral veno-arterial ECMO was initiated by a mobile team in the referring hospital, followed by air transfer to a tertiary center. During the early days of ECMO support, the patient developed complications related to the underlying disease, including anastomotic dehiscence with intraluminal roundworms, bloodstream infection, and cerebral septic emboli, which required repeat surgery and escalation of antimicrobial therapy. After 10 days of ECMO, biventricular and pulmonary recovery allowed decannulation. The patient was discharged one month later in good physical condition, without neurological deficits, and with a pulmonary rehabilitation plan. This case illustrates how extreme socioeconomic disparities can lead to the coexistence of highly preventable parasitic diseases with advanced life-support interventions such as ECMO. Much like the magical realism of One Hundred Years of Solitude, the use of such technology in an isolated, resource-limited setting may seem improbable, yet it reflects a pressing reality. Introduction

In contemporary medicine, persistent disparities in health outcomes, often influenced by socioeconomic and geographical factors, highlight the divergent impact of preventable diseases in urban centers versus more challenging environments. This evident gap underscores the importance of thoroughly understanding healthcare inequalities and implementing targeted efforts to address accessibility issues and improve outcomes.

We present a case report detailing an uncommon presentation of disseminated ascariasis. Despite the generally manageable nature of Ascaris lumbricoides infections [1], the patient’s progression to multiorgan dysfunction and severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) requiring veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) due to refractory hypoxemia, biventricular dysfunction, cardiac arrest, and multiorgan failure [2], followed by a full recovery.

This case illustrates the successful use of advanced technologies such as ECMO in impoverished and inaccessible populations, paralleling Melquiades’ impact on the people of Macondo in Gabriel García Márquez’s “One Hundred Years of Solitude.”

Case presentation

A 2-year-old female, weighing 13 Kg, height 98 cm, with no relevant medical history, presenting with abdominal pain and multiple episodes of diarrhea over one month, was admitted in mid-August of 2023 to a hospital in Monteria, Colombia, from a rural area in the Apartadó region.

Upon admission, macroscopic adult worms were passed in the stool, and microscopic examination revealed characteristic eggs, confirming an Ascaris lumbricoides infection. Additionally, parasitic intestinal pseudo-obstruction was diagnosed with abdominal ultrasound. Treatment was initiated with nasogastric drainage and piperazine, along with piperacillin/tazobactam. The patient’s condition progressively worsened, leading to transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU). Further management included exploratory laparotomy, peritoneal lavage, and intestinal resection. Pulmonary function declined, requiring mechanical ventilation; the patient developed refractory hypoxemia (PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 32) and bilateral alveolar infiltrates. High-dose vasopressors were started (norepinephrine 0.3 mcg/kg/min and vasopressin 0.04 UI/kg/min). Ten days after admission, she experienced cardiac arrest, requiring three minutes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed severe right ventricular dysfunction, prompting treatment with milrinone at 0,75 mcg/kg/min. A second cardiac arrest, requiring four minutes of CPR, occurred two days later.

Due to severe hemodynamic and respiratory compromise, referral for ECMO therapy was considered. Our institution’s mobile extracorporeal life support team was dispatched to Monteria (eleven days after admission) (Fig. 1). Peripheral cervical VA-ECMO cannulation as a bridge to optimal treatment and recovery was initiated through a 14 Fr drainage cannula in the internal jugular vein and a 14 Fr return cannula in the right carotid artery (Fig. 2A). Therapy began with a fresh gas flow of 1 L per minute (LPM), 4100 revolutions per minute (RPM), and an ECMO flow of 1.4 LPM. Arterial blood gases on ECMO showed a pH of 7.31, PaO2 of 302 mmHg, PaCO2 of 49 mmHg, saturation of 99% at 100% FiO2, and lactate of 1,36 mmol/L.

Aerial referral to our center was initiated with the patient under propofol, pancuronium, and ventilator settings set at tidal volume (TV) 4 mL/kg, respiratory rate (RR) 20, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) 9 cmH2O, and FiO2 30%, with peak inspiratory pressure of 23 cm H2O, plateau pressure of 14 cm H2O, and a driving pressure of 5 cm H2O. The patient remained stable throughout the one-hour (700 km) flight, with no issues encountered.

Once stable on ECMO, vasopressor weaning was achieved while inotropes were continued. Heparin was started at a dose of 7–14 UI/Kg/h, guided by an aPTT 1,5 times the control, and deep sedation with neuromuscular blockade was maintained. TTE showed recovery of biventricular function (left ventricular ejection fraction, LVEF 65% and Tricuspid Annular Plane Systolic Excursion, TAPSE 19 mm) with no pulmonary hypertension. In contrast, the chest X-ray showed little to no improvement. Leukocytosis, eosinophilia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia were present, requiring multiple transfusions of red blood cells and platelets. Within the first two days of the ECMO run, another exploratory surgery was needed due to anastomotic dehiscence with roundworms in the small intestine and gallbladder; subatmospheric vacuum closure therapy was initiated for wound management.

Follow-up blood cultures grew yeast and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and brain CT images indicated cerebral septic emboli, requiring an adjustment of broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy with albendazole, meropenem, linezolid, and caspofungin.

On the tenth day of ECMO therapy, the patient showed significant progress. Arterial blood gases showed pH 7.44, PaO2 342,6 mmHg, PaCO2 42,6 mmHg, saturation 99% at 50% FiO2, and lactate 1,99 mmol/L. Furthermore, there was a decrease in acute phase reactants, improved renal function, and radiological signs of improvement with adequate native lung function, leading to successful ECMO weaning and decannulation.

During post-ECMO hospitalization, extubation was achieved on the twentieth day of stay. Targeted antibiotic therapy continued, and comprehensive rehabilitation was provided, leading to a hospital discharge a month after decannulation in acceptable physical condition, with no neurological deficits, and a pulmonary rehabilitation plan (Fig. 2B).

The patient and their family returned to their original region. However, despite several attempts to maintain contact, follow-up could not be established, again falling outside the scope of the healthcare providers.

Discussion

Gabriel Garcia Marquez’s “One Hundred Years of Solitude” recounts the story of a fictional, impoverished, isolated, and rural community near the Colombian coastline. This representation of Latin American culture continues to resonate with the daily realities of many developing populations, drawing clear parallels.

Healthcare in such populations is not immune to this dynamic. Despite advances in modern technology and pharmaceuticals, the practical approach in developing countries and regions can be limited by local knowledge and resources. This hampers proper treatment and often results in difficulties in diagnosing, treating, or preventing conditions like ascariasis caused by A. lumbricoides, one of the most common helminthic infections worldwide.

However, significant disparities can exist within the same country and healthcare system, where cutting-edge technology and highly preventable diseases coexist. The convergence of these two distinct contexts can create opportunities to apply advanced technology to address and prevent the latter, especially in scenarios like the one presented in this report.

Loeffler Syndrome or ARDS caused by nematodes, such as A. lumbricoides, is not a novel characterization for this disease, nor is the management of pediatric ARDS with ECMO support. However, the management of parasitic infections with ECMO is not well-described, and there are several sporadic case reports where non-helminthic acute eosinophilic pneumonia was treated using ECMO [3–6].

ECMO transportation and aerial referral of patients to specialized centers are life-saving interventions. In this case, it culminated in a successful outcome. Like Melchiades’ teachings of alchemy and technology to the people of Macondo, these interventions may seem foreign or even “magical” to many otherwise isolated communities. However, it is far from ideal. ECMO therapy can be costly, especially for resource-limited systems, particularly when the cost of treatment for nematode infections can be measured in US cents per dose [7].

In this unique case presentation, successful VA-ECMO support was bridged for optimal treatment and resolution of disseminated ascariasis. Although V-V ECMO is the standard approach for isolated respiratory failure, in this case, the patient had biventricular dysfunction and experienced cardiac arrest, prompting the use of V-A ECMO. Earlier VV-ECMO may not have been feasible given the rapid hemodynamic deterioration. Nevertheless, due to the rarity of this syndrome, only case reports have been published recently, limiting the ability to adequately characterize the population with disseminated ascariasis and the use of ECMO therapy in these scenarios. This limitation may stem from the significant morbidity and mortality associated with helminthic diseases in developing countries like Colombia, juxtaposed with the availability of highly efficient ECMO rescue, transport, and support. The existing disparity in disease prevention and therapy access creates a knowledge gap that provides further opportunities for reporting and research.

Conclusion

This case exemplifies how extreme socioeconomic disparities can lead to the collision of preventable diseases with highly complex medical interventions. Much like the magical realism of Macondo, the application of ECMO in this setting may seem improbable—but it reflects a pressing and tangible reality. Bridging these contrasts requires not only technological capacity but also public health action and continued scientific reporting to narrow the gaps in knowledge, access, and outcomes.
