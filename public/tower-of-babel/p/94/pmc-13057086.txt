Physicochemical and microbial changes in Raw beef meat induced by radiation
Authors: Ui-Bin Beak, Hack-Youn Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the effects of various electron beam irradiation doses on microbial inactivation and physicochemical properties of raw beef. The main research question was to determine the dose required to effectively eliminate pathogenic microorganisms without substantially compromising meat quality. Raw beef samples were treated with electron beam doses of 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 9 kGy. Microbial inactivation was assessed by enumerating colonies of Salmonella Typhimurium, Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus cereus, and Escherichia coli. Based on the inactivation results, physicochemical properties—including proximate composition, water holding capacity, shear force, pH, color, thiobarbituric acid reactive substances, and volatile basic nitrogen—were evaluated after treatment with 0, 1, 2, and 3 kGy. Bacillus cereus was reduced below detection at 7 kGy, while other foodborne pathogens were eliminated at 3 kGy. No significant changes in proximate composition, pH, and shear force occurred compared to controls (p < 0.05). Water-holding capacity decreased significantly as the irradiation dose increased (p < 0.05), and redness increased in a dose-dependent manner. Although thiobarbituric acid reactive substances and volatile basic nitrogen tended to increase with increasing dose and storage time, these changes were not statistically significant. Electron beam irradiation at 3 kGy effectively inactivatied non-spore-forming foodborne pathogens while maintaining acceptable quality in raw beef. This dose can enhance microbial safety with minimal quality loss.

Introduction

Electron beams, gamma rays, and X-rays are authorized for food sterilization, with electron beams and gamma rays most commonly used. Radiation mutates microbial DNA, inhibiting cell division or destroying cellular components, effectively killing spores of bacteria like Bacillus and Clostridium [1]. However, irradiation can cause chemical and sensory changes, leading to consumer concerns. Consequently, regulations set irradiation limits and require labeling of treated foods.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration permits maximum absorbed dose of 50 kGy for gamma rays, electron beams, and X-rays in pet food sterilization. The U.S., EU, Germany, Japan, and China mandate labeling irradiated foods with the radura symbol or an irradiation statement [2–3]. In korea, sterilization standards allow gamma radiation or electron radiation up to 10 kGy, with mandatory labeling [4]. Despite strong domestic interest in food safety, radiation-based sterilization remains limited due to negative consumer perceptions [5]. Therefore, scientific evidence supporting the safety of radiation sterilization of pet food is essential to ensure product safety and to diversify consumer options.

Foodborne pathogenic microorganisms such as Salmonella, Listeria, and Escherichia coli, which can be detected in uncooked meat, act as vectors of zoonotic diseases and pose health risks to both companion animals and humans. In particular, cross-contamination during the handling of raw pet food is a major factor that increases the risk of infection among pet owners and immunocompromised individuals [6]. Therefore, it is essential to establish the minimum effective radiation dose capable of inactivating pathogenic microorganisms while minimizing quality deterioration in raw pet food.

Consequently, this study aimed to scientifically determine applicable radiation doses for raw beef, a major protein source in pet food, within the domestic permissible irradiation range. Microbial inactivation before and after irradiation was evaluated at doses of 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 9 kGy. To assess potential quality changes, physicochemical characteristics were analyzed before and after irradiation at relatively low doses of 0, 1, 2, and 3 kGy. Through this experimental design, the present study sought to propose a minimum effective radiation dose applicable to raw meat for companion animal feed.

Materials and methods

Samples

The study used raw beef bottom round (Daonchugsan, Yesan, Korea) refrigerated at 4 °C for 24 h after slaughter. Excess fat and connective tissue were trimmed, and samples were then stored at 4℃ for 0, 1, and 2 weeks as experimental storage times.

Preparing microbial strains

To analyze the killing effect of raw beef and its recovery before and after electron beam sterilization, major harmful microorganisms were inoculated into raw beef. The inoculated strains included Salmonella Typhimurium (ATCC 43971, 19525, and DT104), Listeria monocytogenes (ATCC 15313, 19111, and 19115), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 13565 (SEA), 14458 (SEB), 19095 (SEC), 23235 (SED), and FRI 913 (SEE)), Bacillus cereus (ATCC 14579 and 10876), and Escherichia coli (ATCC 13715, 35150, 43889, and 43890). Microorganisms were cultured on tryptic soy agar (236950, BD Difco, Detroit, MI, USA) at 37 °C for 24 h, transferred to tryptic soy broth (211825, BD Difco) for another 24 h incubation, centrifuged at 4,000×g and 4 °C for 15 min to form pellets, washed, and diluted in buffered peptone water (BPW; 218105, BD Difco). Then, 100 µL of inoculum was added to 25 g of raw pet food in vacuum bags, mixed, vacuum-packed, and used for electron irradiation.

Electron irradiation

For electron beam irradiation of raw beef, a linear accelerator (EB-21-HEA10-01, EBtech, Daejeon, Korea) of EBtech was used. The temperature of the sample during electron beam irradiation was − 2℃, and the temperature of the treatment room was 20℃ (room temperature). The dose conditions were 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 9 kGy for the analysis of microbial death and recovery characteristics, and 1, 2, and 3 kGy for 10 min each for the analysis of physicochemical characteristics. The irradiation conditions were energy 10 MeV, beam current 436 mA, and conveyor velocity 4.7 m/min.

Microbial colony count

The microbial killing effect of irradiated raw beef was determined by mixing samples stored for 0, 1, and 2 weeks with 0.1% BPW (BD Difco) in a ratio of 1:10 (25 g: 225 mL; 0, 1 kGy) and 1:1 (25 g: 25 mL; 2, 3, 5 kGy) in a sample bag and stomaching for 1 min with a stomacher (WH4000-2751, 3 M Korea, Seoul, Korea). Then 1 mL of the stomaching was repeatedly diluted with 9 mL of 0.1% BPW to the required multiples. Microbial counts were determined using selective media: MC-Media Pads for Salmonella (SK06B25, JNC Corp, Tokyo, Japan), 3 M™ petrifilm for Listeria (6447, Neogen 3 M, Saint Paul, MN, USA), Microfast™ Staphylococcus aureus count plate (PKI-LR1005, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) for Staphylococcus, Microfast™ coliform & E.coli count plate (PKI-LR1007, PerkinElmer) for E.coli, Microfast™ Bacillus Cereus Count Plate (PKI- LR1010, PerkinElmer) for Bacillus. After dispensing 1 mL of the dilutions onto film media, Salmonella, Listeria, E. coli, and Staphylococcus were incubated at 37℃ for 24 h, and Bacillus was incubated at 30℃ for 24 h. Each microbial colony was then counted and expressed as a log colony-forming unit (log CFU/g) value.

Proximate composition

The proximate composition of the electron beam-treated raw beef was determined according to the AOAC method [7]. Moisture content was determined by the normal pressure drying method (AOAC 950.46) in a 105℃ oven, crude protein content was determined by the Kjeldahl method (AOAC 992.15), crude fat content was determined by the Soxhlet method (AOAC 960.39), and ash content was determined by the direct method (AOAC 920.153) in a 550 °C electrofiring furnace, and all contents are expressed as percentages.

The WHC of the electron beam-treated raw pet food was measured by the compression method. 0.3 g of the sample was weighed from the center of each sample, placed on the center of filter paper (Whatman paper No. 1, Whatman, UK), and compressed for 3 min at a constant pressure using a plexiglass plate device. Shear force

The shear force of the electron beam-treated raw pet food was used in the experiment by cutting the central part of each sample into 1 × 1 × 2 cm sizes. After setting the measurement conditions (test speed, 3.0 mm/s; head speed, 3.0 mm/s; distance, 19.0 mm; force, 5.6 N) of a texture analyzer equipped with a V-blade (TA 1, Lloyd Instruments, Largo, FL, USA), the shear force was measured perpendicularly to the direction of the sample muscle fibers and expressed in kgf.

pH

The pH of the electron beam-treated raw pet food was determined using a glass electrode pH meter (Model S220, Mettler-Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland) calibrated using pH 4.01, pH 7.0, and pH 10.0 buffer solutions (Suntex Instruments Co., Ltd, Taipei, Taiwan). Samples stored for 0, 1, and 2 weeks were aliquoted 3 g and homogenized with 12 mL of distilled water (DW) in an ultra-turrax (HMZ-20DN, Poonglim tech, Korea) at 10,000 rpm for 1 min. The homogenate was measured for pH with a glass electrode pH meter.

Color

The chromaticity of the electron beam-treated raw pet food was measured using a colorimeter (CR-10, Minolta, Japan), which was calibrated with a white standard plate having a CIE L* value of + 97.83, a CIE a* value of -0.43, and a CIE b* value of + 1.98. The colorimeter employed a pulsed xenon lamp as the light source and was equipped with an 8 mm measurement aperture, a 10° observation angle, and a D65 standard illuminant. The CIE L* value indicating lightness, the CIE a* value indicating redness, and the CIE b* value indicating yellowness were measured on the surface of the samples stored for 0, 1, and 2 weeks, respectively, using the colorimeter. Prior to measurement, the samples were allowed to bloom at room temperature for 30 min.

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS)

The lipid oxidation level of the electron beam-treated raw beef was measured by the TBARS method. Each sample (10 g), stored for 0, 1, and 2 weeks, was mixed with 25 mL of 10% perchloric acid and 0.2 mL of 0.3% butylated hydroxytoluene and homogenized at 10,000 rpm for 1 min using a homogenizer (AM-5, Nihonseiki Kaisha, Tokyo, Japan). The homogenate was mixed with 25 mL of DW, and the supernatant was filtered through filter paper (Whatman). The supernatant was mixed with 2-thiobarbituric acid in a 1:1 ratio and reacted in a water bath (JSWB-30T, JSR, Gongju, Korea) set at 100℃ for 10 min. The reactants were cooled for 10 min and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a multi-mode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The measured absorbance was multiplied by 5.2 and expressed as mg malondialdehyde/kg.

Volatile basic nitrogen (VBN)

The volatile basic nitrogen of the electron beam-treated raw pet food was measured by the Conway microdiffusion method. Each 10 g sample stored for 0, 1, and 2 weeks was homogenized with 30 mL of DW at 10,000 rpm for 1 min using a homogenizer (Nihonseiki Kaisha). The homogenate was then filtered through filter paper (Whatman). 1 mL of sample was placed in the outer chamber of the Conway vessel, and 100 µL of Conway reagent, which was prepared by dissolving 1 mL of 0.01 N H3BO3 and 0.066% methyl red solution and 0.066% broncresol green solution in 300 mL of ethanol in a 1:1 ratio, was added to the inner chamber and the lid was closed. After slightly opening the Conway vessel lid, 1 mL of K2CO3 was added, and the vessel was shaken on a flat surface to thoroughly mix the solutions. The Conway vessel was reacted in a dark room at 37℃ for 2 h. Statistical processing

The analysis was conducted at least three times and repeated. The results of the experiment were analyzed using the statistical processing program SAS (Windows version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and the mean and standard deviation were calculated. In addition, significance was verified at the p < 0.05 level using analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) and Duncan’s multiple range test.

Results and discussion

Microbial survival count and recovery characteristics

The number of viable bacteria significantly decreased with increasing electron beam dose in microbial treatments compared to the control. Among them, Salmonella Typhimurium, Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli were reduced below the detection level at doses ≥ 3 kGy. Gumus et al. reported that gamma irradiation up to 4.5 kGy reduced E. coli O157:H7, S. aureus, and S. Typhimurium in beef meatballs below detection during 7 days of storage at 4 °C, consistent with this study’s results [8]. Parallel treatment under refrigerated storage conditions and irradiation can effectively suppress foodborne pathogenic microorganisms [9]. Additionally, ionizing radiation generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), which may damage cellular components of foodborne pathogenic microorganisms, including DNA, and could partly explain the observed inhibition of microbial growth and proliferation [10]. In samples with a high moisture content, the production of ROS by radiation can increase, which may enhance the antimicrobial effectiveness against foodborne pathogens [11]. However, Bacillus cereus spores showed higher resistance, requiring ≥ 7 kGy for inactivation. The spores of B. cereus are composed of a cortex, an outer membrane, coats, and an exosporium, which have been reported to confer relatively high resistance to radiation, owing to ultraviolet-adsorbing pigments, specific photochemical reactions of DNA, and rapid spore recovery abilities [12]. Among the spore layer structures, the outer coat contains pigments that absorb ultraviolet rays, and the spore nucleus has a low water content and a high content of low-molecular-weight pyridine-2,6-dicarboxylic acid, and small acid-soluble proteins bind to DNA, which may contribute to the protection of spores from radiation-induced damage [13]. Additionally, the low moisture content of the microbial nucleus reduces the production of hydroxyl radicals by ionizing radiation, and the high content of divalent ions, such as Ca2+, Mg2+, and Mn2+, may be associated with increased resistance to oxidative stress, possibly explaining the higher radiation resistance of B. cereus spores compared to other microorganisms [14]. The number of viable microorganisms during the storage period was maintained at a similar level in the control for 0–2 weeks, whereas in the treatments, the number of viable microorganisms generally decreased as the storage period increased. Radiation treatment may affect the survival of microorganisms, possibly through DNA damage such as base modifications, strand breaks, and cross-linking [15]. In addition, the low storage temperature of 4 °C may have contributed to the further reduction of microorganisms during storage [16]. As a result, B. cereus was reduced below the detection level at doses of 7 kGy or higher, indicating higher microbial resistance than other microorganisms, whereas non-spore-forming bacteria were reduced below the detection level at doses of 3 kGy or higher in all treatments. Accordingly, the physicochemical composition of the control and treatments was compared and analyzed after treatment with 0, 1, 2, and 3 kGy (Fig. 1).

Proximate composition

There was no significant difference in the proximate composition of raw beef among electron beam treatments up to 3 kGy compared to the control. reported that gamma irradiation above 3 kGy caused no significant changes in the proximate composition of raw beef, consistent with this study’s findings [17]. This suggests that radiation doses below 10 kGy may affect the oxidation and reduction of meat, but do not affect total mass changes [18]. Since the Kjeldahl nitrogen method and Soxhlet extraction method analyze based on total mass regardless of chemical denaturation, no changes in general components are observed if there is no substantial loss of components [19]. Anaerobic conditions such as vacuum packaging and modified atmosphere packaging can limit moisture migration, suppress radiation-induced oxidation, and prevent the formation of secondary degradation products, thereby restricting lipid oxidation more effectively than aerobic conditions [20]. The high protein content in dietary meat can also inhibit free radical activity. Free radical ions (eaq-, H·, H+, OH·, and H2O2) may interact with amino acid side chains, leading to oxidation of amino acids and stabilization of reactive radicals [21]. As a result, these factors may partly explain why electron beam irradiation up to 3 kGy did not result in significant changes in the proximate composition of raw beef, as oxidative modifications induced by radiation are unlikely to cause substantial mass loss detectable by total-mass–based analytical methods, particularly under oxygen-limited packaging conditions (Table 1).

Water holding capacity (WHC) and shear forces

WHC was significantly decreased at 2 and 3 kGy dosed (p < 0.05). Rodrigues et al. treated of beef Longissimus lumborum with 3 kGy of gamma radiation, and the WHC decreased by 0.06% compared with the control, which is similar to the results of this study [22]. Radiolysis of water generates ROS, which are known to react with amino acids and peptide bonds, potentially leading to protein oxidation and reduced surface hydrophilicity, thereby possibly contributing to a decrease in WHC [23]. Electron beam irradiation has been reported to be associated with structural changes in myofibrillar proteins. Changes in protein secondary structure have been suggested to increase random coils and β-turns, which are amorphous structures, potentially resulting in partial unfolding and protein aggregation, which could partly explain the observed decrease in WHC [24]. The decrease in the WHC observed in irradiated meat may be associated with radiation-induced denaturation and oxidation of proteins, which can alter hydrogen and disulfide bonds and may reduce the activity of proteases and calpains. These structural changes have been suggested to limit the degradation of cytoskeletal proteins, thereby decreasing intermyofibrillar space and water retention. Additionally, irradiation has been reported to solubilize myofibrillar and connective tissue proteins and to disrupt costamere linkages, possibly involving cleavage of collagen peptide bonds through free radical formation [25–26]. As a result, the reduction in WHC at doses ≥ 2 kGy may be associated with oxidative and structural modifications of myofibrillar and connective tissue proteins induced by ROS generation and secondary structure alterations; however, the exact mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated (Table 1).

Shear force slightly decreased with increasing electron beam doses, though differences were not significant. treated beef with gamma radiation at 0–9 kGy and reported that the shear force decreased in the 3 and 9 kGy treatments compared with that in the control, which is similar to the results of this study [25]. Ionizing radiation generates ROS, which can abstract hydrogen atoms from protein molecules, leading to the formation of carbon-centered radicals (C·). These radicals can subsequently react with oxygen to form peroxyl radicals (COO·), which may further interact with Fe2+ or hydrophilic superoxide radicals, thereby initiating protein oxidation chain reactions [27]. Previous studies have reported that ROS can influence actomyosin depolymerization, as well as the degradation of contractile proteins such as actin and myosin, and structural proteins including titin and nebulin, which may partly explain the changes in shear force observed after irradiation [28]. Actomyosin is interactions have been reported to weaken under oxidative conditions through through non-covalent interaction between actin and myosin, and partial dissociation and depolymerization have been associated with oxidation-induced structural changes in sulfhydryl group of the myosin head and reduced stability of F-actin [29]. Oxidation and degradation of myosin heavy chain and protein rearrangement are thought to decrease the activity of Ca2+-ATPase, weaken protein contractility, and affect the reduction of shear force [30]. Accordingly, electron beam irradiation at doses ≤ 3 kGy did not significantly affect shear force in raw beef; however, a slight reduction observed may be associated with ROS-mediated oxidation and partial depolymerization of actomyosin, along with degradation and structural rearrangement of myofibrillar and connective tissue proteins, which could contribute to reduced protein contractility and muscle structural integrity (Table 1).

pH and color

The pH was not significantly different between the electron beam treatments and control at week 0, consistent with [17]. Electron irradiation 0–3 kGy did not affect the initial pH of raw meat. This suggests that radiation treatment, a non-thermal process, does not cause substantial changes in the H+ concentration in meat or acid-base alterations. The pH of meat is influenced by glycogen breakdown and lactic acid accumulation and is subsequently stabilized by the buffering capacity of proteins and phosphates [31]. Radiation may inhibits lactic acid synthesis through glycogen hydrolysis and may restricts the growth of pathogenic microorganisms, thereby maintaining pH stability [32]. ROS are reported to cause oxidative damage to glycolytic enzymes such as glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and lactate dehydrogenase, and the SH group of GAPDH has been suggested to be sensitive to radiation, potentially reducing enzyme activity through protein unfolding and S-S bond formation [33]. However, during 1–2 weeks of storage, higher doses increased pH, likely due to reduced microbial production of organic acids, such as lactic acid and acetic acid [34]. As storage time increased, pH decreased in all electron irradiation treatments, suggesting that this may be associated with protein degradation and increased free fatty acid formation [35]. As a result, electron beam irradiation (≤ 3 kGy) did not significantly affect the initial pH of beef, which may be explained by its non-thermal nature, the buffering capacity of muscle proteins and phosphates, and possible ROS-mediated inhibition of glycolytic enzymes such as GAPDH; however, dose- and time-dependent pH changes during storage were observed, with higher doses increasing pH by suppressing microbial organic acid production and prolonged storage decreasing pH potentially due to protein degradation and free fatty acid accumulation (Table 2).

The color of raw beef in the 3 kGy showed the lowest lightness and highest redness at week 0. treated oxymyoglobin and metmyoglobin extracted from beef with 0–60 kGy of gamma radiation, and reported that 4 kGy resulted in brighter and redder colors, which is similar to the results of this study [36]. Electron beam treatment may have contributed to reduced brightness and increased brownness by oxidizing myoglobin directly or converting it to metmyoglobin via free radicals [37]. Radiation-generated ROS have been reported to inhibit Ca2+-ATPase activity, which sould lead to calcium accumulation and impaired mitochondrial function, thereby potentially enhancing ROS production [38]. Ionizing radiation may disrupt Ca2+ homeostasis by impairing ATP-dependent ion handling, leading to cytosolic and mitochondrial Ca2+ overload. This Ca2+ accumulation could contribute to mitochondrial membrane potential collapse and electron leakage from the electron transport chain, thereby possibly amplifying mitochondrial ROS generation [39]. ROS may interact with myoglobin heme iron to oxidize Fe2+ to Fe3+ or generate a highly oxidized intermediate such as ferrylmyoglobin (Mb–Fe(IV) = O), which could interfere with the myoglobin reduction pathway and promote metmyoglobin accumulation [40]. Through Fenton reaction, H2O2, a type of ROS, reacts with Fe2+ to produce Fe3+, OH·, and OH−, and this reaction may contribute to continued metmyoglobin formation and protein oxidation [41]. Ferrylmyoglobin is an unstable, highly oxidized heme iron species that has been suggested to catalyzes oxidation reaction via pseudo-peroxidase activity [42]. As a result, it is suggested that electron beam treatment did not markedly alter the initial color of beef but may have influenced changes in lightness and redness during storage through dose-dependent oxidative processes. These effects are attributable to radiation-induced ROS generation and high-energy electron interactions that could promote myoglobin heme iron oxidation (Fe2+ → Fe3+), possible ferrylmyoglobin formation, potential function, and activation of Fenton reactons, thereby contributing to metmyoglobin accumulation (Table 2).

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS) and volatile basic nitrogen (VBN)

There was no significant difference in TBARS between the control and electron beam treatments at week 0, consistent with likely due to vacuum packaging limited oxygen availability and restricted oxygen migration, thereby reducing ROS formation [17, 43]. However, TBARS values gradually increased with higher doses during weeks 1–2, with 3 kGy showing the highest oxidation. TBARS also increased over storage time, similar to findings by [44]. It is suggested that these results may be related to restricted oxygen permeation and limited ROS activity under vacuum packaging and refrigerated storage conditions during the initial storage period (0–1 weeks) [45]. In addition, the primary radicals generated by water radiolysis have a short lifespan and high reactivity, therefore, they are quickly eliminated or recombined immediately after irradiation and may not exert a significant effect on lipid oxidation [46]. However, despite this initial inhibitory tendency, ionizing radiation may induce physicochemical modifications in lipids through radiolytic processes that persist and gradually accumulate during storage, particularly at higher doses [43]. Lipid modification by ionizing energy has been reported to resemble oxidation, polymerization, and decarbonization reactions caused by heat or oxidation stress, in which various compounds are generated and accumulated. Secondary lipid oxides produced by radiation and microorganisms, including aldehydes such as acetaldehyde, propanal, butanal, pentanal, hexanal, heptanal, and nonanal, may have contributed to the observed increase increase in TBARS values [47]. As a result, TBARS values at weeks 0–2 showed a slight dose-dependent increase following irradiation; however, this effect appears to have been partially limited by vacuum packaging due to restricted oxygen availability. During refrigerated storage (weeks 2), TBARS values gradually increased with storage time and irradiation dose, which may be associated with the accumulation of radiation-induced lipid modifications and microbial metabolites. Nevertheless, these changes remained limited, indicating that irradiation did not result in a marked enhancement of lipid oxidation in beef during storage (Table 3).

VBN increased progressively in all treatments with increasing dose and storage period compared with the control. The increase in VBN with higher doses may be attributed to radiation-induced protein breakdown into smaller nitrogen-containing volatile compounds through deamination, decarboxylation, desulfation, and peptide bond cleavage, both directly and indirectly via radicals generated by water radiolysis [48]. Radiation-induced degradation of proteins and amino acids is generally described as involving direct and indirect effects, and meat with high moisture content may exhibit a greater contribution from indirect effects due to ROS. Direct effects may occur when proteins absorb radiation energy, causing modification of disulfide bonds, carboxyl groups, active sites, and amino acid residues. Indirect effects may occur when ROS, especially highly reactive OH· radicals, induce peptide bond cleavage, amino acid side chain oxidation, and protein structural denaturation [49]. The increase in VBN during storage may be associated with ongoing protein breakdown producing ammonia and volatile bases. Mitochondrial damage and amino acid degradation generate superoxide radicals (O2−), although some volatile compounds decrease over time, others such as dimethyl sulfide and 2,3-dimethyl disulfide may accumulate, potentially contributing to higher VBN values [50–51]. As a result, VBN values did not show significant differences among irradiation doses immediately after treatment and up to 1 week of storage; however, from week 2 onward, VBN increased significantly with increasing irradiation dose. This delayed response may suggests that radiation-induced protein modifications and ROS-mediated indirect effects did not immediately manifest as volatile nitrogen compounds but may have accumulated during storage, possibly leading to enhanced protein degradation and increased formation of ammonia and other nitrogen-containing volatiles at later stages (Table 3).

Conclusion

As companion animals are increasingly regarded as family members, the safety of raw pet food has become a critical concern. Electron beam irradiation was applied to raw beef to evaluate microbial reduction and physicochemical changes. Non-spore-forming bacteria (Salmonella Typhimurium, Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli) were reduced below the detection limit (< 1 log CFU/g) at doses ≥ 3 kGy, whereas spore-forming Bacillus cereus exhibited greater resistance. During refrigerated storage, most microorganisms remained undetectable, demonstrating strong antimicrobial efficacy. No significant differences were observed in proximate composition, pH, shear force, TBARS, or VBN at week 0. However, increasing irradiation dose significantly reduced water-holding capacity and increased redness, which may associated with radiation-induced oxidative modifications of muscle proteins and myoglobin. Overall, electron beam irradiation at 3 kGy effectively ensures microbial safety of raw beef while inducing only limited and acceptable physicochemical changes, supporting its applicability as a non-thermal sterilization strategy for raw pet food.
