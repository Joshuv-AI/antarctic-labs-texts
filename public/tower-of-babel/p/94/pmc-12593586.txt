Effects of microwave vacuum drying on drying characteristics, bioactive components, antioxidant capacity, and starch structure of germinated red adzuki beans
Authors: Weikang Guo, Mingyue Wang, Liuyang Shen, Chenghai Liu, Chai Liu, Xianzhe Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of microwave intensity (8, 10, and 12 W/g), vacuum pressure (0.05, 0.065, and 0.08 MPa), and drying temperature (50, 60, and 70 °C) on the drying characteristics, bioactive components, antioxidant capacity, and starch structure of germinated red adzuki beans (GRAB) under microwave vacuum drying (MVD). Drying temperature was the critical factor affecting drying kinetics, color, and γ-aminobutyric acid content. The retention of total phenolic and flavonoid content was governed by microwave-induced structural disruption and thermal degradation. MVD increased gallic acid, quercetin, and chlorogenic acid, but reduced rutin and epicatechin levels. MVD also enhanced antioxidant capacity of GRAB, reduced its gelatinization enthalpy, induced microstructural changes, disrupted the double-helical structure, lowered the relative crystallinity, and cleaved the α-(1,6) glycosidic bonds. Overall, MVD can be considered an effective method for preserving bioactive components and modifying the starch properties to improve the nutritional and functional quality of GRAB.Keywords: Microwave vacuum drying, Germinated red adzuki beans, Bioactive compounds, Antioxidant capacity, Starch structure, Physicochemical properties

Introduction

Germinated red adzuki beans (GRAB), the sprouted form of red adzuki beans (Vigna angularis), represent a nutrient-dense legume renowned for its high-quality plant-based protein and substantial starch content (Wang et al., 2017). The production of GRAB involves activating endogenous enzymatic systems during seed germination, a process that enhances the concentration of bioactive compounds such as γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), total phenolic content (TPC), and flavonoid content (TFC) (Yu et al., 2024). These bioactive components exhibit remarkable potential in mitigating chronic diseases, particularly cardiovascular disorders, diabetes mellitus, and obesity (Huang et al., 2014), thereby establishing GRAB as a valuable functional food for health promotion. However, fresh GRAB is highly susceptible to deterioration due to its high moisture content exceeding 60% (w.b.), which accelerates spoilage, compromises nutritional integrity, and shortens shelf life. Consequently, drying treatment is imperative to ensure long-term storage stability and preserve the quality of GRAB, making it a viable candidate for sustainable food preservation and utilization (Yuan et al., 2022).

Conventional drying techniques, including solar drying, hot-air drying, fluidized-bed drying, and vacuum freeze-drying (Topal & Şahi̇n, 2025), are capable of reducing the moisture content of granular legumes such as GRAB; however, these techniques have inherent trade-offs in terms of processing efficiency, product quality and operational costs. Notably, while vacuum freeze-drying is highly effective at preserving nutritional integrity, its industrial viability is limited by high energy demands and long processing times (Wu et al., 2023). Recently, emerging physical field-based drying technologies, such as microwave, radio frequency, and infrared drying, have attracted increasing attention in food processing due to their potential to overcome the aforementioned limitations (Skåra et al., 2023). Compared to conventional drying methods, microwave drying has gained recognition as a promising alternative due to its rapid and efficient dehydration capabilities (Zahoor et al., 2023). However, traditional microwave systems often suffer from non-uniform temperature distribution during the later drying stages, leading to localized overheating and subsequent product quality deterioration, such as surface hardening and scorching (Zhang et al., 2024). Microwave vacuum drying (MVD) is a hybrid technology that combines the penetrating heating mechanism of microwaves with the advantages of low temperatures and low pressures in vacuum environments (Zhu et al., 2024). This integration accelerates dehydration while simultaneously suppressing enzymatic activity and oxidative reactions. Consequently, MVD significantly enhances drying efficiency and preserves product quality, offering an innovative solution for high-quality drying of GRAB (González-Cavieres et al., 2021).

As an efficient drying technique, the critical parameters of the MVD process, including microwave intensity, drying temperature, and vacuum pressure level, have a significant impact on the retention of bioactive compounds (Değirmencioğlu et al., 2016). Bioactive compounds, including GABA, TPC, and TFC, are widely distributed in plants, fruits and vegetables, where they contribute to nutritional and functional properties (Silpa et al., 2023). The degradation of these bioactive components during bean drying has been investigated in numerous studies (An et al., 2022). Nevertheless, there are still significant research gaps regarding the impact of MVD conditions on GABA content, TPC, and TFC in GRAB. Furthermore, the selective heating characteristics of microwaves may induce structural modifications in the starch matrix of GRAB (Zhu et al., 2024). Starch, the primary constituent of GRAB, is a semi-crystalline, biodegradable carbohydrate composed of amylose and amylopectin (Zheng et al., 2024). Its structural features, including crystallinity, granule morphology, and molecular organization, fundamentally govern its physicochemical and functional properties, such as gelatinization behavior and digestibility. Current starch modification strategies primarily rely on chemical, enzymatic, or physical approaches (Ge et al., 2021). Among these, physical methods (e.g., heat-moisture treatment, microwave irradiation, ultrasound) are particularly advantageous due to their simplified processing requirements and reduced dependency on specialized equipment (Oyeyinka et al., 2021). Notably, microwave treatment has attracted substantial research interest as a physical modification technique, owing to its exceptional energy efficiency and pronounced volumetric heating effects (An et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024). Therefore, a comprehensive investigation into the effects of MVD parameters on drying kinetics, bioactive compound retention, and starch structural changes in GRAB is essential for enhancing product quality and nutritional value. This would provide critical insights for developing advanced drying technologies tailored to preserve both functional and sensory attributes of GRAB and related agricultural products.

To our knowledge, while MVD has been implemented across diverse food matrices, its specific effects on the drying behavior, bioactive constituents, antioxidant capacity, and starch structural properties of GRAB remain under-investigated. In contrast to conventional drying techniques, we hypothesized that MVD would enhance drying kinetics, preserve bioactive compounds more effectively and mitigate structural damage to starch in GRAB. To test this hypothesis, this study systematically explores the influence of key MVD parameters, including microwave intensity, vacuum pressure, and drying temperature, on the drying kinetics, bioactive component retention, and starch structural properties of GRAB using a temperature-controlled MVD system. Therefore, the specific research objectives are to investigate the drying characteristics of GRAB during MVD; to analyze the changes in its color, bioactive components and antioxidant capacity; and to elucidate the effects of MVD on the starch structure of GRAB using techniques including SEM, DSC, FT-IR, XRD, and 1H NMR. This study improves our understanding of the mechanistic effects of MVD on the quality attributes of GRAB, providing a theoretical basis for MVD of GRAB.

Materials and methods

Materials

Red adzuki beans were purchased from Heilongjiang Cang Natural Ecological Agriculture Co., Ltd. (Harbin, China). The γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) standard was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Rutin and gallic acid standards were supplied by Shifeng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). ABTS powder and DPPH solution were procured from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Reagents including anhydrous methanol, anhydrous ethanol, boric acid, sodium hypochlorite, phenol, Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, and aluminum nitrate were all purchased from Tianjin Zhiyuan Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). All chemicals used were of analytical grade.

Preparation of GRAB samples

The germination process of red adzuki beans was carried out based on the method described by Zhu et al. (2024) with slight modifications. Briefly, the screened red adzuki beans were soaked in clean water for 1.5 h, then evenly spread across germination trays and placed in a constant-temperature environment of 30 ± 0.5 °C. During the 32-h germination period, the beans were sprayed with water for 12 s every 2 h to maintain humidity. This ultimately produced GRAB, which has sprouted to a length of 3–5 mm. The raw GRAB had an initial moisture content of 60 ± 0.5% (w.b.).

MVD experiments of GRAB

Raw GRAB was subjected to MVD using a microwave vacuum dryer (RWBZ-8S, Surui Group, Nanjing, China), operating at 2450 ± 50 MHz with a maximum power output of 3 kW. The dryer was operated in MVD mode with temperature control, allowing the drying temperature to be set between 30 and 80 °C. The raw GRAB was dried using MVD until the moisture content reached the target level of 13%–15% (w.b.) for safe storage. Based on the preliminary experiments, the final MVD experiments of GRAB were conducted based on the following experimental protocol: 1) microwave intensity was set to 8, 10, and 12 W/g at a constant vacuum pressure of 0.065 MPa and drying temperature of 60 °C; 2) vacuum pressure was set to 0.05, 0.065, and 0.08 MPa at a fixed microwave intensity of 10 W/g and drying temperature of 60 °C; 3) drying temperature was set to 50, 60, and 70 °C at a constant microwave intensity of 10 W/g and vacuum pressure of 0.065 MPa.

The experimental flowchart is illustrated in Fig. 1. In brief, a measured quantity of GRAB was weighed and spread evenly on a microwave tray. The sample was then transferred to a drying chamber and subjected to MVD treatment at pre-set parameters. After drying, the samples were promptly collected and freeze-dried. The freeze-dried samples were then ground and sieved through an 80-mesh sieve. The samples were packaged in airtight bags and stored at 3 ± 1 °C for subsequent analysis of quality indicators. Three replications were used for all experiments, with data presented as the mean ± standard deviation.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC)

Determination of total flavonoid content (TFC)

The total flavonoid content (TFC) was determined using an aluminum chloride colorimetric method according to our previously published protocol (Guo et al., 2025), which was adapted from An et al. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a UV spectrophotometer (I9, Hainan Co., Ltd., Jinan, China), and the results were expressed as milligrams of rutin equivalent per 100 g of dry weight (mg RE/100 g).

Determination of phenolic compounds by HPLC

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was employed to determine the monomeric phenolic compounds in GRAB. The experimental procedure followed the method described by De Macêdo Oliveira et al. (2024) with minor modifications. Briefly, measurements were performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system equipped with a diode array detector (DAD) and a C18 column (4.6 × 100 mm, 4 μm). Samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before injection. Chromatographic separation was employed with gradient elution with 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution (A) and acetonitrile (B) as mobile phases. The conditions were as follows: flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, column temperature of 30 °C, injection volume of 20 μL. Detection occurred at wavelengths of 280, 320, 360, and 370 nm, and quantification was performed using the external standard method.

Determination of antioxidant capacity

The DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging capacities were used to assess the antioxidant activity following the method of Zhao et al. (2023) with slight modifications. For the DPPH assay, 2 mL of the sample extract was mixed with 4 mL of a 0.1 mmol/L DPPH solution. The mixture was then left to react for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light. Absorbance was then measured at 517 nm. For the ABTS assay, 0.2 mL of extract was mixed with 3 mL of ABTS radical working solution, and the mixture was left to react for 6 min at room temperature, protected from light. The absorbance was then measured at 734 nm. The DPPH and ABTS scavenging capacities were then calculated and expressed as Trolox equivalents antioxidant capacity (μmol TE/g dm).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Microstructural analysis was performed using a scanning electron microscope (S-3400 N, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), following the previous protocols described by Shen et al. (2021). Cross-sectional samples were mounted on stubs and sputter-coated with gold prior to observation at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV and 300× magnification.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

FT-IR spectra were measured in accordance with previous protocols (Gao et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2025) using an FT-IR spectrophotometer (IRAffinity-1S, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The sample powder was mixed with KBr (1:100, w/w) and pressed into pellets. Spectra were recorded from 400 to 4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1, using a pure KBr pellet as the background reference.

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR)

Starch was extracted from GRAB using the protocols described by Acevedo et al. (2022). For the NMR analysis, 20 mg of the starch was dissolved in 0.6 mL of DMSO‑d6 and analyzed using a 600 MHz NMR spectrometer (Avance NEO, Bruker Ltd., Brooke, Switzerland) at 25 °C. Sixteen scans were acquired and processed using MestReNova V.14.0.

Data analysis

The experimental results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation of three independent replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (Ver. 19.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The significance of differences among groups was assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's test or the LSD test. Differences were considered statistically significant at a p-value < 0.05. Data visualization was conducted using OriginPro software (Ver. 2024b, OriginLab Corp., Massachusetts, USA).

Results and discussion

Analysis of drying characteristics of GRAB under MVD

Fig. 2A shows the effect of varying microwave intensities on the drying characteristics of GRAB under constant vacuum pressure (0.065 MPa) and drying temperature (60 °C). The drying time required for GRAB to attain the target moisture content exhibited a decreasing trend with elevated microwave intensity. Specifically, when the microwave intensity increased from 8 to 12 W/g, the corresponding drying time decreased by 20%, shortening from 20 to 16 min. This aligns with the findings of Zhou et al. (2026) regarding microwave vacuum-dried Chinese yams, which reported a significant 85% reduction in drying time when the microwave intensity was raised from 2 to 10 W/g. This could have been attributed to the enhanced energy absorption of GRAB at higher microwave intensities, resulting in increased volumetric heat generation to create a stronger internal driving force for moisture migration (An et al., 2024). From Fig. 2A′, the drying rate curve exhibited a two-phase pattern: an initial rising-rate stage followed by a subsequent falling-rate stage. This was because GRAB contained abundant internal free water in the initial drying stage, which facilitated efficient absorption of microwave energy and generated a strong driving force for moisture transfer; however, as the drying process progressed, the internal moisture content of GRAB gradually decreased, resulting in reduced microwave energy absorption and a consequent decline in drying rate (Liu et al., 2022).

Drying characteristics of GRAB under different MVD parameters. Note: Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3).

Fig. 2B presents the effect of various vacuum pressures on the drying characteristics of GRAB under constant microwave intensity (10 W/g) and drying temperature (60 °C). Increasing the vacuum pressure from 0.05 to 0.08 MPa resulted in a 36% reduction in drying time (from 25 to 16 min). This was attributed to the enhanced pressure differential across the GRAB sample as vacuum pressure increased, which strengthened the driving force for moisture evaporation and accelerated the migration of water from the interior to the exterior of the sample (Liu et al., 2023). This finding was consistent with the work of Soares De Mendonça et al. (2023) on carrot slices, in which MVD significantly shortened the drying time compared to microwave drying alone at the same power level. This phenomenon is attributed to the vacuum environment, which lowers the boiling point of water and facilitates more rapid moisture evaporation and removal, and thereby enhances the drying rate. In Fig. 2B′, the drying rate profile exhibited a two-phase behavior: an initial rising-rate stage followed by a declining-rate stage. However, it is noteworthy that excessively high vacuum levels may induce undesirable phenomena such as glow discharge or arcing, which could cause localized overheating and irreversible damage to the material (Zhu et al., 2024).

Fig. 2C illustrates the effect of various drying temperatures on the drying characteristics of GRAB under constant microwave intensity (10 W/g) and vacuum pressure (0.065 MPa). Increasing drying temperature from 50 to 70 °C significantly shortened the drying time by 80% (from 50 to 10 min). This acceleration can be attributed to the enhanced kinetic energy of water molecules and the increased mass transfer driving force within the GRAB matrix, which collectively promoted water evaporation (Zhu et al., 2024). Correspondingly, Fig. 2C′depicts the evolution of the drying rate, which also exhibited a two-phase pattern: an initial rising phase followed by a subsequent falling phase. As the drying process progressed, the depletion of free water within the GRAB reduced its capacity to absorb microwave energy, resulting in a gradual decline in drying rate (Liu et al., 2022). Notably, the drying rate increased with rising temperature; however, excessively high temperatures may induce irreversible degradation of heat-sensitive bioactive components in GRAB.

To sum up, within the studied MVD parameters, an increase in microwave intensity and vacuum pressure resulted in a reduction in drying time by 20% and 36%, respectively. Meanwhile, raising the drying temperature resulted in an 80% decrease in drying time. These findings underscore that under combined microwave and vacuum conditions, drying temperature was the most pivotal factor affecting drying kinetics. However, excessively high drying temperatures also posed potential risks to heat-sensitive bioactive components in GRAB. Therefore, in future research, implementing precise temperature control during the MVD process of GRAB is crucial for balancing drying efficiency with quality preservation.

Effects of MVD on color and bioactive components of GRAB

Color changes

Color is a critical quality indicator for dried agricultural products. Table 1 shows the impact of MVD on the color changes in GRAB. Compared with raw samples, the dried GRAB exhibited a significant (p < 0.05) reduction in the L* value, along with notable increases in the a*, b*, ΔE, and BI values. These color changes were primarily attributed to non-enzymatic browning processes, with a particular emphasis on the Maillard reaction, which is a common phenomenon during thermal processing of foods (Chahbani et al., 2018). Among the MVD parameters examined, microwave intensity exerted a selective influence, primarily on the L* value. In contrast, vacuum pressure exhibited no significant (p > 0.05) effects on the color parameters. On the other hand, drying temperature emerged as the most influential factor. Elevated temperatures significantly (p < 0.05) intensified the degree of browning. Increasing drying temperature accelerated the frequency of molecular collisions between amino acids and reducing sugars. This, in turn, promoted the polymerization of intermediate products formed during the Maillard reaction (Zhu et al., 2024). In summary, the results demonstrate that among the MVD parameters, drying temperature exerts the most critical influence on browning. In contrast, microwave intensity and vacuum pressure have comparatively minor effects on color changes, establishing temperature control as essential for achieving the desired product appearance in GRAB.

GABA content

As depicted in Fig. 3, the initial GABA content in raw GRAB was 75.37 ± 1.78 mg/100 g. Fig. 3A reveals that the GABA content initially increased and then decreased with drying time when the microwave intensity ranged from 8 to 12 W/g at 0.065 MPa vacuum pressure and 60 °C drying temperature. Notably, according to Eq. (7), the final GABA retention rate reached a relatively high level of 73.95% (100% × 55.74/75.37) at 12 W/g. Fig. 3B demonstrates that the final GABA retention rate decreased from 72.89% (100% × 54.94/75.37) at 0.05 MPa to 55.71% (100% × 41.99/75.37) at 0.08 MPa as the vacuum pressure increased, while the microwave intensity remained at 10 W/g and the drying temperature remained at 60 °C. Similarly, Fig. 3C shows that the final GABA retention rate declined from 69.76% (100% × 52.58/75.37) at 50 °C to 60.34% (100% × 45.48/75.37) at 70 °C as the drying temperature increased, under the conditions of 10 W/g and 0.065 MPa. Overall, the GABA content in GRAB under various MVD conditions exhibited an initial increase followed by a subsequent decrease. This aligns with the findings of Takahashi et al. (2013) who reported that the GABA accumulated early in the drying treatments of immature edamame and the most effective temperature for stimulating the accumulation was 40 °C; in contrast, elevated treatment temperatures at 60 °C and 80 °C led to a marked reduction in GABA content, indicating the critical role of temperature in regulating GABA content. GABA is a free amino acid produced by the irreversible reaction of α-decarboxylation of L-glutamic acid catalyzed by glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) (Diana et al., 2014). The heat treatment activates GAD activity, thereby facilitating the continuous conversion of glutamate to GABA. Moreover, Zhu et al. (2024) also reported a reduction in GABA content in MVD of germinated brown rice, which is similar to the current study's findings. The observed reduction in the GABA content of the final GRAB may be due to two factors: the cumulative effect of sustained microwave energy suppressing GAD activity and limiting GABA synthesis (Shen et al., 2021); thermal degradation driven by elevated temperatures and moisture loss, combined with accelerated non-enzymatic browning via intensified Maillard reactions between free amino acids and reducing sugars (Guo et al., 2025; Thuwapanichayanan et al., 2015). Therefore, properly controlling the drying temperature in MVD can minimize GABA loss in GRAB. In future work, we could consider using MVD with precise temperature control strategies, such as pulsed microwave drying or an intelligent control system, to increase the GABA content of GRAB while achieving dehydration.

TPC and TFC

The TPC of raw GRAB was 179.27 ± 1.52 mg GAE/100 g. As shown in Fig. 4A, under the conditions of 0.065 MPa and 60 °C, the TPC exhibited a consistent trend across all microwave intensities (8, 10, and 12 W/g): an initial increase followed by a subsequent decline. This phenomenon can be attributed to the initial disruption of the cellular matrix by microwave exposure through non-thermal effects (Zhao et al., 2023), which facilitated the release of bound phenolic compounds. However, as the drying process progressed, the accumulated microwave energy gradually elevated the temperature, leading to partial thermal degradation of phenolics (Kubra & Jagan Mohan Rao, 2012). Therefore, an appropriate microwave intensity is critical to balance phenolic release and thermal stability in GRAB. Fig. 4B shows that under the conditions of 10 W/g and 60 °C, all tested vacuum pressures (0.05, 0.065, and 0.08 MPa) significantly (p < 0.05) enhanced the TPC. This suggests that vacuum pressure plays a pivotal role in preserving phenolic compounds during the drying process. Zeng et al. (2025) similarly found in a study on the effects of MVD pretreatment on date powder quality that the synergistic interaction between the vacuum environment and microwave thermal effects can effectively inhibit the oxidation of bioactive substances in date powder, thereby better preserving its nutritional components. As depicted in Fig. 4C, the final TPC of GRAB decreased at 50 °C due to prolonged oxidative and thermal degradation (Hong et al., 2023). In contrast, at drying temperatures of 60 °C and 70 °C, an initial increase in TPC was observed, which was driven by the microwave-assisted release of phenolics, followed by a moderate decline attributed to thermal degradation (Hong et al., 2023).

The raw GRAB exhibited a TFC of 234.65 ± 1.96 mg GAE/100 g. MVD treatment resulted a significant reduction (p < 0.05) in TFC compared to the untreated sample. From Fig. 4D, under the conditions of 0.065 MPa and 60 °C, the TFC in GRAB decreased progressively with extended drying time across different microwave intensities. This implies an initial phase where microwave-induced cellular disruption enhanced flavonoid release through matrix breakdown (Pashazadeh et al., 2024), followed by a dominant phase of thermally driven degradation processes that ultimately reduced the TFC (Ahmed, AlJuhaimi, et al., 2024). Fig. 4E demonstrates that when varying vacuum pressures at 10 W/g and 60 °C, the final TFC retention rates were 92%, 90%, and 89% respectively. Statistical analysis confirmed that changes in vacuum pressure had no significant effect (p > 0.05) on the final TFC retention, indicating that vacuum pressure played a secondary role in flavonoid stability under the tested conditions. In Fig. 4F, the TFC showed an initial decline followed by partial recovery under different drying temperatures under the conditions of 10 W/g and 0.065 MPa. This may be due to competing mechanisms: thermal degradation of flavonoids at elevated temperatures and enhanced extraction efficiency resulting from dehydration-induced alterations in cell wall permeability (Lou et al., 2015). To sum up, the TPC and TFC retention in GRAB during MVD are determined by the competing effects of microwave-induced structural disruption and thermal degradation.

Individual phenolic compounds

In addition to measuring the TPC, we further determined the individual phenolic compounds in GRAB subjected to various MVD conditions. Table 2 presents the phenolic compound composition of GRAB after MVD treatment, and the main components identified are gallic acid, rutin, quercetin, chlorogenic acid, epicatechin, and p-coumaric acid. Compared with the untreated group, MVD significantly (p < 0.05) increased the contents of gallic acid, quercetin, and chlorogenic acid, decreased rutin and epicatechin, but showed no significant effect (p > 0.05) on p-coumaric acid in GRAB. Specifically, under 0.065 MPa vacuum pressure and 60 °C drying temperature, increasing the microwave intensity from 8 to 12 W/g significantly (p < 0.05) decreased the rutin content from 4.79 to 4.58 mg/100 g, while no significant (p > 0.05) changes in the contents of gallic acid, quercetin, chlorogenic acid, epicatechin, and p-coumaric acid were found. When the microwave intensity and drying temperature were fixed at 10 W/g and 60 °C, respectively, increasing the vacuum pressure from 0.05 to 0.08 MPa significantly (p < 0.05) increased the quercetin content from 7.48 to 8.57 mg/100 g, with no significant (p > 0.05) changes in other phenolics. Furthermore, at 10 W/g microwave intensity and 0.065 MPa vacuum pressure, increasing the drying temperature from 50 to 70 °C significantly (p < 0.05) increased the contents of gallic acid, quercetin, and chlorogenic acid, but decreased those of rutin and epicatechin, without affecting p-coumaric. Overall, MVD significantly increased the contents of gallic acid, quercetin, and chlorogenic acid but lowered those of rutin and epicatechin, with no effect on p-coumaric acid. This increase could result from the microwave thermal effect promoting the dissociation of bound phenolic compounds into free forms, or related to the formation of Maillard reaction products (Özcan et al., 2024). In contrast, the decrease in rutin and epicatechin is likely due to their degradation under microwave heating (Ahmed et al., 2025).

Antioxidant capacity analysis

This study evaluated the antioxidant activity of MVD-treated GRAB using DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging capacity as indicators. As shown in Fig. 5, untreated GRAB samples exhibited DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging capacity values of 1.23 μmol/g and 1.39 μmol/g, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5C, the DPPH radical scavenging capacity under 10 W/g and 0.065 MPa initially increased with the drying temperature from 50 to 60 °C, then decreased at 70 °C. By comparison, changes in microwave intensity (Fig. 5A) and vacuum pressure (Fig. 5B) exhibited a more modest influence on the DPPH radical scavenging capacity relative to temperature variations. The ABTS radical scavenging capacity increased with microwave intensity from 8 to 12 W/g under the conditions of 0.065 MPa and 60 °C (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, at 10 W/g and 60 °C, the ABTS radical scavenging capacity at 0.065 MPa was significantly higher than at other pressures (Fig. 5E). Additionally, raising the drying temperature from 50 to 70 °C at 10 W/g and 0.065 MPa led to a notable enhancement in the ABTS radical scavenging capacity (Fig. 5F). Overall, MVD significantly (p < 0.05) enhanced the antioxidant capacity of GRAB compared to the untreated group. This is in line with the findings of Özcan and Uslu (2024) in their studies on oven-dried and microwave-dried soybean and peanut seeds. The thermal treatment can effectively enhance the antioxidant capacity of seeds, probably by forming Maillard reaction products during MVD (Ahmed, Özcan, et al., 2024; Al Juhaimi et al., 2024). In addition, the non-thermal microwave effects promote the dissolution of antioxidants, and the vacuum low-temperature drying suppresses their thermal degradation, synergistically enhancing the overall antioxidant capacity (D. Hu et al., 2023).

Effects of MVD on the starch structure of GRAB

Starch gelatinization properties

Table 3 shows the gelatinization characteristics of GRAB starch after MVD treatment, including the onset temperature (T0), peak temperature (Tp), conclusion temperature (Tc), gelatinization enthalpy (∆H), and degree of starch gelatinization (DSG). The gelatinization temperature range (ΔT, calculated as Tc − T0) of GRAB starch ranged from 63.01 to 86.82 °C. Compared with untreated samples, MVD-treated GRAB showed a significantly (p < 0.05) decrease in Tc and ∆H, along with a marked increase in DSG. The reduction in Tc was attributed to microwave-induced cleavage of branched starch chains, which raised the proportion of short amylopectin branches and weakened crystalline interlayer interactions, thereby lowering the temperature required for dissociation of starch granules (Ma et al., 2020). The decrease in ∆H, reflecting lower energy absorption during gelatinization, indicated that MVD disrupted the double-helix structure of starch, with higher processing temperatures further reducing the energy needed for unwinding these structures (Hu, Li and Zheng, 2019). The increase in DSG resulted from progressive thermal degradation of ordered crystalline regions, promoting simultaneous dissociation of both crystalline and amorphous domains in the starch. These findings are consistent with those reported by Zhu et al. (2024) in their study on MVD-treated germinated brown rice. Notably, variations in microwave intensity and vacuum pressure had no significant (p > 0.05) effects on ΔH or DSG, confirming that temperature is the dominant factor influencing starch gelatinization under MVD conditions. These results demonstrate that MVD modifies the gelatinization properties of GRAB starch through temperature-driven molecular reorganization of starch polymers (Shen et al., 2021).

Microstructure changes

Fig. 6 presents the microstructural morphology of GRAB treated by MVD at 300× magnification. The raw GRAB starch granules exhibited regular ellipsoidal shapes and a densely packed structure (Fig. 6A). Overall, MVD treatment caused significant morphological changes in the starch granules compared to the untreated sample. When microwave intensity was increased from 8 to 12 W/g under constant vacuum pressure of 0.065 MPa and drying temperature of 60 °C, the granular arrangement was markedly disrupted, resulting in partial melting of starch granules and the formation of large pores (Figs. 6B, C, D). This effect was attributed to the internal stress generated by microwave volumetric heating, which stretched and compressed the starch structure (Zhu et al., 2024). Similarly, at a fixed microwave intensity of 10 W/g and drying temperature of 60 °C, increasing the vacuum pressure from 0.05 to 0.08 MPa led to expansion of starch granules and pore formation (Figs. 6C, E, F). These structural changes were driven by pressure gradients that enhanced water diffusion pathways and accelerated the drying rate (Monteiro et al., 2020). As shown in Figs. 6C, G, H, at constant microwave intensity of 10 W/g and vacuum pressure of 0.065 MPa, increasing the drying temperature from 50 to 70 °C resulted in slight gelatinization, transforming the starch granules from regular ellipsoidal shapes to irregular configurations. This transformation was due to partial gelatinization induced by higher drying temperatures (Li et al., 2022). Overall, MVD treatment induced noticeable volume expansion, partial melting, and pore formation in GRAB starch granules. These alterations resulted from the rapid diffusion and evaporation of internal moisture under microwave volumetric heating (Li et al., 2022). Such structural modifications promoted the development of void channels within GRAB, which improved the drying rate and facilitated starch gelatinization.

Crystalline structure

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to determine the crystal type and relative crystallinity (RC) of the starch. As shown in Fig. 8A, untreated GRAB starch displayed a characteristic A-type crystalline pattern, with strong diffraction peaks at 15°, 17°, and 23° (Maldaner et al., 2021). The positions of these peaks remained unchanged after MVD treatment, indicating that the original crystal structure was retained. The RC of starch was calculated as the ratio of the crystalline peak area to the total diffraction area. As shown in Fig. 8B, the RC of raw GRAB starch was 24.36%. However, MVD treatment led to a significant (p < 0.05) reduction in RC compared to untreated starch. This was attributed to the disruption of the ordered crystalline regions, partial degradation of crystallites, and gelatinization of starch granules induced by MVD (Ge et al., 2021). Under the conditions of 10 W/g and 60 °C, varying the vacuum pressure from 0.05 to 0.08 MPa had little effect on the RC of GRAB starch. In contrast, at a microwave intensity of 10 W/g and a vacuum pressure of 0.065 MPa, increasing the drying temperature from 50 to 70 °C significantly (p < 0.05) reduced the RC from 22.42% to 17.70%. This result was consistent with the gelatinization temperature of GRAB starch, which exceeds 60 °C. The increased thermal energy at higher temperatures probably expedited the breakdown of starch double-helix structures and facilitated the creation of amorphous regions (Tao et al., 2020). Consequently, the drying temperature was pinpointed as the pivotal factor impacting the RC of GRAB starch during the MVD process.

1H NMR spectrum analysis

1H NMR spectroscopy was used to investigate the proton distribution patterns associated with specific chemical bonds and functional groups in GRAB starch subjected to MVD. As shown in Fig. 9, the characteristic chemical shifts were identified as follows: hydroxyl protons appeared at 5.4–5.5 ppm, while signals from CH and CH₂ groups were observed at 3.58 ppm and 3.65 ppm, respectively (Huang et al., 2023). The deuterated solvent DMSO‑d₆ showed a distinct peak at 2.5 ppm, and residual water protons were detected at 3.33 ppm. Notably, the α-(1,6)-glycosidic linkage exhibited a characteristic signal at 4.80 ppm, which was clearly distinguishable from the α-(1,4)-glycosidic bond signal at 5.11 ppm (Acevedo et al., 2022).

1H NMR spectra of GRAB under different MVD parameters.

As shown in Fig. 9A, no significant changes in chemical shifts were observed in the 1H NMR spectra of starch treated with MVD across microwave intensities ranging from 8 to 12 W/g, suggesting structural stability under varying microwave intensities. In Fig. 9B, the intensity of certain characteristic peaks increased markedly as the vacuum pressure was raised from 0.05 to 0.08 MPa. In Fig. 9C, the signal intensity corresponding to α-(1,4) glycosidic bonds increased with drying temperature from 50 to 70 °C. This may be explained by microwave-induced rapid heating, which preferentially cleaves α-1,6 glycosidic bonds in amylopectin due to their lower steric hindrance compared to α-1,4 linkages (Chen et al., 2017). Consequently, the degradation of branched structures led to a higher proportion of amylose chains. Overall, 1H NMR analysis provided a theoretical basis for understanding the structural stability of starch by monitoring changes in α-(1,4) glycosidic bonds and α-(1,6) glycosidic bonds during -MVD treatment.

Possible underlying mechanisms

Fig. 10 illustrates a possible mechanism for the interaction of microwaves with GRAB starch during -MVD, clarifying how this process alters starch structure. An in-depth analysis of the starch gelatinization properties revealed a decline in gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH), suggesting that microwave volumetric heating partially disrupted double-helical structures. SEM observations confirmed that MVD induced melt gelatinization of the starch, due to the continuous accumulation of microwave energy throughout the drying process. FT-IR spectroscopy revealed that MVD induced a physical restructuring of GRAB starch by breaking down hydrogen-bonding networks, with no indication of chemical bond cleavage or formation. XRD results indicated that although MVD preserved the crystalline type of the starch, higher microwave energy inputs resulted in greater decomposition of double-helical structures within the starch molecules. The 1H NMR spectra of GRAB starch exhibited no detectable variations in chemical shifts, further reinforcing the absence of new chemical bond formation during the MVD process. Additionally, 1H NMR spectra further revealed that α-(1,6)-glycosidic bonds were more susceptible to cleavage than α-(1,4) linkages under MVD conditions. Therefore, MVD primarily disrupted the double-helical structure of starch through microwave volumetric heating effects, rather than by changes in chemical bonds. The proposed mechanism offers a comprehensive framework for comprehending how MVD altered the starch structure at molecular and supramolecular levels.

Conclusions

This study investigated the effects of MVD parameters on the drying characteristics, bioactive components, antioxidant capacity, and starch structure of GRAB. Within the studied parameters range, drying temperature was found to be the most critical factor affecting drying kinetics, color changes, and GABA content. The retention of TPC and TFC in GRAB was influenced by the competing effects of microwave-induced structural disruption and thermal degradation. MVD increased the contents of gallic acid, quercetin, and chlorogenic acid, but decreased those of rutin and epicatechin, and had no significant effect on p-coumaric acid. Additionally, MVD significantly enhanced the antioxidant capacity of GRAB. Regarding starch properties, increased drying temperatures markedly reduced gelatinization enthalpy and increased the degree of starch gelatinization. MVD also induced noticeable microstructural changes in GRAB, disrupted the double-helical structure of starch, and cleaved α-(1,6) glycosidic bonds, resulting in a reduction in relative crystallinity. Overall, MVD can be considered an effective method for preserving bioactive components and modifying the starch properties of GRAB, thereby potentially improving the nutritional and functional qualities of legume products. Future work should focus on the optimization of MVD conditions using response surface methodology, the development of precise temperature control strategies, and the evaluation of in vitro starch digestibility and cooking quality to support the practical application of GRAB.
