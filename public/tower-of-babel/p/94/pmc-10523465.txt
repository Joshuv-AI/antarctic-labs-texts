A multiple Kirkendall strategy for converting nanosized zero-valent iron to highly active Fenton-like catalyst for organics degradation
Authors: Jianhua Qu, Zhuoran Li, Fuxuan Bi, Xiubo Zhang, Bo Zhang, Kaige Li, Siqi Wang, Mingze Sun, Jun Ma, Ying Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceNanosized zero-valent iron (nZVI) is a promising persulfate (PS) activator, while its structurally dense oxide shell seriously inhibited electrons transfer to PS activation. Herein, we decorated nZVI through simultaneous sulfidation and phosphorus-doped biochar (SnZVI@PBC) for breaking its pristine oxide shell with formation of FeS and FePO4-containing mixed shell. In this case, the faster diffusion rate of iron atoms compared to shell compounds triggered multiple Kirkendall effects, leading to the formation of “lemon-slice-like” nanocracks. Thus, abundant electrons in the Fe0 core could fast transfer across the mixed shell to activate PS for high-efficient trichloroethylene degradation. The mechanism and practical application performance of SnZVI@PBC/PS system were explored. The study verified great potential of designing Fenton-like catalysts through multiple Kirkendall effects for organics degradation.

Persulfate ( S2O82- ; PS)-based advanced oxidation techniques are widely employed for groundwater remediation due to excellent stability and extensive application scope (1, 2). Among various methods for PS activation (e.g., light, heat, and electricity), nanosized zero-valent iron (nZVI) as an economical and green heterogeneous catalyst has received increasing attention (3, 4). NZVI can corrode to form dissolved Fe2+ or directly activate PS for generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) via electrons transfer for O-O bond cleavage of PS (5, 6). However, nZVI has a typical core-shell structure characterized by Fe0 core enclosed in naturally born dense iron oxide shell, which seriously blocks outward migration of electrons and Fe2+ ions across the shell, thus resulting in low-efficiency activation of PS (7, 8).

Kirkendall effect is a typical metallurgical phenomenon generally embodying unbalanced mutual diffusion across coupled materials’ interface (9, 10). This phenomenon was first put forward for the description of voids formation at the interface of two bulk materials with discrepant interdiffusion rates. For instance, Moreau et al. (11) prepared a hollow nanocage structure by taking the advantage of the fact that the outward diffusion rate of Ag within the core is much higher than the inward diffusion rate of Au. As for nanoscaled core-shell structures, the difference between slow circumferential stress from shell components and rapid adverse circumferential stress from core components can induce Kirkendall effect. And this can further lead to an inward flux of vacancies toward the core and coalesce into Kirkendall pores (i.e., nanocracks) because of radial symmetry and spatial confinement (12, 13). Theoretically, the outward-diffusion of iron atoms across the oxide shell of nZVI is naturally a reflection of Kirkendall effect (14), and the relatively fast diffusion speed of iron atoms within the core compared to iron oxide on the shell can provoke gradual formation of nanocracks (15, 16), which may be beneficial to outward electrons transfer across the shell. Unfortunately, the structurally thick and dense oxide shell seriously restricts mutual mass diffusion at the core/shell interface of nZVI (12, 17, 18). Consequently, a directionally structural design of nZVI with an amplified Kirkendall effect to promote nanocrack formation for speeding up electrons transfer may provide a possibility for high-efficient PS activation.

Recently, sulfidation of nZVI (SnZVI) is widely concerned because of its critical role on improving particle reactivity, electron selectivity, and reactive lifetime (8, 19). Moreover, sulfidation promotes formation of FeSn (e.g., FeS and FeS2) layer with excellent electrical conductivity instead of pristine oxide layer on nZVI surface (20). Compared to nZVI, there exists stronger nonequilibrium mass diffusion between iron atoms and FeSn on core-shell interface of SnZVI, moreover, the formed much thinner FeSn shell is beneficial to the enhancement of mutual diffusion process (21). Consequently, it is hypothesized that sulfidation may trigger amplified Kirkendall effect on nZVI with formation of nanocracks, which in turn facilitates electrons transfer for PS activation.

Severe particle agglomeration is another obstacle during application of nZVI technology (5). Various anchoring materials (e.g., zeolite, graphene, and covalent organic frameworks) have been used to disperse nZVI particles, among which biochar as a kind of porous carbon materials stands out owing to its large surface area, tunable electronics, and low cost (22, 23). Considering that random distribution of nZVI particles on biochar surface may also result in local agglomeration, element doping (e.g., N and P) has been reported to regulate surface properties of biochar for further dispersion of nZVI particles (24, 25). Several researches put forward that the incorporation of electron-rich P heteroatoms could endow biochar abundant P-containing functional groups with excellent affinity to Fe2+/Fe3+, and the consequent liquid phase reduction of Fe2+/Fe3+ could give rise to more even distribution of nZVI particles (26). Meanwhile, such properties as defect density, conductivity, and pore structure of P-doped biochar were also reported to be improved compared to pristine biochar (27). Because the P-containing functional groups also participate in shell formation during Fe2+/Fe3+ reduction process, the shell thickness and composition can also be regulated. Furthermore, it was reported that phosphate widely exists in environmental medium showing excellent affinity to the iron oxide shell of nZVI, and can combine with iron ions to form phosphorus-iron compound (28). It is also well known that O atom diffuses faster than P atom, and the diffusion rate of Fe is faster than that of iron oxide and phosphorus-iron compounds (29). Thus, besides improving nZVI agglomeration, P-doped biochar is also hypothesized to trigger amplified Kirkendall effect and induce surface nanocrack formation on nZVI for enhanced electrons transfer, i.e., “killing two birds with one stone.”

To examine these hypotheses, we prepared different varieties of P-doped biochar-anchored SnZVI (SnZVI@PBC) materials with deep investigation on how sulfidation and P-doped biochar triggered multiple Kirkendall effects for nanocrack formation, and how these nanocracks influenced electrons transfer, ROS generation and utilization in SnZVI@PBC/PS system by using frequently detected trichloroethylene (TCE) in groundwater as a model contaminant. We further explored the underlying TCE dechlorination and mineralization mechanisms by combining DFT calculations and experimental approaches, and also revealed the practical application potential of SnZVI@PBC/PS system.

Results and Discussion

Synergistic Effects of Sulfidation and P-doped Biochar on nZVI Structure and Properties.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images presented that P doping contributed to appearance of distinct multi-layered pore structure with specific surface area (SBET) increasing from 16.8 to 352.8 m2 g−1 compared to relatively smooth BC500 (SI Appendix, Figs. S1 A–C and S2). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis confirmed successful incorporation of P by identifying obvious adsorption peak of P-O at 894 cm–1 for SnZVI@PBC300-700 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) (30). Further from Fe 2p XPS spectra (Fig. 1A), the obvious characteristic peak at 706.9 eV was ascribed to Fe-P bonding of Fe 2p, confirming the formation of phosphorus-iron compound (FePOx) (31), which might be an essential shell ingredient (i.e., phosphorylated oxide shell) of SnZVI@PBC. The overlap peaks of Fe0 and Fe-P were because the PBC anchoring possessed a considerable effect on the surface availability of iron in a reduced form ref. 32. Compared to SnZVI@BC300-700, SnZVI particles were more uniformly distributed on PBC500 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E), which was due to that the formed HPO42- groups on PBC500 surface could effectively adsorb and immobilize Fe2+ for uniformly in-situ production of Fe0 (33), as confirmed by Fig. 1B. Moreover, Fig. 1C presented that sulfidation promoted the formation of FeS shell on SnZVI@PBC, and the well-known poorly ferromagnetic FeS was also beneficial to uniform distribution of nZVI particles (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 E and F).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns exhibited relatively higher degree of carbon (002) crystalline of PBC compared to BC, indicating that P doping was more conductive to enhancement of carbon atom orderliness (Fig. 1D). Moreover, high diffraction peak at 44.6° matched (110) crystalline surface of Fe0, of which the diffraction intensities were masked to a certain extent after sulfidation and PBC anchoring. From Fig. 1E, the domain size of SnZVI was found to increase compared to nZVI, demonstrating that S atoms were successfully doped into the bodycentered-cubic (BCC) Fe crystalline structure (19, 34). Moreover, no other detectable peaks associated to FeS or FeS2 appeared, clearly indicative of the dominating phase of Fe0 and the disordered form of incorporated S atoms with low-degree crystallinity or low concentrations (35–38). Compared to SnZVI@BC, the increased domain size of SnZVI@PBC revealed that PBC facilitated Fe0 growth, which was conducive to electron transfer process.

Inspired by characterization results, the reactivity of SnZVI@PBC with different [P/CS]mass was explored (Fig. 1F). The degradation efficiency of TCE by SnZVI@P0.3BC/PS system was 80.4%, significantly higher than that of SnZVI@P0.15BC/PS process with corresponding kobs enhanced by 1.2 times (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This was because the Fe and S contents on SnZVI@PBC gradually increased with the increase of P-doped amounts (SI Appendix, Table S1), and thus the introduced abundant HPO42- groups on PBC could effectively bind iron ions for dispersedly in situ reduction and sulfidation processes. Nevertheless, as [P/CS]mass continually increased to 0.45, the TCE elimination efficiency was decreased by 11.6%, and this might be attributed to the decreased amount of C2PO2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), which resulted in inferior electron transfer capacity (39). The PS activation efficiency by SnZVI@PBC at different pyrolysis temperatures followed the order 500 °C > 300 °C ≈ 700 °C. The likely reason was that the aromaticity and pore structures of PBC were improved by increasing pyrolysis temperature, whereas higher pyrolysis temperature of 700 °C resulted in the transformation of HPO42- to such other species as H2PO42- and PO43- (Fig. 1B) (26), which were unfavorable for iron ions binding. Notably, the TCE removal efficiency increased from 49.7 to 80.4% when [C/Fe]mass increased from 0.1 to 0.5, while was decreased by 11.7% when further increasing [C/Fe]mass to 1.0. This might be because superfluous C decreased Fe0 loading amount, thus causing decreased active sites. As to [S/Fe]mass, the S0.25nZVI@PBC exhibited best TCE degradation efficiency, while further increasing [S/Fe]mass to 0.5 inhibited TCE decomposition by 14.8%. The likely reason was that large quantities of SO32-and SO42-formed on SnZVI surface at higher S dosage (Fig. 1C), which possessed much lower electrical conductivity compared to the FeS shell (35), thus inhibiting the outward electrons transfer from Fe0 core for PS activation. Overall, SnZVI@PBC with [P/CS]mass = 0.3, [C/Fe]mass = 0.5, [S/Fe]mass = 0.25, and pyrolysis temperature of 500 °C has been demonstrated to possess high-performance catalytic activity for PS, and thus was applied to subsequent TCE degradation experiments.

Multiple Kirkendall Effects-Triggered Nanocrack Formation on SnZVI@PBC.

High-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) images presented necklace-like spherical shapes of nZVI particles with relatively smooth iron oxide shell, of which the thickness was about 10 nm (Fig. 2A). After sulfidation, the nZVI was encapsuled by FeS shell, which was irregular and obviously thinner than original iron oxide shell, and tiny nanocracks appeared in the center of nanosphere structure with gradual extension to the edge (Fig. 2B). And the amount of nanocracks gradually increased with more obvious appearance as the sulfidation time increased from 25 to 50 min (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). This phenomenon might be caused by sulfidation-triggered Kirkendall effect. For nZVI or SnZVI, the outward oxide shell or FeS shell would exert circumferential stress (F′ or F2), while the Fe0 core would apply adverse circumferential stress (F1) on the surrounded shell during its gradual growth (40), which is related to the loads of the object and is generated along the circumference of the object (41). In comparison with nZVI, the nonequilibrium mass diffusion between iron atoms and FeS shell at the core-shell interface of SnZVI was more drastic, and the much thinner FeS shell also enhanced this diffusion process, thus making F1 grow faster than F2 until the attainment of critical force (Fc) (42). Subsequently, Fc conflicted with the constraints of FeS shell and “broke” the structurally dense nZVI accompanied by occurrence of tiny radial nanocracks. Interestingly, radial nanocracks were also observed on nZVI@PBC (Fig. 2C), and the nanocrack structure was more obvious as the synthesis time increased from 25 to 60 min (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), which might be attributed to PBC-triggered Kirkendall effect. Specifically, the unbalanced mass diffusion between iron atoms and FePOx on the shell could also induce Kirkendall effect, which was further enhanced by the relatively thinner phosphorylated oxide shell. While the smaller number of nanocracks on nZVI@PBC was possibly because of the relatively weak circumferential stress (F3) of phosphorylated oxide shell on the Fe0 core caused by limited interaction sites between PBC and nZVI. Amazingly, after synergistic sulfidation and PBC anchoring, the interaction force between F1 and F2+F3 was further strengthened, resulting in more destructive Fc, which triggered multiple Kirkendall effects, and thus distinct and compact radial cracks were found to distribute on the shell (Fig. 2D).

In order to further prove the existence of F1, F2, and F3 in SnZVI@PBC, we supplemented cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves, and Fourier transform extended X-ray adsorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) spectra with corresponding wavelet transform plots. On the one hand, the synthesis process of nZVI, nZVI@PBC, and SnZVI@PBC was respectively simulated by FeCl2·4H2O, FeCl2·4H2O and H3PO4, as well as FeCl2·4H2O, H3PO4 and Na2S (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). The reduction potential of Fe2+ solution shifted from −0.96 V to −1.01 V after being mixed with P-containing ions, indicating that the reduction of Fe2+ was postponed by phosphate ions. After extra addition of S ions, the reduction potential was identified as -1.07 V, suggesting the further postponing of Fe2+ reduction. Notably, the gradually negative increase of reduction potential was beneficial to the decrease of Fe0 supersaturation degree and the breaking of relative balance of nucleation, thus promoting the growth of Fe0 core to be greater than nucleation (43). In this case, more orderly stacked Fe atoms might form within the core, resulting in a more violent outward diffusion action (i.e. stronger F1). Moreover, as shown in the FT-EXAFS spectra (Fig. 3D), the Fe-Fe bond length within the core of nZVI, SnZVI, and SnZVI@PBC was respectively determined as 2.14, 2.18, and 2.20 Å, and the increase of bond lengths also confirmed the stronger F1 exerted by Fe0 in SnZVI@PBC. On the other hand, in light of the control sample of FeS, the lobe of SnZVI presented obvious shift to lower R and k values in comparison with nZVI (Fig. 3E), and this phenomenon was more obvious for SnZVI@PBC, indicating that the Fe-S bond length of SnZVI@PBC was shorter than that of SnZVI (44). In theory, Fe-P and Fe-S bond lengths were similar (45), and therefore the existence of Fe-P bond (Figs. 1A and 3F) on the shell of SnZVI@PBC should promote the increase of Fe-S bond length, whereas the practical results were completely opposite, clearly demonstrating the existence of F2+F3 from the mixed shell of SnZVI@PBC, thus restraining the growth of Fe0 core.

Impressively, high-angle annular dark-field high resolution transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-HRTEM) further revealed that SnZVI@PBC was composed of “lemon-slice-like” nanocracks which penetrated entire zone from Fe0 core to external shell, clearly indicative of the enhancement on outward interfacial electrons transfer capacity of Fe0 core (Fig. 2E). Additionally, Fe+S overlapped mapping results showed retainment of core-shell characteristic with 4 to 7 nm in thickness of FeS shell. However, a small amount of S entered the Fe0 core, which might be related to the precursor of Fe2+ and S2− (46). Obviously, the overlap mapping of Fe+P+O indicated the formation of phosphorylated oxide shell with a thickness of 4 to 7 nm. Furthermore, the total overlap mapping results preliminarily demonstrated that P and S were mainly dispersed around the Fe0 core evenly, implying the formation of mixed shell containing both FeS and FePOx.

To carefully determine the elements distribution of P and S on SnZVI@PBC, we supplemented HRTEM-EDS and HRXPS etching images of different materials. It could be observed from SI Appendix, Fig. S9A that for nZVI, the Fe-line profile quickly reached a peak value at the particle center, clearly indicative of the maximal iron content of Fe0 core. The same results were also found in SnZVI and SnZVI@PBC samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 B and C). Comparatively, for SnZVI and SnZVI@PBC, two peaks of S-line profiles were found to locate in the shell structure, of which the intensities gradually declined and arrived a plateau with the increase of line scanning depth, indicating that the S atoms were mainly located in the shell of SnZVI@PBC. Also, P atoms were shown to be mainly distributed in the shell structure according to the characterization results similar to S-line profile. Further from Fe 2p HRXPS etching results (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A), the peak intensity of Fe gradually increased accompanied by the etching depth increasing to 30 nm, indicating that abundant Fe existed in the core structure. While with the increase of etching depth, the peak intensities of S and P gradually decreased (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 B and C), confirming that the S and P atoms were mainly distributed on the shell structure. To sum up, the P and S atoms could diffuse into the Fe0 core slightly, whereas most of them were concentrated on the shell structure of SnZVI@PBC.

The crystal structures of different materials were determined to further identify the concrete core and shell components of SnZVI@PBC. As observed in Fig. 3A, from the perspective of Fe0 core, the lattice fringe of Fe0 could not be observed in the core of nZVI, which might be caused by stacked nZVI particles or poor local crystallization (47). However, the core of SnZVI and SnZVI@PBC displayed distinct lattice fringes with a distance of 0.205 and 0.208 nm, respectively, corresponding to the (110) facets of α-Fe (Fig. 3 B and C) (48). Obviously, the averaged lattice distance among α-Fe(110) facets of SnZVI was shorter than that of SnZVI@PBC, revealing the stronger F1 exerted by Fe0 in the core of SnZVI@PBC, which also suggested that PBC modification might trigger an amplified Kirkendall effect. In addition, from the perspective of shell structure, the shell of nZVI showed obvious lattice fringe of Fe2O3(109) facets with a space of 0.229 nm (Fig. 3A) (49). At the same time, the corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern exhibited similar diffraction rings with a small number of diffraction points, manifesting the poor crystallization of nZVI (50). The lattice fringe of FeS(310) facets was identified in the shell structure of SnZVI (Fig. 3B) (51). Notably, the overlapped lattice fringes of Fe2O3(109), FeS(310), and FePO4(112) facets with a space of 0.224, 0.240, and 0.270 nm were found in the shell structure of SnZVI@PBC. Moreover, the corresponding SAED pattern of SnZVI@PBC showed multilayer diffraction rings with multiple diffraction points, indicating that SnZVI@PBC was a polycrystalline structure with excellent crystallization (Fig. 3C) (52). Additionally, from the X-ray adsorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 3F), the Fe K-edge XANES spectra of nZVI was in accordance with that of Fe foil, and the corresponding spectra of SnZVI conformed to the control samples of Fe foil and FeS. Furthermore, the existence of Fe0, FeS, and FePO4 of SnZVI@PBC was successfully identified by fitting the corresponding iron species. In conclusion, the above results clearly indicated that Fe0 mainly existed in the core structure, while the mixed shell was mainly composed of FeS and FePO4.

Nanocrack-Enhanced Electron Transfer for Efficient ROS Generation and Utilization in SnZVI@PBC/PS System.

Inspired by abundant nanocracks occurring on SnZVI@PBC, we further explored the electrons transfer process in SnZVI@PBC/PS system. Raman spectra was first used to explore electronic coupling interaction after addition of modified substances for nZVI@BC (Fig. 4A). The characteristic G band at 1602.2 cm−1 associated with nZVI@PBC shifted to the left by 7.1 cm−1 after sulfidation, meaning that electrons were transferred from S element to nZVI@PBC (53). Similarly, after P doping, the characteristic G band at 1605.5 cm−1 corresponding to SnZVI@BC showed leftshift by 10.4 cm−1, revealing the electrons transfer from P element to SnZVI@BC. Overall, sulfidation as well as phosphorus decoration were both beneficial to improvement of electrons amount on SnZVI@PBC, thus making itself an electron-rich catalyst. Subsequently, the electrons transfer ability of as-prepared materials were assessed using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). As presented in Fig. 4B, the radius of SnZVI was smaller than that of nZVI, implying that the FeS shell formed on nZVI surface mediated and accelerated electrons transfer (35). Furthermore, the smallest radius of SnZVI@PBC embodied its most powerful electron transfer capability, which was owing to the outstanding electronic media of PBC for transferring electrons of the catalyst (54–56). In order to identify if electron transfer process contributed to TCE removal in SnZVI@PBC/PS system, K2Cr2O7 was added to the system for quenching electrons (57). As depicted in SI Appendix, Fig. S11, after addition of 20 mM K2Cr2O7, the TCE removal efficiency was seriously inhibited from 97.2 to 51.5% with reaction rate (kobs) declining from 0.0221 to 0.0034 min−1, highlighting the existence of electron transfer-mediated TCE removal process. As presented in Fig. 4A, the characteristic D and G bands of SnZVI@PBC showed clearly right shift after addition of PS, revealing the electrons transfer from SnZVI@PBC to PS activation (53). From current time (i-t) curve (Fig. 4C), the current density of SnZVI@PBC electrode was found to decrease with the addition of PS, further demonstrating that SnZVI@PBC lost electrons to PS activation for ROS generation (58). Simultaneously, a notable increase of current after addition of TCE into the system was attributed to the fact that SnZVI@PBC gradually became electron-deficient along with rapid electron transfer, and therefore extracted electrons from TCE to maintain electrostatic balance, thus achieving direct degradation of TCE (59, 60).

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurement was subsequently employed for confirming the ROS produced in this system. As presented in Fig. 4 D and E, obvious signals of hydroxyl radical ( •OH ), superoxide radical ( O2•- ), and singlet oxygen ( 1O2 ) were observed, demonstrating the participation of these ROS in TCE degradation. While the signal with intensity of 1:1:1:1:1:1 was not found, which was probably ascribed to quick conversion of SO4•- to •OH or the fast transformation of DMPO-SO4 to DMPO-OH through nucleophilic substitution (61, 62). Furthermore, quenching tests with different scavengers were applied to estimate the contributions of different ROS (Fig. 4F and SI Appendix, Fig. S12) (63–65). After addition of enough methanol (MeOH) and tert-butanol (TBA), the TCE degradation efficiency was respectively inhibited by 32.5% and 22.6%, indicating more •OH instead of SO4•- were generated in this system with certain contribution to TCE degradation. Impressively, p-benzoquinone (PBQ) had the strongest inhibitory effect on TCE degradation, confirming O2•- as the dominant reactive species during reaction, which was partly derived from hydrolysis of PS (Eq. 1). While after further addition of TBA into the above process, the TCE removal performance was declined from 27.1 to 14.3%, suggesting that there existed ROS conversion from •OH to O2•- (Eqs. 2 and 3) (4). As observed from Fig. 4G, the signal with intensity of 1:1:1:1 was significantly weakened in SnZVI@PBC/PS system after addition of TBA, also proving the transformation of •OH to O2•- . On the other hand, the TCE degradation efficiency declined from 97.2 to 52.6% after addition of 20 mM furfuryl alcohol (FFA) into the SnZVI@PBC/PS system, revealing that 1O2 contributed to TCE removal. After further addition of PBQ into the above system, the degradation efficiency of TCE was declined from 52.6% to 24.9%, demonstrating the conversion from O2•- to 1O2 . And previous researches also reported that O2•- might participate in 1O2 generation through the recombination of O2•- under anaerobic reaction environment (56, 65–67), as shown in Eqs. 4–6. Notably, the signal peak with intensity of 1:1:1 was obviously weakened in SnZVI@PBC/PS system after adding PBQ (Fig. 4G), which also manifested the transformation of O2•- to 1O2 . After addition of KI for quenching the surface-bound species (59), the TCE removal efficiency was inhibited by 17.5%, demonstrating the main reaction interface for ROS to attack TCE was in the solution.[1]2S2O82-+2H2O→SO4•-+3SO42-+O2•-+4H+. Various chemical indices, including release of Cl-and Fe ions, PS consumption as well as TOC, were further investigated to clarify the enhanced ROS generation and utilization in this system. On the one hand, the leaching curves confirmed the Fe0 in SnZVI@PBC could rapidly corrode to generate Fe(II) and Fe(III) within 60 min, corresponding to 69.6% of PS consumption (Fig. 4H). Moreover, the PS consumption amount in SnZVI@PBC/PS system was 1.33 to 2.61 times that of the other comparison systems (SI Appendix, Fig. S13), and the SnZVI@PBC also presented more impressive PS activation performance for contaminants removal compared to recently reported nZVI-based catalysts (SI Appendix, Table S3). This demonstrated that the surface nanocracks on SnZVI@PBC could enhance the fast release of Fe species into solution for effective PS activation and ROS production, which was also supported by the decreased peak intensity of Fe0 of reacted SnZVI@PBC (SI Appendix, Fig. S14), and the significantly higher inhibition on O2•- and 1O2 in SnZVI@PBC/PS system in comparison with other control systems (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). On the other hand, Fig. 4I presented that the dechlorination and mineralization efficiencies for TCE were only 35.1% and 21.4% in nZVI/PS system, while amazingly increased to 90.6% and 85.4% during SnZVI@PBC/PS process with corresponding kobs respectively enhanced by 8.7- and 8.0-fold (SI Appendix, Figs. S16 and S17), highlighting the efficient utilization of ROS in SnZVI@PBC/PS system.

Additionally, prior reaches have confirmed that excellent adsorbability of carbon support could avoid ineffective consumption of ROS via bridge its distance to target contaminants (68). Therefore, DFT calculations were employed for analyzing the role of adsorption in SnZVI@PBC/PS system on ROS generation and utilization. As observed from SI Appendix, Fig. S18, the SnZVI/PS system possessed higher adsorption energy (Eads) compared to nZVI/PS system, suggesting that sulfidation promoted nZVI to TCE adsorption (69, 70). Furthermore, the Eads of SnZVI@PBC/PS system (−4.5 eV) was significantly higher than that of SnZVI@BC/PS (−2.4 eV) and other two systems, indicating that PBC had more excellent affinity to TCE. The electrostatic potential (ESP) of these four systems was further calculated (71). It was known that the PS molecule presented strong negative potential around its atoms. As presented in Fig. 5, the ESP of nZVI generally showed negative potential trend, indicating that the electron density between nZVI and PS was low, which was not conducive to PS binding on the nZVI. In contrast, the ESP distribution changed significantly after sulfidation and PBC anchoring. Specifically, the ESP of binding sites between Fe-S bond and PS showed positive potential and followed ESPSnZVI < ESPSnZVI@BC < ESPSnZVI@PBC , indicating that the binding between PS and SnZVI@PBC was most likely to happen. Apart from theoretical calculations, the experiment results also demonstrated the high PS binding performance by PBC and high TCE uptake onto SnZVI@PBC (SI Appendix, Figs. S19 and S20), further demonstrating that ineffective consumption of ROS could be effectively avoided in SnZVI@PBC/PS system.

Working Mechanism of SnZVI@PBC/PS System and the Degradation Pathway of TCE.

Inspired by high dechlorination and mineralization efficiencies for TCE in SnZVI@PBC/PS system, possible decomposition pathways of TCE in SnZVI@PBC/PS system were deduced by GC-MS analysis (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Table S2). Moreover, the possible reduction pathways of TCE by SnZVI@PBC, and the possible TCE decomposition pathways in SnZVI@PBC/PS systems via first reduction with subsequent oxidation were likewise verified for aided analysis of the TCE degradation pathways in SnZVI@PBC/PS system (SI Appendix, Figs. S21 and S22). As observed from Fig. 6A, the first pathway is that the TCE could be gradually reduced to dichloroethylene (DCE), chloroethylene (VC), and ethylene through dechlorinated and hydrogenated processes, and the ethylene could be further oxidized into ethanol or formic acid. The second pathway is that the TCE could be directly oxidized to trichloromethane, which could be further oxidized into formaldehyde and formic acid. Another oxidation pathway is that the TCE could be oxidized into propylene glycol, acrolein, and acrylic acid. And such intermediates as ethanol, formic acid, and acrylic acid could finally be mineralized into CO2 and H2O.

(A) Possible degradation pathways of TCE in SnZVI@PBC/PS system. (B) Energy diagrams for ROS-initiated degradation for TCE and its main dechlorination products at the GGA/BLYP level in SnZVI@PBC/PS system.

By taking the TCE dechlorination products of DCE, VC, and ethylene in SnZVI@PBC/PS system as an example, the reactivity between ROS and TCE/dechlorination products was identified by optimizing the transition states (TSs) (70, 72). The energy difference between TSs and reactants is called activation energy (ΔEa), while the energy difference between reactants and products is called reaction heat (ΔH). As observed from Fig. 6B, compared with the ΔEa values for TCE induced by •OH (45.74 kJ/mol) and 1O2 (27.58 kJ/mol), the ΔEa value induced by O2•- (0.42 kJ/mol) was the lowest, indicating that O2•- was most likely to react with TCE. Additionally, compared with other ROS, the O2•--triggered ΔH value during the conversion of TCE to DCE was also lowest of all, which proved that the reaction process was a self-exothermic reaction. Similarly, the ΔEa values of the reaction between O2•- and dechlorination products of TCE were significantly lower than those induced by •OH and 1O2 , demonstrating that O2•- was more reactive with TCE and its dechlorination products, and thus was most likely to trigger the degradation reactions.

Overall, the possible mechanism for TCE degradation in SnZVI@PBC/PS system was proposed. In general, sulfidation and P-doped biochar could induce multiple Kirkendall effects for regulating the nanocrack formation of nZVI. Thus, abundant electrons and Fe2+ in the Fe0 core could fast transfer across the mixed shell via nanocracks for PS activation with enhanced generation and utilization of ROS (i.e. •OH , O2•- , and 1O2 ). The ROS contribution of O2•- , 1O2 , and •OH was calculated to be 77.7%, 10.9%, and 11.4%, respectively, according to mixed quenching results (Fig. 4F and SI Appendix, Fig. S23) in term of corresponding calculation methods (SI Appendix). Obviously, the O2•- which has been demonstrated most likely to trigger the TCE degradation was the dominant reactive species. And the excellent affinity of SnZVI@PBC to TCE and PS could reduce the transmission distance of ROS, enhancing the ROS utilization. Along with rapid electrons transfer, SnZVI@PBC gradually became electron-deficient and thus extracted electrons from TCE, which further indicated the electron-deficient field acted as catalytic sites for nonradical-based degradation. Notably, all these degradation mechanisms were enhanced by Kirkendall effects-triggered nanocracks, thus achieving efficient dechlorination and mineralization of TCE.

Practical Application Potential Analysis.

The elimination performance of TCE by different catalytic systems was first evaluated. As observed from SI Appendix, Fig. S24, the TCE self-decomposition was weak (6.8%), and PS alone was unable to effectively degrade TCE. Moreover, the BC and PBC possessed limited activated performance for PS to TCE degradation. From Fig. 7A, the TCE removal efficiency by as-prepared materials as adsorbents was in the order SnZVI@PBC > SnZVI@BC > SnZVI > nZVI. And from SI Appendix, Fig. S25, it could be found that the adsorptive removal efficiency of TCE by SnZVI@PBC was 7.5%, while the reductive TCE removal efficiency was 46.5%, demonstrating the existence of electron transfer process on SnZVI@PBC for TCE reduction. Furthermore, when these materials were employed as PS activators, similar trend was observed with highest TCE degradation rate obtained in SnZVI@PBC/PS system (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). These results revealed the synergistic promotions of sulfidation and PBC anchoring on nZVI for TCE adsorption and PS activation. The catalytic activity of SnZVI@PBC for different oxidants was then compared (Fig. 7B). At pH of 5.0, the SnZVI@PBC could activate H2O2, PMS, and PI to achieve 63.1%, 48.2%, and 42.3% of TCE degradation. Comparatively, 97.2% of TCE was degraded in SnZVI@PBC/PS system, and the corresponding kobs was generally enhanced by 3.8- to 6.9-fold. Subsequently, common contaminants in groundwater such as sulfadiazine (SDZ), sulfamethoxazole (SMX), carbon tetrachloride (CT), and atrazine (AT) were used for ubiquity assessment of SnZVI@PBC/PS system. As observed from Fig. 7C, this system presented significantly higher degradation efficiencies for SDZ (98.7%), SMX (90.2%), CT (70.2%), and AT (84.8%) than those of nZVI/PS system, and the corresponding pollutants decomposition rates in SnZVI@PBC/PS system were generally enhanced by 1.7- to 6.7-fold (SI Appendix, Fig. S27), demonstrating its strong adaptability to remediate organics-contaminated groundwater.

The catalytic stability of SnZVI@PBC was also explored. As observed in Fig. 7D, the TCE decomposition efficiency in SnZVI@PBC/PS system was only decreased by 7.6% after 5th run, significantly lower than that of nZVI/PS process (30.7%). To further evaluate continuous catalytic performance of SnZVI@PBC, nZVI or SnZVI@PBC (0.2 g/L) and PS (5 mM) were added to TCE-containing solution (20 mg/L). Then, 20 mg/L TCE and 5 mM PS were added into the solution after reaction for 120 min and 240 min, respectively. From the continuous catalytic results (Fig. 7E), nZVI and SnZVI@PBC could activate PS for, respectively, decreasing TCE concentration to 39.6 and 18.4 mg/L after 600-min reaction even though without addition of additional catalyst after 120 min, thus also revealing the excellent catalytic stability of SnZVI@PBC. Furthermore, the proposed system was demonstrated to possess high tolerance to such common coexisting ions as Cl- , NO3- , and SO42- in groundwater environment (Fig. 7F), and only about 10% of TCE removal was inhibited at extremely high concentration of HA (40 mg/L), thus implying excellent remediation potential of SnZVI@PBC/PS system for practical polluted groundwater.

The effect of key factors including initial pH, catalyst dosage, and PS amount on TCE removal in SnZVI@PBC/PS process was finally investigated. As depicted in Fig. 7F, the nZVI/PS system achieved optimal TCE removal (61.7%) at pH of 3.0 and 44.7% at actual groundwater pH of 8.0, while the TCE degradation efficiency was up to 93.6% at pH of 3.0 and increased to 97.2% with the increase of pH to 5.0, and was over 80.0% even at pH of 8.0 in the SnZVI@PBC/PS system. The slight decline on TCE removal at higher initial pH was ascribed to the decreased adsorption of PS according to an increase of negative charge on catalyst surface (pHpzc = 6.2 for SnZVI@PBC, SI Appendix, Fig. S28). Another possible reason was the weakening of active sites due to low iron dissolution and the formation of iron precipitates (SI Appendix, Fig. S29). As presented in Fig. 7G, the increase of catalyst amount could obviously improve the TCE degradation performance in nZVI/PS and SnZVI@PBC/PS processes. Comparatively, at the same dosage of 0.2 g/L, only 60.5% of TCE was eliminated by nZVI/PS process, while as high as 97.2% of TCE decomposition was attained by SnZVI@PBC/PS system with kobs enhanced by 3.8-fold (Fig. 7I). Furthermore, it was found that the optimal SnZVI@PBC dosage (0.25 g/L) was significantly lower than the optimal nZVI amount (2.0 g/L) but with higher TCE removal efficiency, i.e., synergistic promotions of sulfidation and PBC anchoring could reduce over 87.5% of nZVI amount but attained 4.3% higher on TCE degradation efficiency. Similarly, the SnZVI@PBC/PS system exhibited 97.2% of TCE removal at 5 mM of PS, 9.6% higher than that of nZVI/PS system at 16 mM, indicating that SnZVI@PBC/PS system could also reduce 68.8% of PS dosage with achieving higher TCE removal efficiency. Notably, a certain degree of decline (13.9%) was observed for SnZVI@PBC/PS system after further increasing PS amount to 8 mM (Fig. 7H), which was due to that superfluous PS could quench the generated SO4•- by generating S2O8•- with lower redox potential (73), and self-scavenging reactions among the excessive SO4•- also existed (70).

Conclusions

In this work, we synthesized cracked nZVI through introducing sulfidation and P-doped biochar to induce multiple Kirkendall effects. In comparison with control materials, the nanocracks on SnZVI@PBC were most obvious with penetrating the entire zone from Fe0 core to external shell. Thus, abundant electrons in the Fe0 core could fast transfer across the mixed shell via this lemon-slice-like nanocrack structure for PS activation with enhanced generation and utilization of ROS. The ROS contribution of O2•- , 1O2 , and •OH was calculated to be 77.7%, 10.9%, and 11.4%, respectively. Both free radical and nonradical pathways contributed to TCE degradation, which were further enhanced by cracked nZVI, thus achieving excellent mineralization of TCE. Moreover, the developed SnZVI@PBC/PS system possessed tremendous implications in groundwater remediation. In comparison with nZVI, this system could significantly reduce catalyst dosage (87.5%) and PS amount (68.8%) to achieve nearly complete TCE degradation, which was less affected by pH and interfering substances, but was highly stable and efficient to degradation of various common pollutants. This study advanced mechanistic understandings of multiple Kirkendall effects-triggered nanocrack formation on nZVI with rational design of catalysts. Future research should focus on the extension to other elements which could be also used to induce multiple Kirkendall effects for regulating nanocrack structure of nZVI, in order to make full use of its inherent electrons. And the Kirkendall effects-tailored nZVI-based material can be further extended to the high-performance degradation of refractory organic contaminants and the high-efficiency recovery of harmful nondegradable substances.

Materials and Methods

Materials.

Detailed information regarding utilized chemicals is described in SI Appendix.

Material Synthesis and Characterization.

With a certain amount of phosphoric acid, 10.0 g of corn straw (CS) powder was uniformly mixed, kept for 24 h with oven-dried at 80 °C. The compound was subsequently pyrolyzed for 120 min within N2 environment at 5 °C/min. After washing to neutral and drying, the phosphoric acid modified biochar (PBC) was obtained. The SnZVI@PBC compounds were synthesized via liquid-phase reduction process (SI Appendix, Fig. S30). Specifically, a specified quality of PBC was added to the solution (100 mL) containing 1.42 g of FeCl2·4H2O with mechanically stirring for 20 min. NaBH4 was then dispersed in anaerobic water and slowly added to PBC/FeCl2 suspension at 7 mL/min. Subsequently, a specified quantity of Na2S·9H2O was diffused in anaerobic water and added to the above suspension, and the formed black precipitates were magnetically separated for washing and oven-drying in a vacuum desiccator for 12 h. Thus, various SmnZVI@PxBCy,n materials were synthesized with m, x, and n representing mass ratio of S to Fe ([S/Fe]mass = 0.125, 0.25, and 0.5), phosphoric acid to CS ([P/CS]mass = 0.15, 0.3, and 0.45), and C to Fe ([C/Fe]mass = 0.1, 0.5, and 1.0), while y indicated pyrolysis temperatures (300, 500, and 700 °C). The characterization methods for as-prepared materials are presented in SI Appendix.

TCE Degradation Experiments.

In general, the batch TCE degradation experiments were conducted in brown bottles containing 40 mL of anaerobic water with sodium bicarbonate (10 mM) used as groundwater background ions. N2 was used to sweep ultrapure water for 60 min ahead to eliminate dissolved oxygen for preparing anaerobic water, and the original solution pH was modulated to 5.0 using NaOH or H2SO4. During experimental process, the original TCE concentration was set as 20 mg/L, which is within the practical concentration scope of polluted sites in China (74). Subsequently, desired amounts of nZVI or SnZVI@PBC (0.2 g/L) and Na2S2O8 (5 mM) were added to TCE-containing solution for initiating the reaction under stirring in reciprocating shaker bath (200 r/min) at room temperature. Samples (1 mL) retrieved at selected temporal intervals were instantly quenched with excess sodium thiosulfate, which were then filtered and mixed into n-hexane (1 mL) under 3 min of vortex. After that, 1 μL of the n-hexane phase with extracted TCE was employed for subsequent analyses. The detailed sampling methods for TCE and its degradation intermediates are presented in SI Appendix.

Analytical Methods.

The specific information related to analytical methods is elaborated in SI Appendix. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. The data were presented using mean values with error bars reflecting SD. Details of theoretical calculations including adsorption energy, ESP, and transition states are presented in SI Appendix.
