Central Nervous System Iron Content in Pediatric CKD
Authors: Emily J.S. Steinbach, Lucas D.G. Barrett, Chu-Yu Lee, McKade A. Poirier, Diana Zepeda-Orozco, Diana I. Jalal, Janice M.R. Staber, Levi P. Sowers, Vincent A. Magnotta, Ellen van der Plas, Lyndsay A. Harshman
Published: 2025

Introduction

Approximately 50% of all pediatric chronic kidney disease (CKD) diagnoses are due to congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract.1 Half of all patients with CKD with congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract suffer from progressive CKD associated with multisystem sequelae, including disruptions in iron homeostasis and anemia, which occur alongside peak periods of dynamic neurodevelopmental and neurocognitive maturation.2 We have shown focal brain volumetric reductions in the gray matter of patients with CKD relative to their peers, using structural magnetic resonance imaging.3, 4, 5 These differences became more prominent in adolescence, suggesting a divergence from typical brain development in CKD.5,6 The mechanistic basis for these neurodevelopmental changes remains unclear.

CKD disrupts iron homeostasis through alterations in hepcidin metabolism, leading to iron deficiency anemia in patients with CKD, while simultaneously causing excess intracellular iron storage that generates harmful reactive oxygen species.7 Because iron is crucial for brain functions, including mitochondrial electron transport, oxygen handling, neurotransmission, and myelination, these systemic iron metabolic abnormalities in patients with CKD can negatively impact the brain by promoting free radical production, lipid peroxidation, neurotoxicity, neurodegeneration, and neuroinflammation.8,9 Therefore, it is conceivable that systemic aberrations in iron metabolism may negatively impact the brain in persons with CKD.

Quantitative T2∗-weighted magnetic resonance imaging techniques are used to monitor hepatic, cardiac, and pancreatic iron overload states.S1 Microscopic and mesoscopic paramagnetic inhomogeneities, including iron deposits, contrast agents, and deoxyhemoglobin, result in the shortening of the T2 signal.S2,S3 The most abundant paramagnetic material in the central nervous system is iron.S4 T2∗ mapping allows for the in vivo assessment of tissue-laden iron through the measurement of the T2 relaxation times of hydrogen nuclei.S2,S5 Neuroimaging studies suggest that shorter brain T2∗ relaxation times—indicative of increased brain iron content—can be seen in a variety of neurodegenerative diseases, including Parkinson’s Disease, multiple sclerosis, Huntington’s disease, and traumatic brain injury.S2,S6–S8 The use of T2∗-weighted magnetic resonance imaging sequences has not been used as a noninvasive approach for the measurement of brain iron signal as it relates to chronic diseases with disrupted iron metabolism, such as CKD.

Brain T2∗ Relaxation Times in Children With CKD Versus Comparators

The average of all brain T2∗ relaxation times (global) was not different between groups (β = −0.13, 95% confidence interval [CI] = −0.31 to 0.04, false discovery rate [FDR] = 0.29, P = 0.14) (Figure 1). Region of interest analysis for major brain regions showed that T2∗ relaxation time was shorter in the temporal and occipital lobes for the CKD group than in their comparators (β = −0.18, 95% CI = −0.34 to −0.02, FDR = 0.14, P = 0.03 and β = −0.19, 95% CI = −0.34 to −0.04, FDR = 0.14, P = 0.01) (Figure 1). Relaxation time in the fusiform gyrus (β = −0.19, 95% CI = −0.36 to –0.01, FDR = 0.14, P = 0.04), thalamus (β = −0.23, 95% CI = −0.43 to −0.03, FDR = 0.14, P = 0.02), and corpus callosum (β = −0.33, 95% CI = −0.56 to −0.10, FDR = 0.09, P < 0.01) were also shorter for the CKD cohort (Supplementary Table S1). Lower eGFR is Associated With Shorter T2∗ Relaxation Time

Lower eGFR was associated with shorter T2∗ relaxation times in the outer cortex and in the following gray matter regions: temporal gray matter (β = 0.23, 95% CI = 0.06–0.40, FDR = 0.10, P < 0.01), occipital gray matter (β = 0.19, 95% CI = 0.03–0.34, FDR = 0.10, P = 0.02), and fusiform gyrus gray matter (β = 0.24, 95% CI = 0.05–0.42, FDR = 0.14, P = 0.01) (Figure 1 and Supplementary Table S2). Lower eGFR predicted shorter T2∗ relaxation time in subcortical white matter structures, including the corpus callosum (β = 0.29, 95% CI = 0.06 to 0.52, FDR = 0.10, P = 0.02) (Supplementary Table S2).

Total Iron Binding Capacity Predicted Shorter T2∗ Relaxation Time in the Corpus Callosum

Serum iron biomarkers are shown in Table 1. Statistically significant regions of interest from Supplementary Table S1 were analyzed for correlations between T2∗ relaxation time and iron biomarkers in the CKD cohort. Lower total iron binding capacity was a predictor of shorter T2∗ relaxation time in the corpus callosum (β = 0.57, 95% CI = 0.13–1.02, P = 0.01, FDR = 0.18) (R2 = 0.82). No other associations were found between T2∗ relaxation time and iron biomarkers (Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

Iron, the most abundant transition metal in the brain, is essential for cellular homeostasis and mitochondrial function but can catalyze harmful free radical production through Fenton chemistry when present in excess.8 Brain iron accumulation is observed in neurodegenerative diseases and is cytotoxic to neural tissues because of oxidative stress–induced lipid peroxidation, protein damage, and nucleic acid lesions.S8 We hypothesized that aberrant iron metabolism associated with CKD progression may be a critical component of the so-called “kidney-brain axis” through generation of reactive oxygen species.

Children with CKD had increased brain iron content, using T2∗-weighted magnetic resonance imaging, with gray matter appearing particularly vulnerable; specifically, the greatest increases in T2∗ iron signal were seen in the temporal and occipital gray matter. Iron accumulation in gray matter structures has been linked to cognitive and behavioral dysfunction in patients with neurodegenerative diseases and aging adults.S9 We found that children with lower kidney function, as measured with eGFR, had the shortest regional T2∗ relaxation times, suggesting that worsening kidney function is associated with increased brain iron content. Our findings may serve as a basis for understanding the effects of CKD on neurodevelopmental and neurocognitive outcomes.

These results should be considered within the context of limitations. Because pediatric CKD is rare and the study was conducted at a single site, the case sample in this study was relatively modest and included only a small number of females because of increased frequency of CKD because of congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract in males. It is important to recognize that the T2∗ mapping used here represents a surrogate neuroimaging marker for tissue-based abnormalities in iron metabolism, rather than a direct measurement of brain iron content.S3 A better understanding of redox metabolism as it pertains to iron content in the CKD brain may pave the way for translational studies using T2∗ mapping for early identification of CKD-associated neurodevelopmental abnormalities.

Our study represents the first use of quantitative T2∗ mapping of the brain in the pediatric CKD population. Further investigation is critical to elucidate the relationship between altered iron metabolism and neurodevelopment in pediatric CKD.
