Effects of a WeChat-based self-help psychological and rehabilitation intervention on disease acceptance, emotional states, death attitudes, resilience, and mindfulness in breast cancer women
Authors: Xiao Guo, Jie Hu, Zhaoyu Xiong, Danli Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis quasi-experimental study aimed to evaluate the effects of a WeChat-based self-help psychological intervention combined with rehabilitation exercises on disease acceptance, psychological distress, psychological resilience, and mindfulness levels in patients with breast cancer.MethodsFrom February 2018 to February 2019, eighty patients diagnosed with breast cancer were non-randomly assigned to an observation group (n = 40) and a control group (n = 40). The control group received standard WeChat-based health education, while the observation group received an additional intervention involving self-help psychological strategies (e.g., cognitive reappraisal, gratitude journaling) and rehabilitation exercises (e.g., abdominal breathing, aerobic activity). Validated instruments including the Self-Acceptance Questionnaire, Positive and Negative Affect Scale, Death Attitude Profile-Revised (DAP-R), Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), Psychological Distress Thermometer, and Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS) were used to measure outcomes pre- and post-intervention.ResultsPost-intervention, the observation group showed significantly greater improvements in self-acceptance, positive emotions, resilience, and mindfulness compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Psychological distress, negative emotions, and fear-related death attitudes (e.g., escape acceptance, fear of death) significantly decreased in the observation group compared to control (P < 0.05). Nursing satisfaction was also significantly higher in the observation group (95%) than in the control group (75%) (P < 0.05).ConclusionWeChat-based self-help psychological interventions combined with rehabilitation exercises are effective in improving psychological resilience, mindfulness, and disease acceptance while reducing emotional distress in breast cancer patients. These results support the integration of mobile health platforms in psycho-oncological care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is a type of malignant tumor that occurs in the epithelial tissue of the breast [1]. It is the most common type of cancer among women worldwide and imposes a significant burden on public health. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), it is projected that by 2050, the number of new breast cancer cases will reach 3.2 million annually, with 1.1 million related deaths, disproportionately affecting countries with lower Human Development Index (HDI) scores [2]. In China, breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy among women. In 2021, the number of new cases reached 385,837, with 88,106 related deaths. The age-standardized incidence rate rose to 37.00 per 100,000, marking a significant increase compared to 1990 [3].

The diagnosis of breast cancer can be a traumatic and unimaginable experience for any woman, capable of disrupting every aspect of her life [4]. The large population, combined with the steadily increasing incidence rate, has made breast cancer a serious threat to the lives and health of Chinese women. Treatment for breast cancer includes surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy, with surgery being the most important method for BC patients [5]. Postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy can enhance the effects of surgical resection, stabilize immune indicators, and plays a critical role in reducing recurrence and prolonging survival [6, 7]. However, chemotherapy may also harm normal proliferative cells and often leads to various physical adverse reactions. It can also impose significant psychological burdens on patients [8]. Chemotherapy reactions vary depending on the drug type, dosage, and individual differences, but they all can cause different degrees of distress. Adverse reactions include nausea, vomiting, alopecia, insomnia, and more. Additionally, chemotherapy can affect ovarian function, leading to its decline, night sweats, weight gain, genital atrophy, and other symptoms that negatively impact the quality of life [9]. Some studies have shown that breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy tend to suffer from poor psychological conditions, often accompanied by varying degrees of fear, depression, anxiety, and other negative emotions [10]. BC patients usually undergo long chemotherapy cycles and are frequently hospitalized. They must endure surgical trauma, loss of breasts and body image changes, the pain of chemotherapy side effects, as well as financial strain and pressure from work, family, and society. These physical and psychological stressors contribute to the development of depression, pessimism, fear, and anxiety, disrupting the patients’ original biopsychosocial balance and seriously reducing their quality of life [11].

Despite advances in treatment, breast cancer patients continue to face physical and psychological complications after surgery, including fatigue, sleep disturbances, pain, and fear of recurrence. These issues significantly reduce their health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [12]. Compared to the general population and patients with other tumors, breast cancer patients experience higher levels of depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbance. This results in a lower quality of life, poor adherence to medical care, and extended hospitalization. Therefore, developing and implementing evidence-based psychological interventions to reduce cancer-related symptoms and enhance mental health is critical [13]. Clinical guidelines by the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and several breast cancer studies have claimed that addressing the long-term psychological needs of patients can improve their quality of life and overall well-being [14].

One of the ways to enhance overall patient well-being is the use of self-service psychological interventions. This approach refers to interventions where individuals receive basic training from professionals and carry out psychological adjustment using a structured intervention manual [15]. The benefits of this method include short duration, low cost, and ease of implementation. These interventions do not require high professional qualifications from facilitators, which can significantly increase patient autonomy. Moreover, self-help psychological interventions address time limitations and reduce reluctance caused by stigma, thereby improving adherence.

Compared to traditional psychological interventions—such as face-to-face therapist-led cognitive-behavioral or psychodynamic therapy sessions that often require fixed scheduling and in-person access—self-help psychological interventions offer greater flexibility, accessibility, and patient autonomy. These digital and user-driven interventions can be particularly beneficial for patients with limited access to mental health professionals, high stigma sensitivity, or time constraints.

The results of several studies have demonstrated that mobile app-based interventions for breast cancer patients offer numerous benefits, including improving informational needs, increasing physical activity, reducing anxiety and nervousness, boosting confidence, and enhancing personal satisfaction [4, 16, 17]. With the advancement of digital technologies, mobile platforms such as WeChat have become effective tools for delivering health interventions. With over 1.09 billion daily users in China, WeChat provides an ideal platform for delivering health information and psychological support [18]. Additionally, another study showed that nursing support delivered via WeChat improved physical conditions, reduced anxiety and depression, and enhanced social support among post-surgical patients [19]. Also, Lufang Chen et al. (2022) state in their research that WeChat platform-based interactive continuous nursing intervention can help patients master comprehensive self-management skills to achieve good control of the illness, improve their satisfaction toward nursing services, and lower the risk of adverse outcome [20].

Despite these promising findings, research gaps remain regarding digital platform–based self-help psychological interventions. The concept of “self-help psychological intervention” lacks a clear definition, and its distinctions from traditional methods are not yet thoroughly explored. Moreover, the quality of health information disseminated through public WeChat accounts is variable and requires evaluation and improvement [18]. In addition to psychological support, rehabilitation exercise is a vital component of postoperative care for breast cancer patients. According to international guidelines, including those from the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), rehabilitation programs should include at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week—such as walking, aerobics, or yoga—as well as breathing and flexibility exercises. These exercises aim to improve patients’ physical function, reduce fatigue, alleviate lymphedema, and enhance psychological well-being. Their inclusion is based on criteria such as feasibility, safety, and evidence from previous research demonstrating their positive impact on quality of life and emotional regulation in cancer survivors [21].

Recent evidence suggests that integrating self-help psychological interventions with structured rehabilitation exercises can provide synergistic benefits for breast cancer patients. While psychological strategies such as cognitive reappraisal and gratitude journaling enhance emotional regulation and coping, physical exercises including breathing and moderate-intensity aerobic activity contribute to improved physical functioning, reduced fatigue, and elevated mood. Studies have shown that combined interventions addressing both mind and body yield superior outcomes compared to single-modality approaches in oncology care [12, 13]. This dual approach is particularly relevant in the context of mobile health platforms, where personalized, accessible care can be delivered effectively through applications like WeChat [12, 13].

Mobile health (mHealth) technologies have emerged as an accessible tool to deliver psychological and rehabilitative support for cancer survivors. Several systematic reviews have demonstrated the potential of mHealth interventions to improve quality of life and reduce psychological distress in patients with breast cancer, though findings remain heterogeneous across age groups and intervention formats [22, 23]. Despite the rapid growth of digital health, relatively few studies have specifically evaluated WeChat-based interventions in this population. Existing WeChat programs have mainly emphasized health education or symptom monitoring, with limited integration of structured psychological techniques such as gratitude journaling or music therapy. Furthermore, concerns remain regarding the accessibility of such digital platforms for older adults, who may face barriers related to usability, digital literacy, and physical limitations [24, 25]. Addressing these challenges is essential to ensure equitable access and enhance generalizability of mHealth interventions across diverse patient populations.

However, there are few related research reports, and its application value needs to be further demonstrated. This paper presented a WeChat-based self-help psychological intervention and rehabilitation exercise model to guide the post-operative rehabilitation of female BC patients, and examines the impact of this intervention on patients’ disease acceptance, psychological distress, psychological resilience and level of mindfulness.

Methods and materials

Study design, setting, and participants

This study was conducted at the Breast Surgery Department of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Wuhan, China, between February 2018 and February 2019. Eligible women were identified through the hospital’s surgical scheduling system and inpatient ward rosters. Once patients were medically stable after surgery and deemed suitable for participation by their attending physician, they were approached in person by a member of the research team. The researchers provided a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, and confidentiality measures. Patients were given a written informed consent form, and those who agreed to participate signed the form voluntarily. Recruitment occurred continuously until the target sample size of 80 participants was reached. Patients were then non-randomly assigned to the control or observation group using an alternating allocation method upon consent. In the control group, patients accepted WeChat-based health education intervention, while in the observation group, patients accepted WeChat-based self-help psychological intervention and rehabilitation exercises on top of the control group.

Inclusion criteria: (1) women with BC were confirmed by pathological diagnosis, and the diagnostic criteria were referred to the relevant literature [6]; (2) all the women were first-time and needed radiotherapy and chemotherapy after BC operation; (3) A consent form was completed and signed by the patients, who provided their basic information and voluntary participation in the study.

Exclusion criteria: (1) women with severe immune system or heart or renal failure; (2) women with mental illness, or mental disorders, communication disorders, or severe memory loss; (3) patients with other types of cancers; (4) patients participated in other clinical researchers at the same time.

Power analysis calculations with G*Power software indicate that (power = 80%, p = 0.05, number of groups = 2, and number of measurements = 2) 72 participants would be needed to detect an effect size of 0.60. Totally, 80 samples were assessed for eligibility, of which, 80 eligible participants allocated to the two groups.

Treatment methods

Both the control and observation groups began their respective interventions at the same time point, immediately following hospital discharge. Upon enrollment and group allocation, participants in both groups were simultaneously added to the study’s official WeChat platform. The intervention period lasted for 8 weeks and was conducted in parallel for both groups to ensure temporal consistency and reduce potential confounding due to time-related factors. This synchronized initiation and duration of the intervention helped maintain methodological rigor and comparability between the groups.

Routine nursing was provided to the control group. After enrollment, the researcher instructed the participants to follow the study’s official WeChat account and receive the WeChat platform-based health education intervention. The researchers spent about 5 min explaining the use of the WeChat platform, and sent health knowledge about the perioperative period through the WeChat platform at around 9:00 every night. The content of health education included proper diet and physiological rest, pre and post chemotherapy precautions, and activity guidance, and psychological interventions. The researchers have put knowledge about health management and post-operative care. In addition to text and pictures, voice and video formats were convenient to assist patients’ understanding and enhance their interest in learning. The health education knowledge in the WeChat platform can be re-learned at any time when patients need it. During the intervention period, patients in the control group learnt the health education content on their own, without guidance or supervision from the interventionists.

Based on the control group, the observation group was given self-service psychological intervention and rehabilitation exercise based on WeChat. After joining the WeChat group, patients followed the official WeChat account of the study. A regular member of the research team invited the subject and her husband to join the WeChat group, and the researcher conducted a one-to-one training session lasting about 30 min. The training covered the operation of the self-help psychological intervention and the do’s and don’ts. At the beginning of the training, the researcher gave the participants a uniformly printed gratitude diary with an instruction manual and a record card. The instruction manual was a summary of the six intervention strategies for the participants. The portable record card was used to record the use of the intervention strategies. The researchers explained each intervention strategy in the manual one by one, checked the participants’ mastery, and asked the participants to learn and use the contents of the manual frequently. Their use of the various interventions was recorded on portable record cards, which were collected at the end of the experiment. Participants could ask any questions they encountered during use in the WeChat group and someone would be available to answer them. During the intervention, the researcher sent out WeChat messages every evening. Telephone interviews were conducted every Sunday and the researcher reminded the participant to complete the intervention and record it, and the record card uploaded to the WeChat group was tallied every Sunday. At the end of the intervention, the same member deleted the completed intervention items from the WeChat group.

In the observation group, the self-help psychological intervention included six structured components rooted in evidence-based psychological strategies: (1) in this study used cognitive reappraisal as a self-help psychological intervention strategy to guide patients to address their emotions in the right way. Cognitive reassessment can reduce the subject’s dysphoria and prevent patients from experiencing dysphoria when dealing with stressful events. It is a means of regulating emotions. When the patient was confused, the patient could bring it up in a micro-group and then re-evaluate the issue in the light of his or her reality [8]; (2) Social support mobilization, inspired by Lazarus and Folkman’s stress-coping theory, allowed patients to actively seek emotional and instrumental support through family and peer WeChat interaction [26]. In this study, the subject’s family members were invited to join the WeChat group so that they could also learn about depression and Anxiety and. thus give more understanding, encouragement and support to the subject. Instrumental social support and emotional social support were the two main types of social support. Instrumental social support is a problem-solving or problem-solving means of coping, whereby the individual seeks material help or information from others; emotional social support is mainly the emotional support of the individual seeking understanding, encouragement or concern from others. It is an effective measure to ensure that patients seek social support to solve their problems when faced with stressful events; (3) Deep abdominal breathing induced parasympathetic activation, reducing sympathetic over-arousal and mitigating stress response [27]. patients were guided to exercise their diaphragm, mainly by taking long, deep, conscious slow breaths for 3–5 min to prolong breathing and increase abdominal rise and fall, and to perform relaxation exercises. This intervention was easy to perform, could be done lying down or sitting, had no side effects on the patient and was suitable for self-regulation; (4) Aerobic exercise: doing aerobics or walking. It was a commonly used psychological intervention, which was recognized by many scholars. Patients should engage in appropriate activities according to their physical recovery, which can reduce the bad mood caused by prolonged inactivity. Suitable aerobic exercises for patients included aerobics, walking and yoga. The aerobic training interventions will include 150 min in week. The researchers instructed the practice of aerobics during one-on-one training, evaluated the participants’ mastery before discharge, and gave targeted reinforcement. After discharge, the participants continued to refer to the intervention instruction manual and WeChat group video. Aerobic exercise was introduced to activate endorphin release and promote mood elevation [28]; (5) Music therapy, grounded in the theory of affect regulation, was used to reduce stress and enhance emotional recovery; (6) Gratitude journaling, inspired by positive psychology (Seligman et al.) [29] aimed to enhance optimism and emotional resilience by focusing on positive daily experiences [6]. Thanksgiving diary: it is an intervention way to improve the practitioners’ level of gratitude. Gratitude is a kind of positive emotion produced by the goodwill behavior of others, which is conducive to the healthy development of the individual and the society. Gratitude diary has attracted the attention of many scholars, and studies have shown that it can improve individual positive emotions [30–32]. The Thanksgiving diary mainly instructs patients to spend 10 to 15 min every day to recall three moving, happy or memorable people and events and the causes of the events, and to record what they thought at that time in the form of a gratitude diary. When participants kept a Thanksgiving diary, they reconnected with what had happened, found details in their lives to be thankful for, and unconsciously made changes as they recalled and kept a Thanksgiving diary each day. The design came from Saligman’s study, in which researchers provided participants with a uniform diary with blessings to record their memories of happy or thankful things or people [30]. During the training, participants were asked to keep a gratitude diary at least once, either in the field or during their hospital stay, with the researchers giving targeted instructions based on their writing level. The researcher asked the participants to keep at least three gratitude journals within one week of discharge.

Participants engaged with the WeChat-based psychological intervention primarily from home following hospital discharge. All modules, including gratitude journaling, cognitive reappraisal, and music therapy, were designed to be self-administered remotely. Each participant received a structured manual and daily prompts via the WeChat group, and they were encouraged to complete the psychological components in a quiet, private environment at their convenience. For the rehabilitation exercises, initial instruction and demonstration were conducted by trained medical staff during hospitalization or prior to discharge. Aerobic exercises (e.g., walking or simple aerobics) and abdominal breathing techniques were introduced in one-on-one sessions lasting approximately 30 min, during which participants practiced under supervision. After discharge, these exercises were performed independently at home. To ensure adherence and correct technique, participants submitted weekly updates via their WeChat record cards, and researchers conducted scheduled phone calls every Sunday to follow up on progress and address any questions. This hybrid approach allowed for both initial professional guidance and ongoing self-management at home.

Participants in the observation group were required to engage with all six components of the intervention—cognitive reappraisal, social support mobilization, abdominal breathing, aerobic exercise, music therapy, and gratitude journaling—at least once during the intervention period. participants were encouraged to repeat components as needed, but minimum completion of each strategy at least once was mandatory.

Observation index

Degree of self-acceptance: the self-acceptance questionnaire [31] was used to evaluate the patients’ self-acceptance before nursing (at admission) and after nursing (at discharge). There were 8 items in the questionnaire, and the total score of the questionnaire was 160.64. A higher score indicates a greater sense of self-acceptance on the part of the patient. In this study, some items of the questionnaire were revised according to the needs. The Cronbach α coefficient and test-retest reliability of the revised scale were 0.892 and 0.831 respectively. (2) Positive and negative emotion assessment: before nursing (on admission) and after nursing (discharge), the revised positive and negative emotion scale (Chinese version) [32]was used to evaluate the patients’ positive and negative emotions. The scale was classified into two dimensions, namely, positive emotion and negative emotion, with a total of 20 items. Among them, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 meant “almost nothing”, “almost nothing”, “medium”, “more” and “extremely much” respectively. The higher the score of positive emotion and negative emotion dimension, the more positive and optimistic or the more indifferent and negative patients are. The Cronbach α coefficient of the scale was 0.820. (3) Evaluation of death attitude of cancer patients. Before nursing (at admission) and after nursing (at discharge), the Chinese version of death attitude description scale-revised version Death Attitude Profile - Revised (DAP-R) was adopted to assess the attitudes towards death of cancer patients after nursing [32]. The scale included natural acceptance, approach acceptance, escape acceptance, fear of death, escape from death, a total of 32 items, using Likert5 grade scoring method. A higher score indicates a stronger attitude toward the corresponding dimension on the part of the patient. (4) Psychological resilience: the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) [33] was used to evaluate the level of psychological resilience before nursing (at admission) and after nursing (discharge). The scale included 3 dimensions and 25 items, which were classified into tenacity (13 items), self-improvement (8 items) and optimism (4 items). Each item was scored by 0–4 points, with a total score of 100. Patients with higher scores have higher psychological resilience. (5) Psychological pain thermometer: the psychological pain degree of patients was evaluated by psychological pain thermometer before nursing (on admission) and after nursing (discharge). The scale was scored on a scale ranging from 0 to 10. The degree of pain was proportional to the scale of the psychological pain thermometer, with patients scoring ≥ 4 indicating remarkable pain [34]. (6) Mindfulness level: patients’ mindfulness level was evaluated with Chinese version of mindfulness attention Awareness scale (MAAS) [35] before nursing (on admission) and after nursing (discharge). The scale was a uni-dimensional scale covering the cognitive, emotional and physical aspects of an individual’s daily life and consisted of 15 items. Each item would be scored on a scale of 1–6, i.e. 1 for always and 6 for not at all, for a total score of 150.90. A higher score indicates a higher mental capacity. (7) Evaluation of nursing satisfaction: Nursing satisfaction was assessed using the ‘Nurses’ Care Quality’ dimension of the In-Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire, developed by Wei et al., 2015 [36], which consists of 12 items evaluating nurses’ professional skills, attitudes, communication, and responsiveness. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied), yielding a total score range from 12 to 60. A higher total score indicates greater satisfaction. The Content Validity Index (CVI) of the original scale was 0.923, and the CVI in our study was 0.918. The Cronbach’s alpha for the current sample was 0.876.

Data collection and questionnaire completion

The intervention continued at home after hospital discharge, with both intervention and data collection carried out via the WeChat platform. Participants completed the study questionnaires at two time points: once at hospital admission (pre-intervention) and again at the end of the 8-weeks intervention period (post-intervention). Post-discharge, participants were instructed to complete the questionnaires at home and submit them either by uploading photos/scans via the WeChat group or returning paper copies during their scheduled follow-up visit. Weekly reminders and phone calls ensured timely completion. All questionnaires were successfully returned, and no data were missing.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0. Normality of distribution was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Independent sample t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-intervention means between groups, and paired t-tests within groups. Categorical data (e.g., satisfaction rates) were analyzed using Chi-square tests. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically meaningful.

Results

In the observation group, the age of cases ranged from 33 to 61 years, with an average age of 40.41 ± 8.82 years. Body mass index (BMI) was between 19.73 and 24.63 kg/m2, and the average BMI was 23.52 ± 5.63 kg/m2. The education level of cases in the observation group was 17 cases of elementary school and junior high school, 13 cases of high school and technical secondary school, 10 cases of junior college and above. Marital status of cases in the observation group was 31 cases were married, 9 cases were unmarried or divorced; site of disease: 17 cases of left breast, 23 cases of right breast.

The average age of the control group was 41.26 ± 9.45 years. BMI was between 20.06 and 27.08 kg/m2(average BMI = 23.61 ± 6.58 kg/m2). Education level of cases in the control group was 15 in primary and junior high school, 14 in senior high school and technical secondary school, 11 in junior college or above. Marital status of cases in the control group was 33 cases were married, 7 cases were single or divorced; disease site: 19 cases in left breast, 21 cases in right breast. Data at baseline did not show any significant differences (P > 0.05).

Before nursing, no remarkable difference was found in self-acceptance questionnaire score, positive emotion dimension score and negative emotion dimension score (all P > 0.05). After nursing, the scores of self-acceptance questionnaire and positive emotion dimension were remarkably increased, while the score of negative emotion dimension decreased. The improvement effect of the observation group was better (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

After nursing, the scores of natural acceptance and approach acceptance in the observation group were higher, while the scores of escape acceptance, fear of death and escape death in the observation group were lower (all P < 0.05, Fig. 1). Specifically, in the observation group, natural acceptance scores increased from 5.3 ± 1.1 to 6.7 ± 1.0, and approach acceptance improved from 4.9 ± 1.4 to 6.2 ± 1.2 (P < 0.001). No statistically significant pre–post changes were observed in the control group (P > 0.05). These findings reinforce the effectiveness of the intervention in modifying maladaptive death-related attitudes.

Summary of results

In summary, the results of this study demonstrate that the WeChat-based self-help psychological and rehabilitation intervention led to significant improvements across multiple psychological and behavioral domains among breast cancer patients in the intervention group compared to the control group. Specifically, patients in the intervention group showed significantly higher levels of self-acceptance, positive emotions, psychological resilience (including tenacity, self-improvement, and optimism), and mindfulness. Furthermore, they experienced a significant reduction in negative emotions, psychological distress, and death-related anxiety—namely, escape acceptance and fear of death—while reporting increased levels of natural and approach acceptance. The intervention group also reported higher nursing satisfaction scores post-intervention. These findings suggest that the integrated, remotely delivered self-help intervention is both feasible and effective in enhancing psychological well-being and quality of life among breast cancer patients.

Discussion

Breast cancer is one of the most common and high-risk malignant diseases among women, characterized by rapid growth, widespread metastasis, and aggressive tumor behavior. Given the increasing disease burden and projections of rising incidence by 2050 [37], there is an urgent need to develop comprehensive clinical interventions aimed at improving both psychological and physical outcomes for patients.

One of the most significant findings of this study was the notable increase in self-acceptance within the intervention group. Self-acceptance refers to an individual’s readiness to acknowledge and embrace the reality of their illness and to value themselves unconditionally [38]. The use of a gradual information disclosure model in this study, whereby information was delivered in phases aligned with the patient’s psychological readiness, appeared to facilitate this acceptance. As a result, patients progressively internalized the reality of their diagnosis and developed a more positive perception of themselves and their condition. This finding aligns with previous studies indicating that cognitive reappraisal and gratitude journaling are effective tools for improving patients’ self-image and psychological well-being [30, 31].

Gratitude journaling likely exerted its effects by shifting attentional focus from illness-related stressors toward positive daily experiences, thereby promoting adaptive cognitive reappraisal and enhancing resilience. Prior randomized studies have shown that gratitude writing improves sleep, optimism, and well-being by reducing negative affect and fostering positive social cognitions [39, 40]. Similarly, music therapy may benefit psychological health through neurobiological mechanisms such as modulation of the autonomic nervous system and reduction of cortisol levels, as well as through psychosocial mechanisms including mood regulation and emotional expression [41]. These complementary mechanisms likely contributed to the observed improvements in positive affect, resilience, and mindfulness in the intervention group. In addition to psychological and neurobiological pathways, both gratitude journaling and music therapy may exert benefits through social and cultural mechanisms. For example, gratitude interventions among breast cancer survivors have been associated with improvements in spiritual well-being and perceived social support, suggesting that the act of expressing gratitude fosters stronger connections and relational resources [42]. Similarly, recent trials of music therapy (alone or combined with aerobic exercise) in breast cancer patients show significant positive effects on emotional state, quality of life, and sleep, particularly when the music is culturally familiar or personally meaningful and when interventions provide supportive context [43]. Taken together, these findings suggest that interpersonal, social and cultural dimensions should be considered carefully in designing digital health interventions, to enhance acceptability, engagement, and therapeutic impact in breast cancer care.

Improvements in positive emotions and reductions in negative emotions were also observed in the intervention group. These emotional changes can be attributed to the combined impact of several factors, including social support, breathing exercises, music therapy, and regular physical activity. Aerobic exercise, in particular, is known to trigger endorphin release and elevate mood [21]. Additionally, group-based interaction through the WeChat platform and the continuous responsiveness of the care team likely created a sense of security and empathy, which contributed to enhanced emotional well-being.

In terms of psychological resilience, the results demonstrated that patients in the intervention group achieved higher scores in components such as tenacity, self-improvement, and optimism. Psychological resilience is a critical factor in patients’ adaptation to treatment, side effects, and psychosocial stressors related to cancer [44, 45]. In this study, elements such as involving family members in the WeChat group, continuous training, and the provision of emotional regulation tools created a supportive environment for fostering resilience. These findings are consistent with previous research that highlights the role of social support and emotional self-regulation in strengthening resilience among cancer patients [13, 33].

Another noteworthy outcome was the improvement in mindfulness levels. Mindfulness is a state of stable, non-judgmental, present-moment awareness that helps individuals avoid impulsive emotional reactions and intrusive thoughts. In this study, simple techniques such as conscious breathing, sensory focus, and daily positive reflection were found to increase patients’ mindfulness capacity. Studies like that of Goh et al. (2017) [35] have similarly shown that even brief mindfulness interventions can significantly reduce stress and improve the quality of life in cancer patients.

Psychological distress is one of the most serious challenges faced by cancer patients. Fear of death, worries about family, and uncertainty regarding recovery often lead to anxiety and depression [46]. If left unaddressed, these psychological issues can negatively affect treatment adherence, immune function, and even survival outcomes [47]. In the present study, patients in the intervention group showed a significant reduction in distress scores, which can be attributed to the intervention’s focus on structured communication, emotional understanding, and coping skill education. This reduction was evident in both the distress thermometer scores and patients’ subjective reports.

Encouraging results were also seen in terms of death attitudes. After the intervention, scores related to fear of death, death avoidance, and escape acceptance significantly decreased in the intervention group, while scores for natural acceptance and approach acceptance increased. These changes reflect a cognitive and emotional shift in how patients confront death. The structured nature of the intervention, the opportunity for open dialogue, the activation of meaningful memories, and the reinforcement of existential meaning likely contributed to patients experiencing dignity and peace in the face of mortality [36, 48]. As Brudek et al. have noted, acceptance of death is facilitated when individuals are given opportunities to reflect, express, and reconnect with meaning in their lives.

Nursing satisfaction was also markedly higher in the intervention group. Personalized interaction via WeChat, access to multimedia resources, rapid responsiveness, and consistent follow-up made patients feel supported and valued. This sense of connection led to increased trust, reduced isolation, and a stronger sense of self-worth—findings that align with prior studies by Wu et al. (2020) and Chen et al. (2022) [19, 20].

Ultimately, the multi-layered design of the intervention was based on an integrative care model intended to meet both psychological and physical recovery needs of breast cancer patients. Components such as cognitive reappraisal, gratitude journaling, music therapy, and social support—combined with physical practices like abdominal breathing and aerobic exercise—worked synergistically to yield optimal outcomes. This multimodal, patient-centered approach is in line with current psycho-oncology recommendations that advocate for comprehensive, multidimensional care strategies [12, 14].

The combination of multiple intervention strategies in this study was based on an integrative care approach aimed at addressing both psychological and physical recovery needs of breast cancer patients. Cognitive reappraisal, gratitude journaling, music therapy, and social support activation have each demonstrated individual efficacy in improving emotional resilience and reducing anxiety and depression. Likewise, breathing exercises and moderate aerobic activity have been widely supported for their roles in reducing fatigue, enhancing mood, and improving physical function. Based on these findings, combining these complementary components aimed to provide a more holistic and patient-centered intervention, consistent with current psycho-oncology recommendations for multimodal support strategies.

In interpreting our findings, it is important to consider the age range of our participants. In the observation group, cases ranged from 33 to 61 years (mean 40.41 ± 8.82), and in the control group the mean age was 41.26 ± 9.45 years. Although the absence of patients older than 65 years may raise concerns about generalizability to older breast cancer survivors, existing evidence suggests that middle-aged and younger-old adults (up to around 60–65 years) are generally capable of adopting and benefiting from digital health platforms such as WeChat and WhatsApp. Large-scale population studies in China have shown that older adults, including those over 60 years, actively use WeChat and that such use is associated with improved subjective well-being, intergenerational support, and better mental health outcomes [49, 50]. Furthermore, research on social media use in older adults indicates that digital literacy in this age group is steadily improving and that usability barriers can often be overcome with brief orientation and family or peer support [25, 51].

Limitations

Several factors should be considered when interpreting the findings of this research. Firstly, despite the inclusion of a control group to mitigate the Hawthorne effect, it remains possible that the enhanced attention received by the intervention group contributed to their observed improvements. Secondly, the study’s reliance on the WeChat platform, while offering convenient remote delivery of the intervention, may have presented usability challenges for some participants, particularly older adults or those with lower digital literacy, potentially impacting their full engagement. Thirdly, the use of self-reported questionnaires for outcome measurement introduces the possibility of recall bias and social desirability bias, where participants might not remember information accurately or may respond in a way they perceive as more favorable. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size of 80 participants (40 in each group), recruited from a single hospital in Wuhan, limits the statistical power of our study and the generalizability of the findings to broader populations or different cultural contexts. While the alternating allocation method was used for group assignment, the non-random nature of this assignment in a single setting might also introduce selection bias. Future research with larger, multi-center, and randomized controlled trials would be beneficial to confirm these promising results and enhance their applicability. Lastly, the study did not assess certain individual psychological variables, such as pre-existing coping mechanisms or baseline emotional dispositions. These unmeasured factors could have influenced how individual participants responded to the WeChat-based self-help psychological and rehabilitation intervention.

A further limitation is that although participants were guided in gratitude journaling, we did not review or analyze the content of their entries. This limits our ability to draw deeper conclusions about how participants engaged with this component of the intervention. Future studies should incorporate qualitative analysis of journal content to complement quantitative findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this quasi-experimental study provides preliminary evidence that a WeChat-delivered self-help psychological and rehabilitation program can improve resilience, mindfulness, and emotional well-being in women with breast cancer. The qualitative review of gratitude journals and incorporation of music therapy offer further insight into the psychological mechanisms underlying these effects. However, issues of accessibility for older adults and the non-randomized design remain important limitations. Future multi-center randomized trials with age-stratified analyses and longer follow-up are warranted to confirm and extend these findings.
