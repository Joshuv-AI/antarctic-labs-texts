Bandwidth broadening in cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials via additional equivalent stiffness
Authors: Li Bo Wang, Jiu Hui Wu, Jun Fu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe fixed acoustic characteristics of unit-cell cavities fundamentally limit the broadband sound absorption performance of cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials. This study identifies that the primary cause of the fixed acoustic characteristics in unit-cell cavities lies in the acoustic rigidity boundary conditions of the cavity walls. Based on this insight, a band-pass acoustic valve structure is proposed to replace conventional acoustic rigidity boundaries. Additionally, a framework of “band-pass acoustic valve–additional equivalent stiffness–impedance matching” is introduced to broaden the bandwidth of cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials. The method addresses the dual challenges of narrowband absorption limitations and the degradation of absorption peaks caused by cavity boundary gradually transforming states. Experimental results demonstrate that, compared to traditional cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials, the proposed metamaterial incorporating band-pass acoustic valves achieves a 65% increase in sound absorption bandwidth without an increase in size. This work provides a practical approach for achieving high-efficiency broadband sound absorption under spatial constraints.Keywords: Cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials, Broadband sound absorption, Band-pass acoustic valve, Additional equivalent stiffnessSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Noise pollution, particularly low-frequency broadband noise below 500 Hz, poses significant health risks due to its slow energy attenuation and strong penetration capacity1,2. Conventional passive noise reduction structures are constrained by causality principles and inherently narrowband resonant behavior, making them ineffective for achieving compact, low-frequency broadband attenuation3. While acoustic metamaterials have emerged as a promising solution—exhibiting properties such as negative mass density, waveguide manipulation4,5 and high-efficiency sound absorption6–8—their practical application remains hindered by fundamental design limitations.

Cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials, leveraging local resonance mechanisms11, have garnered attention for their structural simplicity and low-frequency performance12–17. However, their broadband sound absorption performance is intrinsically cavity-dependent. Current strategies to broaden absorption bandwidth focus on two aspects: (i) widening individual peaks via impedance matching with porous materials or soft boundaries18–22, and (ii) increasing the number of absorption peaks by introducing multi-frequency resonators or embedded structures23–27. Despite these efforts, a critical bottleneck persists: the acoustic rigidity of cavity walls fixes the acoustic characteristics of each unit cell. This dependency inherently links the absorption frequency to cavity volume and limits the number of effective peaks to the number of physical cavities28,29. Although recent attempts at cavity boundary modulation have generated additional peaks, they suffer from narrow bandwidths and disturbance of intrinsic absorption peaks due to the gradual transformation of boundary states30.

Here, we identify that the primary cause of fixed acoustic characteristics in unit-cell cavities is the acoustic rigidity boundary condition of the cavity walls. To overcome this, we propose a band-pass acoustic valve structure to replace conventional rigid boundaries. We introduce a framework of “band-pass acoustic valve–additional equivalent stiffness–impedance matching” that enables cavity frequency-division multiplexing. This approach simultaneously addresses the dual challenges of narrowband absorption and the degradation of absorption peaks caused by boundary state transitions. The specific contributions of this work are threefold. We demonstrate that replacing rigid boundaries with band-pass characteristics introduces an additional equivalent stiffness mechanism, effectively decoupling absorption performance from physical cavity volume. Building on this mechanism, we design and optimize a mass-plate configured band-pass acoustic valve, which is further refined into an asymmetric mass-plate configuration to mitigate narrowband limitations and peak attenuation. Experimental validation confirms that a 70 mm-thick metamaterial incorporating these valves achieves significant sound absorption across 200–800 Hz, representing a 65% increase in bandwidth compared to traditional designs without any increase in form factor.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The section “Modeling and theoretical analysis” analyzes the role of acoustic rigidity boundaries in cavity dependency. The section “Structural design and discussion” details the design and mechanism of the band-pass acoustic valve. The section “Experimental validation” presents experimental validation, and the section “Conclusions” summarizes the findings.

Modeling and theoretical analysis

This section conducts a theoretical analysis and finite element simulation on the acoustic characteristics of the virtual boundary and the unit-cell of cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterial containing the virtual boundary. The study reveals that the dependency of unit-cell cavities stems from the acoustic rigidity characteristics of cavity boundaries. To address this, the mechanism of additional equivalent stiffness, based on the cavity frequency division multiplexing concept, is introduced. General design principles for virtual boundary acoustic characteristics are also proposed to optimize the sound absorption performance of unit cells.

Modeling of the MVB

Based on the force-acoustic analogy theory, a micro-perforated plate structure with cavities can be represented as a spring-mass-damping system. In this system, the equivalent stiffness depends on the cavity volume, while the equivalent mass is determined by the air mass within the micro-perforations. Consequently, increasing the number and volume of cavities to generate more low-frequency resonance peaks is challenging under structural size constraints. This phenomenon is referred to as the cavity dependence of cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials. To address the conflict between low-frequency and broadband sound absorption, we propose the cavity frequency division multiplexing concept.

Figure 2 illustrates the sound absorption equivalent model of the MVB. Figure 2(a) depicts the state transition of the MVB. When the incident sound wave frequency falls within f1 ~ f2 or f3 ~ f4, the virtual boundary exhibits complete reflection. In this state, the MVB can be regarded as the parallel connection of Unit 1 and Unit 2. Among them, Unit 1 is composed of MPP 1, cavity 1 and acoustic rigidity boundaries of cavity 2; Unit 2 is composed of MPP 2, cavity 2 and the pillar. Thus, the MVB acts as parallel single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) systems with higher equivalent stiffness in this state, resulting in two resonance peaks. When the frequency lies within f2 ~ f3, the virtual boundary exhibits complete sound transmission, merging the cavities of Unit 1 and Unit 2 into a cavity 3. This combined structure, namely Unit 3, which is consisted with MPP1, MPP2, cavity 3 and a pillar, can be regarded as an SDOF system with lower equivalent stiffness, producing a third resonance peak. Thus, the cavity of the MVB transitions between different states across frequency ranges due to the acoustic characteristics of the virtual boundary. This method enables the generation of three resonance peaks through frequency division while maintaining the low-frequency characteristics of the peaks, effectively mitigating the conflict between resonance frequency and resonance quantity caused by cavity dependence.

Figure 4(a) illustrates the sound absorption performance of the MHB structure. The results show two absorption peaks at f1 = 260 Hz and f2 = 512 Hz under the coupling effect of Unit 1 and Unit 2, and the number of absorption peaks matches the number of cavities. Figure 4(b) presents the sound absorption performance of the MVB structure. The MVB generates three absorption peaks at f1 = 260 Hz, f2 = 512 Hz, and f3 = 350 Hz with only two cavities. The absorption peaks A2 and B2 closely resemble A1 and B1 in the MHB. Additionally, the C2 absorption peak exhibits a significantly enhanced sound absorption coefficient around 300 Hz, with similar performance to Unit 3 operating independently in the 300–400 Hz range, followed by rapid attenuation beyond 400 Hz. This behavior indicates that the virtual boundary enables Unit 1 and Unit 2 to merge into Unit 3 between 300 and 400 Hz, allowing the MVB to perform as Unit 3 in this range. Outside this frequency range, the virtual boundary separates Unit 1 and Unit 2, enabling the MVB to retain the coupled acoustic performance of the two units. As a result, the MVB structure improves overall sound absorption performance by 20% compared to the MHB structure.

Figure 4(c) illustrates the relative acoustic impedance ratio of the MHB. According to Eq. (9), optimal sound absorption performance occurs when Re(z) = 1 and Im(z) = 0. As shown in Figure 4(c), Im(z) crosses 0 at points P1, P2, and P3, with corresponding Re(z) values of 1.9, 9.1, and 1.2, respectively. At f1 = 260 Hz and f2 = 512 Hz, corresponding to points P1 and P3, the MHB exhibits absorption peaks with high absorption coefficients. However, at P2, the large Re(z) value leads to an anti-resonance phenomenon, causing an absorption valley between the two absorption peaks of the MHB.

Figure 4(d) illustrates the relative acoustic impedance ratio of the MVB. Compared to the MHB, the MVB demonstrates significant changes in acoustic impedance characteristics due to the influence of the virtual boundary. These changes are primarily reflected in the increased occurrences of Im(z) crossing 0 and the formation of a plateau with Re(z) = 1 within 300–400 Hz. The absorption peaks corresponding to points P1 and P5 remain nearly identical to those of the MHB. However, within 300–400 Hz, the virtual boundary enables the MVB to achieve near-perfect acoustic impedance at P2, producing an absorption peak at f3 = 350 Hz and maintaining efficient sound absorption across this frequency range. At P2 and P4, the ideal characteristics of the virtual boundary are limited by bandwidth constraints, resulting in anti-resonance effects at the corresponding positions.

To further reveal the reasons behind the sound absorption performance of MVB 1 and MVB 2 shown in Figure 6(b), the acoustic impedance characteristics of MVB 1 and MVB 2 are presented in Figs. 6(c) and 6(d). It can be observed that when the virtual boundary between MVB 1 and MVB 2 is in a state of complete sound reflection, the acoustic impedance characteristics of both initially align with the impedance characteristics of Unit 1 and then gradually approach those of Unit 2. These acoustic impedance characteristics further verify that the function of the band-pass characteristics of the virtual boundary is to separate and combine the cavities of the sound absorption structure within a specific frequency range, thereby facilitating a transformation in the physical structure. However, as shown in Figure 6(b), when the band-pass frequency of the virtual boundary is positioned within an inappropriate frequency range, the intervention of the virtual boundary disrupts the original sound absorption characteristics of the structure, transforming them into new structural characteristics. This results in MVB 1 merging Unit 1 and Unit 2 into Unit 3 in the A–B frequency range, causing MVB 1 to lose the efficient sound absorption peak generated by Unit 1 in this range. Instead, it exhibits the low sound absorption coefficient of Unit 3, ultimately leading to a sound absorption attenuation region 1.

In summary, the fixed acoustic impedance characteristics limit the performance of traditional passive cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials due to non-adjustable structural parameters. By replacing the acoustic rigidity boundaries with boundaries exhibiting band-pass characteristics, the cavities within the structure can be separated and merged at specific frequency ranges, thus altering the equivalent volume and stiffness of the cavities. This mechanism generates new resonance peaks in the anti-resonance region between the original resonance frequencies, which is the basis of additional equivalent stiffness derived from cavity frequency division multiplexing. To achieve optimal sound absorption performance, the band-pass range of the boundary should be positioned within the anti-resonance region of the original structure while maintaining a sufficiently wide frequency bandwidth. Furthermore, the larger the band-pass or band-stop amplitude of the boundary, the more pronounced the merging and separation effects between cavities.

Structural design and discussion

Based on the general conclusion of virtual boundary acoustic characteristic design, this section carries out the structural design of band-pass acoustic valves (BAVs) with virtual boundary acoustic characteristics. Structural optimization and corresponding result discussions are conducted to address issues such as the narrow band-pass frequency range and the degradation of the original absorption peak.

Band-pass acoustic valves design

The implementation of specific band-pass using resonance mechanisms is a widely applied method in acoustic transmission31,32 and acoustic focusing33–35. Common resonance mechanisms include local resonance36, Fabry–Pérot (FP) resonance37, and Mie resonance38. While these mechanisms enable sound wave control through compact structural designs, structures based on FP and Mie resonance mechanisms often require additional folded channels for low-frequency operation, resulting in increased structural thickness39. To address this issue, this work designs a band-pass acoustic valve using a thin mass-plate structure, facilitating sound wave transmission at specific frequencies while achieving a compact design. A detailed theoretical analysis of this structure has been provided in prior research40–42. The structural acoustic impedance is input into Eqs. (6)–(8), followed by applying Eq. (9) to calculate the theoretical sound absorption coefficient of the structure incorporating the thin mass-plate.

(4) The sum of the sound reflection coefficient and the sound transmission coefficient of the virtual boundary at any frequency is 1. In contrast, the sum of these coefficients for the BAV is less than 1 across all frequencies, with a more pronounced reduction in the B–D range.

To explain the reasons for the differences discussed above, Figure 8(c) shows the acoustic impedance characteristics of the virtual boundary and the BAV. The acoustic impedance of the virtual boundary is infinite in the frequency ranges a–b and e–f, while the BAV exhibits an acoustic impedance mismatch in the corresponding A–B and D–E ranges. However, this mismatch is insufficient to cause an absolute acoustic rigidity boundary, resulting the sound reflection coefficient in these regions being less than 1. The virtual boundary achieves perfect acoustic impedance matching in the c–d range, and the BAV approaches quasi-perfect acoustic impedance matching in the B–D range. Consequently, the sound transmission coefficient in the B–D stage is close to 1 within a narrow frequency range. The acoustic impedance characteristics of the virtual boundary in the b–c and d–e ranges switch instantaneously, whereas the acoustic impedance characteristics of the BAV in the B–C and C–D ranges change gradually, resulting in a corresponding gradual change in the sound transmission coefficient.

S2 generates an efficient absorption peak at 360 Hz, and the frequencies of the first and second original absorption peaks produced by Unit 1 and Unit 2 do not change significantly. Finally, S3 generates an efficient absorption peak at 420 Hz, but there is a noticeable backward shift in the frequency of the second original absorption peak produced by Unit 2.

To explain the observed phenomena, Figure 10(c) and 10(d) show the acoustic impedance characteristics of S1, S2, and S3. Taking S1 as an example, after introducing BAV 1 into S1, three resonance frequency points (A1, A2, A3) and two anti-resonance frequency points (B1, B2) are generated within the 100–600 Hz range. Among these, A1 and A3 correspond to the original resonance points generated by Unit 1 and Unit 2 of S1, respectively, while A2 is an additional resonance point created based on the band-pass effect of BAV 1. However, introducing additional resonance point A2 also splits the anti-resonance region of the original MHB structure into two parts on either side of A2—namely, B1 and B2. Since B1 is relatively close to A1, it significantly impacts the first original sound absorption peak at A1, leading to a forward shift in this peak and a decrease in sound absorption performance within the 250–300 Hz range. In contrast, B2 is farther from A3, resulting in minimal impact on the second original sound absorption peak. Similarly, the resonance points introduced by BAV 2 are more centrally located, and the two anti-resonance frequency points on either side are far from the original sound absorption peaks, thus causing a smaller impact. In the case of S3, the resonance point introduced is closer to A2, which has a more pronounced influence on the second original sound absorption peak.

Therefore, while BAVs can effectively control the conduction of sound waves to generate additional absorption peaks in the original anti-resonance region of the MHB, they also create new anti-resonance regions. When the frequency of the additional absorption peak is far from the original absorption peaks, the impact of the new anti-resonance region on the original absorption peaks is relatively small. To optimize this effect, when the MHB structure is designed to produce only one additional absorption peak under the influence of a BAV, the frequency of this peak should be positioned as close as possible to the midpoint between the original absorption peaks.

To further expand the bandwidth of additional absorption peaks, adopting the idea of synergetic coupling to increase the number of BAVs and thus increase the number of additional absorption peaks is a potentially feasible solution. Compared with the layout method of using two unit-cells side by side, the unit-cell nesting method shown in this paper can increase the contact surface of the two unit-cells from one to four, thereby providing convenience for setting up more BAVs.

Figure 11 illustrates the acoustic performance of sound absorption structures with varying numbers of BAVs. Figure 11(a) presents the sound reflection and transmission performance of four BAVs. The results indicate that BAV I–BAV IV produce sound transmission peaks near 340 Hz, 370 Hz, 400 Hz, and 430 Hz, respectively. Figure 11(b) shows the sound absorption performance of structures A, B, C, and D, which are composed of the MHB combined with BAV I, BAV I–BAV II, BAV I–BAV III, and BAV I–BAV IV, respectively. As the number of BAVs embedded in structures A–D increases, the corresponding number of additional absorption peaks also increases. Furthermore, the frequencies of these absorption peaks align with the sound transmission peaks of the respective BAVs. However, with increasing BAVs, the two original absorption peaks, P1 and P6, generated by Unit 1 and Unit 2, exhibit noticeable degradation. This degradation manifests as a frequency shift and a reduction in the maximum absorption coefficient. To investigate this phenomenon, Figs. 11(c) and 11(d) present the acoustic impedance characteristics of structures A–D. The results show that as the number of BAVs increases, the anti-resonance peaks AR11–AR14 of structures A–D shift towards lower frequencies, progressively approaching the first original absorption peak (P1). This shift adversely affects the acoustic impedance characteristics of the first original absorption peak, resulting in reduced sound absorption performance. Similarly, for the second original absorption peak (P6), the anti-resonance peaks AR61–AR64 of structures A–D shift towards higher frequencies, approaching P6. This shift also degrades the acoustic impedance characteristics of the second original absorption peak, reducing its sound absorption performance.

Acoustic performance of sound absorption structures with varying numbers of BAVs: a reflection and transmission characteristics of BAV I–BAV IV; b absorption performance of A–D; c real part of the acoustic impedance of A–D; d imaginary part of the acoustic impedance of A–D.

To further investigate the reasons behind the observed phenomenon, Figure 12 illustrates the acoustic characteristics of the cavity boundary as the number of BAVs increases. Figure 12(a) provides a schematic representation of the equivalent acoustic impedance of the cavity boundary. As BAVs progressively replace the acoustic rigidity boundaries of the sound absorption structure, the overall effect can be equated to an increasing number of parallel acoustic impedances between the cavities. Figs. 12(b) and 12(c) depict the real and imaginary parts of the acoustic impedance corresponding to the cavity boundary. With the increasing number of BAVs, the acoustic impedance characteristics of the cavity boundary exhibit two notable trends: (1) the number of regions with approximately perfect impedance matching gradually increases, and (2) the acoustic impedance in non-impedance matching regions decreases. The increase in approximately perfect impedance matching regions arises from introducing additional BAVs, which create more sound transmission frequency bands. Within these sound transmission frequency regions, the acoustic impedance characteristics of BAVs closely resemble those of air, resulting in enhanced impedance matching performance. Conversely, the increase in BAVs reduces the number of acoustic rigidity boundaries in the cavity, leading to a corresponding decrease in the overall acoustic impedance of the cavity boundary. Additionally, unlike the infinite acoustic impedance of an ideal acoustic rigidity boundary, the acoustic impedance of BAVs in quasi-closed regions is finite and relatively large. Consequently, as the number of parallel BAVs increases, the parallel effect further diminishes the acoustic impedance of the cavity boundary. These changes in the acoustic impedance characteristics of the cavity boundary lead to two corresponding alterations in sound transmission performance: (1) the number of sound transmission peaks increases, and (2) the sound transmission coefficient rises, as illustrated in Figure 12(d).

Acoustic characteristics of cavity boundaries with increasing numbers of BAVs: a schematic of the equivalent acoustic impedance of the cavity boundary; b real part of the acoustic impedance of the cavity boundary; c imaginary part of the acoustic impedance of the cavity boundary; d sound transmission characteristics of the cavity boundary.

In summary, increasing the number of BAVs consist with a thin plate and a mass block is a double-edged sword for the performance of sound absorption structures. From the perspective of additional equivalent stiffness, introducing more BAVs can generate more additional absorption peaks, increasing the absorption bandwidth of sound absorption structures. On the other hand, the original sound absorption peak of the structure deteriorates with the increase number of BAVs. The acoustic impedance of the cavity boundary decreases with the increase number of BAVs, which will cause an increase in the sound transmission coefficient in this region. This will gradually transform Unit 1 and Unit 2 from a separated state to a semi-connected state in the quasi-closed region, ultimately damaging the acoustic impedance characteristics of the original sound absorption peak region and resulting in a decrease in sound absorption performance.

Optimization design of band-pass acoustic valves

To balance the number of additional absorption peaks and preserve the characteristics of the original absorption peaks in the sound absorption structure, a feasible approach is to achieve as many band-pass frequency regions as possible by using a minimal number of parallel-arranged BAVs.

Figure 13 presents the acoustic characteristics of the sound absorption structure incorporating the optimized BAV. The optimized BAV features two asymmetrically distributed semi-circular mass blocks and a thin steel plate. Figure 13(a) compares the sound reflection and transmission characteristics of two traditional BAVs (BAV I and BAV III) and the optimized BAV (BAV V). The structural parameters for BAV I, BAV III and BAV V are detailed in Table 2. As shown in Figure 13(a), BAV V generates two band-pass regions around 340 Hz and 400 Hz. However, compared to the independent band-pass regions produced by BAV I and BAV III at similar frequencies, the sound transmission performance of BAV V is slightly diminished, particularly near 400 Hz. Figure 13(b) illustrates the sound absorption performance of structures B, E, and the MHB. Structure B combines MHB with BAV I and BAV III. In contrast, structure E integrates MHB with BAV V. Two notable differences in sound absorption performance are observed between structures B and E: (1) The sound absorption peaks of structure E around 252 Hz and 520 Hz are closer to the original absorption peaks of MHB, effectively mitigating the degradation observed in structure B; (2) While structure E produces additional absorption peaks at 340 Hz and 400 Hz, these peaks are slightly weaker than those of structure B. Notably, the additional absorption peak bandwidth near 400 Hz for structure E is significantly narrower compared to structure B. Figs. 13(c) and 13(d) depict the acoustic impedance characteristics of structures B, E, and MHB. Compared to structure B, the acoustic impedance of structure E around 252 Hz and 520 Hz aligns more closely with that of MHB, contributing to improved sound absorption performance at the original absorption peaks. Conversely, the narrower acoustic impedance matching bandwidths of structure E around 340 Hz and 400 Hz, especially near 400 Hz, result in weaker sound transmission by BAV V at these frequencies. This phenomenon adversely affects the acoustic impedance characteristics of structure E, leading to reduced sound absorption performance in the corresponding frequency regions.

Acoustic characteristics of structures B, E, and the MHB: a reflection and transmission characteristics of traditional BAVs (BAV I and BAV III) and the optimized BAV (BAV V); b absorption performance of structures B, E, and the MHB; c real part of the acoustic impedance of structures B, E, and the MHB; d imaginary part of the acoustic impedance of structures B, E, and the MHB.

Using optimized BAVs can mitigate the degradation phenomenon of the original absorption peaks; however, it may also reduce the bandwidth of additional absorption peaks. This reduction is attributed to the decrease in sound transmission performance. To elucidate the reasons behind this decrease and to further optimize the characteristics of BAVs, Figure 14 illustrates the proportion of sound energy dissipation in structure E and the vibration characteristics of BAV V.

Sound energy dissipation and vibration characteristics of structure E and BAV V: a energy dissipation distribution in each part of structure E; b equivalent density () and vertical vibration mean square displacement (RMSD) of BAV V.

Figure 14(a) shows the distribution of sound energy dissipation within structure E. It is evident that, at most frequencies, the micro-perforations remain the primary site of sound energy dissipation. However, at 400 Hz, the optimized BAV exhibits greater energy dissipation than the micro-perforations. To investigate this phenomenon further, Figure 14(b) presents the equivalent density of BAV V alongside its vertical vibration mean square displacement (RMSD). The optimized BAV demonstrates two distinct vibration peaks at 340 Hz and 400 Hz, with the maximum amplitude at 340 Hz being significantly larger than that at 400 Hz. Examining the equivalent density of the optimized BAV reveals that the equivalent density of the optimized BAV at 340 Hz is zero, indicating resonance and substantial vibration at this frequency. Similarly, at 400 Hz, the equivalent density of the BAV approaches zero, suggesting that the optimized BAV undergoes a certain degree of vibration at this frequency.

Therefore, the suboptimal vibration characteristics of the optimized BAV at 400 Hz are the primary cause of the narrowing of the additional absorption peak in the sound absorption structure. At 340 Hz, the optimized BAV facilitates significant sound transmission, leading to the merging of the cavities within the sound absorption structure. This results in most of the sound energy being dissipated through the micro-perforated plate. Additionally, a small amount of sound energy is consumed due to the damping effect of the thin plate in the optimized BAV during the vibration process. However, at 400 Hz, the vibration of the optimized BAV is not fully excited, leading to insufficient sound transmission. This insufficiency causes the cavity of the sound absorption structure to remain in a semi-connected state, which reduces the sound absorption performance of the structure. When the damping effect of the BAVs is considered, the damping related to BAV vibration compensates for the energy dissipation attenuation at micro perforations caused by insufficient sound transmission. This compensation enables the sound absorption structure to maintain a high sound absorption coefficient at 400 Hz. However, the bandwidth of the absorption peak significantly decreases with the BAV vibration characteristics.

To optimize the performance of the sound absorption structure, Figure 15 examines the effect of the thickness of the thin plate on the performance of the structure. Since the sound transmission characteristics of BAVs are fundamentally based on resonance, variations in the parameters of the thin plate lead to corresponding changes in the parameters of the mass block. When investigating the impact of the thickness of the thin plate on the performance of sound absorption structures, genetic algorithms are employed to adjust the parameters of the mass block under the given conditions. This ensures the BAV achieves the maximum sound transmission coefficient around 340 Hz and 400 Hz. Additionally, due to the constraints imposed by the thin plate and cavity size, the radius and height of the mass block are restricted to a maximum of 22.5 millimeters.

Effect of thin plate thickness on the performance of optimized BAVs in sound absorption structures: a equivalent density of optimized BAVs with plate thicknesses of Tplate=0.1 mm, 0.15 mm, and 0.2 mm; b transmission characteristics of optimized BAVs with plate thicknesses of Tplate =0.1 mm, 0.15 mm, and 0.2 mm; c absorption performance of structures with plate thicknesses of Tplate=0.1 mm, 0.15 mm, and 0.2 mm.

Figure 15(a) illustrates the equivalent density of optimized BAVs with varying thin plate thickness (Tplate). As Tplate decreases, the equivalent density smoothens and approaches zero. At Tplate values of 0.15 mm and 0.1 mm, the equivalent density is zero around 340 Hz and 400 Hz, while at 0.2 mm, this occurs only around 340 Hz. This shows that thinner plates reduce structural stiffness and enhance elastic vibration capacity, enabling multiple resonance peaks at adjacent frequencies. As shown in Figure 15(b), thinner plates increase the sound transmission coefficient across the frequency range, which is in line with the sound insulation law. The bandwidth and maximum transmission coefficient near 340 Hz and 400 Hz improve due to enhanced resonance. Figure 15(c) demonstrates the impact on sound absorption performance. Thinner plates enhance the additional absorption peak bandwidth and maximum absorption coefficient near 340 Hz and 400 Hz, but deteriorate the original absorption peak characteristics.

In summary, the “mass-plate” BAV generates narrowband sound transmission regions due to its resonance characteristics, creating a frequency division multiplexing effect within the cavity of the sound-absorbing unit-cell. However, the narrowband nature of BAV resonance limits the bandwidth of additional absorption peaks. Employing a synergetic coupling approach, where parallel-arranged BAVs replace acoustic rigidity boundaries of the unit-cell cavity, effectively increases the number of additional absorption peaks. Nonetheless, the acoustic impedance constraints of BAVs in complete sound reflection regions, along with the cavity boundary’s impedance characteristics, result in the degradation of original absorption peaks. The optimized “asymmetric mass-plate” BAV enhances the number of additional absorption peaks while preserving better cavity boundary acoustic impedance compared to traditional designs. This approach addresses the narrowband limitations of additional absorption peaks and the degradation of original absorption peaks. Moreover, reducing the thickness of the thin plate improves the vibration performance of BAVs, enhancing the additional absorption peaks. However, excessively thin plates may increase the sound transmission coefficient in sound reflection regions, leading to a decline in the performance of original absorption peaks.

Experimental results discussion

Figure 18 shows the sound absorption performance of the test sample based on the additional equivalent stiffness mechanism. It can be seen that the sound-absorbing sample generated four additional absorption peaks in the range of 310–450 Hz, based on the two inherent absorption peaks located at 235 Hz and 569 Hz. Therefore, the average sound absorption performance of the sound-absorbing sample has increased by 41% compared to the traditional sound-absorbing structure that can only produce two sound absorption peaks, and the sound absorption bandwidth has increased by 65%.

Sound absorption performance of the test sample.

From the consistency between the experimental results, theoretical results, and finite element results, the overall trend of the experimental results is quite consistent, but there are still slight deviations. The deviation of the inherent sound absorption peak is caused by the small diameter of the micro perforations and the accompanying processing errors during the 3D printing process. The deviation of the four additional absorption peaks is due to the deviation of the inherent absorption peaks, which causes a shift in the coupling frequency band, as well as the difference between the ideal conditions and the actual conditions of the thin plate mass structure during the preparation process due to processing errors of the mass block, damping problems of the thin plate, and the use of glue to construct the sample. Therefore, designing new structures or materials with simpler structures and better acoustic conduction/cutoff performance can further enhance the performance of sound-absorbing metamaterials.

Conclusions

This paper addresses the issue of narrow sound absorption bandwidth in cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials caused by cavity dependence. The mechanism of additional equivalent stiffness based on cavity frequency division multiplexing is introduced. Building on this mechanism, a band-pass acoustic valve and cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials incorporating the band-pass acoustic valve are designed and optimized. These advancements alleviate the shortcomings such as the degradation of original sound absorption peaks and the low utilization efficiency of cavities in conventional cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials. Furthermore, this study provides practical noise prevention and control solutions in aerospace, transportation, and indoor environments.

The key conclusions of this paper are as follows: The cavity dependence of cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterials primarily arises from the acoustic rigidity characteristics of cavity boundaries. Replacing the acoustic rigidity boundaries of cavities with ideal boundaries possessing band-pass characteristics can achieve the cavity frequency division multiplexing effect, thereby introducing additional equivalent stiffness into the metamaterial. This approach enhances cavity space utilization and expands the sound absorption bandwidth. Moreover, the ideal boundary must maintain sufficient bandwidth and exhibit a high sound transmission coefficient within the anti-resonance frequency region of the cavity-type sound-absorbing metamaterial. Otherwise, issues such as narrow bandwidth and low sound absorption coefficients may occur for the additional absorption peak. The band-pass acoustic valve with a mass-plate configuration can approximately simulate the acoustic characteristics of an ideal boundary. However, the narrowband characteristics of structural vibration and the synergistic coupling of band-pass acoustic valves result in the attenuation of the original absorption peaks. By employing an optimized band-pass acoustic valve with an “asymmetric mass-plate” configuration, the narrowband limitations of additional absorption peaks and the attenuation of the original absorption peaks can be effectively mitigated using the acoustic impedance tuning mechanism.
