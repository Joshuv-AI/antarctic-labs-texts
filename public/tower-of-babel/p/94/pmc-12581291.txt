Performance of artificial intelligence-assisted ultrasound elastography in classifying benign and malignant breast tumors: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Wenhan Hu, Xi Li, Yong Tang, Xinyue Mao, Chunmei Li, Yi Wang, Zhaohuan Li, Mingliang Zuo, Lixue Yin, Yan Deng, Liqiang Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrecise benign and malignant breast tumors classification is essential for effective treatment planning and outcome prognostication. Medical imaging’s capability to classify breast tumors has been greatly improved by the accelerated advancement of artificial intelligence (AI). This research presents a comprehensive evaluation of the efficiency of AI-assisted ultrasound elastography (UE) specifically applied to classify benign and malignant breast tumors for the first time.MethodsWe conducted extensive literature search in PubMed, Embase, IEEE, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang database, and China Biology Medicine disc (CBM) to identify relevant studies that applied or developed AI algorithms for classifying benign and malignant breast masses employing UE. We used bivariate mixed-effects model for statistical analysis, obtaining binary diagnostic accuracy data to generate pooled estimates (e.g., sensitivity and specificity). The Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-AI (QUADAS-AI) tool was applied to assess the methodological quality of the included research. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to verify the robustness of the findings, and Deeks’ funnel plot was employed to examine potential publication bias. Meta-regression analysis was used to investigate the sources of heterogeneity. Clinical applicability was evaluated by Fagan nomogram.ResultsThe meta-analysis comprised sixteen relevant studies. Summary estimates indicated high diagnostic accuracy: the pooled sensitivity was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.85–0.94), the pooled specificity was 0.88 (0.81–0.93), the positive likelihood ratio (PLR) was 7.5 (4.7–11.9), and the negative likelihood ratio (NLR) was 0.11 (0.07–0.18). The diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) was 67 (33–137), and the area under the summary receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.95 (0.93–0.97).ConclusionAI-assisted UE demonstrates outstanding performance in benign and malignant breast tumors classification. This study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024590031).Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-01982-w.

Introduction

Breast carcinoma constitutes the most common malignant tumor diagnosed among women globally and represents the second primary reason of cancer-associated deaths among this population [1, 2]. Early detection through advanced screening and diagnostic techniques prior to tumor progression or metastasis is clinically essential; crucially, timely intervention significantly increases cure rates, prolongs patient survival, and enhances quality of life [3–5]. Although mammography reduces breast carcinoma mortality by approximately 30% as a primary screening modality, its diagnostic accuracy remains substantially reduced in individuals with radiographically dense breast tissue [6–8]. Furthermore, elevated breast density correlates with heightened risk of malignancy. Significant variations in breast density distribution exist across racial groups, with women of Asian descent typically exhibiting denser parenchymal patterns relative to those of African and White descent [9, 10].

Ultrasound demonstrates superior diagnostic performance compared with mammography in detecting abnormalities within dense breast tissue [6]. Its affordability, accessibility, and lack of radiation hazards make it a promising technique for breast carcinoma screening [11]. To standardize imaging interpretation for practitioners and inform clinical management decisions, the American College of Radiology (ACR) established the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) Atlas [12]. Within this framework, radiologists classify abnormalities according to malignancy probability—ranging from benign to highly suspicious. This classification relies on morphological features including tumor shape, margin characteristics, and echogenicity patterns. However, the diagnosis results are largely dependent on the operator due to the frequent overlap of sonographic characteristics among benign and malignant tumors. Consequently, substantial diagnostic subjectivity is introduced, necessitating time-intensive interpretations [13, 14]. Furthermore, ultrasound exhibits high false-positive rates and limited biopsy specificity. These limitations may prompt unnecessary invasive procedures for ultimately benign lesions, representing a significant clinical concern [15]. Therefore, it is imperative to accurately distinguish between benign and malignant breast lesions.

Ultrasound elastography (UE) enhances the diagnostic specificity of B-mode ultrasound through assessing variations in tissue stiffness. This modality utilizes two principal methodologies: strain elastography (SE) and shear wave elastography (SWE) [16–18]. SE yields semi-quantitative data (e.g., strain ratio) by comparing the stiffness difference between the lesion and the adjacent healthy reference tissue caused by external force to classify benign and malignant breast masses [19]. However, SE exhibits a few limitations. Image quality exhibits marked sensitivity to compressive force intensity. Moreover, Region of Interest (ROI) delineation introduces inherent subjectivity, resulting in substantial operator-dependent variability during measurement and interpretation [20]. SWE delivers quantitative assessment of lesion rigidity by accurately tracking shear wave speed transmission through tissue [15]. Although SWE is superior to SE in terms of standardization and repeatability, its images, from acquisition to interpretation, highly depend on the operator’s skill and experience. Consequently, developing accurate automated diagnostic tools for breast tumors represents a critical research priority.

The 21st century has ushered in substantial progress in artificial intelligence (AI) applications for medical imaging diagnostics. These developments offer viable approaches to addressing the aforementioned diagnostic limitations. Machine learning (ML) has been clinically implemented for decision-support roles since the early 2010s. However, its reliance on manual feature extraction constrained early AI systems. This constraint impeded the promotion of AI systems across diverse imaging devices and limited meaningful augmentation of diagnostic capabilities in clinical practice. Transformative advances in deep learning (DL) have revolutionized medical AI development. DL facilitates automated feature extraction directly from imaging data by algorithms [21, 22]. Evidence indicates DL enhances both efficiency and consistency in medical image interpretation, achieving diagnostic sensitivity and specificity comparable to board-certified specialists [23–25].

Currently, no systematic quantitative synthesis comprehensively summarizes the evidence on AI-assisted UE for benign and malignant breast tumors classification. This research represents the first systematic review and meta-analysis to methodologically assess the efficacy of AI-enhanced UE in differentiating between benign and malignant breast lesions.

Methods

Literature search

We conducted a prospective registration at PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024590031) prior to its initiation. This meta-analysis followed the reporting standards listed in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [26].

Comprehensive electronic search was implemented in PubMed, Embase, IEEE Xplore, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang database, and China Biology Medicine disc(CBM). The search covered literature published from January 1, 2015, to September 3, 2025, examining AI applications in UE for classification of benign and malignant breast tumors. The included studies comprised both the development of novel AI algorithms and the implementation of established AI methodologies. The search strategy employed a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms. No language restrictions were applied during the initial search. When searching Chinese databases, two investigators (Hu and Li) conducted a subject search in CBM to identify relevant Chinese MeSH and free-text terms, and then combined them to form search queries for literature retrieval. The complete database-specific search strategies are detailed in Table S1.

Study eligibility

Two investigators (Hu and Li) independently screened all records through title and abstract review. Eligible studies underwent full-text reading and subsequent assessment. Disagreements during selection were settled by consulting with a third reviewer (Deng).

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Original research assessing AI-assisted UE for the classification of breast tumors as benign or malignant; (2) Histopathological verification serving as the reference standard; (3) Studies based on lesion level; (4) Sufficient data for calculating true positives (TP), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN), and true negatives (TN); (5) Full-text availability.

Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) Reviews, case reports, conference proceedings, academic dissertations, editorials, commentaries, consensus, guidelines, abstracts and animal studies; (2) Studies using histopathological slide images as input; (3) Publications with duplicate or overlapping patient cohorts; (4) Research focusing on non-classification tasks.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (Hu and Li) individually extracted the data. The extracted information included: (1) General study features comprising authorship, country, research design, sample size and the year of publication, etc.; (2) Methodological information; (3) Diagnostic accuracy metrics encompassing TP, TN, FP and FN. Specific calculation methods for diagnostic indicators are detailed in Table S2. Multiple studies stratified datasets into distinct subgroups: training sets, testing sets, validation sets, or external validation cohorts. To ensure objective performance assessment, only data from testing or validation cohorts were extracted. If a study presents both internal and external validation results, we extract only the external validation results, as they provide a more accurate representation of the model’s utility and reliability in routine clinical practice. For studies reporting multiple AI models, we selected the option in each study that was explicitly labeled as the final model with the highest performance, as quantified by the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Because AUC has the property of being independent of the classification threshold and can comprehensively evaluate the model’s discriminative ability. In cases where models had identical AUC values, the model demonstrating the highest sensitivity at predefined specificity was chosen. Extracted contingency tables are provided in Table S3.

Quality assessment

The quality of studies were independently evaluated by two researchers (Hu and Li) utilizing the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-AI (QUADAS-AI) tool [27]. This guideline—adapted from QUADAS-2 [28] and QUADAS-C [29]—evaluates methodological risk across four domains: subject selection, index test, reference standard, and work-flow. Furthermore, it assesses applicability concerns in three domains: subject selection, index test, and reference standard. Quality assessment was conducted by using RevMan 5.4. Detailed quality ratings are recorded in Table S4. If there are any differences regarding the quality assessment, we resolved through consultation with the third researcher (Deng).

Statistical analysis

In this study, a bivariate mixed-effects model was used to combine the effect sizes employing StataMP 16 software [30]. We calculated pooled estimates for sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), with results reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI). We generated the summary receiver-operating characteristic (SROC) curve and calculated AUC. All included studies conducted meta-analyses at the lesion level. Then we fitted the hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic (HSROC) curve and recorded the relevant parameters: shape parameter (Lambda) and threshold (beta) [31]. The beta estimate indicates whether the SROC is symmetric, and the Lambda estimate reflects the diagnostic value of the test. Compared with the classical SROC method, the HSROC model adopts a random effect model, fully considers the variation of each level, and reasonably considers the nonlinear relationship between sensitivity and specificity [32]. Then, We performed heterogeneity tests on the included studies. Heterogeneity between studies can be caused by threshold effects and non-threshold effects. To evaluate the potential presence of a threshold effect, the Spearman correlation coefficient was computed using Meta-Disc (version 1.4) software [33]. A statistically significant correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{p} < 0.05$$\end{document}) or the observation of a “shoulder arm” pattern in the SROC plot indicated a threshold effect. Heterogeneity caused by non-threshold effects may come from differences in study design, variations in study subjects, etc. Heterogeneity was quantified applying I2 statistics in conjunction with Cochran’s Q test [34]. If the P-value of Cochran’s Q test was < 0.05 or the I2 value was > 50%, it indicated that the heterogeneity resulted from the non-threshold effect was significant, and it was necessary to analyze the source of heterogeneity and conduct Meta-regression analysis according to the factors that may cause heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses evaluated the robustness of results, and Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test was used to assess publication bias [35]. Clinical utility was evaluated using Fagan nomogram [36]. All data extraction sheets used for the meta-analysis are presented in Table S5-9.

Results

Study selection

Through a comprehensive literature search, we obtained 799 items. After eliminating 203 duplicate entries, we excluded 338 articles during title and abstract review. Full-text scrutiny was performed on the remaining 258 articles. Sixteen studies (with 16 fourfold tables) met the eligibility criteria and were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flow diagram of study selection

Study characteristics

Thirteen research were published in English, and three were published in Chinese. All included studies were retrospective. Among them, only five studies incorporated external validation with independent datasets, while multicenter designs were employed in three studies. Five studies adopted unimodal models, contrasting with eleven using bimodal or multimodal approaches. Elastography methods were classified as SWE (n = 6), SE (n = 9) and the combination of SWE and SE (n = 1). Quality assessment

The QUADAS-AI tool was used to evaluate the quality of the included studies. Figure 2 presents detailed evaluation results: in the “Patient Selection” domain, 13 out of 16 studies were rated as low risk and 1 out of 16 studies was rated as high risk, mainly because they fail to explicitly describe the inclusion and exclusion criteria of patients. The remaining 2 out of 16 studies were rated as unclear risk due to unclear description of the dataset partitioning. In the “Index Test” domain, 11 out of 16 studies were rated as low risk and 4 out of 16 studies were rated as high risk because of insufficient external validation. The remaining 1 out of 16 studies were rated as unclear risk due to uncertainty regarding whether the interpretation of the index test results was conducted without knowledge of the reference standard result. In the “Reference Standard” domain, 12 out of 16 studies were rated as low risk and 4 out of 16 studies were rated as unclear risk because of the uncertainty regarding whether the interpretation of the reference standard result was conducted without knowledge of the index test results. In the “Flow and Timing” domain, 11 out pf 16 studies were rated as low risk and 5 out of 16 studies were rated as unclear risk due to they did not mention the time interval between the index test and the reference standard.Fig. 2Quality assessment of included studies according to QUADAS-AI

Pooled performance of AI algorithms

Our meta-analysis indicated that these AI models exhibit high classification accuracy for benign and malignant breast tumors. The pooled sensitivity was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.85–0.94) and specificity was 0.88 (0.81–0.93), achieving an AUC of 0.95 (0.93–0.97) for all AI algorithms (See Fig. 3 for details). The τ2 of sensitivity and specificity were 0.66 (0.21–2.12) and 0.91(0.38–2.20), respectively. The PLR was 7.5(4.7–11.9), the NLR was 0.11 (0.07–0.18) and the DOR was 67(33–137). Heterogeneity analysis

The results showed a Spearman correlation coefficient of −0.078 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{p} = 0.774$$\end{document}). Furthermore, the distribution of data points from the included studies in the SROC curve did not exhibit the characteristic “shoulder arm” configuration, collectively suggesting the absence of a substantial threshold effect. I2 statistics and Cochran’s Q test were used to evaluate heterogeneity: pooled sensitivity (Q = 83.25, I2 = 81.98% (73.93%–90.03%), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{p} < 0.05$$\end{document}) and pooled specificity (Q = 216,35, I2 = 93.07% (90.71%–95.42%), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{p} < 0.05$$\end{document}), suggesting that there was heterogeneity caused by non-threshold effect in the included studies.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

No substantial publication bias was detected through visual inspection of Deeks’ funnel plot (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{p} = 0.66$$\end{document}; see Fig. 5 for details). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis indicated that the pooled results remained stable without substantial fluctuations upon sequential exclusion of individual studies, supporting the robustness of the conclusions (Fig. S1). However, significant heterogeneity persisted across studies.Fig. 5Deeks’ funnel plot for publication bias

Deeks’ funnel plot for publication bias

Then, a sensitivity analysis was carried out by excluding studies with small sample size (n < 100). The results showed that the pooled sensitivity (0.91[0.85–0.94]) and pooled specificity (0.88[0.81–0.93]) did not change significantly after excluding these small-sample studies. Besides, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding high risk studies. The results showed that the pooled sensitivity (0.91[0.82–0.95]) and pooled specificity (0.87[0.77–0.93]) did not change significantly after excluding these small-sample studies. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis by including only external validation research. It was found that the pooled sensitivity (0.98[0.69–1.00]) and pooled specificity (0.79[0.59–0.90]) changed significantly after excluding internal validation studies. Therefore, the form of validation might be a source of heterogeneity.

Meta-regression analysis

The random-effects model incorporating 16 studies showed significant value in classifying breast tumors as benign or malignant, but significant heterogeneity existed between studies (I2 = 81.98% for sensitivity, I2 = 93.07% for specificity). This study employed meta-regression analysis to explore the sources of heterogeneity. Ultimately, findings from the meta-regression indicated that sample size (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\leq 300$$\end{document} or > 300) exerted a statistically significant influence on both sensitivity and specificity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{p} < 0.05$$\end{document}). Algorithm type (ML or DL) was observed to exert a significant influence on the sensitivity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{p} < 0.05$$\end{document}). Discussion

The growing adoption of AI in medical imaging has accelerated the development of various AI models designed to differentiate between benign and malignant breast lesions. To our knowledge, no prior systematic review or meta-analysis has specifically assessed the diagnostic performance of AI applied to UE for this purpose. To ensure methodological robustness, we conducted comprehensive literature search spanning both biomedical and engineering databases.

Following meticulous screening of relevant studies, AI-enhanced UE exhibited marked proficiency in distinguishing between benign and malignant breast tumors. Our analysis rigorously examines multiple influential factors, including variations in AI algorithms, modality, types of validation, cohort sizes, publication years, and geographical origins. Potential origins of heterogeneity were investigated throughout meta-regression and sensitivity analysis. A key methodological strength lies in the deployment of the QUADAS-AI tool [27] to assess quality and bias risk across included studies.

Conventional ML establishes predictive models through feature engineering and classification, demonstrating significant value in medical image analysis and clinical prediction. Key limitations manifest in two primary domains: substantial reliance on expert-guided manual feature extraction introduces subjectivity, while shallow network architectures constrain representational capacity. These constraints impede acquisition of complex pathological features in medical imaging [53]. DL emerged to address these limitations through multi-layered neural networks. This methodology employs end-to-end learning to autonomously extract high-dimensional radiomic features, thereby enhancing predictive performance [54]. However, clinical implementation of DL confronts persistent challenges. First, models may generate high-confidence errors during analysis of atypical cases [55]. Second, performance exhibits pronounced sensitivity to data quality; biased or inadequate datasets can produce skewed predictions or facilitate outcome manipulation. Third, adversarial examples constitute critical vulnerabilities—minimal input perturbations induce substantial output deviations [56–58]. Finally, opaque decision-making processes compromise clinical interpretability [59]. When clinicians cannot comprehend the process and rationale behind the AI’s diagnosis, they are unlikely to trust and accept its recommendations, particularly when these diverge from their own clinical judgment. Explainable AI (XAI) has been developed to meet this challenge [60]. Certain methodological approaches focus on constructing simplified approximations to emulate the underlying mechanisms of intricate AI systems [61]; others aim to quantify the relative contributions of various input features toward the model’s final predictions [62]. Dong et al. [63] has introduced XAI to explore the decision-making mechanism of DL models for lesion classification with newly defined regions of evidence, which helps to establish a connection between AI decision-making and the patient in the field of breast ultrasound risk assessment. Alom et al. [64] has proposed a Deep Neural Breast Cancer Detection system based on histopathological and ultrasound images. The system employed sophisticated preprocessing methods alongside a CNN framework, augmented with the interpretable Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping method to enhance visual interpretability of the decision-making process.

Malignant tissues are typically tougher than normal tissues. UE uses the characteristic stiffness variation to measure tissue rigidity, which increases diagnostic specificity. SE is a semi-quantitative method for evaluating the hardness of tumors relative to surrounding tissues [65]. The operator can use inherent physiological activity (such as respiration and cardiac pulsation) or actively press the probe to cause tissue deformation. When it comes to determining stiffness by identifying minute changes in tissue strain, SE is more sensitive than SWE [50]. However, it has the following limitations: First, SE primarily employs qualitative evaluation or semi-quantitative scoring methods (such as the Tsukuba Five-point scoring system). These methods are highly dependent on operators’ experience and subjective visual assessment [66]. Such subjectivity is particularly evident when lesion characteristics fall within the benign-malignant borderline region (e.g., scores between 3 and 4), which may significantly compromise diagnostic reliability. Through deep learning of massive expert scoring criteria, AI can achieve automated and standardized scoring of SE images, greatly increasing the evaluation’s consistency and reliability [67]. However, noise interference can affect the quality of SE images, especially when it comes to deep-seated bodily structures. Studies have proposed methods such as Robust Principal Component Analysis (RPCA) to remove random noise, significantly promoting the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and Contrast-to-Noise Ratio (CNR). This demonstrates that AI technology can effectively enhance the imaging effect of SE imaging on deep-seated tissues [68]. By contrast, SWE provides quantitative parameters to help with lesion classification. However, manual assessment of SWE parameters is susceptible to measurement error, while inconsistent interpretation criteria among devices from different manufacturers can introduce visual assessment bias [69, 70]. Furthermore, the detection efficiency is intimately linked to the lesion’s anatomical features. For example, deep lesions in thicker breast tissue may contribute to false negative misjudgment due to signal attenuation, while smaller lesions are difficult to capture due to their size falling below the system’s resolution limit [71]. In clinical practice of SWE, radiologists should manually set ROI to obtain quantitative parameters. The selection of ROI’s position, size, and shape varies by operator, resulting in significant intra- and inter-observer variability. Yu et al. [72] improved the diagnostic efficiency of SWE by developing methods to accelerate lesion segmentation and eliminate the need for empirical tumor margin selection. Besides, SWE images may be disturbed by various artifacts, such as posterior acoustic shadowing, mirrored artifacts, vertical striped or push artifacts [73]. Manual identification of artifacts and assessment of their impact are highly subjective and prone to error. Yin et al. [74] developed SWENet which can significantly suppresse the artifact problems caused by wave diffraction and noise in traditional SWE, providing a new paradigm for high-precision elastography. Therefore, the application of computer-aided technology in elastography is expected to overcome some limitations of manual assessment.

B-mode ultrasound’s ease of use has made it the most popular technique for classifying breast tumors. Although it can display key ultrasound features, including morphology, direction, margins, echogenicity, and posterior acoustic attenuation, its accuracy is still limited by the overlapping and diverse manifestations of malignant tumors on B-mode ultrasound [75, 76]. Prior research indicates malignant tissue typically exhibits greater stiffness than benign tissue [77]. By providing information of tissue stiffness, UE improves benign-malignant discrimination and has a higher specificity than B-mode imaging [20, 78]. Radiologists can improve diagnostic accuracy by integrating B-mode and elastography. Liao et al. [79] presented a CNN-based deep feature fusion model integrating B-mode and SE modalities, outperforming single-modality models. Zhang et al. [80] developed an AI architecture automatically extracting features from combined SWE and B-mode images, employing a deep polynomial network for classification. Their findings confirmed bimodal approaches capture tumor information more comprehensively than unimodal methods, enhancing diagnostic capability. Age has a significant impact on diagnosis because it is a known risk factor for breast carcinoma. In order to produce a final risk score that directs the therapy of BI-RADS 4 lesions, Zha et al. [50] developed a multimodal AI system that comprising clinical data (such as age), B-mode radiomics, and SE characteristics, which exhibited superior performance compared to unimodal AI systems. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis examining DL-assisted computer-aided diagnostic system in breast ultrasound revealed that models incorporating multimodal ultrasound data demonstrate enhanced diagnostic accuracy over those relying exclusively on B-mode imaging [81].

AI algorithms demonstrate strong consistency, whereas radiologists’ assessments exhibit subjective variability. By training on extensive datasets and optimizing algorithms, AI delivers stable and reliable outputs, reducing inter-observer variability and improving diagnostic consistency [82]. We found that in 2 studies where AI-assisted UE models and radiologists used B-mode ultrasound to classify breast masses as benign or malignant, AI models had higher specificity but lower sensitivity than radiologists [41, 42]. These results suggest that these AI models are optimized to reduce false positive rates, which is a key goal in elastography to avoid unnecessary biopsies. But the drop in sensitivity means more malignant lesions will be missed. In breast imaging, the trade-off between reducing biopsies of benign lesions and avoiding missed cancers is crucial. This reminds us that in actual clinical applications, radiologists need to make comprehensive judgments based on their own diagnostic experience and, if necessary, combine it with the output of AI models to make comprehensive judgments, rather than relying entirely on AI models for diagnosis.

The objective of model validation is to assess how effectively a model generalizes to populations originating from diverse geographical areas. It is therefore methodologically advantageous to incorporate datasets sourced from multiple independent institutions. Failure to include such external validation data may result in performance estimates that are optimistically biased, thereby undermining the broader applicability of the model. Among the studies we included, only five conducted independent external validation. The current scarcity of studies incorporating external validation may be attributed to the challenges associated with conducting multi-center research. We stress the continued critical importance of external validation for AI model development. External validation tests the model’s generalizability by evaluating its performance on an independent external dataset, verifying whether the model can maintain its performance under unknown data distributions. Through diverse external data, external validation identifies the model’s sensitivity to noise, distribution shifts, or rare cases, thereby enhancing its resistance to interference and assessing the model’s robustness.

The AI models included in our study used various types of data, including elastography features, semantic imaging features, clinical features (such as patient age) and radiomics data. These models employed either one type of these features or a combination of two or more features (See Table S10 for details). Semantic imaging features refer to the image characteristics subjectively described by radiologists through visual inspection of ultrasound images, based on their medical knowledge and clinical experience, which carry specific clinical significance. These features are not numerically measured by computers but rather represent subjective, qualitative descriptions. Common semantic features include morphology, orientation, margin, posterior acoustic features and vascular characteristics. In our included studies, three studies used semantic features in their AI models [47, 49, 50]. Radiomics employs automated, high-throughput extraction of quantitative imaging biomarkers to evaluate medical images in a rapid and consistent way, thereby supporting clinicians in their diagnostic and therapeutic decisions [83, 84]. The extracted features are broadly grouped into two types: hand-crafted features and those derived through deep learning methodologies. Hand-crafted features can be further classified into five groups, including morphological features, intensity-based features, texture features, transform-based Features, and fractal features [85, 86]. Through dimensionality reduction techniques (such as Principal Component Analysis, Linear Discriminant Analysis), highly correlated features can be reduced to a single representative feature to identify and eliminate redundancy, thereby avoiding overfitting [87]. Subsequently, the selected features are utilized to develop predictive algorithms through various ML or DL methods. It is advisable to evaluate multiple modeling strategies to identify the optimal approach tailored to the particular data characteristics. Ultimately, the robustness of the model must be rigorously assessed via external validation, with a high-performing model exhibiting consistent reproducibility and reliable generalizability across diverse cohorts.

Although our meta analysis have confirmed the excellent ability of AI-assisted UE in differentiating benign and malignant breast masses, its successful integration into routine clinical practice necessitates addressing several challenges related to workflow integration, ethical compliance, and regulatory standards. From a practical standpoint, a key priority involves developing specialized software capable of incorporating AI algorithms to support efficient interpretation and assessment of UE examinations. Such tools may offer real-time guidance during image acquisition, reducing the potential for missed lesions, and can facilitate training for less experienced practitioners. Looking ahead, these systems may further be integrated with mammography and MRI to form comprehensive decision-support platforms, offering radiologists a more holistic diagnostic workflow. On ethical and regulatory fronts, AI tools must be designed and implemented in accordance with core principles such as transparency, fairness, and patient safety. A major ethical concern involves algorithmic bias, which could result in disparities in diagnostic accuracy across different demographic or geographic populations. Mitigating such risks will require large-scale, multinational studies utilizing diverse datasets. Additionally, model interpretability remains critical to sustaining clinician and patient trust [88, 89]. Regulatory approval of AI-assisted UE software must also adhere to region-specific guidelines governing medical device safety and efficacy. Addressing these multifaceted challenges is essential to ensuring the clinically effective adoption of AI-enhanced UE in everyday practice.

There are several restrictions on this study. First, since more women in Europe and America have non-dense breast tissue, these patients more frequently undergo mammography, resulting in fewer studies from Europe and America. The vast majority of studies included in our analysis were conducted on Asian populations. There are significant differences in genetic background, lifestyle, environmental exposure, and disease spectrum among different ethnic groups. These factors may affect the pathophysiological process of breast tumors. Therefore, the findings of this study have strong evidence-based value in the Asian population, but whether they can be directly extrapolated to other ethnic groups needs to be verified by studies in more diverse populations in the future. Second, the included studies used equipment from different manufacturers, which may introduce potential measurement heterogeneity. Future multi-center studies need to pay attention to whether the current findings have cross-platform universality. Third, all included researches utilized retrospective data, which could result in missing data and selection bias. Therefore, to confirm the validity of the results, more prospective research in actual clinical settings is needed in the future.

Conclusion

After performing a preliminary quantitative synthesis of the available evidence, this systematic analysis highlights the potential of AI-assisted UE for benign and malignant breast tumors classification. However, it is worth noting that the varying quality of existing studies may affect the accuracy of AI models. Although we have identified some sources of heterogeneity in this study, the conclusion should be interpreted with caution due to the high heterogeneity. To further improve the generalizability of AI-assisted UE for benign and malignant breast lesions classification, future efforts should concentrate on carrying out more prospective, multicenter, and multi-ethnic trials.
