A new approach to evaluation and recalibration of the reference evapotranspiration models based on pan evaporation for Iran’s coastal regions
Authors: Navid Gholampoor, Pooya Mohammadi, Fereshteh Modaresi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the most important challenges for the reference evapotranspiration (ETo) estimation is the selection of the best model. Since all of the ETo models are empirical, there is no an explicit benchmark model to evaluate the others. However, based on the pan evaporation for ETo estimation, there is a principle that the pan coefficient (Kp) must be less than one. Regarding this principle as the benchmark, the aim of this study was to assess four famous ETo models including FAO Penman–Monteith, Hargreaves–Samani, modified Hargreaves–Samani and Droogers–Allen based on the Kp indirectly derived from them according to the pan evaporation, and recalibrating the best of them for Iran’s northern and southern coastal regions. For this purpose, monthly data of air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, solar radiation, precipitation, sunshine hours and pan evaporation were collected for a 30-year period (1994–2023). Results showed that the Kp derived from FAO Penman–Monteith, Hargreaves–Samani and modified Hargreaves–Samani was frequently more than one across most stations and months. In contrast, the Droogers–Allen model consistently yielded the Kp < 1 and had the lowest error compared to the FAO-56 pan coefficients in both northern and southern coastal regions. Also, regional recalibration of this model based on the FAO-56 pan coefficients led to a considerable promotion (more than 100%) based on the Nash-Sutcliffe index in almost all stations.Keywords: FAO-Penman-Monteith, Hargreaves-Samani, Droogers-Allen, Optimization, Pan CoefficientSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Reference evapotranspiration (ETo) represents the atmospheric evaporative demand over a standardized reference vegetated surface and serves as the basis for estimating crop water requirements. Therefore, accurate estimation of ETo is of paramount importance for water resources management, irrigation scheduling, managing of reservoirs, and agricultural planning, particularly in regions characterized by water scarcity.

ETo estimation can be conducted in two ways, directly by the methods like lysimetric, which has high accuracy but is costly and complex to implement and maintain, and indirectly by the methods ranging from physically based models to empirical and temperature-based approaches. These models are such as FAO-Penman-Monteith, Hargreaves-Samani, Modified Hargreaves-Samani and Droogers-Allen, Priestley–Taylor, Turc, etc. which use meteorological data for ETo estimation1,2.

Among these, the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith3 method requires a complete and high-quality dataset, including solar radiation, wind speed, humidity, and temperature, which are often unavailable or incomplete, particularly in long-term historical records; This affects the quality of the ETo estimations by this method. To overcome data limitations, simplified empirical models such as the Hargreaves–Samani, modified Hargreaves–Samani, and Droogers–Allen equations have been widely applied in diverse regions around the world, particularly in areas with limited meteorological datasets4–6. These methods rely on fewer climatic variables and are therefore attractive for operational use. However, since all of the mentioned ETo estimation methods are empirical, the main problem is which method is more accurate for ETo estimation?

Although the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith method is an empirical model, it was applied as the benchmark for evaluation of the other ETo estimation models in several studeis because of its robust physically-based formulation; For example, in the study by Raziei & Pereira7 it was compared with Hargreaves–Samani and FAO-56 Penman–Monteith temperature methods across 40 Iranian stations covering multiple climatic zones. Similarly, Pandey et al.8 assessed several empirical and temperature-based ETo models against FAO-56 Penman–Monteith across diverse agro-ecological regions in Northeast India, and Lang et al.9 evaluated eight different ETo estimation methods relative to Penman–Monteith in southwestern China. However, since the Penman- Monteith method is an empirical model, its results is not necessarily accurate for all regions. Moreover, several studies have demonstrated that the performance of reference evapotranspiration estimation methods is highly dependent on local climatic conditions, such that no single model performs best across all regions; for example, Aydin et al.10 found that in the GAP region of Turkey, the Hargreaves–Samani and Turc methods performed comparably to the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith under limited data conditions, while in Southern Italy, the Hargreaves method outperformed other temperature-based and simple methods in a warm Mediterranean climate11— highlighting climate-specific model suitability. In contrast, a spatial ranking study across Pakistan showed that the Hamon model was most appropriate in many grid locations, whereas the mass-transfer Penman-based model performed better in other climate zones12. These findings confirm that ETo model performance varies with climate, and emphasize the need for regional evaluation and calibration13,14.

An alternative and widely used indirect approach for estimating ETo is based on evaporation measurements from Class A pans. Pan evaporation (Epan) represents evaporation from a free water surface and can be converted to reference evapotranspiration through the application of a pan coefficient (Kp)3. Several empirical methods have been presented to estimate directly the pan coefficient like FAO56, Doorenbos & Pruitt, Cuenca, Snyder and Raghuwanshi & Wallender. FAO56 is one of the most famous, providing monthly values for Kp for a region based on its climatic conditions such as average wind speed, average relative humidity, and harvesting distance15.

According to the relation of ETo estimation based on the pan evaporation, the Kp represents the ratio of reference evapotranspiration to pan evaporation. Physically, evaporation from a free water surface, like a pan, generally exceeds evapotranspiration from a reference vegetated surface due to differences in surface resistance, heat storage, and aerodynamic conditions; by this definition, Kp must be less than one to ensure physical realism11. Recent studies have highlighted that many evaporation-based reference ETo models implicitly assume that the resulting pan coefficients (Kp) remain within physically acceptable limits, without explicitly verifying this condition. However, only a limited number of investigations have examined whether Kp values derived from different ETo formulations satisfy the fundamental physical constraint of Kp<1, particularly over long-term datasets16,17. This issue becomes especially a challenge when ETo is independently estimated using the models based on the meteorological data and subsequently compared with pan evaporation observations; therefore, the inconsistencies between the two approaches may lead to physically unrealistic interpretations18,19.

Although the most area of Iran lies in arid, semi-arid zones, its northern (Caspian Sea) and southern (Persian Gulf and Oman Sea) coastal regions exhibit markedly different climatic regimes, ranging from humid temperate to hot and humid subtropical conditions20, where atmospheric conditions strongly influence evaporation processes7,21–23. Moreover, no study to date has simultaneously examined northern and southern coastal stations over a multi-decadal period with explicit attention to the constraint that pan coefficients must remain below unity.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate four more applicable empirical models including: FAO-Penman-Monteith, Hargreaves-Samani, Modified Hargreaves-Samani and Droogers-Allen which use different meteorological data for ETo estimation in northern and southern coastal zones of Iran, separately. For this purpose, monthly data of air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, solar radiation, precipitation, sunshine hours as well as pan evaporation were collected for a 30-year period (1994–2023) from six coastal and also one island stations in Iran, located in the northern (Babolsar, Nowshahr, Ramsar and Bandar Anzali) and southern (Bandar Abbas, Bandar Lengeh and Kish) zones. Unlike the previous studies, where the FAO-Penman-Monteith model was considered as the benchmark, in this study, all of the models were assessed based on the Kp values derived from them according to the principle that Kp must be less than one as a fundamental benchmark. Also, regarding that the previous studies emphasized that empirical Epan–ETo models developed under inland conditions may not adequately capture local aerodynamic and moisture effects, highlighting the necessity of site-specific coefficient adjustment17,18, the best model for each of the northern and southern studied areas as well as the studied island was regionally recalibrated and optimized based on the FAO56 model for Kp by a classic optimization technique for attending the absolute optimized values of the model parameters.

Materials and methods

Study area and data

Iran, with an approximate area of 1,640,195 km², is located between 25°03’ and 39°47’ northern latitudes and 44°14’ and 63°20’ eastern longitudes. Due to its geographical position and climatic diversity, Iran encompasses humid, semi-humid, semi-arid and arid climates; this diversity is particularly evident in coastal areas along the Persian Gulf, Sea of Oman, and Caspian Sea. In these regions with specific climatic conditions such as high relative humidity, variable temperatures and coastal winds, estimation of hydrological variables like evapotranspiration is a main challenge.

In this study, a 30-year monthly data (1994–2023) of maximum, minimum and average temperature (T), precipitation (P), maximum, minimum and average relative humidity (RH), average wind speed at the height of 2 m (U2), sunshine hours (N) and pan evaporation (EP), were obtained from the Iran Meteorological Organization (https://www.irimo.ir) for seven coastal and island synoptic stations. The studied stations were including Babolsar, Nowshahr, Ramsar and Bandar Anzali along the Caspian Sea coast and Bandar Abbas, Bandar Lengeh and Kish Island along the Persian Gulf and Oman Sea coasts. These stations were selected due to their geographical locations and the availability of long period of data, making them suitable for evaluating ETo estimation methods and calculating the Kp. Empirical methods for calculation of reference evapotranspiration (ETo)

Four empirical methods were selected in this study to estimate reference ETo including, FAO-Penman-Monteith, Hargreaves-Samani, Modified Hargreaves-Samani and Droogers-Allen. These methods were chosen for their varying data requirements and applicability to data-sparse coastal environments where full meteorological datasets may be unavailable. The FAO-Penman-Monteith serves as a physically robust method, while the temperature-based Hargreaves-Samani and Modified Hargreaves-Samani offer simplicity for regions with limited measurements and Droogers-Allen incorporates precipitation to better capture humidity variations common in coastal zones. In the following, a brief description of each method, including its physical basis, advantages, limitations and performance in coastal or similar climates, is presented followed by the equation and parameter explanations.

FAO-penman-monteith method

FAO-Penman-Monteith method combines aerodynamic and energy balance principles to estimate ETo from a hypothetical grass reference crop3. It has a strong physical basis by integrating both radiative and advective components of evaporation, making it the most physically comprehensive among empirical methods. However, this method requires a complete data set of meteorological variables like, temperature, humidity, wind speed and solar radiation which may not be available at some stations, particularly in coastal regions with limited infrastructure. The previous studies suggested that its performance in coastal or similar climates is generally good in both arid and humid coastal regions due to its inclusion of humidity and wind effects, but data gaps in maritime environments can reduce accuracy, with studies in Mediterranean coastal zones showing it outperforms simpler methods after calibration, though it may have error in windy and humid settings13,14.

Hargreaves-Samani method

The Hargreaves-Samani method is based on extraterrestrial radiation and temperature range as proxies for solar radiation and humidity24. The main advantage of this method is that it requires only temperature data, making it ideal for data-sparse regions. The other advantage of this method is that it is computationally simple and widely applicable in arid/semi-arid climates where radiation dominates25. However, its limitations are that it tends to overestimate ETo in humid or windy conditions due to lack of humidity and wind inputs; also, its empirical coefficients may need regional calibration to reduce biases. The performance of this method in coastal or similar climates is reliable in arid coastal areas like Tunisia as well as southern Iran but underperforms in humid coastal zones (e.g., Mediterranean) due to unaccounted marine humidity, often requiring calibration to avoid overestimation by up to 20%7,26,27.

Modified Hargreaves-Samani method

The Modified Hargreaves-Samani method is a temperature-based adaptation of the original Hargreaves-Samani equation in which site-specific coefficients are adjusted to improve ETo estimation by considering local climatic characteristics such as solar radiation and temperature range28. By calibrating the radiation and temperature exponent coefficients, the modified form often reduces systematic bias and improves the fit to observed reference evapotranspiration compared to the original Hargreaves-Samani model, particularly in regions where the standard coefficient does not reflect local conditions29. One major advantage of Modified Hargreaves-Samani methods is that it retain the simplicity and low input data requirement of the original Hargreaves-Samani approach while offering enhanced accuracy through local calibration, making it suitable where only temperature data are available29,30. However, despite these improvements, the method remains empirical and temperature-dependent, and does not explicitly account for humidity or wind effects; its performance depends on local calibration and may vary across climates, with reduced robustness under extreme wind or arid conditions unless additional variables are included or additional calibration is performed30,31. Therefore, although modified Hargreaves model can outperform the original method under certain conditions, its transferability without local calibration is limited.

Droogers-Allen method

The Droogers-Allen method is a simplified empirical approach that combines temperature-based estimation with a precipitation term to improve reference evapotranspiration estimates under limited data conditions, and has been successfully applied in recent studies11. It is essentially a modification version of the Modified Hargreaves-Samani equation by incorporating the precipitation variable, which has significantly improved the estimation of ETo1. Since this method accounts for the effect of moisture from precipitation in estimating ETo, it can be a good choice for humid regions with limited data.

The advantages of this method are in requiring minimal data (temperature and precipitation), which can be obtained from observed or remote sensing measurements, and also better handling humidity variations than pure temperature methods. The limitations of this method are that it is empirical and may underestimate in arid conditions without calibration; although its performance in humid coastal areas with variable rainfall (e.g., outperforming Hargreaves-Samani in Mediterranean humid zones) is good, it needs calibration in arid coasts as the studies in Iranian humid regions showed it reduces biases by incorporating precipitation effects32,33.

Calculation of the pan coefficient (Kp)

The Class A evaporation pan method is one of the widely used approaches for estimating ETo due to its simplicity, low cost, and applicability in regions with limited data34. This method operates by measuring evaporation from a free water surface in the pan and applying the pan coefficient (Kp) to convert it to ETo. The Class A pan has a diameter of 4 feet and a depth of 10 inches and is mounted on a wooden base at a height of 6 inches above the ground35. To calculate the pan coefficient (Kp), a number of empirical methods were proposed such as FAO56, Doorenbos & Pruitt, Cuenca, Snyder and Raghuwanshi & Wallender15.

Results

Analysis of climatic variables

Given the influence of temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, sunshine hours and precipitation on evapotranspiration and consequently on the pan coefficient, the variations of these variables over the 30-year period (1994–2023) at the study stations were evaluated and analyzed before calculating and analyzing the pan coefficient.

Air temperature

Figure 2 shows the monthly averages of minimum (Fig. 2a), mean (Fig. 2b) and maximum (Fig. 2c) temperatures at Iran’s coastal stations during the study period (1994–2023). The minimum temperature (Fig. 2a) ranged between 4.5 and 24.0 °C in northern stations and 12.0–31.5 °C in southern stations, with the lowest values in winter (January and February) and the highest in summer (July and August). The mean temperature (Fig. 2b) ranged between 8.0 and 30.0 °C in northern regions and 17.0–35.0 °C in southern regions, peaking in the warm months (June, July and August) in the south. The maximum temperature (Fig. 2c) ranged between 10.5 and 31.5 °C in northern stations and 23.0–39.0 °C in southern stations, with peaks in July and August. These differences are attributed to the cold and humid climate in the north and the warm and humid climate in the south of Iran.

Monthly averages of minimum (a), mean (b) and maximum (c) temperatures at Iran’s coastal stations during the period (1994–2023).

Relative humidity

Relative humidity (RH), by affecting vapor pressure, is one of the key factors in reducing or increasing evaporation rates44. Figure 3 shows the monthly averages of minimum (Fig. 3a), mean (Fig. 3b) and maximum (Fig. 3c) RH during the study period (1994–2023). The minimum RH (Fig. 3a) ranged between 60% and 77% in northern stations and 35%–55% in southern stations, with the lowest values observed in spring (May and June) for the north and in summer (June to September) for the south. The mean RH (Fig. 3b) ranged between 74% and 87% in northern regions, peaking in autumn and winter, and between 57% and 72% in southern regions, with a decrease during warm months. Similarly, the maximum RH (Fig. 3c) ranged between 85% and 95% in northern stations (with two peaks in March and October) and 72%–90% in southern stations (with a decrease in summer).

Monthly averages of minimum (a), mean (b) and maximum (c) relative humidity at Iran’s coastal stations during the period (1994–2023).

Wind speed and sunshine hours

Wind speed and sunshine hours are critical factors in providing energy and facilitating water vapor transport in the evapotranspiration process35. Figure 4 shows the monthly averages of wind speed (Fig. 4a) and sunshine hours (Fig. 4b) at Iran’s coastal stations during the study period (1994–2023). The average of daily wind speed (Fig. 4a) in southern stations ranged between 2.0 and 3.6 m/s, with a peak in spring (2.5–3.6 m/s) particularly noticeable in Kish, which is higher than the northern stations (1.0–2.1 m/s). Similarly, the average daily sunshine hours (Fig. 4b) indicate that the southern stations experienced 8–11 h of sunshine, with peaks in May and June, significantly higher than northern stations, which recorded 3–9 h of sunshine, peaking from June to August.

Assessment of pan coefficients derived from the studied methods

In this section, the monthly pan coefficient (Kp) of the FAO-56 method as well as the monthly Kp values derived from the four studied reference evapotranspiration models were assessed using violin plots for seven coastal stations over the period 1994–2023. The analysis using violin plot exhibits the statistical characteristics of the Kp values, including dispersion, skewness, and the frequency of physically realistic values (Kp < 1); so that the thick and thin sections of each violin imply more and less frequency of the values, respectively, while the more elongation of each violin expresses the more dispersion of the Kp values in that violin. The skewness of the Kp values in each violin also can be inferred based on the degree of symmetry of violins. This approach allows a more comprehensive evaluation of model stability and physical consistency across different climatic settings.

FAO-56 pan coefficient

The monthly FAO-56 pan coefficients obtained from the Eqs. 6 and 7 for the studied stations (Fig. 6) shows that in all of the months and all stations, the Kp values are less than one, which is physically acceptable. However, at the northern stations, the narrower and more concentrated shape of violins indicates less data dispersion, greater stability of climatic conditions, and relative uniformity of the evaporation pan coefficient behavior over different years. In contrast, the wider violin shapes at southern stations indicate more data dispersion and higher annual variability of the evaporation pan coefficient, which could be due to more severe temperature fluctuations, more noticeable changes in relative humidity, wind speed changes, and the occurrence of extreme weather events in the warm southern climate of Iran.

The monthly dispersion of FAO-56 pan coefficient values at seven coastal stations in Iran over 1994–2023.

Also, comparison of the violins at the northern and southern stations indicates that in general, the stations located on the northern coasts of Iran, including Babolsar, Nowshahr, Ramsar, and Bandar Anzali, had lower values of the evaporation pan coefficient than southern stations in most months of year, which can be attributed to the humid Caspian climate, higher relative humidity, greater cloud cover, more abundant rainfall, and reduced intensity of direct solar radiation in these areas.

FAO-Penman-Monteith model

Assessing the monthly pan coefficients (Kp) derived from FAO-Penman-Monteith method for the studied stations over the 30-year period (Fig. 7) reveals that in the stations such as Babolsar and Bandar Anzali there is a wide distribution with pronounced upper tail. This indicates a frequent occurrence of Kp values exceeding unity, especially during winter months. In some cases, extreme Kp values are observed, reflecting significant overestimation of ETo relative to pan evaporation. This behavior can be attributed to the strong sensitivity of the FAO Penman–Monteith formulation to solar radiation, wind speed, and temperature gradients, which are highly variable under the cold and humid conditions of the Caspian Sea coastline. During winter, enhanced cloud cover, high relative humidity, and reduced pan evaporation amplify discrepancies between modeled ETo and observed pan evaporation, resulting in high values of Kp.

The monthly dispersion of pan coefficient values derived from the FAO-Penman-Monteith model at seven coastal stations in Iran over 1994–2023.

In contrast, at the southern coastal stations, the Kp distributions are shorter, with mean values generally below 1. At the stations such as Bandar Abbas and Bandar Lengeh, the dispersion is limited, suggesting a more balanced interaction between meteorological inputs. The lowest dispersion of Kish Island among all stations, with Kp values almost entirely below unity, exhibits a stable and physically consistent response of the FAO Penman–Monteith model under island climatic conditions.

Also, the comparison of the monthly mean values of FAO-Penman-Monteith Kp (Kpma) with those of FAO-56 Kp (Kpoa) as the reference value with physically accepted values of Kp, reveals that in all month and all stations except Kish Island, the Kpma is higher than Kpoa. This difference is considerable from April to October.

Hargreaves-Samani Model

The violin plots of monthly Kp values derived from the Hargreaves-Samani model for the studied stations over the period 1994–2023 (Fig. 8) demonstrates the highest frequency of Kp values greater than 1, particularly in northern coastal stations. Also, in spring and summer months, the violin plots at Babolsar, Nowshahr, and Ramsar stations are characterized by long upper tails and wide interquartile ranges, indicating substantial overestimation and temporal instability.

The monthly dispersion of pan coefficient values derived from the Hargreaves-Samani model at seven coastal stations in Iran over 1994–2023.

This pronounced variability originates from the simplified structure of the model, which relies exclusively on-air temperature and diurnal temperature range as proxies for evaporative demand, while neglecting critical factors such as relative humidity, wind speed, and solar radiation. In humid coastal environments, where temperature differences are weakly correlated with actual evaporation, this limitation leads to exaggerated ETo estimates and, consequently, unrealistically high Kp values.

Although the violins in southern stations are also strength in the months of spring and summer, the dispersion of Kp values is significantly lower during autumn and winter months in these stations; so that there is a slight difference between the mean of the modeled Kp values (Kpma) and that of the FAO-56 Kp (Kpoa). This suggests that in autumn and winter months when the air humidity is lower, the effect of temperature on evaporation is greater.

Modified Hargreaves-Samani model

The Kp derived from the Modified Hargreaves-Samani model for the seven coastal stations over the period 1994–2023 (Fig. 9) shows a moderate improvement over the original Hargreaves–Samani model, reflected on the one hand, by a reduction in dispersion and fewer extreme Kp values, and on the other hand by lower difference between the mean of the modeled Kp values (Kpma) and that of the FAO-56 Kp (Kpoa), especially in southern coastal stations. This improvement is associated with the calibration of empirical coefficients, which partially compensates for climatic influences. However, in northern coastal stations, the violin plots still reveal wide distributions and a high proportion of Kp values exceeding one. Despite improved central tendency, the persistence of long upper tails suggests that calibration alone is insufficient to fully capture the combined effects of humidity, cloudiness, and aerodynamic conditions typical of the northern coastal climate. Overall, while the modified version reduces systematic overestimation compared to the original Hargreaves–Samani model, its performance remains inconsistent across different climatic zones.

The monthly dispersion of pan coefficient values derived from the Modified Hargreaves-Samani model at seven coastal stations in Iran over 1994–2023.

Droogers-Allen model

The pan coefficient (Kp) values derived from the Droogers-Allen empirical model for the seven coastal studied stations over 1994–2023 (Fig. 10) suggests relatively good accuracy of this model in terms of acceptable values of Kp, particularly during the warm months of the year for all stations, and during the other months for southern stations.

Among all evaluated models, the violin plots of Droogers–Allen method (Fig. 10) exhibits the most compact distributions and the lowest dispersion of Kp values across both northern and southern coastal stations. In northern regions, although some variability persists during cold months, the frequency and magnitude of Kp values exceeding unity are substantially lower than those observed for the other models.

In southern coastal stations, particularly at Bandar Abbas, Bandar Lengeh, and Kish, the violin plots are narrow and symmetric, with Kp values consistently remaining below 1 throughout the year. The incorporation of precipitation as an explanatory variable enables the model to implicitly account for atmospheric moisture conditions, leading to more physically realistic estimates of ETo relative to pan evaporation.

Although the Kp values derived from this model are physically acceptable (Kp<1) in almost all of months and stations, there is a significant difference between its mean values (Kpma) and those of the FAO-56 Kp (Kpoa) from April to September; but in autumn and winter months especially from November to February, a little difference is there between Kpma and Kpoa.

Also, the results of Table 2 exhibit that the performance of all models for southern stations is considerably better than the northern stations. However, based on both distributional behavior and threshold-based metrics, the Droogers–Allen model emerges as the most physically consistent and robust approach for estimating reference evapotranspiration in Iran’s coastal and island environments.

Optimization results and validation

To improve the accuracy of the Droogers-Allen model, which revealed the best performance in the previous section in diverse coastal environments, the optimization of its coefficients was conducted using the GRG Nonlinear algorithm. This process was performed separately for the northern (all four stations collectively), and the southern (Bandar Lengeh and Bandarabbas) regions as well as the Kish Island, regarding the climatic contrasts of the regions and the statistical values resulted from the previous section.

The original and optimized coefficients for each region are presented in Table 4. Compared to the original Droogers–Allen coefficients (a = 0.0013, b = 17.0, c = 0.0123, d = 0.76), substantial adjustments were obtained through regional optimization to better represent local climatic conditions; in the northern coastal region, the optimized values of a (0.0011), b (3.6392), c (0.0020) and d (0.5865) indicate a reduced influence of temperature and precipitation terms, together with a lower exponent on the temperature component, reflecting the dominant role of high humidity, frequent cloud cover, and reduced vapor pressure deficit in this humid environment. In the southern mainland coastal region (Bandar Lengeh and Bandar Abbas), the optimized coefficients (a = 0.0013, b = 3.0808, c = 0.0100, d = 0.7600) suggest a stronger contribution of precipitation relative to temperature range, while maintaining a temperature exponent close to the original formulation, consistent with hot and semi-arid coastal conditions. For Kish Island, the optimized coefficients (a = 0.0013, b = 16.7805, c = 0.0010, d = 0.7600) reveal a markedly higher b value and a substantially reduced precipitation coefficient, highlighting the distinct island microclimate dominated by marine advection, high atmospheric moisture, and limited precipitation control on evapotranspiration processes.

Discussion

This study presented a new approach to evaluate evapotranspiration models based on the pan coefficients (Kp) derived from them and applied it to assess four widely used methods, including FAO Penman–Monteith, Hargreaves–Samani, modified Hargreaves–Samani, and Droogers–Allen, across Iran’s northern and southern coastal regions over a 30-year period using six coastal stations and one island station. Unlike conventional validation studies that focused primarily on statistical agreement with a reference model—most commonly FAO Penman–Monteith—the present analysis explicitly incorporated the fundamental physical requirement that pan coefficients must remain below unity (Kp < 1). This requirement reflects the physical principle that evaporation from a free water surface exceeds evapotranspiration from a standardized vegetated reference surface3,40. By integrating this constraint, the proposed framework provides a robust basis for identifying physically inconsistent model behavior, particularly in climate-sensitive and data-limited coastal environments.

Analysis of the climatic variables derived from the 30-year observations (1994–2023) clearly indicated distinct atmospheric regimes between Iran’s northern and southern coastal stations. As demonstrated by the temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, sunshine hours, and precipitation patterns (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5), the northern coastal stations along the Caspian Sea are characterized by persistently high relative humidity, frequent cloud cover, lower sunshine duration, and smaller diurnal and seasonal temperature ranges. These conditions result in reduced vapor pressure deficits and suppressed pan evaporation, particularly during cold and transitional seasons. In contrast, the southern coastal stations exhibited significantly higher air temperatures, longer sunshine duration, stronger coastal winds, and markedly lower seasonal precipitation, leading to enhanced evaporative demand and more stable evaporation processes throughout the year. The pronounced north–south climatic contrasts identified in this study directly exhibit the observed differences in pan evaporation and model-derived ETo behavior and reinforce the strong linkage between local climatic controls and evapotranspiration dynamics. These findings are consistent with documented characteristics of humid temperate coastal climates and hot–humid to semi-arid maritime environments reported for similar regions in East Asia and the Mediterranean basin17–19.

Further analysis of climatological variables in relation to pan evaporation revealed that in humid northern stations, high atmospheric moisture substantially reduces vapor pressure deficits and suppresses free-water evaporation, resulting in lower Epan values during cold and transitional seasons. However, temperature-based and radiation-driven ETo models remain highly sensitive to diurnal temperature range and solar input, which can lead to inflated ETo estimates and, consequently, unrealistically large Kp values. This mismatch has been widely reported in humid and coastal climates, where simplified empirical models tend to overestimate evaporative demand when humidity and cloudiness are not explicitly represented16,46. In contrast, southern coastal stations experience climatic conditions more favorable for stable evaporation processes, where higher wind speeds and sunshine duration enhance both Epan and ETo, thereby reducing discrepancies between modeled and reference values. Similar improvements in empirical ETo model performance under hot and relatively stable coastal climates have been reported in previous studies13,14.

The analysis of FAO-56 pan coefficient clearly indicated that the all its values in all months and all studied stations were lower than one and physically acceptable. Also, the results showed that the behavior of the FAO evaporation pan coefficient is a direct function of the climatic conditions of the region, and the variables such as air temperature, solar radiation, wind speed, and relative humidity are considered the most important factors controlling its changes, such that the southern regions of the country, due to warmer and drier conditions, have had the highest value and the largest range of changes in this coefficient, while the northern regions, due to their humid and temperate climate, have shown the lowest value and the least dispersion.

The analysis of pan coefficients derived from the four evapotranspiration models demonstrated that model performance varied considerably across months and stations. A key outcome of this study was that only the Droogers–Allen model consistently produced Kp values below unity across all stations and months, satisfying the fundamental physical constraint of pan-based evapotranspiration estimation. In contrast, FAO Penman–Monteith, Hargreaves–Samani, and modified Hargreaves–Samani frequently generated Kp values exceeding one, particularly in humid northern stations and during cold months. The occurrence of Kp > 1 indicates that modeled ETo exceeds pan evaporation, which is physically implausible under standard conditions and has been identified as a major limitation in indirect pan-based ETo studies19,40. Similar overestimations have been reported in coastal China and Mediterranean environments when temperature-based models are applied without adequate humidity or wind corrections17,18. Therefore, rejecting models based on physical inconsistency rather than relying solely on statistical metrics represents a more robust screening strategy.

The contrasting behavior of empirical evapotranspiration models relative to the FAO Penman–Monteith benchmark has also been observed in other climatic contexts. For instance, in a Mediterranean climate in Portugal, the Hargreaves–Samani model showed strong correlation with FAO-56 Penman–Monteith estimates when appropriate calibration adjustments were applied, suggesting that temperature-based models can perform adequately in warm and thermally stable environments47. Conversely, studies in the Duero Basin (Spain) demonstrated that model performance varies strongly with seasonal and spatial factors, with rankings changing depending on climate conditions and calibration strategies48. These findings support the present study’s results, which reveal high dispersion and climate-dependent behavior for several empirical models and confirm that no single model consistently outperforms the FAO-56 benchmark across all climatic regimes.

The superior performance of the Droogers–Allen model can be attributed to its inclusion of precipitation as a proxy for atmospheric moisture, which partially compensates for the absence of direct humidity inputs. This characteristic has been shown in previous studies to improve model stability in humid and transitional climates2,32. The NRMSE and PBIAS-based intercomparison further confirmed that Droogers–Allen exhibited the lowest absolute bias relative to FAO-56 Kp values while maintaining physical consistency (Kp < 1), whereas negative CRM values for other models indicated systematic overestimation. It should be emphasized that FAO-56 Kp values were not treated as absolute truth but rather as a physically grounded reference framework. By combining statistical analysis with physical constraints, the proposed approach avoids circular validation and evaluates model behavior in accordance with established pan evaporation theory17,46.

Following model selection, regional optimization of Droogers–Allen coefficients substantially enhanced agreement between modeled ETo and pan-derived reference evapotranspiration (Epan × FAO-56 Kp), as evaluated using the Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency. Model performance improved by approximately 91.7% in the humid northern coastal region and 77.9% in the southern mainland coastal region relative to the maximum attainable performance after optimization. These improvements confirm findings from previous studies showing that regional calibration significantly enhances the accuracy of empirical ETo models in variable climates29,33,49. The enhanced performance in northern coastal stations is primarily attributed to precipitation-related adjustments that effectively mitigate underestimation under high-humidity conditions common along the Caspian coast.

Nevertheless, Kish Island exhibited persistently lower improvements compared to mainland coastal stations. This behavior reflects the island’s distinct microclimate, characterized by enhanced marine advection, high reflected radiation from surrounding water surfaces, and reduced fetch heterogeneity; also, sudden changes in weather conditions of islands compared to coastal zones can result in lower performance of the models in islands as compared to the coastal area49; Similar challenges have been reported in island and coastal-island environments, where conventional evapotranspiration models struggle to adequately represent complex aerodynamic and energy exchange processes32,40,50; for example, showed the applying wind speed together with temperature can improve model performance for ETo estimation in Kish Island45. Station-specific variations, such as greater performance gains in humid northern stations, highlight the benefits of group-based regional optimization for model transferability while emphasizing the need for tailored modeling strategies in island environments14.

Overall, the results emphasize that pan-based reference evapotranspiration estimation in coastal regions must simultaneously account for physical consistency and climatic specificity. While the physically constrained framework proposed in this study improves model selection and regional calibration, limitations remain due to the absence of direct lysimeter observations and reliance on monthly aggregated data. Future studies incorporating higher temporal resolution data and explicit wind-based formulations may further enhance evapotranspiration modeling in coastal and island environments.

Conclusion

One of the most important challenges for the reference evapotranspiration (ETo) estimation is the selection of the best model. Since all of the ETo models are empirical, there is no an explicit benchmark model to evaluate the others. This study introduced and applied a new approach based on a physically constrained framework according to the pan coefficient (Kp) value for evaluation of four reference evapotranspiration estimation methods in Iran’s northern and southern coastal regions using long-term observations. By explicitly enforcing the principle that pan coefficients must remain below unity, the analysis was conducted beyond purely statistical comparisons and enabled to identify the best physically consistent model.

In this study, four ETo estimation methods including FAO Penman–Monteith, Hargreaves–Samani, modified Hargreaves–Samani and Droogers–Allen were evaluated, where different meteorological variables are applied for ETo estimation. For this purpose, a 30-year period (1994–2023) of air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, solar radiation, precipitation, sunshine hours and pan evaporation data were collected.

The results revealed that the Kp derived from FAO Penman–Monteith, Hargreaves–Samani, and modified Hargreaves–Samani frequently exceeded unity across most stations and months. Among the evaluated methods, the Droogers–Allen model consistently satisfied physical constraints across all stations and months, making it the most suitable candidate for further refinement. Regional optimization of its coefficients substantially improved agreement with pan-derived reference evapotranspiration, with model performance increasing by approximately 91.7% in the humid northern coastal zone and about 77.9% in the southern mainland coastal stations. The analysis further demonstrated that coastal island environments, exemplified by Kish Island, exhibit distinct evaporation dynamics that differ from mainland coastal stations, highlighting the necessity of treating island stations as a separate hydro-climatic class rather than grouping them with general coastal regions.

Overall, the results highlight that evapotranspiration estimation in coastal areas requires a combination of physical consistency checks, regional calibration, and climate-specific model selection. The proposed framework provides a practical and scientifically grounded basis for improving reference evapotranspiration estimation in data-limited coastal and island environments and can support more accurate irrigation planning and water resource management.
