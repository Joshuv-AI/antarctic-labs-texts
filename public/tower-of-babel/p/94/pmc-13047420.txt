Metal Fatigue Fracture After Revision Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Retrieval Analysis
Authors: Michael A. Kurtz, Jeremy L. Gilbert, Aarti A. Shenoy, Gregg R. Klein, Arthur L. Malkani, Steven M. Kurtz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMost revision total knee arthroplasty patients experience excellent clinical outcomes. However, case studies document rare occurrences of metal component fractures promoted by the high cycles and low stresses that occur during daily use. In vivo, the mechanism that initiates metal fatigue fracture remains unclear. To address this gap, we collected implants from two retrieval programs. We asked: (1) what is the role of implant fixation in promoting fatigue fracture? (2) within modular junctions, what damage modes are associated with crack initiation? (3) how do equiaxed Ti‐6Al‐4V and wrought CoCrMo microstructures influence crack propagation? First, we compared clinical data for n = 8 fracture and n = 489 stemmed revision patients. Then, we performed retrieval analysis, combining radiographs with scanning electron microscopy and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy. We found no differences in weight (p = 0.07) or implantation time (p = 0.10) when comparing revision and fracture cohorts. Radiographs of fractured components showed insufficient metaphyseal fixation at or near the stem's modular junction. At the taper interface, we identified Ti‐6Al‐4V thread wear and CoCrMo pitting. Sectioning near a Ti‐6Al‐4V crack initiation site revealed an oxidized interface and an intergranular corrosion attack. On the free surface, TiO increased (p < 0.0001) and TiO2 decreased (p < 0.0001) after 20 min of sputter time. Fatigue cracks propagated through equiaxed Ti‐6Al‐4V and wrought CoCrMo grains. These data support a hypothesized mechanism where insufficient implant fixation with bone or cement promotes localized damage accumulation within the modular junction, fatigue crack initiation and subsequent fracture.

Introduction

Primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a successful clinical procedure with high survivorship [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, a fraction of primary TKA patients may undergo revision due to infection, loosening, and mechanical complications [5, 6, 7]. Surgeons typically replace the primary components with increasingly modular devices, treating patient bone loss with cones and sleeves, and stabilizing the implants with stems [8, 9, 10, 11]. In rare occurrences, case studies document the fracture of these modular metal components in vivo [12, 13, 14]. Compared to primary joint arthroplasty, fractures in revision TKA remain understudied and the mechanism poorly understood.

Revision TKA fractures reported in the literature can be classified into two groups: (1) fracture of interconnecting bolts and (2) fracture at or near modular taper junctions [12, 13, 14]. Most bolt fractures may be associated with a since‐updated sleeve, though corrosion at the sleeve‐adapter interface and subsequent biological complications remain concerns [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Stem fractures in vivo are associated with trauma (e.g., the patient fell) or insufficient metaphyseal fixation [23, 24]. Within a tibial bore taper, digital images document crack progression marks on the embedded and fractured stem, characteristic of fatigue [25, 26]. However, few studies investigate revision TKA fatigue fracture on a microscopic scale.

Fatigue fracture of metal components consists of three stages. During stage I, localized damage accumulates, resulting in persistent slip bands, intrusions and extrusions, and crack initiation. This typically occurs at a stress concentrator like the curved lip of a tibial baseplate, or the mouth of a bore taper [27, 28]. In a high cycle fatigue regime, devices may spend as much as 95% of their fatigue life in Stage I [29]. During stage II, the crack propagates. As the crack front progresses through the material, it leaves behind fatigue striations, hypothesized to form during an alternating mechanism of tip blunting and resharpening by slip [30, 31, 32]. Last, in stage III, the crack becomes unstable, overload occurs, and the two fracture faces separate. In vivo, fatigue is highly sensitive to both the alloy microstructure and the loading regime [27]. Furthermore, microscopic features are associated with each fatigue stage. For equiaxed and bimodal Ti‐6Al‐4V, the crack typically initiates at facets, cleaved alpha crystals that appear flat under scanning electron microscopy (SEM) [33, 34, 35, 36]. During overload (stage III), dimple‐like features populate the fracture surface, resulting from microvoid formation and coalescence [32, 36].

Because we have investigated both fatigue fracture and corroded TKA implants in the past, it is useful to explicitly state how this study differentiates and builds upon the existing literature. Previously, we characterized fatigue crack propagation through bimodal Ti‐6Al‐4V, lamellar Ti‐6Al‐4V and cast CoCrMo tibial baseplates [27]. However, these fractures occurred on metal components without modular junctions, where corrosion, fretting and crevice geometries are not concerns. Additionally, most revision TKA fractures are presented in the form of narrative case reports, with less emphasis on retrieval analysis. In this exploratory study, we use radiographs, SEM, nano computed tomography (NanoCT), and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to investigate component fracture following revision total knee arthroplasty. We asked: (1) what is the role of implant fixation in promoting fatigue fracture? (2) within modular junctions, what damage modes are associated with fatigue crack initiation? (3) how do equiaxed Ti‐6Al‐4V and wrought CoCrMo microstructures influence fatigue crack propagation?

Fractography

Three representative implants were selected for detailed retrieval analysis and assessed using various imaging modes at different resolutions. First, digital images (1‐5x) were acquired of the modular implant system. Then, the fracture faces were imaged using digital optical microscopy (20x, DOM, Keyence VHX‐7000, Keyence Corp., Itasca, IL). To identify fatigue as the fracture mechanism, micrographs were acquired using two SEMs: (1) Quanta 600 ESEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA; (2) Apreo 2S Lo Vac, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA. Fatigue striations were generally identified at magnifications ≥5000x. A mix of secondary and backscattered electron (BSE) imaging was used and resulting micrographs were adjusted for brightness and contrast.

Metallographic Preparation

To compare fracture features with alloy microstructure, implant components were separated manually or sectioned using an abrasive waterjet cutter (G2 PRO, WAZER, Yonkers, NY). The resulting surfaces were polished using: (1) decreasingly abrasive silicon carbide strips; (2) water and alumina (1 to 0.3 µm) suspensions; (3) a mix of 60% colloidal silica and 40% 9.8 M H2O2. After a mirror finish was achieved, micrographs were acquired using SEM with a backscatter detector as well as the Apreo's ‘T1’ imaging mode, a proxy for BSE imaging. Accelerating voltages of 15 kV and 10 kV were used for cobalt chrome and titanium alloy components, respectively. Post acquisition, micrographs were adjusted for brightness and contrast.

NanoCT Imaging

Fractures frequently left a portion of the stem taper fixed within the bore. To non‐destructively assess the taper interface, NanoCT (Xradia 620 Versa, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) was used to acquire images at high voltage settings (in this case, 130 kV). Data were postprocessed in DragonFly 3D World (Version 2024.1, Free Academic License, Comet Technologies Canada Inc., Montreal, Canada; software available at https://dragonfly.comet.tech/) and validated using digital optical microscopy after sectioning with a Pistorius GC140 wet saw (Allison C120‐J‐RA abrasive cut‐off wheel, Lancaster, Kentucky).

Free Surface Analysis

For the three representative implants selected for retrieval analysis, the free surface (the plane orthogonal to the crack initiation site) was investigated using SEM and energy dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDS, Apreo 2S Lo Vac, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Point spectra (n = 6) were acquired for 120 s over areas of contrasting oxide debris using an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. Additionally, false‐color count maps corresponding with individual elements (carbon, oxygen, titanium, and aluminum) were acquired near the crack initiation site. Afterwards, the free surface was mounted, sectioned, and reimaged.

Oxide debris on the free surface was characterized using XPS (VersaProbe 5000 X‐ray photoelectron spectrometer, Physical Electronics, Chanhassen, Minnesota). The experiment consisted of three parts. First, high resolution spectra (27 eV pass energy, 0.05 eV step size, 100 ms time per step) were acquired on the surface. Second, lower resolution spectra were acquired (55 eV pass energy, 0.2 eV step size, 50 ms time per step), before 1 min of sputtering at 2 kV and spectra reacquisition. This was repeated for 20 cycles. Third, after 20 min of sputter time, high resolution spectra were acquired. All spectra were acquired in triplicate (n = 3) and analyzed in CasaXPS Version 2.3.26PR1. For high resolution peaks, adventitious carbon referencing was performed, shifting the carbon 1 s peak from 284.7 ± 0.8 eV to 285.0 ± 0.0 eV (n = 6). Data acquired during sputtering were referenced with the Ti 2p 3/2 peak, translated from 459.2 ± 0.2 eV to 458.8 ± 0.0 eV (n = 63). Statistical Methods

The fracture patient cohort (n = 8) and a larger sample of patients with stemmed components (n = 489) were compared. First, normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test. Resulting p‐values were > 0.05 for the fracture cohort and < 0.05 for the stemmed cohort, thus a Mann‐Whittney test was selected (α = 0.05). To compare TiO, TiO2 and Ti2O3 concentrations acquired over 20 min of sputter time, a two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, with Fisher's Least Significant Difference test (α = 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated revision TKA components, associating (1) insufficient metaphyseal fixation, (2) thread taper wear, and (3) pitting and intergranular corrosion with fatigue crack initiation. First, we compared the implantation times and weights of our fracture cohort (n = 8) to a larger sample of revision patients (n = 489) with stemmed TKAs. Then, we performed retrieval analysis of three fractures that occurred at mixed and same alloy modular junctions. For both equiaxed Ti‐6Al‐4V and wrought CoCrMo, cracks propagated through individual grains (i.e., transgranularly). When the cracks terminated, we did not document any microscopic features of ductile overload or fast fracture, consistent with a high cycle, low stress fatigue regime. Combined, our data support the following hypothesized mechanism: Insufficient metaphyseal fixation promotes cantilever bending on stems, generating a moment at the modular taper junction. Under cyclic loads (exceeding 106 cycles per year), damage accumulates within the modular taper as asperities repeatedly interrupt the passive oxide film, or components wear. Finally, fatigue cracks initiate (Stage I) at the modular junction where they propagate (Stage II) until failure.

Our patient cohort shares several atypical features, including the complexity of their cases and their extensive revision histories: Six of the eight patients experienced two prior revisions. Patient 6, who had no revision history before the removal of their fractured implant, presented with a 30‐degree recurvatum deformity due to neuromuscular disease and an impaired gait due to a foot drop. This likely resulted in increased stress on the implant contributing to a fatigue fracture at the modular junction of the hinge post of the femoral component. Patient 7, who also had no revision history, underwent an additional three arthroplasties. While weight may be associated with increased revision risk, we did not document significant differences between our fractured and stemmed revision cohorts (p = 0.07) [40]. Furthermore, unlike the fatigue fracture of primary tibial baseplates, we found no difference in the implantation times (p = 0.10) [27]. The likelihood of repeat component fracture remains unclear: one patient (Patient 1 and Patient 5 in this cohort) fractured a combined four revision arthroplasty components, two each in his left and right knees. Thus, in addition to implant fixation, patient‐specific factors like gait and activity levels can influence these in vivo fractures.

On the free surface of a fractured tibial stem, we documented extensive corrosion generated within the titanium‐titanium modular taper junction. In this context, insufficient fixation and corrosion within the modular taper may promote a positive feedback loop: the cantilever forces necessary for the fatigue crack initiation likely also generate increased micromotion within the crevice, amplifying damage accumulation within the modular taper. It is important to note here that corrosion within conical tapers in the knee is ubiquitous, particularly for titanium‐titanium junctions [41, 42, 43]. In a previous retrieval study, we documented visual evidence of corrosion on 92% of titanium alloy stem tapers, including severe corrosion (Goldberg Score 4) on 22% [41, 44]. We caution against overstating the relationship between corrosion and subsequent fracture: while most titanium‐titanium junctions corrode in vivo, this rarely translates to component revision [25].

Independent of the fracture, the micrographs we acquired of the Ti‐6Al‐4V corrosion front provide mechanistic insights in vivo. We documented multiple oxidation states at the titanium alloy interface, intergranular corrosion, and large scale (≥50 µm) pitting. While previous studies report selective dissolution of the β phase, we show degradation of the boundaries between primary α grains [41, 45]. We hypothesize that the α fraction at the corroding interface governs this mechanism where in the absence of the β phase, corrosion initiates at α grain boundaries. The structure of the microns‐thick debris layer at the corrosion front, its electrochemical properties, and the transport phenomenon that may occur at the metal‐oxide interface represent areas for future research. Furthermore, the biological conditions that may generate chemically driven damage modes at Ti‐6Al‐4V interfaces remain unclear [46, 47, 48, 49].

This study experienced several potential limitations. Despite combining implants from two independent retrieval programs, our sample size consisted of eight retrievals, reflective of the rarity of in vivo fractures. Although we combined the fracture patients' clinical data, the comparison group of stemmed revision TKA patients came from a single institution, limiting the statistical analyses. Next, our analyses relied on qualitative methods, including the extensive use of microscopy. To improve methodological rigor, we used multiple imaging modes (SEM, X‐ray, digital, NanoCT) to show different fracture features at various spatial scales ranging from 1 to 50,000x. Additionally, we paired our qualitative analyses of taper corrosion with XPS, quantifying oxidation states at the titanium alloy interface. Like all retrieval studies, we performed our analysis after revision arthroplasty. Thus, the temporal relationship between damage modes and fatigue crack initiation is unclear, and a degree of uncertainty remains regarding causation. Finally, destructive analyses limited our assessment of free surfaces (i.e., the interface within the modular taper junction), removing material during cross‐sectioning. The association between corrosion and fatigue crack initiation remains an important topic for future research.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the clinical factors and implant damage modes associated with modular TKA fatigue crack initiation. Radiographs acquired prior to revision documented insufficient metaphyseal fixation and analyses of the explants revealed thread wear, pitting and an intergranular corrosion attack. On a fractured and corroded stem taper, we resolved multiple titanium oxidation states, including TiO, Ti2O3 and TiO2. These data support the following hypothesized mechanism: Over the course of a high cycle‐low stress fatigue regime, the loss of fixation contributes to cantilever bending, localized damage accumulation within modular tapers and subsequent crack initiation (Stage I). Once initiated, fatigue cracks propagate (Stage II) through equiaxed Ti‐6Al‐4V and wrought CoCrMo grains. Thus, corrosion‐based mechanisms, along with fretting (mechanically assisted crevice corrosion) may combine with fatigue loading to fracture metal revision components. Surgeons can mitigate in vivo stem fractures by using contemporary cones and sleeves, protecting the modular junction and achieving durable metaphyseal fixation.
