“Beyond the scale: body mass index as a hidden player in post-extraction complications”
Authors: Deepti Virupakshappa, Rajashekhara Bhari Sharanesha, Alwaleed Abushanan, Abdullah Bin Nabhan, Mazin Algahtani, Saad M Almanea, Mohamad Alshammari, Faisal Nasser Mulhiq Almishrafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBody mass index (BMI) is gaining recognition as a factor influencing surgical healing, but its precise effect on post-extraction complications is still not fully understood. This study investigated the association between BMI category and a comprehensive spectrum of post-extraction outcomes.MethodsThis prospective observational study was conducted on 247 patients stratified into four BMI groups: underweight (n = 52), healthy weight (n = 68), overweight(n = 73), and obese (n = 54). Outcome variables included post-operative pain measured by the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at 24 h, 72 h, one week, and two weeks; facial swelling at 72 h; a wound healing score ranging from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent); Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHIP-14); dry socket; and post-extraction infection. Statistical analysis included Shapiro-Wilk normality testing, Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn post-hoc (Bonferroni correction), Chi-square, Spearman rank correlation, binary logistic regression, and multilinear regression, performed using SPSS v. 31.0. software.ResultsAll continuous outcomes differed significantly across BMI groups (Kruskal-Wallis tests, all p < 0.001). The largest effect was observed for wound healing score (H = 144.564 ,η2 = 0.583): the healthy weight group achieved predominantly excellent healing (72.1% of score 5) while overweight and obese patients showed predominantly poor-to-fair healing (87.7% and 87.1% scores 1–2, respectively). BMI was the strongest Spearman correlate of wound healing (ρ = -0.564, p < 0.001) and a significant independent predictor in logistic regression for dry socket( OR = 1.306; 95% CI :1.195–1.427, p < 0.001) and infection(OR = 1.534; 95% CI: 1.187–1.984 ; p = 0.01). Multiple linear regression confirmed BMI as the primary predictor of wound healing score (B =-0.118, p < 0.001; R2 = 0.339) and OHIP-14 impairment (B =- 0.117 p < 0.01; R2 = 0.280).ConclusionsElevated BMI is a significant, independent predictor of adverse post-extraction. Healthcare providers should include BMI in pre-extraction risk assessments and consider BMI-specific post-operative management protocols to optimize patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09085-w.Keywords: BMI, Post operative complications, OHIP-14, Dental extraction, Obesity, Pain, Wound healing, Tooth extraction, Swelling

Background

Tooth extraction is one of the most common dental procedures worldwide, and is required for a variety of reasons, such as tooth decay, infection, overcrowding, or trauma [1, 2]. It is a common occurrence; however, post-operative complications following tooth extraction, including infection, delayed healing, dry socket, and excessive bleeding, can complicate the recovery process and affect patients’ quality of life [3, 4]. The success of this procedure depends on many factors, such as the patient’s overall health, age, smoking status, oral hygiene, and the complexity of the extraction [5, 6]. Body mass index (BMI) is an important factor that is often overlooked and deserves more attention, as it may influence post-extraction outcomes. While BMI itself is not a perfect measure of body fat or health, it is widely used as an indicator of obesity, which has been shown to impact healing outcomes. However, as noted in the Obesity Medicine Association (OMA) Clinical Practice Statement (CPS) 2022, BMI alone can be an unreliable measure for diagnosing obesity, as it does not account for factors such as muscle mass or fat distribution. The BMI has been used for a long time as a screening tool to provide an indication of overall health status and as a method to assess the risk of obesity-related health problems [7, 8]. BMI is a simple index of weight-for-height that is used to define underweight, healthy weight, overweight, and obesity in adults; it is a non-invasive measure of nutritional status and an indicator of the risk of nutrition-related diseases (e.g., cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and some cancers) [9, 10].

Evidence suggests BMI also affects dental procedure outcomes, with higher BMI linked to increased complication risks, delayed healing, infection, and discomfort, possibly due to obesity-related immune, vascular, and inflammatory issues [11, 12, 13–15]. Additionally, having too much facial fat, particularly in the neck and head areas, can make it harder to reach the area for tooth extraction and post-operative care, which can lead to even more complications [16, 17].

On the other hand, patients with a lower BMI, especially underweight patients, can experience difficulties in post-extraction healing [6, 18]. These patients often have compromised immunity, limited nutrient reserves, and slower tissue regeneration, all of which impair their ability to fight infections and repair tissues. Additionally, dehydration and nutrient deficiencies further slow the wound healing process [19, 14]. Both ends of the BMI spectrum being either overweight or underweight may face significant risks of poor post-operative recovery following tooth extraction [13].

The evidence on the impact of BMI on dental surgery outcomes is still emerging, although a few unifying factors have emerged [20, 21]. For example, obesity is linked to systemic inflammation, which can make the body’s reaction to surgical trauma worse, causing swelling and pain to persist longer.

Furthermore, individuals with high BMI are more prone to conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and sleep apnea, which can indirectly affect healing and recovery after surgery. These obesity-related comorbidities are frequently seen as independent risk factors for poor wound healing and a higher risk of surgical site infections [22–24]. With the growing recognition of the association of BMI, the possibility exists that post-surgical care will be changed accordingly so that patients who are at risk for complications will receive a customized treatment plan and follow-up care [23, 25, 26]. Despite rising obesity rates, limited research investigates how BMI affects dental extraction complications and outcomes. Most studies focus on general surgery or specific health issues, leaving a gap in understanding the link between BMI and dental procedures. The mechanisms by which BMI affects healing and complication rates are also poorly understood.

This study aimed to examine the association between BMI category and outcomes after surgical tooth extraction, with emphasis on pain, swelling, wound healing, dry socket, infection, and patient-reported recovery. By comparing postoperative findings across BMI categories, the study sought to provide clinically relevant evidence that may assist risk stratification and follow-up planning in dental surgery.

Study hypothesis

This study addresses a gap in the dental literature by evaluating whether postoperative recovery differs across BMI categories in a clinical extraction setting. The study hypothesis was:

H1: Higher BMI is associated with less favorable postoperative outcomes after tooth extraction, including greater pain, swelling, delayed healing, and higher dry socket frequency.

Clarifying the study’s contribution:

By testing the hypotheses, the study aimed to clarify whether BMI category is associated with differential postoperative recovery and to inform more individualized preoperative assessment in dental practice.

Materials and methods

This prospective observational study was conducted in the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at Prince Sattam Bin Abdulaziz University. Consecutive adults aged 18 to 64 years who required surgical tooth extraction and provided written informed consent were enrolled. The study received approval from the institutional ethics committee (SCBR-502/2025, dated May 27, 2025) and adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was calculated using a power analysis for a One-Way Analysis of Variance, which is the standard statistical method for comparing multiple means across independent groups. To detect significant differences in clinical outcomes among the four BMI categories, the calculation was based on an alpha level of 0.05 and a target power of at least 0.80. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s), typical in dental clinical research on post-extraction complications, a minimum total sample of 180 participants was needed. To improve the robustness of the results and account for possible non-normality in variables like pain scores and wound healing indices, the final sample was increased accordingly. This sample size allows for equal group allocation, with an estimated post-hoc power of about 0.84, ensuring the study can reliably detect meaningful differences in postoperative pain, facial swelling, and wound healing.

Preoperative assessment

All patients underwent a comprehensive preoperative patient assessment.

Medical and dental history

Recording systemic diseases, medications, smoking habits, and previous surgical history.

Clinical oral examination

Assessment of oral hygiene, presence of infection, tooth mobility, and surrounding soft tissue conditions.

Radiographic evaluation

Panoramic and/or periapical radiographs were taken to assess root morphology, proximity to vital structures, and bone quality. The radiographic evaluations were performed using standardized imaging protocols. A qualified radiology technician took the radiographs in accordance with institutional guidelines, ensuring consistency by focusing on proper positioning and maintaining uniform exposure settings.

Laboratory investigations

A Complete Blood Count (CBC), C-reactive Protein (CRP), Random Blood Sugar (RBS), and HbA1c can help to guide further diagnostic and treatment decisions regarding a patient’s health.

Anthropometric measurements

Height was recorded using a standard stadiometer (SECA 213, SECA GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany), while weight was measured with a digital scale (SECA 875, SECA GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany) to calculate BMI.

Patients were categorized based on BMI according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification [29] :

Tooth selection and procedure

The posterior teeth, anticipated to pose moderate surgical difficulty, were completed in less than 60 min. The procedure type was specified as surgical extraction (including flap elevation, bone removal, or tooth sectioning). The surgical extractions were analyzed to account for their higher complication rates (EBA05-demanding extraction includes tooth separation without raising a flap, EBA10-operative extraction includes osteotomy and separation of tooth, EBA20-Hemisection includes raising a flap, separation, and extraction of root ) [30] .

Surgical procedure

All participants were asked to rinse with 15 mL of 0.2% chlorhexidine mouthwash before surgery. Appropriate local anesthesia (2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine) was administered. When required, a triangular or envelope flap was raised, minimal bone removal was performed under continuous saline irrigation, and tooth/root sectioning was undertaken for multirooted teeth. After extraction, the socket was debrided, hemostasis was achieved, and the wound was closed with 3 − 0 silk sutures. Standard postoperative instructions were provided, and ibuprofen 400 mg was prescribed every 6 h for 5 days.

Complications, particularly alveolar osteitis (dry socket), were diagnosed using predefined clinical criteria, including exposed bone, severe localized pain, and halitosis. Postoperative assessments were performed by independent evaluators who were blinded to BMI category. To ensure consistency and minimize inter-operator variability with a reliability of 0.8 (ICC-value), and to uphold the integrity of the study as well as procedural consistency, all extractions were performed by a team of four oral surgeons.

Postoperative assessment and tests

Patients were followed up at 24 h, 72 h, 1 week, and 2 weeks post-extraction.

The following outcomes and tests were recorded:

Pain Assessment: Pain intensity was measured on a 0–10 visual analog scale (VAS), where 0 represented no pain and 10 represented the worst possible pain [31].

Pain Assessment: Pain intensity was measured on a 0–10 visual analog scale (VAS), where 0 represented no pain and 10 represented the worst possible pain [31].

Infection assessment

Clinical signs of infection, such as redness, pus discharge, fever, and lymphadenopathy, were assessed [33].

Wound healing assessment

Healing at 2 weeks was assessed using a Landry-based composite wound-healing score derived from the Landry, Turnbull, and Howley criteria [34] tissue color, bleeding on palpation, granulation tissue, and epithelialization, presence of pus, with higher scores indicating better healing.Wound healing was graded on a scale of 1to 5, where 1 indicates poor healing, and 5 indicates excellent healing [35].

Hemorrhage assessment

Any occurrences of bleeding following surgery were documented in detail, capturing the severity, length of bleeding episodes, and any medical interventions needed to manage the bleeding.

Alveolar osteitis (dry socket) occurrence

Presence of exposed bone at the extraction site, which is a sign of potential complications. The patient also reported experiencing severe pain in that area, and there was a noticeable foul odor, or halitosis, suggesting possible infection or tissue necrosis. These findings are important for diagnosing the extraction site, which was documented for dry socket [36, 37].

Patient satisfaction and functional assessment

Patients were surveyed regarding mastication, Speech, and overall patient satisfaction. Functionality and satisfaction levels were assessed using the OHIP-14 [38] questionnaire and VAS [Visual Analog Scale] score for pain experienced during mastication, speech, and recovery.

Patients were asked to rate their pain:

During mastication: Pain was assessed while the patient was chewing food.

During mastication: Pain was assessed while the patient was chewing food.

During speech: Pain was assessed while speaking, specifically focusing on any discomfort related to mouth movement.

During speech: Pain was assessed while speaking, specifically focusing on any discomfort related to mouth movement.

During recovery: Overall pain during the recovery, covering a range of activities and at rest, was also recorded.

During recovery: Overall pain during the recovery, covering a range of activities and at rest, was also recorded.

Statistical analysis

After collecting and compiling the data, statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 31.0 software. The normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test; the majority of the subgroups showed non-normal distributions, so nonparametric methods were used for the analysis. To the effect size measurement across BMI groups, the Kruskal-Wallis H test with eta squared (η2 )was used for the pairwise comparison. Post-hoc comparison test was performed using the Dunn test with Bonferroni correction. Associations between BMI category and categorical complications or outcomes, using Pearson and Chi–square tests with Cramér’s V to assess effect size. Spearman rank correlations [ρ] were calculated between continuous BMI values and post-extraction outcomes. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of dry socket and infection. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. Multiple linear regression was used to identify predictors of the OHIP-14 score and wound healing score. Age differences across BMI groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post hoc. Statistical significance was set at alpha equal to 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

This prospective observational study included 247 patients with a mean age of 35.1 ± 12.4 years (range 18 to 64). The study group included 118 males (47.8%) and 129 females (52.2%). Participants were categorized into four BMI groups based on WHO standards; underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2; n = 52, 21.1%), healthy weight (BMI 18.52–24.9 kg/m2; n = 68, 27.5%), overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m2; n = 73, 29.6%), and obese (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2; n = 54, 21.9%). The prevalence of diabetes and smoking was 8.1% and 10.5%, respectively. The mean BMI was 25.46 ± 7.28 kg/m2. Age differed significantly across BMI groups by ANOVA and Tukey post hoc tests, with underweight patients notably younger than those who were obese. Spearman’s rank correlation between BMI and clinical outcomes

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis showed significant associations between BMI and all clinical outcomes [Table 5; Figs. 6 and 7]. The strongest negative correlation was between BMI and wound healing score [ρ = -0.564,p < 0.001], indicating that higher BMI relates to worse healing, with the score from 1 = poor to 5 = excellent. BMI positively correlates with pain at 72 h [ρ = 0.535,p < 0.001], and two weeks [ρ = 0.535,p < 0.001], implying sustained pain in higher BMI patients. A moderate positive correlation appeared between BMI and facial swelling at 72 h [ρ = 0.439,p < 0.001], OHIP-14 impairment [ ρ = 0.426,p < 0.001 ], and dry socket[ρ = 0.397,p < 0.001]. A weak but significant positive association was observed between BMI and pain at 24 h [ ρ = 0.289, p < 0.001] and at one week [ ρ = 0.130, p = 0.041]. BMI also positively correlated with age[ ρ = 0.317,p < 0.001], consistent with ANOVA results, and weakly with infection[ρ = 0.137,p = 0.031].

Predictors of dry socket and post-extraction infection [binary logistic regression]

Binary logistic regression identified independent predictors of dry socket and post-extraction infection [Table 6]. The model for dry socket was significant [McFadden R2 = 0.429, AIC = 157.2]. BMI was a significant predictor [OR = 1.306, p < 0.001], with each 1-unit increase in BMI raising dry socket odds by 30.6%. Male sex [ OR = 10.587, p < 0.001], current smoking [OR = 4.360, p < 0.05], and age [OR = 1.053, p < 0.01] were also significant, while diabetes mellitus was not. The model for post-extraction infection was also good [McFadden R² = 0.515, AIC = 66.8]. BMI was the only significant predictor [OR = 1.534, p < 0.01], with each 1-unit increase in BMI increasing infection odds by 53.4%. Age, sex, diabetes, and smoking were not significant, suggesting BMI is the main modifiable risk factor.

Predictors of OHIP-14 score and wound healing score [multiple linear regression]

Multiple linear regression identified significant predictors of both oral health-related quality of life [OHIP-14] and wound-healing outcomes [Tables 7–8]. The OHIP-14 regression model was statistically significant [F[5, 241] = 18.771, R2 = 0.2803, Adjusted R2 = 0.2654, p < 0.001], explaining 28.0% of the variance in OHIP-14 scores. BMI was a significant positive predictor [B = 0.1174, 95% CI: 0.082–0.153, p < 0.001], indicating that each unit increase in BMI was associated with a 0.1174-point increase in OHIP-14 score [higher scores = greater impairment]. Male sex was associated with a lower OHIP-14 score [B = -0.6699, p < 0.01], and diabetes mellitus was a significant negative predictor [B = -2.4468, p < 0.001], suggesting that patients with diabetes reported lower quality-of-life impairment, possibly reflecting adaptation or different pain perception. The wound healing regression model explained a greater proportion of variance [ F(5, 241) = 24.693, R² = 0.3388, Adjusted R² = 0.3250, p < 0.001]. BMI was the strongest predictor of wound healing score [ B = -0.1177, 95%CI: 0.0141–0.094, p < 0.001] with each unit increase in BMI associated with a 0.1177- point decrease in wound healing score [ i.e., worse healing, since 1 = Poor and 5 = Excellent]. Older age was a significant positive predictor of healing [B = 0.0246, p < 0.001], suggesting that older patients had marginally better wound-healing scores. Male sex [ B = -0.3236, p = 0.05 ] and diabetes mellitus[ B = -0.5901, p = 0.05] were also significant negative predictors of wound healing quality.

Discussion

This prospective observational study of 247 patients examines the link between body mass index and a wide range of post-extraction complications, using a comprehensive multi-test statistical approach. Our results show that BMI is a strong and reliable independent predictor of adverse post-extraction outcomes, such as poor wound healing, prolonged pain, significant swelling, higher complication rates, and reduced oral health-related quality of life. These findings support and expand the growing body of research on how metabolic factors influence healing in oral surgery [1, 3, 8, 39].

Wound healing and BMI

This study’s most notable finding was the strong link between higher BMI and poor wound healing. The healing score [1 = poor, 5 = excellent] showed the largest Kruskal-Wallis ’ effect size among all outcomes [H = 144.56, η²= 0.583], and it had the strongest Spearman correlation with BMI [α = -0.564, p < 0.001]. The healthy-weight group mostly achieved excellent healing [72.1% scored 5], whereas the overweight and obese groups mainly showed poor to fair healing [87.7% and 87.1% scored 1–2, respectively]. These results align with experimental evidence indicating that obesity impairs bone healing in animal models by disrupting osteogenic signaling, reducing angiogenesis, and altering immune cell function [9]. Mechanistically, adipose tissue in obese individuals secretes higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α] and interleukin 6 [IL-6], which interfere with the phases of wound healing (hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling) [13, 14].

The link between obesity and periodontal healing has been thoroughly explored in recent studies, which found that inflammatory mediators from adipose tissue create a systemic pro-inflammatory state that hinders periodontal tissue regeneration [14].In contrast, adipose tissue is described as an active inflammatory organ whose irregular secretions directly interfere with oral wound healing [13]. Linear regression analysis confirmed BMI as the strongest independent predictor of wound healing score [B=-0. 1177, p < 0.001], even after adjusting for age, sex, diabetes, and smoking, highlighting adiposity as a key modifiable risk factor. Additionally, the notable negative impact of diabetes [B = -0.5901, p = 0.043] aligns with existing evidence that hyperglycemia impairs gingival fibroblast function and delays socket repair [15, 17, 18].

Post-extraction pain and swelling

Our study reveals a clear BMI-dependent gradient in post-extraction pain, with obese patients reporting significantly higher VAS scores at all time points compared to those with a healthy weight [p < 0.001 after Bonferroni correction]. The strong correlation between BMI and VAS score at 72 h [ρ = 0.535] and at two weeks [ρ = 0.535] indicates that pain sensitivity in obese individuals persists beyond the immediate recovery period. This may be due to ongoing inflammatory dysregulation. These findings align with those of a study that identified BMI as a key predictor of acute post-extraction pain in a multifocal analysis [1] and with another study that found that BMI influences post-extraction pain after impacted third molar extraction in a multicenter prospective cohort [3].

Facial swelling at 72 h was significantly higher in overweight and obese groups compared with healthy-weight patients [Kruskal-Wallis H = 75.64, p < 0.001, η²= 0.229, Dunn test p < 0.001 for both comparisons]. There is a moderate positive correlation between BMI and swelling [ρ = 0.439, p < 0.001], likely reflecting increased systemic inflammation in individuals with more adipose tissue. Elevated levels of leptin, resistin, and adiponectin in obese patients influence vascular permeability and neutrophil movement, intensifying post-surgical edema [13, 14, 21]. Additionally, excess adipose tissue may mechanically compress lymphatic vessels, hindering lymphatic drainage and delaying edema resolution [23].

Dry socket and post-extraction infection

The prevalence of dry socket[alveolar osteitis] showed a clear BMI-dependent pattern, with the highest occurrence in the obese group at 27[50%], compared with 10[14.7%] in patients of healthy weight. Chi-square analysis confirmed a strong association [χ²(3) = 40.476,p < 0.001, Cramer’s V = 0.405]. In a logistic regression model, BMI was an independent predictor of dry socket[OR = 1.306, 95% CI: 1.195–1.427,p < 0.001], reflecting a 30.6% increase in the odds ratio for each 1-unit increase in BMI. These results align with epidemiological research identifying systemic factors that influence the risk of alveolar osteitis [22, 23].

Current smoking was a significant predictor of dry socket in our model [OR = 4.360, p = 0.032], consistent with the known mechanism where nicotine-induced vasoconstriction reduces blood flow to the extraction site, impairing clot stabilization [25, 26]. Male sex was also a strong predictor [OR = 10.587, p < 0.001], possibly due to anatomical differences in socket vascularity or hormonal effects on clot formation and fibrinolysis [22, 40]. The role of estrogen in improving fibrin clot stability might partly explain why females in this group experienced lower rates of dry socket. Alveolar osteitis is a significant post-extraction complication with multiple causes [40]. Our findings introduce BMI as a measurable, modifiable risk factor.

Post-extraction infection was significantly associated with BMI category [x2(3) = 20.933, p < 0.001, Cramer’s V = 0.291], and BMI was the only significant independent predictor in the logistic regression analysis [OR = 1.534, 95% CI: 1.187–1.984, p = 0.001]. Each unit increase in BMI corresponds to a 53.4% higher odds of infection, likely due to obesity-related immune impairment, such as neutrophil dysfunction, reduced macrophage phagocytosis, and decreased antimicrobial peptide production in inflamed adipose tissue [13, 14, 23]. Additionally, obesity-related dysbiosis of the oral microbiome may increase susceptibility to microbial colonization of the socket after extraction [41, 40].

Oral health -related quality of life[ OHIP-14]

OHIP-14 scores were notably higher in overweight and obese patients compared to those with a healthy weight [Kruskal-Walllis H = 75.63, p < 0.001, η²= 0.299; Dunn test p < 0.001 for both comparisons], indicating increased impairment. A moderate positive correlation between BMI and OHIP-14 [ρ = 0.426, P < 0.001] indicates that greater adiposity is linked to a higher subjective overall health burden after tooth extraction. The linear regression analysis confirmed BMI as a significant independent predictor of OHIP-14 impairment [B = 0.1174, P < 0.001], even when accounting for confounders. This aligns with Khanal et al., who found notable OHIP-14 Impairment following impacted third molar removal, especially in patients with complications [17]. Increased pain, swelling, and complications collectively contribute to the quality-of-life reduction observed in higher-BMI individuals.

Biological mechanism linking BMI to post-extraction complications

The mechanistic basis for BMI-mediated post-extraction complications is multifactorial. In obese individuals, adipose tissue functions as an endocrine organ, secreting elevated levels of pro-inflammatory adipokines, including leptin, resistin, visfatin, and TNF-α, while producing reduced levels of anti-inflammatory adiponectin [13, 14, 21]. This imbalance in pro-inflammatory adipokines sustains ongoing low-grade systemic inflammation, interfering with the precisely controlled inflammatory response needed for proper wound healing. Notably, increased levels of TNF-α and IL-6 hinder fibroblast growth, collagen production, and cell migration, key processes for closing soft-tissue sockets [42]. Leptin is required for normal immune function; it’s paradoxically elevated in obesity and promotes excessive macrophage activation and prolonged pro-inflammatory M1 polarization, delaying the transition to the proliferative healing phase [13].

At the osseous level, obesity hampers bone healing via multiple pathways: increased TNS–alpha inhibits osteoblast differentiation and stimulates osteoclast activity; insulin resistance diminishes IG-1e-driven osteogenic signals; and fat cells infiltrating the bone marrow displace hematopoietic stem cells, thereby decreasing the osteoprogenitor cells needed for socket regeneration [9, 17]. The study showed that metabolic conditions associated with adipogenesis impair how endothelial cells respond to titanium surfaces, suggesting that vascular issues in obese patients could also obstruct angiogenesis in extraction sockets [43]. Overall, these mechanisms help explain the link between higher BMI and reduced wound-healing scores observed in our cohort.

The hyperglycemic aspect of obesity, particularly in diabetic patients, further impairs wound healing. Studies show that elevated blood sugar in obese diabetic rats significantly hampers pulp wound healing, leading to reduced blood vessel formation and poor dentin bridge development [18]. It was reported that insulin resistance hampers gingival fibroblast wound healing by reducing Akt phosphorylation, and that metformin can partially counteract this effect [17]. The notable negative impact of diabetes on wound healing in our regression analysis [B = -0.5901, p = 0.043] aligns with these mechanistic insights and highlights the importance of controlling blood sugar levels before elective dental extractions [44].

Clinical implications

This study’s findings have notable clinical relevance for dental practitioners. First, BMI should be regularly recorded and included in pre-extraction risk evaluations, especially for patients with a BMI of 25 kg/m2 or higher. However, logistic regression results indicate that with each unit increase in BMI, the risk of dry socket rises by 30.6%, and the risk of infection rises by 53.4%. These thresholds offer practical guidance for implementing targeted prophylaxis and increased postoperative vigilance.

Second, wound-healing data indicate that obese patients should be informed about their notably higher risk of poor healing. They might also benefit from longer follow-up after extraction. Socket preservation techniques, antimicrobial irrigation, and possibly socket-sealing materials could help reduce the risk of dry socket and infection in patients with higher BMI. Third, the strong link between current smoking and dry socket [OR = 4.360] highlights the need for preoperative smoking cessation advice, especially for overweight and obese individuals who already have a higher baseline risk [27, 45] .

From a public health standpoint, the rising worldwide rate of overweight and obesity [6] indicates that dental professionals will increasingly encounter high-risk patients for post-extraction complications. Developing BMI-based post-extraction care protocols, including improved pain management, targeted antibiotic prophylaxis for individuals with high BMI, and structured dietary and activity plans, are practical steps to translate these research insights into practice. Managing tooth extraction in older patients with diabetes and obesity requires careful consideration of drug interactions and strategies to optimize healing [6].

This study has some limitations. First, because it is a prospective observational study, it cannot establish definitive causal inference. Longitudinal studies with blinded outcomes are needed to confirm these associations. Second, the healing scoring system relies on subjective assessment. In future studies, incorporating objective measures, such as optical coherence tomography, for initial socket-healing assessments can yield more accurate insights. This study’s strengths lie in its prospective observational design, which ensures systematic data collection and improved reliability. By categorizing 247 patients into four BMI groups, the study facilitates detailed comparisons across different body weights. Various outcome measures, including pain at multiple time points, facial swelling, wound healing, OHIP-14, dry socket, and infection, provide a comprehensive evaluation of post-extraction complications. Robust statistical methods, such as non-parametric tests, correlation, logistic, and multilinear regression analyses, confirm the validity of the results.

Conclusions

This prospective observational study demonstrates that Body Mass Index (BMI) serves as a significant and independent predictor of postoperative complications following tooth extraction, affecting various outcome measures. Higher BMI levels are associated with increased postoperative pain and swelling, a greater incidence of dry socket compared to infections, and a more pronounced decline in oral health-related quality of life. This study shows a clear link between BMI and post-extraction results, with higher BMI levels associated with poorer wound healing, more pain, increased facial swelling, and lower oral health-related quality of life. Notably, healthy weight patients mostly experienced excellent healing, while overweight and obese patients mostly had poor-to-fair healing. These results emphasize the importance of considering BMI when predicting and managing post-extraction issues, suggesting that customized clinical approaches are needed to improve outcomes for patients with elevated BMI.
