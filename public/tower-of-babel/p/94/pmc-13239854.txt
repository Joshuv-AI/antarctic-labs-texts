Lecanemab and amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities: Real‐world data from a single center experience
Authors: Alexander Wallace, Oliver Zhou, Minh‐Nguyet Hoang, Gabriella Porter, Claudia Padilla, Cindy Marshall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONLecanemab has demonstrated both safety and efficacy in clinical trials; however, routine clinical data are sparse. We contribute to the growing body of real‐world safety data, with a special focus on amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA).METHODSWe conducted a retrospective chart review of 146 patients treated with lecanemab according to appropriate‐use recommendations at a specialty memory center clinic.RESULTSReal‐world safety and efficacy data closely resemble published data in the Clarity‐AD trial. Thirty‐three patients (23%) discontinued treatment; all remaining patients completed at least 12 months of treatment. We observed a positive trend toward increasing ARIA edema/effusion (ARIA‐E) with increasing mean arterial pressure and a mean amyloid clearance of −90.6 at 18 months.DISCUSSIONWhen used according to appropriate‐use recommendations, lecanemab is as safe as reported in the Clarity‐AD trial. Further investigation into real‐world data is necessary for a more complete evaluation of lecanemab's safety and efficacy.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, amyloid plaque clearance, amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies, APOE ε4, lecanemab, magnetic resonance imaging, real‐world data

BACKGROUND

Amyloid‐targeting therapies (ATTs) are the first approved disease‐modifying therapies for Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 One such medication, lecanemab, is a humanized immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) monoclonal antibody that preferentially targets aggregated amyloid beta soluble protofibrils. 2 , 3 , 4 Results from the Phase III Clarity‐AD trial showed that treatment with lecanemab slowed cognitive decline (27%) in early AD when compared to placebo as measured by changes in the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) and lessened progression of caregiver burden and resulted in a mean amyloid plaque clearance of 55.48 Centiloids (CL). 3 , 4 Adverse events were observed, including amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), a known side effect of ATTs. 5 In the Clarity‐AD trial, 113 (12.6%) and 155 (17.3%) of the total 859 patients treated with lecanemab developed ARIA edema/effusion (ARIA‐E) and ARIA hemorrhage (ARIA‐H), respectively; in total, 193 patients (21.5%) developed ARIA of any kind.

Another such medication, donanemab, was evaluated in a similar Phase III TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ 2 trial. 6 In this study, higher rates of ARIA were noted: 205 (24.0%) and 268 (31.4%) of 853 patients treated with donanemab developed ARIA‐E and ARIA‐H, respectively, for a total of 314 (36.8%) unique cases of ARIA. In addition, this study also discovered a unique association between ARIA‐E and hypertension, as those who had higher baseline mean arterial pressures were more likely to develop ARIA‐E. However, a higher plaque clearance was also seen with donanemab treatment, with a mean decrease in amyloid burden of 88.0 Centiloids (CL).

Widespread adoption into routine clinical practice has been somewhat impeded due to the risk of ARIA, which pose significant safety concerns for both patients and clinicians alike and require frequent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies for safety monitoring. 4 , 6 , 7 ARIA are radiographic abnormalities associated with ATT treatment and are broadly classified into ARIA‐E (edema/effusion) and ARIA‐H (hemorrhage). 5 ARIA‐E may manifest with or without symptoms. 5 ARIA‐H includes microhemorrhages, hemosiderin deposition, and superficial siderosis, which are often asymptomatic although detectable on susceptibility‐weighted imaging or gradient echo sequences of MRI. 5 Most cases seen in clinical trials have been mild, but fatalities have been reported as well. 5 , 8

Although the incidence of ARIA in research trials is well documented, there is a limited but growing body of real‐world data on ARIA incidence in a clinic setting. 4 , 9 , 10 , 11 Shields et al. recorded that 12 patients (24%) in their sample of 50 developed ARIA, with 3 (6%) having only ARIA‐E, 5 (10%) having only ARIA‐H, and 4 (8%) having both ARIA‐E and ARIA‐H. Somewhat similar findings were noted in the study conducted by Bregman et al.; 16 (18.6%) patients of 86 developed ARIA, 3 (3.4%) having developed ARIA‐E, and 13 (15%) having developed ARIA‐H. Participants in clinical trials are selected through strict eligibility criteria and may not accurately represent the broader patient population. 7

For this study, we conducted a retrospective chart review of lecanemab in our real‐world patient cohort, with a particular focus on the incidence rate of ARIA and risk stratification for ARIA by apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype and vascular markers. Specifically, we sought to investigate:

1. Whether the rates of ARIA (total, ARIA‐E, and ARIA‐H) in our real‐world cohort were consistent with those reported in the Clarity‐AD study.

METHODS

In this study, patients who received lecanemab and had at least one post‐treatment MRI between March 2023 and September 2024 at the Baylor AT&T Memory Center within Baylor University Medical Center in Dallas, TX, were chosen for retrospective chart review. Each patient had a participating care partner.

Cohort selection criteria

Patients were selected according to the appropriate‐use recommendations published by Cummings et al. 12 Patients were diagnosed with either mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD or mild AD dementia based on the National Institute on Aging‐Alzheimer's Association clinical criteria. 13 , 14 All participants had a baseline MRI, neuropsychological testing, and biomarker confirmation of amyloid pathology (amyloid positron emission tomography [PET] or cerebrospinal fluid [CSF]). Ninety‐two percent of patients underwent PET testing, and 8% underwent CSF testing. Blood testing for amyloid pathology was not performed.

Nearly every patient completed neuropsychological evaluations at the Baylor AT&T Memory Center. This comprehensive testing included Test of Premorbid Functioning (TOPF), Mini‐Mental Status Examination (MMSE), Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status Update (RBANS Update), Controlled Oral Word Association (Letter Fluency; Semantic Fluency: Animals), Boston Naming Test (BNT – 30‐Item, odd), Clock Drawing Test, Trail Making Test Part A and Part B, and Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), and Apathy Scale (AS).

Patients were included and excluded from treatment according to the appropriate‐use recommendations. The following MRI findings necessitated exclusion: advanced microvascular disease defined as Fazekas score >3 or as per visual inspection of the interpreting neuroradiologist, superficial siderosis, >4 baseline microhemorrhages, >2 lacunar infarcts or major vascular territory stroke, macrohemorrhage >10 mm, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy‐related inflammation. Other exclusionary medical conditions included seizure, immunologic or bleeding disorders, as well as transient ischemic attack (TIA) in the last year. Use of anticoagulants was also an exclusion criterion.

Treatment regimen

Treatment with lecanemab was initiated at an outpatient infusion center at a dose of 10 mg per kg, scheduled every 2 weeks for 18 months, and then every 4 weeks thereafter. MRI was performed before the initiation of treatment; prior to the 5th, 7th, and 14th infusions; and on an ad hoc basis for additional monitoring for select patients with ARIA or other safety concerns. MRI was not performed uniformly prior to Infusion 3. MRI studies were read by experienced neuroradiologists who graded ARIA according to the radiographic severity ratings in the appropriate‐use recommendations. 12 If ARIA were detected, discussions were conducted with the patients and their families. ARIA monitoring and management strategies were implemented according to the algorithm outlined by the appropriate use recommendations.

Statistics

In this study, the patients were assessed for their APOE genotype and incidence of ARIA development. Results were compared to results of the Clarity‐AD trial. The participants were also evaluated for hypertension and use of antihypertensives during lecanemab treatment, and how these factors correlated with ARIA incidence. Finally, amyloid clearance was evaluated by baseline and 18‐month PET scan CL scores, when available. Statistical significance was assessed via chi‐square test of independence.

Ethics

The trial was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Baylor Scott & White. The data were collected and analyzed by Baylor Scott & White employees, students, and statisticians. All patient information was stored securely in Microsoft OneDrive and was accessible only to key study personnel.

When we examined the timeline of ARIA development, we found that over 75% of all ARIA cases occurred before Infusion 14. Approximately 75% of ARIA‐E cases and 70% of ARIA‐H cases were mild. We observed a total of four cases of either severe (radiographically or clinically) or symptomatic ARIA in our sample.

One patient was discovered to have simultaneous moderate ARIA‐E and severe ARIA‐H at MRI performed before Infusion 7; despite the severity of this patient's ARIA, the only symptom was an isolated headache. Due to the severity of ARIA, the patient discontinued treatment. At a 3 month follow‐up post‐discontinuation, the ARIA‐E resolved without other intervention and the ARIA‐H remained unchanged.

The second patient, who had concurrent mild ARIA‐E and moderate ARIA‐H, developed a single episode of headache, nausea, and vomiting and discontinued treatment just before Infusion 5. Again, a 3‐month post‐discontinuation follow‐up MRI demonstrated resolution of ARIA‐E and stability of ARIA‐H without any other intervention.

The third patient developed moderate ARIA‐E and severe ARIA‐H by the pre‐Infusion 5 MRI. This patient was completely asymptomatic but was admitted for 3 days of inpatient treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone. A 1‐month post‐hospitalization follow‐up visit showed resolution of ARIA‐E and no interval change in ARIA‐H; this was reconfirmed at 1‐year follow‐up MRI.

Finally, the fourth patient, an ε4 homozygote, developed severe ARIA‐E and ARIA‐H and exhibited significant confusion, unsteady gait, and urinary incontinence; the severity of ARIA in this case required inpatient treatment with 5 days of intravenous methylprednisolone. This patient's mental status improved significantly during the hospital stay but did not entirely normalize; the patient was discharged to an inpatient rehabilitation hospital with only mild residual confusion. By a 6‐month post‐discontinuation follow‐up, the patient's ARIA‐E had resolved, although ARIA‐H remained unchanged. Unfortunately, this patient died due to acute leukemia ≈3 months later.

Rates of ARIA by APOE genotype

When examining our patient sample for rates of ARIA according to APOE ε4 carrier status, we found that the rates of overall ARIA and ARIA‐H varied significantly. However, the same was not true for ARIA‐E. We found that the ε4 homozygotes had a higher risk relative to ε4 non‐carriers for developing ARIA, ARIA‐E, and ARIA‐H, with calculated relative risks of 3.2, 8.9, and 2.9, respectively. Infusion reactions

Infusion reactions were common: 44 of 146 patients (30%) developed infusion reactions. Most reactions were mild and included such symptoms as headaches, fatigue, dizziness, and mild nausea in association with the infusions.

Discontinuation rates

Of 146 patients, 33 patients discontinued treatment, for an overall discontinuation rate of ≈23%. The most common reason for discontinuation was the development of ARIA (12 cases). Aside from those who discontinued treatment early, as stated above, all patients completed infusions up to the end of the study data‐collection period. The majority of patients completed 12 months of infusions. Patients discontinued treatment at various points throughout the study. A few stopped treatment after only a few infusions and some discontinued treatment ≈1.5 years after treatment initiation.

ARIA associations with blood pressure

First, we examined the effect of a known diagnosis of hypertension on the development of ARIA‐E. We found no statistically significant association, with 12 of 87 hypertensive patients developing ARIA‐E. In patients without a hypertension diagnosis, 5 of 59 developed ARIA‐E (RR 1.6, 95% CI 0.61–4.38).

We then examined whether mean arterial pressure (MAP) at baseline was a factor for the development of ARIA‐E. Patients were categorized according to MAP in the following groups: <93.0 mmHg, 93.0–96.9 mmHg, 97.0–106.9 mmHg, and ≥107.0 mmHg. We then compared all patients relative to those with MAP <93.0 mmHg. Although there seemed to be a trend toward more frequent ARIA‐E cases as MAP increased, there was no statistically significant effect by MAP category on the development of ARIA‐E. We also examined the effect of MAP on the development of ARIA‐H and found a slight difference. Although those in the two lower MAP groups of 93.0–96.9 mm Hg and 97.0–106.9 mm Hg were not at a higher risk of developing ARIA‐H than those in the lowest group of <93.0 mm Hg, those with MAP ≥107.0 mm Hg were significantly more likely to develop ARIA‐H, with a calculated RR of 2.88 (95% CI 1.28–6.51). Finally, we examined whether antihypertensive use was protective against ARIA‐E as was observed in the TRAILBLAZER trial. We found no statistically significant association of antihypertensive use with the development of ARIA‐E, with 12 of 80 patients using antihypertensive agents having developed ARIA‐E and with 4 of 66 patients not using antihypertensive agents having developed ARIA‐E (RR 0.40, 95% CI 0.14–1.19).

Amyloid clearance on 18‐month PET scan

As of the time of submission, 70 patients completed an 18‐month follow‐up PET scan to check for amyloid clearance. Of the 70 patients who completed the 18‐month follow‐up, pre‐lecanemab CL data were available for 58. For the remaining 12 patients, pre‐treatment values were unavailable either because the imaging center could not retrospectively calculate CL values or because CSF was used as the baseline biomarker. For the 58 patients with both pre‐ and post‐treatment measures, the average baseline and 18‐month follow‐up CL scores were 114.5 and 23.9, respectively, with an average net change of −90.6. The average CL score for all 70 patients at 18‐month follow‐up was 25.4.

Feasibility of treatment

Feasibility of implementation was supported by an established memory center infrastructure with two full‐time clinicians, two full‐time nurses, and neuropsychology support. A multidisciplinary workgroup was convened to streamline workflows and address implementation barriers, resulting in the development of electronic medical record order sets and thrombolytic alerts. Memory center leadership collaborated with radiology to implement ARIA monitoring protocols. Infusions were administered primarily through community‐based infusion centers, which managed insurance authorization and scheduling. Initially, memory center nurses tracked infusions and maintained MRI schedules alongside their usual clinical duties. A dedicated full‐time infusion coordinator was later established through philanthropic support. ARIA management was conducted by physicians in accordance with the package insert and appropriate use recommendations.

DISCUSSION

In this investigation, we sought to examine lecanemab from a real‐world perspective with a special focus on the development of ARIA. We found that patients treated with lecanemab according to appropriate‐use recommendations developed ARIA at similar rates as patients in the Clarity‐AD trial. We also found no significant difference relative to Clarity‐AD trial data in the proportions of each APOE genotype who developed symptomatic ARIA‐E, total ARIA‐E, and total ARIA‐H. Taken together, these findings indicate that, when lecanemab is used according to appropriate‐use recommendations in the clinical setting, ARIA can be expected to develop at rates similar to those recorded in the Clarity‐AD study. The data recently reported by Bregman et al. demonstrated a slightly lower overall rate of ARIA (18.6%) compared to the rate observed in the Clarity‐AD study (21.5%), although the authors attributed this lower rate to their exclusion of ε4 homozygotes from lecanemab treatment. 10 Paczynski et al. found that 22% of patients developed ARIA, a rate very similar to those observed in the Clarity‐AD trial and by us in our study. 11 However, Paczynski et al. found slightly more ARIA‐E (15%) and slightly less isolated ARIA‐H (6.7%) than we did.

Our findings agree with prior research indicating that all forms of ARIA occurred in numerically higher amounts in ε4 homozygotes than in all other groups. 2 , 10 , 15 In our sample, ε4 homozygotes were at increased risk for total ARIA, ARIA‐E, and ARIA‐H when compared to non‐carriers, which agrees with the existing data. 2 , 15 Furthermore, most cases of ARIA were mild; although a select few cases did require inpatient treatment and discontinuation of the medication, these patients demonstrated improvement in ARIA severity and symptomatology as assessed by follow‐up MRI and clinical assessments. Our results suggest that blanket exclusion of ε4 homozygotes on the basis of increased ARIA risk alone, as recommended by Villain et al. in their set of appropriate‐use recommendations for French memory clinics, may not be entirely justified, 16 given the mild nature of most cases of ARIA.

We were also interested in the sample's overall discontinuation rates and whether ε4 homozygotes were more likely to discontinue than other subgroups. We found that there was no significant difference overall in discontinuation rates by ε4 carrier status by chi‐square test of independence, with each group having equal likelihood to stop lecanemab usage. When the RR for ε4 homozygotes relative to ε4 heterozygotes and ε4 non‐carriers was calculated, this finding was demonstrated again. Notably, the most common reason for discontinuation for lecanemab was the development of ARIA, with just under half of all discontinuations having occurred due to ARIA. It is possible that our small sample size and overall low discontinuation rate may have contributed to the absence of any significant difference. More investigation with larger sample sizes and with consistently longer periods of data collection are needed to evaluate this possibility.

In addition, we evaluated whether a known diagnosis of baseline hypertension, elevated MAP, and antihypertensive use were associated with increased risk of ARIA‐E as found in the TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ2 donanemab post hoc study. In our study, there were no statistically significant associations between any of these factors and the development of ARIA‐E. However, we did observe a trend toward increased ARIA‐E risk with higher MAP. Notably, the trend nearly became statistically significant at the highest MAP range of ≥107.0. We believe that the small sample size was one reason no statistically significant difference was discovered, as only 14 patients had this degree of hypertension. In contrast to the absence of MAP associations for ARIA‐E, we did find a significantly increased risk for ARIA‐H for those with MAP ≥107.0 mmHg relative to those with a MAP <93.0 mmHg. Regarding the lack of association between antihypertensive use and ARIA‐E, we put forth two possibilities: that patients who were recorded as using antihypertensives may have been non‐compliant, and that patients who did not use antihypertensives likely did not have the same underlying risk factor for ARIA‐E (i.e., were not hypertensive) as those who did use antihypertensives. We suggest that further investigation into the effect of blood pressure on ARIA be conducted to further characterize these associations.

Finally, we briefly investigated amyloid clearance via CL values in patients who completed 18 months of treatment and found an average clearance of −90.6 at 18 months, much greater than the reported value of −55.48 in the Clarity‐AD trial. It is not clear why our amyloid reduction was notably higher than the reported value in the Clarity‐AD trial. Our baseline amyloid plaque burden was higher than the noted plaque burden in the Clarity‐AD trial (114.5 compared to 77.92), and our average MMSE score was also the same (25.5 in both).

Our study has several limitations, with many related to the retrospective and real‐world nature of our dataset. First, our sample size was small, with only 146 patients, compared with the much larger samples of randomized, controlled trials such as Clarity‐AD or TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ 2, with 898 and 860 patients in the treatment arms of each. Second, our patient charts include routine clinical documentation, in contrast to a research database. Third, patients who began treatment earlier had more data available for study relative to those who began treatment later. Fourth, we are unable to comment meaningfully on cognitive outcomes due to a lack of scheduled neurocognitive measures throughout treatment. However, our data do show that lecanemab effectively clears amyloid as measured by CL scoring, in congruence with the Clarity‐AD trial.

In agreement with new reports regarding real‐world use of lecanemab, we conclude that lecanemab is generally as safe and effective in routine clinical practice as reported in the Clairity‐AD trial when treatment is administered according to the existing appropriate‐use recommendations. Future avenues of work should focus on treatment efficacy as assessed in real‐world studies with larger sample sizes and more detailed follow‐up cognitive assessments.
