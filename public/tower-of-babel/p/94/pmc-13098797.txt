Securing space domain awareness overview: decisions in the nick of time
Authors: Nesrine Benchoubane, Gunes Karabulut Kurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpace domain awareness (SDA) has evolved in parallel with the growing recognition of space as a domain. It has not developed in isolation; the inexorable presence of cyberspace and electromagnetic spectrum domains further complexifies it. SDA is not only about improving global awareness of space itself; it ultimately guides all decision-making made in it. This review traces the evolution and characteristics of the space domain, the architecture and operational principles of SDA, and the interplay with cyber and spectrum capabilities. It focuses on securing the end-to-end decision chain and highlights how architectural choices, scaling, and integration between physical and non-physical domains influence the resilience of SDA. Emphasis is also placed on forward-looking initiatives, both technical and non technical, that aim to safeguard data, operations and their timing; the linchpin of SDA.Subject terms: Engineering, Religion

Introduction

Five domains—land, sea, air, space, and cyberspace—form the strategic and tactical landscape of modern operations. This structure is articulated by several conceptual models. One such model embeds the domains within the information environment-a pervasive sphere that contains them all1. Other variants instead favor treating information itself as a distinguishable domain, encompassing cyberspace2. Despite the variations, a shared and common thread runs through these models: the distinction between physical and non-physical domains. The latter, sometimes termed “functional”, are more fluid conceptually and, in some models, also include the electromagnetic spectrum3.

For the scope of this review, the first framing is adopted: domains situated within the information environment. This avoids prematurely fixing information as a distinct domain, which implies a certain boundary. Also, it recognizes the existence of other domains that have not yet been exploited.

Domain dynamics

Historically, domains tend to “emerge” organically once new technologies, infrastructures, and operational dependencies accumulate around them, and once states recognize that adversaries may exploit them to gain an advantage. This dynamic interaction between capacity development and strategic competition accounts for why certain domains, hitherto peripheral, became increasingly important in the core area of operations.

The evolutionary trajectory illustrates this emergence clearly. Land and sea are the oldest and most intuitively defined domains. The twentieth century expanded operations vertically with the addition of air and then space. The cyberspace domain, by contrast, differs fundamentally in being non-physical. One of the most firmly established and adopted definitions posits cyberspace as a “global domain within the information environment, consisting of interdependent networks of information technology infrastructures and resident data”4,5. In the allocution of the term, it emphasizes that its components can reside simultaneously in physical and virtual space—not to be conflated with space as a domain; rather, it highlights that it is situated both within and outside of geographic boundaries5.

The next domain to exhibit such a ubiquitous status is the electromagnetic spectrum, which in this aspect may well be the closest match to cyberspace. Paradoxically, its boundaries are clearer than those of the latter, as it is confined by precise physical and temporal properties. And at this level, there is no divergent definition, as it consists solely of the electromagnetic radiation that is organized in terms of frequency (or wavelength). It’s noteworthy to state that the electromagnetic spectrum is perhaps the oldest space-related domain and has been recognized as such since access to space began in the Space Race.

Interestingly, when examining how operations are conducted in all these domains, one sees that the strict separation of domains, with operations confined to a single domain, is now largely obsolete. Even more, growing interdependencies and the rise of joint operations have fostered the development of multi-domain operations and their evolution towards joint all-domain operations, where the defining principle is that no domain operates in isolation. Instead, it depends on the coordinated use of capabilities across all domains6. In the nick of time

One can further assert that the foundation for understanding those developments and directing actions across domains is Domain Awareness (DA). While not formally codified, DA represents (1) an operational knowledge of a domain (2) an ability to assess how other domains influence and are influenced by said domain7. It is therefore domain-centric, but provides both an understanding of the domain itself, also known as Situational Awareness (SA), and an understanding of cross-domain effects. It then forms a solid basis for coordinated action, the exploitation of synergistic effects across domains, and careful management of antagonistic ones.

There are two vital dimensions in any DA: time and data. No decision can be made without one or the other, whether it is good or bad. Together, these determine the credibility, quality and usefulness of the decision. Naturally, when it comes to the data dimension, it lives in the definition itself of DA; as previously defined. Yet the second dimension, that of time, is not fully and accurately captured in definitions of DA, whether admittedly or not, but the qualifiers and how it’s designated are used interchangeably: real time, near real time, and “timely” in all the doctrines. It’s altogether disconcerting seeing as these concepts are very distinct.

Transformative domains

In the broader picture, to date, the domains that stand out as the most transformative are the electromagnetic spectrum, cyberspace, and space. The latter two are relatively new, compared to the electromagnetic spectrum, with its history dating back to awareness of the visible spectrum. The trio, taken together, has reshaped the conduct of operations and is a the catalyst for cross-domain action owing to their accelerated evolution, interdependence, and centrality to multi-domain operations8–10.

In terms of DA, currently, both cyberspace and electromagnetic spectrum lack a fully articulated concept of “Cyber Domain Awareness” (CDA) and “Electromagnetic Domain Awareness” (EMDA) respectively, though related notions of “cyber situational awareness”11 and “electromagnetic situational awareness”12 exist, their pervasiveness across all domains marks them as both the most unifying and disruptive elements in modern operations13,14. Note that absence of CDA and EMDA doesn’t mean that there’s a lack of understanding or ability to take action in either domain, but rather that they’ve spread across domains to such an extent that they can be found in the awareness of the recognized domains. Because of this, the analysis of any physical domain should always account for both.

Space, in turn, is the last physical domain to emerge and has undergone rapid transformation from a restricted, state-dominated theater to a congested, contested, and contentious stage, shaping thus its DA differently over time15,16. DA in space, commonly referred to as Space Domain Awareness (SDA), is well developed and has been evolving rapidly to address the growing complexity and scale of activities within this domain. Scale is particularly pressing, and this is reflected in the upcoming chapter, as well as in the various forms of integration achieved by space systems, which also strain the current capabilities of SDA.

Scope

All the points touched on briefly so far are woven together to examine (1) how decisions are made in the space domain and thus identify strain points and ways in which current strategies can be improved (2) how they are influenced by the dimensions of DA, (3) and what is the true “time" qualifier of SDA? Since both cybernetic and spectrum capabilities are engineered into space systems, they will also be analyzed to demonstrate their role in this decision pipeline.

This review therefore situates SDA with a strong focus on cyberspace and electromagnetic spectrum within a unified framework: first assessing space domain-specific capabilities and then mapping their interactions within the operational pipeline. Next, the governing legal and policy implications are examined. Finally, all points are analyzed to find the true time qualifier of SDA. In closing, the main takeaways and an outlook on the efforts currently being made to make space stronger, more secure and engaged are reported.

Space as a domain

Although by name it may be seen as boundless, space is well demarcated within certain definitions. Two well-known lower physical boundaries constitute the first frontier from the air: the Kármán line, which has been calculated at 83.8 km but (is conventionally placed at 100 km, and the “Astronaut Badge” line at 80 km; both from the Earth’s surface (Ⓢ)17. These lines establish the starting point for what is considered space at the operational level. In addition to the lower limits, an upper operational limit is useful for defining the scope of this analysis, and this is set at the lower limit of Earth’s sphere of influence (SOI). This sphere refers to the region around a celestial body where its gravitational pull dominates the motion of orbiting objects. For Earth, this extends to about 925,000 km, encompassing the Moon’s orbit from the Earth’s center (Ⓒ). The choice of this boundary is ultimately related to operations themselves being largely carried and limited to the Earth’s SOI as this region features the highest density of current space activity.

Architectural evolution at scale

The acceleration in the pace of space operations is evident from the impressive number of objects currently in orbit, whether from present or past activities. As of early 2025, a total of 30,000 objects had been cataloged, of which just under 14,000 represent spacecraft object types. The rest consists mainly of fragments, debris, and rocket bodies19. Much of this has been made possible by the effective commercialization of space and the entry of multiple private operators into the scene. This transformation is often described as “New Space”-an era characterized by the growing role of commercial actors, rapid innovation cycles, and the deployment of proliferated architectures20,21.

These sheer numbers have also been made possible by the evolution in the architecture of space systems, which are now required to be highly scalable and capable of performing multiple missions. Traditional monolithic architectures, in which a single entity controls the design, launch, operation, and ground infrastructure of spacecraft, are increasingly being supplemented or supplanted by hybrid space network (HSN) architectures22. Typically, these architectures are characterized by a high degree of fragmentation, with multiple entities involved in all phases. In addition, the assets themselves have also evolved into different types of distribution schemes23,24. An essential trait of these is miniaturization, which is reflected in reduced manufacturing and launch costs, which facilitates access to space and expansion of proliferated architectures that can be built from them. Scaling within these architectures occurs along two complementary dimensions. One is vertical, which leverages layered orbital assets, from VLEO through GEO (so still within the space domain), and with the potential to extend into cislunar space where appropriate. It goes on to include systems that are within the lower domains, which means more depth and resilience in terms of coverage and service25,26. This vertical point of view parallels closely with the Non-Terrestrial Network (NTN) paradigm gaining traction in 5G and beyond27,28, which envisions a layered network fabric combining terrestrial, aerial, and space-based platforms under unified control or interoperable frameworks to extend connectivity and enhance robustness29–32.

The other dimension concerns horizontal scaling, which exploits the growing number of assets deployed and the expansion of system capabilities through proliferation. However, it also involves nuanced concepts related to duality in terms of dual-use and dual-purpose33,34. Space systems with dual-use characteristics can operate multiple functions, either at the same time or alternately (also referred to as dual capable) based on needs35. Meanwhile, dual-purpose is used to characterize how systems can possess latent capabilities that could be redirected for offensive or disruptive actions. Emerging mission types, including in-orbit refueling, satellite servicing, active debris removal, and in-space assembly, operate squarely within this functional duality35. This duality is probably one of the most essential characteristics of “New Space”, as it brings new areas of risk, with actions intended for benign purposes potentially creating cascading effects or opening up possibilities for exploitation that are not necessarily known at this time.

Operational Integration

Different notational architectures have been developed to structure space operations, each emphasizing a distinct perspective. The first view, widely adopted in system engineering, is the segment-based view. It divides operations into the ground segment, the space segment, the user segment, and the link segment. The ground and space segments are obvious by name, while the user segment captures the endpoint of service delivery spanning all physical domains. The link segment encompasses all communication pathways, which, in the NTN paradigm, are further refined into: (1) feeder links connecting ground and space (GSLs), (2) service links connecting space and users, and (3) space-to-space links, also known as inter/intra-space links (ISLs) or crosslinks. Backhaul is found in (1) on the ground in traditional architectures while fronthaul is found in (2) and (3) linking the rest of the integrated network. However, this is evolving with more capabilities projected to be deployed on-orbit, where a set of backhaul ressources would be elevated above terrestrial assets, in the air and/or in space domains.

The second is adapted from military doctrine, using concepts of lines of communication (LOC), lines of operation (LOO), and data lines of communication (DLOC), which hinge on two aspects: data flow and operational flow36. For data-centric considerations, DLOC and data zones of communication (DZOC) describe the medium through which data flows and the volumetric regions in which it exists, respectively. For operational control, LOOs represent the hierarchical or distributed paths along which commands and decisions propagate. An adaptation to the LOC was also introduced as celestial communication lines (CLOC), which capture the physical and non-physical pathways through which elements move into, through, and out of the space domain37. All CLOCs rely on two primary types of communication systems: radio frequency (RF) and free-space optical (FSO), both operating at distinct regions of the electromagnetic spectrum. First, RF communication utilizes frequencies ranging from 3 kHz to 300 GHz (across different bands), while FSO communication operates in the optical range, typically within 550 nm to 1500 nm wavelengths corresponding to visible and near-infrared bands. In between, is what is known as the “THz gap" with frequencies ranging from 0.1 THz to 10 THz38, planned for future 6G integration39.

Each comes with trade-offs since the shift toward higher frequency bands is primarily driven by the demand for higher data rates. However, operating at these frequencies introduces environmental impairments of greater attenuation to the links40. To counter these effects, systems must employ higher transmission power, high-gain antennas, and narrower beamwidths41,42. While FSO systems can achieve significantly higher data throughput, their shorter wavelengths produce extremely narrow beams, requiring precise and accurate pointing and tracking of communication terminals. While historically, most missions have relied mostly on RF, there are now emerging designs increasingly adopting a hybrid RF/FSO architecture-combining the robustness of RF with the high bandwidth of FSO43–47 and emphasizing better spectrum allocations48,49.

Both perspectives and all CLOCs also map the functions of Telemetry, Tracking, and Telecommand (TT&C) and Payload Data Transmission (PDT). TT&C consists of three lines: monitoring the status and operation of space systems (telemetry), determining their position and orbit (tracking), and executing operational commands (telecommand)50. PDT consists of one line featuring mission-related data. Putting all concepts together, this provides complementary ways of representing the architecture of space operations seen through different lenses. Awareness in space

Space awareness has evolved alongside the growing complexity of space activities and the proliferation of usage and assets. At its core, Space Situational Awareness (SSA) focuses on the detection, tracking, cataloging, and prediction of the location and trajectories of Resident Space Objects (RSOs)-a term that encompasses all human-made objects in orbit, including active satellites, defunct spacecraft, mission-related debris, and fragmentation debris51–53. As such, SSA serves as the fundamental sensing and monitoring function necessary to maintain safe space operations and avoid collisions.

Domain awareness is broader, as it is not limited to knowledge of the kinematics of objects, as it also extends to behavioral analysis, intent inference, threat characterization, and decision support for operational and strategic purposes54. With an underlying SSA structure focused primarily on the detection of RSOs, SDA can then become a systemic capability that integrates SSA and other capabilities. These include:

(i)Space Weather (SWx) which concerns the study and monitoring of the space environment. In particular, the galactic activity and solar environment, where these two are inversely proportional in a course of a decennium55,56. The aim is to provide feedback to SSA and SDA via dedicated missions capable of measuring these phenomena, all of which are liable to impact all systems, both in space and on Earth.

SDA is therefore not simply a detection layer; it is an intelligence-based cross-domain fusion process that combines data from and about assets in the relevant space domain and across, all with the aim of deriving actionable knowledge.

This differentiation is also why the terms “SSA” and “SDA” aren’t interchangeable. While SSA refers to the technical capability to observe and characterize space objects and their environment, SDA represents the operational exploitation of this information to inform decisions, manage threats, and maintain situational and domain advantage.

Given the new architectural extensions, and taking into account the characteristics of the orbits, as these also have a significant influence on the state and timing of operations, it becomes crucial to employ a refined SDA taxonomy55. (i)Low-Ground Space Domain Awareness (LG-SDA) regime which covers near-Earth orbits from VLEO through GSO, is characterized by relatively the densest object populations, frequent orbital maneuvers, and low-latency communications. SDA operations in this regime require rapid data collection, high-fidelity fusion, and reliable decision-making.

It bears mentioning that the dynamics expressed themselves can be part of other known decision flows. The predominant one is loop-based decision making of “Observe, Orient, Decide, Act” (OODA)62, as evidenced63. It’s been adapted here to explicitly highlight where the role of nodes is exercised in the decision process.

Now, looking at the architecture connecting the nodes, the classical SDA’s backhaul is centralized, with all processing and decision-making capabilities primarily held and performed by ground stations on land. While this configuration leverages mature infrastructure and simplifies coordination, it introduces critical choke points that impose changes today: high latency, single points of failure, and limited scalability64. With increasing node numbers in the space domain and operational complexity, the centralized model struggles to maintain fast and resilient decision-making under stress.

Recent developments are pushing toward distributed architectures, moving actuators into orbit65. By collocating more data sourcing and actuator nodes in the space domain, it minimizes latency, increases resilience to failures,66 and enables a reduction in the time to decision. It can also be advantageously used in conjunction with the duality in the space systems observed, which allows additional nodes to be easily and multi-laterally added67,68. As a further development, a hybrid distributed model is envisioned in which more actuators are spread across multiple domains. In this way, tasks can be dynamically assigned to the node best suited, with decision flows parallelized. Also, in terms of placement, for a given operational need, this reduces latency, improves responsiveness to situations, and maximizes coverage across heterogeneous assets. Distant hybrid architectures inherently require a regime-aware, hybrid links and reference-aware perspectives particularly for the assets residing in the space domain in the higher regimes69,70.

Interplay with cyberspace and electromagnetic spectrum domains

"Exquisite” SDA data, which is timely, accurate, and comprehensive information by itself, is an effective cyber countermeasure in space. This concept is defined in the Space Attack Research & Tactic Analysis (SPARTA) framework published by The Aerospace Corporation. Indeed, information derived from the decision-making process enables operators to effectively distinguish between intentional and unintentional actions in space due to its component of inference (refer to 2.2).

Thus, while acting as an implicit countermeasure to improve the security of space operations, it is also permeated at all levels by cyberspace itself. This creates a coalescence: SDA feeds are essential for cyber defense in space, while cyber defense measures are required to preserve the fidelity and timeliness of SDA. This kind of coordinated interaction shows a form of “convergence”, where these domains jointly shape operations71. The same applies to the electromagnetic spectrum which under most doctrines, is almost always bundled within cyberspace, but a clear distinction between the two here is always maintained.

The convergence of the trio, which the outcome is ultimately the decision process of SDA, requires utmost safeguarding to ensure operational security. The dramatic 118% year-on-year escalation in reported cyber threats between 2024 and August 2025, including spectrum-related threats, further illustrate the urgency72,73. Interestingly, the primary objective of these attacks lies in disrupting operations, but equally, and perhaps more to the fore, in eroding trust in space72.

Attack surfaces

A closer look is taken at how non-kinetic cybernetic and spectrum capabilities can have a destabilizing potential which manifests in the use of cyber anti-satellite weapons (ASAT)74, and spectrum-ASAT to adversely affect operations.75 This leads us to an examination of the attack surface, which, in a formal definition, corresponds to the exploitation of a set of system resources that can be used to attack said system. For the SDA, this boils down to two axes: (1) the assets themselves (nodes and links) (2) the architecture itself in how it connects and distributes the assets.

First, on the assets themselves. When a node and/or line is vulnerable, whether intentionally or not, it becomes a privileged target. High accessibility and relatively low barriers to exploitation make these particularly attractive, especially for ground assets and RF links76. Various frameworks provide a holistic view of how this can be done, covering from the supply chain entry points to operational dependencies77–79. It is also increasingly recognized that adversaries themselves do not necessarily have of “anthropogenic origin"; i.e another nation or group of actors, but can also be nature itself as they impact links and the communication channels in quality and availability77,80, which can then open up windows of exposure. The second axis pertains to the architectural choices that significantly influence its level of vulnerability. In the current centralized SDA architecture, the ground segment is a prime target. Although this reduces the number of high-value nodes and simplifies management, it also results in single points of failure: the compromise of one element could render the entire system blind and ineffective. In contrast, distributed architectures spread functions across many nodes, which limits single points of failure for a larger, more heterogeneous attack surface that is also dependent on the scaling direction.

For both axes, the threat techniques used can be of two types: passive and active, where passive means they have no residual impact, and active means the opposite. The following lists for each type are not exhaustive and are provided as a means to illustrate the threat landscape. Furthermore, the threats presented here are indexed from the SPARTA framework77; however, bear in mind that cross identification with most frameworks is also achievable.

On the passive side, it can take the form of illicit eavesdropping-from RF to optical to capture the communication (REC-0005), to Side-Channel Exfiltration (EXF-0002), all the way to signal interception (EXF-0003).

What is crucial is to continuously and punctually examine the attack surface for both types, where capabilities are constantly growing (of SDAs and its adversaries)81–83. Any open attack surface would ultimately influence the decision process, whether the effect is minimal or significant if it manifests. The impact, however, is mitigated, minimized, or transferred by implementing the right protection techniques. It is with respect to that that all the frameworks mentioned above already specify the necessary techniques for each threat technique and are always evolving. It is also worth referring to special systems used to secure space domain as a whole. Jamming, as an active attack, is a prime concern for all nations, even more so than covert interception. For this reason, the Protected Anti-Jam Tactical satellite communication (PAT), as a communication bandwidth sharing framework for high-speed communications, was announced this year to enable secure tactical communication between allies under such attacks84. This highlights the degree of resilience that is needed, now, to stay operational, and especially to carry ongoing communication and thus delivery of what is needed for the decision process that cannot be halted under any circumstances.

It’s not just a matter of securing the space ecosystem or certain missions as components of SDA, rather ultimately SDA itself. Even when the assets and architecture are secure, the attack surface won’t stop there. As a data-centric capability, the data entering, traversing, and exiting the pipeline needs to be safeguarded at all times. The accent is placed firmly on how it is ingested, fused, analyzed, and executed. Compromising at any of these points (entry, transit, exit) is more difficult and demands greater sophistication; yet, it is not beyond reach. It can be argued that this is related to the second axis of architecture, but it is more than that. Since there is a growing dependence on multi-party, multi-mission data streams to deliver higher coverage and persistence, it goes beyond what constitutes an internal SDA architecture. It depends on the trust established with the different collaborators whose architecture is external. Here, the relevant techniques for attack techniques are for instance: trusted relationships (IA-0009); hosted payloads (LM-0001), as examples of what can be operationalized to compromise.

Security tenets

The impact on the decision process is twofold; on the data and time, manifesting in errors that can propagate forward non-linearly over time, or in time; delays and interruptions to delivery of what SDA needs to deliver. As a data-centric capability, it creates multiple requirements for mechanisms linked to data provenance, source authentication, integrity protection, and utility evaluation71, on top of the protection techniques engineered on the systems. All these requirements are sourced from fundamental security tenets.

The strongest framework that the review aligns with for the security tenets is the Parkerian hexad, which builds on the traditional CIA triad-confidentiality, integrity, and availability85. To put it succinctly: confidentiality ensures secrecy, integrity ensures completeness, and availability guaranties usability for the purpose. The hexad adds three security tenets: authenticity, possession, and utility, where the latter by its name emphasizes that data remains consistent with its original purpose. Authenticity emphasizes validity and genuineness to protect the intrinsic value of data itself. Finally, possession emphasizes control and ultimately encompasses even the loss of ownership.

Incontrovertible Importance of Governance

Undeniably, as seen, plenty of respects have shaped space, its SDA, its security discourse (that doesn’t look too bright) and perhaps the one respect left is that of governance. This has been present since the emergence of space, and has played a decisive part and continues to do so. It begins with the way space is defined.

Under the Outer Space Treaty (OST), space is “a global commons”; states cannot claim ownership of territory, but they retain jurisdiction over objects they have registered86. In addition, space, which encompasses all celestial bodies, is also the subject of a treaty called the Moon Agreement, which further recognizes celestial bodies, the Moon and others, as “the common heritage of mankind”87. The two treaties, along with other legislation, have shaped current space operations, and their conduct. In the following, a cursory account of current space legislation, how it pertains to SDA and its cyber and spectrum capabilities is given to inform a fuller understanding of the current state, followed by a presentation of the most promising efforts currently underway that will ultimately safeguard global space stewardship for all stakeholders.

In force

In addition to the OST and the Moon Agreement, other legislation has been enacted to supplement these fundamental instruments. The Artemis Accords88, for example, serve as a framework for international cooperation in space exploration, particularly in the context of the United States and other nations planning to return to the Moon. But it doesn’t stop there, as there is eve strong momentum for new legislation to improve the sustainability of space operations, debris reduction, and environmental integrity and fairness in this field for all actors89–91. It serves to remind that sustainability of space operations has always been an ongoing challenge. Even back in 1996, there were 2400 tons of ASOs reported still in orbit from the Cold War92, and this has exponentially grown since.

SDA, for its part, has been shaped by all of these enactments. More than the sustainability of assets and operations, it intrinsically deals with data and timing, how it is ingested and returned, which puts data sharing and transparency at the forefront. These have also been reinforced by the guidelines89, which stresses how the sustainability of operations on the safety level starts with effective, accurate and updated data sharing; of objects, events and standards even. To this respect, the new STC standard93, within STM capacity, and complementing established Consultative Committee for Space Data Systems (CCSDS) standards94, both pursue effective coordination, through interoperability, and are perhaps the closest to recognized international standards at this level.

Yet any discussion of data sharing inevitably leads to a need to account for security requirements to protect it. Whether data is proprietary or shared, in use or transit or at rest, this implies (1) strengthening cybersecurity within the systems themselves through new standards95–97, which extend to the entire operational workflow. (2) significantly reinforcing at the governance level98 to ensure alignment on security requirements. In the mediums, through which is shared, where electromagnetic spectrum capabilities are concerned, this falls under the remit of the International Telecommunication Union (ITU)99,. ITU is instrumental in managing the global spectrum used for satellite communications in the entire RF spectrum and associated resources in LG-regime. However, the ITU, as stated, mainly in its current functions focuses on RF spectrum management and does not have a specific standard for optical communications.

Clearly, existing governance structures are trying to promote global cooperation to address challenges common to all stakeholders. A thorough country-wide inventory at the global scale has been created by the United Nations Institute for Disarmament Research (UNIDIR) under the Space Security Portal100, which maps the global landscape of space security governance for all nations. However, it is also clear that the commercialization of the domain has resulted in swifter change than anticipated or regulation at the time by the prevailing governance frameworks. It is also evident that as a direct conclusion, isolationism and non-interventionism are not propitious for space sustainability, safety and security; all current known and future concerns will not disappear on their own accord nor with time, but rather require tangible concerted commitment from all.

Silver Lining

Above all, it should be emphasized that there is no dearth of unified, peaceful efforts and initiatives already underway on a global scale. This entry will attempt to highlight some of these efforts, but it is by no means exhaustive.

Within cooperation and collaboration frameworks, several initiatives are underway to push toward this cooperative exchange, which also achieves greater transparency and thus aids in the effort of sustainability, safety and security52,101,102. This is also explicitly supported, by the U.S. Space Force in August 2025, acknowledging that it does not hold the majority of SDA data and intends to leverage commercial capabilities to augment government-owned sources. At this front, the ITU is also organizing the future World Radiocommunication Conference (RWC), following consultations, in 2027, for which the agenda is available on here, aiming to achieve harmonized global or regional spectrum strategies, ensuring compatibility and avoiding interference with existing terrestrial networks and others; all of which will be essential stepping stones towards better operational management and standardized usage of the link segments across all vertical layers.

With regard to utilization aspects, and before reaching overutilization, the Working Group on Legal Aspects of Space Resources Activities103, within The United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA), has already started legal consultations on the principles governing the exploration and use of outer space, including space resources activities, with an emphasis on the advancement of all humanity and in the interests of all nations. Although these are not binding principles, to date, they represent a major breakthrough for peaceful purposes, in which legal efforts are ahead of the technical ones on the curve since MG access is at a very early stage.

Furthermore, the current utilization that (already) interferes (heavily) with other services in space has also found its match104. For instance, the radio astronomy working group has recently consolidated recommendations for LEO orbits for more sustainable use (impact on radio astronomy capabilities directly interferes with other SDA capabilities in terms of SWx and even SSA)105. To add to this, even if the recommendation to date is on LEO orbits for now, the future recommendation will certainly include VLEO satellites next year with their expected proliferation.

As a recent exertion, one view is to establish further regulations in the higher Heavens (MG and higher regimes of SDA)106. Together with the observation that this can be in line with the new dichotomy proposed between dark space and blue space to address the competitive and contentious nature of space known today byL delimitation107. The dichotomy proposed in here could, in fact, provide an intrinsic change in the doctrines themselves to establish joint lines of effort in collaboration, where possible, and thus expand missions and utilization (as long as it aligns with proper principles given above); all the while remaining aligned with an evolving national security posture of all.

In the nick of time

Perhaps the first thing to agree on is that the timescale observed so far in the space sector is long-term; in current challenges, effects, emergence and so on. Bear in mind that this can take several forms, given that various sectors are and will become even more dependent on space – there is an immediate future, a near future, and a distant future108. Even if some missions or instances are short-term, or even very short-term109, the distinct actors in space domain at large, as in any physical domain, embody a long-term vision. Here, the discussion will start on the standard of time. This is important before deciding how swiftly a decision should be made, which will be discussed next. The concluding point looks at the now and then workings of SDA.

L’Heure (A)Normale

Standard time, known as “heure normale” in French, refers to the globally standardized time, which is Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) on Earth. However, as mentioned earlier, when it comes to the MG-SDA regime’s time reference, no standard has been established; thus, it is considered “heure anormale”. Adding to this, the emphasis placed previously on orbit references (with surface Ⓢ and center © on Earth) adds to the complexity, where the need to establish this is apparent in MG regime.

The establishment of a universal lunar time standard is presently underway, with (1) the United States and its partners (signatories of Artemis Accords) aiming to create a unified standard called Coordinated Lunar Time (LTC) by the end of 2026110. (2) China and Russia, which are collaborating on a separate International Lunar Research Station (ILRS) program, are not signatories of the Artemis Accords and are expected to form a new time standard; although they stick to UTC so far with establishment of time correction processes instead111.

Beyond the question of whether consensus will be reached on this front and whether there will ever be an agreed-upon time (one or many) for and on the Moon, what is certain is that the establishment of the standard(s) is a strict requirement. Notably, it impacts communication, positioning, navigation, and timing (PNT) infrastructure, which is the basis for time dissemination and synchronization. As time flows 56 microseconds per Earth day faster on the Moon than on Earth due to lower gravity, PNT systems must operate in a highly synchronized manner, also across the regimes as these are not expected to operate in isolation.

Another key challenge identified in this respect involves the alignment of standards (Earth and Moon), between the two regimes, as the transition from the UTC time base on Earth to lunar time standards will not be straightforward, given their current maturity. This is therefore a crucial area of current research, as it concerns not only the MG regime, but also how best its interoperability and seamless coordination can be achieved with the LG regime in space and downstream domains.

Timeliness

For all regimes, the decision process shall be timely. This is the most frequently used label to describe the current state of SDA, or perhaps more accurately, “tactically relevant timelines”56. In such elocutions, it reminds that delivery timelines are not always the same for all capabilities, attack surface readiness, and the full process end-to-end. What is also stressed is that this is speeding up, not slowing down, due to the increase in scale and integration from both angles, sheer numbers, and the attack surface of all players involved. However, this is not precise enough, which is why it is also possible to say, “just in the nick of time.”

These labels used so far are “qualitative,” and a more significant divergence arises when attempting to quantify the swiftness of SDA. It should be noted here that all doctrines use the terms “real time” and “near real time” indiscriminately, whether by misstatement or inadvertence. However, these two terms are distinct and can not be used simultaneously to characterize the speed with which 1) the products of SDA are delivered; space environment monitoring, conjuction assessment in early phases of space systems and deorbiting/re-entry 2) the effects and impacts of open attack surfaces, taking into account all various threats, 3) and the end-to-end decision process; where all three are encountered at unequal rates.

What is crucially missing, and what this analysis highlights, is that SDA will need to align itself with a “real-time” knowledge system soon enough, such as real-time situational awareness (RTSA) or near real-time situational awareness (NRTSA) that exist in contiguous domains. It would become “real-time space domain awareness (RTSDA) or “near real-time space domain awareness” (NRTSDA), which does not exist today. This also goes to reaffirm that SSA is not equivalent to SDA. Even with RTSA for space (which is not the case)112, SSA remains a component of SDA, which means that the decision-making process has not yet resulted in a decision that will certainly not include zero latency, making SDA at best at NRT level.

What can be disentangled further in this analysis are the differences between real time and near real time, namely: 1) latency, 2) data freshness, 3) the variability of both over time. Here, the term “latency” encompasses everything from generation to fusion, including processing and all operations to be included. The freshness is not only the capabilities of the SDA, but also those of partnerships. The addition of the last element of variability is of crucial importance: these quantifications would vary depending on the operation, the environment, the regime, the orbit, and the space system itself, depending back also on its requirements.

Future work will further develop all the terms introduced here in order to break it down by operation performed and include formally notions of age of information that encompasses further metrics, namely peak and average age of information (PAoI and AAoI), age of critical information (AoCI)113,114. Where AoI captures the worst-case staleness, AAoI reflects average freshness, and AoCI focuses on time-sensitive updates. Taken together, they can be utilized to evaluate and optimize how quickly and reliably data propagates through the end-to-end process to lead to an effective accurate depiction of the time state of SDA.

It should be noted that this must be taken into account along with the tenets mentioned beforehand. It is not a question of solely carrying out massive data operations and handling enormous data volumes, increasing freshness endlessly for instance; usefulness is an underestimated measure that allows us to “spare time” to ascertain the best suitable node to task (actuator to sourcing nodes), what interval, what resources to put in place, which effectively would speed up the end-to-end process.

Key takeaways

With all the dynamics seen at play, it’s evident that securing SDA hinges on many aspects. When it comes to the SDA network itself, its design dictates resilience. With both horizontal (more nodes and greater functional diversity) and vertical (more in-depth curation and greater precision) evolutions in design, the attack surface evolves in a non-linear way with time. Additionally, the topology itself dictates how performance may degrade and recover under diverse adverse conditions. Then again, so do the different regimes, because beyond the density and dualities of the systems at different levels, each regime imposes its own constraints on its time, nodes and lines, shaping what is technically achievable.

At the operational level, SDA strives to make faster decisions. This can be achieved through the operationalisation of c redible, u seful and higher q uality data. This entails protecting the security tenets in all data operations at rest, in transit and in use over time through the reinforcement of cyber and spectrum capabilities from within the systems themselves and extending all the way to the end-to-end decision process. This vision is considerably reinforced at the governance level, with it generating a strong impetus for new legislation to improve the sustainability, safety and security of space operations. What is certain is that any new legislative and normative frameworks for more sustainable operations will, in turn, also shape the long-term success of SDA for all nations, spacefaring or not.

Yet, as a concluding remark, although this review has not delved into the dimension of kinetic threats, what would impede any future operations in space are precisely these threats. Unlike non-kinetic cybernetic and spectrum capabilities discussed, their kinetic spin-offs are irreversible, nevertheless both weigh heavily on the overall security calculus of space operations.

Taking all together, the trajectory of SDA highlights a pressing need for more mature and sustainable architectures, capable not only of ensuring continuity of operations but also of serving as a credible layer of deterrence against any destructive means in the space domain for all nations, spacefaring or not.
