Secondary hyperparathyroidism in adult congenital heart disease: the CHD-HYPER-study
Authors: Friederike Löffler, Justus Christian Garlichs, Kirsten Linhorst, Ann-Sophie Silber-Peest, Sabrina Uehlein, Holger Leitolf, Christoph Terkamp, Katja Deterding, Johann Bauersachs, Mechthild Westhoff-Bleck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsIn non-congenital heart disease, secondary hyperparathyroidism (sHPT) is associated with an elevated risk of new-onset heart failure (HF) and an increased incidence of HF-related hospitalizations. Yet, for adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD), the role of sHPT and the factors contributing to its development remain poorly understood.Methods and resultsThis cross-sectional, single-centre study assessed the prevalence of sHPT in 754 patients with ACHD. Independent predictors of sHPT were identified in both, within the entire cohort and specifically in ACHD with biventricular physiology.FindingsWe found a high prevalence of sHPT in ACHD at 14.9%, with the highest rates in patients with Eisenmenger syndrome/PAH-CHD (39.1%), Ebstein’s anomaly (29.2%), Fontan palliation (25%), and pulmonary atresia (25%). SHPT was more common in patients with univentricular physiology (29.6%) than biventricular physiology (13.3%) (P < 0.001). In multivariate analysis, glomerular filtration rate (P < 0.001), serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D₃ (P = 0.004), use of loop diuretics (P = 0.001), oxygen saturation (P = 0.03), and liver stiffness (P = 0.033) were independently associated with sHPT. Among patients with biventricular physiology, right ventricular free wall longitudinal strain (P = 0.028)—rather than left ventricular global longitudinal strain—showed a significant association with the presence of sHPT.ConclusionsHPT is observed across the spectrum of ACHD but is more common in patients with complex and more severe disease, particularly those with predominant right heart involvement.

Introduction

In non-congenital heart disease, secondary hyperparathyroidism (sHPT) is linked to an elevated risk of developing new-onset heart failure (HF) in older patients.1 Several studies in elderly cohorts have demonstrated that serum concentrations of parathyroid hormone (PTH) are significantly elevated in patients with HF and are associated with left ventricular ejection fraction, NT-proBNP, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, renal function, and the use of loop diuretics.2–4 Furthermore, higher rates of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, and a greater likelihood of hospitalization due to HF,5–7 PTH has also been proposed as a biomarker for HF.8

The activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system leads to hyperaldosteronism, which causes calcium loss through the faeces and kidneys and results in elevated levels of PTH. Loop diuretics, commonly used to treat HF, also contribute to sHPT by further increasing calcium loss.9 PTH also fosters the progression of HF by directly increasing aldosterone secretion through the stimulation of the adrenal PTH receptor and by enhancing angiotensin II-induced aldosterone secretion.10 PTH also increases intracellular calcium concentration in cardiomyocytes, leading to intracellular and mitochondrial calcium overload, oxidative stress, myocardial cell death, and eventually, myocardial fibrosis.11,12 Moreover, sHPT may exacerbate cardiovascular complications and contribute to bone metabolism disorders.13

In adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD), only one study has investigated PTH levels and vitamin D deficiency. This study, conducted in a cohort of 103 hospitalized patients, found that sHPT was associated with elevated NT-proBNP levels and more advanced New York Heart Association (NYHA) class.14Additionally, sHPT has been reported with an observable prevalence in both children and adults with Fontan circulation.15,16

In recent decades, life expectancy of ACHD significantly increased due to advances in medical and surgical care.17 In non-CHD populations, the complications associated with elevated PTH levels, such as cardiovascular morbidity and bone density disorders, are well documented.1,12,18 Although sHPT is known to carry potential severe complications, data on its prevalence in the ACHD population remain limited. Given that ACHD patients represent a growing and particularly vulnerable group, further research into the prevalence and clinical impact of sHPT within this population is warranted.

The primary aim of this study is to assess the prevalence and key determinants of sHPT across a broad spectrum of ACHD patients. Although sHPT has been reported in patients with Fontan circulation, data on other ACHD subgroups remain limited. Our goal is to address this gap by systematically analysing sHPT prevalence beyond the Fontan population and identifying clinical characteristics associated with sHPT.

Materials and methods

Study group

This study was a single-centre observational study which was conducted at Hannover Medical School. The trial was approved by the ethics committee of Hannover Medical School. All patients included in our study provided written informed consent. Between August 2023 and January 2024, we screened 844 patients. At least 755 adult patients were included in the study (Figure 1).

Patient inclusion. Model 1 includes the total patient cohort. Model 2 includes patients with biventricular physiology, excluding patients with a systemic morphologic right ventricle. Model 3 includes patients with biventricular physiology, together with patients with a systemic morphologic right ventricle.

We obtained patients’ baseline clinical data from electronical medical records, including the initial cardiac diagnosis, Bethesda class, cardiovascular operations and interventions and cardiovascular complications such as a history of arrhythmias or hospital admission due to cardiac decompensation. Arrhythmias were defined as sustained supraventricular or sustained ventricular arrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, and ventricular fibrillation. Cardiac decompensation was defined as hospital admission due to clinically documented worsening of HF, based on signs and symptoms requiring escalation of therapy.

All other patient data were obtained from the inclusion visit: NYHA class, gender, age, height, weight, BMI, pulse oximetry, laboratory data, current medication, and echocardiographic findings.

The complexity of congenital heart defects was classified using the Bethesda system, which categorizes defects as simple, moderate, or complex based on anatomical criteria.19 For analytical purposes, the Bethesda categories were numerically coded as: complex = 1, moderate = 2, simple = 3.

Exclusion criteria were defined as age <18 years, pregnancy, current systemic infection, active malignancy, and lacking written informed consent.

Since congenital heart disease encompasses a heterogeneous variety of heart defects, patients were classified into 14 distinct groups as follows: simple shunt lesions (including isolated, hemodynamically insignificant atrial septal defects [ASDs], ventricular septal defects [VSDs], and patent foramen ovale [PFO]); complex shunt lesions (including atrioventricular septal defects [AVSDs] and total or partial anomalous pulmonary venous return [TAPVR/PAPVR]); Ebstein anomaly; anomalies of the left ventricular outflow tract; coarctation of the aorta; other conotruncal anomalies (including d-transposition of the great arteries [d-TGA] after arterial switch, double outlet right ventricle [DORV], and truncus arteriosus with biventricular repair); congenital pulmonary stenosis; Tetralogy of Fallot; pulmonary atresia with biventricular repair (with or without ventricular septal defect); systemic morphological right ventricle (including d-TGA palliated with Mustard or Senning repair and congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries); congenital aortopathy (including Marfan syndrome, Loeys–Dietz syndrome, vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, and other familial aortopathies supported by genetic or clinical evidence but not meeting full syndromic criteria); heart defects corrected through Fontan repair; Eisenmenger syndrome or precapillary pulmonary hypertension associated with congenital heart disease (PAH-CHD), including patients with functionally univentricular heart defects that are either unpalliated (native) or partially palliated; and miscellaneous heart defects that could not be otherwise classified (for a more detailed classification, see Supplementary material online, Appendix A1).

Laboratory measurements and transient elastography

Serum levels of sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphate, and liver function tests (including alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyltransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, cholinesterase, and bilirubin), as well as kidney function tests (creatinine clearance), albumin, thyroid-stimulating hormone, HbA1c, lipid levels, and complete blood count, were measured using standard laboratory techniques. Markers of systemic inflammation, such as CRP and GDF-15, as well as NT-proBNP were also assessed. The APRI score was utilized to assess liver fibrosis and cirrhosis.20 Additionally, transient elastography (FibroScan®, Echosens, Paris, France) was used to assess liver stiffness.

To evaluate vitamin D levels and hyperparathyroidism (sHPT), measurements of 25-hydroxyvitamin D, alkaline bone phosphatase, and intact PTH were conducted using standard laboratory procedures. Based on the Endocrine Society’s Practice Guidelines on Vitamin D, vitamin D deficiency was defined as a 25-hydroxyvitamin D level below 20 ng/mL and vitamin D insufficiency as a 25(OH)D of 21–29 ng/mL.21

sHPT was identified by an intact PTH level above 65 pg/mL with normal serum calcium levels, which represents the cut-off value of the institutional biochemistry laboratory. No patient exhibiting primary hyperparathyroidism, indicated by elevated serum calcium and elevated PTH levels, was identified in our study.

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed using a Philips EPIQ CVxI system. A standardized imaging protocol was applied across all patients. This protocol included comprehensive assessment of sub-aortic and sub-pulmonary ventricular function using the modified biplane Simpson’s method and myocardial strain analysis. Diastolic function of both ventricles was systematically evaluated, alongside detailed assessment of valvular function. Additional measurements included aortic diameters, size and collapsibility of the inferior vena cava, and spleen dimensions. All studies were conducted by two experienced echocardiographers (F.L. and M.W.B.) both specialized in congenital and structural heart disease imaging. To ensure diagnostic accuracy and consistency, all echocardiographic studies were independently interpreted and subsequently verified by both examiners.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS II version 29.0.1.0 (SPSS Inc, Troy, NY). Differences between groups in continuous and categorical variables were calculated using unpaired t-test, Mann–Whitney U-test or the χ2 test as appropriate. Two-sided tests were used throughout. A value of P < 0.05 was considered as significant.

Multivariate binary logistic regression was performed to identify variables independently associated with sHPT, which served as the dependent variable in all models. Variables considered for inclusion in the multivariate models were selected based on a combination of statistical significance in univariate analyses and clinical relevance to the pathophysiology of sHPT.

We constructed three distinct models: one for the entire cohort (model 1) and two alternative models specifically for patients with biventricular physiology (model 2 and 3).

The variables incorporated into model 1 (n = 740) encompassed metabolic syndrome (defined as the presence of at least three of the following criteria: waist size ≥ 88 cm (female) or ≥102 cm (male), high density lipoproteins < 50 mg/dL (female) or <40 mg/dL (male), blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg systolic or ≥ 85 mmHg diastolic, HbA1c ≥ 5.7%), Bethesda class, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), glomerular filtration rate (GFR), NT-proBNP, GDF-15, 25-hydroxyvitamin D3, the APRI score, liver stiffness estimated through transient elastography, as well as the administration of aldosterone antagonists and/or loop diuretics (Table 4).

The variables integrated into model 2 (n = 358) included metabolic syndrome, Bethesda class, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), GFR, NT-proBNP, GDF-15, 25-hydroxyvitamin D3, the APRI score, liver stiffness assessed via transient elastography, the utilization of aldosterone antagonists, and/or loop diuretics. Furthermore, parameters relating to ventricular function, such as left ventricular global strain (LV GLS) and right ventricular free wall longitudinal strain (RV FWLS), were incorporated into the analysis. LV GLS was applied only to systemic morphologic left ventricles, while RV FWLS was applied only to sub-pulmonary morphologic right ventricles. Patients with a systemic morphological right ventricle were excluded from the multivariate binary logistic regression analysis because the measurements of sub-aortic and sub-pulmonary strain may not be accurate due to inadequate alignment of the two-chamber view (Table 4).

Model 3 (n = 582) included patients with biventricular physiology, including those with a systemic morphologic right ventricle. Ejection fraction, assessed using Simpson's method, and RV S’ were used as alternative indicators of systemic and sub-pulmonary ventricular function, independent of ventricular morphology, replacing LV GLS and RV FWLS (Table 4).

All multivariate binary logistic regression models were adjusted for age and sex.

sHPT in ACHD

The overall prevalence of sHPT was 14.9% (n = 112) (Figure 3). The highest prevalence of sHPT was found in the patient group with Eisenmenger syndrome/PAH-CHD (39.1%, n = 9), followed by Ebstein’s anomaly (29.2%, n = 7), Fontan palliation (25%, n = 12), pulmonary atresia (25%, n = 5), congenital aortopathy (20.9%, n = 9), systemic morphological right ventricle (19%, n = 12), and complex shunt lesions (17%, n = 8) (Figure 2). The prevalence of sHPT in the seven patient groups mentioned above was higher than the mean prevalence of sHPT in the entire patient cohort.

In univentricular hearts, the prevalence of sHPT was 29.6% (n = 21), while in biventricular hearts, it was 13.3% (n = 91) (P < 0.001) (Figure 3).

Direct comparison between patients with sHPT and those without showed significant differences in NYHA class (P < 0.001), and O2 saturation (P = 0.001; CI = −3.28—(−0.84)) (see Table 2). Furthermore, patients with sHPT had significantly higher levels of NT-proBNP (P = 0.001; CI = 238.68–919.24) and GDF-15 (P < 0.001; CI = 415.33–1318.08). Additionally, CRP was significantly higher in patients with sHPT (P = 0.029; CI = 0.11− 2.05). Regarding renal function, the group of patients with sHPT had a lower GFR (P < 0.001, CI = −18.82—(−9.45)). Liver stiffness measured by FibroScan® was significantly higher in patients with sHPT (P = 0.006; CI = 1.14–6.75), and yGT and bilirubine levels were worse compared to patients without sHPT (all P < 0.045), Table 2. Discussion

Our study presents the first comprehensive analysis of sHPT prevalence and associated factors in ACHD, revealing an overall rate of 14.9%. Certain subgroups showed markedly higher prevalence, including patients with Eisenmenger syndrome/PAH-CHD (39.1%), Ebstein’s anomaly (29.2%), Fontan palliation (25%), pulmonary atresia (25%), congenital aortopathy (20.9%), a systemic morphological right ventricle (19%), and complex shunt lesions (17%).

These findings indicate that sHPT is particularly prevalent among patients with more complex and severe forms of CHD. In univentricular physiology, the prevalence of sHPT was significantly higher at 29.6% compared to 13.3% in biventricular physiology, further underscoring the link between disease complexity and sHPT. Comparison between patients with and without sHPT revealed significant differences: those with sHPT presented with more complex heart disease and worse NYHA classifications, lower oxygen saturation levels, and higher biomarkers such as NT-proBNP and GDF-15. Additionally, loop diuretics and aldosterone antagonists were more frequently prescribed in the sHPT group, suggesting advanced HF.

In non-congenital heart disease, prior studies in elderly patients found that elevated PTH levels were associated with higher NYHA class, decreased left ventricular function, and increased NT-proBNP levels.7,22–24 However, these studies primarily focused on left ventricular HF, leaving the potential role of the right ventricle unclear. In our study, in patients with biventricular physiology, excluding those with a systemic morphologic right ventricle, multivariate binary regression analysis identified right ventricular free wall longitudinal strain (RV FWLS) as significantly associated with the presence of sHPT. According to multivariate regression analysis, a more negative RV FWLS is associated with a lower likelihood of sHPT. Conversely, sub-aortic LV GLS and systemic ventricular ejection fraction were not independently associated with sHPT in our patient cohort. These findings suggest that sub-pulmonary ventricular dysfunction may be more closely linked to sHPT in ACHD than systemic ventricular function. However, given the cross-sectional nature of our study, no causal conclusions can be drawn. It remains possible that sHPT reflects the severity of underlying HF rather than being a causal factor of sub-pulmonary ventricular dysfunction.

In ACHD, right HF is highly prevalent, as many congenital heart defects primarily involve the right ventricle.25 It is well known that patients with an absent right ventricle, like those with Fontan circulation, are at an increased risk of developing sHPT due to persistently elevated venous pressures.15 In the full cohort, multivariate analysis showed that reduced liver stiffness is linked to a decreased risk of sHPT. Liver stiffness reflects central venous pressure and remains elevated post-recompensation, indicating hepatic remodeling.26,27 However, in patients with biventricular physiology, estimated sub-pulmonary pressures were not significantly associated with sHPT. Chronic hepatic congestion may impair vitamin D synthesis and calcium absorption, contributing to sHPT. Additionally, reduced gastrointestinal perfusion may lead to protein loss and nutrient malabsorption. Lower albumin levels—observed in our sHPT group—can diminish calcium transport, further promoting hypocalcemia and sHPT.28

Multivariate regression analysis also revealed that O2 saturation is associated with sHPT, consistent with the findings of Izumi et al.14 Hypoxia may promote inflammation through several mechanisms, including hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha, the stimulation of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-alpha,29 and the activation of NF-kB, a key regulator of inflammatory responses.30 In this context, GDF-15 is expressed and secreted in response to inflammation, oxidative stress, and hypoxia, among other factors.31 In our patient cohort, GDF-15 levels were significantly higher in individuals with sHPT compared to those without, suggesting increased inflammation in patients with sHPT.

Hypoxia causes kidney damage, leading to renal inflammation, fibrosis, and a subsequent decline in renal function,32 thus also impairing the hydroxylation of vitamin D into its active form, contributing to the development of sHPT.

It is not surprising that renal function and the use of loop diuretics were also found to be independent predictors of sHPT in adult patients with CHD. Renal function is crucial for maintaining calcium and phosphate homeostasis and for activating vitamin D, which regulates PTH secretion.33 In our multivariate analysis, the use of loop diuretics was strongly associated with sHPT. In contrast, thiazide diuretics are known to reduce urinary calcium excretion by enhancing calcium reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubule, which can lead to mildly elevated serum calcium levels and subsequently suppress PTH secretion.34 However, this effect is generally modest and likely had little relevance in our study population, where loop diuretics were more commonly prescribed due to HF and only 15 patients (2%) received thiazide therapy.

Aldosterone antagonists may also help reduce urinary calcium excretion and support serum calcium levels, potentially attenuating PTH secretion.35 They were more frequently used among patients with sHPT, reflecting the presence of more advanced HF. Their potential protective effect was not evident, likely because it was outweighed by stronger contributing factors—most notably the impact of loop diuretics—on the development of sHPT.

The overall prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in the cohort was 43%, with a higher prevalence in patients with univentricular physiology (52.1%). This incidence was lower than the 61.6% reported in the German Health Interview and Examination Survey for Adults (DEGS1) conducted between 2008 and 2011.36 Only 17.2% of all patients had sufficient vitamin D levels, highlighting a critical area for intervention, as adequate vitamin D levels are essential for managing sHPT.

Persistent sHPT has been linked to complications such as osteoporosis, fractures, renal impairment, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease in other patient populations.34,37,38 Although our study did not assess clinical outcomes directly, these established associations highlight the potential relevance of sHPT in ACHD and underscore the need for further research in this area. Identifying and addressing sHPT may represent an opportunity to mitigate additional risk factors in this vulnerable population. Whether interventions such as optimized vitamin D supplementation could help prevent complications related to sHPT in ACHD patients remains to be clarified in future studies.

Limitations

The first limitation of our study is that it was conducted at a single centre, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Nevertheless, our study features a typical ACHD cohort, consistent with those presented in previous ACHD studies. Additionally, the small sizes of the subgroup populations restricted the scope and depth of subgroup analysis. Furthermore, it was not possible to assess sub-aortic ventricular global myocardial strain and sub-pulmonary ventricular strain in all patients due to limited echocardiographic windows. Another limitation is the cross-sectional design of our study, which precludes any conclusions about causality. sHPT may simply reflect the severity of underlying HF rather than being directly associated with sub-pulmonary ventricular dysfunction. Longitudinal or interventional studies are needed to clarify the directionality and underlying mechanisms of this relationship.

Conclusion

sHPT is observed across the spectrum of ACHD but is more common in patients with complex and more severe disease, particularly those with predominant right heart involvement.
