Respiratory function in children and young adults affected by neurofibromatosis type 1 treated with selumetinib
Authors: Elettra Zuliani, Francesco Baldo, Sergio Ghirardo, Andrea Magnolato, Irene Bruno, Massimo Maschio, Egidio Barbi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlexiform neurofibromas (PNs) are benign tumors that can cause pain, disfigurement, and disability in patients with neurofibromatosis type 1. Selumetinib, a MEK1/2 inhibitor, is the first drug who showed efficacy in inducing PNs shrinkage, reducing the associated morbidities in pediatric patients. Overall, selumetinib showed a good safety profile. However, multiple adverse effects have been reported both in pediatric and adult patients, including respiratory ones. Serial spirometry is suggested in patients with thoracic neurofibromas to detect changes that may underly PN’s modifications. The aims of this study are to investigate the trend of pulmonary function in pediatric patients with NF1 treated with selumetinib and to determine the onset of respiratory adverse events.MethodsThis is a retrospective study conducted at the Pediatric Department of the Institute for Maternal and Child Health IRCCS “Burlo Garofolo” in Trieste, Italy. We identified all the patients diagnosed with NF1 at our institution who received selumetinib. We excluded patients unable to perform spirometry at treatment start, and patients above 18 years of age. We registered patients’ forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) for the first two years of follow-up and record any possible respiratory adverse effect since the beginning of the treatment.ResultsA total of 21 patients were included (7 females, 14 males) out of the 24 treated with selumetinib. Patients’ median age when selumetinib was started was 12 years. Neurofibromas were located mostly in the upper body, but 13 of them were in the thoracic area. The duration of the clinical follow-up ranged from 9 months to 72 months (6 years), with a mean follow-up length of 37 months. During the follow-up period, none of the patients reported pulmonary symptoms and no significant changes of the spirometry values were detected. Among those with thoracic plexiform neurofibromas, no correlation was found between spirometry values and PN’s volumetric changes at the MRI.ConclusionsOur experience suggests that selumetinib does not impact on pulmonary function in children and young adults. Serial spirometry should not be mandatory or could be at least distanced since it is not an appropriate method to precisely monitor plexiform neurofibromas changes over time.

Introduction

Neurofibromatosis type 1 is a relatively common autosomal dominant disease, affecting approximately 1 in 3.000 people worldwide. It is caused by pathogenic variations of NF1, which encodes for neurofibromin that normally downregulates the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK pathway. Loss of function of NF1 results in uncontrolled cell proliferation, leading to the development of various benign tumors, including peripheral neurofibromas, gliomas and plexiform neurofibromas (PNs). The latter, despite being benign tumors, are often responsible for pain and disfigurement, and can also cause significant disability, leading to airway, bowel, bladder, and motor dysfunction. Furthermore, about 10% of PNs transform into malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs).

In the past, surgery was considered the only effective treatment for PNs. This approach, however, never offered consistent results, due to the difficulty in obtaining a complete surgical resection and, therefore, to the high risk of tumoral recurrence. Various medical treatments have also been tested, including imatinib and sirolimus, but with poor results.

In 2016 selumetinib, a MEK1/2 inhibitor, showed to be effective in inducing PN’s shrinkage and reducing associated morbidities in pediatric patients [1]. Further studies later confirmed the positive results found by Dombi et al., and selumetinib was finally approved for the treatment of inoperable plexiform neurofibromas by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on April the 10th, 2020, and by the European Medicines Society on April the 22nd, 2021 [2]. Overall, selumetinib showed a good safety profile in the pediatric population. Acneiform rash, asymptomatic increase in creatin phosphokinase, gastrointestinal symptoms, and paronychia are the most common adverse effects, and none of them usually leads to drug discontinuation [3]. Nonetheless, dyspnea has also been reported, affecting around 10% of patients [2]. In the adult population, in which selumetinib is not approved for PNs’ treatment but is included in multiple chemotherapy protocols including pulmonary malignancies, various pneumological adverse effects have been reported [4]. Dyspnea is usually the most frequent, but interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pneumonia have also been described [5]. For example, Takashi et al. described two patients with solid malignancies who presented with severe pulmonary adverse effects while treated with selumetinib: one developed interstitial lung disease requiring hospitalization, and the other one experienced nonbacterial pneumonitis, resulting in drug discontinuation [6]. Both were using selumetinib as monotherapy. Furthermore, lung toxicity has been associated with other MEK inhibitors, especially trametinib [7].

Currently, patients treated with selumetinib are monitored with visits every 3 months during the first year of treatment, and every six months thereafter, following the protocol established by Dombi et al. in 2016. The follow-up consists in a full clinical examination, a comprehensive ophthalmic assessment, a complete cardiological evaluation with electrocardiogram and echocardiogram, blood tests and a pneumological visit with a spirometry. This rigorous monitoring was motivated by the fact that selumetinib was initially approved as an adjuvant of chemotherapy in adult oncology for the treatment of other RAS- or RAF-mutation-positive tumors (such as melanoma and small cell lung cancer), in which it caused numerous adverse effects. Serial spirometry is suggested in patients with thoracic neurofibromas to detect changes that may suggested PN’s shrinkage.

The aims of this study are to investigate the trend of pulmonary function in pediatric patients with NF1 treated with selumetinib, and to determine the onset of respiratory adverse effects.

Methods

This is a retrospective study conducted at the Pediatric Department of the Institute for Maternal and Child Health IRCCS “Burlo Garofolo” in Trieste, Italy. We identified all patients diagnosed with NF1 followed at our institution and treated with selumetinib. We excluded patients who could not perform serial spirometry due to age-related low compliance, patients under mechanical ventilation and patients above 18 years of age. The drug was administered at a dosage of 25 mg/m2 twice a day, as currently recommended. For each eligible patient, we collected spirometry’s results during the follow-up. The main variables measured in the test were the forced vital capacity (FVC) and the forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1); these variables are expressed in terms of Z-SCORE, as stated in the last ERS/ATS technical standard [8]. The plexiform neurofibromas’ volume measurement and 3D evaluation were performed on axial scans with Horos™. Discrete variables are reported as numbers and percentages, while continuous variable are reported as mean and standard deviation if normally distributed or median plus first and third quartile. The normality of data was evaluated visually and using Shapiro-wilk test for normality. We used Anova test to compare FVC recorded at each appointment, and we did the same for FEV1. We used Student test to compare pre-selumetinib FVC and FEV1 with values after 1-year and 2-year. The Alpha error was considered 0.05. We used linear regression to calculate if reduction in size of NFs lead to improve in FVC and FEV1.

Results

Of the 24 patients with NF1 currently on selumetinib, a total of 21 patients were eligible for the study (7 females, 14 males). According to the previously reported criteria, 3 patients were excluded: two were not able to perform a spirometry, one due to his young age (3-year-old at selumetinib start) and the other because he was mechanically ventilated; another patient was excluded because she was older than 18 years. Patients’ median age when selumetinib was started was 12 years (range 3–17 years, 1o quartile - Q1–9, 3o quartile - Q3 -14). Neurofibromas were located mostly in the upper body (15 in the head and neck region, 13 in the chest or back area). Most of the patients had two or more neurofibromas (56%). The median duration of clinical follow-up was 33.5 months (range 9–72 months, Q1 22, Q3 47).

During the follow-up period, none of the patients reported respiratory symptoms.

Before the initiation of selumetinib, median FVC Z-score was − 0.01 (Q1 -1.05, Q3 + 0.47; min − 2.37, max + 0.97) and median FEV1 Z-score was 0 (Q1 -1.24, Q3 + 0.95; min − 2.81, max + 2.04). After 6 months, median FVC was + 0.12 (Q1 -1.23, Q3 + 0.64; min − 3.26, max + 1.31) and median FEV1 was + 0.23 (Q1 -1.32, Q3 + 0.67; min − 2.30, max + 0.92). After one year of treatment, median FVC was − 0.04 (Q1 -1.35, Q3 + 0.67; min − 2.30, max + 0.92) and median FEV1 -0.08 (Q1 -1.40, Q3 + 1.25; min − 2.47, max + 1.87) and after 18 months median FVC was − 0.03 (Q1 -0.80, Q3 + 0.87; min − 2.32, max + 1.39) and median FEV1 was + 0.02 (Q1 -1.06, Q3 + 0.98; min − 2.50, max + 1.88). At 2 years after the beginning of the treatment, median FVC was + 0.02 (Q1 -0.97, Q3 + 1.07; min − 2.24, max + 1.09%) and median FEV1 was + 0.06 (Q1 -1.24, Q3 + 1.81, min − 2.66, max + 2.96). Anova test reported p = 0.57 for FVC and p = 0.88 for FEV1. Student test reported non significative differences in comparing pre-selumetinib FVC and FEV1 to mean FVC and FEV1 values at 12 months (p = 0.39, p = 0.87) and pre-selumetinib to 24 months (p = 0.96, p = 0.99) as well as with pooled FVC (p = 0.68) and pooled FEV1 (p = 0.83).

The median size of PNs with thoracic localization was 87 mL (Q1 40, Q3 204.5) at the start of selumetinib. To date the median size has reduced to 77 mL (Q1 28.5, Q3 170.5), with a median reduction of 11 mL (Q1 6, Q3 55.5). Linear regression analysis of PNs shrinkage and FVC and FEV1 trend after 24 months was performed on data of patients with PNs involving the chest. For both values, the correlation was found to be not significant (p = 0.80 and p = 0.83).

Discussion

This study analyzes a monocentric series of 21 patients affected by NF1 with inoperable and/or symptomatic plexiform neurofibromas treated with selumetinib.

To date, only few studies reported data about spirometry assessment in patients with NF1 treated with this drug. In the first study that reported the efficacy of selumetinib in NF1, Gross et al. tested 11 patients out of 16 with potential airway morbidity [2]. 7 of them presented an improvement of at least 12% in FEV1, which the authors considered as the threshold for a meaningful change. In the following study7, however, out of 6 patients with potential airway morbidity, no clinically significant improvement or worsening of FEV1 from baseline value was identifiable [9].

The objectives of this study were to evaluate the impact of selumetinib on pulmonary function and to monitor the onset of pulmonary adverse effects, with the aim of providing further evidence regarding the tolerability of this novel drug and of excluding airway impairment as potential adverse effect. In our opinion, the latter was particularly relevant, since a possible causative role of selumetinib in producing pulmonary fibrosis was previously described [10].

In our cohort of patients, no one experienced respiratory adverse effects in a relatively long follow-up (to our knowledge, the second longest in the medical literature). Furthermore, no significant change of the spirometry values was detected during the follow-up period, since the mean percentage of predicted FEV1 and FVC remained stable through the periodic assessments. Finally, our study doesn’t show any correlation between spirometry values and thoracic PNs’ volume changes at the MRI during the follow-up period.

A limitation of this study is its relatively small sample size. However, it must be considered that, due to the rarity of severe pediatric cases of NF1 currently treated with selumetinib, our cohort it still one of the largest in the world. In addition, the spirometry data studied only two years of periodic follow-up, therefore it is not possible to exclude later complications. Moreover, spirometry alone cannot completely exclude pulmonary fibrosis, especially in the early stages, as other methods like DLCO can do; thus, spirometry usefulness cannot be completely ruled out.

Conclusion

Our experience suggests that selumetinib does not impact on pulmonary function. Therefore, serial spirometry should not be mandatory or could be at least distanced to reduce the burden of medical assessments that pediatric patients with NF1 treated with this drug must undergo. Eventually, larger multicentric studied are needed to confirm these results.
