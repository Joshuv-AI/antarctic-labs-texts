GOT2-mediated suppression of CoQ10 biosynthesis drives ferroptosis with divergent effects in lung adenocarcinoma and atherosclerosis
Authors: Han Zhang, Xinsheng Gu, Yidan Sun, Pengcheng Chen, Qiangsheng Hu, Hao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe roles of glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase 2 (GOT2) in malate-aspartate shuttle (MAS), fatty acid binding/trafficking, and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) delta axis have been documented. However, its function in ferroptosis remains unexplored. Here, we report that GOT2 promotes ferroptosis by disturbing mitochondrial redox homeostasis through inhibiting the synthesis of radical scavenger coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10). Mechanism, by fueling MAS, GOT2 increases net influx of NADH into mitochondria and enhances aerobic respiration, increasing cellular ATP generation. The high ATP/ADP ratio inactivates adenosine 5′-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and PPARα, downregulating the transcription of core enzymes in CoQ10 synthesis pathway (FDPS, PDSS1/2, COQ3/5/6). In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), high expression of GOT2 restrains tumor progression by activating ferroptosis and igniting antitumor immunity. Whereas, the activation of GOT2-mediated ferroptosis exacerbates lesion in atherosclerosis disease. Our study reveals that GOT2-AMPK-PPARα-CoQ10 axis is a novel pro-ferroptosis pathway, and modulating GOT2-mediated ferroptosis suggests an intriguing method to treat LUAD and atherosclerosis. Subject terms: Necroptosis, Mechanisms of disease, Atherosclerosis, Non-small-cell lung cancer

Introduction

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxides and reactive oxygen species (ROS), culminating in membrane damage and cellular rupture1. Distinct from apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy, ferroptosis involves specific metabolic pathways and antioxidant systems, such as GPX4-GSH axis2, FSP1-CoQH2 pathway3,4, DHODH-CoQH2 axis5, and GCH1-BH4 system6, which operate in discrete subcellular compartments7. The involvement of ferroptosis in diverse pathologies, including malignancies and cardiovascular diseases, has attracted substantial interest, positioning ferroptosis as a promising therapeutic target for the regulation of cell fate8.

Mitochondria are central regulators of cellular metabolism and redox homeostasis, acting as hubs for bioenergetic and biosynthetic processes9. The malate-aspartate shuttle (MAS) is a principal mechanism for translocating reducing equivalents into mitochondria, thereby maintaining the NAD+/NADH balance and supporting oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)10. Glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase 2 (GOT2), a key mitochondrial enzyme in MAS, catalyzes the conversion of aspartate (Asp) and α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) into oxaloacetate (OAA), and glutamate (Glu). Beyond its canonical metabolic functions, GOT2 has been implicated in fatty acid transport11, nuclear receptor signaling (for example, PPARδ activation)12, and context-dependent effects on cancer progression13. For instance, GOT2 promotes immunosuppression via COX-2 induction in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma12, whereas its downregulation is associated with accelerated hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression through alterations in glutamine metabolism14.

Despite these insights, the role of GOT2 in ferroptosis remains unelucidated. Given the intimate link between mitochondrial metabolism, cellular redox balance, and ferroptotic susceptibility, we hypothesized that GOT2 might modulate ferroptosis through metabolic reprogramming. In this study, we employed metabolomics, lipidomics, and genetic models to investigate the regulatory role of GOT2 in ferroptosis across distinct cellular and disease contexts. We found that GOT2 enhances mitochondrial NADH influx and OXPHOS, leading to elevated adenosine triphosphate (ATP)/ADP ratios. This energetically replete state suppresses adenosine 5′-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylation and attenuates downstream peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) activity, leading to transcriptional downregulation of key enzymes in coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10, also known as ubiquinone) biosynthesis, including farnesyl diphosphate synthase (FDPS), prenyl diphosphate synthase subunit 1/2 (PDSS1/2), and Coenzyme Q3/5/6 (COQ3/5/6). The resultant depletion of CoQ10 compromises mitochondrial antioxidant capacity, favors accumulation of lipid peroxides, and sensitizes cells to ferroptotic death.

Importantly, the pathophysiological consequences of GOT2‑mediated ferroptosis are context dependent. In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), high GOT2 expression augments ferroptosis, enhances cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, and suppresses tumor growth, correlating with improved patient survival. Conversely, in atherosclerosis, GOT2-driven ferroptosis of lesional macrophages increases lipid peroxidation and cell death, destabilizes plaques, and accelerates disease progression. Collectively, these data define a previously unrecognized GOT2-AMPK-PPARα-CoQ10 regulatory axis that links mitochondrial metabolic state to ferroptosis sensitivity and supports context‑specific therapeutic strategies targeting GOT2 in cancer and cardiovascular diseases.

Results

Metabolomics analysis reveals GOT2 as a ferroptosis regulator

To systematically investigate the metabolic function of GOT2, we performed untargeted metabolomics in WT and GOT2−/− HT-1080 cells (Fig. S1A). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant alterations in the alanine, Asp, and Glu metabolism pathway, consistent with the canonical role of GOT2 (Fig. 1A, B). Notably, the ferroptosis pathway was among the most enriched, suggesting a previously unrecognized role of GOT2 in ferroptotic regulation (Fig. 1B).

To validate this finding, we treated HT-1080 cells with the ferroptosis inducer RSL3. GOT2 deficiency markedly attenuated RSL3-induced cell death, as evidenced by increased cell viability (Fig. 1C), reduced LDH release (Fig. 1D), decreased propidium iodide uptake (Fig. 1E–G), and suppression of lipid peroxidation (Fig. 1H, I). Transmission electron microscopy revealed that RSL3-treated HT-1080 cells exhibited characteristic morphological alterations, notably presenting with small, shrunken mitochondria and condensed cristae. However, this ferroptosis-associated mitochondrial phenotype was abolished in GOT2-deficient cells (Fig. 1J). Moreover, the anti-ferroptotic effect of GOT2 deficiency was consistently observed across multiple cell types, including the mouse macrophage iBMDM (Fig. S1B–D), human embryonic renal epithelium 293 T (Fig. S1E and G), human cervical cancer cell HeLa (Fig. S1F and H), and the mouse LUAD cell line LLC (Fig. S1I, J). The cell death induced by RSL3 could be completely inhibited by the ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstain-1 (Lpx-1) or ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1)15 (Fig. S1K), but not inhibited by the apoptosis inhibitor z-VAD-FMK, the necroptosis inhibitor necrostatin-1, nor the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (Fig. S1L), confirming the ferroptotic nature of cell death. In accordance with the inhibition of plasma membrane lipid peroxidation, loss of GOT2 also significantly suppressed mitochondrial ROS accumulation upon RSL3 treatment (Fig. 2K, L). Moreover, GOT2-deficient cells also exhibited significant resistance to other ferroptosis inducer erastin and sorafenib (Fig. S1M, N). Collectively, these results suggest that GOT2 is a novel ferroptosis regulator and its role prevails in various cell types.

The transaminase activity and mitochondrial localization of GOT2 are essential for its pro-ferroptotic function

Ferroptosis is characterized by the disorder of redox homeostasis, which ultimately leads to lipid peroxidation. To counteract it, multiple radical scavenger mechanisms exist, among which GPX4-GSH plays pivotal roles16. On the contrary, ACSL4 catalyzes the synthesis of poly-unsaturated fatty acid to promote the happening of ferroptosis17. Our results showed that the level of GSH, GSSG, and their ratio (Fig. 2A–C), as well as the protein level of GPX4 and ACSL4 did not change upon GOT2 depletion (Fig. 2D, E), indicating an unrevealed regulatory mechanism of GOT2 on ferroptosis.

GOT2 functions as a transaminase localized in the mitochondria18. The N-terminal transit peptide, consisting of amino acids 1–29, facilitates its import into the mitochondrial chamber post-translationally19. While the C-terminal amino acids 52–425 forms its transaminase domain. Specially, the 262aa arginine plays crucial roles in stabilizing the α-helical organization through electrostatic interactions. It has been reported that mutation of the arginine to glycine (R262G) reduces its enzyme activity to 21%20. To demonstrate the potential mechanism by which GOT2 potentiates ferroptosis, we generated rescue constructs expressing either wild-type GOT2 (GOT2WT), a transaminase inactivated mutant (GOT2R262G), or a mitochondrial localization-deficient mutant (GOT2∆N1-29) (Figs. 2F and S2). Immunofluorescence and colocalization analysis confirmed the loss of mitochondrial localization in GOT2∆N1-29 expressing cells (Fig. 2F–H). Subsequent assays revealed that only GOT2WT, but not GOT2R262G or GOT2∆N1-29, restored RSL3-induced ferroptosis in GOT2−/− cells (Fig. 2I–K). These data indicate that mitochondrial localization and transaminase activity of GOT2 are indispensable for its role in promoting ferroptosis.

Metabolites derived from GOT2 activity modulate ferroptosis sensitivity

GOT2 transfers amidogen from Glu to oxaloacetic acid to generate α-KG and Asp in mitochondrial matrix, which is a key step in the MAS (Fig. 3A)21. Using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), we confirmed the reduction of Asp and α-KG while the increase of Glu and OAA in GOT2−/− cells (Figs. 3B, C and S3A, B). Given the essential role of the transaminase activity of GOT2 in regulating ferroptosis, we therefore interrogated whether these metabolites influence ferroptosis. It showed that pretreatment with L-Glu sensitized cells to RSL3, increasing mitochondrial ROS (Fig. 3D, E), lipid peroxidation (Fig. 3J, K), and cell death (Fig. 3F). In contrast, α-KG exerted protective effects (Fig. 3G–I, L, M). OAA and L-Asp showed similar but weaker effects (Fig. S3C, D). Furthermore, the influence of these metabolites on ferroptosis sensitivity could be verified in iBMDM cells (Fig. S3E–H). These results highlight the critical role of GOT2-derived metabolites in ferroptotic regulation.

GOT2 promotes ferroptosis by repressing CoQ10 biosynthesis

Lipids are the substrates of ferroptosis and play critical role in ferroptosis susceptibility22. To demonstrate potential changes of lipids in the context of GOT2 depletion, we performed none-targeting lipidomics. According to the results, the “Ubiquinone and other terpenoid-quinone biosynthesis” pathway was significantly enriched (Fig. 4A, B). According to the LC-MS analysis, the CoQ10 levels increased in GOT2−/− HT-1080 cells (Fig. 4C). The elevated content of CoQ10 interpreted the resistance to ferroptosis upon GOT2 ablation, since CoQ10 is a potent free radical scavenger23. CoQ10 functions by trapping ROS in the bio membranes, thereby terminating lipid peroxidation and preventing ferroptosis3,4. Furthermore, treatment with L-Glu resulted in the exhaustion of CoQ10, whereas treatment with α-KG increased CoQ10 levels (Fig. 4D), linking GOT2 metabolic activity to CoQ10 abundance.

CoQ10 is primarily synthesized in mitochondria and acts as the major ROS repressor in this compartment, particularly in the condition of mtGPX4 inhibition5. Ferroptosis inducers can be classified into four types, namely type I–IV24,25. Type I and II inducers, such as Erastin and RSL3, block the Xc−-GSH-GPX4 system to trigger ferroptosis. While the type III inducers, like Fin56, work by depleting both GPX4 and CoQ10 (Fig. 4E). To functionally test the involvement of CoQ10, we employed FIN56. Unlike their resistance to RSL3, GOT2−/− cells were not resistant to Fin56 (Figs. 4F, G and S4A), and were even more sensitive than their WT counterparts. This supports the hypothesis that CoQ10 may mediate the defense against ferroptosis upon GOT2 deletion. Moreover, pretreatment with CoQ10 synthesis inhibitor Simvastatin (SIM, inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase) and 4-Chlorobenzoic acid (4-CBA, inhibiting COQ2) re-sensitized GOT2−/− cells to RSL3-induced ferroptosis in HT-1080 (Fig. 4H, I) and iBMDM (Fig. S4B and D). Besides, high doses and prolonged treatment with SIM or 4-CBA alone could independently induce ferroptosis, which could not be inhibited by GOT2 depletion (Figs. 4J, K and S4C and E). These results provided novel insights of GOT2 in promoting ferroptosis by limiting CoQ10 synthesis.

GOT2 supports mitochondrial aerobic respiration by promoting NADH influx

The important role of GOT2-fueled MAS is to translocate cytosolic NADH into the mitochondrial matrix for subsequent oxidation by the electron transport chain (ETC) and ATP generation via OXPHOS26. Consistent with this role, GOT2 depletion resulted in an increase in the mitochondrial NAD+/NADH ratio (Fig. 5A) and cellular ADP/ATP ratio (Fig. 5B). Correspondingly, supplementation with the GOT2 substrate L-Glu decreased the mitochondrial NAD+/NADH ratio (Fig. S5A) and cellular ADP/ATP ratio (Fig. S5B), whereas treatment with the GOT2 product α-KG elevated these ratios.

Aerobic respiration is the predominant ATP-generating pathway in most cells. Via OXPHOS, a single molecule of glucose yields approximately 30–32 molecules of ATP, whereas glycolysis alone generates only 2 ATP per glucose27. The changes of cellular ATP status might be derived from the metabolic reprogramming of OXPHOS and glycolysis. To validate it, we next assessed cellular respiratory function using Seahorse assay. It showed that GOT2 knockout produced a marked reduction in oxygen consumption rate (OCR) (Fig. 5C), with declines observed in basal OCR (Fig. 5D), ATP-Linked OCR (Fig. 5E), maximum OCR (Fig. 5F), and spare OCR (Fig. 5G). On the contrary, the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) remarkably increased upon GOT2 depletion (Fig. 5H), involving glycolysis (Fig. 5I), glycolytic capacity (Fig. 5J), glycolytic reserve (Fig. 5K). These data indicated a reprogramming of the cellular energy metabolism, shifting from aerobic respiration to anaerobic glycolysis, after GOT2 depletion. What’s more, treatment with L-Glu augmented OCR and concomitantly reduced ECAR, whereas α-KG produced the opposite effects (Fig. S5C–K). Collectively, these results support a model in which GOT2 facilitates mitochondrial aerobic respiration by promoting MAS-mediated NADH import into mitochondria.

GOT2 deficiency activates AMPK-PPARα axis to upregulate the transcription of CoQ10 synthesis enzymes

Cell energy status is tightly monitored by AMPK. Under energy stress (such as nutrient deprivation), elevated concentration of ADP and AMP activates AMPK by phosphorylating AMPKα subunit at Thr17228. After GOT2 depletion, phosphorylated AMPKα (p-AMPKα) was elevated while total AMPKα remained unchanged (Fig. 6A, B), indicating AMPK activation. AMPK is reported to regulate PPARα, and the AMPK-PPARα axis plays a central role in lipid metabolism29. In our results, enhanced AMPK phosphorylation was accompanied by increased phosphorylated PPARα (p-PPARα) (Fig. 6A, B), supporting the activation of AMPK-PPARα axis. Correspondingly, L-Glu treatment attenuated AMPK and PPARα phosphorylation, whereas α-KG augmented their phosphorylation (Fig. 6C, D). Besides, pharmacological inhibition of GOT2 using the pan-transaminase inhibitor aminooxyacetic acid enhanced the phosphorylation of AMPK and PPARα (Fig. 6C, D), consistent with the effects observed upon GOT2 deficiency (Fig. 6A, B) or α-KG treatment (Fig. 6C, D).

To further confirm the role of AMPK-PPARα pathway in CoQ10 biosynthesis, we treated GOT2−/− HT-1080 cells with AMPK antagonist (Compound C) or PPARα antagonist (GW6471) and measured the CoQ10 level. Consistent with former results, inhibition of AMPK or PPARα reversed the increase of CoQ10 in GOT2−/− cells (Fig. 6E). Crucially, inhibition of either AMPK or PPARα re-sensitized GOT2−/− HT-1080 cells to RSL3-induced ferroptosis (Fig. 6F).

PPARα belongs to the nuclear receptor superfamily, with two other members PPARδ and PPARγ, constituting a subfamily30. Upon heterodimerization with retinoid X receptors (RXRs) and binding to peroxisome proliferator response elements in target promoters, PPARs regulate the transcription of target genes involved in lipid and glucose metabolism, vascular biology, cell proliferation, and differentiation31. The synthesis of CoQ10 is strictly regulated by a series of enzymes. In the cytosol, farnesyl pyrophosphate and 4OH-benzonate (4-HB) are synthesized via the mevalonate pathway and tyrosine pathway, respectively32,33. These precursors are transported into mitochondria, where PDSS1/2, COQ2, and the multisubunit CoQ synthome (including COQ3–COQ9 and COQ11) catalyze sequential steps leading to CoQ10 production (Fig. 6G)33. Using quantitative PCR, we found the mRNA levels of FDPS, PDSS1/2, COQ3, COQ5, COQ6 were upregulated upon GOT2 depletion, while restored to wild-type levels following GOT2 complementation (Fig. 6H). To directly assess PPARα’s transcriptional regulation of these genes, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP). The results reveal significantly increased PPARα binding to the promoters of PDSS1, COQ5, and COQ6 in GOT2−/− cells compared with wild-type controls (Fig. 6I), directly linking PPARα activation to specific CoQ10 pathway genes. In contrast, no significant enrichment was detected at the FDPS, PDSS2, or COQ3 promoters, suggesting that their upregulation may occur through indirect mechanisms or involve other transcriptional regulators. These data effectively demonstrate that GOT2 suppresses CoQ10 biosynthesis by suppressing AMPK-PPARα axis.

Low expression of GOT2 accelerates tumor progression by suppressing ferroptosis activated antitumor immunity in LUAD

Recent studies have highlighted ferroptosis induction as an effective antitumor strategy7. In LUAD, ferroptotic cell death frequently occurs and substantially influences tumor behavior34,35. Building on the finding that GOT2 promotes ferroptosis, we hypothesized that GOT2 may function as a tumor suppressor in LUAD. Analysis of TCGA data revealed that LUAD patients with high GOT2 expression had significantly longer overall and disease-free survival (Fig. 7A, B). To validate these associations in an independent cohort, we compared tumor samples from patients who experienced recurrence within three years after resection with patients who remained recurrence-free. Western blotting revealed significantly lower GOT2 protein levels in the recurrence group versus the non-recurrence group (Fig. 7C, D). Because ferroptosis can elicit immunogenic cell death and promote antitumor immunity via release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)36,37, we evaluated lipid peroxidation and immune infiltration in these tumors. Immunohistochemistry showed reduced 4-HNE staining and decreased infiltration of inflammatory cells (CD45+), T cells (CD3+), and cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD8+) in the recurrence group compared with the non-recurrence group (Fig. 7E–J), consistent with attenuated ferroptosis and an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

In the murine LLC allograft model (Fig. 7K), we observed a significant acceleration of tumor growth in Got2−/− LLC tumors, as reflected by increased tumor volume and weight (Fig. 7L–N), and were accompanied by splenomegaly (Fig. S6A–C), a systemic feature associated with progressive LLC burden38,39. In vitro proliferation assays showed comparable growth rates for Got2−/− and wild‑type LLC cells (Fig. S6D), indicating that the accelerated in vivo growth depends on the tumor microenvironment rather than intrinsic proliferative differences. Consistent with reduced ferroptosis in vitro, GOT2-deficient tumors exhibited lower 4-HNE staining (Fig. S6E), while Ki-67 and cleaved caspase-3 levels were similar between genotypes (Fig. S6E), indicating no major changes in proliferation or apoptosis. Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed a decrease in the infiltration of leukocytes (CD45+, Figs. 7O and S7A), T cells (CD3+, Figs. 7P and S7B), and cytotoxic T cells (CTLs, CD8+, Figs. 7Q and S7D), but an increased ratio of exhausted T cells (CD3+ PD1+, Figs. 7R and S7C) and CTLs (CD8+ PD1+, Figs. 7S and S7E), in Got2−/− tumors. These changes were also supported by immunohistochemistry (Fig. S6F). These results underscore the importance of GOT2 in promoting ferroptosis and anti-tumor immunity in LUAD.

GOT2 deteriorates atherosclerosis while dietary α-KG provides protection

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease of medium and large arteries, hallmarked by extensive macrophage infiltration and intimal lipid accumulation, which predispose to major clinical events such as myocardial infarction and stroke40. Emerging evidence indicates that ferroptosis is implicated in human atherosclerotic plaques, contributing to necrotic core expansion and plaque instability41. Given our observation that GOT2 promotes ferroptosis in macrophages (Figs. S1B–D and S1N), we speculated that GOT2 may accelerate the progression of atherosclerosis by activating ferroptosis of infiltrated macrophage. To confirm it, we generated macrophage-specific Got2 knockout mice on the Apoe−/− background (Apoe−/−;CD68CreGot2fl/fl, hereafter referred to as Got2ϕKO). Under 12–16 weeks of high-fat diet, the Apoe−/− mouse could develop atherosclerosis in aortic artery (Fig. 8A)42. We found the atherosclerosis lesions of Got2ϕKO mice were significantly alleviated compared to the control mice (Apoe−/−;CD68CreGot2+/+, hereafter referred to as CtrlCre) (Fig. 8B), supported by reduced lesion and plaque areas, decreased lipid content, increased collagen deposition, and lower 4-HNE staining (Fig. 8B–G). However, pharmacological inhibition of CoQ10 biosynthesis with dietary 4-CBA abrogated the protective phenotype of Got2 deletion, restoring lesion burden and oxidative lipid markers (Fig. 8B–G), consistent with a mechanism dependent on mitochondrial antioxidant capacity.

Discussion

Our study uncovers a previously unrecognized function of the mitochondrial transaminase GOT2 in the regulation of ferroptosis and delineates its divergent pathological roles in LUAD and atherosclerosis. Mechanistically, GOT2 potentiates the MAS via its transaminase activity in mitochondria, promoting net cytosolic NADH import into mitochondria and thereby enhancing OXPHOS. The resulting increase in cellular ATP/ADP ratio suppresses AMPK phosphorylation and downstream PPARα signaling, which in turn reduces transcription of key enzymes in the CoQ10 biosynthetic pathway (e.g., FDPS, PDSS1/2, COQ3/5/6). Because CoQ10 is an essential mitochondrial lipophilic antioxidant, inhibition of its synthesis diminishes mitochondrial antioxidant capacity, promotes lipid peroxide accumulation, and ultimately sensitizes cells to ferroptosis. Importantly, activation of this GOT2-AMPK-PPARα-CoQ10 axis produces distinct outcomes in different disease contexts. In LUAD, GOT2 overexpression enhances tumor-cell ferroptosis, releases DAMPs, and activates antitumor immune responses (such as increased CD8+ T cell infiltration), thereby restraining tumor progression and correlating with favorable prognosis. By contrast, in atherosclerosis, GOT2-mediated ferroptosis in macrophages exacerbates lipid accumulation, necrotic core expansion, and plaque instability, thereby aggravating disease. The desirability of its induction is entirely context-dependent: eliminating proliferative cancer cells is therapeutic, whereas killing plaque-stabilizing macrophages in atherosclerosis is detrimental.

The discovery that GOT2 deficiency leads to opposite disease outcomes highlights the fundamental principle that ferroptosis is a double-edged sword in pathology. In oncology, ferroptosis represents a promising strategy to eliminate apoptosis-resistant tumors43. For instance, in HCC, compounds like erastin and sorafenib can trigger ferroptosis, and valproic acid sensitizes HCC cells to this process by increasing the labile iron pool via enhanced ferritinophagy44. Similarly, in LUAD, as demonstrated by our study, the GOT2-AMPK-PPARα axis suppresses CoQ10 biosynthesis to promote ferroptosis and exert tumor-suppressive effects. Conversely, in metabolic and inflammatory diseases, ferroptosis often contributes to pathogenesis. In metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), hepatic iron overload drives disease progression through c-Myc-ACSL4-mediated ferroptosis45, while in atherosclerosis, GOT2-mediated ferroptosis in macrophages exacerbates plaque instability. Similarly, in neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s, ferroptosis contributes significantly to neuronal loss46. This dichotomy necessitates precise therapeutic modulation: promoting ferroptosis in cancers using inducers (erastin, RSL3, etc.) or metabolic modulators (targeting GOT2/CoQ10), and inhibiting it in metabolic diseases using specific blockers (Ferrostatin-1), iron chelators (FOT1)45, or pathway-specific blockers. The interplay with other cell death modalities, such as pyroptosis via autophagy, further underscores its complex regulatory network. Future therapeutics will likely involve combination strategies and patient stratification based on specific ferroptosis pathway vulnerabilities.

Our findings align with and extend the canonical metabolic role of GOT2. Previous studies identified GOT2 as an essential component of MAS that maintains cellular redox homeostasis and supports bioenergetics10,47. Here, we establish a direct link between MAS activity and ferroptosis susceptibility by demonstrating that GOT2-mediated alterations in NADH trafficking and mitochondrial respiration lead to transcriptional control of mitochondrial antioxidant capacity via CoQ10 biosynthesis. This adds a novel metabolic aspect to the ferroptosis regulatory network, which includes parallel defense systems such as GPX4-GSH, FSP1-CoQH2, and DHODH-CoQH23,5,16,24. Mitochondria, as central executioners of ferroptosis, continue to reveal new mechanistic insights48. Besides, recent literature has emphasized organelle interplay in ferroptosis execution: mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum-mitochondria contact sites (EMCSs) in particular have been proposed as important platforms for ferroptotic processes49. And the activity of ETC complexes I–III has been linked to ferroptosis sensitivity8,50. Our data demonstrate that GOT2, by modulating mitochondrial NADH levels, indirectly influences ETC-driven respiration and thereby ferroptosis susceptibility, which is consistent with these recent advances. Regarding the role of CoQ10 in ferroptosis, our study offers a novel regulatory dimension. We identify the GOT2-AMPK-PPARα axis as a new pathway influencing ferroptosis sensitivity by regulating CoQ10 biosynthesis. This mechanism is distinct from the antioxidant functions of FSP13 and DHODH5, which reduce CoQ to CoQH2. Our work underscores the critical importance of the mitochondrial CoQ10 pool and reveals that its biosynthesis is directly modulated by cellular metabolic status. Specifically, the energy state of the cell (ATP/ADP ratio) can directly determine antioxidant capacity, which is a significant addition to the metabolic basis of ferroptosis.

Previous reports on the role of GOT2 in cancer appeared contradictory. For instance, Abrego et al. found a GOT2-PPARδ axis that, via nuclear fatty-acid transport of arachidonic acid and COX-2 induction, recruited immunosuppressive macrophages and suppressed anti-tumor immunity in PDAC12. Conversely, Yang et al. reported a tumor-supportive role for GOT2 in PDAC18, while reduced GOT2 expression was found to promote tumor progression in HCC14. Our research provides a new framework for reconciling these discrepancies: the function of GOT2 is highly dependent on its specific molecular mechanisms (metabolic enzyme activity vs. nuclear lipid transport function) and the cellular/tissue context. We successfully link GOT2-mediated tumor cell ferroptosis to the activation of antitumor immunity, consistent with the findings of Wang et al. that CD8+ T cells kill tumor cells by inducing ferroptosis37. However, our approach is novel in that we demonstrate that an intrinsic metabolic enzyme within tumor cells can determine their immunogenic potential. In LUAD, the pro-ferroptotic effect mediated by GOT2’s metabolic activity dominates, resulting in a tumor-suppressive phenotype, which contrasts with its immunosuppressive role in PDAC. This highlights that GOT2 cannot be simply categorized as an oncogene or tumor suppressor. Its role must be interpreted within a specific mechanistic and pathological context.

Another key finding of our study is that metabolites modulate ferroptosis sensitivity, thereby influencing disease progression. The substrate and product of the GOT2-catalyzed reaction (Glu and α-KG) exhibit opposing effects: Glu promotes, whereas α-KG inhibits, MAS flux and ferroptosis. This phenomenon aligns with the general regulatory principle wherein substrates and products modulate enzymatic reactions. For example, Mao et al. showed that dihydroorotate (a substrate of DHODH) accelerates the DHODH-catalyzed reduction of ubiquinone to ubiquinol, conferring resistance to ferroptosis, while orotate (the product of DHODH) produces the opposite effect5. A similar regulatory pattern has been observed in reactions catalyzed by glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 2 (GPD2)51. Furthermore, α-KG exhibits pleiotropic effects in disease: it acts as a cofactor for epigenetic enzymes to inhibit HIF-1α and glycolysis, exerting antitumor effects52, while also supporting AMPK protein synthesis via the TET-YBX1 axis to protect against disulfidptosis and promote tumor growth53. In aging and metabolic diseases, α-KG demonstrates protective effects, including delayed senescence through inhibition of mTOR pathway54 or promotion the production of IL-1055, improved insulin sensitivity56, and suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation leading to attenuated atherosclerosis progression57. In our atherosclerosis models, dietary α‑KG reduced lesional ferroptosis and ameliorated plaque progression, extending survival, consistent with its reported protective roles and supporting the translational potential of metabolic dietary interventions.

Despite these insights, several limitations remain. First, our study primarily relied on cell lines (e.g., HT-1080, LLC) and conditional knockout mouse models. Although these systems permit precise manipulation and causal inference, they cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of human disease microenvironments. In atherosclerosis, lesions involve multiple interacting cell types (endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, T cells, and diverse myeloid populations)40. While macrophage‑specific GOT2 deletion provided clear evidence of GOT2’s role in lesional macrophages, the functions of GOT2 in other plaque cell types remain to be defined. Future studies employing co-culture systems with cells derived from human atherosclerotic plaques or utilizing human organoids and patient-derived xenograft models would better mimic the human pathophysiological context. Second, the roles of organelles such as mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and lysosomes, and their inter organellar crosstalk in ferroptosis, require further exploration. The recent finding that MAMs form execution platforms for ferroptosis49 raises the question of whether GOT2 participates in this process. Third, the reasons underlying the opposing outcomes of the same pathway in LUAD and atherosclerosis remain incompletely understood. Beyond cell type differences (epithelial cells vs. macrophages), microenvironmental variables such as hypoxia, nutrient availability, inflammatory signals, and intercellular communication likely shape whether ferroptosis triggers protective immunity or tissue‑damaging inflammation. Single‑cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‑seq) and spatial transcriptomics of LUAD tumors and atherosclerotic plaques will help resolve cell‑type-specific GOT2 expression patterns and link them to immune infiltration, lipid metabolism, and oxidative stress signatures, thereby elucidating the determinants of tissue‑specific outcomes.

In conclusion, we define a novel GOT2-AMPK-PPARα-CoQ10 axis that links mitochondrial metabolic state to ferroptosis sensitivity. This axis advances our understanding of the metabolic control of ferroptosis and highlights potential therapeutic strategies: inhibiting GOT2-mediated pro-ferroptotic signaling (such as supplementation with CoQ10 or α‑KG) may be beneficial in atherosclerosis, whereas promoting GOT2‑dependent ferroptosis (such as the use of statins) could be leveraged for antitumor therapy in LUAD. Future studies should aim to develop specific small‑molecule modulators that precisely modulate the enzymatic activity of GOT2 or its downstream AMPK/PPARα pathway, evaluate their in vivo efficacy and safety. Ultimately, clinical trials are necessary to validate these findings. For instance, can GOT2 expression levels in LUAD patients serve as a biomarker for predicting the efficacy of ferroptosis inducers (e.g., RSL3, Erastin)? Could supplementation with α-KG or CoQ10 become an effective nutritional intervention or adjuvant therapy for patients at high risk of atherosclerosis? These investigations will further deepen our understanding of the metabolism-ferroptosis-immunity axis and provide new strategies for the treatment of related diseases.

Methods

Reagents

The sources of the antibodies, chemicals, commercial kits, and cell lines used in this article are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Cell culture

All cells were maintained in high glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, penicillin (100 U/ml), streptomycin (100 µg/ml), and glutamine (2 mM) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in an incubator. For metabolites supplementation experiments, L-Glutamate (L-Glu, Sigma, #G8415), Oxaloacetic acid (OAA, Sigma, #O4126), α-KG (Sigma, #349631), or L-Aspartate (L-Asp, Sigma, #A6558) were added into the cell culture medium at indicated doses and incubated for indicated time prior to experiments. For cell proliferation assay, 2000 cells per well were seeded in 96-well white plate and cultured in the incubator. ATP cell viability was measured every day for 6 days.

Cell death and viability assays

Cell death was measured based on LDH release in the supernatant using CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay kit (Promega, #G1780). Cell viability was measured based on ATP levels in whole cell lysate using CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay kit (Promega, #G7571). To distinguish lytic cell death from apoptosis, cells were stained with propidium iodide (Invitrogen #P3566) plus Annexin V Alexa Fluor 350 (Invitrogen, #A23202) for 30 min before fluorescence microscope imaging. Additionally, Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection kit (Yeasen, #40302ES50) was used according to manufacturer’s instructions followed by flow cytometry analysis.

Lentivirus generation and infection of cells

Lentiviruses were produced by calcium phosphate transfection of 293 T cells (80% confluence in 10 cm dish) with envelope plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G, along with transfer plasmids. Twelve hours post-transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete DMEM. The supernatant containing virus was collected after 48 h incubation and filtered through 0.22 µM filter. The virus was sub-packaged into 1.5 ml EP tubes and stored at −80 °C for up to 3 months before infection.

For lentivirus infection, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured overnight to reach 50% confluency. The next day, the medium was replaced with 1 ml of fresh complete DMEM mixed with 1 ml of virus plus 2 µg/mL polybrene, and incubated for 24 h for infection.

Gene knockout and knockdown

To knockout GOT2 in HT-1080 cells, lentiCRISPRV2 plasmids expressing sgRNAs with the following sequences were used: sgRNA#1: AAGCTCACCTTGCGGACGCT, sgRNA#2: AACAGCGAAGTCTTGAAGAG). For LLC and iBMDM cells, distinct sgRNAs encoded by the same vector system were employed: sgRNA#1: CTGGTGGACCCATGTTGAAA, sgRNA#2: ACACTTCATTGTTCTCGCCC. To knockdown GOT2 in 293 T and HeLa cells, pLKO.1 plasmids expressing shRNAs with the following sequences were adopted: shRNA#1: GCTACAAGGTTATCGGTATTA, shRNA#2: GCCTTCACTATGGTCTGCAAA. After 48 h of lentivirus infection, cells were selected with puromycin (5 µg/ml) for 3 days before being seeded in 96-well plates at a dilution of 15 cells/ml (approximately 1.5 cells per well on average).

Quantitative PCR with reverse transcription

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted to assess the relative mRNA expression levels of target genes. Briefly, total RNA isolation was performed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, #15596018) under RNase-free conditions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was reverse-transcribed from total RNA with 4× EZscript RT Mix II (EZBioscience, #EZB-RT2GQ). qRT-PCR amplification was carried out in triplicate using Power SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, # 4367659) on an ABI QuantStudio™ 7 Flex qPCR platform, adhering to the manufacturer’s specifications. The threshold cycle (Ct) values of target genes were normalized against the endogenous control β-actin, and relative quantification was calculated via the comparative ∆∆Ct method. Primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunoblotting

For cultured tissues, cells were lysed in Tris-Triton buffer (1% Triton ×-100, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH8.0) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and 1 mM PMSF. The lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm, 4 °C for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a new tube and mixed with 5× loading buffer (with 2-mercaptoethanol). For cryopreserved human LUAD tissues, a bulk of 5 × 5 × 5 cm in size was grinded, lysed, and centrifuged using Column Tissue & Cell Protein Extraction Kit (Epizyme #PC201plus). Then the total protein in soluble component was quantified by PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo, #23225), uniformed to equal concentration, and mixed with 5× loading buffer (with 2-mercaptoethanol). After boiled in 100 °C for 5 min, the samples were then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature to remove any insoluble material. For SDS-PAGE, the denatured proteins were loaded onto 9 or 12% polyacrylamide gels and separated by electrophoresis. The separated proteins were then transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with TBST buffer (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20) containing 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with appropriate primary antibody: GOT2 (Proteintech, #14800-1-AP, 1:2000), ASCL4 (Proteintech, #22401-1-AP, 1:2000), GPX4 (Proteintech, #67763-1-Ig, 1:2000), AMPKα (Cell Signal, #2793, 1:1000), phospho-AMPKα (Cell Signal, #2531, 1:1000), PPARα (Santa Cruz, #sc-398394, 1:1000), phospho-Ser/Thr (Abcam, #ab117253, 1:1000), HA-Tag (Cell Signal, #3724, 1:10,000), β-Actin (Cell Signal, #3700, 1:10,000). Primary antibodies for endogenous proteins were incubated overnight at 4 °C, while primary antibodies for tag epitopes were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. After washing with TBST buffer 3 times for 6 min each, the membranes were incubated with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 1:10,000 dilution. Following another round of washing with TBST buffer, the protein bands were detected using ChemiDoc™ Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using a commercial kit (Beyotime, #P2080S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HT-1080 cells were cross-linked, collected, and sonicated to shear DNA into fragments of 200–500 bp. Lysates were incubated overnight with anti-PPARα antibody (Santa Cruz, #sc-398394) or control IgG. Immunoprecipitated DNA was purified and analyzed by quantitative PCR. Sequences of the ChIP primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial ROS measurements

To measure lipid peroxidation, cells were seeded in 12-well plate and cultured overnight until reaching 60–70% confluence. 6 µM BODIPY-C11 (Invitrogen, #C10445) was added into the medium and incubated for 30 min following the induction of ferroptosis. Subsequently, cells were washed twice with PBS to remove excess dyes and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS with 2% FBS) for flow cytometry analysis. The FITC channel was utilized to detect the emission peak 510 nm, which was proportional to oxidated lipids.

For mitochondrial ROS measurement, cells were treated similarly as described above, except for adding 1 µM MitoSOX Red (Invitrogen, #M36008) for 30 min. The cells were then imaged using an inverted fluorescent microscope to capture signal in RFP channel. After removing excess dyes by washing with PBS twice, cells were resuspended in 200 µl PBS. Subsequently, 100 µl of the cell suspension was transferred into a 96-well black plate for a plate reader to detect fluorescence intensity at the Ex488nm/Em610nm setting.

Mitochondria isolation

Mitochondria are isolated by differential centrifugation according to the Cold Spring Harbor Protocol58. In brief, cell pellet was resuspended in 11 mL of RSB (10 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH = 7.5) to swell for 5–10 min. Cell swelling was confirmed under microscope. The cells were then homogenized using a homogenizer, and the extent of cell rupture was verified again under microscope (approximately 8–9 naked nuclei per intact cell remaining was ideal). 8 mL of 2.5× MS solution (525 mM mannitol, 175 mM sucrose, 12.5 mM Tris-HCl pH = 7.5, 2.5 mM EDTA pH = 7.5) was added promptly to restore isotonic condition, and the tube was inverted to mix. The volume was adjusted to 30 mL with 1× MS (210 mM mannitol, 70 mM sucrose, 5 mm Tris-HCl pH = 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, pH = 7.5), followed by centrifugation at 1300 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube, and the pellet (containing nuclei, intact cells, and large cell debris) was discard. This step was repeated twice. The collected supernatant was centrifuged at 7000–17,000 × g for 15 min, and the resulting supernatant was discarded. The pellet (mitochondria) was resuspended in 1× MS and washed once under the same centrifugation conditions (7000–17,000 × g, 15 min). After discarding the supernatant, the final pellet containing the purified mitochondria was obtained.

Seahorse assay

Cells were seeded in Seahorse XF96 cell culture microplates (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) at 5000 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, with corner wells serving as blank controls containing medium only. The sensor cartridge was hydrated with 200 μL deionized water per well at 37 °C in a non-CO₂ incubator for 12 h. On assay day, culture medium was replaced with 180 μL XF medium for 60-min starvation at 37 °C (non-CO₂), and the hydrated cartridge was loaded with 200 μL Seahorse XF Calibrant followed by sequential drug injection into designated ports. For oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurement, 15 μM oligomycin, 20 μM FCCP, and 5 μM rotenone/antimycin A were sequentially injected. For extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurement, 10 mM glucose, 15 μM oligomycin, and 100 mM 2DG were sequentially injected. After 20-min sensor calibration, tests were performed with three baseline measurements and three post-injection measurements per compound (10 min interval, total duration 2 h). OCR and ECAR data were analyzed using Wave Desktop Software (v2.6.3.5), with key parameters (including basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve) normalized to cell count.

Untargeted metabolomics by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)

LLC cells were cultured in 10 cm dishes with complete DMEM containing 10% FBS until reaching approximately 80% confluence. The cells were then trypsinized, counted, and washed twice with PBS. The cell pellets were collected in EP tubes and any residual liquid was aspirated. The pellets were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and store at −80 °C until further processing. To extract adequate metabolites, more than 107 cells per sample were used. The cell pellets were resuspended in pre-chilled 80% methanol by vortexing then melted on ice and whirled for 30 s. After sonification for 6 min, the samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm, 4 °C for 1 min. The supernatant was freeze-dried and dissolved in 10% methanol. Finally, the solution was injected into the LC-MS system for analysis.

UHPLC-MS analyses were performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (ThermoFisher, Germany) coupled with an Orbitrap Q ExactiveTM HF-X mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher, Germany) in Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Samples were injected onto a Hypersil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) using a 17-min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. For the positive polarity mode, eluent A was 0.1% formic acid in water and eluent B was methanol. For the negative polarity mode, eluent A was 5 mM ammonium acetate at pH 9.0, and eluent B was methanol. The solvent gradient was set as follows: 2% B, 1.5 min; 2–85% B, 3 min; 85–100% B, 10 min；100–2% B, 10.1 min；2% B, 12 min. Q ExactiveTM HF-X mass spectrometer was operated in positive/negative polarity mode with spray voltage of 3.5 kV, capillary temperature of 320 °C, sheath gas flow rate of 35 psi, and aux gas flow rate of 10 L/min, S-lens RF level of 60, Aux gas heater temperature of 350 °C. The raw data files generated by UHPLC-MS were processed using the Compound Discoverer 3.1 (CD3.1, ThermoFisher) for peak alignment, peak picking, and quantitation of each metabolite.

The metabolites were identified using the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/ kegg/pathway.html), HMDB database (https://hmdb.ca/metabolites), and LIPIDMaps database (http://www.lipidmaps.org/). Statistical significance (P-value) was determined through univariate analysis (t-test). Metabolites with VIP > 1, P-value < 0.05, and fold change ≥ 2 or FC ≤ 0.5 were classified as differential metabolites. The KEGG database was used to explore the functions and metabolic pathways associated with these metabolites. A metabolic pathway was considered enriched when the ratio x/n > y/N was satisfied, and a P-value < 0.05 indicated statistically significant enrichment.

Measurement of CoQ10 content

The total level of CoQ10 was determined by LC-MS or ELISA methods.

For the LC-MS method, a lipidomics method was employed. For lipid extraction, 0.75 mL methanol was added to a 100 μL cell pellet and placed into a glass tube with a Teflon-lined cap. The tube was then vortexed to ensure proper mixing. Next, 2.5 mL of MTBE was added to the mixture, and the entire solution was incubated for 1 h at room temperature in a shaker. To induce phase separation, 0.625 mL of MS-grade water was added to the mixture. After a 10-min incubation period at room temperature, the sample was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min. The upper (organic) phase was carefully collected, while the lower phase was subjected to re-extraction using 1 mL of the solvent mixture (MTBE/methanol/water, 10:3:2.5, v/v/v). The upper phase was collected once again. The combined organic phases were then dried and dissolved in 100 μL of isopropanol for storage. Next, the sample was analyzed using LC-MS. Samples were injected onto a Thermo Accucore C30 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm) using a 20-min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. The column temperature was set at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of buffer A (acetonitrile/water, 6/4, with 10 mM ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid) and buffer B (acetonitrile/isopropanol, 1/9, with 10 mM ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid). The solvent gradient was as follows: 30% B, initial; 30% B, 2 min; 43% B, 5 min；55% B, 5.1 min；70% B, 11 min; 99%B, 16 min;30%B, 18.1 min. The Q ExactiveTM HF mass spectrometer was operated in positive polarity mode with the following parameters: sheath gas at 40 psi, sweep gas at 0 L/min, auxiliary gas rate at 10 L/min (7 L/min), spray voltage at 3.5 kV, capillary temperature at 320 °C, heater temperature at 350 °C, S-Lens RF level at 50, scan range from 114 to 1700 m/z, automatic gain control target at 3e6, normalized collision energy at 22 eV; 24 eV; 28 eV 12, injection time at 100 ms, isolation window at 1 m/z, automatic gain control target (MS2) at 2e5, and dynamic exclusion at 6 s. The raw data files generated by UHPLC-MS were processed using the Compound Discoverer 3.1 (CD3.1, ThermoFisher), with the signal intensity determined as the Peak Area.

For the ELISA method, a commercial CoQ10 ELISA Kit (CUSABIO, #CSB-E14081h) was utilized following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 1 × 106 cells with indicated treatments were collected and homogenized in 100 μl PBS. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 5000 × g, 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatants were diluted to 1/100 and loaded into an assay plate, mixed well with HRP-conjugate, then incubated for 40 min at 37 °C. After five washes with wash buffer, TMB Substrate was added to each well and incubate for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark. Finally, the stop solution was added and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

GSH/GSSG, NAD/NADH, ADP/ATP measurement

The intracellular levels of NAD+ and NADH were measured with NAD/NADH assay kit (Applygen, #E2033). The intracellular levels of ADP and ATP were quantified by ADP/ATP Ratio Assay Kit-Luminescence (DOJINDO, #A552). The intracellular levels of GSSG and GSH were assayed using GSSG/GSH Quantification Kit II (DOJINDO, #G263). The measurements were performed according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol, respectively.

Confocal microscopy

Cells were seeded in 12-well plates with a 12 mm coverslip for adhesion overnight. For mitochondrial staining, MitoTracker (Invitrogen #M7510) was added to the culture medium at a concentration of 200 nM for 30 min. After treatment, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton ×-100 for 5 min at room temperature. Following blocked in blocking buffer (5% BSA in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature, the coverslips were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer for another 1 h at room temperature. The coverslips were then washed with PBS three times for 5 min each and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature. After incubation, the coverslips were washed three times for 5 min each in PBS and stained with DAPI in ddH2O for 5 min. After another washing in PBS for three times, the coverslips were mounted with Fluoromount™ aqueous based mounting medium (Sigma, #F4680). After air drying in dark overnight, images were captured using an Olympus SpinSR10 Ixplore confocal microscopy system with a 63× oil objective.

Prognosis of LUAD patients in TCGA

To reveal the relationship between GOT2 gene expression and prognosis in LUAD, the most updated TCGA dataset (LUAD, TCGA, Firehose Legacy, n = 586) was downloaded from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/datasets). To determine the optimal cutoff value for GOT2 low or high expression groups, the X-tile software was adopted according to developers’ instructions (https://medicine.yale.edu/lab/rimm/research/software/). The cutoff with maximum χ2 value of log-rank test was considered as the best cut-points for dividing prognostic difference subpopulation.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were conducted according to the national animal research guidelines, and the protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital, Tongji University School of Medicine. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Mice were housed under controlled environmental conditions (temperature of 22.5 ± 1 °C, relative humidity of 50 ± 5%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle). Food and water were provided ad libitum. Wild-type and Apoe−/− C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles River. Got2fl and CD68CreERT2 C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center. And Rosa26fl-Stop-fl-Got2 C57BL/6 mice were customized at Shanghai Model Organisms Center. Apoe−/− and CD68CreERT2 mice were bred to generate Apoe−/−;CD68CreERT2 mice (CtrlCre). To specifically knockout Got2 in macrophage, Got2fl mice were bred with CtrlCre mice to generate Apoe−/−;CD68CreERT2;Got2fl/f mice (Got2ϕKO). To specifically overexpress Got2 in macrophage, Rosa26fl-Stop-fl-Got2 mice were bred with CtrlCre mice to generate Apoe−/−;CD68CreERT2; Rosa26fl-Stop-fl-Got2 mice (Got2 ϕOE). For Cre-mediated gene inactivation experiments, tamoxifen (TAM, Sigma, #T5648) was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 75 mg/kg body weight daily for 10 days. Unless otherwise specified, Cre-expressing strains lacking the floxed target gene were used as negative controls in all related experiments.

To establish the atherosclerosis model, 6-week-old mice were fed with high fat diet (FHD, Xietong Shengwu, #XT108C) for 16 weeks. Throughout the dietary intervention, the following metabolites were administered to assess their effects on disease progression: 4-CBA (Aladdin, #C104333) via intraperitoneal injection at 20 mg/kg every 4 days, dimethyl α-KG (Sigma Aldrich, #349631) by daily gavage at 50 mg/kg, or coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10, Aladdin, #C111044) via daily gavage at 100 mg/kg. The survival of mice was monitored throughout the study. Aortae were collected either upon death or at the endpoint of the experiment (16 weeks) for further study.

To establish the tumor burdening model, WT or Got2−/− LLC cells were injected into the right flank of WT male C57BL/6 mice at 9 weeks of age. In brief, the cells were washed and resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 1.5 × 106 cells/ml. A volume of 100 μl of cells suspension were injected per mouse. Prior to injection, the fur on the right flank of the mice was shaved using an electric shaver one day in advance. The growth of subcutaneous tumor mass was observed, measured, and recorded every 2 days until the endpoints (30 days or any mouse with the maximum diameter of tumor reaching 2 cm).

Histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For IHC of mouse tissues, the fresh tumors were segmented into bulks measuring 5 × 5 × 5 mm in size. Tissues were washed three times with PBS to remove red blood cells, then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. After programmed dehydration and embedding in paraffin, the tissues were sliced into sections with a thickness of 5 μm. Serial sections were performed to ensure uniformity of the slices. After rehydrating, the slices were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) assays. For IHC, primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were used: CD45 (1:1000, Servicebio #GB11066), CD3 (1:100, Servicebio, #GB13014), CD8 (1:800, Servicebio, #GB114196), PD1 (1:400, Servicebio, #GB12338), Ki67 (1:500, Servicebio, #GB121141), cl-casp3 (1:800, Servicebio, #GB11532), 4-HNE (1:100, Bio-techne, #MAB3249-SP). Staining was conducted using VECTASTAIN® Elite® ABC-HRP Kit and DAB peroxidase substrate kit (Vector Laboratories). Random fields were chosen at 400× magnification (at least 5 images per sample) using Olympus BX35 microscope.

For IHC of human tissues, the paraffin-embedded LUAD samples were treated as described above. Primary antibodies used were as follows: GOT2 (1:400, Proteintech, #14800-1-AP), 4-HNE (1:100, Bio-techne, #MAB3249-SP), CD45 (1:2000, Proteintech, #20103-1-AP), CD3 (1:200, CST, #85061), CD8 (1:400, CST, #85336). For the quantification of GOT2 and 4-HNE signals, the IHC scores by multiplying the positivity and intensity scores were used. For the quantification of CD45+, CD3+, CD8+ cells, the number of positive cells was counted at 20× field. For each sample, five random fields are quantified, and the average values were adopted.

Masson & Oil Red O staining

For Masson staining, the fully dewaxed and rehydrated slices were stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin for 5–10 min, followed by rinsing under running water. Differentiation was performed in 1% hydrochloric acid-alcohol for several seconds, and the sections were then washed in running water for several minutes. Subsequently, the sections were stained with Ponceau-acid fuchsin solution for 5–10 min and briefly rinsed with running water. Next, the sections were treated with phosphomolybdic acid solution for approximately 5 min. Without water rinsing, the phosphomolybdic acid solution was decanted. The sections were then counterstained with aniline blue solution for 3–5 min, and the staining solution was decanted (again without water rinsing). Then, the sections were rinsed with 1% glacial acetic acid solution (prepared by adding 1 mL glacial acetic acid to 99 mL distilled water) until no blue dye runoff was observed. If necessary, this step was monitored under a microscope. Finally, the sections were dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam.

For Oil Red O staining, frozen sections (6–8 μm) were briefly rinsed in 70% ethanol for 5 s, followed by staining in Oil Red O working solution for 5–10 min. Excess dye was removed by briefly washing with 70% ethanol, and then rinsed under running water for 30 s. Nuclei were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 2 min, followed by washing in running water for 5 min. Excess moisture around the sections was blotted dry with filter paper. Finally, the sections were mounted with glycerin jelly.

Flow cytometry

The tumor masses were isolated and a similar volume (5 × 5 × 5 mm) was separated for digestion and single cell suspension preparation. After lysing the erythrocytes, the cells were stained with antibodies in FACS buffer for 30 min at 4 °C. The following antibodies were used: CD45 (30-F11, APC/Cyanine7, Biolegend, #103116, 1:400), CD3ε (500A2, Alexa Fluor 700, Biolegend, #152315, 1:200), CD4 (RM4-5, FITC, Biolegend, #100510, 1:200), CD8a (53-6.7, APC, Biolegend, #100712, 1:200), PD1 (29 F.1A12, Brilliant Violet 605, Biolegend, #135220, 1:200), Zombie Aqua (Biolegend, #423101, 1:400). The cells were then fixed in 2% PFA and subjected to flow cytometry analysis using BD FACS Celesta analyzer. The acquired data was analyzed by Flowjo software (TreeStar, v10.8.1).

Statistical analysis

The histogram, X–Y graph, and survival curve were plotted and analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (v9.3). The volcano plot was generated with TBtools (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools). Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for histogram and mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) for X–Y graph. To compare data between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used; For comparisons involving more than two groups, a two-way analysis of variance was performed with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001, ns (not significant).
