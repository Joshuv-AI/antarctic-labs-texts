Central Message

SVC syndrome is rare in the pediatric population and can become life-threatening. Extra-anatomic venous reconstruction can be offered to select patients when endovascular treatment is not feasible.

Superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome encompasses a range of clinical signs and symptoms resulting from obstruction of venous blood flow through the SVC. Obstruction of blood flow, whether partial or complete, can lead to symptoms such as swelling of the face, neck, and upper extremities; cough; dyspnea; headache; and facial plethora. The most common causes of SVC syndrome include mediastinal malignancies, chest radiation, thrombosis, or stenosis attributable to indwelling intravascular catheters.1 Although SVC syndrome is more commonly encountered in adults, it is rare in the pediatric population. In such cases, nonoperative interventions are typically used unless symptoms are severe or refractory. We present a case of a pediatric patient with SVC syndrome who was nonresponsive to nonoperative management and ultimately required surgical bypass of the venous obstruction. Institutional review board approval was not required, and the need for informed consent was waived.

Case Presentation

A 17-year-old female patient presented with SVC syndrome secondary to fibrosing mediastinitis caused by previous radiation and chemotherapy for mediastinal B-cell lymphoma. The patient's weight was 62 kg, height was 4′11″, and she had a body mass index of 29. Her medical history was significant for extensive upper extremity venous thromboses, including the SVC, which was treated with chronic rivaroxaban. Despite the development of collateral venous return, she continued to experience symptoms of SVC syndrome. These included facial, neck, and chest edema; hyperpigmented patches on her face; headaches; and chest pressure exacerbated by breathing and swallowing. Her symptoms had previously necessitated admission to the intensive care unit for airway swelling caused by SVC obstruction. This was managed with steroids and diuresis. Venography performed by interventional radiology revealed chronic occlusion of the SVC and bilateral brachiocephalic veins. Despite attempting to canalize multiple times through the right common femoral vein, and bilateral brachial veins, interventional radiology was unable to cross the occlusion (Figure 1). Computed tomography angiography of the chest confirmed these findings (Figure 2). Given her young age, significant symptoms, and failure of other treatment modalities, she was deemed a candidate for surgical intervention. The patient was admitted to the hospital for heparin bridging in preparation for surgery. She was intubated under general anesthesia uneventfully. The procedure was approached via a median sternotomy. The upper part of the pericardium and the innominate vein had formed dense adhesions with no identifiable true lumen. The SVC also had significant fibrosis and scar tissue. Therefore, we planned on a direct anastomosis to the right atrial appendage, which would also avoid cardiopulmonary bypass. Bilateral internal jugular veins were exposed through 2 separate anterior sternocleidomastoid incisions. Using a pair of tonsils, an 8-mm ringed PTFE graft was tunneled from the neck to the mediastinum, bilaterally. Both the grafts were tunneled under the clavicle. Heparin was administered to achieve an activated clotting time goal of greater than 200. The grafts were anastomosed to the internal jugular veins in an end-to-side fashion using 5-0 PROLENE sutures. Upon unclamping the veins, excellent flow through the PTFE grafts into the mediastinum bilaterally was observed (Video 1). Next, a side-biting clamp was placed on the right atrial appendage, and 2 atriotomies were created. With the use of Metzenbaum scissors, the trabeculations were removed. The distal end of each PTFE graft was then anastomosed to the right atrial appendage using 4-0 PROLENE sutures (Figure 3). After adequate hemostasis and chest tube placement was achieved, the sternum was closed. We decided not to intervene upon the chest wall collaterals and upper extremity thromboses to avoid disrupting any of these collaterals, in case our graft thrombosed in the future.Figure 3Tunneled PTFE graft anastomosed to internal jugular veins and right atrial appendage. Postoperatively, the patient recovered well in the cardiac surgery intensive care unit. She was extubated on postoperative day 1 and continued to recover uneventfully. She was discharged home on postoperative day 4 with a regimen of rivaroxaban and her home medications.

Discussion

SVC syndrome is rare in the pediatric and adolescent population. The most common cause of SVC syndrome in this patient population is lymphoma.2 In our patient, the etiology of SVC syndrome was most likely extensive clot burden extending to SVC as well as fibrosing mediastinitis from chemoradiation. Patients can present with facial swelling, cough, dyspnea, or with life-threatening emergencies such as respiratory compromise from airway swelling. Treatment of SVC syndrome in this patient population is directed toward the underlying cause, which is lymphoma or leukemia in most cases. As such, chemotherapy is administered to reduce tumor burden and corticosteroids are administered to reduce airway pressure and edema.3 Anticoagulation is indicated when tumor pressure causes thrombosis of veins, as seen in our patient.

Studies have reported successful treatment of SVC syndrome with catheter-directed therapies, such as, endovascular stenting, balloon angioplasty, and thrombectomy.4 However, surgical reconstruction is required when none of these interventions are feasible. When performing surgical reconstruction, saphenous vein, pericardium, or PTFE grafts can be used as conduits. We chose PTFE graft to avoid extrinsic compression while tunneling the conduit from the neck to the chest. Long-term patency rates are greater for SVC grafting compared with endovascular interventions, although endovascular techniques can be used to extend the patency of the SVC grafts.5 The patency rate for PTFE graft used in this setting is unknown, therefore, we had planned on obtaining a venous duplex ultrasound and CT chest with IV contrast for surveillance postoperatively, and continuing long-term anticoagulation. However, the patient was lost to follow-up, and we were unable to obtain any postoperative imaging.

Conclusions

SVC syndrome is rare in the pediatric population and can present with life-threatening emergencies. Patients with significant symptoms should be offered interventions, including surgical bypass of the venous obstruction when endovascular treatment is not feasible.
