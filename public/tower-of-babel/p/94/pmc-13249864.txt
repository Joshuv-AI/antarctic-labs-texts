Exome sequencing identifies known and candidate genes in hearing impairment in Cameroon
Authors: Ambroise Wonkam, Edmond Wonkam-Tingang, Abdoulaye Yalcouyé, Jean Jacques Noubiap, Seraphin Nguefack, Valentina Josiane Ngo Bitoungui, Lettilia Xhakaza, Kalinka Popel, Thashi Bharadwaj, Rachel Latanich, Cesar A Fortes-Lima, Carmen de Kock, Kevin Esoh, Hong Zhang, Isabelle Schrauwen, Ulrich Mueller, Suzanne M Leal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHearing impairment (HI) is one of the most common sensory disorders globally. There is limited data on the genetics of HI in Africa. We aim to identify the genetic causes of HI in Cameroon.MethodsWe used whole-exome sequencing (WES) to study the cause of HI in multiplex families from Cameroon. Cell-based experiments using HEK293T assessed the pathogenicity of targeted variants, and a knockout mouse model was used to investigate a candidate gene.ResultsHere, we report forty-six multiplex families totaling 106 affected individuals with HI. Most families present with non-syndromic HI (NSHI) (87%, n = 40/46). Causative variants in known genes are identified in 76% (n = 35/46) of families, including nine genes that cause both syndromic HI (SHI) and NSHI, ten that underlie NSHI, and six that cause SHI. MYO15A and TMC1 are the most common genes underlying HI and are observed in six and three families, respectively. Three candidate genes (SUN2, TGM6, and TMC3) are identified. In vitro studies find that a biallelic variant in SUN2 [c.475 G > A:p.(Val159Ile); NM_015374.3] leads to mislocalization and decreased expression in HEK293T cells. In mice, SUN2 protein expression localizes explicitly to the supporting cells of the inner hair cells. Auditory thresholds of the Sun2 knockout mice are comparable to those of wild-type, suggesting that SUN2 is not a credible HI gene.ConclusionThis study reports a high diagnostic rate using WES for HI and highlights the opportunity for variant and gene discoveries in African populations. These findings inform the prospects for gene therapy in HI.Subject terms: Disease genetics, Paediatric research

Introduction

Hearing impairment (HI) is among the most common neurosensory disorders, and is globally reported with a prevalence of 1–2/1000 newborns1. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that approximately 5% of the world’s population suffers from disabling HI, which is expected to increase up to 10% by 20502,3. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is heavily burdened by HI, especially with a birth incidence of 5-6/1000, which is up to 6 times more reports from high-income regions4. HI is caused by several environmental and genetic factors5. However, these data are likely underestimated in most African countries where newborn hearing screening is not routinely implemented and remain limited to small-scale or urban pilot initiatives, and nationwide screening is largely absent6–8. HI is clinically and genetically heterogeneous, with nearly 150 causing genes identified to date9,10. Current estimation shows that ~1000 genes might be involved in the process of hearing, and their mutations may potentially lead to various HI phenotypes11. Genetic HI is caused by genetic variants that affect the development of various parts of the auditory system, and in more than 50% of the affected individuals, these variants cause congenital HI1,12. While significant progress has been accomplished in understanding the genetic spectrum of HI in developed countries and its potential actionability, the knowledge gaps remain wide in SSA compared to developed countries13,14.

Globally, the GJB2/GJB6 genes account for a significant proportion of HI. However, numerous affected individuals of HI remain undiagnosed after exclusion of GJB2/GJB615,16. As previously reported in Cameroon17, GJB2/GJB6 do not contribute significantly to HI, like in most SSA populations, with few exceptions reported in Ghana and Senegal17–24. Despite the fact that African populations harbour the highest genetic diversity in humans, genomic studies have not been extensively conducted in hearing-impaired individuals in Africa. In a recent review, we found at least one genetic study on HI in only 17/54 (31.5%) of African countries, and most studies mainly investigated targeted genes25.

The increasing use of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies in genetic research has enabled the refinement of the profile of known HI gene variants and the discovery of several genes previously undescribed to be associated with HI26,27, including a recent study from Ghana26. Although no approved therapies exist for genetic HI, considerable milestones have been made in developing gene therapy and cell engineering to cure hearing disorders, exemplified by a successful gene therapy in OTOF-related HI28–30, leading to a renewed impetus for the discovery of druggable HI variants and genes.

In this study, we employ WES to investigate multiplex families with HI from Cameroon. Admixture mapping identifies shared genetic similarities among Bantu and non-Bantu speakers. WES data analysis finds that several known and novel variants in known genes are associated with HI, to the best of our knowledge. Interestingly, three candidate genes, including SUN2, TGM6, and TMC3, have been identified. In vitro studies find that a biallelic variant in SUN2 [c.475 G > A:p.(Val159Ile); NM_015374.3] leads to mislocalization and decreased expression of the product in HEK293T cells. In mice, we find that SUN2 protein expression localizes explicitly to the supporting cells on the modiolar side of the inner hair cells. Auditory thresholds of the Sun2 knockout mice are comparable to those of wild-type controls. These data suggest that SUN2 is not a credible HI gene, at least within the context of peripheral auditory function assessed in this model. This study reports a high diagnostic rate using WES for HI and highlights the opportunity for variant and gene discoveries in African populations.

Methods

Ethics declaration

This study was conducted in full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Relevant institutional ethical approvals were granted by the University of Cape Town’s Faculty of Health Sciences’ Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC 484/2019), the Institutional Research Ethics Committee for Human Health of the Gynaeco-Obstetric and Paediatric Hospital of Yaoundé, Cameroon (No. 723/CIERSH/DM/2018), the Johns Hopkins Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB00431552) and the Institutional Review Board of Columbia University (IRB-AAAS2343). Written and informed consents were obtained from all participants aged 21 years or older, and assent forms for minor participants.

Participants and clinical assessments

A total of 106 affected and 92 non-affected individuals from 46 families with HI were ascertained. Clinical presentations were consistent with syndromic HI in five affected individuals (two Usher syndromes, two Waardenburg syndromes, and one Bartter syndrome) and 41 nonsyndromic HI. Participants were enrolled at schools for people with deafness and during community engagement activities across different regions of the country, following a previously reported procedure31,32. The detailed personal history and medical records of affected family members were reviewed by a general practitioner, a medical geneticist, and an ear, nose, and throat (ENT) specialist. Clinical examinations were performed, and pure tone audiometry (PTA) was conducted. The severity of HI was classified according to recommendation number 02/1 of the Bureau International d’Audiophonologie (BIAP; www.biap.org). Detailed descriptions of sociodemographic, pedigree analysis, clinical phenotypes of hearing loss in patients and families included in this study are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

In the context of the present study, multiplex families are defined as those with two or more individuals affected by hearing impairment (HI).

Exome sequencing

DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using the QIAGEN Blood DNA Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Germantown, MD, USA). Exome sequencing was performed in families with more affected siblings. In each family, two affected siblings were included. When possible, both parents, regardless of their affected status, were included. At least one unaffected sibling was also included if available, at Omega Bioservices (Norcross, GA, USA). Library preparation and hybridization were performed using an Illumina Nextera Rapid Capture Exome Kit® (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) or the SureSelect V4 + UTR 71 Mb All Exon Capture Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California). Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencer using the pair-end 150 bp run format. The detailed protocol of the WES was previously published32. We used single-sample variant calling with the GATK HaplotypeCaller module, which generated genome variant call files (GVCF) per sample. The CombineGVCFs module was then used to merge the GVCF files into a multi-sample GVCF file. Lastly, the GenotypeGVCF module was used to jointly call variants.

For mitochondrial variants, because we used whole-exome sequencing, we were able to evaluate nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes. However, this method does not provide sufficient or uniform coverage of mitochondrial DNA. Therefore, mtDNA variants and heteroplasmy levels could not reliably be assessed in this study.

Annotation and filtering strategy

The detailed workflow of variant prioritization, filtering using ANNOVAR, and custom scripts was published in previous reports32,33. Briefly, variants were first prioritized based on the inheritance pattern. Next, variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.005 or <0.0005 in all populations of the genome aggregation database (gnomAD, version v4.1.0) were retained for autosomal recessive and autosomal dominant inheritance, respectively. Additionally, known pathogenic or likely pathogenic HI variants listed in ClinVar were also retained, regardless of their frequencies. Furthermore, variants that were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics34,35 (ACMG) criteria were considered causal for HI (Supplementary Table 2). Variants classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) were considered possibly causal. SpliceAI and Human Splice Finder (HSF) tools were also used to investigate the functional impact of intronic and splice variants identified (Supplementary Table 3). Lastly, putative variants were considered based on the following criteria: (1) the variant occurred in a known HI gene; (2) it was predicted to affect protein function or pre-mRNA splicing (including nonsense, missense, frameshift, or splice-site variants); and (3) it co-segregated with the HI phenotype within the family. Detailed descriptions of the annotation and filtering strategies were previously published32.

Population structure and admixture pattern

The vast majority of the 106 affected individuals from the 46 families self-identified as Bamileke (n = 30), Massa (n = 17), Beti (n = 15), and Nso (n = 11), while other individuals self-identified as Bassa, Moundang, Mbo, Bamoun, Bobli, Mafa, and Foulbe (range = 1-6). To investigate their genetic diversity, we compared the generated dataset with representative populations presented in previous studies (Supplementary Table 4)36–41. We included whole-genome sequencing and genome-wide genotype data of comparative populations from Cameroon and neighbouring African countries (Benin, Nigeria, CAR, and Chad), representing groups with different linguistic affiliations (speaking non-Bantu Niger-Congo, Bantu, and Ubangi languages) and different lifestyles (agriculturalists and hunter-gatherers). Before merging the datasets, we used PLINK v1.942 to perform quality control (QC) steps of each dataset, keeping only autosomal biallelic variants, individuals and variants with high-genotyping rates (≥90%), and variants that passed the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium test (using PLINK: –chr 1-22 --mind 0.1 –geno 0.1 --hwe 0.0000001). After merging and QC, we obtained 723 individuals encompassing 25 sub-Saharan African populations (Supplementary Table 4)36–41.

To explore patterns of genetic affinities among all studied individuals from Cameroon and comparative populations, we first used principal component analysis (PCA) using smartPCA from the EIGENSOFT package43. To include related individuals from the studied families, we employed the projection approach for PCA, which controls kinship-related biases. To do so, we first computed PCA on a set of unrelated individuals from all the reference populations and only one individual from each studied family in Cameroon. This PCA defined the principal component coordinates of those unrelated individuals. We then projected the related individuals onto the pre-calculated PCA space, allowing us to visualize their positions relative to each other and the unrelated reference individuals. To investigate patterns of admixture and population structure, we performed clustering analysis using ADMIXTURE software v1.3.042. To include all individuals from the families, ADMIXTURE analyses at K = 4 were first performed for all unrelated individuals using unsupervised clustering analysis and then using ADMIXTURE projection mode (-P) for related individuals and comparative populations. ADMIXTURE results were visualized with bar plots for each individual from each population using PONG44.

Sanger sequencing of candidate genes

Sanger sequencing was performed in available family members to analyse the segregation of variants in families with variants in candidate genes (SUN2, TGM6, and TMC3), and variants were segregated in available family members. Primer pairs covering the variants of interest were designed on the free-access Integrated DNA Technologies platform in our laboratory. PCR-amplified DNA products were Sanger sequenced using a BigDyeTM Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit and an ABI 3130XL Genetic Analyzer® (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) in the Division of Human Genetics, University of Cape Town, Cape Town, South Africa.

Evolutionary conservation of amino acid

For each candidate gene, we aligned the sequences spanning the variants of the candidate genes found here with similar proteins from non-human species to evaluate the conservation of the respective amino acid (aa). We performed a PSI-BLAST search against the non-redundant protein database. The multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was performed using MEGA X software.

In silico predictions of variants in candidate genes, synonymous and splicing variants

SpliceAI45 and Human Splice Finder (HSF)46 tools were used to investigate the functional impact of intronic and splice variants identified (Supplementary Table 3) in CLCNKB (c.1929+5 C > G; NM_000085.5), MITF (c.448+1 G > A; NM_001354604.2), OTOF (c.1048-2 A > C; NM_194248.3), USH1C (c.496+1 G > A; NM_153676.4), and CLIC5 (c.63+1 G > A; NM_016929.5).

In addition, we assessed the potential splicing impact of variants identified in candidate genes, including SUN2, TMC3, and TGM6.

Secondary structural changes and three-dimensional modelling of the missense variants and genes

The isoforms of the candidate genes (SUN2: NM_001199580.2, TMC3: NM_001080532.3, and TGM6: NM_198994.3) and their respective protein sequences NP_001186509.1, NP_001074001.1, and NP_945345.2 were downloaded in FASTA format from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The secondary structures were predicted using the PSIPRED Workbench47 for wild-type and mutant proteins. The three-dimensional (3D) structures were modelled on the SWISS-MODEL48 with Alphafold structures (Q9UH99, Q7Z5M5) as templates. Then, the newly predicted structures were refined using the GalaxyWeb server49. Pymol software was used for visualization and structural analysis50.

Expression pattern of candidate genes in human and mouse data

Expression patterns of candidate genes SUN2, TGM6, and TMC3 were investigated using freely accessible databases on gene expression analysis resources (gEAR)51 data of the human ear atlas, human inner ear organoids, and mouse cochlear tissue.

In vitro functional HEK293T assay of the candidate genes

Site-directed mutagenesis

Mammalian expression plasmids expressing GFP-tagged SUN2 (wild-type and mutant), wild-type GFP-tagged TGM6, and GFP-tagged TMC3 were purchased from Origene Technologies. We performed site-directed mutagenesis (SDM) with custom primers (for TGM6 and TMC3) acquired from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, IA, USA). SDM was performed in our laboratory at the Department of Genetic Medicine, Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, USA. SDM protocol is available upon request, and mutagenic primers used are reported in Supplementary Table 5. Long-read sequencing of the plasmids was performed at Plasmidsaurus (Eugene, OR, USA).

Cell culture, transfections, and visualization using confocal microscopy

Human embryonic kidney (HEK293T) cells were cultured in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The HEK293T cells were plated in 4-chamber dishes (density 4 × 105 cells per mL) 18 hours before transfection. Cells were transiently transfected using TurboFect transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Waltham, MA, USA) with 250 ng of plasmid (Empty, GFP-only, GFP-tagged wildtype, and mutants) for SUN2, TGM6, and TMC3 proteins. Cells were stained with Hoechst dye (dilution 1:1000) for co-visualization of nuclear material. Live viewing was performed at 48 hours after transfection using an Olympus FV3000RS Confocal Microscope. Images were then visualized and processed using the FV31S-W viewer software.

Western blot protocol

HEK293T cells were plated (density 6 × 105 cells per mL) in 6-well plates 18 hours before transfection. Cells were then transfected using TurboFect transfection reagent (Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with 2000 ng of WT or MT-SUN2, TGM6, and TMC3 plasmids fused with the GFP tag and a negative control with no plasmid DNA. Total protein lysates were collected at 48 hours for both genes using radio-immunoprecipitation (RIPA) buffer and stored at −20 °C. Proteins were briefly sonicated, and concentrations were checked using the Qubit5 fluorometer. Then, 30ug total protein was denatured (70 °C, over 10 minutes) and loaded onto NovexTM WedgeWellTM 4-20% Tris-Glycine Gel for SUN2, TGM6, and TMC3. Gel electrophoresis was run at 100 V over 90 minutes. Proteins were transferred onto iBlot® 2 regular Stacks, nitrocellulose (NC), regular size (Invitrogen, Thermo Fischer, Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Membranes were stained with Revert TM 700 Total Protein Stain (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, Nebraska). The membranes were incubated with primary monoclonal Rabbit anti-GFP antibody (Abcam, product# ab183734, cat#1033451-10, dilution 5:1000) for both SUN2 and TMC3 (dilution 1:1000), Rabbit monoclonal anti-TGM6 antibody (Abcam, product# ab180959, cat#1072342-1, dilution 1:1000). Secondary antibody Donkey polyclonal anti-Rabbit was used for SUN2, TGM6 and TMC3 (Invitrogen, product#A11374, cat#2599186, dilution 1:10000).

Mouse experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine (Protocol #MO22M215). Mice were maintained on a 14-hour light/10-hour dark cycle in pathogen-free facilities with controlled temperature and humidity. All mice were group-housed and provided with ad libitum access to food and water. Both male and female mice were used, with no significant differences observed between sexes. All animals used were seemingly free of infection or health abnormalities and had not been previously used in experiments. Littermates of both sexes were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Sun2 KO mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (B6;129S6-Sun2tm1Mhan/J, Strain# 012716).

Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR) measurements

ABR measurements were performed as previously described52. Briefly, mice aged P21 were anesthetized with Ketamine (100 mg/kg) and Xylazine (20 mg/kg) and placed on a heating pad inside a sound attenuating chamber. Body temperature was monitored throughout the test session with a rectal temperature probe. Subdermal platinum needle electrodes (E2, GrassTechnologies, West Warwick, RI) were placed on the left pinna (inverting), vertex (non-inverting), and leg muscle (ground). ABR stimuli were delivered through a free-field speaker (FD28D, Fostex, Tokyo, Japan), positioned 10 cm from the animal’s head. Stimuli generation and signal acquisition were controlled by BiosigRz software interfacing with a TDT WS4 high-performance computer workstation. ABR stimuli consisted of clicks or 5 ms tone pips at varying frequencies (4, 8, 16, 24, and 32 kHz) and presented at a rate of 21/s. Stimuli were presented in descending sound levels from the maximum speaker output level in 10 dB increments. Responses were collected using a low impedance head stage (RA4L1; TDT), pre-amplified and digitized (RA4PA preamp; TDT), and sent to an RZ6 processing module. The signal was filtered (300–3000 Hz) and averaged over 512 presentations. Threshold was determined statistically based on the ABR input/output function and defined as the sound level at which the peak-to-peak ABR signal magnitude was two standard deviations above the average background noise level52.

Whole-mount imaging

For whole-mount staining, cochleae were collected from P21 mice perfused with 10 mL 1x DPBS (pH 7.4), followed by 10 mL 4% (vol/vol) paraformaldehyde in DPBS. After overnight post-fixation at 4 °C, tissues were decalcified either in 100 mM EDTA for 2 days. Fixed tissues were washed three times in 1x PBS, the bony cochlear shell was removed, and the organ of Corti was separated from the modiolus and collected for immunostaining. Reissner’s membrane and the tectorial membrane were dissected away, and the organ of Corti was blocked in 1x DPBS containing 10% normal goat serum, 0.6% TritonX-100, and 0.4% NaN3 for 1 hour at room temperature. The tissues were then incubated with monoclonal rabbit anti-Sun2 antibody (Abcam, product#ab124916, cat# EPR6557, dilution 1:200) and guinea pig polyclonal anti-Calb1 (Synaptic Systems, product#214004, dilution 1:500) overnight at 4 °C. The following day, tissues were washed three times with 1x DPBS and then incubated with secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, product#AB_2535851, cat#A21430, dilution 1:1000, Jackson Immunoresearch, product#106-606-003, cat#169091, dilution 1:1000) for 1 hour at room temperature. After three additional washes with 1x DPBS, tissues were mounted on slides using ProLong Gold mounting medium (Invitrogen) and imaged using a 63x objective on a confocal microscope (LSM 800, Zeiss).

Statistics and reproducibility

For colocalization analyses, Manders’ coefficients (M1, M2) and Pearson’s correlation coefficient (Rr) were calculated over entire image frames for wild-type and mutant SUN2, following confocal live images acquisition. Quantitative colocalization analyses were performed in biological duplicates (n = 2) using the Olympus CellSens colocalization module. Statistical comparisons between wild-type and mutant SUN2 were done using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t tests, with significance defined at P < 0.05, for both confocal images and western blot data. All statistical analyses are performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.1.2; San Diego, CA, USA). Biological replicates are defined as independent experiments performed on separate samples under identical conditions. Sample sizes (n) and the number of replicates for each experiment are reported in the corresponding figure legends.

For the mouse experiment, statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2). Statistical comparisons of ABR thresholds were performed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with significance indicated P < 0.05. Data are presented as box-and-whisker plots.

Results

Patients’ sociodemographic and phenotypic data

The workflow of patients’ recruitment is provided in Fig. 1A. Overall, 46 multiplex families were enrolled, totalling 106 affected individuals (48 males and 58 females) with a sex ratio of 0.82. The average age at molecular diagnosis was 18.43 years (range: 1–62 years). Parental consanguinity was reported in 4.3% of families (n = 2/46). Among solved families, the inheritance pattern was consistent with autosomal recessive in 80% (n = 28/35), autosomal dominant in 17.1% (n = 6/35), and X-linked in 2.9% (n = 1/35) (Fig. 2A-C, Supplementary Fig. 1). HI was congenital prelingual in most affected individuals (63.2%, n = 67/106), post-lingual (34%, n = 36/106), and undetermined in 2.8% (n = 3/106). Audiometry data were recorded in 64% of patients (n = 68/106). HI was bilateral and classified as severe to profound in 97% (n = 66/68) and moderate in 3% (n = 2/68). Following clinical examination, the inheritance pattern was consistent with autosomal recessive in 82.6% (n = 38/46), autosomal dominant in 17.4% (n = 8/46). Nonsyndromic HI (NSHI) was predominant in our cohort in 87% (n = 40/46) and syndromic HI in 13% (n = 6/46). The details patients’ sociodemographic and phenotypic data are provided in supplementary Table 1.

Exome sequencing analysis

While the expected coverage was a minimum of 120X, the average target region coverage was about 200X, with 96.30% of targeted regions covered at 10X or more53. The following variants were considered after applying various filtering criteria, as described in the method section.

Variants in known genes associated with hearing impairment

Following ClinGen gene-disease curation guidelines, we considered a case solved when patients carry pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in genes with strong or definitive gene-HI evidence. Patients harbouring pathogenic, likely pathogenic, or VUS variants in genes with limited, disputed, or no established gene-HI validity were classified as potentially solved, as this requires additional evidence to establish the gene-disease relationship. Among the 46 families, 76% (n = 35/46) were considered solved (n = 22/35; 62.9%) or potentially solved (n = 13/35; 37.1%), and in 24%, no candidate variants were found (n = 11/46).

Variants in solved cases

Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants occurred in known HI genes, accounting for 61.8% of variants (n = 21/34). VUS and likely benign occurred in 38.2% of variants in known HI genes (n = 13/34). These genes included MYO15A in five families, TMC1 in three families, and CDH23, SLC26A4 in two families each. In addition, MITF, USH2A, ACTG1, BSND, CIB2, COL11A1, MYO7A, OTOF, OTOG, and USH1C were found in one family each (Fig. 1B, C; Supplementary data 1). Variants in two different HI genes were found in two families, including Fam3 (CDH23 and PCDH15) and Fam18 (CLCNKA and CLCNKB,) with Usher syndrome type 1D and Bartter syndrome type 4b, respectively (Fig. 1B, Supplementary data 1). This finding suggests possible digenic inheritance in these families.

In known genes, 32.4% (n = 11/34) of variants were previously described as associated with HI, and 67.6% (n = 23/34) were not previously reported as associated with HI (Fig. 1E). Regarding the nature of variants, missense variants were the most detected (n = 16; 47.1%) followed by insertion, deletion, or duplication variants (n = 6; 17.6%), splicing variants (n = 4; 11.8%), nonsense variants (n = 7; 20.6%) and synonymous (n = 1; 2.9%) variants (Fig. 1C). Compound heterozygote variants were identified in 54.5% of families (n = 12), followed by heterozygotes (n = 2; 9.1%), homozygous variants (n = 8; 36.4%) were identified (Fig. 1D). Several in silico predictions showed that these variants were deleterious. Additionally, other variants in known HI genes that did not segregate with the phenotype within the families were identified (Supplementary data 1).

Variants in potentially solved cases

As described above, we classified as potentially solved, patients harbouring pathogenic, likely pathogenic, or VUS variants in genes with limited, disputed, or no established ClinGen gene-HI validity. These variants were identified in gene including TJP2 in two families and CATSPER2, GRM8, CLIC5, DMXL2, GREB1L, GPRASP2, CLCNKA, CLCNKB, SUN2, TGM6, TMC3, in one family respectively (Supplementary data 1, Supplementary Table 2,6).

Splicing and intronic variant predictions

The splicing site variant in USH1C c.496+1 G > A was predicted to disrupt the splice donor site with a high score (delta=0.98), while USH1C c.105-2 A > G is expected to perturb the splicing with a score of 0.99, indicating a delta acceptor loss. For MITF (c.448+1 G > A; NM_001354604.2), the delta score was 0.98, and for CLIC5 (c.63+1 G > A; NM_016929.5), the donor site loss was predicted with a score of 0.98. Additionally, the variant OTOF (c.1048-2 A > C; NM_194248.3) was predicted to disrupt the acceptor site with a delta score of 0.99 and to cause acceptor gain with a score of 0.72. However, no significant impact on splicing was predicted for the variant CLCNKB (c.1929+5 C > G; NM_000085.5), as well as variants in SUN2 [c.475 G > A: p.(Val159Ile); NM_015374.3], TGM6 [c.245 G > C:p.(Gly82Ala); NM_198994.3], TMC3 [c.3089 G > A:p.(Gly1030Glu) and c.1343 C > T:p.(Pro448Leu); NM_001080532.3] (Supplementary Table 3).

Population structure and admixture

Population genetic methods revealed a pattern of genetic diversity in our studied dataset consistent with the geographical distribution and historical background of the populations. On PC1, Nilo-Saharan speaking populations from Chad (e.g., Toubou) split from Niger-Congo speaking populations (Fig. 3A). On PC2, rainforest hunter-gatherers (e.g., Baka) split from other studied populations, as expected. On PC3, Niger-Congo-speaking populations from the southern region of Chad (e.g., Sara and Laal) and Ubangi-speakers split from populations in West-Central Africa. On PC4, Bantu-speaking populations from CAR, Gabon, and Cameroon split from other studied populations. Interestingly, some individuals from Cameroon share genetic similarities with populations that are Bantu and non-Bantu speaking populations, highlighting the complex genetic and linguistic diversity of populations in Cameroon and West-Central Africa in general. Admixture results also showed genetic differences between the Baka (blue component), Toubou (green component), Laal (purple component), and Niger-Congo speakers from Nigeria (red component) (Fig. 3B).

Allele frequencies across different ancestry populations in gnomAD

A comparative analysis of allele frequencies across different ancestry populations, we found that common pathogenic variants reported in gnomAD for NSHI genes were not identified in this study, except for CDH23 [c.5237 G > A:(p.Arg1746Gln); NM_022124.6], which occurs at frequencies of 0.00634249 and 0.00705467 in Cameroon and Ghana, respectively. This variant is homozygous in a Ghanaian family, and we previously reported it as the cause of HI in the family26. The variant is heterozygous in Cameroon and not known to be causative in the families sampled (Fig. 4A). In contrast, most common pathogenic variants identified in NSHI genes in this study were mostly absent from gnomAD (Fig. 4B).

SUN2, TMC3, and TGM6 candidate genes for hearing impairment

Putative candidate variants were identified in three families in genes not previously associated with HI, including SUN2, TMC3, and TGM6 genes. A homozygous variant [c.475 G > A:p.(Val159Ile); NM_015374.3], identified in SUN2 was found segregating with profound NSHI in multiplex Fam7 (Fig. 2A). However, a non-segregating heterozygote variant in PCDH15 has been identified in one individual also having the SUN2 variant. Furthermore, a homozygote variant [c.245 G > C: p.(Gly82Ala); NM_198994.3] was identified in TGM6 candidate gene in Fam19 with NSHI (Fig. 2B. Compound heterozygote variants [c.3089 G > A:p.(Gly1030Glu) and c.1343 C > T: p.(Pro448Leu); NM_001080532.3] were found in TMC3 in Fam11. (Supplementary data 1, Figs. 1B; 2C).

Sanger sequencing and in silico predictions of variants in SUN2, TMC3, and TGM6 candidates

Putative variants identified in candidate genes were sequenced using Sanger sequencing and confirmed segregation of the variant with HI within the families. Segregations were confirmed in affected families for all three SUN2, TMC3, and TGM6 candidate genes, and amino acid residues at variant sites were found to be conserved across species (Fig. 2D-F). These variants were predicted to be damaging by several in silico prediction tools.

Secondary structures and 3D prediction of candidate SUN2, TMC3, and TGM6

The NCBI PSI-BLAST search of the candidate genes SUN2, TMC3, and TGM6 against the non-redundant protein database found residues variably conserved among different species (Fig. 2G–I). Interestingly, Val159 appears to be conserved only in humans and primates across a wide range of species, which suggests Val159 might have a specific role in humans and primates compared to other species.

In silico protein modelling revealed several structural changes in the mutant proteins. In SUN2, the helical structure at 4RSQ6 and at 40RTLK46 was extended by one amino acid in the mutant. The helical structure at 84SS85 was completely lost due to the mutation. In addition, the mutant protein partially lost several helical structures and beta sheet, as well as an extension of some helical structures (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Also, Val159 has a direct bonding interaction with Arg156, which is lost due to the variant. These changes were apparent in the 3D structure of the SUN2 mutant (Supplementary Fig. 3A).

In TGM6, the helices located at 18AA17 and 222AM223 were extended by one amino acid each. The helical structure located at 237ESW239 was transformed into a beta sheet. Furthermore, the mutant protein gained a new helix at 422IR424. Furthermore, several beta sheet structural changes were also observed (Supplementary Fig. 2B). These changes were apparent in the 3D structures. In addition, the wild-type amino acid Gly82 has a direct hydrogen bond interaction with Glu80, which is lost due to the mutation (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

In the TMC3, variant p.(Gly1030Glu), led to a partial loss of the helix at 195TVW197, 207VLFY201, and 401VLYLGNLYSLIIALLDKVN419. Furthermore, the beta-sheet located at 205YS206 was extended in the mutant by two amino acids. In addition, the variant p.Pro448Leu introduced significant changes both in the helical structures and beta strands. Helical structures were extended, including those located at 71MGQKLRALRQAKNIVLKF78 and the helix at 755SQL757 was completely lost in the mutant. In addition, other minor changes were also observed (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D). These structural changes were apparent in the 3D images (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D).

Expression of candidate genes in humans and mice

The expression patterns of candidate genes (SUN2, TMC3, and TGM6) in the human inner ear atlas and organoids and the mouse cochlear tissues were investigated. These genes showed variable expression patterns in inner ear tissue, with SUN2 moderately expressed in hair cells and strongly in chondrocytes, TMC3 less expressed in hair cells, and moderate to strong in otic cells and chondrocytes, and TGM6 was merely expressed in hair cells and other cell types (Fig. 5A–C).

In vitro cell experiments of candidate genes

Quantitative colocalization analysis of SUN2 in biological duplicates showed consistently high Manders’ coefficients for both WT and MT proteins (WT: m1 = 0.95–0.98; m2 = 0.89–0.92; MT: m1 = 0.99–0.99; m2 = 0.95–0.95, Supplementary Fig. 5A), indicating that most signals occupied the same compartments. Western blot experiments confirmed that both WT and MT SUN2 bands were at the expected size. Although a lower expression of mutant SUN2 was found compared to wildtype, statistical analysis did not show a significant difference in the mean of the two groups with p = 0.2287 (Supplementary Fig. 5C–E). No significant difference in fluorescence or localization was noted for TMC3 and TGM6 mutants compared to wild type, which were consistent with the western blot findings (Fig. 6A–D).

Mouse experiments for candidate gene Sun2

The auditory thresholds of the Sun2-knockout (Sun2KO) mutant mice were comparable to those of the heterozygous and wild-type controls at postnatal day 21 (P21), with no significant differences observed (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Table 7). To assess the knockout efficiency in Sun2KO mutant mice, we performed immunostaining on the organ of Corti from both Sun2KO and WT mice. As SUN2 is previously identified to localize to the nuclear membrane in the cells54, we observed that SUN2 protein expression is exclusively in the supporting cells on the modiolar side of the inner hair cells. No SUN2 expression was detected in the hair cells or other supporting cells. In Sun2KO mice, SUN2 expression was not detectable in the supporting cells, confirming the effective deletion of the Sun2 gene (Fig. 7C).

Unsolved families through whole exome sequencing

Families with non-segregating variants or no putative variants were considered unsolved, representing 24% of families (n = 11; Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1). No candidate variants were identified in nine studied families (Fam2, Fam25, Fam29, Fam31, Fam34, Fam35, Fam40, Fam41, and Fam42). Additionally, non-segregating variants were found in CDH23 [c.2560 C > T:p.(Arg854Cys); NM_022124.6] and PCDH15 [c.1205 G > C:p.(Gly402Ala); NM_001384140.1] in Fam8, and a heterozygote variant in SIX5 [c.1802C>T:p.(Pro601Leu); NM_175875.5] in a family with a suspected autosomal recessive inheritance. Furthermore, a non-segregating compound heterozygote [c.3470 A > C:p.(His1157Pro) and c.5794 C > G:p.(Leu1932Val); NM_001379500.1] in COL18A1 was identified Fam20, with one affected individual having only one variant.

Discussion

Next-generation sequencing has tremendously revolutionized the current field of medical genetics, with WES shown to be efficient and cost-effective in diagnosing monogenic diseases55–57. To the best of our knowledge, this is the most comprehensive study on the genetics of hearing impairment in a single country in Africa, i.e., Cameroon. The study used WES, population genetics structure, admixture approaches, pedigrees, and segregation analysis, in silico investigations of genetic variants. In addition, in vitro cell-based experiments were used to explore the pathogenicity of the targeted variant in SUN2, TGM6, and TMC3 candidate genes, human inner ear organoids, and mouse cochlear tissue targeted tissues expression of candidate genes, and a Sun2KO mice model was also used to investigate their functional evidence in HI. The results emphasize the opportunities for highly diverse African populations to unravel unreported variants or disease-gene associations.

Population genetic methods revealed a pattern of genetic diversity in our studied dataset consistent with the geographical distribution and historical background of the African populations37. The admixture pattern of the studied populations of Cameroon suggested population structure between them and their neighbouring populations, highlighting events of migration and gene flow between those populations (Fig. 3), in agreement with previous genome-wide studies37,58,59.

Corroborating with previous reports60,61, NSHI was the most common phenotype identified in our study, and the underlying mechanism was predominantly sensorineural62. The rate of consanguinity observed in this study is low and similar to the previous report from Cameoon24,31. Consequently, compound heterozygote variants were frequently identified, supporting previous findings in Cameroonian populations32. Notably, common pathogenic variants in NSHI were not identified in this study. Similarly, most variants found in the commonest HI genes in this study had not been previously reported in the gnomAD database, which will significantly contribute to improving global HI-Genes pairs curation (Fig. 4).

Importantly, variants in three candidate genes were identified (SUN2, TGM6, and TMC3). First, the SUN2 gene (OMIM: 613569) located at 22q13.1, with SUN1 gene (OMIM: 607723) encode for inner nuclear membrane proteins that play a crucial role in nuclear-cytoplasmic connection by forming a ‘bridge’ across the nuclear envelope, known as the LINC complex63–65. While SUN1 has been implicated in HI65–67, SUN2’s role in HI is not yet well established to date. A homozygous variant was identified in SUN2 in Fam7 family with NSHI phenotype. HEK293T cells experiments showed that the variant SUN2:p.(Val159Ile) led to the mis-localization of the mutant protein and with lower expression compared to wildtype SUN2, suggesting that this variant in SUN2 may disrupt the LINC complex. However, the Sun2-knockout mutant mice do not exhibit hearing phenotypes at p21. Nevertheless, the amino acid Val159 is not conserved in mice, suggesting that Sun2 may be dispensable in mice. Furthermore, human SUN2 seems to be LOF intolerant with observed and expected variants ratio o/e = 0.73 (gnomAD v4.1.0). Therefore, mice models appear not to be suitable to investigate Sun2-related HI. However, the hearing threshold of Sun2-knockout mice was measured at a single time point, which could have missed progressive HI. An alternative approach would be for non-human primates in which the amino acid Val159 is well conserved or a double knockout involving SUN1 gene, which, together with SUN2 gene, forms the LINC complex. Moreover, reports of additional unrelated families implicating SUN2 in HI will be critical in furthering functional investigations.

Second, TGM6, located at 20p13, encodes transglutaminase 6 protein, previously associated with spinocerebellar ataxia type 35, a dominant neurodegenerative condition68,69. A homozygous variant [c.245 G > C:p.(Gly82Ala] was identified in the TGM6 gene in Fam19 with NSHI and segregated with HI. While this homozygous variant was predicted to be pathogenic with in silico tools, cell-based experiments in HEK293T did not show significant differences between wild-type and mutant proteins. Further research, using animal models such as mice or zebrafish could provide further insights on the potential involvement of TGM6 in hearing pathways.

Third, the TMC3 gene (OMIM: 617196), located at 15q25.1, encodes the transmembrane channel-like protein 3 and has not been reported to cause human disease70,71. However, TMC3 is part of the transmembrane channel-like (TMC) gene family, among which the TMC1 gene is known to cause HI71–73. Compound heterozygote variants were identified in the TMC3 gene in Fam11. However, these variants were not found to impact expression patterns in HEK293T cells. In addition, previous research on TMC3-knockout mice did not replicate significant hearing phenotypes in mice74,75. suggesting additional studies are needed to confirm TMC3 as an HI gene.

Among known genes, MYO15A was the most common gene, followed by TMC1, consistent with data in the literature76. These findings are similar to reports from Mali, and from Ghana using a similar approach after excluding GJB2 variants26, and as well other studies in non-African populations26,77,78 in which the most common genes identified, besides GJB2 when exiting, included MYO15A, TMC1, SLC26A4, CDH23, TJP2, and GREB1L genes. These genes represented 45% of the known genes identified in this study, encompassing 14 families from Cameroon.

Furthermore, among syndromic HI, in Fam3 (CDH23 and PCDH15) and Fam18 (CLCNKA and CLCNKB) families, a possible digenic inheritance was found, including a family of Usher syndrome type 1D and Bartter syndrome type 4b, respectively. Although little is known about digenic inheritance in these genes, previous reports support that possibility79,80, underscoring the genetic heterogeneity of genetic HI in understudied African populations. Corroboratively, the majority of variants reported in known genes were not previously associated with HI or reported in the variant databases, including gnomAD. This suggests that genetic studies in understudied and genetically diverse African populations could improve efforts to better understand the pathogenesis of genetic disorders and further expand the gene-phenotype pairs curation in HI.

Conclusion

This study further supports the utility of WES in identifying genetic variants associated with HI, with a relatively high rate of successful molecular diagnosis (76%) in 46 multiplex families from Cameroon, with MYO15A reported as the most common, followed by TMC1. Most variants identified in this study were not previously associated with HI or reported in variant databases. In three unrelated families in Cameroon, candidate genes previously undescribed to be associated with HI were identified: SUN2, TGM6, and TMC3, however were not confirmed in cell-based experiments and/or a mouse model for SUN2. This study emphasizes that genetic studies of underserved and genetically diverse populations in Africa, further supported by admixture mapping, will reveal numerous unreported variants and candidate genes. Such research in other African unstudied populations is critically important with the evolving promise of developments in gene therapy for HI, globally.
