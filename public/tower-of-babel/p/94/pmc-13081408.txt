Differential associations of sleep duration, sleep disturbance and falls among middle-aged and older adults with and without cardiovascular disease status: results from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)
Authors: Peiyu Song, Yinjiao Zhao, Ying Cai, Bing Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to investigate the associations between sleep duration, sleep disturbances and falls in different cardiovascular disease (CVD) states in a Chinese middle-aged and older adult population.MethodsThis study used two waves (2011 wave1 and 2013 wave2) of data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to collect information on social, economic, and health status by tracking Chinese individuals aged 45 and above. The study used a logistic regression model to examine the association between sleep parameters and falls. Restricted cubic spline was used to analyze the association of sleep duration with falls in the overall population, and in the population with or without CVD.ResultsThe overall incidence of new falls among all subjects was 18.35%. It was 24.54% in the population with CVD and 17.36% in the population without CVD. After adjusting for multiple factors, short sleep duration (less than 6 h) was significantly associated with an elevated risk of falls in total populations and with or without CVD, with OR values (95%CI) of 1.267(1.143,1.405), 1.227(1.095,1.376) and 1.442(1.130,1.841), respectively. Long sleep duration (longer than 9 h) was significantly associated with an increased risk of falls only in the CVD population, with OR values of 2.042(1.175,3.550). Sleep disturbance more than 3 days per week was significantly associated with elevated fall risk in the total population and in the population without CVD, and more than 5 days was significantly associated with elevated fall risk in the CVD population.ConclusionsThis study reveals the association between sleep parameters and falls, particularly among patients with cardiovascular disease, where long sleep duration is associated with falls. This suggests that long sleep may be an important factor in the risk of falls in CVD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05663-6.

Introduction

Falls are the second leading cause of unintentional injury-related death in the world. In the middle-aged population, the incidence of falls is showing a sharp upward trend [1]. Among the elderly, there is a severe trend of a 41% surge in fall-related deaths over the past decade [2, 3]. In China, falls are the leading cause of injury-related death among people over 65 years old, and the chain of health damage (fractures, disability, and death) caused by falls poses a significant burden on the social medical system [4, 5]. Given such a heavy burden of morbidity, fall prevention interventions that identify which modifiable fall risk factors to target are necessary.

Currently, a large number of studies have confirmed that sleep disorders can indirectly increase the risk of falls through deterioration in attention, memory problems, and difficulty concentrating, but there is still controversy about the dose–response relationship between sleep duration and falls. Some studies show a U-shaped curve, while others only show a unilateral risk of short sleep [6–9]. In-depth analysis found that this divergence may be due to the heterogeneity of the health status of the population. The U-shaped association is more common in groups with poor health and more comorbidities, while unilateral risks are more common in relatively healthy people [8, 10–12]. This suggests that health status may regulate the relationship between sleep and falls. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death among Chinese residents (accounting for more than 40% of deaths) and has a huge impact on health status, which just constitutes the core watershed of the health status of middle-aged and elderly people [13].

The impact of CVD on falls has a bidirectional pathophysiological mechanism. On the one hand, the decline in cardiopulmonary function and impaired gait stability caused by the disease itself can directly increase the risk of falls; on the other hand, CVD patients often have disordered sleep structure, which increases the risk of falls through circadian rhythm disorders [14, 15]. It is worth noting that the stage characteristics of the CVD course (from subclinical state after 45 years of age to high-risk state after 65 years of age) may cause the impact of sleep duration on falls to change dynamically [16], but there is currently a lack of stratified evidence.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to explore the association between sleep duration and falls in Chinese middle-aged and older adults with different CVD states, which will provide more detailed evidence for screening and lifestyle interventions in high-risk groups with falls.

Method

Study population

The data were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [17]. The CHARLS is a nationally representative longitudinal survey of Chinese people aged 45 years or older and their spouses, which aims to collect a set of high-quality microdata representing households and individuals aged 45 and above in China to analyze the aging of China’s population and promote interdisciplinary research on aging, including assessments of social, economic, and health status of community residents. The samples covered 450 villages, 150 counties, and 28 provinces, involving more than 17 000 people from about 10 000 households. The datasets can be downloaded at the CHARLS home page at http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en. The CHARLS survey project was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University, and all participants were required to sign informed consent.

Baseline interview data were collected during the initial wave of surveys carried out by CHARLS in 2011 (wave 1), while follow-up data were obtained from the third wave conducted in 2013 (wave 2). All participants are required to complete an annual health screening and a detailed questionnaire on lifestyle and disease history. Subsequently, these participants were regularly followed up every two years following the baseline survey. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) aged below 45 years, (2) did not participate in wave 2 follow-up, (3) lack of data on nighttime sleep duration and sleep quality, (4) lack of data on CVD (including heart disease and stroke) at baseline, (5) lack of information on falls at follow-up time.

A total of 17,706 people participated in the study. Due to missing values or meeting the exclusion criteria, 5026 participants were excluded, and the remaining 12,680 participants were included in the final analysis.

Fall accidents

The primary outcome was new-onset falls accidents in 2013. In 2011, at baseline, participants were asked if they had ever experienced a fall. At the subsequent follow-up (2013), participants were asked about any new-onset falls that had occurred since the last interview. The response options were limited to a binary choice of “yes” or “no”. We define the occurrence of falls in 2011 as the prevalence of falls, and the incidence of new falls during follow-up in 2013 as the incidence of falls.

Assessment of CVD events

The study outcome was CVD events, including heart disease and stroke. Similar to previous studies, CVD events were assessed by the following questions: “Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with a heart attack, angina, coronary heart disease, heart failure, or other heart problems?” or “Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with a stroke?”. Participants who reported heart disease or stroke were defined as having CVD.

Assessment of sleep parameters

Sleep duration was available from the lifestyle and health behaviors section of the CHARLS questionnaire. More specifically, the item was ‘During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for one night). The participants provided the answers in hours and minutes. Based on the results of previous studies, we divided sleep duration into five groups (≤ 6 h, 6–8 h as a reference group, 8–9 h, and > 9 h) [18].

Sleep disturbance from combined sources of having trouble falling asleep, frequently nighttime awakenings and earlier waking was measured in days per week on a scale of four: “none (< 1 day)”, “little (1–2 days)”, “occasionally (3–4 days)” and “most (5–7 days)” [8].

Nap duration was evaluated using the question: How much sleep did you typically get during daytime naps in the past month? Participants were segmented into four groups: no nap (0 min), short nap (< 30 min), medium nap (30–90 min), and long nap (> 90 min) [19].

Covariates

Data on all covariates were obtained at baseline by trained interviewers via standard questionnaires. The questionnaire included inquiries about age, gender, marital status, and educational level. The educational level was classified as illiteracy, elementary school, middle school, high school and above. Health-related factors included self-reported current state of smoking and drinking status and self-reported physician-diagnosed medical conditions (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, cancer, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, and osteoarthritis). This study used standardized tests for height and weight. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared.

Statistical analysis

The baseline sociodemographic, lifestyle and health-related characteristics of participants were presented according to fall and CVD classification. In the analysis of differences for baseline characteristics, continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation using t-test or Mann–Whitney U-tests, and categorical variables were presented as percentages using chi-square test. Restricted cubic splines were used to observe the overall shape of the relationship between sleep duration and fall (created by R 4.05). Logistic regression analysis was used to analyze the relationship between sleep duration and fall in the whole population and with or without CVD. The results were expressed by OR, 95% CIs and corresponding p value. The confounding factors of age, gender, education, marital status, living conditions, drinking, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, osteoarthritis, lung diseases and history of falls were adjusted. P value < 0.05 was considered as significant difference. SPSS v26.0 and R 4.05 software were used for data statistical analysis.

The association between sleep duration and fall in total population and with or without CVD is shown in Fig. 1. After multifactorial adjustment, sleep duration of less than 6 h was significantly associated with an elevated risk of new onset of falls in the overall population, in patients without CVD and in patients with CVD, with ORs of 1.61 (1.46,1.77), 1.55 (1.40,1.73) and 1.73 (1.37,2.17), respectively. Sleeping longer than 9 h was significantly associated with a higher risk of falls in patients with CVD with an OR of 2.04 (1.18,3.55), whereas there was no significant association in the total population and in the absence of CVD. Discussion

This study explored the association of sleep duration and sleep disorders with the risk of fall in different cardiovascular disease states. The results showed that short sleep duration was significantly associated with an elevated risk of fall occurrence in the total population and in patients with and without cardiovascular disease, there was a U-shaped association between sleep duration and the risk of fall occurrence in patients with cardiovascular disease. Moreover, we found that the presence of sleep disturbances 3 times per week was associated with an elevated risk of falls in the overall population and in the population without cardiovascular disease, and in patients with cardiovascular disease, sleep disturbances of more than 5 days per week were associated with a risk of falls. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to observe a direct and complex association between cardiovascular disease, sleep, and falls, and will provide a theoretical basis for sleep treatment strategies for fall prevention in different cardiovascular disease states.

The association between sleep and falls has been explored in studies, however, the current evidence points to inconsistencies. Several studies have shown that short sleep duration is significantly associated with the risk of falls [8, 9], and the same finding was echoed in our study, where the risk of falls was significantly elevated in those who slept less than 6 h in the overall population and in those with or without cardiovascular disease. Problems with falling asleep and maintaining sleep ability (such as insomnia) are common in the elderly population, and there is evidence to suggest that older adults with poor sleep are less likely to maintain physical activity and performance, which is directly related to an increased risk of falls [20, 21], research has shown that elderly individuals with subjective sleep duration less than 6 h have a 3.3-fold increased risk of developing mobility disorders during a 6-year follow-up period compared to those with normal sleep [22]. Secondly, there is a correlation between short sleep duration and increased risk of cardiovascular disease and inflammation levels. Studies have shown that for every hour of reduced sleep duration compared to standard sleep time, tumor necrosis factor alpha levels increase by 8%. Tumor necrosis factor has been shown to be associated with prolonged reaction time, impaired attention and memory, and increased risk of falls [23–25]. In terms of basic research, sleep deprivation causes cerebral white matter lesions, which has been shown to be a strong risk factor for falls [26, 27] Studies have shown that individuals with white matter lesions in the highest quartile of distribution have twice the risk of accidental falls compared to those with lesions in the lowest quartile (relative risk is 2.32). The increase in lesion volume is also associated with poor gait and greater gait variability [27]. Several other studies have shown that excessive sleep duration is also significantly associated with an elevated risk for the occurrence of falls. Our study showed that long sleep duration (> 9 h) was associated with an elevated risk of fall occurrence only in patients with cardiovascular disease. Another study from China showed that sleep duration greater than 10 h was associated with an increased risk of falls; however, this association was only seen in those who slept earlier than 21:00 h at night and had multiple falls during follow-up, and the prevalence of myocardial infarction, stroke, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus was much higher in those patients than in the group with no falls and single falls [28]. The mechanism by which long sleep duration is associated with an increased risk of falls remains incompletely elucidated and is key in current research. It has been suggested that long sleep duration may be a marker of frailty and disease states and poor health, which is associated with poor physical performance and increased risk of falls [29]. Another possible reason is that underlying diseases can lead to increased habitual sleep time and elevated levels of inflammation (cytokines, CRP), which can also increase the risk of falls [23]. Therefore, observing the association between sleep duration and falls in populations with specific disease states may be more helpful in the precise development of fall prevention strategies.

The association between decreased sleep quality and increased risk of falls has been elucidated in some studies [30, 31]. Similar to previous studies, our study showed that sleep disturbances of more than 3 days per week were significantly associated with an increased risk of falls in the general population and in the population without CVD. In the CVD population, sleep disturbances of more than 5 days per week were significantly associated with an increased risk of falls. The results differ from what we originally envisioned, namely that a lower frequency of sleep disturbances would be significantly associated with an elevated risk of falls in patients with CVD compared with the general population. Possible reasons for this include, first, the extremely high proportion of falls patients with CVD who had sleep disturbances more than 5 days per week (35.28%), which is close to the proportion of falls group of patients without CVD who had sleep disturbances more than 3 days per week, and, second, the low sample size of the falls group with CVD, which may have contributed to the non-significant results.

Similar to our research findings, both short and long sleep are associated with an increased risk of falls in cardiovascular diseases, and there is no significant association between long sleep duration and fall risk in non-cardiovascular diseases [32]. However, this study was limited to the elderly population, with a small sample size, and did not further observe gender differences in sleep duration and falls. On the basis of this research, we further explore, we found that the results for women were similar to those of the total population, and no significant association was found in men. Similar to some studies, the distribution of fall risk in the general population is highly overlapping with that in women [12]. Therefore, being female may be an independent risk factor in the association between sleep and falls, but the underlying mechanism still needs further study and verification.

This article still has some limitations. First, sleep variables, including sleep duration, and sleep disorders were self-reported by patients, which may be in error compared to the results measured by objective equipment. Second, the number of subjects with long sleep duration (> 9 h) was small, accounting for only 5%, which may make the logistic regression results highly sensitive to outliers. Third, the diagnosis of falls relied on subject recall, and thus may have been influenced by recall bias. Fourth, the article lacks data on physical activity, which is strongly associated with sleep and falls, and this may have affected the results. Fifth, due to the lack of key information, a large proportion of subjects were excluded in this study, which may result in non-randomness in the exclusion process. It is hoped that future studies can reduce the dropout rate and increase the rigor of the argument. Finally, this study focused only on the Chinese population; however, the burden of disease varies across countries and regions, and the decision to focus on subjects with the disease may need to be based on a research judgment. Therefore, conducting similar large-scale surveys or studies in different countries and regions would increase the generalizability and applicability of the findings.

Conclusion

In summary, the results of this two-year cohort study in China reveal a complex association between sleep variables and the risk of falls in individuals with different cardiovascular disease states. These findings provide a more precise theoretical basis for developing targeted sleep management strategies to prevent falls in individuals at various stages of cardiovascular health.
