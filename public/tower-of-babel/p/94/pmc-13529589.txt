The three-dimensional structure of fine-scale, vertical velocities in the Southern Ocean inferred from space
Authors: Andrew F Thompson, Lilian A Dove, Yann-Treden Tranchant, Benoit Legresy, Beatriz Peña Molino, Laura Herraiz Borreguero, Zichang He, Patrice Klein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUpper ocean vertical velocities link surface properties to the ocean interior and are typically intensified at small-scale fronts. The small footprint of these velocities and their intermittent nature presents a challenge for determining vertical transport over large regions and long time periods. Here, we diagnose vertical velocities by exploiting the high resolution sea surface height observations collected by the Surface Water and Ocean Topography altimeter along with interior hydrographic measurements from ocean gliders. A surface-height-based reconstruction of interior density and velocity fields permits an estimate of vertical velocities by applying the quasi-geostrophic omega equation at fine spatial scales. Vertical velocities are deep-reaching, have horizontal scales as small as 10 km, and commonly exceed 150 m day−1 throughout the upper 1000 m. The statistical relationship of vertical velocity with vorticity and strain fields are consistent with high-resolution numerical models. This analysis is a step towards generating temporally-varying vertical tracer fluxes for the circumpolar Southern Ocean.Subject terms: Physical oceanography, Physical oceanography, Carbon cycle

Introduction

The ocean’s vertical velocity field carries heat, dissolved gases, nutrients, and other tracers between the ocean’s surface boundary layer and the ocean interior1–4. Transfer across the base of the mixed layer sequesters dissolved gases from exchange with the atmosphere and helps regulate primary productivity through nutrient delivery, highlighting the importance of ocean vertical velocities in the Earth system5–7. Vertical velocities at mesoscale and larger scales (roughly 100 km and larger), associated with small Rossby numbers, are typically 1–10 m day−1 with local intensification within coherent mesoscale eddies8,9. Vertical velocities become elevated at smaller scales where the Rossby number approaches unity and ageostrophic motions become more prominent, with magnitudes occasionally reaching many tens to hundreds of m day−1 10–12. Various approaches have attempted to estimate the global impact of vertical motions and fluxes in the upper ocean, either from direct diagnosis of high resolution numerical models or from the application of parameterizations to more readily observed, coarse-resolution ocean properties. Su et al. (2018)13 analyzed a 2 km resolution global model and found that submesoscale heat fluxes at 40 m depth are up to five times larger than mesoscale fluxes; this heat flux is systematically upward, making a leading order contribution to the upper ocean heat budget. Omand et al. (2015)14 used a parameterization of submesoscale baroclinic instability to suggest that small-scale vertical advection accounts for up to 50% of the total export of particulate organic carbon in the North Atlantic, the Kuroshio extension and the Southern Ocean.

Previous studies of upper ocean vertical velocities have largely focused on surface boundary layer processes, yet recent observations suggest that intensified vertical motions may extend well below the base of the mixed layer. In energetic regions of the Southern Ocean, deep anomalies in dissolved oxygen concentrations and spice (density-compensated temperature/salinity variations) have been attributed to intense subduction events that extend hundreds of meters below the base of the mixed layer15,16. In the North Atlantic, data from a dense mooring network was used to infer vertical motions that reached 100 m day−1 throughout winter, extending at least 200 m below the base of the mixed layer12. Vertical velocities with a similar 100 m day−1 magnitude, at depths of 300–500 m, were inferred from density measurements obtained from an instrumented elephant seal in the Southern Ocean, with vertical velocities amplified at the edges of coherent mesoscale eddies17. By correlating these vertical velocities with temperature measurements, this study further showed that heat fluxes were predominantly upward with a magnitude of ~ 1000 W m−2. The diagnosis of vertical velocities in Siegelman et al. (2020)17 relied on low-resolution gridded altimetric data as well as a two-dimensional, approximate form of the quasi-geostrophic (QG)-omega equation. The present study introduces a new approach that leverages the high-resolution Surface Water Ocean Topography (SWOT) altimetry observations18 to reconstruct three-dimensional density and velocity fields from the surface to 1000 m depths.

The use of remote sensing to infer vertical velocities is attractive because of the potential for assessing the integrated impact of these dynamics over basin scales and seasonal to inter-annual periods. Coincident measurements of density and velocity fields permit the application of the QG omega equation19 to estimate vertical motions associated with fronts and eddies8,20–22. However, in situ data is typically sparse. Surface QG theory (SQG) and the effective-SQG (eSQG) approach provide alternative frameworks for deriving interior velocities from surface observations23. SQG and eSQG theories assume that interior vertical and horizontal density variations, respectively, are weak, which may not hold in strong frontal regions of the Southern Ocean or in western boundary currents. Furthermore, gridded radar altimetry products contain dynamical information for scales of ~ 150 km and larger, and are often too coarse for eSQG theory to be applicable. The SWOT altimetry mission, by comparison, provides gridded observations of sea surface height (SSH) variations at 2 km scales. Carli et al. (2024)24 assessed the potential of the eSQG method to reconstruct vertical velocities and vertical heat fluxes in the upper 1000 m of the water column in the Southern Ocean’s Polar Front by comparing to a numerical model. This study found that eSQG reproduces 90% of the model RMS vertical velocity variability at horizontal scales down to 30 km with a magnitude peaking at tens to 100 m day−1. Below we show that accounting for interior density variability can produce vertical velocities with a greater magnitude and finer spatial scales as compared to estimates by eSQG.

The strong vertical coherence of circulation features in the Southern Ocean, referred to as its equivalent barotropic structure25,26, allows inference of interior hydrographic properties from surface proxies, such as dynamic height. Specifically, Southern Ocean studies have used the gravest empirical mode (GEM) method to relate the integrated water column density, estimated via dynamic height or acoustic travel time, to full-depth profiles of temperature and salinity27,28. Meijers et al. (2011)29 extended the GEM relationship to satellite SSH observations, SatGEM, producing slowly-evolving, full-depth temperature, salinity, and density fields from altimetry measurements alone. SatGEM-2 further improved this product by incorporating gradient-wind velocity corrections that account for curvature in time-evolving SSH fields30.

This study applies the QG-omega equation with a local GEM approach to infer high-resolution upper ocean vertical velocities. The density reconstruction uses observations collected from two ocean gliders as a component of the FOCUS (Finescale Observations in the Antarctic Circumpolar Current Under SWOT) field campaign, a contribution to the SWOT regional calibration and validation activities. Vertical velocities are shown to be enhanced in narrow O(10 km) regions, typically found at the edges of mesoscale eddies, consistent with numerical studies31,32, but with magnitudes that exceed recent estimates from eSQG24. The combination of SWOT observations and in situ data provides a path toward future circumpolar estimates of upper ocean vertical velocities and tracer fluxes.

Results

Hydrographic conditions of the FOCUS study region

The FOCUS field campaign consisted of a five-week research cruise on the R/V Investigator that surveyed an energetic standing meander of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC). South of Tasmania, the ACC encounters the Southeast Indian Ridge and the Macquarie Ridge (Fig. 1a). The region between these ridges is associated with enhanced levels of eddy kinetic energy (EKE)33, cross-frontal exchange34,35, poleward eddy heat transport36, frontogenesis37, ventilation38,39, and turbulent mixing40. This region is well characterized by previous process-based field studies and numerical simulations41. The FOCUS region is characterized by persistent straining motions (diagnosed from altimetry-based surface velocity fields)37,39 and deep subduction events, extending to 1 km depths15,37.

Two ocean gliders, SG659 and SG681, surveyed a cyclonic eddy shed from the Polar Front (PF) (boxed region, Fig. 1b; Supplementary Fig. 1). This mesoscale eddy is a strong feature, with an SSH anomaly of almost a meter between the eddy core and the surrounding waters. These features are not uncommon in energetic regions of the ACC, and their strength is likely underestimated by gridded altimetry products42,43. The SWOT altimeter collects SSH observations along two 50 km wide swaths, separated by a “nadir” gap of 20 km. SWOT passes occasionally capture part of mesoscale eddies (Fig. 1c,d) or eddies are spread across left and right swaths (Fig. 2b). The cyclonic eddy and the PF dominate the SSH variations in this region.

Reconstruction of three-dimensional density and horizontal velocity fields

The equivalent barotropic nature of the ACC gives rise to an inverse relationship between absolute dynamic topography (ADT) and depth anomalies of upper-ocean density surfaces (Fig. 2). Regions of cyclonic vorticity are associated with local minima in ADT and anomalous shoaling of subsurface waters; regions of anti-cyclonic vorticity are associated with ADT local maxima and a downward displacement of subsurface waters. This relationship holds for large-scale ACC fronts as well as at the scale of individual mesoscale eddies. By interpolating the gridded DUACS ADT product (Methods) in both space and time to the position of glider SG681, this SSH-potential density, or SSH-σ0, relationship holds to at least the depth of the glider sampling (1 km) (Fig. 2). The interpolated ADT values have similar along-track variations as dynamic height, referenced to 900 m (ϕ900, Methods). The magnitude of ϕ900 anomalies are roughly 60% of the ADT fluctuations, implying that SSH variations in this region arise from density anomalies that extend deeper than 1000 m. On day 12 (Fig. 2a), the ADT time series exhibits sharp variability that is not characteristic of the rest of the record. At this time, the glider was located at the edge of the cyclonic eddy (Fig. 2b, c). This suggests that uncertainty in the interpolated, gridded DUACS product is a potential source of error in the generation of the SSH-σ0 relationship. This uncertainty will be large in regions of strong SSH gradients or when mesoscale eddies evolve rapidly. Interpolation of SWOT SSH measurements to the glider position would provide a higher fidelity SSH-σ0 relationship because of its snapshot nature, but the gliders sample infrequently beneath the SWOT swath and do not capture the full range of SSH values (red curves in Fig. 2a).

A local GEM is produced by discretizing the range of ADT values sampled by the gliders and producing a vertical density profile for each ADT bin. For this analysis, we construct the GEM from observations collected from glider SG681. For simplicity, the bins have a uniform width of 0.02 m, motivated by balancing a desire for fine resolution in SSH space with a need for sufficient glider profiles in each bin. The gliders sample ADT values within the core of fronts, such as the PF, less frequently because of the sharp SSH gradients here44 (Fig. 3a). A composite density profile is produced from all glider profiles that fall within a given ADT bin, based on interpolation of the gridded DUACS altimetry product. In each bin, the mean yearday is calculated and profiles acquired more than one standard deviation from the mean are discarded (Supplementary Fig. 2). The SSH-σ0 relationship uses the mean of the remaining vertical density profiles (see Methods for additional details). The density relationship σ0η,z, where η is ADT and z is depth (Fig. 2b), captures the relatively abrupt transition in vertical density structure at η = − 0.49 m that aligns with the core of the PF45 (Supplementary Fig. 3). This relationship can be used to reconstruct the interior density along the trajectory of both gliders by interpolating the DUACS ADT to glider positions (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Fig. 4). Differences between the inferred and measured density fields are typically less than 10% of the full density anomalies (Fig. 3c), except at the edge of the cyclonic eddy. This elevated difference is attributed to uncertainty in interpolation of the DUACS ADT to the glider position, which becomes less problematic when applying the SSH-σ relationship to SWOT observations. Critically, the differences between observed and reconstructed density for glider SG659, which was not used in constructing the SSH-σ0 relationship, are comparable to the uncertainty in the reconstruction of SG681 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

The SSH-σ0 relationship becomes a powerful tool to reconstruct three-dimensional interior density fields with the two-dimensional SWOT observations. Within each SWOT swath, a density profile is projected downward at each η(x, y) value, provided at 2 km horizontal resolution, which generates a snapshot σ0x,y,z (Fig. 4). The high spatial resolution of the SWOT ADT produces interior density gradients with O(10 km) scales, consistent with glider observations. Estimates of vertical and lateral density stratification, N2 and M2 respectively, (Fig. 4e, f), reveal substantial interior density variability, inconsistent with SQG assumptions. Additionally, a three-dimensional interior velocity field is constructed from an estimate of surface velocities as well as the thermal wind relationship (Methods). Here, surface velocities are determined using cyclogeostrophic balance as described in Tranchant et al. (2025)42. Within the upper 1000 m, horizontal velocities exceed 1 m s−1, consistent with this energetic region of the ACC. The inferred Rossby number, Ro ≡ ζ/f, where ζ = ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y is the relative vorticity and f is the Coriolis frequency, reaches values of Ro ~ 0.5 in frontal regions throughout the upper 1000 m (Fig. 4f; Supplementary Fig. 5). This indicates that ageostrophic motions are prevalent well below the mixed layer. The elevated values of Ro raise some caveats about the application of the QG-omega equation, but we validate our approach using model output below and provide a brief discussion of the application of the Sawyer-Eliassen equation (Methods).

Reconstruction of vertical velocities

Three-dimensional buoyancy and velocity fields allow inference of the magnitude and spatial structure of vertical velocities using the QG-omega equation. This approach does not capture the full vertical velocity field; it focuses on the ageostrophic response to imbalance arising from geostrophic shear and convergence at fronts. While the assumption that Ro ≪ 1, which underlies QG theory, does not hold everywhere in our analyzed region, vertical velocities derived from the QG-omega equation capture much of the subinertial variance in a high-resolution numerical simulation of the same region of the ACC (§2.4). ∇h2=∂xx+∂yy, N2¯(z) is the horizontally averaged vertical stratification over the swath, w is the vertical velocity, and b=gρ0−1σ0¯−σ0 is the buoyancy field. The inferred u, v, and b fields allow calculation of the right-hand “forcing” in (1). After each spatial derivative, fields are smoothed using a Gaussian filter with a kernel that has a 12 km full width half maximum, applied both to remove noise and to approximate the balanced component of the flow. The sensitivity of w to this kernel is shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. There are a growing number of studies focused on isolating the balanced component of the SSH field using different techniques46,47, which could be explored in future work. SSH fields are tiled in along-track and cross-track directions to create a doubly periodic domain that is used to invert for w48 using the xinvert Python package49 (Methods). This choice of boundary condition will introduce errors in w near the swath edges (see further discussion in §3). The xinvert package requires Dirichlet boundary conditions at both the top and bottom of the domain. We set w = 0 at the sea surface and at 2000 m depth, after extending N2 and Q values at 1000 m to this greater depth. This approximates a Neumann boundary condition, ∂w/∂z = 0, at 1000 m. Applying this method to a numerical model (§2.4) gives confidence that these boundary condition choices result in small errors to w estimates.

Narrow regions of intense upward and downward motion, typically 10 to a few 10’s of km wide, characterize the w field (Fig. 4h). Elevated vertical velocities align with regions of strong lateral interior density gradients, linked to the periphery of mesoscale eddies and the PF (Fig. 4). Vertical velocities in these compact regions often exceed 100 m day−1. Finally, vertical velocities extend coherently to at least 1000 m, the depth sampled by the gliders.

Considering 18 SWOT passes covering the FOCUS region in January 2024, we find vertical velocities and Rossby number that reach 100 m day−1 and 0.5, respectively, in almost all swaths (Fig. 5a, b). Distributions of w and Ro may be either positively or negatively skewed within a single SWOT pass (Fig. 5c, d), but a consistent skewness is not evident. This lack of skewness is likely due to the limited spatial and temporal coverage in this study although previous studies have shown that w or the divergence field tends to exhibit weaker skewness than ζ. When w is conditionally averaged on Ro and the strain α≡αn2+αs2, where αn = ux − vy and αs = ux + vy50, w tends to take the opposite sign of Ro, at least for small to moderate Ro values (Fig. 6a). This relationship is consistent with a tendency for an ageostrophic overturning circulation to re-stratify geostrophic frontal currents51. Large values of downward velocity (w < 0) also coincide with regions of elevated strain. The vertical variability in w, expressed as a standard deviation, peaks around 600 m depth (Fig. 5f) and has a similar structure to that diagnosed from a high-resolution numerical model (Fig. 7c). The standard deviation of w is also large for large Ro and α/f values (Fig. 6c).

Comparison with numerical simulation

In the absence of independent observations of w, we turn to a regional numerical model to assess the applicability of the density reconstruction and 3D QG-omega approach. The model has a 3 km grid spacing and an effective resolution of ~ 20 km, consistent with the QG assumptions used here. Full model details are provided in the Methods. The aim of this experiment was to assess whether SWOT-like SSH fields, when combined with vertically reconstructed hydrography, allow for accurate estimation of vertical velocities. Specifically, we evaluate how much of the vertical velocity variance in the sub-inertial frequency band is captured by this approach.

Following §2.2, we construct a local GEM for each SWOT-like model section (Fig. 7a) by binning SSH in 0.02 m intervals and associating each bin with a vertical density profile. The GEM provides a SSH-σ0 relationship that is used to reconstruct a synthetic 3D density field along the section for each model snapshot. The three-dimensional geostrophic velocity field is then calculated from the thermal-wind relationship. Finally, we solve the QG-omega equation forced by the reconstructed buoyancy and geostrophic shear to infer vertical velocity, w, as described in §2.3. This replicates the procedure, based on the glider sampling, to produce a full 3D w field within a SWOT swath. We repeat this process independently for each day from 2023-11-20 to 2023-12-20, and then quantify the skill of the density and w estimates against the 3D model fields (Fig. 7b, c).

The GEM-based reconstruction recovers the large-scale cross-frontal and eddy patterns, with noticeable misfits in small-scale features and in the upper layer (Fig. 7d, e). The density error profile (Fig. 7b) shows minimal mismatch in the interior and a clear increase in the upper ~ 100–200 m, which reflects a limitation of the SSH-σ0 relationship in the mixed-layer (see also Supplementary Fig. 7). Here, strong ageostrophic variability decorrelates surface density from SSH7. Reconstructed vertical velocities generally show clear phase and amplitude coherence with model fields (Fig. 7f, g). The depth-dependent RMS of w (Fig. 7c) indicates that the QG-omega reconstruction recovers most of the sub-inertial w variance, most effectively in the interior, with a maximum around ~ 600 m, consistent with vertical variability in w obtained from the glider reconstruction (Fig. 5e). These model experiments, similar to the results in Liu et al. (2019)52 and Liu et al. (2021)48, support the applicability of the GEM/thermal-wind/QG-omega approach to glider transects and SWOT snapshots to reconstruct realistic 3D density fields and vertical velocities in the ocean interior.

Discussion

The inferred vertical velocities arising from the QG-omega solutions suggest a spatial partitioning of upward and downward motions when w is large (Fig. 6a). The statistical distribution of w should be viewed with caution due to the regional and short-term analysis of this study. Nevertheless, the patterns largely agree with the dynamical understanding of vertical velocities localized to fronts and regions of strong strain. Previous studies have suggested that downward motions occur in more compact regions and are more intense than upward motions53,54. We do not observe a skewness in vertical motions. While many studies have shown that relative vorticity is characterized by a strong positive skewness ( ~ 1.5 − 2), divergence is characterized by a much smaller skewness ( ~ 0.1 − 0.4). This difference occurs because there is only weak radial strain around axisymmetric eddies, which minimizes frontogenesis and the associated divergence55,56. The numerical simulation used in this study reveals a clear positive skewness in vorticity (cyclonic dominance, skew = 0.4), while the horizontal divergence remains only weakly skewed (skew = -0.15) (Supplementary Fig. 8). The SWOT-derived downward motions predominantly occur in regions of high strain (α/f > 0.5; eddy edges) as well as in areas of strong cyclonic vorticity. Upward motions are localized to regions of weaker strain and anti-cyclonic vorticity (eddy centers) (Fig. 6). An anti-correlation also exists between relative vorticity ζ and w for weak strain values, which is broadly in agreement with restratification of a front that involves upward motions on the buoyant (anti-cyclonic) side of the front and downward motions on the dense (cyclonic) side of the front. For flows with Ro ~ 1, previous studies have shown that ζ is skewed with stronger cyclonic motions51,57 and amplified downward vertical velocities50. Averaging over a longer time period may be required to produce the expected positive vorticity skewness57.

Vertical velocity distributions inferred from SWOT observations in this study have important differences from prior w estimates using eSQG approaches24,58. First, the magnitude of w diagnosed from the QG-omega equation commonly exceeds 100 m day−1 (Fig. 5a), especially at the edges of mesoscale eddies and at strong frontal currents, such as the PF. Second, spatial variations in w have a smaller horizontal scale using QG-omega as compared to eSQG. The eSQG approach typically produces strong vertical velocities that are correlated with the core of mesoscale eddies24, while the QG-omega approach produces narrow bands of elevated w that coincide with strong, lateral interior density gradients. Thus, QG-omega suggests that vertical exchange below the base of the mixed layer is localized to strong frontal features at the periphery of mesoscale eddies. This spatial relationship between vertical velocities and the mesoscale density structure is consistent with previous numerical and observational studies11,31,32,59–61. A unique feature of using SWOT is that it becomes possible to provide a three-dimensional and temporally evolving perspective of w distributions. The extension of the QG-omega approach outside of the FOCUS region will help to validate this subsurface behavior over broader spatial scales and in different dynamical regimes.

The QG-omega solutions indicate that elevated vertical velocities below the mixed layer are common in the Southern Ocean17,62. Surface (horizontal) velocity observations from drifters42 and density observations from gliders reveal that lateral velocity shear and deep-reaching lateral density gradients occur frequently at scales comparable to or smaller than the deformation radius. While the ocean mesoscale gives structure to the vertical velocity field, cores of enhanced w occur over scales smaller than the mesoscale features, i.e., at submesoscales. One limitation of the QG-omega approach, related to QG assumptions, is its ability to represent near-surface vertical velocities. The SSH-σ0 relationship (Fig. 3b) does not capture mixed layer density variations that arise from surface buoyancy fluxes and mixing, rather than from interior geostrophic flows. Future work is needed to understand how vertical motions that are adequately captured by QG theory interface with surface dynamics, such as mixed layer overturning circulations63, to determine vertical transport properties.

A natural extension of this work would aim to estimate vertical heat and other tracer fluxes. Siegelman et al. (2020)17 produced heat flux estimates by combining inferred w values with temperature observations from an instrumented seal. Using SWOT, w estimates can be extended to regions not sampled by the gliders. Thus, a heat flux estimate requires cloud-free conditions or the development of a relationship between sea surface temperature (SST) and SSH29,30. The latter is likely complicated at these regional scales because the glider samples both temperature- and salinity-stratified regions of the ACC. Vertical velocities could be combined with SST or ocean color data to estimate heat and carbon transport, respectively, assuming sufficient concurrent observations; this is the focus of future work. More subtly, w estimated from QG-omega implies that strong vertical transport is located at eddy edges and fronts where temperature and biogeochemical gradients are typically strong64,65. Accurate representation of tracer fluxes will require careful validation of w and tracer anomaly correlations at eddy edges, and is beyond the scope of the current study.

The dense hydrographic sampling by the gliders, and their coverage of a wide range of absolute dynamic topography values, is essential for the SSH-σ relationship that allows reconstruction of the interior density field (Supplementary Fig. 7). Extension of this approach to other regions, or the entire ACC, will require an understanding of regional, seasonal and inter-annual variations in the SSH-σ0 relationship. There are a few locations in the Southern Ocean where persistent monitoring may enable this approach. Long-term XBT-XCTD sampling in Drake Passage66, south of Tasmania67, and elsewhere span multiple decades with surveys every few months. While the salinity sampling is sparse in space, the sampling covers a broad range of SSH regimes in a relatively small geographic region; the GEM method was also extensively tested in Drake Passage68. Long-term instrumentation of elephant seals on Kerguelen and South Georgia islands is another opportunity to build small-scale, seasonally varying SSH-σ relationships to explore variability in Southern Ocean vertical velocities69. Ultimately, observations collected from the Argo float array will provide useful input because of their broad spatial coverage. These relationships can also be interrogated in high-resolution numerical models and is the focus of ongoing work that extends the results in §2.4.

Conclusions

This study offers a novel approach to estimate upper-ocean vertical velocities that leverages the substantial increase in spatial resolution of SSH field provided from the SWOT altimetry mission70,71. SSH observations are combined with dense information about the ocean’s stratification collected from ocean gliders to provide w fields with enhanced spatial resolution and spatial coverage. The inferred vertical velocities are deep-reaching features confined to narrow frontal regions associated with high vorticity and strain. These results largely agree with recent high-resolution numerical studies50,72, but suggest that in energetic, weakly stratified waters, w reaches a few hundred m day−1 at depths of 1000 m. This study also indicates that these vertical velocities are statistically robust and are expected to occur frequently in energetic regions of the Southern Ocean. While additional work is needed to test the reliability of this approach in various dynamical regimes, the spatial coverage of SWOT offers an opportunity to explore broad patterns and statistics of vertical velocities and, through the combination of other remotely-sense surface fields, tracer transport properties.

Methods

SWOT altimetry data

The SWOT altimetry mission was launched in December 2022 and has subsequently provided high-resolution, two-dimensional snapshots of SSH variations over the global ocean with a 21-day exact repeat period. SWOT applies a synthetic aperture radar interferometry (InSAR) approach73,74 by flying two SAR antennas separated by a 10 m mast on a single satellite platform. SWOT is a joint mission of NASA and CNES. The radar system in SWOT, called the Ka–band radar interferometer (or KaRIn), makes measurements over a swath of 120 km with a 20 km nadir gap that is sampled with coarse resolution along the centerline by a conventional altimeter. The resolved wavelength of SSH variations is roughly 5 km from SWOT, an order of magnitude larger than the 50 km resolved by conventional altimetry. Understanding the link between SSH variations measured by SWOT and surface (balanced) motions is a topic of ongoing study42,75. This study is based on version V1.0.2 of the SWOT L3 standard product76. We estimate cyclogeostrophic surface currents directly from the original SWOT SSH by applying a local 2D polynomial fit on a 13-point stencil at each 2 km pixel42.

DUACS altimetry data

Daily estimates of SSH relative to the geoid for the duration of the FOCUS field program were obtained from the L4-gridded satellite altimetry product provided by Copernicus Marine Services. The data product includes absolute dynamic topography (ADT), sea level anomaly (SLA), total geostrophic velocity, and eddy geostrophic velocity. The latter two fields are associated with the gradients of ADT and SLA fields, respectively.

Measurement noise in radar measurements limits the along-track spatial resolution to roughly 50 km77. To provide two-dimensional coverage, along-track measurements from several altimeters are combined to produce a gridded product. While these data products are provided on a 25 km spatial grid and at daily temporal sampling, the dynamically resolved scales are considerably coarser, roughly 200 km in wavelength and multiple-week period78.

Ocean glider data

Two Seagliders (SG659, SG681) were deployed in early December 2023 and sampled the region surrounding the PF downstream of the Southeast Indian Ridge. The gliders were initially deployed south of the PF and followed the trajectories of the floats and drifters within the FOCUS study region (Fig. 1). In early January 2024, both gliders moved northward and spent multiple weeks sampling a large cyclonic mesoscale eddy that detached from the PF (Fig. 1). The gliders profiled in a V-shaped vertical pattern to a maximum depth of 1000 m, collecting temperature, salinity, and dissolved oxygen concentration data using a SeaBird unpumped CT-Sail and an Aanderaa optode. The typical speed across ground that these gliders can achieve is roughly 25 cm s−1 ( ~ 20 km day−1). In this region, surface currents can often exceed 1 m s−1 with an equivalent barotropic structure that supports strong currents over the upper 1000 m. Therefore, the gliders were piloted with limited ability to sample perpendicular to strong frontal currents. This further motivates the exploration of leveraging relationships between SSH and interior hydrography.

The raw glider data were processed using the GliderTools toolbox79. The data were mapped onto a regular grid with 5 m vertical spacing and 2.5 km horizontal spacing. However, when linking glider profiles to SSH observations, downcast and upcast periods were treated as separate profiles. SSH locations are interpolated to the central position of each cast, assuming the glider completes a linear V pattern over the dive cycle. Errors associated with the uncertainty in the true subsurface glider position O(100) m are small compared to the uncertainty associated with the use of DUACS gridded SSH data products, which involve interpolating along-track SSH observations over much larger spatial and temporal scales. Density fields were generated using the Gibbs Seawater Toolbox80 and the EOS-80 neutral density package81; for the relationship between SSH and density, we choose to use potential density, referenced to the surface.

While this uncertainty will propagate into estimates of density gradients, velocity gradients, and vertical velocity, it is not sufficient to qualitatively change the magnitudes and statistics of the vertical velocity estimates.

QG-omega equation

In addition to using SSH to infer the interior density field, SSH data can be used to infer the interior velocity field given a surface velocity distribution and given the assumption that vertical velocity variations are dominated by the thermal wind balance. The physics associated with the QG omega equation (Eq. (1)) relates thermal wind balance to vertical velocities. The geostrophic strain field (ux, vx, uy, vy) increases the buoyancy gradients (bx, by), steepening isopycnals and destroying the thermal wind balance. To restore this balance, a secondary circulation, and in particular a vertical velocity field wQG, develops, which reduces buoyancy gradients by flattening isopycnals.

The narrow swath width of the SWOT altimeter presents a challenge for the determination of vertical velocities. Here, the left and right swaths are separately tiled to create a doubly-periodic domain. As each swath is generally narrower than the horizontal scale of a mesoscale eddy (Fig. 1d, e), tiling can produce unrealistic velocity and stratification configurations at the swath boundaries. While the diagnosed structure of w near the swath boundary may be unrealistic, the amplitude and relationship with eddy and frontal features should be similar. Preliminary efforts to use spline interpolation to “fill in” the nadir gap did not qualitatively impact the statistics of w. More sophisticated approaches to interpolate over the nadir gap, such as the use of covariance functions75, should be explored in future studies. The appropriate application of boundary conditions will also be critical for estimating vertical fluxes.

Numerical model

The model is a regional implementation of COMPAS83 with a horizontal grid spacing of 3 km, the parent grid of a nested simulation cascade currently being developed as part of the SWOT validation framework and to support the FOCUS experiment. The model covers the ACC meander south of Tasmania, extending longitudinally from 150 Â to 160 Â and latitudinally from 58 Â to 52 Â, including both the upstream and downstream regions beyond the Macquarie Ridge. It is forced at surface with atmospheric forcing from ERA5 reanalysis, including 10 m winds, atmospheric pressure, air temperature, net shortwave and longwave radiation, and latent and sensible heat fluxes. Wind stress is computed from wind velocities relative to the surface currents using Large and Pond (1981) bulk formula. Ocean forcing at lateral boundaries are derived from the BRAN2023 reanalysis84. Tidal forcing includes eight constituents (M2, S2, N2, K2, K1, O1, P1, Q1) from the TPXO global tide model85. Bathymetry is based on GEBCO2023, supplemented by high-resolution multibeam data from the FOCUS voyage.

Inertial-gravity waves, including both tidal and inertial oscillations, are effectively filtered out by daily-averaging model fields. Since SWOT observations are snapshots and cannot be temporally averaged in the same way, we reconstruct vertical velocities on individual model snapshots and compare the result to the filtered vertical velocities. In order to remove barotropic contributions to SSH that are corrected for in SWOT outputs (i.e. tide and DAC), we compute the model SSH as the steric height relative to 3000 m. In the region, the 3000 m reference level captures most of the steric contribution reflected in SSH.
