Respiratory management in neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy during therapeutic hypothermia: a binational analysis of current practice
Authors: Julien Gotchac, Elvina De Witte, Yaniss Belaroussi, Julie Guichoux, Thomas Dienst, Olivier Brissaud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe respiratory management of neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) undergoing therapeutic hypothermia (TH) is a challenging issue, but remains poorly standardized. The aim of this study was to describe current practice regarding respiratory management, particularly tracheal intubation, in neonates undergoing TH in France and French-speaking Belgium. In this binational cross-sectional study, current practices about respiratory management were collected by an online-based survey sent to all tertiary neonatal intensive care units providing TH in France and French-speaking Belgium. Practices were analyzed to assess their heterogeneity and compared between centers that routinely intubate for TH and those that did not. The survey was completed by 57/68 (84%) centers. Routine intubation during TH was reported by 33 (58%) centers. Motivations behind routine intubation during TH included concerns about pain, discomfort, and neuroprotection. Among the 24 other centers, there was significant heterogeneity in the respiratory care of patients, while practices regarding sedation-analgesia appeared more consensual. The country was the only characteristic independently associated with routine intubation during TH (France vs Belgium: OR = 9.333, 95% CI 1.035–84.202, p = 0.047).ConclusionRespiratory management of neonates with HIE undergoing TH is highly heterogeneous. Most centers routinely intubate neonates undergoing TH, especially in France. These findings highlight the need for more evidence-based studies and guidelines to optimize respiratory care during TH. Introduction

Therapeutic hypothermia (TH) is the standard of care for neonates with moderate to severe hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), improving survival and neurological outcome [1, 2]. While the respiratory management of neonates undergoing TH is difficult because of the effects on the pulmonary function of both hypoxia-ischemia and TH [3, 4], it is a crucial aspect of the neuroprotective strategy, as hyperoxia and hypocapnia are associated with poor outcomes [4–8]. However, the optimal respiratory management during TH remains uncertain.

The guidelines of the French Society of Neonatology do not recommend systematic intubation [9, 10], but specify that most neonates will require mechanical ventilation, and that sedation-analgesia is recommended even if it leads to intubation [10]. Indeed, pain and stress caused by shivering and cold sensation may interfere with the neuroprotective effect of TH [11], and sedation-analgesia itself has a significant neuroprotective effect, even in neonates with severe HIE [12]. Moreover, as cerebral blood flow is partially regulated by the partial pressure of carbon dioxide, brain injuries might be attenuated by complete control over ventilation [13]. On the other hand, the multiple adverse effects of mechanical ventilation are well recognized. Therefore, the balance between the benefits and the risks of tracheal intubation during TH is a complex and unresolved issue.

Understanding current clinical practices is essential before developing and evaluating respiratory care bundles. The aim of this study was to describe the respiratory management of neonates with HIE receiving TH in neonatal intensive care units (NICU) in France and French-speaking Belgium, with a specific focus on the practice of routine tracheal intubation and its associated factors. We hypothesize that there is significant heterogeneity of practices on this issue.

Materials and methods

Data collection

In this cross-sectional multicenter descriptive observational study, data regarding the respiratory management of neonates with HIE undergoing TH in France and French-speaking Belgium were collected using an online-based survey.

The questionnaire was developed by the authors after a literature review, and revised after feedback from two independent NICU physicians. The final version of the questionnaire included 11 to 26 questions, divided into two parts. The first part included seven questions focusing on the respondent’s personal and professional characteristics, as well as local epidemiological data. The last question of this part was about routine intubation of neonates receiving TH. The answer to this last item determined the subsequent questions. For NICU reporting the use of TH in non-intubated neonates, the second part of the survey consisted of 17 to 19 questions about ventilatory support and sedation-analgesia. For NICU reporting routine intubation, the second part of the survey included four questions exploring the motivations behind the routine intubation of neonates under TH. Multiple-choice questions were favored, but respondents could insert a free-text comment for each item. Response time ranged from 10 to 15 min, and no formulation or semantic problems were identified.

The questionnaire was released by e-mail in January 2022 after the identification of one corresponding physician for each of the 68 tertiary NICUs in France and French-speaking Belgium that provided TH for neonates with HIE. All correspondents were permanent physicians, designated by the head physician of their unit. They were asked to answer the survey based on institutional practice during the past 12 months, rather than on their own individual beliefs, in patients with an indication for TH according to current guidelines [9, 10]. The online survey remained open for a 10-month period during which four reminders were sent by email in order to maximize the response rate.

Data analysis

Results are presented on tables, distinguishing answers between NICU that does and does not routinely intubate neonates receiving TH. Quantitative variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when distribution was normal or median with interquartile ranges (IQR) otherwise. Qualitative variables were described as frequencies and proportion. Ethical considerations

In accordance with the current French legislation, the approval of an ethics committee was not required as this research does not involve patients and as the survey was voluntarily completed.

Results

The survey was completed by 57/68 NICU (84% response rate). The reasons for not including the other NICU were the absence of response from the head physician (ten centers) and refusal to participate (one center).

Neonates with HIE were routinely intubated for TH in 33/57 (58%) NICUs. Discussion

This study reports that most NICUs in France and French-speaking Belgium routinely intubate neonates with HIE undergoing TH. Even in centers that do not routinely intubate, TH without intubation is uncommon: few neonates start TH non-intubated, and most of them will eventually be intubated during the course of TH. In addition, extubation during the course of TH seems rare, and unplanned extubation often leads to reintubation, even in clinically stable and comfortable neonates.

Several studies focusing on the respiratory management of neonates with HIE under TH found a statistical association between mechanical ventilation and adverse outcomes, but intubated newborns also suffered from significantly more severe HIE [5, 7]. On the one hand, intubation enables deep sedation and thus complete control over ventilation and gas exchanges (especially carbon dioxide), a prevention against intracranial pressure rises induced by nociceptive stimuli, and a reduction in cerebral oxygen demand. Moreover, sedation and analgesia can provide specific neuroprotective effects [11–13]. On the other hand, these agents could be harmful to the developing brain as demonstrated in preterm infants [14], and intubation exposes neonates to complications such as ventilator-induced lung injury or altered cerebral hemodynamics. Moreover, laryngoscopy itself can expose them to significant intracranial pressure rises [15]. Overall, the benefit-risk ratio of mechanical ventilation remains uncertain in this situation. Its neuroprotective effect could probably be beneficial in some cases, whereas a lighter sedation preserving spontaneous breathing might be sufficient in other cases.

The intubation rate in our study appears relatively high in comparison with the existing literature. In a similar survey conducted in 2018 in the UK, only 4/27 NICU reported routine intubation during TH, regardless of their respiratory status [16]. However, the many clinical trials and retrospective studies in which respiratory care was left to the clinician’s own judgment report intubation rates ranging from 71% for the most recent [5, 7] to almost 100% for the earliest [17, 18]. The intubation rate was 65% in the Vermont Oxford Neonatal Encephalopathy Registry, which enrolled 4232 patients in 95 centers between 2006 and 2010. However, this registry was not limited to moderate-to-severe HIE, as it included patients with evidence of all-cause neonatal encephalopathy within the first 3 days of life (including a 5-min Apgar score of 3 or less), and only 38% of the 4232 patients actually received TH [19]. A recent retrospective Italian study found that only 40% of patients were intubated during TH and reported comparable short-term neurological outcomes in ventilated and non-ventilated infants, despite more severe conditions at birth in the ventilated group [20]. Interestingly, intubation criteria were explicit in this study: excessive respiratory effort (Silverman score > 6) or absent/irregular spontaneous breathing, a fraction of inspired oxygen ≥ 35%, hypercapnia > 60 mmHg (but not hypocapnia), apneic crisis, or progressive respiratory acidosis. Two studies have also addressed the question of extubation during TH. A recent Canadian survey found that 71% of the 24 centers included attempted to extubate the neonates as soon as possible, and that 54% of centers do not keep the neonates intubated during TH [21]. In a retrospective Canadian cohort of 198 neonates, half of them were extubated during the first 4 days of life, mostly on day 1 or 2, and hypocapnia despite minimal ventilation settings was often mentioned as the reason for extubation. Interestingly, 12% of the extubated newborns required reintubation [7]. In the last decade, TH has also been used for neonates with mild HIE [22]. The ventilatory management of these patients seems to be different. In a national survey of practice in the UK, 75% of the centers cooled babies with mild HIE, and only two units (6%) ventilated all patients undergoing TH [23]. In a Canadian retrospective study including 1089 neonates with mild HIE, 36% received TH and among them, 50% were mechanically ventilated versus 29% in the standard care group [24]. In another study in the US, only 34% of mild HIE included were intubated [25].

In our study, neither NICU size nor its affiliation to a university hospital was associated with routine intubation for neonates undergoing TH. The country of origin was the only NICU characteristic significantly associated with this type of management, which seems to be more frequent in France than in French-speaking Belgium. Among NICUs that do not routinely intubate, the proportion of patients starting TH under invasive ventilation was higher in French than in Belgian centers. Moreover, the proportion of neonates that are intubated during the course of TH seems to be higher in French centers. These differences could be explained by the more frequent existence of a local protocol for the respiratory care of neonates undergoing TH in Belgium, and by the absence of official Belgian guidelines, whereas French guidelines specify that most neonates will require mechanical ventilation and that sedation-analgesia is recommended even if it leads to intubation [9, 10].

This study reveals significant heterogeneity in the respiratory management of neonates receiving TH for HIE in France and French-speaking Belgium. In NICUs using TH in non-intubated neonates, only 3/10 questions about respiratory care received a strong agreement (i.e., ≥ 75% of centers indicating the same answer). The first one was the routine intubation of severe HIE, as previously described in the literature [5, 7, 20]. This approach might be motivated by the pursuit of optimal neuroprotection for the most severe patients, but also by the respiratory consequences of HIE itself, which are proportional to its severity [3, 4]. The second one was the non-systematic use of non-invasive ventilation in non-intubated neonates. This probably underlines that respiratory care is often managed independently of the neurological status, although 50% of the respondents stated that the respiratory management of neonates undergoing TH is different from that of other neonates. The third one was that TH discontinuation is not more frequently reported in non-intubated neonates. This highlights the feasibility of this approach. Analgesia and sedation practices appeared more consensual. In most centers, all non-intubated newborns systematically received sedation-analgesia, as recommended by the French guidelines [10]. Moreover, morphine is the preferred treatment, as previously reported worldwide [16, 21–23]. However, NICUs were more divergent regarding the benefit/risk balance between moderate discomfort and tracheal intubation.

This study presents several limitations. First, there might have been a participation bias, as the NICU most concerned and most comfortable with this topic would be more likely to respond to the survey. However, the relatively high participation rate reduces the impact of this bias. Only one physician per center completed the survey, probably the one with the most knowledge in the field. However, this does not affect the conclusions of the study, since the responses were heterogeneous even among these potentially expert physicians. Nonetheless, one respondent per NICU may not capture intra-center variability. Second, the nature of the study induces a reporting bias, since the survey may reflect idealized practices rather than real-life respiratory management. However, this would not have altered the conclusions of this study, as routine intubation for neonates receiving TH is not recommended by the guidelines. Third, the survey did not include specific information about ventilatory management, such as carbon dioxide monitoring or ventilator settings. Although it would have been interesting, we chose to focus on the issue of routine intubation in order to shorten the survey and thus maximize the participation rate. Finally, this cross-sectional study provides a point-in-time picture of current practices and was not designed to assess the efficacy of those practices. Although of interest, this was outside the scope of this study.

Conclusion

Most centers surveyed in France and French-speaking Belgium routinely intubate neonates with HIE undergoing TH, especially in France. Respiratory management seems highly variable in NICUs that do not routinely intubate for TH. Our findings underline the absence of consensus regarding this topic, reflecting a lack of clear, evidence-based guidelines. Before considering a randomized trial, further studies comparing different ventilation strategies during TH in neonates with HIE of comparable severity are warranted.
