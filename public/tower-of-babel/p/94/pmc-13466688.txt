Fine-Needle Aspiration Cytology of Hepatic Masses: A Retrospective Observational Study and Assessment of Risk of Malignancy Based on the Proposed World Health Organization Reporting System for Liver Cytology
Authors: Neha Nigam, Neha Kumari, Jyoty Singh, Anil Singh, Nandita Chaudhary, Gaurav Pandey, Ram N Rao, Vinita Agrawal, Prabhakar Mishra, Nirbhay Kumar, Amit Rastogi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Hepatic masses present a diagnostic challenge due to their variable etiology and overlapping imaging features. While radiology plays a pivotal role in lesion detection and characterization, fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is a rapid and minimally invasive method. This study reclassifies liver FNAC cases using the World Health Organization (WHO) Reporting System for Liver Cytology and assesses the diagnostic performance and risk of malignancy (ROM) for each category.Materials and Methods:This retrospective study analyzed 1741 ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspirations performed on hepatic lesions over 8 years at a tertiary care center. Cytological specimens were reclassified into WHO categories: nondiagnostic, benign, atypical, suspicious for malignancy, and malignant. Histopathology follow-up served as the reference standard. Diagnostic metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and overall accuracy, were calculated. ROM was determined for each cytological category.Results:Of the 1741 FNAC cases, 1660 yielded adequate material. Malignant lesions constituted the majority (n = 1254; 72.0%), with metastatic adenocarcinoma being most prevalent. The sensitivity and specificity of FNAC for detecting malignancy were 98.46% and 92.64%, respectively, with a diagnostic accuracy of 97.04%. The ROM for WHO categories ranged from 4.9% in the benign group to 100% in the malignant group.Conclusion:FNAC, particularly when classified according to the WHO system, demonstrates high diagnostic accuracy and predictive reliability for hepatic space-occupying lesions. Standardized application of the WHO reporting framework enables objective ROM stratification and facilitates effective clinical decision-making. FNAC remains a vital diagnostic tool in the evaluation of hepatic masses, especially in resource-limited or time-sensitive settings.

INTRODUCTION

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is an effective method for evaluating hepatic masses due to its simplicity, minimal invasiveness, low cost, and rapid turnaround time. Cytological samples collected by FNAC can be quickly assessed for malignancy, facilitating informed clinical decisions and effective treatment planning.[1] However, the diagnostic accuracy of FNAC for hepatic lesions, in terms of its sensitivity and specificity, remains an ongoing area of discussion and investigation.[2,3,4,5,6,7,8]

The World Health Organization (WHO) has proposed a standardized liver cytology reporting system to ensure consistency and accuracy in FNAC interpretations. The system categorizes liver cytology results in a structured manner and promises to improve reproducibility and clinical utility in differentiating benign, atypical, and malignant lesions. As a result, it may facilitate better interobserver agreement and provide a more reliable way of stratifying malignancy risk.[3]

In this study, we analyze the sensitivity and specificity of ultrasound (US)-guided FNAC of hepatic lesions, as well as its diagnostic contribution to the clinical diagnosis of malignant liver tumors. Only a few studies have examined the risk of malignancy (ROM) associated with FNAC of the liver.[4] In addition, we have classified liver cytology according to the proposed WHO reporting system,[9] which categorizes lesions into five diagnostic categories: nondiagnostic/unsatisfactory (ND), benign, atypical, suspicious for malignancy, and malignant, and assessed the ROM within each cytological category using histopathology findings with or without immunohistochemistry.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and case selection

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Sanjay Gandhi Postgraduate Institute of Medical Sciences, Lucknow, within the Department of Pathology, in collaboration with the Departments of Radiology, Gastroenterology, and Hepatology. The study spanned 8 years, from January 2017 to December 2024. During this period, out of 75,400 total cytology specimens, 23,040 were fine-needle aspiration (FNA) specimens from various lesions. FNAC obtained from the liver using US or computed tomography (CT) guidance included 1741 patients with clinically and radiologically suspected liver masses. For each patient, demographic details, clinical data, laboratory results, cytology, histopathology, and imaging reports were retrieved. Imaging reports, including sonography, CT, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), were reviewed to assess the location, size, and characteristics of the hepatic lesions. Fine-needle aspiration cytology procedure

Informed consent was obtained from all participants before the procedure. US-guided FNAC was performed by an experienced radiologist using a 22- to 25-G needle, with strict adherence to aseptic precautions. At least two passes were made to obtain an adequate sample. A pathologist confirmed on-site material adequacy, and both air-dried and alcohol-fixed smears were prepared. For Papanicolaou and hematoxylin and eosin staining, slides were fixed in 95% alcohol, while air-dried smears were subjected to May–Grünwald–Giemsa staining. Any remaining material, if available and required, was processed for cell block preparation.

Cytological evaluation and World Health Organization reporting system

Cytology smears were evaluated by two independent cytopathologists (NN and JS) who were blinded to the histopathological outcome. All cases were reclassified according to the proposed WHO Reporting System for Liver Cytology (2022), which categorizes lesions into five diagnostic categories.[9]

Histopathological correlation

Histopathological examination of cell block, core needle biopsy, or surgical resection specimens, with or without immunohistochemistry, was used to establish the final diagnosis. The ROM indicating the likelihood of malignancy for each cytological category, along with the cytology sensitivity and specificity for malignancy, was then calculated based on histopathological (cell block/biopsy/resection specimen) follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Quantitative variables are presented as means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%). Categorical variables were compared between groups using the appropriate chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. The results of US-guided FNA from hepatic space-occupying lesions were compared with the final cytological and histopathological diagnoses to calculate the accuracy of FNAC, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and diagnostic precision. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between variables. Significance was defined at a P value of <0.05.

RESULTS

We analyzed 1741 FNAC specimens of hepatic lesions, yielding significant insights into patient demographics, lesion characteristics, and diagnostic performance metrics.

Patient demographics and sample characteristics

Out of a total of 23,040 US-FNA procedures done, 1741 were US-FNA of a hepatic mass lesion. The mean age of patients was 51.9 ± 14.01 years. Of these cases, 930 were male, and 811 were female, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 1.1:1.

Clinical and radiological characteristics of the hepatic lesions

Radiological details (USG, CT, and MRI) were available for all the cases included in the study. The size of the hepatic lesion varied from 0.2 to 21.1 cm (mean size 3.31 ± 2.47 cm). The majority of lesions were multicentric (n = 1125; 64.6%) and involved the right lobe. On radiology, 294 (16.88%) cases were benign, 1425 (81.84%) were malignant, and the diagnosis was indeterminate in 22 (1.26%) cases. Cytological characteristics of the hepatic lesions

Cytological classification based on the World Health Organization reporting system

FNAC was successfully performed in 1660 cases, with an inadequate sample obtained in 81 cases (4.65%). The categorization of hepatic masses by FNAC was performed according to the WHO reporting system. Histopathological (biopsy, cell block, or surgical specimen) work-up was available in 980 patients. ROM across the WHO categories, ND, benign, atypical, suspicious for malignancy, and malignant, was 6.1%, 4.9%, 40.7%, 96%, and 100%, respectively. Details are given in Table 1.

Cytological diagnostic spectrum of liver lesions

The most common cytomorphological diagnosis was suspicious or definite malignancy (n = 1254), of which metastatic malignancies (n = 1132; 90.3%) were the most prevalent. Diagnoses included adenocarcinoma (n = 856; 75.6%), followed by ductal carcinoma of the breast (n = 68; 6.0%), cholangiocarcinoma (n = 54; 4.8%), and neuroendocrine tumor (n = 53; 4.7%). The common primary sites metastasizing to the liver were from gallbladder (n = 460; 40.6%), pancreas (n = 129; 11.4%), and breast (n = 68; 6.0%). Primary malignant hepatic tumors (n = 122; 9.7%) included HCC (n = 106; 86.9%), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (n = 13; 10.6%), hepatoblastoma (n = 2; 1.7%), and angiosarcoma (n = 1; 0.8%). The most common benign (n = 372) pathology was inflammatory lesion (n = 258; 69.3%) followed by simple cyst (n = 39; 10.5%), hemangioma (n = 24; 6.5%), hydatid cyst (n = 14; 3.8%), cirrhosis (n = 13; 3.5%), biliary cystadenoma (n = 4; 1.1%), and focal nodular hyperplasia (n = 3; 0.8%). Inflammatory lesions comprised most commonly of abscess (n = 178) and granulomatous pathology (n = 80). WHO diagnostic categories: “atypical” included 34 cases, whereas “suspicious for malignancy” included 39 cases. Histopathological correlation with cytology

Histopathological correlation was available for 947 cases. Inadequate cytology cases were excluded from the diagnostic accuracy analysis.

For the WHO cytological category “benign,” cytohistological correlation was available for 225/372 cases, of which 11 were diagnosed as malignant and the remaining cases as benign on histopathology. The “atypical” category included 34 cases, of which histological correlation was available for 27. Of these, 16 (n = 16/27; 59.3%) were benign, including inflammatory pathology (15), hepatoma (1), and cirrhosis (1). Eleven (n = 11/27; 40.7%) cases were malignant, including adenocarcinoma (7), non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (3), and HCC (1). The WHO category “suspicious for malignancy” included 39 cases, of which histological correlation was available for 28 cases. All came out malignant on histological correlation except one, which showed benign histomorphology of chronic hepatitis C. All cases in the cytology category “malignant” were also diagnosed as malignant on histology.

Diagnostic performance of radiology and cytology

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of radiology to diagnose hepatic lesions when compared to histological examination were 95.65%, confidence interval (CI) (93.88%–97.02%), 64.31%, CI (58.10%–70.19%), 88.20%, CI (86.36%–89.81%), and 84.12%, CI (78.77%–88.33%), respectively. Diagnostic accuracy was 87.37%.

The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of US-FNAC cytology in diagnosing hepatic lesions were 98.46%, CI (97.27%–99.23%), 92.64%, CI (88.48%–95.65%), 97.64%, CI (96.33%–98.50%), and 95.11%, CI (91.54%–97.22%), respectively. DISCUSSION

We applied the proposed WHO 2022 Reporting System for Liver Cytology to classify hepatic FNAC samples, evaluated the diagnostic efficacy of FNAC compared to other modalities, including radiology and histopathology, and analyzed ROM across the WHO diagnostic categories.[9]

The majority of hepatic tumors involved the right lobe (~64%), similar to Gatphoh et al.’s report that nearly half involved the right lobe alone.[10] On cytology, metastatic adenocarcinoma was the most frequently diagnosed malignant lesion, followed by HCC. The findings of Barbhuiya et al.[11] are in line with those of previous studies, which also reported a predominance of metastasis over primary liver cancer.

In the WHO Reporting System for Liver Cytology, FNAC samples are assigned one of the five diagnostic categories, enabling standardized communication and risk assessment of malignancy. It is essential to determine the ROM across various cytological categories, as they warrant different clinical management protocols. There is relatively little data on ROM in hepatic cytopathology. Gonzales et al.[12] reported that the ROM was 100% in categories positive for malignancy and suspicious for malignancy. It was 66.6% in the atypical category, 17.6% in the negative-for-malignant category, and 13.2% in the ND category. These results are in line with our study results. In this study, the ROM for the “benign category” was 4.9%, the “malignant” category was 100%, and the “suspicious for malignancy” category was 96%, demonstrating high predictive value. The “atypical” category showed a ROM of 40.7%. The value of ROM in our study aligns with the WHO classification system for liver cytology’s definition of ROM. To establish a more evidence-based liver cytopathology reporting system, it is essential to conduct more large-scale studies and trials. The low inadequacy rate (4.65%) in our study reflects optimal sampling facilitated by image guidance.

We found that FNAC had a sensitivity of 98.46% and a specificity of 92.64%, outperforming radiological evaluation with a sensitivity of 95.65% but a significantly lower specificity of 64.31%. In line with previous literature, FNAC has proven reliable in diagnosing hepatic malignancies and has high diagnostic accuracy when interpreted by experienced teams.[6,7] Our results indicate that the sensitivity of guided FNAC for diagnosing hepatic malignancies (98.64%) is higher than that of radiological investigations. Compared with histopathological diagnosis, Nakabori et al.[3] found that conventional cytology was 97.6% sensitive in diagnosing hepatic malignancy. Other studies have found a variable sensitivity for the diagnosis of hepatic malignancies, ranging from 70% to 93%.[3,4,5]

FNAC has demonstrated high specificity; however, false negatives remain a concern, particularly when distinguishing between poorly differentiated HCC and metastatic cancer or well-differentiated HCC and benign lesions.[8] In liver masses, false positives can be caused by overlapping features of benign and malignant conditions, such as reactive hepatocellular changes, inflammation, and regenerative nodules that mimic malignancy. Sample artifacts, blood contamination, necrosis, and poor specimen preservation can also lead to misinterpretations. The presence of cellular atypia secondary to inflammation or regenerative activity may also be mistaken for malignant characteristics.

There were 12 cases with cytological and histological discordance. Eleven malignant cases were misclassified as benign on cytology in our series. These errors are frequently attributed to sampling limitations, tumor heterogeneity, and overlapping features between benign hepatocellular lesions and HCCs.[9,13] One malignant case on cytology showed benign histomorphology of chronic hepatitis C. In ambiguous cases, ancillary techniques such as cell block preparation, immunocytochemistry, and molecular studies may enhance diagnostic precision.[11,14]

In this study, the specificity was 92.6% for hepatic malignancy, with a range of 69%–100%, as reported in various studies. In time-sensitive clinical situations where delayed histopathology may delay timely cancer treatment initiation, FNAC offers distinct advantages. Nakabori et al.[3] noted that rapid cytologic diagnosis can guide immediate clinical decisions, especially for patients with advanced disease but preserved liver function.

While radiological imaging is essential for locating and characterizing lesions, it has limited specificity. Differentiating HCC from dysplastic nodules or metastases and from benign lesions, such as hemangiomas or focal nodular hyperplasia, can be challenging, especially in cirrhotic livers. Our data reaffirm the complementary nature of radiology and FNAC, with cytology significantly enhancing diagnostic confidence when radiologic findings are equivocal. In summary, FNAC remains a vital, rapid, and cost-effective tool for the early evaluation of liver cancer. The predominance of metastatic carcinoma, as observed in our study, reinforces the need for thorough systemic evaluation in patients with liver mass. FNAC can provide a definitive diagnosis in most cases when combined with clinical and radiological findings and supplemented with immunocytochemistry.

CONCLUSION

FNAC, particularly when reported according to WHO guidelines, provides valuable information for the diagnostic work-up of hepatic masses. In addition to improved specificity and diagnostic accuracy compared to radiography, the procedure can also quantify the ROM across standardized cytological categories. A prospective, multicenter study incorporating newer molecular techniques will likely establish FNAC as a more powerful frontline diagnostic tool for hepatobiliary cancers.
