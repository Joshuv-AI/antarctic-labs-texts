The molecular mechanisms and therapeutic implications of PANoptosis in ischemic diseases
Authors: Jiangpeng Wu, Xunkai Wang, Jin Peng, Jinxi Liang, Jun Lv, Lin Huang, Shenmei Wu, Chen Yao, Guiyun Jin, Tang Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The singular forms of programmed cell death (PCD), including pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, are inadequate for comprehensively elucidating the complex pathological mechanisms underlying ischemic diseases. PANoptosis is a unique lytic, innate immune, and inflammatory cell death pathway, initiated by innate immune sensors and driven by caspases and RIPKs through PANoptosome complexes. In diseases like cerebral ischemia, retinal ischemia, myocardial ischemia, renal ischemia, and spinal cord ischemia, targeting key regulatory factors of PANoptosis can help mitigate tissue damage. Therefore, the therapeutic potential of PANoptosis in ischemic diseases—from molecular mechanisms to clinical applications—merits further investigation. However, effectively regulating PANoptosis to achieve therapeutic outcomes remains a critical scientific challenge that must be addressed. This review focuses on the molecular mechanisms of PANoptosis in ischemic diseases and its potential therapeutic implications, aiming to provide new insights and a theoretical foundation for precision treatment in these conditions.

Introduction

Programmed cell death (PCD) is a core mechanism in maintaining tissue homeostasis and responding to pathological injuries. Dysregulation of PCD is closely associated with the onset and progression of various diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune diseases, cardiovascular conditions, and infectious diseases [1]. With the integration of cutting-edge technologies such as multiomics and high-resolution imaging in recent years, significant progress has been made in understanding classical PCD pathways—specifically pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis—revealing the intricate regulatory mechanisms involved at the molecular signaling and protein–protein interaction levels. PANoptosis, as a unique innate immune cell death pathway, has rapidly become a research focus in the field of cell death due to its distinctive mechanism [2].

In the context of ischemic diseases, such as myocardial infarction (MI) and stroke, the mechanisms underlying cell death are intricately linked to processes like tissue ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI), the formation of inflammatory microenvironments, and vascular remodeling. Traditional research has often focused on the roles of individual PCD pathways, such as apoptosis-mediated neuronal loss or pyroptosis-induced inflammatory cascades. However, these isolated perspectives often fail to capture the full complexity of the disease. The concept of PANoptosis offers a new direction for the study of ischemic diseases. Its PANoptosome can be activated by upstream sensors such as Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1), absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), receptor-interacting serine/threonine kinase 1 (RIPK1), NLR family pyrin domain containing 12 (NLRP12), NLR family caspase recruitment domain containing 5 (NLRC5), and NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) [3–5].

In-depth study of the role of PANoptosis in ischemic diseases not only enhances our understanding of the pathological processes involved in ischemic diseases but may also reveal new targets and strategies for treatment. Current treatment options for ischemic diseases include traditional pharmacological therapies, surgical interventions, and interventional procedures. However, these approaches still have certain limitations regarding efficacy and safety. Therefore, exploring the therapeutic potential of PANoptosis in ischemic diseases presents promising opportunities to overcome this medical challenge. This article aims to provide a comprehensive review of the relationship between ischemic diseases and PCD, particularly PANoptosis, to establish a theoretical basis for future research and clinical applications.

Ischemic diseases and programmed cell death

PCD is an intrinsic cell-autonomous process of cellular demise, precisely encoded by genes within a highly conserved molecular regulatory network. This process initiates a cascade of molecular events by activating endogenous signaling pathways (such as the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway and the death receptor pathway) or exogenous stress responses (such as endoplasmic reticulum stress and DNA damage response), ultimately leading to the programmed disassembly of cellular structures [6]. The progression of ischemic diseases is closely related to PCD, which unfolds in two distinct stages: ischemia and reperfusion. During ischemic injury, cells transition to anaerobic metabolism, leading to the downregulation of α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and the conversion of xanthine dehydrogenase to xanthine oxidase (XO), leading to a metabolic shift dominated by hypoxanthine metabolism [7]. Due to the lack of oxygen in ischemic tissue, hypoxanthine cannot be metabolized prior to reperfusion, preventing the generation of free radicals. Prolonged ischemia results in a depletion of cellular energy, inhibition of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity, impaired efflux of Na⁺ and Ca2⁺, elevated intracellular ion concentrations, and subsequent cell swelling (Fig. 1). If ischemia is alleviated through treatment, secondary damage may arise during the subsequent reperfusion phase due to the excessive release of free radicals, calcium overload, heightened activation of the inflammatory responses, and activation of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). During mitochondrial ischemia and hypoxia, oxidative phosphorylation is impaired, and upon reperfusion, the electron transport chain is not fully restored, leading to electron leakage and the production of superoxide anion radicals [9]. XO catalyzes reactions that utilize molecular oxygen as a substrate, further generating superoxide anion radicals. Additionally, catecholamines undergo autoxidation, leading to the production of oxygen free radicals. The overproduction of free radicals leads to mitochondrial damage, neutrophil activation, increased XO levels, and aggravated catecholamine autoxidation, severely disrupting cellular structure and function, ultimately inducing PCD [10]. On one hand, free radicals can activate apoptotic signaling pathways through oxidative stress, promoting cell apoptosis [11]; on the other hand, they can also activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting pyroptosis. Activated caspase-1 cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD) to generate the N-terminal domain, forming membrane pores and releasing inflammatory factors, which leads to pyroptosis. During calcium overload reperfusion, the reverse transport of Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers and Na⁺/Ca2⁺ exchangers results in calcium influx. The dysfunction of the sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA) and the abnormal opening of RyR/IP3R exacerbate intracellular calcium release [12]. Excessive mitochondrial uptake of Ca2⁺ triggers sustained opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), leading to membrane potential collapse, reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, and cytochrome C release, which activates apoptosis [13]. Meanwhile, calcium overload activates calcium/calmodulin-dependent phosphatases (such as calcineurin) and kinases (such as CaMKII), promoting NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and NF-κB inflammatory signaling, while the activation of phospholipase A2 (PLA2) and proteases (such as calpain) disrupts membrane structure, thereby inducing necrosis [14]. Dysfunction of mitochondria-endoplasmic reticulum contact sites further exacerbates spatial regulation of calcium signaling, creating a positive feedback loop with oxidative stress, ultimately leading to metabolic-contractile uncoupling and a mixed mode of cell death [13].

During excessive inflammatory activation, ischemia–reperfusion triggers the immune response, resulting in the aggregation and activation of immune cells, complement system activation, and onset of sterile inflammation. As a large number of cell adhesion molecules, chemokines, and cytokines are produced, leukocytes migrate to the ischemic tissue. This migration activates pathogen recognition receptors and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family signaling pathways, resulting in excessive inflammatory activation [15]. As neutrophils aggregate and adhere, they alter microvascular blood flow and release free radicals, proteases, and ROS, which contribute tissue cell damage [16]. The release of various inflammatory mediators within the inflammatory microenvironment can activate cell surface death receptors, triggering the extrinsic apoptotic pathway. Meanwhile, inflammation-related signals interact with PCD pathways, which activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby inducing pyroptosis and exacerbating tissue damage [17].

The damaging role of iNOS activation in IRI is primarily mediated by pathological accumulation of NO and its derivatives, which are produced by iNOS. During the later phase of reperfusion, inflammatory cells, such as macrophages and neutrophils, significantly express iNOS through the NF-κB/STAT1 pathway. This leads to the production of NO, which rapidly reacts with concurrently released superoxide anions (O₂⁻) to form the potent oxidant peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻). ONOO⁻ contributes to mitochondrial toxicity, DNA damage, impairment of protein function, and the amplification of inflammatory cascades [18].

Furthermore, iNOS-derived NO can directly inhibit electron transport chain complex IV, exacerbating the mitochondrial energy crisis during reperfusion. The imbalance between NO and O₂⁻ induces “nitrosative stress,” which synergizes with oxidative stress and calcium overload [19].

Welt et al. [20] demonstrated that reperfusion therapy for acute ST-segment elevation MI (STEMI) significantly improves patient prognosis. However, the process of reperfusion may induce MIRI, leading to further myocardial cell necrosis, microvascular dysfunction, and adverse cardiac remodeling, thereby increasing the risk of heart failure. This study aimed to explore current therapeutic strategies, such as ischemic preconditioning and pharmacological interventions, which primarily regulate redox balance, inhibit calcium overload, and reduce inflammation, to exert cardioprotective effects. For example, colchicine can effectively inhibits pyroptosis by blocking the oligomerization and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, while also suppressing the pro-survival effects of the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby reducing the incidence of RIPK3-dependent necroptosis [21]. Additionally, colchicine increases the Bcl-2/Bax ratio and inhibits caspase-3 activity, which reduces the occurrence of apoptosis, demonstrating its potential for multi-target therapeutic intervention [22]. Further investigation into the connections between these mechanisms may reveal new targets and strategies for the treatment of related diseases.

Ischemic diseases and pyroptosis

Pyroptosis, first proposed by Cookson and Brennan in 1999 [23], is a form of inflammatory, caspase-dependent PCD characterized by membrane pore formation, cell swelling, plasma membrane rupture, chromatin fragmentation, and the release of pro-inflammatory substances from the cell [24]. This process can occur through classical and non-classical pathways. The classical pathway is mediated by inflammasomes, such as NLRP3, NLRC4, and AIM2. In the case of the NLRP3 inflammasome, when cells are stimulated by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), toll-like receptors (TLRs) activate the NF-κB pathway. This activation induces the expression of pro-interleukin (IL)-1β and pro-IL-18. Meanwhile, NLRP3 activation leads to oligomerization via its nucleotide-binding domain (NACHT) domain. This process recruits apoptosis-associated Speck-like protein containing a CAR (ASC), which in subsequently recruits pro-caspase-1, forming the NLRP3-ASC-caspase-1 complex. Within this complex, pro-caspase-1 undergoes autolytic cleavage, resulting in the activation of caspase-1. When activated, caspase-1 serves two functions: it cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 to produce their active forms, which are then secreted to promote inflammation, and it also cleaves GSDMD to produce an active N-terminal fragment (GSDMD-N) that forms pores in the membrane, causing cell swelling and rupture, thereby inducing pyroptosis [25]. The non-classical pathway of pyroptosis is mediated by caspase-4, caspase-5, and caspase-11. When bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from gram-negative bacteria, enter the cell, these caspases recognize and bind to LPS, leading to oligomerization and activation. This activation results in the cleavage of GSDMD, producing the GSDMD-N, which triggers pyroptosis. In addition, activated caspases-4, -5, and -11 can cleave pro-caspase-1, indirectly promoting the maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18, thereby enhancing the inflammatory response [26].

In the MIRI model, the NLRP3 inflammasome-caspase-1/GSDMD canonical pathway has been established as a key pathway for myocardial cell pyroptosis. Hypoxia activates NLRP3 through the release of mitochondrial ROS, promoting the formation of the ASC/pro-caspase-1 complex. Activated caspase-1 then cleaves GSDMD to generate GSDMD-N, which forms pores in the myocardial cell membrane, leading to ATP depletion and lactate dehydrogenase release [27]. Single-cell sequencing studies have shown that microglial cells in the ischemic core express elevated levels of GSDM family members (GSDMD/GSDME), and their activation is correlates significantly with the degree of blood–brain barrier disruption [28]. Notably, ischemic brain injury can induce the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), which initiates the synthesis of pyroptotic precursors via the TLR9/NF-κB pathway, creating a positive feedback loop of “mitochondrial damage-pyroptosis-inflammatory storm” [28, 29].

Ischemic diseases and apoptosis

Apoptosis, first proposed by Kerr et al. [30] in 1972, is a non-inflammatory form of PCD that encompasses intrinsic, extrinsic, and other pathways. Morphologically apoptosis is characterized by cell shrinkage, membrane blebbing, chromatin condensation, and nuclear collapse [31]. The intrinsic mitochondrial pathway is activated by stress signals such as DNA damage and oxidative stress. Under normal conditions, Bcl-2 family proteins (including anti-apoptotic like Bcl-2 and Bcl-XL, as well as pro-apoptotic like Bax and Bak) maintain mitochondrial stability, and their balance determines the cell’s sensitivity to apoptosis.

In response to apoptotic stimuli, Bax and Bak undergo conformational changes and translocate to the outer mitochondrial membrane, resulting in increased mitochondrial membrane permeability, loss of membrane potential, and the release of apoptotic proteins such as cytochrome c and Smac/DIABLO [32]. Cytochrome c binds to Apaf-1 to form an apoptosome, which activates caspase-9, thereby triggering the activation of effector caspases (such as caspase-3, and -7) and initiating apoptosis [33]. Smac/DIABLO inhibits inhibitors of apoptosis proteins (IAPs), thereby relieving the suppression of caspases and promoting apoptosis [34]. The extrinsic death receptor pathway is initiated by the binding of extracellular death ligands to cell surface death receptors belonging to the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) superfamily, such as Fas and TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1). Death ligands (such as FasL and TNF-α) bind to their receptors, leading to receptor trimerization and the recruitment of Fas-associated protein with a novel death domain (FADD), which then binds to the precursor of caspase-8, forming the death-inducing signaling complex, which activates caspase-8. This activation subsequently triggers the activation of downstream effector caspases, initiating apoptosis. In addition, caspase-8 can cleave Bid, linking the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways, amplifying the apoptotic signal, and forming the caspase-8-mediated mitochondrial apoptosis pathway [35].

In the study of the pathological mechanisms underlying ischemic cerebrovascular diseases, the regulation of neuronal PCD plays a crucial role. Research using focal cerebral ischemia models, such as the Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion (MCAO) model, has confirmed that the activation of dual apoptotic pathways in the penumbra is a key pathological cause of deterioration of neurological function [36]. In peripheral circulatory disorders, represented by chronic limb-threatening ischemia, the stress-induced apoptotic mechanisms in tissue cells exhibit distinct characteristics. Clinical sample analysis revealed abnormal activation of the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 signaling axis of the unfolded protein response (UPR) in peripheral ischemic skeletal muscle. The terminal effector molecule, CHOP, antagonizes the anti-apoptotic factors of the Bcl-2 family, thereby inducing a mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis cascade [37].

Ischemic diseases and necroptosis

Necroptosis was first discovered in 2005 and is a form of programmed inflammatory cell death [38]. It is characterized by cellular swelling, rupture of the cell membrane, and leakage of cytoplasmic contents [39]. Necroptosis can be triggered through several pathways, including the TNF-induced necroptosis signaling pathway. When TNF binds to TNFR1, leading to the trimerization of TNFR1 and the recruitment of Tumor necrosis factor receptor type 1-associated death domain protein (TRADD), which then forms complex I containing RIPK1 and cellular inhibitors of apoptosis proteins 1 and 2 (cIAP1/2). Within this complex, cIAP1/2 ubiquitinates and modifies RIPK1, inhibiting its kinase activity and directing the signal toward cell survival or inflammation. When cIAP1/2 is inhibited or degraded, RIPK1 becomes deubiquitinated and activated, recruiting RIPK3 to form necrosomes (complex IIb). Once RIPK3 is activated in the necrosome, it further phosphorylates and activates MLKL [40]. As the terminal effector of necroptosis, MLKL translocates to the cell membrane, inserts into it, and forms pores, ultimately leading to membrane rupture and the leakage of cellular contents, resulting in necroptosis.

In chronic ischemic myopathy induced by peripheral artery disease, histological analysis reveals a significant enhancement of RIPK3-mediated skeletal muscle necroptosis. Its activation level is significantly negatively correlated with the regenerative potential of muscle satellite cells [41]. Mechanistically, the ischemic and hypoxic microenvironment induces the co-expression of TNF-α and IFN-γ, which activates RIPK3 kinase activity through dual pathways: the TNFR1-dependent classical pathway and the ZBP1-dependent viral sensing pathway. This activation triggers MLKL-mediated membrane perforation, initiating a vicious cycle of inflammatory factor release, DAMPs recognition, and amplification of cell death signaling [42, 43].

Ischemic diseases and PANoptosis

PANoptosis is a unique lytic, innate immune, and inflammatory cell death pathway, initiated by innate immune sensors and driven by caspases and RIPKs through PANoptosome complexes [4]. Compared to other cell death pathways, PANoptosis responds to various triggers, such as microbial infections and changes in cellular homeostasis. It recruits other key molecules to form the corresponding PANoptosome through innate immune sensors, including ZBP1, AIM2, RIPK1, NLRP12, NLRC5 and NLRP3. Subsequently, the activation of the membrane pore protein GSDMD, caspase-3/7, and the phosphorylation of MLKL can lead to the occurrence of PANoptosis. The results is a mix of morphological changes, including cell membrane rupture, chromatin condensation, and organelle dissolution [44].

Yan et al. [44] conducted a comprehensive multidimensional analysis, which, at the morphological level, effectively revealed key molecules involved in PANoptosis, using TUNEL staining (indicating Caspase-3), EthD-III membrane integrity assessment (indicating MLKL), and PI staining (indicating GSDMD). At the molecular level, Western blot analysis combined with the detection of pathway-specific marker proteins, as well as immunofluorescent colocalization of key molecules (e.g., NeuN co-staining with cleaved caspase-3/GSDMD/MLKL), confirmed the existence of PANoptosis. At the functional level, experiments using pathway-specific inhibitors such as Z-VAD-FMK, Disulfiram, and necroptostatin-1 demonstrated that, compared to the use of individual inhibitors, the combined application of these inhibitors significantly reduced cell death rates (for instance, the use of a triple inhibitor regimen reduced the TUNEL-positive rate from 40 to 15%), thus confirming the complex mechanisms of action of key molecules in PANoptosis.

Research conducted by LiPB et al. has indicated that Piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (Piezo1), acting as a cardiac mechanosensor, drives the progression of cardiac IRI through caspase-8-mediated cardiomyocyte PANoptosis. As a mechanosensitive ion channel, Piezo1 specifically interacts with caspase-8, a key initiating activator of the PANoptosome, thereby activating the cardiomyocyte PANoptosis signaling pathway, ultimately leading to cardiac dysfunction. Furthermore, the study observed that in cardiac tissue subjected to IRI, the mRNA and protein expression levels of the PANoptosome significantly increased with prolonged reperfusion time [45].

PANoptosis and PANoptosome

PANoptosis is an innovative concept in cell death research, emerging from a detailed exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying PCD. Initial studies on cell death primarily focused on apoptosis, but the identification of PANoptosis marked an expansion in scientists’ understanding of cell death mechanisms. In 2019, Malireddi et al. [2] first proposed this novel mode of cell death, which integrates characteristics of multiple cell death pathways, presenting a PCD process characterized by distinct changes in cell morphology. Traditionally, different cell death pathways were regarded as independent; however, studies have revealed extensive cross-talk and synergy in signal transduction, effector molecule activation, and pathophysiological regulation [39].

PANoptosis is regulated by upstream receptors and molecular cascade signals, which assemble into a polymeric complex, termed the PANoptosome. During the signal transduction process, the PANoptosome occupies a pinitol position, receiving and integrating various signals, thus initiating a series of molecular events that affect cell fate. PANoptosomes are multiprotein complexes that form in response to innate immune sensor activation and can contain multiple caspases as well as RIPK1 and RIPK3. These complexes induce the activation of gasdermins and MLKL, leading to membrane pore formation and the release of DAMPs (such as HMGB1), cytokines, and chemokines [46]. Receptors play a crucial role in the process of cell death, particularly by promoting the formation of PANoptosomes, which drive the occurrence of PANoptosis. The assembly of the PANoptosome relies on the synergistic action of multiple key proteins. Receptors not only recruit these proteins but also serve as a “molecular scaffold” that facilitates the assembly and activation of inflammasomes, as well as the initiation of various cell death pathways [47]. The PANoptosis signaling process generally begins with the recognition and sensing of endogenous or exogenous danger signals by specific receptor proteins. Once these signals are detected, the receptor proteins activate downstream adaptor proteins, which, through binding to the receptor proteins, transmit the signal to downstream effector proteins such as caspases, RIPK, and MLKL (Fig. 2). ZBP1-PANoptosome

ZBP1, also known as the DNA-dependent activator of interferon (IFN) regulatory factors, functions as an innate nucleic acid sensor and immune receptor, playing a role in specific regions of the genome [49]. The receptor-interacting protein (RIP) homotypic interaction motif (RHIM) contained in ZBP1 can initiate and mediate cell death programs under certain cellular environments and signaling, which is critical for the elimination of pathogen-infected cells and the preservation of cellular homeostasis [50]. The Z-α2 domains of ZBP1 enable precise recognition and binding of Z-DNA, allowing for the rapid detection and binding of RNA products generated by exogenous viral invasion or the detection of abnormal changes and danger signals in endogenous nucleic acid ligands produced by cellular damage, stress, and other factors [51].

The ZBP1-PANoptosome is consists of ZBP1, ASC, NLRP3, RIPK3, caspase-1, caspase-8, and caspase-6 [52]. Activated ZBP1 is involved in NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent pyroptosis; in apoptosis and drives the process through FADD-CASP8; in necroptosis, it is driven by RIPK3-MLKL, and CASP6 can facilitate the association between ZBP1 and RIPK3 [53]. In PCD, ZBP1 is a key regulatory factor, and its absence effectively blocks the occurrence of pyroptosis, apoptosis, necroptosis, and PANoptosis [54, 55].

AIM2-PANoptosome

AIM2 is a member of the PYHIN protein family and is a classic cytoplasmic innate immune receptor, containing an N-terminal pyrin domain (PYD) and a C-terminal HIN-200 domain. As a member of the death domain superfamily, PYD mediates protein–protein interactions and playing a vital role in inflammasome assembly and signal transduction. The HIN-200 domain contains two tandem oligodeoxynucleotide/oligosaccharide-binding folding domains (OB-folds), which specifically recognize double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and are essential elements for AIM2’s functions as a DNA sensor [56]. The AIM2-PANoptosome consists of AIM2, ASC, ZBP1, Pyrin, RIPK1, RIPK3, caspase-1, and caspase-8 [57]. During these infections, the absence of AIM2 leads to a decrease in the expression levels of Pyrin and ZBP1. This suggests that AIM2-mediated signaling serves as an upstream regulator of Pyrin and ZBP1, playing a role in the assembly and activation regulation of the AIM2-PANoptosome. Both HSV1 and F. novicida can activate AIM2, bind to the Zα domain of ZBP1, and inhibit the activity of Rho GTPase-activating protein (Rho-GTP). This characteristic enables them to form a unique complex that includes AIM2, Pyrin, and ZBP1 [58].

Lee et al. [59] found that following herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV1) and Francisella novicida infection, the activation of inflammatory cell death-related molecules (caspase-1, GSDMD, GSDME), apoptotic molecules (caspase-8, caspase-3, caspase-7), and necroptosis molecules (RIPK3, MLKL) was significantly reduced in Mefv–/– and Zbp1–/– bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) compared to (wild-type) WT BMDMs. Moreover, the activation of these molecules was completely absent in Aim2–/– and Mefv–/–Zbp1–/– BMDMs. Collectively, these data suggest that HSV1 and F. novicida infections induce PANoptosis through the coordinated activation of AIM2 and ZBP1.

RIPK1-PANoptosome

RIPK1 belongs to the RIP serine/threonine kinase family, characterized by an N-terminal kinase domain, an intermediate domain, and a C-terminal death domain [60]. Transforming growth factor-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) serves as a core regulatory component of RIPK1-dependent PANoptosome. Deletion or functional abnormalities of the TAK1 gene can trigger the assembly of RIPK1-dependent PANoptosomes, inducing PCD and contributing to autoimmune inflammation [61]. It interacts with ASC, NLRP3, RIPK3, caspase-1, and caspase-8 to form a multi-protein complex following activation of innate immune sensors, thereby mediating the occurrence of PANoptosis [55].

NLRP12-PANoptosome

NLRP12, as an important member of the NLR family, features a modular structure that includes an N-terminal pyrin domain, a central nucleotide-binding domain, and a C-terminal leucine-rich repeat that contribute to its role in regulating inflammatory responses and immune signaling. The N-terminus plays a crucial role in protein–protein interactions and signal initiation. The central region contains the NACHT domain, which is essential for sensing intracellular danger signals, carrying out nucleotide exchange, and activating oligomerization. The C-terminus consists of a leucine-rich repeat sequence and is primarily responsible for recognizing PAMPs and endogenous danger signals, as it initiates downstream immune responses [62, 63]. The NLRP12-PANoptosome is composed of ASC, NLRP3, caspase-8, caspase-1 and RIPK3 [64]. Upon encountering PAMPs or TNF co-stimulation, NLRP12 is rapidly activated. This activation process involves the activation of Toll-like receptors 2/4 (TLR2/4), which induces the expression of interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1) and the production of substantial ROS. [65]. ROS, an important signaling molecule, further upregulates NLRP12 expression, forming a positive feedback loop that ultimately leads to efficient activation of the PANoptosome [64].

NLRC5-PANoptosome

NLRC5 is a highly expressed and enigmatic member of the NLR family, particularly in hematopoietic cells [66]. Research by Sundaram et al. [4] has shown that NLRC5 can interact with NLRP3 and NLRP12 to drive PANoptosis in response to their specific ligands. Additionally, based on studies showing that NLRP12 interacts with ASC, caspase-8, and RIPK3 to drive PANoptosis in response to heme plus PAMPs, in order to determine the role of NLRC5 in this complex, the team performed endogenous immunoprecipitation on WT, Nlrp12−/−, Pycard−/−, and Nlrc5−/− (BMDMs after treatment with heme plus Pam3. A multi-protein complex containing NLRC5, NLRP12 ASC, RIPK3, caspase-1, caspase-8, and NLRP3 was observed to form in WT BMDMs, and the formation of this complex was abolished in the absence of NLRP12, ASC, or NLRC5. These results suggest that NLRC5, along with NLRP12 and ASC, are components of the PANoptosome complex, which can respond to heme plus PAMP stimulation to drive PANoptosis.

NLRP3-PANoptosome

NLRP3, one of the most extensively studied inflammasome sensors, can recognize a variety of infectious and non-infectious stimuli, thereby inducing inflammatory cell death, pyroptosis, and the activation and release of inflammasome-dependent cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 [67]. Recent studies by Sharma [5] and colleagues have confirmed that NLRP3 can function as a sensor for the PANoptosome, driving the formation of a PANoptosome complex composed of NLRP3, ASC, caspase-8, caspase-1, and RIPK3, thereby inducing PANoptosis. Using the LPS and ATP stimulation model, the team found that delayed lytic cell death could be induced in caspase-1 or GSDMD-deficient cells; however, this phenomenon was not observed in NLRP3-deficient cells. In another classic NLRP3 inflammasome trigger model involving LPS combined with nigericin, similar results were observed, suggesting the existence of a lytic cell death pathway independent of the classical pyroptosis route. Further molecular mechanism studies showed that LPS combined with ATP stimulation could induce the activation of PANoptosis-related molecules such as CASP1, CASP8, CASP3, GSDMD, and GSDME in WT cells. These data confirm that the NLRP3-PANoptosome can induce PANoptosis and, for the first time, elucidate the existence and molecular composition of the NLRP3-PANoptosome.

The relationship between PANoptosis and ischemic diseases

Cerebral ischemic diseases

Ischemic stroke is a complex and heterogeneous condition caused by transient or permanent narrowing or occlusion of the brain’s supply arteries, leading to cerebral infarction. The final infarction area is influenced by multiple factors, including ischemic duration, severity, infarct location, and age [68]. According to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease report, the incidence of ischemic stroke in China increased from 117 per 100,000 in 2005 to 145 per 100,000 in 2019 [69]. Various forms of cell death have been identified in studies related to ischemic stroke and mortality. For instance, Cao et al. [70] constructed a MCAO model and found that during inflammatory responses, the expression of NLRP1 was upregulated, leading to increased levels of caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 in rat brain cells, which triggered pyroptosis. Iwai et al. [71] observed neuronal apoptosis in a transient forebrain ischemia model in gerbils. Yang et al. [72] confirmed the presence of necroptosis through ischemic stroke and oxygen–glucose deprivation models, noting upregulation of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL protein expression. Reperfusion after cerebral ischemia is an effective treatment for ischemic stroke, and the PANoptosis mechanism plays a significant role in cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury (CIRI). Yan et al. [73] conducted a systematic analysis of 57 experimental data points from 62 publications and confirmed that PANoptosis is a novel and independent mode of cell death, capable of occurring within the Retinal ischemic diseases

Retinal ischemia is caused by alterations in ocular hemodynamics, leading to persistent insufficient retinal perfusion. Common causes include age-related macular degeneration, central retinal vein occlusion, diabetic retinopathy, and glaucoma. Ischemia causes a deficiency in retinal nutrients and oxygen supply, triggering intracellular stress responses such as enhanced glutamatergic stimulation and increased oxidative stress, which result in severe neuronal damage and functional decline of the retina. Although prompt reperfusion can mitigate some damage (Table 1), excessive production of ROS and inflammatory responses continue to accelerate neuronal degeneration and death [76].

Glaucoma, a typical neurodegenerative disease, is characterized by progressive optic nerve atrophy and visual field loss, making it the leading irreversible cause of blindness worldwide. Epidemiological studies estimate that, due to the aging global population and lifestyle changes, the number of glaucoma patients worldwide may reach approximately 111.8 million by 2040 [82].

Li et al. [83] introduced saline into the anterior chamber of the right eye to create a model of ocular hypertension. They confirmed the existence of PANoptosis in retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) through immunofluorescent co-localization of caspase-3, GSDMD, and p-MLKL. Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) combined with confocal microscopy was used to observe the interactions and complex assembly between ZBP1, RIPK3, ASC, and caspase-8. ELISA and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) were used to analyze the expression levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ in the retina, validating the anti-inflammatory effects of TAT-N24. Ultimately, it was confirmed that TAT-N24 effectively alleviates RGC loss and retinal damage by inhibiting pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis in an acute glaucoma model. Additionally, it exerts anti-PANoptosome effects by inhibiting microglial activation, downregulating NLRP3 and NLRC4 inflammasomes, and suppressing the expression of ZBP1-PANoptosome, thereby inhibiting PANoptosis.

Myocardial ischemic diseases

Cardiovascular diseases are a leading cause of mortality worldwide, presenting a significant public health challenge, with coronary artery ischemia being a primary contributor to MI [84]. While reperfusion can effectively inhibit MI, it may also worsen damage to energy metabolism, electrophysiological function, and the structural integrity of the heart [85]. In a study by Cui et al. [43], the anticholinergic drug pentylhexylquinuclidine (PHC) was administered in a rat model of MIRI. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis indicated that PHC intervention led to the downregulation of the ZBP1 pathway. To further validate the role of ZBP1 in MIRI, the researchers employed PCR techniques and discovered that overexpression of the ZBP1 gene significantly exacerbated MI in rats. Conversely, silencing ZBP1 gene expression using gene silencing methods led to an improvement in MI. Additionally, western blot and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining confirmed that the levels of PANoptosis-related proteins in MIRI were significantly reduced following PHC pretreatment. These findings demonstrate that PHC can effectively alleviate MIRI by inhibiting ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis in the ischemic penumbra (Table 1).

In their study on the impact of the Piezo1 mechanosensitive ion channel on MIRI, Li et al. [45] analyzed microarray data and found that, compared to the sham surgery group, the mRNA expression levels of PANoptosis-related genes in the hearts of mice significantly increased at critical time points of 6 or 24 h after MIRI injury. To validate this observation at the protein level, the research team conducted a detailed analysis using western blotting. The results indicated that a progressive increase in the expression levels of PANoptosis-related proteins in ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) hearts compared to sham-operated heart samples. Collectively, these findings strongly suggest the occurrence of PANoptosis in cardiac tissue under I/R stress conditions.

Zuurbier et al. [86] highlighted the dual role of innate immune mechanisms in cardiac IRI among STEMI patients, with both damaging and protective effects. For instance, in the NLRP3 pathway, the upregulation of NLRP3 expression leads to it assembly into the NLRP3-ASC-caspase-1 oligomer, which then activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, resulting in the production of active caspase-1 and the release of IL-1β and IL-18, while simultaneously inducing pyroptosis. However, during ischemic preconditioning (IPC), NLRP3 mediates a protective effect via the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway, and inhibiting NLRP3 negates the protective benefits of IPC.

Wu et al. [87] discovered that the cardioprotective effects of Xianling Gubao Capsule (XLGB) in MI is closely related to the regulation of oxidative stress, inflammatory cascade, and the PANoptosis process. Network pharmacology analysis confirmed that XLGB targets key genes such as IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and caspase-3. Furthermore, molecular docking studies revealed significant affinity between XLGB’s bioactive components and receptors like NLRP3, caspase-3, and RIPK1, forming stable, complex structures. Immunofluorescence detection revealed elevated expression levels of NLRP3, cleaved-caspase-3, and p-MLKL. The mechanism of PANoptosis is complex, and through the validation of key molecules involved in PANoptosis, they found that immunofluorescence staining showed an increase in the expression levels of NLRP3, cleaved-caspase-3, and p-MLKL. Western blot analysis revealed that in the MI mouse model, NLRP3/cleaved-caspase-1/NN-GSDMD, cleaved-caspase-3, and p-RIP1/p-RIP3/p-MLKL were significantly upregulated, while treatment with XLGB markedly reversed these changes. The study results suggest that XLGB exerts a cardioprotective effect by inhibiting oxidative stress and inflammation, and blocking key molecules of PANoptosis.

Renal ischemic diseases

Renal IRI occurs when blood flow is restored to the kidneys after a brief ischemic episode, triggering a cascade of complex pathophysiological changes. This condition is a major cause of acute kidney injury (AKI) and plays a key role in the progression of acute renal dysfunction and kidney disease [88]. AKI affects approximately 12.2% of hospitalized patients, with a higher prevalence in low- and middle-income countries due to limited healthcare resources and a heavy burden of underlying health issues. If left untreated, AKI poses a severe threat to patient health, with an adult AKI-related mortality rate of 23.9% (Table 1). The mortality rate is even higher among patients undergoing renal replacement therapy, creating a substantial burden on families and society [89].

Uysal et al. [90] investigated the effects of 3,4-methylenedioxy-β-nitrostyrene (MNS) which targets the PANoptosome on PANoptosis in a renal ischemia–reperfusion model and its underlying protective mechanisms. As a specific NLRP3 inhibitor, MNS binds to the NLRP3 protein with its unique molecular structure, inhibiting its ATPase activity and reducing the activation level of the NLRP3 inflammasome. The study involved 32 rats divided into the control, sham, DMSO, and MNS groups. Electron microscopy and histopathological examination showed that damage to glomeruli, renal tubules, and mesangial cells was alleviated in the MNS group compared to the sham and DMSO groups. IHS analysis showed that the staining levels of caspase-3, GSDMD, and MLKL were lower in the MNS group. Due to the complex mechanism of PANoptosis, the study reveals that through verifying the dynamic changes of certain key molecules, PANoptosis plays a significant role in the progression of the disease during renal ischemia–reperfusion.

Spinal cord ischemic diseases

Spinal cord IRI (SCIRI) commonly occurs following spinal decompression surgery for the relief of spinal cord compression from conditions such as spinal intradural lesions (e.g., disc herniation, spinal tumors, spinal tuberculosis); intrathecal lesions (e.g., intrathecal tumors, epidural abscesses, vertebral fractures, etc.); as well as during thoracoabdominal aorta clamping and release during surgery. The incidence of SCIRI ranges from 2 to 40%, with significant variation based on disease type, surgical technique, and individual patient factors [91]. The pathogenesis of SCII involves multiple systems, including the nervous, circulatory, and immune systems, resulting in a complex and varied process. The resulting functional impairments related to the spinal cord significantly diminish patients’ quality of life, causing physical and psychological distress, as well as economic burdens on patients and their families (Table 1). Furthermore, it exacerbates the consumption of social healthcare resources and economic pressures [69, 92].

Xie et al. [93] investigated the neuroprotective gas hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) and its role in the mechanism of SCIRI), confirming the presence of PANoptosis. The study used the H₂S donor GYY4137, known for its sustained-release properties, to pre-treat SCIRI model rats. The results showed that pre-treatment effectively alleviated lower limb motor dysfunction caused by SCIRI. Western blotting and IHC analyses were performed on the pre-treated rats. Treatment of PANoptosis and ischemic diseases

Ischemic diseases encompass a range of conditions with widespread effects and serious adverse impacts on the body, triggering complex pathophysiological cascades across multiple organ systems. With advancements in medical research and continuous innovations in diagnostic and therapeutic technologies, treatment strategies targeting PANoptosis in ischemic diseases have become increasingly diversified. Traditional drug therapies, modern surgical interventions, and interventional treatments remain important approaches [94–96]. At the same time, advanced targeted therapies and gene therapies are gradually emerging [97, 98]. Additionally, extensively studied Chinese herbal medicines offer unique advantages in treating ischemic diseases [99–101]. Many Chinese herbs are rich in various active components and possess multiple pharmacological effects, such as antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, pyroptotic, and necroptosis properties [102–104]. Since PANoptosis plays a crucial role in the pathological progression of ischemic diseases, further investigation into its mechanisms provides new targets and insights for treatment. Current therapeutic interventions for ischemic diseases primarily focus on regulating pathways like pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, as well as inhibiting inflammation and oxidative stress mechanisms. However, there is limited research that describes how existing treatments cure diseases from the perspective of PANoptosis, a novel form of cell death. Based on the understanding that PANoptosis contributes to the development of ischemic diseases, we can speculate that inhibiting the PANoptosis pathway could effectively improve the progression of ischemic diseases. This provides a new perspective on the mechanisms by which current treatments work and offers a new direction for the future treatment of such diseases and the development of novel drugs.

Traditional drug treatment for ischemic diseases

Antiplatelet drugs play a critical role in the treatment of ischemic diseases. They primarily reduce the risk of thrombosis by inhibiting platelet aggregation, thereby improving blood supply to ischemic tissues [94]. For instance, aspirin, a cyclooxygenase inhibitor, inhibits COX-1 to reduce platelet aggregation and is commonly used for both primary and secondary prevention of ischemic diseases such as coronary heart disease and stroke [105]. Clopidogrel, an adenosine diphosphate [ADP] receptor antagonist, inhibits the P2Y12 receptor to reduce ADP-induced platelet aggregation and is widely used in treating acute coronary syndromes (ACSs) [106]. Furthermore, antiplatelet drugs can modulate the platelet apoptosis pathway, alleviating microcirculatory damage caused by thrombosis. They may also exert anti-apoptotic effects by modulating intracellular calcium ion concentrations, thereby improving microcirculation in patients with ischemic diseases [107].

Heparin is a classic and widely used anticoagulant that enhances the activity of antithrombin III, inhibits the generation of prothrombinase, interferes with thrombin activity, and suppresses thrombus formation [108]. In conditions such as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and ACS, heparin not only improves hemodynamics through anticoagulation but also reduces blood coagulation-related factors and alleviates ischemic damage caused by thrombotic occlusion. Furthermore, it promotes fibrinolytic activity to dissolve thrombi, prevents tissue hypoxia and cell apoptosis, and plays a crucial role in preventing and treating complications associated with ischemic diseases [109].

Statins, which are HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, are valuable in the prevention and treatment of ischemic diseases by inhibiting generalized apoptosis. Their potent lipid-lowering effects can stabilize atherosclerotic plaques and reduce the risk of plaque rupture, a key mechanism in the development of ACS and other cardiovascular diseases. Statins also regulate the expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), promote nitric oxide (NO) production, dilate blood vessels, and improve microcirculation. In addition, statins have multifaceted vascular protective effects: They possess antioxidant properties, scavenge free radicals, and alleviate oxidative stress-induced damage to the vascular wall; they exhibit anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the release of inflammatory mediators and slowing vascular inflammation; they modulate the immune system to reduce immune hyperactivity-induced vascular damage; they maintain the normal function and quantity of progenitor cells, promoting vascular repair and regeneration [110].

Surgical and interventional treatment of ischemic diseases

Surgical vascular reconstruction improves blood supply to ischemic tissues through direct surgery, such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), which is commonly performed for severe coronary atherosclerotic heart disease. Studies have shown that patients undergoing CABG experience a reduction in infarct size and a decrease in myocardial cell apoptosis markers. This improvement is attributed to the restoration of blood flow during surgery, which reduces ROS production, inhibits mitochondrial apoptosis signaling pathways, and consequently decreases myocardial cell apoptosis, thereby alleviating the impact of generalized apoptosis [95].

Intravenous thrombolysis involves the precise delivery thrombolytic agents to the site of a thrombus via a catheter. For instance, in cases of acute cerebral infarction, intra-arterial administration of recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) is commonly used for thrombolysis. This therapy can rapidly dissolve the thrombus, reopen the occluded cerebral vessels, restore cerebral blood flow, and prevent irreversible neuronal injury. Timely restoration of blood flow can reduce the release of inflammatory mediators, mitigate inflammation-induced neuronal damage, and decrease the level of generalized apoptosis [96].

Mechanical thrombectomy can rapidly remove intracranial large vessel thrombi and restore blood flow perfusion. Among these, endovascular thrombectomy is one of the key therapeutic methods for treating ischemic diseases, and it plays a crucial role in the management of acute large vessel occlusive ischemic stroke [111]. Research indicates that after thrombectomy, the expression of pro-apoptotic protein Bax in the brain tissue surrounding the infarction zone is reduced, while the expression of anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 is increased. This suggests that thrombectomy exerts its protective effects by modulating the expression of apoptosis-related proteins, thereby inhibiting widespread apoptosis triggered by ischemic brain injury, which is significant for protecting neuronal cells and reducing neurological deficits [112].

Targeted therapy of ischemic diseases

Z-VAD-FMK is a cell-permeable, irreversible broad-spectrum inhibitor of cysteine proteases, playing a crucial role in regulating of PCD. Hui Zhu et al. [97] investigated the therapeutic effects of delayed injection in the field of neuroscience by constructing a MCAO model of focal cerebral infarction in rats. They performed daily neurological severity score (NSS) assessments on the rats and measured the infarct area at post-surgery days 1, 4, and 7. The results showed no significant difference in NSS scores between the two groups at 4 h, day 1, and day 2 after surgery; however, significant differences emerged starting from day 4. Regarding the infarct area, no differences between the two groups were observed on day 1 post-surgery, but significant differences were observed on days 4 and 7. Delayed injection of Z-VAD-FMK can accelerate the recovery of neurological deficits, improve prognosis, and reduce infarct size, as it inhibits the activity of caspase family members (including key apoptotic and necroptosis caspases). Although broad inhibition of caspase activity does not selectively inhibit the occurrence of PANoptosis, the results suggest that Z-VAD-FMK can treat tissue damage caused by PANoptosis. This reveals the critical role of PANoptosis in ischemic brain injury and provides experimental evidence for its clinical application [113].

Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1) is a specific inhibitor of the necroptosis pathway, capable of blocking the phosphorylation of RIPK1 and preventing cells from entering the necroptosis program [114]. In a study on ischemic AKI in non-heart-beating donor rats, Dokur et al. [115] found that Nec-1 pretreatment resulted in lower expression levels of caspase-3, GSDMD, and MLKL in the rats. Histopathological and biochemical assessments indicated that Nec-1 prevents IRI by inhibiting necroptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses. It also interacts with RIPK1 and ZBP1, weakening the caspase-1 cascade, inhibiting GSDMD activation, and suppressing pyroptosis. In ischemic diseases, pyroptosis, apoptosis, necroptosis, and PANoptosis all play a role, making it difficult for a single pathway target to address the complexity of the pathological processes [45]. The combined application of Z-VAD-FMK and Nec-1 can regulate multiple cell death pathways simultaneously, providing broader cellular protection and offering a potentially more effective strategy for the treatment of ischemic diseases [116].

Gene therapy of ischemic diseases

Gene editing is an emerging therapeutic approach that utilizes specific technologies to modify the genetic sequences of organisms, enabling efficient and precise insertion, deletion, or replacement of genes, thereby altering genetic information and phenotype. Technologies such as Zinc Finger Nucleases, Transcription Activator-Like Effector Nucleases, Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats, and Base Editing provide powerful tools for gene function research, driving their development. These advancements also open new therapeutic avenues and strategies in biomedical science, with immense potential to address complex diseases and revolutionize traditional medical models [98].

In ischemic diseases, the regulation of PANoptosis-related genes can alleviate cell damage and death caused by ischemia. By regulating apoptosis-related genes, such as p53, Bcl-2 family proteins, caspases, and other apoptosis-regulating molecules, gene therapy can selectively inhibit or activate specific apoptotic pathways, thereby precisely controlling cell fate. For example, overexpression of the anti-apoptotic factors Bcl-2 or Bcl-xL can inhibit endogenous apoptotic pathways, reducing cell death following ischemia; activation of cell death-inducing genes (such as FasL and TNF-α) can enhance cell tolerance to ischemic injury [117].

Lebek et al. [118] employed CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing technology to investigate Ca2⁺/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase IIδ (CaMKIIδ). Through gene editing, they replaced the methionine residues Met281 and Met282 in the regulatory domain of CaMKIIδ to inhibit its oxidation and excessive activation, both of which are key pathogenic factors in MIRI in humans and mice. This study provides new insights and approaches for elucidating the mechanisms of MIRI and for developing preventive and therapeutic strategies. Apoptosis-related gene therapy holds promise as a significant approach to improving the prognosis of ischemic diseases, especially within the framework of precision medicine and personalized treatment.

Treatment of ischemic diseases with traditional chinese herbal medicine

Astragalus, first documented in Shennong’s Materia Medica, is revered as the “chief among tonic herbs.” It has a mildly warm nature and a sweet taste, and is associated with the spleen and lung meridians. Astragalus is known for its ability to tonify qi, lift yang, strengthen the defensive qi, and consolidate the exterior, making it effective for treating conditions such as qi deficiency and prolapse of the middle qi [99]. The key active ingredient, astragaloside IV (AS-IV), is a triterpene saponin of the cycloartane type, and it exerts its effects in the prevention and treatment of ischemic diseases through anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and antioxidant actions. A study by Xiao et al. [119] demonstrated that AS-IV can reduce the levels of NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, GSDMD, and GSDMD-N in the CIRI model. It inhibits activation of the NLRP3/Ccspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathway; activates Nrf2, a negative regulator of this pathway; and inhibits pyroptosis. Zhang et al. [102] used AS-IV to intervene in a MI mouse model and confirmed its protective effect on cardiac function. AS-IV alleviated MI-induced myocardial fibrosis and cardiac remodeling, reduced the expression of IL-18 and IL-1β, and reversed NLRP3 activation-induced pyroptosis by inhibiting ROS release during oxidative stress.

Danshen (Salvia miltiorrhiza), first recorded in Shennong Bencao Jing (The Divine Farmer’s Materia Medica), is characterized by its bitter taste and slight cold nature. It is attributed to the Heart and Liver meridians and is known for promoting blood circulation and resolving blood stasis, making it useful the treatment of conditions such as dysmenorrhea and chest and abdominal pain [100]. Danshen exhibits strong antioxidant activity, capable of scavenging ROS, regulating endothelial cell permeability, inhibiting platelet aggregation, and suppressing endothelial dysfunction and vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation. Its active components include tanshinones (e.g., tanshinone IIA and I) and salvianolic acids (e.g., salvianolic acid B). Tanshinones possess broad biological activities, such as antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, while salvianolic acids exhibit antioxidant and anticoagulant properties [120]. Hu et al. [103] confirmed that preconditioning with tanshinone IIA can alleviate cell hypoxia/reoxygenation (A/R) injury, reduce ROS levels, maintain mitochondrial integrity, and inhibit cardiomyocyte apoptosis, playing a positive role in the prevention and treatment of MIRI.

Ginseng, a perennial herb from the Araliaceae family, is found in China, Korea, Japan, and other regions. With a history of over two thousand years of use, it has a sweet and slightly bitter taste and a mildly warm nature. It is associated with the Lung, Spleen, and Heart meridians and is renowned for its ability to greatly replenish vital energy, earning the title “King of Herbs” [121, 122]. Modern medical research shows that ginseng has significant effects in reducing oxidative stress and in various other aspects [122]. Ginseng or ginsenosides (Rd) are positively correlated with the therapeutic effects of ischemic stroke. They can modulate the endogenous antioxidant defense system, eliminate ROS and RNS, and have the antioxidant potential to alleviate ischemic stroke-induced damage. Additionally, they regulate inflammation through nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB). A study by Harai et al. [123] demonstrated that ginsenoside Rb1 can inhibit NF-κB and its DNA binding activity, prevent neuronal apoptosis, and reduce IL-6 levels in ischemic brain tissue. Discussion and prospect

PANoptosis, as an emerging mechanism of cell death, has attracted considerable attention in the field of life sciences in recent years. Current research is gradually elucidating the complex roles that PANoptosis plays in various diseases. This form of cell death exhibits dual characteristics—both beneficial and detrimental—in the progression of various diseases. For example, in tumors [124], moderate activation of PANoptosis can effectively suppress tumor growth, while in ischemic diseases, excessive activation of PANoptosis manifests as diverse damaging effects in different tissues, leading to massive cell death and significantly accelerating disease deterioration. This phenomenon underscores the importance of precise regulation of PANoptosis plays a crucial role in disease intervention.

In the realm of ischemic diseases, research on PANoptosis is still in its infancy, with relatively few studies conducted. This may be attributed to limitations in detection technologies, the complexity of disease models, and a lack of understanding of the PANoptosis pathway. The specific and intricate regulatory mechanisms of the PANoptosis pathway. The specific and intricate regulatory mechanisms of PANoptosis require further exploration and detailed investigation. For example, the interaction network between core PANoptosis proteins has not yet been fully elucidated. Understanding how these proteins collaborate to regulate cell death signaling pathways necessitates deeper analysis. Additionally, defining the activation threshold of PANoptosis across various cell types and within the structural framework of tissue microenvironments presents a significant challenge in current scientific research.

Based on existing research findings and literature, and considering future development trends, research on PANoptosis is expected to expand its applications through emerging cutting-edge technologies. Identifying effective therapeutic targets for disease treatment requires a comprehensive and multidimensional evaluation of various factors. On one hand, it is essential to explore the key hub proteins in the PANoptosis and to regulate them precisely using targeted intervention strategies, thereby achieving accurate control of the PANoptosis process. For example, the innovative development of small molecule inhibitors targeting specific key enzymes or receptors could be explored. On the other hand, it is important to closely monitor the effects of intracellular environmental factors on PANoptosis. A deeper analysis of the intrinsic mechanisms linking oxidative stress, metabolic disorders, and PANoptosis may provide novel insights and pathways for discovering new therapeutic targets.

Network pharmacology can construct a multi-dimensional drug-target-disease network system, utilizing high-throughput screening methods to explore potential PANoptosis-regulating drugs. Additionally, it can predict the synergistic effects of drug combinations, providing a solid theoretical foundation for the practice of precision medicine [125]. Single-cell sequencing technology enables in-depth analysis of the heterogeneity of PANoptosis-related gene expression at the single-cell level, allowing for precise delineation of the sensitivity differences exhibited by different cell subpopulations in response to PANoptosis stimuli. This technology facilitates the discovery of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets [126].

Spatial transcriptomics sequencing technology allows for the precise analysis of the spatial distribution patterns of PANoptosis-related gene expression at the tissue level in situ, revealing the spatially specific modes of PANoptosis regulation exhibited by different cell types within the tissue microenvironment. This technology provides crucial insights into the tissue-specific mechanisms of PANoptosis in both physiological and pathological states, significantly advancing the innovation and optimization of PANoptosis-related therapeutic strategies based on tissue spatial dimensions [127]. Furthermore, the development of specific PANoptosis probes is vital for real-time monitoring of dynamic changes in PANoptosis in vivo. These probes can accurately capture relevant information, providing critical evidence for the early precision diagnosis of diseases and supporting objective assessments of treatment efficacy, thus becoming indispensable tools that drive the scientific advancements in medical decision-making processes [3].

As these technologies continue to advance and expand within the field of PANoptosis research, they are expected to further clarify the underlying mechanisms of PANoptosis. Additionally, they will pave the way for new breakthroughs and provide strategic guidance for innovative therapeutic strategies for various diseases, thereby offering robust support and a solid foundation for the advancement of medical science in related fields.

Ischemic diseases represent a major category of diseases that pose serious threats to human health, presenting major challenges to global public health due to their high incidence, high disability rates, and high mortality rates. While progress has been made in the diagnosis and treatment of ischemic diseases, numerous critical challenges in clinical practice remain to be addressed. PANoptosis, a recently identified form of PCD, plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of ischemic diseases.
