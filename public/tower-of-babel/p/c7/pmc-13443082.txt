Association of triglyceride–cholesterol–body weight index with in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with heart failure
Authors: Yachen Xie, Ke Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Malnutrition and metabolic abnormalities are common in patients with heart failure (HF) requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission and are associated with poor outcomes. This study evaluated the association between triglyceride–cholesterol–body weight index (TCBI) and in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with HF and developed a machine learning–based model to assess its predictive value. This multicenter retrospective study included 1,537 HF patients in a derivation cohort and 3,537 patients in an external validation cohort. Kaplan–Meier, Cox regression, and restricted cubic spline analyses were used to assess the association and nonlinearity. An XGBoost model integrating TCBI and clinical variables was developed for risk prediction. Among 1,537 patients in the derivation cohort, 295 (19.2%) died during hospitalization. Mortality decreased across increasing TCBI tertiles (22.6% vs. 18.4% vs. 16.6%, P = 0.043). Patients in the highest tertile had lower mortality risk than those in the lowest (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.39–0.85). A nonlinear inverse association was observed (P for nonlinearity = 0.001) and confirmed in the external cohort (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.59–0.94). The model achieved area under the curve values of 0.791, 0.744, and 0.709 in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, outperforming SAPS II and SOFA. Decision curve analysis indicated superior net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. Lower TCBI was independently associated with higher in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with HF. Incorporating TCBI improved risk prediction and may aid early risk stratification in this high-acuity population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54399-y.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents a clinical manifestation of advanced cardiovascular disease, and its incidence and mortality rates continue to rise worldwide, placing a heavy burden on patients’ quality of life and healthcare systems1,2. Despite continuous advances in pharmacological therapies and device-based interventions in recent years, the mortality rates among patients with HF remain high3,4. Current estimates suggest that more than 64 million individuals worldwide are living with HF. With population aging and improved survival from cardiovascular diseases, the prevalence and incidence of HF continue to increase, resulting in a substantial healthcare and economic burden5. Hence, the early recognition of patients at elevated risk and the refinement of risk stratification systems are vital for individualized clinical management.

Metabolic and nutritional factors are closely associated with clinical outcomes in patients with HF, particularly in critically ill or hospitalized populations6. Chen et al. found that lower prognostic nutritional index was associated with higher all-cause mortality and adverse events in HF patients7. A study by Shi et al. involving elderly patients with HF who underwent left ventricular assist device implantation found that patients with a higher preoperative risk of malnutrition experienced worse clinical outcomes during follow-up, further highlighting the important role of nutritional status in the risk stratification of patients with HF8. Furthermore, a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Fărcaș et al. showed that an elevated Controlling Nutritional Status score was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality in both acute and chronic HF9. These findings suggest that malnutrition and metabolic abnormalities are important risk factors for poor prognosis in HF.

Dyslipidemia is also closely linked to the development and progression of HF, particularly in hospitalized and critically ill patients. Through Mendelian randomization analysis, Dai et al. provided evidence supporting a potential causal link between lipid levels and HF risk, indicating that disturbances in lipid metabolism may contribute to the pathogenesis of HF10. A large-scale prospective cohort study showed that diet-related lipid metabolism factors are closely associated with cardiovascular disease and mortality risk11. The TCBI is a comprehensive indicator that combines lipid levels and body weight to assess overall nutritional and metabolic status, which may be particularly relevant in acutely ill or critically ill patients with HF12. TCBI has been linked to frailty assessment and postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation13. Furthermore, Wang et al. reported that TCBI was significantly associated with mortality in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy14. However, the prognostic value of TCBI in HF, especially in critically ill and ICU populations, remains limited, particularly due to the lack of large cohort studies and external validation.

In conclusion, further multicenter studies are needed to confirm the association between TCBI and in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with HF and to validate its generalizability in this high-acuity population. Constructing interpretable machine learning–based risk prediction models incorporating clinical characteristics and key laboratory indicators may improve the accuracy and clinical utility of prognostic risk stratification in HF. Our objective was to assess the relationship between TCBI and in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with HF, develop the model in an ICU-based internal cohort and validate it in an independent external cohort. Meanwhile, machine learning approaches were applied to evaluate the value of TCBI in comprehensive risk prediction, providing evidence to support clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study data sources

Data for this study were sourced from two publicly available ICU databases: MIMIC-IV database and the eICU Collaborative Research Database. The MIMIC-IV database, developed through collaboration between the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) Computational Physiology Laboratory and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, provides comprehensive clinical information on patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) between 2008 and 2022. The database includes a wide range of clinical data, such as demographic characteristics, diagnostic information, laboratory measurements, vital signs, and hospital outcomes15,16. One of the authors (Ke Tong; certification number: 67127076) completed the required training and obtained access to the database.

The eICU database served as an external cohort to validate the findings, and it includes clinical data from ICU patients across multiple hospitals in the United States between 2014 and 2015, including demographic information, laboratory indicators, treatment measures, and clinical outcomes17. Both databases were rigorously de-identified and approved by institutional review boards, making them suitable for research purposes. As the data were anonymized, informed consent was waived. The MIMIC-IV cohort was used for model development, and the eICU cohort for external validation.

Study population

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with HF based on ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes; and (2) first admission to the ICU. Only the first ICU admission was considered for patients with multiple admissions. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) ICU stay < 24 h; and (3) missing key variables required for TCBI calculation, including triglycerides, total cholesterol, or body weight. The final study cohort therefore consisted of critically ill patients with HF admitted to the ICU (Fig. 1). Data collection and variables

Data were extracted using Structured Query Language (SQL) via PostgreSQL18, and variables with > 30% missing values were excluded.

Demographic variables included age, gender, and body weight. Comorbid conditions considered in this study included sepsis, hypertension, acute kidney injury (AKI), acute myocardial infarction (AMI), chronic kidney disease (CKD), liver cirrhosis, stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and diabetes. Vital signs measured at ICU admission included heart rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation (SpO₂). Disease severity was evaluated using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Treatments administered during the ICU stay, including mechanical ventilation and vasoactive drug use, were also recorded. Laboratory tests included complete blood count, biochemical markers, and coagulation parameters. Hematological parameters included hemoglobin, red blood cell count (RBC), white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC). Biochemical parameters included albumin, blood glucose, electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride, and calcium), and anion gap. Liver and kidney function indicators included creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and total bilirubin. Additionally, blood gas parameters (pH, PCO₂, and PO₂) and coagulation parameters (INR, PT, and APTT) were collected. Lipid-related parameters included total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). For variables with multiple measurements, the first value recorded after ICU admission was used for analysis to minimize bias from repeated measurements.

Definition of TCBI and outcome

Nutritional and metabolic status was evaluated using TCBI, which was calculated as triglycerides (mg/dL) × total cholesterol (mg/dL) × body weight (kg) / 100019,20. Patients were grouped into TCBI tertiles to compare clinical features and outcomes, with in-hospital mortality—defined as death during the same hospitalization—as the primary endpoint. All outcome information was obtained from hospitalization outcome records in the database. Length of hospital stay was also recorded and used for survival analysis.

Model development and validation

Prediction models were developed in the primary cohort to evaluate in-hospital mortality risk. First, candidate variables were screened to reduce redundancy and improve model stability. Initial feature selection was performed using LASSO regression, followed by Boruta to identify key outcome-associated variables. Variables retained after feature selection were used for subsequent model construction.

The primary cohort was randomly divided into training (70%) and test (30%) sets. An XGBoost model was developed in the training set using selected variables, with hyperparameters optimized via five-fold cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the ROC curve (AUC). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to quantify each feature’s contribution to individual and overall model predictions. After model development, its stability and generalizability were further evaluated using an independent external validation cohort. Model performance was assessed using ROC curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the potential clinical utility of the model.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0). For variables with missing values, multiple imputation was used to reduce potential bias caused by missing data. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, which generated several imputed datasets, and the results were pooled after analysis21,22. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR) and compared using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests.

Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to compare survival, and Cox regression was applied to assess the association between TCBI and in-hospital mortality. According to previous literature and clinical relevance23, stepwise adjustment models were established. Model 0 included TCBI only. Model 1 adjusted for age, gender, and comorbidities, including sepsis, hypertension, AKI, liver cirrhosis, stroke, CKD, AMI, COPD, and diabetes. Model 2 further adjusted for laboratory variables, including heart rate, SBP, DBP, respiratory rate, SpO₂, hemoglobin, platelet count, RDW, WBC, albumin, anion gap, electrolytes (calcium, sodium, potassium), blood glucose, arterial blood gas parameters (PCO₂, pH, PO₂), coagulation parameters (PT, APTT), liver and renal function parameters (ALT, total bilirubin, creatinine and blood urea nitrogen). Model 3 further adjusted for disease severity scores and treatment measures, including SOFA score, SAPS II, GCS, mechanical ventilation, and vasoactive drug use. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) prior to regression analysis24, and the results are presented in Fig. S2 A-B. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis explored potential nonlinear associations between TCBI and in-hospital mortality, and subgroup analyses tested the stability of this relationship. To account for the potential competing risk of discharge alive, competing risk analysis was performed using the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model. Model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and AUC, compared with conventional severity scores, and DCA assessed net clinical benefit across thresholds. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Study population characteristics

A total of 1,537 patients from the MIMIC database were ultimately included. The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 19.2%. Patients were divided into three groups according to TCBI tertiles: T1 (TCBI < 806.27, n = 513), T2 (806.27 ≤ TCBI < 1520.47, n = 512), and T3 (TCBI ≥ 1520.47, n = 512).

Several baseline characteristics differed significantly among the TCBI groups. Patients with higher TCBI levels were younger and had higher body weight than those with lower TCBI levels. DBP was also significantly elevated in the high TCBI group. Regarding comorbidities, the incidence of sepsis and diabetes increased with higher TCBI levels, whereas liver cirrhosis and stroke were more prevalent in the low TCBI group. In terms of laboratory parameters, hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelet count, RBC, and albumin levels increased progressively with higher TCBI levels. Lipid-related parameters, including LDL-C and total cholesterol, were significantly higher in the high TCBI group. In addition, several metabolic and coagulation-related indicators, including glucose, sodium, PCO₂, pH, PT, and INR, differed significantly among the TCBI groups. Regarding disease severity, patients in the highest TCBI group had significantly lower SAPS II compared with the other two groups. The proportion of ventilation also differed significantly among the TCBI groups. Association between TCBI and in-hospital mortality

Cox regression analysis demonstrated that higher TCBI was independently associated with lower in-hospital mortality. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each standard deviation (SD) increase in TCBI was associated with a 25% reduction in mortality risk (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.57–0.99), with consistent results observed across Models 0–2 (Table 2). When analyzed as a categorical variable, patients in the highest TCBI tertile (T3) had a significantly lower risk of in-hospital mortality compared with those in the lowest tertile (T1) (Model 3: HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.44–0.84), whereas no significant difference was observed between T2 and T1. RCS analysis revealed an L-shaped relationship between TCBI and in-hospital mortality, with mortality risk declining sharply at lower TCBI levels and leveling off at higher values (P < 0.001; P-nonlinearity = 0.001) (Fig. 2A). We evaluated the association between TCBI and in-hospital mortality as well as the generalizability of the model in the eICU database. This cohort included 3,537 patients, with an in-hospital mortality rate of 15%. Discussion

This study examined the association between TCBI and in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with HF requiring ICU admission, showing that lower TCBI levels were linked to higher mortality risk. This relationship persisted even after sequential adjustments for demographic factors, comorbid conditions, vital signs, laboratory measurements, and disease severity scores. The persistence of this association after adjustment for established severity and nutritional indicators suggests that TCBI may provide additional prognostic information beyond conventional clinical measures in this high-acuity population. Additionally, RCS analysis suggested a possible nonlinear association, highlighting a possible nonlinear relationship between TCBI and prognosis in critically ill patients with HF. In addition, machine learning analysis showed that the predictive model integrating TCBI with clinical variables demonstrated good discriminative performance in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, and exhibited favorable clinical utility in ROC and decision curve analysis. Overall, these findings provide new evidence for risk stratification in critically ill patients with HF, suggesting that TCBI may serve as a simple and effective prognostic indicator that reflects integrated metabolic and nutritional status beyond conventional clinical measures.

Malnutrition is a common and clinically significant problem in HF patients. Previous studies have demonstrated that HF patients are prone to negative energy balance and increased protein catabolism due to chronic inflammation, gastrointestinal malabsorption, and neuroendocrine activation, which ultimately leads to weight loss and deterioration of nutritional status25,26. Recent evidence suggests that the prevalence of malnutrition among outpatients with HF can exceed 50% and is closely associated with increased hospital readmission rates27. In addition, patients with HF frequently develop cardiac cachexia and sarcopenia. A systematic review including 21 studies and more than 60,000 patients with HF reported that the overall prevalence of sarcopenia was approximately 31%, and sarcopenia was significantly associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes28. These findings suggest that weight loss not only reflects impaired nutritional status but may also indicate disease progression and metabolic disturbances. Metabolic abnormalities in patients with HF typically include increased protein catabolism, altered lipid oxidation, and reduced efficiency of skeletal muscle energy utilization29,30. Elevated levels of chronic inflammation and proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α, can further promote muscle catabolism and inhibit protein synthesis, ultimately resulting in reduced muscle mass and impaired muscle function31,32. Therefore, changes in body weight may not only reflect a patient’s nutritional reserves but also indicate disease severity and overall metabolic status. In critically ill HF, body weight may be influenced by fluid retention and volume overload, and therefore does not exclusively reflect nutritional status. However, given that key indicators of disease severity and nutritional status were already adjusted for in the present study, the prognostic value of TCBI is unlikely to be fully explained by body weight or nutritional status alone. Instead, as a composite index integrating triglycerides, total cholesterol, and body weight, TCBI may reflect a more comprehensive metabolic–nutritional state, capturing multidimensional alterations in energy balance, inflammation, and metabolic reserve in patients with critically ill HF.

In the present study, the observed inverse association between TCBI and in-hospital mortality may be partly consistent with the “cholesterol paradox” described in critically ill HF. Recently, an increasing number of studies have reported a so-called “cholesterol paradox” between lipid levels and prognosis in patients with HF33. Multiple observational studies have demonstrated that lower levels of cholesterol are associated with an increased risk of mortality in patients with HF. In patients with chronic HF, a follow-up decrease of ≥ 10% in total cholesterol was linked to a markedly higher risk of all-cause mortality34. Likewise, HF patients with LDL-C levels ≤ 100 mg/dL exhibited an elevated risk of death compared with those with higher LDL-C35. These findings suggest that lower cholesterol levels may not simply indicate reduced cardiovascular risk but may instead reflect underlying malnutrition or systemic inflammatory states. This paradoxical phenomenon has been proposed several potential mechanisms. First, cholesterol serves as a key structural element of cell membranes and is crucial for modulating immune function and inflammatory processes. Lower cholesterol levels may impair the body’s ability to neutralize endotoxins, thereby exacerbating systemic inflammatory responses36,37. Second, patients with HF often exhibit metabolic remodeling and disturbances in energy metabolism, and alterations in lipid metabolism may represent an important manifestation of disease progression38. Furthermore, low cholesterol levels may be closely associated with weight loss and malnutrition, thereby indirectly reflecting disease severity. Therefore, by integrating lipid parameters with body weight, TCBI may reflect multidimensional alterations in metabolic reserve, inflammation, and nutritional status, which may partly account for its observed prognostic association. Importantly, these findings likely reflect reverse epidemiology in critically ill patients, in whom short-term outcomes are more strongly influenced by metabolic reserve and overall physiological status than by long-term atherosclerotic risk. Thus, the role of lipid levels in this setting should be interpreted within the specific pathophysiological context of acute and critical illness. Accordingly, low TCBI is more likely to reflect underlying metabolic and nutritional derangements rather than a direct therapeutic target, and should be interpreted as a risk marker rather than a modifiable treatment target.

TCBI, which integrates nutritional and metabolic parameters, has shown potential as a prognostic marker in different cardiovascular patient populations. Among patients receiving coronary artery bypass grafting, reduced TCBI levels have been linked to a higher incidence of postoperative atrial fibrillation39. In structural heart disease, evidence indicates that TCBI may serve as a predictor for mortality and adverse cardiovascular events in individuals undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement40. Similarly, TCBI has shown prognostic value in predicting mortality among patients with acute coronary syndrome, myocardial infarction, and critically ill cardiovascular conditions requiring mechanical circulatory support41–43. However, studies evaluating TCBI specifically in patients with HF remain relatively limited. Earlier studies have indicated that reduced TCBI levels are closely linked to a higher risk of long-term mortality in patients with acute decompensated HF44. Additionally, a recent post hoc analysis of a prospective cohort confirmed the prognostic significance of TCBI in acute HF patients45. Similarly, we found that lower TCBI levels were linked to poorer outcomes in critically ill HF, highlighting its potential utility as a prognostic marker for risk assessment. Notably, our findings extend previous evidence by demonstrating that TCBI remains independently associated with short-term outcomes even after comprehensive adjustment, suggesting that it may provide incremental prognostic value beyond traditional risk factors. Clinically, identification of low TCBI may help identify high-risk patients and may prompt further clinical evaluation and comprehensive management in critically ill patients with HF.

Unlike conventional statistical methods, machine learning can manage high-dimensional datasets and capture complex nonlinear patterns, offering distinct advantages for building clinical prediction models46. In the field of HF, machine learning models have been increasingly applied to predict various clinical outcomes, including mortality, readmission, and disease progression. By integrating multiple clinical features and laboratory parameters, machine learning approaches may enable more accurate individualized risk stratification47,48. Then, we constructed a predictive model using machine learning that incorporated TCBI alongside various clinical variables. The model exhibited good discriminative ability in ROC analysis and provided considerable net clinical benefit according to DCA. Notably, compared with conventional risk scores, the machine learning model incorporating TCBI showed improved predictive performance, suggesting that nutritional and metabolic indicators may provide important additional value in critically ill HF risk assessment. Furthermore, feature importance analysis indicated that TCBI contributed to predicting adverse outcomes in patients with critically ill HF. This finding suggests that metabolic–nutritional status captured by TCBI provides complementary prognostic information within the overall severity-based prediction framework. Therefore, this model should be interpreted as a risk stratification tool reflecting overall illness severity in critically ill HF patients, rather than a disease-specific prognostic instrument.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective observational study, and residual confounding cannot be completely excluded despite multivariable adjustment. Although AMI was adjusted for and explored in subgroup analyses, residual confounding related to underlying ischemic heart disease may persist. Second, the analysis was based on a clinical database, and some potential confounders, such as dietary factors, body composition, and certain inflammatory markers, were not available. Third, only baseline TCBI was evaluated, and dynamic changes over time were not considered, which may introduce time-related bias and limit the assessment of evolving metabolic and nutritional status. Future studies incorporating longitudinal measurements of TCBI are warranted to better clarify its temporal relationship with clinical outcomes. Fourth, exclusion of patients with missing TCBI components may have introduced selection bias and affected generalizability. In addition, HF diagnosis was based on ICD codes without echocardiographic, natriuretic peptide, or hemodynamic confirmation, which may result in diagnostic misclassification, particularly in critically ill patients with overlapping acute conditions. Recent literature has also highlighted that HF defined by administrative coding is subject to substantial phenotypic heterogeneity and potential misclassification, reflecting broader conceptual limitations of ICD-based phenotyping, particularly in HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) populations49. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted within the context of an ICD-defined critically ill HF cohort rather than a strictly pathophysiologically confirmed HF population. Although this approach is widely used in large critical care databases, any potential misclassification is likely to be non-differential. Furthermore, as a composite index, TCBI integrates triglycerides, total cholesterol, and body weight, which may reflect heterogeneous and partially independent biological signals rather than a single unified mechanism. Finally, this study focused on critically ill patients with HF in the ICU, and further prospective and multicenter studies are needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings in broader populations.

Conclusion

In summary, our study found that TCBI levels are significantly associated with in-hospital mortality risk in critically ill HF patients, with lower TCBI indicating a higher likelihood of adverse outcomes. Machine learning–based analyses further highlighted the potential of TCBI as a predictor of poor prognosis. Owing to its simplicity, availability, and affordability, TCBI could serve as a practical tool for early risk stratification and may help support comprehensive management strategies, including nutritional support, optimization of critically ill HF therapy, and control of inflammatory conditions.
