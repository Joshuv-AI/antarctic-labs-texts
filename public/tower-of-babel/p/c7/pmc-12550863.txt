Association Between Weight Stigma Experiences in Healthcare and Self‐Reported Healthcare Avoidance in a National Sample
Authors: Kathleen M Robinson, Aaron M Scherer, Alithea N Zorn, Michelle A Mengeling, Helena H Laroche
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesAlthough weight stigma in healthcare is common, existing measures may not capture the full range of patients' weight stigma experiences in clinical practice. We aimed to examine how physical examination by healthcare providers, communication, and the built environment perpetuate weight stigma and impact healthcare avoidance. Items from the previously developed Weight Stigma in Healthcare Inventory (WSHCI) were tested to assess weight stigma experiences and assess association with healthcare avoidance.MethodsTwo sample groups, identified through ResearchMatch and a Qualtrics survey panel, completed the WSHCI online. We analyzed the association between individual items and healthcare avoidance, and between a summation of weight stigma experiences and healthcare avoidance, adjusting for demographics.ResultsFive‐hundred twenty four surveys were received. All weight stigma items were associated with healthcare avoidance, with the strongest associations found for items related to experiencing stigma during physical examination. Greater numbers of stigmatizing experiences were associated with healthcare avoidance (OR 1.31, 95% CI [1.26, 1.37]).ConclusionsTo reduce healthcare avoidance due to weight stigma, all aspects—the physical examination, communication, overall experiences and the built environment—need to be addressed. Reducing the overall number of negative experiences and reducing weight stigma experiences during physical examination may be especially important to reduce healthcare avoidance.Keywords: healthcare access, questionnaire, weight stigma

Introduction

Weight stigma is defined as “the social devaluation and denigration of individuals because of their excess body weight, and can lead to negative attitudes, stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination.” [1] Individuals with obesity encounter weight stigma across a wide array of contexts, including healthcare environments [2]. In one study, 69% of respondents in a weight loss support group reported experiencing weight stigma from a doctor at least once [3]. In a study among Weight Watchers participants, at least 41% reported such experiences [4]. These encounters with weight stigma — along with internalized weight bias—were associated with avoidance of medical professionals, delays in care, reduced cancer screenings [5], depression, and psychological distress [6, 7]. Weight stigma impairs patients' ability to lose weight and maintain weight loss [8] and is associated with binge eating behavior [9, 10] and reduced physical activity [11].

In a 2022 study of 2380 primary care patients, researchers used a conceptual path model to test the relationship between body mass index (BMI) and delayed health care utilization and doctor shopping, as mediated by stigmatizing experiences in healthcare, patient‐centered communication, and perceived respect from one's primary care provider (as assessed by a separate measure) [12]. The study included questions on cruel weight‐related remarks, blaming unrelated physical problems on weight, unwanted diet recommendations, and healthcare providers saying weight is a health problem when the patient was in good health (Sean Phelan, Ph.D., email, March 13, 2025). Stigmatizing experiences were associated with lower perceived respect, delayed care, and doctor shopping [12]. While this study provided invaluable insight into how weight stigma in healthcare communication may negatively impact the utilization of medical services, it did not examine other potentially stigmatizing aspects of care. Physical examinations and built environment likely influenced healthcare avoidance, but this relationship remains understudied.

Negative experiences during physical examination may be highly contributory to weight stigma. Using a U.S. healthcare research panel managed by Qualtrics, Mensinger et al. developed a conceptual model for the relationship between higher BMI and healthcare avoidance. The authors found that general experiences of weight stigma (including one question related to healthcare experiences) were associated with increased body‐related shame, which was associated with greater healthcare stress and ultimately healthcare avoidance [13]. Based on these findings, it was anticipated that stigmatizing experiences during physical examination may contribute to fewer medical visits [14, 15].

The built environment may also play a substantial role in the perpetuation of weight stigma. While many current surveys on weight stigma ask about the availability of medical equipment (e.g., blood pressure cuffs, gowns), they do not include items on accessibility (transportation, furniture). Ethnographic studies show that for people with obesity, “misfitting” in daily life is a distressing event that can heighten felt stigma [16]. Lack of accommodation for size in medical environments may be a significant, underrecognized concern [17].

Surveys conducted among patients with overweight or obesity show variable frequency and intensity of weight stigma experiences in healthcare [3, 12, 18, 19]. This may be due to variability in clinical populations or measures used. It is possible that current survey tools do not consistently capture the full range of stigmatizing experiences in clinical practice.

The authors previously compiled available weight stigma surveys, generated new questions, and conducted cognitive interviews with 48 patients to develop the Weight Stigma in Healthcare Inventory (WSHCI) [20]. The WSHCI expands on prior measures [3, 18, 19, 21] by including questions related to physical examinations, the appropriateness of facility/clinic medical equipment, and the accessibility of the built medical environment. In developing the WSHCI, the study team emphasized measuring specific events that contribute to the experiences of weight stigma and treated them as exposures, rather than conceptualizing weight stigma as a unified construct. Drawing on survey methodologies from sociology, and education research [22], the team created an inventory rather than a scale. The goal was to provide healthcare institutions with a flexible set of items capturing a range of practices and events that may perpetuate weight stigma. Healthcare institutions may choose to adopt as many or as few items as needed to assess exposure to stigmatizing events, and identify opportunities for change in clinical practice.

In prior work, face and content validity of the WSHCI were established, but healthcare avoidance was not examined. This study aimed to identify which aspects of healthcare patients find stigmatizing, and to assess associations between weight stigma experiences and self‐reported healthcare avoidance. The researchers hypothesized that stigma during physical examination and within the built environment would be associated with avoidance, and that a greater number of stigmatizing experiences would be associated with self‐reported healthcare avoidance. Evidence supporting these hypotheses may encourage healthcare organizations to use the WSHCI to identify where weight stigma reduction interventions may be most effective, a significant need in the field [23].

Materials and Methods

Procedure

The study team programmed the WSHCI into the Qualtrics online survey platform and recruited two sample groups to take the survey. In 2020, the study team obtained an online sample group through ResearchMatch, a nonprofit organization partnering researchers with over 155,000 volunteers recruited primarily through their website and participating medical centers across the U.S. [24]. Initially 3000 participants were emailed and after approximately 1 month, they were sent one reminder email. The study was advertised as investigating whether individuals with high body weight experience stigma in healthcare settings. The study team received 136 responses.

The second group was a stratified random sample of respondents with Qualtrics Research Services (Qualtrics) utilizing their online survey panels in 2021. Qualtrics employed a probability‐weighted random process to select panel members. Inclusion criteria were U.S. residence, age 18+, English‐speaking, and BMI 25 kg/m2 or higher. Per the study team's specifications, Qualtrics recruited a sample approximately evenly divided between males and females (with an additional 1%–2% unspecified sex), age groups (18–34, 35–54, and 55+), and rural, urban, and suburban respondents. The sample was designed to approximate the U.S. population with regard to race/ethnicity. Qualtrics panel respondents received incentives/cash honorarium provided by Qualtrics, with variable compensation per company protocol, averaging a value of $5.60 per respondent. The researchers received 395 responses from the second sample (unknown how many panel participants contacted) for a total of 531.

This study was approved by the University of Iowa IRB (10/17/2019, IRB# 201910736).

Measures

The WSHCI was developed by compiling questions from several pre‐existing tools assessing experiences of stigma in general contexts and in healthcare [3, 18, 19, 21, 25] with the addition of novel items. The study team then conducted cognitive interviews and completed an iterative revision process [20]. This inventory is divided into four sections, including five questions on Overall Experiences of weight stigma, eight questions on Clinical Communication, five questions on the Physical Examination, and six questions on the Built Environment (Supporting Information S1: Supplement 1a). Items were scored on a five‐point scale with options “never” (1), “occasionally” (2), “sometimes” (3),” often” (4), and “always” (5). The survey also offered the response options “prefer not to say” and “not applicable” for some items. Most items asked about negative experiences such that 5 corresponded with a worse experience, but a few items asked about positive experiences. Items were presented in a fixed order.

The researchers assessed self‐reported healthcare avoidance using two questions based on a measure developed by Tipirneni et al. (Supporting Information S1: Supplement 1b) [26, 27]. Each item was scored according to the same five‐point scale. For the outcome measure, a respondent was considered to have experienced healthcare avoidance if they responded “sometimes,” “often,” or “always” to either or both of the two self‐reported healthcare avoidance questions. Those who responded “never” or “occasionally” to both questions were considered to not have experienced healthcare avoidance. The study team chose to dichotomize the outcome measure so that odd ratios could be calculated. Odds ratios are more easily interpretable for hospitals and clinicians as compared to measures of association between two continuous variables.

The survey also asked questions on perceived health status (“In general, how would you describe your health,” with options “poor,” “fair,” “good,” “very good,” and “excellent”) and demographics (age, sex, height/weight, income, education, race/ethnicity, zip code, bariatric surgery status) [22, 28]. Participant residential status was determined using census tracts based on the Rural Urban Commuting Area (RUCA)‐zip code approximation method [29]. RUCA codes were categorized into urban, suburban, and rural based on guidelines from the USDA and the University of Washington [30, 31].

Statistical Analyses

Primary analyses examined the association between individual WSHCI items and self‐reported healthcare avoidance using odd ratios. For negative experiences, the response “never” was used as the reference group and other responses were combined into “sometimes/occasionally” and “always/often.” For positive experiences, the response “always” was used as the reference group and other responses were combined into “often/sometimes” and “occasionally/never.” In both cases, the most positive or least negative response option was used as the referent. The team chose to collapse some response categories because for many items the last response option was rarely used (measure invariance). In these analyses, “Did not respond” was treated as its own category. These respondents were included to show response patterns among non‐respondents, and to better highlight non‐response rates for each item. To assess for confounding factors, the study team repeated these analyses adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, health status, annual income, education, rurality, and history of bariatric surgery. A Benjamini‐Hochberg correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons.

To determine the best method for creating a summation of an individual's experience with weight stigma, responses were assigned a numeric value on a dichotomous and a five‐point scale and two separate summations of weight stigma experiences were calculated based on those values. Using logistic regression, the study team assessed the relationship of each summation of stigmatizing experiences on self‐reported healthcare avoidance and compared predictive performance for the dichotomous and five‐point scale summations to choose a final analysis based on which summation method had the highest AUC (Supporting Information S1: Supplement 2). An adjusted model for the final summation of weight stigma experiences was also analyzed to account for age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, health status, income, education, rurality and history of bariatric surgery. In the summation analyses, researchers assumed that individuals who did not respond did not experience weight stigma. To ensure this assumption did not distort results, the study team performed a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation for the missing responses (Supporting Information S1: Supplement 3). Based on the results of the sensitivity analysis, in the final modeling, it was assumed that respondents who left items blank did not experience weight stigma.

In this study, weight stigma experiences were treated as exposures. The study team did not treat the items as measuring different aspects of the same construct, so Cronbach's alpha was not calculated. Similarly, as the research team was not seeking to create a scale, factor analysis was not performed [22].

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4. Forest plots were created via the forestploter R package (v1.1.3; Dayimu 2025) using R Statistical Software (v4.4.3; R Core Team 2025) [32].

Results

The study team received 531 responses. Of these, seven respondents did not provide outcome data and were excluded from analysis for a total (N) of 524. The survey sample was predominantly non‐Hispanic White (70.4%) and female (58%) with a mean age of 46.4 years (SD 16.4), and a mean BMI of 33.1 kg/m2 (SD 7.6). Most respondents lived in urban areas (79%) with no post‐high school degree (43%). Of the respondents, 35% endorsed poor or fair health and 65% endorsed good, very good, or excellent health. Eight percent of the sample had a history of bariatric surgery (Table 1).

In the item‐by‐item analysis, individuals who experienced the least amount of weight stigma were treated as the reference group (Table 2). On every individual item, respondents reporting stigmatizing experiences were statistically more likely to report healthcare avoidance. This result remained consistent after adjusting for multiple comparisons. For those reporting the most frequent experiences of weight stigma on an item, the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of reporting healthcare avoidance ranged from 1.6 (healthcare provider showing compassion, 95% CI [0.8, 3.1]) to 47.5 (not conducting an adequate physical examination, 95% CI [19.6, 115.6]) compared to those reporting no stigmatizing experiences on those items.

For most of the items, respondents who always experienced that type of weight stigma reported more healthcare avoidance than those who reported sometimes or occasionally experiencing that type of weight stigma. However, for items asking about positive experiences (items that were reverse‐scored, marked with “*” in Table 2), this relationship was not consistent. The most frequently reported stigmatizing experiences included: lack of compassion, too much emphasis on weight loss, feeling looked down on, not feeling able to speak freely. For the majority of items, the non‐response rate was less than 10%. However, for two items related to the built environment, an item on healthcare providers bringing up weight, and an item on healthcare providers showing compassion, the study team had more non‐responders (marked with # in Table 2), which is explored in the Discussion. The results of the sensitivity analysis using imputed data did not substantially differ from the primary analyses (Supporting Information S1: Supplement 3).

Dichotomizing responses on a two‐point scale and modeling the summation as a total number of experiences resulted in higher predictive performance (AUC = 0.91) relative to the five‐point scale (AUC = 0.89) approach that placed higher value on the frequency of stigmatizing experiences, though both approaches performed well (Supporting Information S1: Supplement 2). Among survey respondents, the median number of stigmatizing experiences was 9 of 24 possible experiences (1 point for each question; Figure 1). The odds of reporting healthcare avoidance increased for participants as the total number of weight stigmatizing experiences increased (OR: 1.31, 95% CI [1.26, 1.37]; Table 3). In the adjusted analyses, increasing total number of weight stigmatizing experiences also resulted in greater odds of reporting health care avoidance (aOR: 1.34, 95% CI [1.26, 1.42]) after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, race/ethnicity, health status, income, education, rurality, and history of bariatric surgery.

Discussion

The results of this study reaffirm that weight stigma is associated with healthcare avoidance and add that certain stigmatizing aspects of the physical exam are also associated with healthcare avoidance.

The highest adjusted ORs for self‐reported healthcare avoidance were associated with two questions in the WSHCI physical exam section. One of these questions was related to healthcare providers not performing an adequate physical examination due to weight, and the other related to healthcare providers expressing distaste with the respondent's weight during physical examination. These were novel items developed by the research team. Medical encounters, especially those centered on stigmatized conditions such as obesity, can be a source of shame for patients [13]. The physical examination is a portion of the medical encounter during which patients are especially exposed and vulnerable [14, 33], which may make it a uniquely important area for reducing weight stigma.

Several authors have suggested that negative experiences weighing can be problematic in fostering healthcare avoidance [34, 35]. Results of the current study showed that while stigmatizing experiences with weighing were associated with increased adjusted odds of healthcare avoidance, this was not as strongly associated as lack of an adequate exam or the provider expressing distaste during physical examination. This may be due to the especially sensitive nature of physical examination in medical practice, but this finding requires further investigation.

Study authors anticipated that items asking about lack of an accessible built environment/appropriate equipment would also be positively associated with self‐reported healthcare avoidance. Although the OR of self‐reported healthcare avoidance increased with the lack of an accessible built environment/appropriate equipment, the ORs for the items in this section were not as high as the ORs for items related to other topics covered by the WSHCI. Previous qualitative work has shown that the lack of adequately sized gowns, blood pressure cuffs, and other medical equipment is a major barrier to care [36]. Furthermore, access to appropriately sized imaging equipment such as MRI machines can become a critical access concern [37, 38]. The study team suspects that several of the questions on equipment and the built environment did not adequately detect stigmatizing experiences. While most of the questions on the WSHCI asked about negative stigmatizing experiences, five of the six questions in this section asked about positive experiences. Respondents experiencing weight stigma may have accidently selected higher numbers (corresponding with more frequent negative experiences on other questions), and therefore under‐reported negative experiences with equipment/built environment. Study researchers believed this to be the case given that the negatively framed questions showed a “dose response” relationship with healthcare avoidance (more stigma associated with more healthcare avoidance in a graded fashion). However, the positively framed questions (that were reverse‐scored) did not consistently show this relationship. In survey research, it is a common practice to include both positively and negatively framed questions [39]. This finding requires further investigation. In keeping with standard research practice, positively and negatively framed questions may be retained, but the study team will avoid concentrating positively framed questions around one topical area covered by the survey.

Some negative communication experiences were highly associated with self‐reported healthcare avoidance, including providers focusing on weight rather than the presenting concern, making insensitive comments related to weight, and rude non‐verbal communication. Working to enhance patient communication remains a vital means of addressing weight stigma in healthcare [12].

Over 40% of respondents endorsed experiencing some degree of weight stigma in response to the eight WSHCI items. Negative experiences were most reported in response to the statement “Health care providers showed compassion when I talked about how difficult it is to be overweight” (59.9% responding occasionally/never or only often/sometimes). Several other common negative experiences were related to communication, such as healthcare providers talking about weight too much or focusing on weight rather than an important health problem, or not being able to speak freely about weight. Respondents also frequently reported that healthcare providers did not seem to believe in information about eating and exercise habits. Other common negative experiences included feeling looked down on by healthcare providers, feeling pressure to be weighed, or being weighed in a manner that did not respect privacy.

Infrequently reported weight stigma experiences included encountering furniture that was too small or buildings that were inaccessible, a lack of suitable transportation to/from/within the medical environment, and a lack of physical assistance with walking or moving in the clinical environment. The latter two of these questions had high non‐response rates (52.0% and 43.7%, respectively), limiting the research team's ability to draw conclusions. These items previously performed well in cognitive interviews [20]. Survey respondents may not have had these experiences or may have found the question not applicable, but it is also possible that the questions were poorly worded or too lengthy. Additionally, these were the last two items on the survey and participants may have been fatigued. For the question on encountering furniture that was too small or buildings that were inaccessible, the non‐response rate was 11.5%. It was hypothesized that survey respondents did not often encounter inappropriately sized furniture and buildings. This may reflect a promising change in the clinical environment, given that inappropriately sized furniture has been previously reported [40, 41].

The median number of negative experiences was 9 (of 24 possible weight stigma experiences). Although it is difficult to directly compare between measures, our respondents appeared to endorse a greater number of weight stigma experiences as compared to other samples from non‐treatment seeking populations [12, 18, 42]. This finding may be due to weight loss seeking patients being more likely to take our survey. However, the high number of reported weight stigma experiences may also be due to the study's inclusion of a greater range of potentially stigmatizing experiences and a higher number of items as compared to other surveys.

Consistent with previous literature [4, 13], the study team found that having more experiences of weight stigma in healthcare was associated with healthcare avoidance in unadjusted and adjusted analyses. In the study analyses, dichotomizing the responses to each question yielded the model with the greatest association between the number of weight stigma experiences and healthcare avoidance. Study authors concluded that the frequency with which respondents endorsed having a particular weight stigma experience (e.g., often, always, sometimes) may have been less important for understanding healthcare avoidance than the number of different weight stigma experiences. This suggests that it may be important to ask respondents a wide array of questions related to weight stigma to fully capture such negative experiences. The study team anticipates that questions from the survey may be utilized in combination to generate a more comprehensive understanding of weight stigma experiences in healthcare.

Strengths of the study include the use of a sample from the general population rather than restricting respondents to those from weight loss support groups or bariatric surgery or weight loss clinics. A greater proportion of men were included in the study, whereas prior studies examining weight stigma in healthcare included no men or had fewer than 10% male respondents [4, 18, 19]. The study team also included questions on general experiences in healthcare, communication, physical examination and the medical built environment, allowing for a fine‐grained analysis of weight stigma experiences in healthcare. The current study provided evidence of criterion validity for the WSHCI items by showing an association with self‐reported healthcare avoidance.

Limitations of the study included the use of a sample that was mostly urban, with a high proportion of non‐Hispanic White respondents. There was a high degree of missingness for some items, which may have been due to respondents opting not to answer the questions rather than selecting the “not applicable” option, the order of question presentation, and/or the length of the questions. Our advertising strategy may also have created selection bias toward those experiencing weight stigma. Finally, some of the questions asking about positive experiences may not have performed as well as those asking about negative experiences. The study team anticipates that more work is needed to demonstrate test‐retest reliability and to further demonstrate criterion validity by examining response to WSHCI items compared to other measures of weight stigma in healthcare. Further testing of the survey items is required.

Given the high prevalence of weight stigma in healthcare and its strong association with healthcare avoidance, more work is needed to reduce stigmatizing practices in the healthcare environment. The current study especially pointed to physical examination as a sensitive time during the medical encounters, when weight stigma experiences may have had a particularly pernicious effect. The most common weight stigma experiences, which must also be addressed, included inappropriate communication, and weighing practices. Negative experiences with the built environment may not be as common or detrimental, but more research is needed to confirm this finding given that some of these items performed poorly.

Further work is needed to analyze the relationship between weight stigma in healthcare and healthcare avoidance in a more diverse group, and to determine whether certain demographic groups are more likely to experience weight stigma in healthcare. In a future data collection, the study team plans to determine how weight stigma experiences are associated with actual healthcare utilization patterns as determined through chart review. The study team anticipates utilizing questions from the WSHCI will help to establish the prevalence of weight stigma experiences within individual healthcare systems, establish the extent to which weight stigma experiences correlate with avoidance of healthcare, and allow the research team and others to better develop and evaluate weight stigma reduction measures.

Clinicians and public health practitioners should be aware that weight stigma experiences in clinical practice are common and constitute a significant barrier to healthcare utilization. More work on weight stigma in clinical practice is urgently needed.
