Gut-microbiota-derived 3-indoleacrylic acid alleviates neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis by inhibiting epithelial necroptosis
Authors: Xiaoliang Dong, Mengwen Wang, Bingxin Wang, Hongli Li, Zeyun Cao, Binbin Li, Xun Zou, Xuan Kong, Huanxin Yang, Yufeng Zhou, Le Zhang, Li-Long Pan, Jia Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a severe inflammatory disease primarily affecting premature infants, characterized by high mortality and morbidity, with limited therapeutic options available. While emerging evidence links microbiota to NEC pathogenesis, the role of commensal metabolites in NEC and safe metabolite-based therapies remain unexplored. Metabolomic profiling reveals a significant reduction in 3-indoleacrylic acid (IA), a tryptophan-derived metabolite, in NEC infants and animal models. IA supplementation alleviates intestinal inflammation and tissue damage. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis reveals the activation of STAT1 signaling as a necroptosis driver in intestinal epithelial cells, which is suppressed by IA. Protection is abolished by aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) inhibitor CH-223191 or in intestinal epithelium-specific AhR knockout mice. Oral administration of Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis containing the IA-producing fldI gene prevents NEC in mice. Our study uncovers IA as a microbiota-derived therapeutic inhibiting STAT1-driven necroptosis via the AhR-SOCS5 axis, offering a targeted strategy for NEC management.Keywords: necrotizing enterocolitis, 3-indoleacrylic acid, necroptosis, aryl hydrocarbon receptor

Introduction

Neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a severe inflammatory disease characterized by intestinal inflammation and necrosis, with a high mortality rate of 30%–50% in extremely low-birth-weight infants.1,2,3 Current clinical management of NEC is restricted to breastmilk feeding, antibiotics, and surgical resection. However, these treatments primarily focus on symptom management rather than targeting the underlying pathogenic mechanisms.4 The lack of targeted therapies represents a significant limitation in clinical practice, highlighting the urgent need for new treatment strategies.5 Recent advancements suggest that exploring gut commensal metabolites and their role in NEC pathogenesis may offer promising insights for both advancing our understanding of the disease and developing therapeutic approaches.

Microbial metabolites derived from amino acids has attracted increasing attention in NEC research.6 Metagenomic analyses of infant fecal samples have indicated elevated bacterial gene expression related to tryptophan metabolism in NEC, suggesting increased microbial tryptophan utilization.7 Indole derivatives generated through gut microbial tryptophan metabolism8 are crucial for intestinal barrier integrity and mucosal immune regulation.9,10 However, their therapeutic potential in NEC and the mechanisms through which they may confer protection remain unexplored.

The intestinal epithelium acts as a critical barrier against luminal pathogens and toxins. Epithelial damage is a hallmark feature of NEC. Necroptosis emerges as a pivotal mechanism driving epithelial cell death and damage,11,12,13 mediated by key molecules such as receptor-interacting serine/threonine protein kinase 3 (RIPK3) and mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL).14,15,16 This process culminates in membrane rupture, release of inflammatory factors, and exacerbation of tissue injury.17 Understanding the interactions between tryptophan metabolism and necroptosis provides critical insights into NEC pathogenesis.

In the present study, we observed a significant reduction in fecal 3-indoleacrylic acid (IA), a tryptophan-derived metabolite produced by the gut microbiota, in NEC patients and animal models. IA supplementation effectively inhibited intestinal epithelial necroptosis and alleviated NEC symptoms in both NEC mice and rats, a process dependent on epithelial aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) suppressor of cytokine signaling 5 (SOCS5) activation to suppress the signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1)-cylindromatosis (Cyld) axis. Moreover, supplementation with Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis containing the fldI gene, which facilitates IA production, significantly mitigated NEC in mice. These findings highlight IA as a promising therapeutic candidate for NEC intervention through the inhibition of epithelial necroptosis.

Results

The microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolite IA protects against experimental NEC

Given the critical role of microbial metabolites in regulating intestinal homeostasis,18 we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling of fecal samples from NEC patients and experimental NEC models. IA emerged as a consistently and significantly reduced metabolite across all datasets (Figures 1A–1E). Targeted metabolite quantification in the 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid solution (TNBS)-induced mouse NEC model confirmed a marked decrease in IA levels (Figure 1F). We then evaluated the therapeutic potential of IA. IA supplementation exhibited no detectable toxicity (Figure S1) and conferred dose-dependent protection, including improved survival rates (Figure 1G), increased body weight (Figure 1H), decreased disease activity index (DAI) scores (Figure 1I), reduced small intestine shortening (Figure 1J), and diminished histopathological damage (Figure 1K) in NEC mice. Notably, IA treatment surpassed breastfeeding, a standard supportive intervention for clinical NEC, in mitigating key pathological features (Figures 1G–1K). Because the protective effects were comparable at 50 and 75 mg/kg, the lower dose 50 mg/kg was selected for subsequent experiments. Consistent efficacy was also observed in a hypoxia hypothermia formula (HHF)-induced rat NEC model (Figure S2). To determine whether downstream metabolites contributed to the observed benefits, we measured indole-3-propionic acid (IPA). IPA levels remained unaltered (Figure 1L), supporting a direct protective role for IA rather than mediation through downstream conversion. Collectively, these findings identify IA as a robust microbiota-derived metabolite with therapeutic potential for NEC across species and preclinical models.

IA protects against NEC by inhibiting intestinal epithelial necroptosis

To explore the mechanism underlying the protective effects of IA, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of intestinal epithelial cell (IEC) subtypes in neonatal NEC mice was performed (Figure S3). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis identified necroptosis as one of the most significantly upregulated cell death pathways in NEC (Figure 2A). Consistent with this, necroptotic activation was confirmed by markedly elevated phosphorylation of RIPK3 and MLKL (p-RIPK3 and p-MLKL) in the intestines of NEC mice (Figure 2B). Notably, exogenous IA treatment in a dose-dependent manner inhibited the activation of ileal RIPK3 and MLKL in NEC mice (Figure 2C). Furthermore, immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed that exogenous IA supplementation dose-dependently reduced ileal p-RIPK3 expression, which predominantly co-localized with fatty-acid-binding protein 2 (FABP2)-positive IECs (Figure 2D). Similar protective effects against necroptotic activation were observed in the NEC rat model (Figure S4).

To determine whether IA directly inhibits intestinal epithelial necroptosis, we next performed in vitro experiments using MODE-K IECs. TSZ stimulation (tumor necrosis factor α [TNF-α], SM-164, and Z-VAD-FMK) for 6 h robustly induced necroptosis as evidenced by increased p-RIPK3 and p-MLKL levels (Figure 2F). IA exhibited no cytotoxicity at concentrations of 25–100 μM following 24-h exposure (Figure 2G). Consistent with our in vivo observations, pretreatment with IA (50 μM) for 2 h significantly improved cell viability in TSZ-treated cells (Figure 2H) and markedly suppressed TSZ-induced p-RIPK3 and p-MLKL expression (Figure 2I). IF staining further corroborated that IA reduced TSZ-elevated p-RIPK3 level (Figure 2J). Together, these findings demonstrate that IA directly suppresses IEC necroptosis, thereby alleviating NEC pathology.

IA suppresses intestinal epithelial necroptosis by inhibiting STAT1-CYLD pathway

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which IA alleviates intestinal epithelial necroptosis in NEC mice, we reassessed scRNA-seq data from NEC models. Among the top 20 KEGG pathways upregulated in NEC compared with controls, the TNF, nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and Janus kinase (JAK)-STAT signaling pathways were prominently enriched (Figure 3A). Validation experiments showed that phosphorylation of p65 (p-p65) was markedly increased in both the ileum of NEC mice and in TSZ-stimulated IECs. However, IA treatment did not alter p-p65 levels (Figures 3B and 3C), indicating that IA does not act through NF-κB suppression. Subsequently, we shifted our focus to the Stat family. An integrated analysis combining the hTFtarget database with our scRNA-seq data (Figure 3D) revealed that Stat1 exhibited the strongest overall target gene activation among Stat family members, as evidenced by its highest comprehensive score, target gene activation rate, average activation strength, and number of highly activated genes (Figure 3E). We therefore examined STAT1 activation and observed that phosphorylated STAT1 (p-STAT1) was elevated in the ileum of NEC mice but were reduced by IA treatment (Figures 3F and 3G). Consistently, p-STAT1 levels were induced in TSZ-stimulated IECs but significantly inhibited by IA treatment (Figures 3H and 3I). To functionally validate STAT1’s role, we transfected IECs with either wild-type (WT) STAT1 or a constitutively active (Active) STAT1 construct. Notably, transfection with the STAT1(Active), but not the STAT1(WT), abrogated IA-mediated inhibition of necroptosis markers p-RIPK3 and p-MLKL in TSZ-induced cells (Figure 3J). IF staining further confirmed that IA failed to suppress TSZ-induced p-RIPK3 expression in STAT1(Active)-transfected IECs (Figure 3K). These results demonstrate that IA mitigates intestinal epithelial necroptosis by inhibiting the STAT1 signaling, identifying STAT1 as a key molecular target underlying IA’s protective effect in NEC.

To further elucidate how STAT1 activation promotes necroptosis, we performed Cleavage Under Targets & Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) assays for STAT1 on ileal tissues from neonatal mice. The results showed that STAT1 binding was enriched near transcription start sites (TSSs) across all three groups. Compared with controls, the NEC group exhibited significantly increased STAT1 binding, which was markedly reduced by IA treatment (Figure 4A). Approximately 45% of the high-binding-intensity peaks were located in promoter regions, a pattern consistent in both NEC-upregulated and IA-downregulated peaks (Figure 4B). Differential peak analysis revealed 3,304 annotated genes with significantly increased STAT1 binding in the NEC group, whereas no genes showed decreased binding. In contrast, IA treatment significantly reduced STAT1 binding at 973 annotated genes, with only four genes showing increased binding (Figure 4C). KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted necroptosis among both NEC-upregulated and IA-downregulated genes (Figure 4D). Cross-analysis identified key necroptosis-related genes: Ripk3, Cyld, Ripk1, Tnfrsf1a, Spata2, Ticam1, and Hsp90aa1 exhibited increased STAT1 binding in NEC, whereas Ripk3, Cyld, Capn2, Traf5, and Vdac2 showed reduced binding following IA treatment. Notably, Ripk3 and Cyld, a deubiquitinase known to promote necroptosis by facilitating RIPK1 activation,19 were common to both comparisons (Figure 4E). STAT1 was found to bind to the promoter region of Cyld and a distal intergenic region of Ripk3 (Figure 4F). Subsequent qPCR validation confirmed that Cyld mRNA expression was significantly elevated in the NEC group and reduced by IA treatment. In contrast, Ripk3 mRNA levels remained unchanged (Figure 4G), which aligns with our earlier observation that STAT1 activation does not alter RIPK3 protein expression (Figure 3I). Together, these findings suggest that STAT1 promotes necroptosis predominantly through transcriptional upregulation of Cyld, thereby driving necroptotic signaling.

IA mitigates epithelial necroptosis and NEC dependent on AhR-SOCS5 signaling

IA is known to exert beneficial effects through activation of AhR in IECs.20 To determine whether its protective effects against NEC are AhR-dependent, we treated NEC mice with the AhR inhibitor CH-223191 prior to IA administration. CH-223191 reversed the protective effects of IA in NEC mice, as evidenced by increased mortality (Figure S5A), reduced body weight (Figure S5B), shortened small intestine length (Figure S5C), increased DAI score (Figure S5D), and exacerbated histological damage (Figure S5E). Furthermore, CH-223191 reversed IA-mediated reduction of epithelial necroptosis in NEC mice, as demonstrated by increased levels of p-RIPK3 and p-MLKL in the ileum (Figure S5F). IF staining further confirmed that CH-223191 restored p-RIPK3 expression in FABP2-positive IECs despite IA treatment (Figure S5G). Consistent with the in vivo findings, CH-223191 treatment reversed the effects of IA on p-RIPK3 and p-MLKL in TSZ-induced MODE-K IECs (Figures S5H and S5I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that IA inhibits intestinal epithelial necroptosis and alleviates NEC, through epithelial AhR activation.

To elucidate the role of epithelial AhR in mediating the protective effects of IA in NEC, we generated intestinal-epithelial-cell-specific AhR knockout (AhRΔIEC) mice. Compared with WT littermates, AhRΔIEC NEC mice exhibited significantly aggravated disease outcomes, including decreased survival rates (Figure 5A), reduced body weight (Figure 5B), increased DAI scores (Figure 5C), shortened ileal length (Figure 5D), and more severe ileal histological damage (Figure 5E). Notably, the protective effects of IA in NEC were abolished in AhRΔIEC mice, with no improvements in these metrics observed following IA treatment (Figures 5A–5E). However, AAV-mediated reconstitution of AhR expression specifically in IECs restored IA responsiveness, whereas an empty vector control had no effect (Figures 5A–5E). Furthermore, AhRΔIEC mice exhibited elevated necroptosis markers, p-RIPK3 and p-MLKL, compared to WT NEC mice. IA treatment failed to reduce these markers in AhRΔIEC NEC mice (Figure 5F). IF staining corroborated these findings, showing that IA did not reduce p-RIPK3 expression in FABP2-positive IECs in AhRΔIEC NEC mice (Figure 5G). Collectively, these results establish epithelial AhR as an essential mediator of IA-driven suppression of necroptosis and protection against NEC.

To elucidate the mechanistic basis of IA-mediated STAT1 inhibition, we initially investigated its interaction with AhR. Molecular docking using AutoDock Vina predicted a strong binding affinity between IA and AhR, with a binding energy of −7.6 ± 0.1 kcal/mol. PLIP analysis further indicated that IA forms hydrophobic interactions with the Val124, Leu110, and Tyr135 residues of AhR (Figure 6A). This predicted interaction was experimentally validated using a biotin-streptavidin pull-down assay, which confirmed the direct IA-AhR binding in vitro (Figures 6B and 6C).

We next examined the functional relevance of this interaction in vivo by comparing p-STAT1 levels in WT and AhRΔIEC mice. As showed in Figures 6D and 6E, intestinal-epithelial-cell-specific AhR deletion abrogated IA-induced suppression of STAT1 phosphorylation. Subsequent transcriptional profiling of the suppressor of cytokine signaling (SOCS) family members (Socs1-7 and CISH)21 revealed selective downregulation of Socs2, Socs5, and Socs7 in NEC mice, with IA treatment specifically restoring Socs2 and Socs5 expression (Figure 6F). Notably, IA-mediated rescue of Socs5 expression was abolished in AhRΔIEC mice (Figure 6F). Using a dual-luciferase reporter assay, we further demonstrated that AhR activation directly enhances Socs5 promoter activity (Figures 6G and 6H). IF staining corroborated these findings, showing that IA failed to elevate SOCS5 expression in IECs in AhRΔIEC NEC mice (Figure 6I). Notably, IA-mediated upregulation of SOCS5 was also observed in the NEC rat model (Figure S6). Lastly, co-immunoprecipitation experiments revealed a physical interaction between SOCS5 and STAT1 (Figures 6J and 6K). Together, these results demonstrate that IA binds to AhR and promotes the transcription of SOCS5, facilitating SOCS5-STAT1 interaction and thereby suppressing STAT1 activation to exert anti-necroptotic effects in NEC.

An IA-producing probiotic alleviates NEC by inhibiting necroptosis

To explore the therapeutic potential of IA, we referred to the list of bacterial species approved for use in infants by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China (NHC)22 and those granted Qualified Presumption of Safety (QPS) status by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA).23 Based on these safety criteria, we selected a strain of Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis (B. infantis JCM11660) isolated from infant feces. Importantly, genomic analysis showed that this strain lacks key human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs)—utilization genes yet contains the IA-producing gene fldI (Figure 7A), making it well suited for preterm infants with limited HMO intake. For comparison, we also included a commercially available B. infantis strain EVC001 as the reference control. Treatment with B. infantis JCM11660 significantly increased IA levels in intestinal contents (Figure 7B) and attenuated NEC severity in mice, as evidenced by improved survival rate (Figure 7C), increased body weight (Figure 7D), reduced DAI scores (Figure 7E), preserved ileal length (Figure 7F), and improved ileal histological structure (Figure 7G). Notably, JCM11660 outperformed EVC001, particularly by elevating intestinal IA levels (Figure 7B) and achieving greater improvement in DAI scores and ileal histology (Figures 7E and 7G). Critically, co-treatment with the AhR inhibitor CH-223191 abolished the protective effects of B. infantis JCM11660 in NEC mice (Figures 7B–7G). Furthermore, B. infantis JCM11660 significantly reduced p-RIPK3 and p-MLKL levels in NEC mice, and these effects were similarly reversed by CH-223191 (Figures 7H and 7I). Together, these findings demonstrate that B. infantis JCM11660 alleviates NEC by increasing intestinal IA levels and suppressing necroptosis through an AhR-dependent mechanism, supporting its potential as a promising therapeutic strategy for NEC.

Discussion

Our study identifies reduced production of tryptophan metabolite IA and enhanced necroptosis as key contributors to the pathogenesis of NEC. We demonstrated that exogenous IA supplementation alleviates NEC-associated symptoms and mitigates intestinal epithelial necroptosis, primarily through AhR-SOCS5-mediated STAT1 pathway inhibition. Furthermore, we employed a strain of Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis possessing the fldI gene, essential for IA production, which effectively inhibited intestinal epithelial necroptosis and alleviated NEC symptoms. This probiotic represents a promising therapeutic strategy for combating NEC.

Tryptophan metabolites produced by commensal bacteria, particularly indole derivatives, play crucial roles in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and modulating immune homeostasis.8,9,10 However, the specific metabolite with the greatest therapeutic relevant in NEC has remained largely undefined. Our metabolomic analyses revealed a consistent and marked reduction of IA in fecal samples from NEC infants and in intestinal contents of NEC rodent models. This deficiency correlates with disease severity and points to IA as a relevant metabolite whose loss may contribute to NEC pathogenesis. IA supplementation in preclinical models effectively restored intestinal homeostasis, reinforcing its functional importance and supporting its potential as a targeted therapeutic candidate. Prior studies have shown that tryptophan metabolites such as kynurenic acid and IA can alleviate intestinal damage caused by Candida albicans infection in mice by reducing intestinal permeability, enhancing intestinal integrity, and alleviating intestinal inflammation.24 Moreover, IA production has been shown to promote intestinal epithelial barrier function and reduce inflammatory responses.24,25 These findings align closely with the results of our study.

The death of IECs is a hallmark of NEC pathology, and preventing dysregulated cell death is crucial for preserving intestinal integrity and improving patient survival.13 Necroptosis, a regulated form of necrotic cell death first described by Degterev et al. in 2005,26 is characterized by nuclear condensation, cell membrane rupture, and mitochondrial swelling. Emerging evidence has highlighted the occurrence of necroptosis in NEC,14 particularly within IECs. Studies have shown that inhibiting necroptosis either through pharmacological inhibitors or through genetic knockouts reduces pathological damage in NEC mouse models, underscoring the pivotal role of IEC necroptosis in NEC-related tissue injury.14 Consistent with these findings, our results demonstrate that IA effectively mitigates necroptosis-associated tissue damage in NEC.

Tryptophan metabolites, particularly indole derivatives, signal through receptors such as AhR and pregnane X receptor to exert immunomodulatory and barrier-protective effects.27,28 AhR activation is known to mediate immune regulatory responses and maintain intestinal barrier integrity, and ligands such as indole-3-aldehyde have been reported to alleviate intestinal inflammation.10,29,30,31 Here, our findings demonstrate that IA supplementation alleviates NEC-associated symptoms and reduces IEC necroptosis through AhR-dependent mechanisms. Furthermore, our work extends this paradigm by demonstrating that IA binds to AhR and induces SOCS5, a suppressor of cytokine signaling.32 SOCS5 functions as a negative regulator of interleukin (IL)-4-JAK/STAT6 signaling cascade in immune regulation.33 Here, our findings extend this functional repertoire by identifying SOCS5 as a previously unrecognized modulator of STAT1 activity in intestinal epithelium. This aligns with the emerging understanding that SOCS proteins exhibit context-dependent specificity toward distinct STAT family members. For instance, SOCS1 is a potent inhibitor of STAT1 activation in interferon signaling pathways, a mechanism crucial for preventing excessive inflammation,34 whereas cytokine-inducible SH2-containing protein (CISH) primarily targets STAT5.35,36 The SOCS5-STAT1 interaction we describe may therefore represent a previously underappreciated regulatory checkpoint, particularly relevant in inflammatory settings where STAT1-driven gene programs play a dominant pathogenic role. Although alternative necroptosis pathways (e.g., TLR4–RIPK3) may also contribute to NEC, our findings establish the AhR-SOCS5-STAT1 axis as a central mechanism through which IA limits epithelial necroptosis.14

A particularly notable finding is the differential regulatory effect of STAT1 on two key necroptosis-related genes, Cyld and Ripk3. Although CUT&Tag profiling revealed STAT1 binding at both loci, subsequent validation showed that STAT1 activation transcriptionally upregulates Cyld, whereas Ripk3 expression remained unchanged at both the mRNA and protein levels. This discrepancy may reflect the distinct nature of the STAT1 binding sites: STAT1 binds the promoter region of Cyld, which typically confers direct transcriptional control, whereas its binding to a distal intergenic region near Ripk3 may correspond to an enhancer-like element whose activity requires additional co-factors or chromatin states not engaged under our experimental conditions. Furthermore, RIPK3 is primarily regulated post-translationally through phosphorylation rather than through transcriptional induction, which may further explain why STAT1 binding near the Ripk3 locus did not yield measurable changes in expression. Thus, our data support a model wherein STAT1 promotes necroptosis predominantly through transcriptional upregulation of Cyld, rather than through direct modulation of the core necroptotic executers RIPK3 and MLKL.

Within this mechanistic framework, we selected B. infantis JCM11660—a strain from a species approved for infant use by the NHC and granted QPS status by the EFSA, which significantly alleviated NEC pathology and directly mitigated intestinal epithelial necroptosis via IA production. These findings align with the long-standing view of probiotics as a promising strategy to prevent NEC by enhancing intestinal barrier function and restoring microbial homeostasis.4,20,37,38,39 Genetically, this strain carries the fldI gene required for IA synthesis but lacks key HMOs-utilization genes,40 making it particularly suitable for preterm infants with limited HMOs intake and mechanistically distinct from the reference strain B. infantis EVC001, which exerts anti-inflammatory effects primarily through HMO metabolism. Our results demonstrate that JCM11660 protects against NEC through the AhR-SOCS5-STAT1-necroptosis axis, thereby not only validating this signaling cascade but also positioning IA as a central metabolite for developing targeted, mechanism-based probiotic or postbiotic interventions. Although NEC is multifactorial, affected by risk modifiers such as diet-driven dysbiosis, antibiotic exposure,41 and genetic susceptibilities,42 and although metabolic disruptions beyond IA depletion may also contribute (e.g., reduced short-chain fatty acids from Lactobacillus, which exert beneficial effects against NEC via GPCRs43), our probiotic strategy, leveraging fldI-dependent IA production independent of HMOs, represents a clearly defined, mechanistically distinct approach. Its clinical efficacy warrants further investigation within the complex landscape of coexisting microbial pathways in the preterm infant gut.

Collectively, our study delineates a microbial metabolite-host pathway in which IA, acting via AhR–SOCS5, inhibits STAT1-driven CYLD expression and necroptosis, thereby alleviating NEC. These findings establish IA as a central microbial metabolite with therapeutic potential and provide a mechanistic foundation for developing metabolite-based interventions against NEC and related intestinal inflammatory disorders (Figure S7).

Limitations of the study

Our study establishes an original role for gut-microbiota-derived IA and a defined probiotic strain in alleviating NEC through inhibition of epithelial necroptosis. However, several aspects warrant further investigation. First, as with any animal model of human disease, there are inherent limitations in recapitulating the complex and multifactorial etiology of human NEC. Although our findings were robust across complementary rodent models, the clinical efficacy and safety of IA-based intervention must be validated in rigorously designed human studies. Second, the relevance of the IA concentrations used in our murine models to the human neonatal context remains to be fully determined. Although our dosing was effective and well tolerated in mice, future work is needed to characterize the pharmacokinetics and optimal dosing regimen of IA in preterm infants. Finally, while B. infantis JCM 11660 shows promise as a defined probiotic therapeutic, its engraftment stability and efficacy within the dynamically developing gut microbiota of preterm infants require further evaluation. These limitations, common to translational research, identify key directions for future investigation without undermining the mechanistic insights and therapeutic potential demonstrated in the present work.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and cell culture

MODE-K cells (338300, BNCC) were obtained from the BeNa Culture Collection. MODE-K cells were cultured in DMEM (319-005, Wisent) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 04-001-1ACS, Biological Industries) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (ST488S, Beyotime). They were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2. The cell line was authenticated by the supplier using short tandem repeat profiling. All cells were tested monthly via TransDetect PCR Mycoplasma Detection Kit (FM3-11-01, Transgen Biotech) to ensure they were free of mycoplasma.

Animals

Mice: C57BL/6 mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China), intestinal epithelial cell-specific AhR knockout (AhRΔIEC) mice, on a C57BL/6 background, were generated by crossing AhRflox/flox mice with Villincre transgenic mice. Rats: Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China).

All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions the Animal Center of Wuxi School of Medicine, Jiangnan University, with controlled temperature (23°C–25°C) and a 12-h light/dark cycle. For necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) modeling, 10-day-old male and female mouse pups and 2-day-old male and female rat pups were used. Experimental groups were randomized and blinded. Both male and female animals were included; no sex-specific differences were observed. All experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University (JN. No20240330c0481015 [163]) and performed in according with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Clinical fecal samples

Fecal samples from 42 neonates were obtained from the Neonatology Department of Wuxi Children’s Hospital, comprising 20 infants diagnosed with NEC and 22 infants without NEC (samples were allocated to groups based on clinical diagnosis). Prior to sample collection, written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating infants after they were fully informed about the purpose and use of the clinical samples. The study protocol and consent procedure were approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi Children’s Hospital (Approval No. WXCH2021-09-012) and conducted under the supervision of attending physicians.

Method details

Culture and preparation of probiotics

Bifidobacterium longum subsp. Infantis 11660 (BMZ010548, Mingzhou Biotech, Ningbo, China) was cultured anaerobically in modified PYG medium (DSMZ Medium104, Mingzhou Biotech, Ningbo, China) at 37°C for 48 h. The bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 4000×g for 15 min, washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in 0.9% saline to a final concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mL for intragastric administration.39

NEC experimental models

The NEC mouse model was established according to previous studies.44 10-day-old male and female mice were administered 2,4,6-Trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid solution (TNBS) (P2297, Sigma-Aldrich, USA, 50 mg/kg of 30% ethanol, wt/vol, 2 doses) by gavage and enema. Control mice received an equivalent volume of solvent. For rat NEC modeling, we implemented a validated multimodal stress paradigm45 combining: cyclic hypoxic exposure (5% O2/95% N2 mixture) with cold stress (4°C ambient temperature, 10-min duration) following each feeding session, hyperosmolar enteral nutrition using reconstituted formula 15 g Similac 60/40 [Ross Pediatrics] in 75 mL Esbilac [Pet-Ag], 836.8 kJ/kg/day) and progressive feeding regimen initiating at 0.1 mL/feeding with incremental escalation to 0.4 mL/feeding maximum.

To evaluate the effect of IA on NEC, the mouse was orally administered IA at the indicated doses on the 7th day and the rat was orally administered IA on 3rd day.46,47 After four consecutive days treatment, the animals were sacrificed by euthanasia, and tissue samples were collected for subsequent analysis.

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis

Samples were prepared following established protocols.48 Ileal contents (100 mg) were mixed with 400 μL of pre-cooled methanol, vortexed for 30 s, and homogenized with grinding beads at 60 Hz for 5 min. After centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, the supernatant was dried under vacuum and reconstituted in 200 μL of methanol:water (4:1, v/v). Then, centrifuge at 14,000 rpm at 4°C for 15 min. Following filtration through through a 0.22-μm filter, samples were analyzed, with quality control prepared by pooling aliquots of all samples.

scRNA-seq data preprocessing

Raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) were processed and aligned to mouse reference genome using MobiVision software (version 2.1), with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) summarized for each barcode. The resulting UMI matrix was then analyzed using Seurat R package (version 4.0.0).49 To ensure data quality and remove low-quality cells or likely multiplet captures, filtering was performed based on the following criteria: (1) gene numbers (gene numbers <200), (2) UMI (UMI <1000), (3) log10GenesPerUMI (log10GenesPerUMI <0.7), (4) percentage of mitochondrial RNA UMIs (proportion of UMIs mapped to mitochondrial genes >10%) and (5) percentage of hemoglobin RNA UMIs (proportion of UMIs mapped to hemoglobin genes >5%). To further eliminate potential doublets and multiplets, the DoubletFinder package (version 2.0.3)50 was applied. To obtain the normalized gene expression data, library size normalization was implemented by the Seurat’s NormalizeData function. Specifically, the global-scaling normalization method “LogNormalize” normalized the gene expression measurements for each cell by the total expression, multiplied by a scaling factor (10,000 by default), and log-transformed the results.

The Top 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were calculated using the Seurat function FindVariableGenes (mean.function = FastExpMean, dispersion.function = FastLogVMR). Dimensionality reduction was performed using Principal-component analysis (PCA) via RunPCA function. Graph-based clustering of cells was performed using the FindClusters function based on PCA-reduced data. Cell clusters were visualized using a 2-dimensional UMAP algorithm via the RunUMAP function.

Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function with the presto method (test.use = “presto”). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups or clusters were determined using the FindMarkers function (test.use = “presto”), with significance defined as p < 0.05 and |log2foldchange| > 0.58. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs were respectively performed using R (version 4.0.3) using hypergeometric distribution-based methods.

Sequencing and bioinformatics analyses were provided by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Histopathological examination

Ileum tissue (approximately 0.5 cm) adjacent to the cecum were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, processed through graded alcohol dehydration, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4-μm-thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin staining were performed, and slides were imaged using a digital slide scanner (Panoramic, 3DHISTCH, Budapest, Hungary). Histopathological assessment was conducted according to a scoring system utilized in previous studies.44,51,52 This included evaluating crypt loss (0 for normal, 2 for 90%), mucosal edema (0 for normal, 2 for mild, 4 for moderate, 6 for severe), and inflammatory infiltration (0 for normal, 2 for mild, 4 for moderate, 6 for severe). The histological score was determined by summing the scores of these three indicators.

Western blotting

Western blot was performed as previously described.31,53 Ileal tissue was collected, and the intestinal lumen contents were rinsed with cold PBS. The tissue was homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentration was determined via BCA assay (MA0082-2, meilunbio, Dalian, China). Protein samples (25–30 μg) were separated by SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred to nitrocellulose membrane (0.22 μm) and probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, following HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h. The protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescent substrate (Gel imaging Tanon 4600, China) and quantified with ImageJ software (ProteinSimple, USA).

RNA isolation and qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from animal tissue using a Total RNA extraction kit (19202ES60, Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA with an All-in-One RT Premix Kit (R333-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qPCR was carried out using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), with β-actin as the internal control. Gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt quantification method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Cell transfection

Cell transfection was conducted as described in our previous research.53 In brief, MODE-K cells were cultured and transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (L3000015, Invitrogen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Specifically, plasmid DNA and P3000 reagent were mixed in Opti-MEM Medium (31985070, Gibco, Shanghai, China), followed by the addition of Lipofectamine 3000. The mixture was gently combined and incubated at room temperature for 15 min to form the DNA-lipoplexes, which were then added to the cell culture medium. The total amount of DNA was normalized by including an empty control plasmid.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was detected as previously described.53 MODE-K cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 5,000 cells per well and cultured overnight. The cells were then treated with various concentrations (0, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400 μM) of IA for 6, 12, 24, and 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (BMU106, Abbkine, Wuhan, China). A 10-μL aliquot of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubating for 1 h in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer (BIO-TEK, USA).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

IF staining was performed on 4-μm paraffin-embedded sections of distal ileum tissue.54 Sections were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval by heating in sodium citrate buffer at boiling temperature for 1 h. After cooling to room temperature, sections were blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against p-RIPK3 (1:100) and FABP2 (1:100, 67691-1-Ig, Proteintech, China). The following day, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies, goat anti-rabbit cy3 (1:200, ab6939, Abcam, USA) and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:200, ab150077, Abcam, USA), for 1.5 h at room temperature. Finally, the sections were stained with DAPI (C0065, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 15 min, washed, covered with a coverslip, and imaged using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager2, Germany).

For cell staining, we adhered to the established detection protocol.53 MODE-K cells (3×104 cells per well) were seeded onto glass coverslips in a 24-well plate and culture overnight. Following treatment, cells were rinsed thrice with cold PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Cells were blocked with 5% BSA blocking solution for 2 h followed by incubation with the anti-p-RIPK3 primary antibody (1:100) overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature, counterstained with DAPI for 15 min, and washed thoroughly. Coverslips were mounted, and visualized using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager2, Germany).

Systematic screening of STAT transcription factor family

scRNA-seq data from enterocytes in the NEC model were analyzed, comprising gene expression matrices from control groups and disease groups. Raw gene expression counts were normalized using standard scRNA-seq pipeline and log-transformed for subsequent analyses. Comprehensive expression analysis of the Stat transcription factor family (Stat1, Stat2, Stat3, Stat4, Stat5a, Stat5b, Stat6) was conducted using two-sided Student’s t test to assess differential expression between NEC and control conditions. Log2 fold change values were calculated as log2(mean expression in NEC group/mean expression in control group), with statistical significance defined as p-value <0.05.

To evaluate the transcriptional activity of each STAT family member, we performed systematic analysis of their target genes obtained from the hTFtarget database. Curated STAT family target genes were retrieved and standardized to match our expression dataset, retaining only targets present in our scRNA-seq data. The activation status of target genes was evaluated using three complementary criteria: statistical significance (p-value <0.05), expression threshold (minimum mean expression >0.05 in disease group), and activation magnitude (log2FC > 0.5 for positive regulation). A weighted scoring system was developed to quantify transcriptional activation potential: Comprehensive_Activation_Score = (Base_Activation_Rate × 2) + Mean_Activation_Strength + (Highly_Activated_Count × 0.2), where Base_Activation_Rate represents the proportion of target genes meeting all activation criteria, Mean_Activation_Strength represents the average log2FC of significantly activated targets, and Highly_Activated_Count represents the number of targets with strong activation (log2FC > 1).

All computational analyses were performed using Python (v3.10) with SciPy (v1.8.0) for hypothesis testing, Pandas (v1.4.0) for data frame operations, and Matplotlib (v3.5.0) and Seaborn (v0.11.2) for visualization. Multiple testing correction was applied using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate control. The selection of Stat1 for experimental validation was based on convergence of evidence from multiple analytical dimensions: comprehensive target gene analysis revealed Stat1 had the highest activation score, indicating robust transcriptional activity despite its mRNA downregulation; and Stat1 ranked first in both activation rate and comprehensive activation score when compared systematically against all other Stat family members.

CUT&Tag assay

The CUT&Tag assay was performed by Shanghai GeneCowin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. using neonatal mouse ileum tissues from three experimental groups: control, NEC, and IA-treated NEC. The procedure was conducted as follows:

Nuclei were isolated from frozen ileum tissues via homogenization and density gradient centrifugation. Nuclei viability and concentration were assessed using trypan blue staining and fluorescence-based counting. Since STAT1 is a transcription factor, nuclei were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, followed by quenching with glycine. After cross-linking, samples were incubated at 62°C for 10 min in cell membrane permeabilization buffer and then equilibrated in dilution buffer at 37°C for 30 min.

Prepared nuclei were bound to Concanavalin A-coated beads via rotation at room temperature for 10 min. Bead-bound nuclei were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-STAT1 primary antibody (F0263, Selleck, Shanghai, China) diluted in primary antibody buffer. The following day, samples were washed and incubated with a secondary antibody in Antibody Buffer for 1 h at room temperature with rotation. After washing, the nuclei were incubated with pA-Tn5 transposase (ChiTag) for 1 h at room temperature to enable antibody-directed tagmentation.

Tagmentation was performed in tagmentation buffer at 37°C for 1 h and terminated using stop buffer at 55°C for 10 min. DNA was purified using Tagment DNA Extract Beads. Libraries were amplified via high-fidelity PCR (13–20 cycles) in a 50 μL system, followed by bead-based purification. Library quality was assessed using Qubit for quantification and Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer for size distribution. Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina platform with a 2 × 150 bp paired-end strategy.

IA-AHR interaction assay

The Interaction between IA and AHR was evaluated using a Streptavidin–Biotin System.55 Briefly, 10 mg of IA (HY-W015273, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in 5.342 mL of MES buffer (M885671, Macklin, Shanghai, China) (50 mM, pH 6.0) to prepare a 10 mM IA solution, with ultrasonication used to assist dissolution. To this, 53.42 mg each of NHS (H6231, Macklin, Shanghai, China) and EDC (BJS2544046, Picasso, Shanghai, China) were added (final concentration: 10 mg/mL). The pH was monitored, and the mixture was incubated on a rotator at room temperature for 10–15 min. Next, 100.02 mg of biotin-PEG2-amine (B863624, Macklin, Shanghai, China) (final concentration: 50 mM) was added and the reaction continued a rotator for 2–4 h at room temperature. Subsequently, 100 μL of IgG (500 μg/mL; Ab170221, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) diluted in ddH2O was added and incubated on a rotating for another 2–4 h. Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (HY-K0202, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) were added and incubated at 4°C for 2 h. After bead removal, the supernatant was collected. Half the volume of the supernatant was mixed with 250 μL of Streptavidin Agarose Resin (P2159, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), incubated on a rocker for 30 min, and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. The resin was washed three times with MES buffer. Then, 50–400 μL of Recombinant hFc1-tagged AHR protein (CSB-EP001481HU6, CUSABIO, Wuhan, China) (5 μg/mL) was added to the resin and incubated on a rocker at 4°C for at least 1 h without vortexing. After incubation, the resin was centrifuged again at 500×g for 5 min. The supernatant was collected for further analysis. The resin was resuspended in 50 μL of SDS-PAGE loading buffer, boiled, and subjected to Western blot detection to assess protein interaction using Goat Anti-Human IgG, Fcγ fragment specific, HRP conjugated-second antibody (CSB-PA160506, CUSABIO, Wuhan, China).

Dual luciferase reporter assay

A DNA fragment encompassing the region from −1500 bp to the transcription start site (TSS) of the SOCS5 promoter was amplified using genomic DNA extracted from the ileum tissue of C57BL/6J mice as the template, and subsequently cloned into the pGL3.0-Basic luciferase vector (P0193, MiaoLingPlasmid, Wuhan, China). Using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000008, Invitrogen, Shanghai, China), MODE-K cells were co-transfected with the constructed pGL3.0-SOCS5-promoter plasmid, a pLV3-CMV-Ahr(mouse)-3×FLAG-CopGFP-Puro plasmid (P70783, MiaoLingPlasmid, Wuhan, China), and pRL-TK plasmid (P0372, MiaoLingPlasmid, Wuhan, China). After 48 h, luciferase activities were measured using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (RG027, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for transfection efficiency, and the data are presented as relative luciferase activity.

Protein–ligand docking

The crystal structures of AHR (PDB: 4M4X) were retrieved from the Protein DataBank (www.rcsb.org). Protein preparation, including the removal of water molecules and the addition of polar hydrogens, was performed using PyMol software (v.4.6). The structure of the IA (CAS:1204-06-4) was obtained from PubChem and subsequently subjected to energy minimization using the OpenBabel software (v.2.4.1). Molecular docking of IA into the AHR binding pocket was carried out using AutoDock Vina (v.2.5.5), with each docking experiment repeated at least three times. The binding affinity was evaluated based on the calculated docking score (kcal/mol). The binding poses with the most favorable (lowest) docking energy was selected for further analysis. Protein-ligand interactions were visualized with PyMol, and non-covalent bonds between IA and AHR were systematically identified using the online Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) tool (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-IP assays were conducted on mouse ileum tissue lysates to examine the endogenous interaction between AHR and STAT1, following an established protocol.53 Briefly, fresh ileum tissues were homogenized in a pre-cooled RIPA Lysis Buffer (Weak) (P0013D, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with InStab Protease Cocktail (20124ES03, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). The homogenates were then centrifuged at 12,500 × g for 15 min at 4°C to collect the supernatant. For each Co-IP reaction, 2 μg of the specified primary antibody or corresponding control IgG was incubated with 20 μL of pre-washed Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (HY-K0202, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 2 h with rotation to form the antibody-bead complex. The complex was then incubated with the pre-cleared tissue lysates overnight at 4°C. After extensive washing with lysis buffer, the immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted by boiling in 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer and subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.1.2, GraphPad Software Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA). At least 5 mice per group were used to reach statistical power. For in vitro studies, at least three biological repeats were carried out. The survival data were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method and analyzed using the log rank test. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test was used to compare the differences between two different treatment groups, whereas analysis of experiments with more than two groups was performed using a one-way analysis of variance followed by a Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
