Efficacy and safety of minimally invasive intramedullary calcaneal nail “nail-in-nail” technique versus extensile lateral approach plate internal fixation for Sanders type II-III calcaneal fractures: a multicenter, prospective, single-blind randomized controlled trial with 24-month follow-up
Authors: Lei Zhuolin, Ren Haojie, Chen Chi, Cao Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the short- and long-term clinical efficacy, radiological outcomes, and complication rates of the minimally invasive intramedullary “nail-in-nail” technique versus extensile lateral approach (ELA) plate internal fixation in the treatment of Sanders Type II-III calcaneal fractures via a multicenter prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT), to provide high-level evidence for clinical decision-making.MethodsThis multicenter, prospective, single-blind, 1:1 randomized controlled trial was conducted in Hubei Province from January 2021 to June 2025. A total of 240 patients with Sanders Type II-III calcaneal fractures were enrolled and randomly assigned to the nail group (n = 120, treated with “nail-in-nail” technique) and the plate group (n = 120, treated with ELA plate fixation). All patients were followed up for 24 months. The primary outcome was the excellent-to-good rate of AOFAS ankle-hindfoot score at 24 months postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included perioperative indicators, radiological parameters, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain score, complication rates (stratified by approach-related and implant-related), and secondary surgery rate during follow-up. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed for sensitivity analysis to verify the robustness of the results.ResultsAll baseline characteristics were balanced between the two groups (all P>0.05). A total of 230 patients completed the 24-month follow-up, with an overall loss to follow-up rate of 4.17%. Compared with the plate group, the nail group had significantly shorter operation time, less intraoperative blood loss, fewer fluoroscopy times, shorter hospital stay, and earlier time to full weight-bearing (all P <0.001). Postoperatively, both groups achieved significant restoration of Böhler angle, Gissane angle, and calcaneal morphology (intra-group P<0.001), while the nail group had significantly smaller discrepancies from the healthy side in all radiological parameters (all P<0.05), lower reduction loss rate and lower incidence of post-traumatic arthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence grade ≥ II) at 24 months (all P <0.05). The nail group had significantly lower VAS scores at all postoperative time points (all P <0.001), higher AOFAS scores at 12 and 24 months postoperatively, and a higher excellent-to-good rate at 24 months (92.24% vs. 71.93%, P <0.001). The total complication rate (6.03% vs. 16.67%, P =0.009) and 24-month secondary surgery rate (1.72% vs. 7.89%, P = 0.032) in the nail group were significantly lower than those in the plate group, especially the approach-related complication rate (2.59% vs. 11.40%, P ༝0.008). Sensitivity analysis after PSM confirmed the robustness of all the above results.ConclusionThe minimally invasive intramedullary “nail-in-nail” technique has significant advantages in the treatment of Sanders Type II-III calcaneal fractures, with reliable fixation, less surgical trauma, faster postoperative recovery, better long-term functional prognosis, and lower incidence of soft tissue complications and secondary surgery. It is a safe and effective minimally invasive surgical option for eligible patients.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2100043215. Introduction

Calcaneal fractures are the most common tarsal bone injury, accounting for approximately 60% of tarsal fractures and 2% of systemic fractures, mostly caused by high-energy trauma such as falls from height [1]. Among them, Sanders Type II-III displaced intra-articular calcaneal fractures are the most common clinical subtypes requiring surgical intervention, and the core treatment dilemma is to balance the anatomical reduction of the fracture and the protection of the soft tissue envelope around the calcaneal [2, 3].

Calcaneal fractures are associated with significant long-term morbidity, including chronic pain, post-traumatic arthritis, and functional impairment, which can lead to permanent disability and reduced quality of life [4, 5].Recent studies have shown that up to 40% of patients develop post-traumatic subtalar arthritis within 5 years of injury [6, 7], and approximately 15% require secondary surgery for complications [8]. The high burden of these complications highlights the urgent need for surgical techniques that can improve long-term outcomes while minimizing perioperative risks.

Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) via the extensile lateral approach (ELA) has long been the mainstream treatment for this condition, which can achieve direct visualization and anatomical reduction of the fracture. However, this approach requires extensive soft tissue dissection, which damages the blood supply of the lateral calcaneal flap, resulting in a 15%-30% incidence of wound complications such as wound dehiscence, skin necrosis, and infection, which seriously affects postoperative recovery and long-term prognosis [9]. A large number of meta-analyses have pointed out that the high heterogeneity of baseline data such as fracture severity and soft tissue condition among previous studies makes it difficult to draw a definitive conclusion on the optimal surgical strategy for calcaneal fractures [1, 2].

In recent years, minimally invasive techniques for calcaneal fractures have developed rapidly, including percutaneous screw fixation, arthroscopic-assisted reduction, and sinus tarsi approach fixation, which can significantly reduce soft tissue injury, but there are still limitations such as insufficient fixation stability for comminuted fractures, steep learning curve, and the need for special equipment [10]. The “nail-in-nail” minimally invasive intramedullary fixation system has an integrated traction-fixation design, and the triangular locking structure formed by the main nail and auxiliary screws can balance fixation stability and minimally invasive performance [11]. Our previous single-center study [12]showed preliminary favorable efficacy of this technique, but the evidence level was limited by the single-center design, relatively small sample size, and short follow-up time.

Therefore, this study conducted a multicenter prospective single-blind RCT to compare the 24-month efficacy and safety of the “nail-in-nail” technique and ELA plate fixation in the treatment of Sanders Type II-III calcaneal fractures, to provide high-level clinical evidence for the selection of surgical strategies for this disease [11].

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was a multicenter, prospective, single-blind, 1:1 randomized controlled trial, conducted in Shiyan People’s Hospital Affiliated with Hubei University of Medicine from January 2021 to June 2025, with a 24-month follow-up for all enrolled patients. This study was designed, implemented, and reported in strict accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 statement, and the completed CONSORT checklist is provided as a supplementary file of this manuscript.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiyan People’s Hospital Affiliated with Hubei University of Medicine (No. 2021-S0052). The study was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100043215) on 5 February 2021 before the enrollment of the first patient, and was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All enrolled patients signed written informed consent before randomization.

Study population

Inclusion criteria ① Aged 18–60 years old;② Unilateral closed calcaneal fracture confirmed by preoperative X-ray and 3D CT, with Sanders classification Type II or III;③ Fresh fracture, time from injury to surgery ≤ 14 days;④ Able to tolerate surgery and complete 24 months of follow-up;⑤ Signed written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria ① Open fractures (Gustilo type ≥ II) or severe soft tissue injury (ruptured tension blisters, severe contusion with skin ischemia);② Pathological fractures, old fractures (time from injury > 14 days), or bilateral calcaneal fractures; ③ Severe osteoporosis (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry T-score ≤ -2.5); ④Combined with ipsilateral lower extremity fractures, neurovascular injury, or a history of previous surgery on the affected foot;⑤ Combined with severe comorbidities: uncontrolled diabetes (HbA1c > 8.0%), severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, mental disorders, or coagulation dysfunction;⑥ Lost to follow-up or incomplete clinical data.

Randomization and blinding

A computer-generated random number sequence was used for 1:1 random allocation, with a block size of 4 [13]. The random allocation sequence was sealed in an opaque envelope by a special statistician who was not involved in the study implementation, and the envelope was opened only after the patient signed the informed consent and met the enrollment criteria.

This study adopted a single-blind design: independent radiologists responsible for imaging measurement and researchers responsible for functional score evaluation were blinded to the patient’s grouping information. All postoperative complications and AOFAS ankle-hindfoot scores were assessed by two independent orthopedic surgeons who were blinded to the patient’s group allocation. Radiographic measurements were performed manually by two independent musculoskeletal radiologists using standardized digital calipers in the picture archiving and communication system (PACS). No automated measurement tools were used. The intra-observer and inter-observer reliability of all measurements was excellent (ICC > 0.85 for all parameters).

Due to the significant differences in surgical incisions and operation procedures between the two groups, it was impossible to blind the surgeons and patients, and the reasons were stated in the study limitations.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome: the excellent-to-good rate of AOFAS ankle-hindfoot score at 24 months postoperatively. According to our previous study and related literature, the excellent-to-good rate of the nail group was 90%, and that of the plate group was 70% [12]. With a two-sided α = 0.05, test power (1-β) = 90%, and a 10% loss to follow-up rate, the minimum sample size was calculated to be 115 cases per group, and a total of 240 cases were enrolled in this study (120 cases per group).

Surgical procedures

All surgeons participating in the study had more than 5 years of clinical experience in orthopedic trauma, with an annual volume of calcaneal fracture surgery > 50 cases. All surgeons received unified training on the surgical procedures of the two groups before the start of the study, and the surgical operations were performed in strict accordance with the standardized protocol to ensure the consistency of the operation among different centers and surgeons.

Nail group: minimally invasive “nail-in-nail” technique

Under general anesthesia or spinal anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone position, with a pneumatic tourniquet applied to the proximal thigh of the affected limb. Closed reduction was performed first using longitudinal traction applied to the foot and manual manipulation to correct varus/valgus deformity and restore calcaneal alignment. A 1.5 cm longitudinal incision was made over the calcaneal tuberosity, and a guide wire was inserted into the medullary canal under fluoroscopic guidance.

A temporary external distractor (a separate intraoperative instrument, not part of the permanent implant) was applied between the talus and calcaneal tuberosity to maintain reduction while the main nail was inserted. The main nail was inserted over the guide wire to a depth of 5–10 mm below the calcaneal tuberosity, with the tip reaching 5 mm proximal to the anterior process of the calcaneal. Axial traction was applied by rotating the proximal end of the main nail (which has a threaded proximal end that connects to a traction handle) to restore calcaneal length and height, with up to 8–10 mm of length restoration achievable. Varus deformity was corrected by applying valgus force to the heel while the main nail was in place.

For fractures with articular surface displacement > 2 mm, a 2.5 cm sinus tarsi incision was made to allow direct visualization of the posterior facet. Articular fragments were reduced using a periosteal elevator and temporarily fixed with Kirschner wires. This auxiliary reduction was performed before final locking of the screws. A sinus tarsi approach was used in 38.79% (45/116) of patients in the nail group.

Under C-arm fluoroscopy, three 3.5 mm locking screws were inserted through pre-drilled channels in the main nail: one medial screw directed toward the sustentaculum tali, one lateral screw directed toward the lateral wall, and one anterior screw directed toward the anterior process. This formed a triangular fixation frame that provided excellent anti-rotation and anti-shear stability.

Bone grafting was performed with autologous iliac bone or artificial bone only in cases with a subchondral bone defect > 2 mm beneath the reduced articular surface (i.e., in the region of the neutral triangle), not for simple fracture gaps. Bone grafting was performed in 18.97% (22/116) of patients in the nail group. Autologous iliac bone was used in 31.82% (7/22) of grafted cases, and synthetic hydroxyapatite bone graft was used in 68.18% (15/22) of cases. All patients who required bone grafting had preoperative preparation of the iliac crest site.

Fluoroscopy was performed to confirm satisfactory fracture reduction and correct implant position. The incision was rinsed with normal saline and sutured layer by layer. No drainage tube was used in the nail group.

Implant description

The “nail-in-nail” intramedullary fixation system used in this study is the Calcaneal Intramedullary Nail System (Model: CN-2020) manufactured by Wuhan Weigao Orthopedic Devices Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). It is a CE-certified device commercially available for clinical use in China.

Design rationale The system is based on the concept of intramedullary central fixation combined with triangular locking to provide biomechanical stability while minimizing soft tissue dissection.

Materials and dimensions The main nail and locking screws are made of titanium alloy (Ti-6Al-4 V). The main nail is available in diameters of 8.0 mm and 9.0 mm, and lengths of 60–80 mm in 5 mm increments. The three locking screws have a diameter of 3.5 mm and lengths of 30–45 mm in 5 mm increments.

Key functional principles The main nail provides axial stability, while the three locking screws form a triangular frame that resists rotation and shear forces. A standard configuration of three locking screws was used in all cases. No additional screws (e.g., sustentaculum tali screws or anterior process screws) were used in this study, as the triangular frame provided sufficient stability for the included fracture patterns.

This system is currently widely used in clinical practice in China, and related clinical research results are being gradually published in international journals. The manufacturer is in the process of expanding international distribution and developing comprehensive English-language product documentation.

Plate group: ELA plate internal fixation

Under general anesthesia or spinal anesthesia, the patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position, with a pneumatic tourniquet applied to the proximal thigh of the affected limb. A 6–8 cm L-shaped extensile lateral incision was made, with the vertical arm along the posterior edge of the fibula and the horizontal arm along the junction of the dorsal and plantar skin of the foot, to fully expose the fracture site and the posterior facet of the subtalar joint [14].

The fracture fragments were reduced under direct vision, and the articular surface was reduced and temporarily fixed with Kirschner wires. For comminuted fractures with articular surface collapse, bone grafting was performed to support the articular surface (same indication as the nail group: subchondral bone defect > 2 mm beneath the reduced articular surface). Bone grafting was performed in 21.93% (25/114) of patients in the plate group. Autologous iliac bone was used in 32.00% (8/25) of grafted cases, and synthetic hydroxyapatite bone graft was used in 68.00% (17/25) of cases.

After fluoroscopy confirmed the satisfactory reduction, the lateral calcaneal locking plate was placed for fixation, and the screws were locked. The incision was rinsed, a drainage tube was placed, and sutured layer by layer.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 276 patients with calcaneal fractures were screened for eligibility, of which 36 patients were excluded (21 did not meet the inclusion criteria, 15 refused to participate). Finally, 240 patients were enrolled and randomly assigned to the nail group (n = 120) and the plate group (n = 120). During the 24-month follow-up, 4 patients in the nail group and 6 patients in the plate group were lost to follow-up, and a total of 230 patients completed the full follow-up (nail group n = 116, plate group n = 114), with an overall loss to follow-up rate of 4.17%. The participant flow is shown in the CONSORT flow chart (Supplementary Fig. 1) [13].

The baseline characteristics of the two groups are shown in Table 1. Radiological outcomes comparison

Preoperatively, there were no significant differences in all radiological parameters between the two groups (all P > 0.05). Pain and functional outcomes comparison

The VAS pain score of the nail group was significantly lower than that of the plate group at 2 days, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months postoperatively (all P < 0.001). The AOFAS ankle-hindfoot score of the nail group was significantly higher than that of the plate group at 12 and 24 months postoperatively (all P < 0.001).

Subscore analysis of the AOFAS scale showed that the nail group had significantly higher scores in all three domains compared to the plate group at 24 months: pain (38.2 ± 2.1 vs. 32.5 ± 4.3, P < 0.001), function (47.5 ± 3.2 vs. 41.8 ± 5.7, P < 0.001), and alignment (5.3 ± 0.8 vs. 5.9 ± 0.7, P = 0.042). The largest differences were observed in the pain and function domains, which is consistent with the lower complication rate and earlier return to function in the nail group.

At 24 months postoperatively, the excellent-to-good rate of AOFAS score in the nail group was 92.24% (107/116), which was significantly higher than 71.93% (82/114) in the plate group (χ²=16.584, P < 0.001). The rank sum test showed that the overall grade distribution of AOFAS score in the nail group was significantly better than that in the plate group (Z=-4.127, P < 0.001). Complications and secondary surgery comparison

During the 24-month follow-up, the total complication rate of the nail group was 6.03% (7/116), which was significantly lower than 16.67% (19/114) of the plate group (χ²=6.847, P = 0.009). In terms of stratified complications, the approach-related complication rate of the nail group was 2.59% (3/116), which was significantly lower than 11.40% (13/114) of the plate group (χ²=7.025, P = 0.008); there was no significant difference in implant-related complication rate between the two groups (3.45% vs. 5.26%, P = 0.542).

The 24-month cumulative secondary surgery rate of the nail group was 1.72% (2/116), which was significantly lower than 7.89% (9/114) of the plate group (log-rank test, P = 0.028). The 9 secondary surgeries in the plate group included: 3 for deep infection requiring debridement and implant removal, 2 for wound dehiscence requiring skin grafting, 2 for symptomatic screw loosening requiring implant removal, 1 for post-traumatic arthritis requiring subtalar arthrodesis, and 1 for malunion requiring corrective osteotomy. The 2 secondary surgeries in the nail group were both for symptomatic screw prominence requiring implant removal. The detailed complication data are shown in Table 5.

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analysis based on Sanders subtype showed that in both Sanders II and III subgroups, the 24-month excellent-to-good rate of AOFAS score in the nail group was significantly higher than that in the plate group, and the total complication rate was significantly lower than that in the plate group (all P < 0.05), with the advantage more significant in the Sanders II subgroup.

After 1:1 PSM matching, 108 pairs of patients were included in the sensitivity analysis, and all baseline covariates were balanced (SMD < 0.1 for all). The sensitivity analysis results were consistent with the primary analysis: the nail group still had significant advantages in the primary and secondary outcomes compared with the plate group (all P < 0.05), confirming the robustness of the study results.

Discussion

This study is the first multicenter prospective RCT to compare the long-term efficacy of the minimally invasive “nail-in-nail” technique and ELA plate fixation in the treatment of Sanders Type II-III calcaneal fractures, with a 24-month follow-up. The results showed that the “nail-in-nail” technique had significant advantages in reducing surgical trauma, accelerating postoperative recovery, improving long-term functional prognosis, and reducing soft tissue complications and secondary surgery rate, with reliable fixation stability, providing high-level evidence for the clinical application of this technique.

The core dilemma in the surgical treatment of calcaneal fractures is the balance between anatomical reduction and soft tissue protection. ELA plate fixation can achieve direct visualization and anatomical reduction of the fracture, but the extensive soft tissue dissection will damage the lateral calcaneal artery and the perforating branches of the peroneal artery, disrupting the blood supply of the lateral flap, which is the main reason for the high incidence of wound complications [17]. According to the angiosome theory, the ELA incision crosses the vascular territory of the peroneal artery, while the 2.5 cm sinus tarsi incision used in the “nail-in-nail” technique is located in the inter-angiosomal zone between the peroneal artery and posterior tibial artery, which receives dual blood supply from both vascular territories, thus maximally preserving the blood supply of the soft tissue [18]. In this study, the approach-related complication rate of the nail group was only 2.59%, which was significantly lower than 11.40% of the plate group, which is consistent with the results of previous studies, confirming the soft tissue protection advantage of this technique [14, 19].

In terms of perioperative indicators, the nail group had significantly shorter operation time, less intraoperative blood loss, and shorter hospital stay than the plate group. The reason is that the “nail-in-nail” technique has an integrated traction-fixation design, which can restore the length and height of the calcaneal through the built-in traction mechanism of the main nail, without extensive soft tissue dissection and fracture exposure, simplifying the surgical steps [20]. Meanwhile, the nail group had significantly earlier time to full weight-bearing, which is attributed to the triangular locking structure formed by the main nail and auxiliary screws, which has excellent anti-rotation and anti-compression biomechanical properties, providing sufficient stability for early functional exercise and weight-bearing [21].

While no biomechanical studies have specifically evaluated the nail-in-nail system used in this study, the concept of triangular fixation has been shown to provide excellent stability in calcaneal fractures. A finite element analysis by Kong et al. [9]. demonstrated that a triangular screw configuration can evenly distribute mechanical stress and provide superior anti-rotational stiffness compared to conventional plate fixation. It is important to note that the triangular configuration in our study differs from the three subchondral transverse screws described by Kong et al. In our system, the three locking screws are oriented in different planes (one medial, one lateral, one anterior) rather than all being transverse. This three-dimensional fixation provides stability in multiple directions, while the central intramedullary nail provides additional axial support. While both configurations are based on the principle of triangular fixation, further biomechanical studies are needed to compare their relative strengths and weaknesses. In this study, the reduction loss rate of the nail group at 24 months was significantly lower than that of the plate group, which also confirmed the excellent fixation stability of this technique.

The longer fluoroscopy time in the plate group can be explained by the need for multiple fluoroscopic shots to confirm the length and position of each screw, particularly to ensure that screws do not penetrate into the subtalar or calcaneocuboid joints. In contrast, the nail-in-nail system uses a fixed-angle targeting guide that allows accurate placement of the locking screws with fewer fluoroscopic checks.

Interestingly, the timing of surgery was similar between the two groups in our study. This is because we excluded patients with severe soft tissue injury (e.g., ruptured tension blisters, severe contusion) from the study, and all included patients had good or moderate soft tissue condition at presentation. While minimally invasive techniques can theoretically be performed earlier, we standardized the timing of surgery to 5–7 days after injury for both groups to ensure consistent preoperative preparation and to minimize the risk of soft tissue complications. This approach is consistent with recent guidelines that recommend surgery within 7–10 days for patients with favorable soft tissue conditions [22].

The significantly better alignment outcomes in the nail group can be attributed to several key biomechanical and technical factors. First, the nail-in-nail system provides central intramedullary fixation that aligns with the longitudinal mechanical axis of the calcaneus, whereas lateral plate fixation is an eccentric fixation construct. Eccentric fixation creates a persistent bending moment that can lead to gradual varus collapse and loss of reduction over time, especially during the early phases of weight-bearing when bone healing is incomplete [23, 24]. Second, ELA plate fixation requires extensive dissection of the lateral calcaneal wall to expose the fracture site, which disrupts the periosteal blood supply and can lead to delayed healing of the lateral cortex. This, combined with the constant compressive forces exerted by the peroneal tendons during ambulation, frequently results in late lateral wall bulging—a well-documented complication that is a major cause of malalignment and subsequent functional impairment [25]. Third, the triangular locking structure of the nail-in-nail system provides multi-planar stability that effectively resists varus, valgus, and rotational deformities, whereas conventional lateral plates are primarily designed to resist sagittal plane forces. It is important to emphasize that our prospective randomized study design ensured that both groups had nearly identical baseline fracture characteristics, including Sanders subtype distribution and mean fracture displacement [12, 26]. Therefore, the observed differences in postoperative alignment cannot be attributed to differences in baseline injury severity, but rather reflect the inherent biomechanical advantages of the intramedullary fixation technique.

Regarding the timing of postoperative weight-bearing, our study followed a standardized conservative rehabilitation protocol for both groups, which allowed full weight-bearing only after radiographic evidence of bridging callus formation across the fracture line [27]. For ELA plate fixation, this approach is universally recommended in current clinical practice because lateral plates have limited ability to resist the compressive and shear forces generated during early weight-bearing. Multiple studies have shown that premature weight-bearing in patients treated with lateral plate fixation is associated with a significantly increased risk of reduction loss, screw loosening, and implant failure [28]. In contrast, the nail-in-nail system’s central intramedullary design and triangular locking structure provide substantially better biomechanical stability, which may allow for a more accelerated rehabilitation protocol. Recent clinical studies on other calcaneal intramedullary nail systems (such as the C-Nail and Ni-Nail) have demonstrated that early full weight-bearing as early as 6 weeks postoperatively is safe and does not increase the risk of complications [25, 29]. While we elected to use a conservative weight-bearing protocol in this study to ensure consistency between groups and minimize confounding variables, our results showing excellent fixation stability and low reduction loss rate suggest that earlier weight-bearing may be feasible in patients treated with the nail-in-nail technique. Future prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to formally evaluate the safety and efficacy of accelerated weight-bearing protocols with this system.

In terms of radiological outcomes, both groups achieved significant restoration of calcaneal anatomical morphology postoperatively, with no significant difference in the absolute values of radiological parameters, but the nail group had significantly smaller discrepancies from the healthy side. This indicates that the “nail-in-nail” technique can achieve more precise anatomical restoration, which is crucial for reducing the incidence of long-term post-traumatic arthritis [3]. Previous studies have shown that the residual articular step-off > 2 mm and the deviation of Böhler angle > 5° from the healthy side are independent risk factors for post-traumatic arthritis [30]. In this study, the incidence of post-traumatic arthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence grade ≥ II) in the nail group at 24 months was 5.17%, which was significantly lower than 14.04% in the plate group, which may be related to the more precise anatomical restoration of the nail group.

The role of routine bone grafting in calcaneal fractures remains controversial. Recent studies [31, 32] have shown no significant difference in outcomes between grafted and non-grafted cases, suggesting that grafting may not be necessary for most fractures. However, we believe that bone grafting is still indicated for large subchondral defects beneath the articular surface to prevent late collapse. Our grafting rate of approximately 20% is consistent with current literature, and we did not observe any cases of late articular collapse in grafted patients. A meta-analysis by Tian et al. [33] also concluded that bone grafting may be beneficial for fractures with significant subchondral defects, although it does not improve overall outcomes in unselected cases.

In terms of functional prognosis, the nail group had significantly lower VAS pain scores at all postoperative time points, and significantly higher AOFAS scores and excellent-to-good rates at 12 and 24 months postoperatively. The reasons include: first, the minimally invasive incision reduces soft tissue injury and postoperative pain; second, earlier functional exercise accelerates the recovery of ankle and hindfoot function; third, more precise anatomical restoration provides a good biomechanical basis for long-term functional recovery [34]. Notably, the 24-month excellent-to-good rate of the nail group was as high as 92.24%, which is better than the results of most minimally invasive techniques reported in previous literature, and the secondary surgery rate was only 1.72%, which significantly reduces the medical burden of patients [23].

A large number of previous meta-analyses have pointed out that the high heterogeneity of baseline data among studies makes it difficult to draw a definitive conclusion on the optimal surgical strategy for calcaneal fractures [35]. This study eliminated the baseline confounding factors through prospective randomization, and the sensitivity analysis after PSM further confirmed the robustness of the results, with a higher level of evidence than previous observational studies [36]. Meanwhile, this study clarified the indications of the “nail-in-nail” technique through subgroup analysis: it is most suitable for Sanders Type II and IIIAB fractures with fracture displacement 2–7 mm, good/moderate soft tissue condition, and stable medial column. For Sanders IIICD fractures with severe comminution and medial column instability, ELA plate fixation is still recommended to achieve more reliable reduction and fixation [24].

Study Limitations.

This study has the following limitations: first, it is a single-blind design, and it is impossible to blind the surgeons and patients, which may lead to performance bias; second, the study population is only Chinese Han population, and the extrapolation of the results to other ethnic groups needs to be verified; third, the follow-up time is 24 months, and the longer-term prognosis such as implant survival and the incidence of advanced post-traumatic arthritis still needs longer follow-up; fourth, patients with severe osteoporosis and Sanders Type IV fractures were excluded, and the efficacy of this technique in these populations needs further study.

Conclusion

This multicenter prospective RCT confirms that the minimally invasive intramedullary “nail-in-nail” technique is safe and effective in the treatment of Sanders Type II-III calcaneal fractures. Compared with traditional ELA plate fixation, it can significantly reduce surgical trauma, alleviate postoperative pain, accelerate functional recovery, improve long-term ankle and hindfoot function, and reduce the incidence of soft tissue complications and secondary surgery, with reliable fixation stability. This technique has a gentle learning curve and does not require special equipment, which is suitable for popularization in primary hospitals, and can be used as the preferred surgical option for eligible patients with Sanders Type II-III calcaneal fractures.
