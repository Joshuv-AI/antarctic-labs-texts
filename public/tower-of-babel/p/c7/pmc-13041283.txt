A comprehensive dataset of homologous and non-homologous isofunctional enzymes across the tree of life
Authors: Fernanda Cristina de Oliveira, Emanuel Umbelino, Sérgio Lifschitz, Edward Hermann Haeusler, Ana Carolina Ramos Guimarães, Antonio Basílio de Miranda, Marcos Catanho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConvergent evolution, the independent emergence of similar traits, is increasingly recognized as a pervasive force shaping molecular and metabolic diversity. A striking manifestation of convergence at the molecular level is represented by non-homologous isofunctional enzymes (NISE), distinct proteins with no detectable common ancestry that catalyze identical biochemical reactions. Despite their conceptual and practical relevance, NISE are often treated as exceptional cases, and no large-scale, systematically curated resource has been available to explore their distribution and properties across all domains of life.Data descriptionHere we present a curated dataset of homologous and non-homologous isofunctional enzymes (HISE and NISE) derived from UniProtKB release 2025_01, encompassing both reviewed (Swiss-Prot) and unreviewed (TrEMBL) entries. Using Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers to define catalytic equivalence and SUPERFAMILY (SCOP structure superfamily) annotations to infer evolutionary relationships, we implemented a transparent and reproducible pipeline to classify enzymes into homologous and non-homologous functional groups. The dataset comprises over 200,000 Swiss-Prot and 27 million TrEMBL enzymes with complete EC and SUPERFAMILY annotations, organized by domain of life, enzyme class, and structural domain composition. Multiple output files, including presence/absence matrices, clustered enzyme groups, phyloprofiles, and full annotation tables, are provided to facilitate downstream evolutionary, functional, and comparative analyses. This resource offers a global view of molecular convergence and divergence in enzymatic functions, highlighting the widespread nature of NISE across taxa and enzyme classes. It provides a foundation for studying metabolic evolution, functional redundancy, drug target discovery, and the evolutionary constraints shaping biochemical solutions.

Objective

Convergent evolution reflects the constrained nature of biochemical solutions to life’s functional demands and is increasingly recognized as a widespread feature of molecular evolution [23]. One of its most striking manifestations is the existence of non-homologous isofunctional enzymes (NISE), distinct proteins that catalyze the same biochemical reaction despite lacking detectable common ancestry. Previous studies have demonstrated that NISE are involved in diverse biological processes [15, 17, 24], antimicrobial resistance [9, 12, 19], pathogenicity [10, 18], and drug target discovery to treat infeccious deseases [5, 6, 11, 13] or to control phytopathogens (da Silva et al., 7).

The objective of this work was to generate a comprehensive and reusable dataset of homologous and non-homologous isofunctional enzymes across the tree of life by integrating functional and evolutionary annotations from UniProtKB [22]. Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers were used to define catalytic equivalence, while SUPERFAMILY phylogenetic assignments [16] of SCOP protein structure superfamilies [3] were employed to infer shared or independent evolutionary origins. This strategy has been shown to effectively discriminate between homologous and non-homologous enzymes even in the absence of experimentally determined three-dimensional structures [17].

The resulting dataset enables large-scale analyses of enzymatic convergence and divergence, supports comparative and evolutionary genomics studies, and provides a resource for identifying alternative biochemical solutions to the same metabolic reactions. We are currently preparing a manuscript exploring this dataset to support our hypotesis that enzymatic activity, rather than gene lineage, serves as a unit of selection, with homologous and non-homologous isofunctional enzymes behaving analogously to alleles within microbial populations. We identified numerous enzymatic activities performed by structurally unrelated enzymes often coexisting in phylogenetically related taxa. These enzyme variants demonstrate population-level distributions, phylogenetic biases, and expression divergence consistent with selection acting on catalytic function, independent of molecular ancestry. Our results extend prior work on biased horizontal gene transfer and functional replacement [1, 2], and provide a mechanistic basis for the persistence of functional traits via convergent molecular solutions, aligning with recent theoretical proposals that emphasize differential persistence over differential reproduction in microbial evolution [4, 8]. This framework supports a pluralistic view of evolutionary units, where enzyme activity plays the role of an adaptive trait and gene variants - homologous or non-homologous - serve as its alleles.

Overview of data files deposited in the repository

Primary annotation tables: Two groups of comprehensive tables (Swiss-Prot/TrEMBL_HISE.tsv and Swiss-Prot/TrEMBL_NISE.tsv) provide full annotation for homologous (SwissProt_HISE.tsv.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/SwissProt_HISE.tsv.gz%3E and TrEMBL_HISE.tsv.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/TrEMBL_HISE.tsv.gz%3E [14]) and non-homologous (SwissProt_NISE.tsv.gz > < https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/SwissProt_NISE.tsv.gz%3E and TrEMBL_NISE.tsv.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/TrEMBL_NISE.tsv.gz%3E [14]) isofunctional enzymes, respectively. Each row corresponds to a UniProtKB entry and includes accession, protein and gene names, EC number, SUPERFAMILY annotation(s), organism name, and taxonomic classification from domain to genus.

Presence/absence matrix: SUPERFAMILY presence/absence matrices summarize domain usage across enzymes sharing the same EC number (SwissProt_NISE.mtx <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/SwissProt_NISE.mtx.gz%3E and TrEMBL_NISE.mtx <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/TrEMBL_NISE.mtx.gz%3E [14]).

Clustered enzyme files: Enzymes are grouped by EC number and clustered by SUPERFAMILY profiles into tables of homologous or non-homologous isofunctional enzymes (SwissProt_NISE.clust <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/SwissProt_NISE.clust.gz%3E and TrEMBL_NISE.clust <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/TrEMBL_NISE.clust.gz%3E [14]). Separate files are provided for bona fide monomeric (SwissProt_NISE.bnf <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/SwissProt_NISE.bnf.gz%3E, SwissProt_HISE.bnf <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/SwissProt_HISE.bnf.gz%3E,

TrEMBL_NISE.bnf <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/TrEMBL_NISE.bnf.gz%3E, and TrEMBL_HISE.bnf <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/TrEMBL_HISE.bnf.gz%3E [14]), multimeric (SwissProt_NISE.multi.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/SwissProt_NISE.multi.gz%3E, TrEMBL_NISE.multi.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/TrEMBL_NISE.multi.gz%3E, SwissProt_HISE.multi.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/SwissProt_HISE.multi.gz%3E, and TrEMBL_HISE.multi.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/TrEMBL_HISE.multi.gz%3E [14] ), and singleton (SwissProt_HISE.sgl.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/SwissProt_HISE.sgl.gz%3E and TrEMBL_HISE.sgl.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/TrEMBL_HISE.sgl.gz%3E [14]) enzymes, enabling fine-grained analyses of domain architecture and functional convergence.

Phyloprofile matrices: For each EC number, SUPERFAMILY presence/absence profiles describe the taxonomic distribution of NISE and HISE across domains, kingdoms, and lower taxonomic ranks. These files (*.phyprof) - packed and compressed in files SwissProt_PhyloProfiles.tar.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/SwissProt_PhyloProfiles.tar.gz%3E and TrEMBL_PhyloProfiles.tar.gz <https://zenodo.org/records/18008936/files/TrEMBL_PhyloProfiles.tar.gz%3E [14] - are compatible with the PhyloProfile software <https://github.com/BIONF/PhyloProfile%3E. PhyloProfile is a bioinformatics tool designed for comparative genomics and phylogenetic profiling [20, 21]. Its core functionality is to assess patterns of presence and absence of features (genes, domains, pathways) across multiple taxa and to visualize these patterns in a phylogenetic or taxonomic context. The software might adds biological insight, placing NISE/HISE taxonomic profiles into a comparative evolutionary framework, in which: enzyme families distributed across domains of life (Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya, Viruses) can be visualized; patterns of conservation, gain, loss, or convergence can be detected; whether certain domain architectures correlate with specific lineages, metabolic capabilities, or evolutionary events can be investigated.

Methodological summary

Proteins annotated with catalytic activity were filtered to retain sequences with a single, complete EC number and at least one SUPERFAMILY annotation. For each EC, enzymes were classified as homologous or non-homologous isofunctional based on whether they shared identical or distinct SUPERFAMILY profiles. A detailed, step-by-step description of the filtering, clustering, and classification pipeline is provided in the repository documentation.

Limitations

This dataset has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting or reusing the data.

First, reliance on EC numbers (even full four digits assignment) might obscure functional nuances, such as moonlighting (a single protein performing two or more distinct, physiologically relevant biological functions) or condition-dependent activity, particularly for enzymes grouped under broad EC categories. Although promiscuous enzymes were excluded to reduce ambiguity, some functional diversity within EC classes might remain unaccounted for.

Second, structural homology is inferred from SUPERFAMILY annotations rather than experimentally determined protein structures. While SUPERFAMILY has proven effective for discriminating homologous and non-homologous enzymes even in the absence of three-dimensional structures [6], very distant evolutionary relationships might not be detected.

Finally, viral enzymes are comparatively underrepresented, reflecting current annotation biases rather than true biological absence. As protein annotation and structural coverage improve, future updates of the dataset may expand and refine these classifications.
