Lumbar stabilization exercise with and without real-time ultrasound imaging biofeedback in chronic low back pain patients: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Raheem Sarafadeen, Sokunbi Oluwaleke Ganiyu, Aminu Alhassan Ibrahim, Mukadas Oyeniran Akindele, Adedapo Wasiu Awotidebe, Bashir Kaka, Bashir Bello, Peemongkon Wattananon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Reduced lumbar multifidus (LM) cross-sectional area (CSA) has been linked with non-specific chronic low back pain (NCLBP); hence, lumbar stabilization exercise (LSE) is typically used as treatment. Real-time ultrasound imaging (RUSI) biofeedback can enhance performance and retention in LM activation. This study aimed to compare the effects of LSE with or without RUSI-biofeedback in patients with NCLBP. A single-blind, three-arm, parallel randomized controlled trial involving 90 participants, randomized into LSERUSI-biofeedback, LSEno-biofeedback, or minimal intervention (n = 30 per group) was conducted. All interventions were administered twice weekly for 8 weeks, and outcomes (LM-CSA, pain, disability, and quality of life) were evaluated before and at 8-week and 20-week follow-ups. Compared to LSEno-biofeedback and minimal intervention, LSERUSI-biofeedback demonstrated a greater increase (p < 0.05) in LM-CSA at 8 weeks (mean [95%CI] difference: 0.81 [0–1.62] and 1.79 [0.91–2.65], respectively) and at 20 weeks (1.61 [0.79–2.42] and 2.66 [1.79–2.53], respectively). LSERUSI-biofeedback showed a greater improvement (p < 0.05) in mental health scores at 8 weeks (3.63 [0.32–6.94]) and 20 weeks (7.34 [4.03–10.60]) compared to LSEno-biofeedback. However, LSEno-biofeedback showed a greater physical health scores at 20 weeks (− 4.89 [− 7.92 to − 1.86]) compared to LSERUSI-biofeedback. No significant differences (p > 0.05) were found between LSERUSI-biofeedback and LSEno-biofeedback for pain or disability. The increase in LM-CSA significantly correlated with changes in pain (r = − 0.40 to − 0.50, p < 0.05) and disability (r = − 0.38 to − 0.42, p < 0.05). In conclusion, LSE with or without RUSI-biofeedback improved LM-CSA, pain, disability, and quality of life. However, LSERUSI-biofeedback led to a greater increase in LM-CSA. Such an increase appears to be related to changes in pain and disability. Trial registration: The study was registered in the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (16/01/2018; PACTR201801002980602).

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is a highly prevalent musculoskeletal disorder, and a major global public health concern1. Over the past three decades, it has been the leading cause of disability globally2, leading to reduced quality of life (QOL) and imposing an enormous clinical and economic burden3,4. Approximately 619 million people globally have LBP in 2020, and 843 million will be affected by 20502. Given the momentous burden of this disorder in addition to its high recurrence rate, evidence-based, optimized treatments are highly desirable3.

Although 90–95% cases of LBP are non-specific5, several factors contributing to pain have been proposed6, with muscular dysfunction, particularly in the deep stabilizing muscles of the spine, playing a critical role7. Among these muscles, the lumbar multifidus (LM), which is essential for maintaining spinal stability, is commonly implicated8. Specifically, alterations in the morphology of this muscle, including atrophy and reduced cross-sectional area (CSA)9–11 have been documented in patients with non-specific chronic LBP (NCLBP) and suggested as a possible reason for its persistence8.

Traditional rehabilitation approaches for NCLBP often include therapeutic exercises targeting the trunk musculature. However, general exercise may not effectively address LM dysfunction12. Thus, lumbar stabilization exercise (LSE), designed to train LM, has gained increasing attention as a targeted intervention13. This exercise focuses on improving neuromuscular control and stability, which are vital for ameliorating back pain symptoms14,15.

The integration of real-time ultrasound imaging (RUSI) as biofeedback during re-training of muscle contraction has emerged as a novel approach16. RUSI provides visual feedback, allowing individuals to observe the contraction and activation of their deep trunk muscles in real-time17,18. Given that LSE is based on motor control principles designed to enhance sensorimotor integration (i.e., use sensory input to generate appropriate motor output), RUSI can be used to augment the sensory input to generate more accurate motor output19. Importantly, experimental studies on asymptomatic individuals have shown the utility of the RUSI in augmenting activation performance and retention in the ability to activate the deep trunk muscles, including LM19,20. Thus, enhancing precise trunk muscle activation and control could lead to better pain reduction and improved function21.

Despite the potential benefits of integrating RUSI biofeedback with LSE, limited research substantiates its specific effects, particularly on LM CSA in patients with NCLBP. A previous randomized controlled trial (RCT) showed that LSE is as effective as general exercise for improving LM thickness and reducing disability22. Another RCT comparing LSE against two other general back extensor exercise regimens showed positive results for LM CSA increase in favor of one of the general exercise approaches23. While these trials contradict the notion that general exercise would be inadequate to restore LM dimensions, none of them integrates RUSI biofeedback in the LSE program. In an earlier trial24 exploring the effects of LSE with RUSI biofeedback compared to standardized medical care (analgesics and advice) for acute LBP patients with between-sides asymmetry in LM CSA of > 11%, long-term follow-up at 1 and 3 years showed a link between improvement in LM CSA and reduced pain recurrence in favor of LSE. Similarly, a study25 with a pretest–posttest design showed improved LM CSA and pain scores among cricketers with current or previous LBP who received LSE training with RUSI, compared with cricketers without LBP who did not receive LSE. Also, our pilot study26 showed significant improvements in LM CSA, pain, and disability scores in patients with NCLBP receiving LSE with RUSI biofeedback, paving the way for a large-scale powered RCT to elucidate these findings.

To the best of our knowledge, no RCT has tested the assertion that LSE training with RUSI biofeedback has added benefits in a sample of patients with NCLBP using LM CSA, pain, disability, and QOL as outcomes. Moreover, there is limited evidence to suggest that post-LSE changes in LM size may be related to changes in pain and disability21. Therefore, the present study was conducted to evaluate the effects of LSE training with RUSI biofeedback on these key outcomes, providing valuable insights into the role of targeted rehabilitation for NCLBP. Our experimental hypothesis was that LSE training with RUSI biofeedback would be more effective at improving LM CSA, pain intensity, functional disability, and QOL compared to LSE training without RUSI biofeedback or minimal intervention. We also hypothesized that post-intervention change in LM CSA would be associated with post-intervention changes in pain intensity and functional disability.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a prospective single-blind, three-arm RCT. This trial followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials Statement (CONSORT)27, and a detailed protocol has been published previously28, with no significant changes made in the present study that are different from the protocol.

Study settings

This study was conducted at the physiotherapy department, National Orthopaedic Hospital, Dala, Kano State, Nigeria, between March 2018 and September 2021.

Ethical consideration

The Research and Ethical Committee of National Orthopedic Hospital, Dala, Kano, approved this study (Ref: NOHD/RET/ETHIC/60). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before inclusion in the study. All procedures conformed to the Helsinki Declaration. The study was registered in the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (16/01/2018; PACTR201801002980602).

Participants’ selection

The participants were recruited at the physiotherapy outpatient clinic, National Orthopaedic Hospital Dala, through referral from a general practitioner or orthopaedist. The inclusion criteria were: (1) male or female aged 18 to 60 years, (2) LBP with or without leg pain of ≥ 12 weeks, and (3) ability to read and understand Hausa, English, or both. The exclusion criteria were: (1) history of spine surgery, (2) obvious deformities affecting the trunk or extremities, (3) serious spinal pathology (e.g., infection, fracture, metastases, cauda equina syndrome), (4) history of medical or surgical conditions that could hinder exercise performance, and (5) pregnancy or lactation.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated to detect a minimum difference of 2.0 points in LM CSA between the LSERUSI-biofeedback and minimal intervention group and 1.0 point between LSERUSI-biofeedback and LSEno-biofeedback, assuming a common standard deviation of 2.5 points based on our pilot study26. Using an effect size of 0.32, an alpha of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.80, an attrition rate of 35%, and a correlation of 0.5 among repeated measures, for repeated measures, between factors ANOVA, a total of 90 participants (30 per group) were required.

Randomization and blinding

After obtaining signed informed consent and assessing baseline demographic and clinical characteristics as well as self-report questionnaires, a record officer, who was not involved in other study aspects, prepared consecutively numbered, sealed opaque envelopes using a computer-generated randomization sequence. This blinded data assessors to ensure unbiased outcome assessment. Permuted blocks of varying sizes (3 and 6) were used to maintain an equal number of participants in each group. The participants were randomly allocated into one of three groups (i.e., LSERUSI-biofeedback, LSEno-biofeedback, minimal intervention [control]), of equal size (n = 30). Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of the physiotherapist responsible for providing treatment was not possible.

Outcome assessment

Self-report questionnaires for pain intensity, functional disability, and QOL were assessed by a physiotherapist with over 3 years of clinical experience, while LM CSA was assessed by a consultant radiologist with over 10 years of diagnostic imaging experience. Both were blinded to the study conditions and patient group allocation. All outcomes were assessed before (baseline) and at 8 weeks (immediately) and 20 weeks after intervention.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the LM CSA measured using a RUSI machine (Edan D3 version 1.6, China) with a 5-MHz curvilinear transducer. Participants lay in a prone position on a plinth with arms by their sides and head turned to their preferred side. A pillow was placed under the hips to maintain a neutral lumbar spine (lumbosacral angle of < 15°). The L5 spinous process was palpated and marked for reference, and an acoustic coupling gel was applied. The transducer was placed longitudinally to confirm the L5 level, then transversely over the L5 spinous process to capture images of the spinous process, laminae, and LM on both sides26,29. Transverse images of the LM CSA were obtained bilaterally, with the smaller side considered the LM CSA. Measurements were taken during maximal voluntary isometric contraction, and average values from three images were used for accuracy. Higher values indicate better CSA. Our pilot study showed a high intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.96 for this measurement.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes were pain intensity, functional disability, and QOL assessed using self-report questionnaires as described in detail in the protocol paper28. Pain intensity was assessed using the Numerical Pain Rating Scale (NPRS, 0–10 cm). Both the English30 and Hausa31 versions were used and have been shown to be reliable, valid, and responsive30–32. Functional disability was assessed using the Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ, 0–24), where 0 indicates no disability and 24 indicates maximum disability33. Both the English33 and Hausa34versions were used and have been shown to be reliable and valid33,34. For QOL, the 12-item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12)35 was used to assess physical and mental health, using the physical component scores (PCS-12, 0–100%) and mental component scores (MCS-12, 0–100%), respectively, with higher scores indicating better QOL. Both the English35 and Hausa36 versions were used, and both are reliable and valid35,36.

Interventions

The study interventions began immediately following randomization. All interventions were provided twice a week for 8 weeks. The primary investigator, with over 15 years of clinical experience, applied LSE in the two experimental groups, while another therapist with over 6 years of clinical experience applied the minimal intervention. Participants in the LSERUSI-biofeedback and LSEno-biofeedback groups also received the intervention provided to the minimal intervention group. All participants were advised against engaging in any exercise-related interventions during the study. They were encouraged to perform group-specific exercises at least twice daily at home and to continue these exercises after the 8-week treatment. Details of the interventions can be found in the published protocol paper28.

LSEno-biofeedback group

Initially, the participants were educated using videos and images on the lumbar spine anatomy and key stabilizing muscles, focusing on LM. They were then taught active isometric contraction of the LM, in synergy with other key deep stability muscles such as transverse abdominis (TrA), while lying prone with a pillow under their hips for neutral spine positioning. Contractions were held for 10 s, repeated 10 times, with 30- to 60-s rest intervals. Exercise progression was achieved by increasing the duration of contractions from 10 to 15 s by the 4th week.

LSERUSI-biofeedback group

The LSE program was similar to that described for LSEno-biofeedback group, with the addition of RUSI biofeedback to enhance LM contraction precision. While maintaining a neutral spine in a prone lying, the ultrasound transducer was placed transversally over the L2–L5 levels. Participants were instructed to perform isometric contractions of the LM in synergy with other deep stabilizing muscles, focusing on the monitor to track changes in LM thickness and maximize effort with each contraction (Fig. 1A–D). Contractions were held for 10 s, repeated 10 times, with 30- to 60-s rest intervals. Minimal intervention (control) group

Three exercises commonly prescribed for chronic LBP disorders were performed. They include knee to chest, lumbar rotation, and bridging. Each exercise was held for 10 s, repeated 10 times, with 5- to 10-s rest intervals between repetitions. Exercise progression was achieved by increasing the duration of contractions from 10 to 15 s by the 4th week.

Statistical analysis

Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was performed, including all randomized participants with available outcome data, regardless of follow-up. Normality of the dependent variables was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. One-way ANOVA was used to compare baseline continuous variables, while the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare baseline categorical variables across the three groups. To analyze the effects of treatment, a linear mixed-effects model was used by including time (baseline, 8 weeks, 20 weeks), treatment (LSERUSI-biofeedback, LSEno-biofeedback, minimal intervention), and a time × treatment interaction as fixed effects, and a random intercept to account for within-subject correlations. Treatment effect was defined as the adjusted between-group difference and the associated 95% confidence intervals (CI). Post-hoc analysis was performed with Bonferroni correction for the primary outcome and Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) for the secondary outcomes. Effect sizes were computed and interpreted using Cohen’s d criteria as trivial (< 0.2), small (≥ 0.2 and < 0.5), moderate (≥ 0.5 and < 0.8), and large (≥ 0.8)37. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to examine the relationship between post-treatment mean change scores in LM CSA and post-treatment mean change scores in pain intensity and functional disability in the experimental groups. Pearson correlation coefficients were interpreted as very weak < 0.20), weak (0.20–0.39), moderate (0.40–0.59), strong (0.60–0.79), and very strong (> 0.80)38,39. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA), at an alpha level of 0.05.

Results

Participation flow and sample characteristics

The flow of the participants through the study is depicted in Fig. 2. Of the 117 individuals with chronic LBP screened for eligibility, 90 (49 males [54.4%] and 41 females [45.6%]) with a mean (SD) age of 46.3 (11.2) years fulfilled the study criteria and were recruited. Most participants had a secondary school education (36%) and worked as civil servants (29%). During the 8-week intervention period, 5 participants withdrew from the study (LSERUSI-biofeedback group = 3, 10.0%; LSEno-biofeedback group = 1, 3.3%; minimal intervention group = 1, 3.3%). An additional 16 participants withdrew during the 20-week follow-up (LSERUSI-biofeedback group = 3, 11.1%; LSEno-biofeedback group = 4, 13.7%; minimal intervention group = 9, 31.0%) (Fig. 2). Dropouts were primarily due to loss of contact, illness, or personal issues. However, 5 participants in the LSEno-biofeedback group and 2 in the minimal intervention group reported temporary exacerbation of pain. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first RCT to examine if the incorporation of RUSI biofeedback during LSE would enhance LM CSA, pain intensity, functional disability, and QOL compared to LSE without biofeedback in patients with NCLBP.

Although the LSE program effectively improved LM CSA, both with and without biofeedback, compared to minimal intervention, adding biofeedback using RUSI yielded a greater effect, with a large effect size being observed at 20-week follow-up. Additionally, the magnitude of the group differences (~ 2.7 points) was slightly above the MCID threshold values (2.0 points) that were used to power the study. The maintenance of this effect, even though within the span of 20 weeks, is crucial to the efficiency of the LM to maintain stability and control of the spine, which is believed to prevent the recurrence of LBP. Proper and lasting activation of the LM is, therefore, crucial in LBP rehabilitation. RCTs on the additive effect of LSE training with RUSI biofeedback on LM CSA are limited. In a small controlled laboratory study, no significant difference in LM thickness was found after a single LSE session with or without RUSI biofeedback40, which is not on par with our study. However, the study applied a single treatment session, which may not have been adequate to elicit any significant change. On the other hand, a previous systematic review found RUSI feedback to be more effective than tactile and/or verbal biofeedback for improving LSE performance and retention success16, which is in agreement with our study.

The results that both pain intensity and functional disability were significantly improved in participants receiving LSE with and without biofeedback, compared to those receiving minimal intervention, support the efficacy and utility of this exercise program. Though the experimental groups were comparable, the percentage change in pain intensity and functional disability scores achieved by the LSERUSI-biofeedback group (57.7% and 67.9%, respectively) was slightly better than those achieved by the LSEno-biofeedback group (52.6% and 61.9% respectively) at 20-week follow-up. Importantly, the changes from baseline in both groups exceeded the MCID threshold of 30% improvement proposed for both pain intensity and functional disability measured using the NPRS and RMDQ41. In this regard, our study aligns with the existing literature supporting the efficacy of LSE in alleviating pain and disability in patients with NCLBP42,43.

Health-related quality of life is another important core outcome domain in non-specific LBP trials44, with improving this outcome being among the key goals of rehabilitation. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported the effectiveness of LSE in enhancing various domains of QOL45–50. However, only a few studies have reported QOL as physical and mental summary scores using the SF-12 or SF-36 health surveys26,51. Improvement in QOL may be attributed to improvement in pain and function52,53 as reduced QOL has been closely linked to these outcomes in NCLBP54. Notably, we found that individuals receiving LSERUSI-biofeedback had superior improvement compared to those receiving training without biofeedback or minimal intervention only for mental health at all time points, whereas training without biofeedback was superior for physical health at 20-week follow-up. The LSERUSI-biofeedback group may have shown greater mental health gains due to the motivational impact of visual biofeedback, enhancing confidence and self-efficacy. In contrast, better physical health in the LSEno-biofeedback group may reflect simpler, physical engagement with the exercises. Supporting our findings, a pilot study by Shaughnessy and Caulfield48 reported significant improvements across various domains of QOL using the SF-36 health survey in favor of the LSE program compared to no intervention. Specifically, greater improvements were observed in physical health domains (physical functioning, role physical, and bodily pain) and mental health domains (vitality, social functioning, role emotional, and mental health)48. Similarly, Burut and colleagues reported significant improvements in mental health, physical role, bodily pain, and vitality domains in favor of LSE plus conventional physiotherapy over conventional physiotherapy alone55. In contrast, other studies reported comparable effects on physical and mental health between LSE and graded activity50, as well as patient education51.

The results showed that post-intervention changes in LM CSA scores were weakly to moderately correlated with changes in pain intensity and functional disability scores. However, the study cannot verify the hypothesis that an increase in the LM size is influenced by changes in pain and disability. Instead, it appears that an increase in the LM size is related to changes in pain and disability. This is contrary to the conclusion of a previous systematic review21, which is based on the inclusion of two studies, reported no evidence that post-LSE increases in LM resting thickness in patients with acute non-specific LBP56 or LM CSA in patients with NCLBP57 were related to a reduction in pain. Additionally, no evidence suggested that post-LSE increases in LM CSA were related to changes in disability56. It is worth noting, however, that only one study was conducted on patients with NCLBP, and the authors did not incorporate RUSI biofeedback in LSE training57. The lack of correlation between changes in LM thickness and pain in the acute non-specific LBP study56 could be due to the fact that reduced LM thickness, which also reflects activation deficit, is likely to be caused by neural response in acute LBP, whereas reduced LM CSA results from disuse atrophy, fatty infiltration, change in muscle fiber type, which is commonly seen in chronic LBP58. The lack of correlation observed in the NCLBP study57, however, may be attributed to differences in intervention protocols, such as the absence of incorporating RUSI biofeedback or variations in the LSE training methods used. Further studies are warranted to verify these associations to enhance understanding of the role of LM in LBP.

This study has some limitations, which should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results. First, LM CSA was measured only at the L5 level, limiting the detection of changes at other lumbar levels despite evidence of L5-specific aberrations and recovery with rehabilitation25,59. Second, we could not rule out potential variability associated with ultrasound measurements of LM CSA, which can be operator-dependent and sensitive to subtle differences in probe placement and participant positioning, despite efforts to standardize the procedure. Third, the delivery of LSE by a single, highly experienced clinician may have introduced performance bias, potentially influencing the outcomes of the experimental groups. Fourth, incorporating RUSI biofeedback into the LSE program requires additional time, training, and resources for both patients and providers; hence, its use may be limited in clinics without access to ultrasound equipment. Fifth, although ITT analysis was conducted, the high attrition rate in the minimal intervention group at the 20-week follow-up may still bias the results by reducing group comparability and potentially overestimating the effectiveness of the experimental groups. Sixth, this study evaluated outcomes only in the short term, which limits our understanding of the long-term sustainability of the observed effects. It would be crucial to determine whether the changes in LM CSA are maintained over time, given the high recurrence rate of LBP. Lastly, while our study focused on the LM, excluding the TrA would limit interpretation of the intervention’s full neuromuscular impact. Future research should assess the TrA and consider incorporating diaphragm retraining, given its synergistic role in core stability alongside the LMM, pelvic floor, and TrA.

Conclusions

This study suggests that LSE training, with or without RUSI biofeedback, led to short-term improvements in LM CSA, pain intensity, functional disability, and QOL. However, LSE with RUSI biofeedback led to greater effects on LM CSA and mental health compared to training without biofeedback. Furthermore, an increase in LM CSA appears to be related to changes in pain intensity and functional disability.
