Cessation of Betel Quid Chewing, Smoking, and Alcohol Drinking and Risk of Oral Precancer and Oral Cancer
Authors: Fangya Mao, Tseng-Cheng Chen, Kelly J Yu, Li C Cheung, Wan-Lun Hsu, Ming-Hsui Tsai, Chung-Jan Kang, Chih-Yen Chien, Chun-Hung Hua, Hsiu-Hsi Chen, Sam L Chen, Amy M Yen, Barry I Graubard, Pei-Jen Lou, Chien-Jen Chen, Allan Hildesheim, Cheng-Ping Wang, Anil K Chaturvedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSETo quantify risk reduction in oral precancer and cancer with time since cessation of betel quid chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking.PATIENTS AND METHODSWe conducted a multicenter case-control study in Taiwan of patients with invasive oral cancer (n = 768), visual/clinical oral precancer (n = 1,998), and hospital-based controls (n = 717, recalibrated/reweighted to represent the Taiwan general population). Weighted logistic regression was used to evaluate associations of duration, intensity, and time since cessation of betel quid chewing without tobacco, smoking, and alcohol drinking (modeled using splines and categorically) with oral precancer and cancer risk versus controls.RESULTSRisk of oral precancer increased with increased duration and intensity of chewing and smoking, and increased intensity of alcohol drinking. Risk of oral cancer increased with increased duration and intensity of chewing, increased duration of smoking, and increased intensity of alcohol drinking. For oral precancer, when compared with current users (defined as current users and individuals who quit for ≤2 years before study recruitment), odds ratios (ORs) for 10-year cessation of behaviors were as follows: chewing = 0.63 (bootstrap 95% CI, 0.43 to 0.83); smoking = 0.30 (95% CI, 0.18 to 0.41); and alcohol = 0.60 (95% CI, 0.34 to 0.86). Likewise, risk of oral cancer decreased with increasing time since cessation; ORs for 10-year cessation were as follows: chewing = 0.74 (95% CI, 0.44 to 1.05); smoking = 0.45 (95% CI, 0.22 to 0.67); and alcohol drinking = 0.55 (95% CI, 0.20 to 0.90). Adjusted population-attributable fractions show that cessation of chewing for ≥10 years would prevent an estimated 23.2% of oral precancers and 24.2% of oral cancers; cessation of smoking for ≥10 years would prevent 51.5% of oral precancers and 44.6% of cancers in Taiwan.CONCLUSIONOur findings support the potential for primary prevention of oral precancer and cancer through cessation of betel quid chewing (without tobacco), smoking, and alcohol drinking among current users.

BACKGROUND

Chewing of smokeless tobacco and areca nut/betel quid with or without tobacco, smoking tobacco, and alcohol drinking are established risk factors for cancers of the oral cavity,1-4 which cause substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide (375,000 annual cases and 175,000 deaths).5 Smokeless tobacco and areca nut caused an estimated 30.8% of all oral cavity cancers worldwide (over 120,000 cancers) during 2022, with high population-attributable fractions (PAFs) in the Indian Subcontinent and Southeast Asia, where the burden of oral precancer and oral cancer is notably high.5 The profile of chewing exposure, however, differs across Asia. Chewing of smokeless tobacco and areca nut with tobacco is common in the Indian Subcontinent and chewing of betel quid without tobacco is common in Southeast Asia.5-7

Few studies have evaluated how risk of oral precancer and oral cancer decreases by time since cessation of risk behaviors, particularly for areca nut/betel quid chewing with or without tobacco.6,7 Indeed, given such paucity of published studies, the Internation Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) Handbook 19 on Oral Cancer Prevention (published in 2023) included primary data analysis in the evidence evaluation. Of note, our study presented herein contributed, in part, to the primary analysis for the Handbook evaluation.6,7 Based on such primary analyses, the Handbook 19 concluded that there is sufficient evidence that cessation of chewing areca nut/betel quid with or without tobacco, smoking, and alcohol drinking is associated with reduced risk of oral cavity precancers and cancers.6,7

Characterization of how risks of oral precancer and oral cancer decrease by time since cessation of chewing, smoking, and alcohol use has important implications for prevention and risk mitigation. Here, we report results from a multicenter case-control study of oral precancer and cancer in Taiwan, a country with high oral cancer burden due to high prevalence of betel quid chewing without tobacco.5 We specifically quantified how risk of oral precancer and oral cancer increases with increasing duration and intensity, and importantly, how risk decreases with increasing time since cessation for betel quid chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study Design

During 2013-2019, we recruited 1,998 patients with clinical/visual oral precancer (leukoplakia, erythroplakia, and oral submucous fibrosis),8 768 patients with invasive oral cavity cancer (lip, oral tongue, gingivae, floor of mouth, hard palate, and other mouth including buccal mucosa), and 717 hospital-based controls.9 The study was conducted at four medical centers in Taiwan: National Taiwan University Hospital (NTUH), Taipei; China Medical University Hospital (CMUH), Taichung; Chang-Gung Memorial Hospital-Linkou, Taoyuan; and Chang-Gung Memorial Hospital-Kaohsiung (CGMH-K). Patients with oral precancer were part of a prospective cohort study to investigate the natural history of multistate oral carcinogenesis.9 Here, we used data from the baseline, recruitment visit of precancer patients to conduct cross-sectional comparisons across the control–precancer–cancer continuum.

Methodological details of the study design and participant recruitment have been described previously.9 Briefly, patients with clinical oral precancer were identified through visual and tactile examination by ENT clinicians at each participating center. Patients with visually/clinically identified precancerous lesions with at least 0.5 cm in greatest diameter were eligible for recruitment. Precancer patients also provided at least one biopsy of visually/clinically identified lesions for histopathologic evaluation. Patients with histopathologically confirmed invasive oral cavity cancer were recruited before initiation of cancer treatment. Hospital-based controls were recruited at ENT departments at each hospital. A majority of controls (>50%) attended the ENT clinics for oral cancer screening, as part of Taiwan's national oral cancer screening program, and were found to be negative for clinical/visual oral precancerous/cancerous lesions. Controls were frequency matched to the combined group of precancers and cancers by age (5-year groups), sex, and history of ever smoking and ever chewing betel quid. Such matching for risk factors was to ensure adequate exposed controls for biomarker investigations.9

Individuals age 21+ years without a prior history of cancer were eligible for recruitment. Response rates for study participation, evaluated during the pilot phase of the study (July 2013-July 2014), were >90% for controls, patients with precancer, and patients with cancer.9

Ethical Review

The study was reviewed and approved by the US National Cancer Institute Special Studies Institutional Review Board (IRB, protocol No. 14CN018) and by the IRBs of NTUH, CMUH, GCMH-L, and CGMH-K. Study participation was contingent upon written informed consent (administered in Mandarin).

Exposure Assessment

At recruitment, through an interviewer-administered questionnaire, participants provided detailed information on demographics, socioeconomic status, anthropometrics, risk behaviors, medical history, self-reported oral health, and familial cancer history. Information on risk behaviors encompassed ever and current chewing of betel quid, smoking, and alcohol drinking, ages at initiation and cessation (if former user) of each habit, and history of use (duration and intensity) over the life course, starting with current age and across decades of life to the age at initiation.9 Data on alcohol drinking were collected separately by beverage type, and alcohol intensity was calculated as the sum of number of drinks per week of hard liquor, rice/sorghum spirits, beer, and wine.

Statistical Analyses

Analyses were cross-sectional and used exposure information and disease status at recruitment. Patients with clinical precancers and cancers recruited into the study were similar in demographic (age and sex), clinical (type of oral precancer, prevalence of histopathologic dysplasia grades, and anatomic site of precancer/cancer), and behavioral characteristics (ever chewing and ever smoking) when compared with data from the Taiwan national oral cancer screening program (for precancers) and to the Taiwan national cancer registry (for cancers). However, owing to matching on age, sex, and ever chewing and ever smoking status of cases (precancers + cancers), controls were more likely to be men and ever chewers/smokers when compared with the Taiwan general population. Given such nonrepresentativeness of our study controls, to enable unbiased statistical comparisons, we recalibrated our controls to the 2017 Taiwan National Health Interview Survey by developing sampling weights using raking ratio estimation (Appendix Tables A1 and A2 and Fig A1).9 Thus, our analyses used a weight of 1.0 for precancers and cancers and the estimated sampling weights for controls. Such recalibration gives higher weights to never chewers and never smokers in the study controls (Appendix Tables A1 and A2) to make risk factor distributions similar to the Taiwan general population.

All analyses were conducted separately for precancers (v controls) and cancers (v controls) using weighted binary logistic regression models, with associations quantified through odds ratios (ORs) and bootstrap 95% CIs that accounted for weighting. Based on reported life course exposures to risk factors, we created cumulative durations of betel quid chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking and average intensity of use (number of betel quid pieces chewed daily, number of cigarettes smoked daily, and number of alcoholic drinks [beer, wine, liquor, and rice spirits] consumed per week). Time since cessation of each risk behavior was calculated as the difference of current age and age at cessation. We used cubic B-splines (linear, quadratic, and cubic) with boundary knots at the minimum and 95th percentile of the observed values to accommodate potential nonlinear effects of duration, intensity, and time since cessation of each risk behavior.10 Analyses of the effects of duration and intensity of each behavior on risk of oral precancer or cancer were conducted in the full study population (never and ever users) and included duration and intensity of the habit under consideration and intensity of other habits. For example, models for betel quid chewing included duration and intensity of chewing and intensities of smoking and alcohol drinking. Such modeling was done to avoid multicollinearity from highly correlated durations of betel quid chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking (Appendix Table A3). All models were adjusted for age, sex, and highest level of education.

For the effect of cessation of each risk behavior, we initially evaluated evidence for reverse causation (ie, the onset of symptoms leading to cessation of risk behavior) by comparing risk of precancer or cancer among never users (reference group) versus current users and former users stratified by time since cessation (≤1 year before study recruitment, 2 years, 3-5 years, 6-10 years, 11-15 years, and >15 years). We then evaluated the relationship of time since cessation of each risk behavior (using cubic splines) in separate weighted binary logistic regression models restricted to ever users of the respective behavior. These models included adjustment for age, sex, level of education, time since cessation of the respective risk behavior, and intensities of chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking. For example, analyses of the effect of time since cessation of chewing were restricted to ever chewers and included age, sex, level of education, time since cessation of chewing, chewing intensity, smoking intensity, and alcohol intensity. We also estimated the effect of age at cessation of risk behaviors in alternative models that used age at cessation (as a linear variable) instead of age at recruitment, with similar covariate adjustment noted herein.

Multiplicative statistical interactions across intensities of betel quid chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking were evaluated through product terms; these models included linear terms for intensities and were adjusted for age, sex, education, chewing duration, and intensities of chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking. We estimated the impact of cessation of each behavior (betel quid chewing, smoking, and alcohol) for at least 10 years through PAFs using adjusted PAF = PE|D x (OR-1)/OR, where PE|D is the prevalence of exposure precancer or cancer cases and OR is the adjusted OR.11 Briefly, for each behavior, current users and former users who quit for <10 years were counterfactually moved to 10 years since cessation for PAF estimation. Such counterfactual PAFs did not change the intensity of each risk behavior. Bootstrap 95% CIs (estimated from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 500 bootstrap estimates) were estimated around PAFs.

Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.0) and SAS version 9.4. Two-sided P values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Table 1 shows the characteristics of study controls (unweighted and reweighted/recalibrated to the Taiwan general population), patients with clinical oral precancer, and patients with invasive oral cavity cancer. Among controls, weighted prevalence of ever chewing, ever smoking, and ever drinking was 11.2%, 24.9%, and 21.1%, respectively. By contrast, prevalence of ever chewing, ever smoking, and ever drinking was substantially higher among patients with oral precancer or cancer. Among precancers, 55.5% reported cessation of chewing before study recruitment, 18.5% reported cessation of smoking, and 17.7% reported cessation of alcohol drinking (Table 1). Among cancers, 66.1% reported cessation of chewing before study recruitment, 31.0% reported cessation of smoking, and 26.8% reported cessation of alcohol drinking.

Associations of Chewing

Compared with controls, risk of oral precancer and cancer increased significantly with increasing duration of chewing (Fig 1A: OR for chewing duration of 20 years for precancer = 3.5 [95% CI, 2.3 to 4.7] and for cancer = 4.2 [95% CI, 2.0 to 6.4]) and increased intensity of chewing (Fig 1B: OR for 20 pieces per day for precancer = 3.2 [95% CI, 2.0 to 4.4] and for cancer = 4.5 [95% CI, 2.2 to 6.7]).

We found strong evidence for reverse causation among former chewers (Fig 1C). Compared with never chewers, former chewers who quit ≤1 year before recruitment or 2 years before recruitment had high risks of both precancer and cancer. Such risks among former users were notably higher than risks observed among current users (v never users), indicating that the onset of symptoms in the mouth from precancer or cancer led to cessation of chewing. To guard against such bias from reverse causation, we defined current chewers as individuals who reported chewing at recruitment and those who reported cessation of chewing within ≤2 years of study participation. Results were similar in sensitivity analyses that used alternative cutoffs of 1-year or 3-year quitters as current chewers (Appendix Fig A2).

Among ever chewers (Fig 1D), when compared with current chewers (current + cessation ≤2 years), increasing time since cessation was associated with strongly decreased risk of oral precancer and cancer (OR for 10 years of cessation of chewing for precancer = 0.63 [95% CI, 0.43 to 0.83] and for cancer = 0.74 [95% CI, 0.44 to 1.05]). Cessation of chewing at older ages was significantly associated with increased risk of cancer (1.7% increase per year increase in age at cessation, P = .045).

The magnitude of increased risk with increasing duration and intensity of chewing was generally higher for cancer than precancer; however, these differences were not statistically significant. Reduced risks with increasing time since cessation was stronger for precancer than cancer, but these differences were again not statistically significant.

Associations of Smoking

Risk of oral precancer increased significantly with increasing duration of smoking (Fig 2A: OR for smoking duration of 20 years for precancer = 5.3 [95% CI, 2.0 to 8.5]) and intensity of smoking (Fig 2B: OR for 20 cigarettes per day for precancer = 2.4 [95% CI, 1.0 to 3.7]). Risk of cancer also increased with increasing duration of smoking (Fig 2A).

We found some evidence for reverse causation among former smokers (Fig 2C), but the magnitude of such bias was relatively lower for smoking than for chewing. Among ever smokers (Fig 2D), when compared with current smokers (current + cessation ≤2 years), increasing time since cessation was associated with strongly decreased risk of oral precancer and cancer (OR for 10 years of cessation of smoking for precancer = 0.30 [95% CI, 0.18 to 0.41] and for cancer = 0.45 [95% CI, 0.22 to 0.67]). Results were similar in sensitivity analyses that used alternative cutoffs of 1-year or 3-year quitters as current smokers (Appendix Fig A2).

Age at cessation of smoking was not statistically significantly associated with risk of oral precancer or cancer (not shown).

The magnitude of increased risk with increasing duration and intensity of smoking was nonsignificantly higher for precancer than cancer, while reduced risks with increasing time since cessation were nonsignificantly stronger for precancer than cancer.

Associations of Alcohol Drinking

Duration of alcohol drinking was not associated with risk of oral precancer or cancer, while intensity of drinking was associated with moderately increased risk of precancer or cancer (Fig 3). However, increasing time since cessation of alcohol drinking was significantly associated with reduced risk of oral precancer (OR for 10 years of drinking cessation for precancer = 0.60 [95% CI, 0.34 to 0.86] and for cancer = 0.55 [95% CI, 0.20 to 0.90]). Age at cessation of alcohol drinking was not significantly associated with risk of oral precancer or cancer (not shown).

Interactions Across Intensities of Chewing, Smoking, and Alcohol

For both oral precancer and oral cancer, we found evidence for submultiplicative statistical interactions between chewing intensity and smoking intensity and between chewing intensity and alcohol intensity (Appendix Tables A4-A6).

PAFs for Cessation of Chewing, Smoking, and Alcohol for at Least 10 Years

An estimated 23.2% of oral precancers and 24.2% of oral cancers could be prevented in the Taiwan population if chewers achieved at least 10 years of cessation of chewing (Table 2). Likewise, an estimated 51.5% of oral precancers and 44.6% of cancers could be prevented in the Taiwan population if smokers achieved at least 10 years of cessation of smoking. PAFs for cessation of alcohol drinking were not statistically significant and lower in magnitude. Of note, these PAFs for cessation are relatively lower than our previously reported PAFs for removal of exposures (chewing, smoking, and alcohol)9 because the effects of intensity and duration of use up to the age at cessation have already accumulated.

DISCUSSION

Our case-control study across the control–clinical/visual oral precancer–invasive oral cancer continuum in Taiwan provides strong evidence for the beneficial effects of cessation of betel quid chewing (without tobacco), smoking, and alcohol drinking in reducing risk of both (visual/clinical) oral precancer and cancer. Such reductions in risk of precancer were apparent within 5-10 years of cessation of behaviors. Furthermore, we estimate that among ever chewers, cessation of betel quid chewing for at least 10 years could prevent approximately 23%-24% of oral precancers and cancers in the Taiwan population, and among ever smokers, cessation of smoking for at least 10 years could prevent approximately 44%-51% of oral precancers and cancers in Taiwan.

There are few published studies that have reported the pattern of risk reduction from time since cessation of betel quid chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking. For example, in the IARC Handbook 19 evaluation of the primary prevention through cessation of betel quid chewing without tobacco,6,7 six published studies were identified for oral precancer12-17 and four published studies were identified for oral cancer.13,18-21 Of these, only one study for oral cancer reported risk reduction with time since cessation18; all other studies only reported risks for former betel quid chewers (v current chewers or never chewers). Likewise, few published studies have reported the impact of time since cessation of smoking or cessation of alcohol drinking on risk of oral precancer. Only one study reported risk reduction for oral precancer by time since cessation of smoking, and there are no studies for time since cessation of alcohol drinking.6,7 Our results from this large, comprehensive case-control study in Taiwan advance the state of the science on primary prevention by showing that cessation of betel quid chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking is associated with reduced risk of oral precancer and cancer.

Few tools are currently available to enable cessation of risk behaviors, particularly for betel quid chewing.6,7 This contrasts with several evaluations of interventions for cessation of smokeless tobacco chewing and smoking.6,7 Likewise, the impact of primary prevention policies (eg, taxation and prohibition of spitting in public places) on cessation of betel quid chewing remains unstudied.6,7 Thus, our observations point to the need for evaluations of behavioral and pharmacologic interventions to enable cessation of betel quid chewing among current users.

We note the study limitations. Given the retrospective assessment of exposure (ie, at or after diagnosis of oral precancer or cancer), it is likely that reported behaviors for betel quid chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking had recall bias. Such misclassification, most likely differential by disease status, may have led to overestimation of the effects of duration and intensity of chewing, smoking, and alcohol drinking on risk of oral precancer and cancer. Similarly, the onset of symptoms of oral precancer or cancer in the mouth could have affected risk behaviors (ie, reverse causation). Indeed, we noticed evidence for such a phenomenon, with individuals who quit betel quid chewing and smoking for fewer than 2 years before study recruitment having higher risk of oral precancer and cancer when compared with current users. To avoid such bias from reverse causation, we classified recent quitters (≤2 years of cessation) with current users for each behavior. Our definition of oral precancer was based on clinical/visual detection of left-censored lesions (ie, lesions were present/persistent for an unknown duration before study recruitment), and thus represents a mix of lesions with heterogeneous risk of progression to cancer. We note that each of the patients with prevalent oral precancer received a biopsy for histopathologic diagnosis and a wide range of lesion-specific (oral brush cytology), oral mucosal field–specific (oral rinses and saliva), and systemic (blood) biospecimens. Ongoing and future investigations are anticipated to refine the definition of oral precancer by integrating pathologic, molecular, and genomic features to better estimate the benefits of cessation of risk behaviors on reducing risk of oral precancer with greater risk of progression to cancer. The strengths of our study include the large sample size and population representation of the study participants (controls through post-stratification/reweighting, precancers, and cancers) to the respective target Taiwan population.

In conclusion, our study provides strong evidence for the potential for primary prevention of oral precancer and cancer through cessation of betel quid chewing (without tobacco), smoking, and alcohol drinking among current users. These results could enable patient counseling and policy recommendations regarding the benefits of cessation of risk behaviors.

APPENDIX 1. SUPPLEMENTAL METHODS AND RESULTS

Imputation of Missing Variables

Individuals in cohort had missing values related to education (n = 2), chewing behavior (n = 188), smoking behavior (n = 208), or alcohol use (n = 89). Missing variables were imputed to the weighted mode (for categorical variables) or the weighted median (for continuous variables) according to their baseline status (control, precancer, or cancer). For controls, weights derived by the raking ratio estimation procedure were used; for precancers and cancers, a weight of 1 was used. Some missing variables have a restricted range of valid values due to a nonmissing response to another variable (eg, a former chewer’s missing age in which they quit chewing cannot exceed their current age). For such missing variables, the imputed value was set to the median difference among individuals with nonmissing values for both variables (eg, current age minus the median years since individuals quit chewing).

Recalibration of Study Controls With Taiwan’s 2017 National Health Interview Survey (reproduced from supplement of Wang et al9)

Study controls were sampled to have a similar distribution of sex, age, and chewing/smoking status as study precancers. Although this design is useful for biomarker studies, for some analyses, population-representative controls are needed. Therefore, we developed sampling weights for study controls using raking ratio estimation (ie, sample-balancing) procedures (Deville and Särndal 1992; Kalton 1983) such that the marginal totals of the weighted controls for characteristics used for control selection would agree with the corresponding totals from the Taiwan population, age 20 years or older.

Raking ratio estimation among controls was conducted separately by sex (643 males and 74 females) to match the following sex-specific distributions in the Taiwan population (9,190,414 males and 9,644,054 females): (a) the joint distribution of ever smoking status and age groups of 20-<50 and 50+ years, (b) the joint distribution of ever chewing status and age groups of 20-<50 and 50+ years, and (c) ever smoking and chewing status.
