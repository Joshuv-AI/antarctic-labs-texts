Effect of Xanthan gum-based edible coating enriched with cloves and cinnamon for extending the shelf-life of pomegranate fruit during cold storage
Authors: Chaimae El-Rhouttais, Kaoutar Elfazazi, Zahra El Kettabi, Salah Laaraj, Mourad Elgoulli, Mohammed Al-Zharani, Fahd A Nasr, Ashraf Ahmed Qurtam, Yassine Bouslihim, Souad Salmaoui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe edible coating used for preserving fruits during storage is receiving heightened interest due to its biodegradable properties and its effectiveness in extending shelf life. In the present study, we investigated the effect of Xanthan gum based edible coating, applied individually or enriched with clove or cinnamon essential oil, on the postharvest quality of pomegranate (cv. ‘Sefri Ouled Abdellah’) during cold storage at 4 °C for a period of 75 days. The results demonstrated that both xanthan gum coatings enriched with clove essential oil (XANCL) and cinnamon essential oil (XANCN) effectively preserved fruit quality compared to the uncoated control. Among these, XANCN was the most effective, delaying weight loss (0 to 9.67%), stabilizing pH (4.14 to 4.18) and TA content (0.18 to 0.14), and preventing the degradation of TPC (580.6 to 503.94 mg GAE 100 mL−1), TAC (528,24 to 560,49 mg L−1) and TFC (33.670 to 36.87 mg RE 100 mL−1). Xanthan gum enriched with cinnamon essential oil proved particularly effective in maintaining the quality of pomegranate fruit during cold storage at 4 °C. These findings highlight the potential of xanthan gum-based coatings as a cost-effective and environmentally friendly strategy for extending the shelf life of pomegranates while maintaining their technological and biochemical attributes. Introduction

The pomegranate is still one of the most popular agricultural products in the tropical and subtropical regions of the world, with a particular focus on the Mediterranean countries due to their favorable climatic conditions1,2. The high phenolic content and antioxidant properties of this fruit contribute to its status as a superfood for human consumption3,4. As production increases, implementing effective marketing strategies and storage conditions is essential in order to provide consumers with high fruit quality for both domestic and export markets. The non-climacteric nature of the fruit leads to both qualitative and quantitative losses during post-harvest, resulting in a very short shelf life at room temperature5–7. Depending on the cultivar, the optimum storage temperature for pomegranates ranges from 0 to 10 °C, with a storage period of 2 weeks to 5 months7–9. Apparently, Cold storage is beneficial in extending the shelf life of pomegranate after harvest, but can induce symptoms of cold damage. These symptoms may include changes to the peel, increased incidence of rot, internal decay, and ultimately lead to a loss of quality10,11.

In recent years, there has been a significant increase in worldwide demand for pomegranate fruits, largely due to their perceived abundance of therapeutic phytochemicals10. This rising demand and consumer interest have consequently led to a steady increase in production within the producing countries12 Efficient conservation of the fruit is crucial to the successful preservation of fruits quality until it reaches the retail market. Several techniques are available for preserving the quality and prolonging the shelf life of pomegranate fruits during cold storage13,14. This underscores the need for the development of innovative technologies to improve their visual features, increase their shelf life, and offer better storage conditions15,16. These methods include intermittent heating, curing, film wrapping, application of polyamines, controlled atmosphere storage, modified atmosphere packaging and waxing, among others13,14,17,18.

Given current consumer trends, there is a growing demand and preference for environmentally friendly packaging that is free of plastic. In response, various biodegradable postharvest technologies have been proposed to reduce postharvest losses in fresh produce19,20. However, only a limited number of studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of biodegradable alternatives in providing enhanced packaging solutions for various types of fresh product, including pomegranate. Nevertheless, the application of biodegradable and edible coatings has recently shown considerable potential as an alternative postharvest method for preserving the quality and extending the storage duration of fresh fruit21–23.

Edible coatings, also known as food coatings or food films, are thin layers of edible substances applied directly to the surface of fresh fruit24. These coatings can be applied by brushing, dipping, or spraying25. They serve multiple functions, including protecting against moisture loss and microbial proliferation24. Apparently, they can extend the storage duration of a product, preserve its freshness, enhancing its visual appeal, and improve its overall quality26. Edible coatings can be derived from natural substances such as proteins (e.g., gelatin, whey protein)27,28lipids (e.g., waxes, oils)28carbohydrates (e.g., starches, gums)27and other food-grade additives.

The use of edible gums is gaining popularity among researchers and pomegranate producers due to their natural, cheaper, encapsulation, film forming, and emulsification properties27,29,30.This leads to the reduction of water, respiration and oxidation reaction by creating a modified atmosphere. They form a semi-permeable barrier that limits the movement of oxygen, carbon dioxide, solute and moisture27,30. Numerous research studies have demonstrated the efficacy of edible gums in extending the shelf life of different pomegranate cultivars during cold storage. In this case, several gums have been used to improve the storability of fruit, including Tragacanth gum31Persian gum32 and gum Arabic33.

The Xanthan Gum (XG), an exo-polysaccharide synthesized by Xanthomonas campestris under unfavorable circumstances, is a compound generally recognized as a safe (GRAS)34. The xanthan gum has demonstrated superior capabilities in controlling enzyme inactivation and browning pigmentation compared to acacia Senegal and karaya gums27. Its exceptional resistance to enzyme deterioration makes it highly effective as a coating for both fresh-cut and whole fruits, resulting in a long-lasting protection35. Incorporating XG into food products allows for the dual benefits of extending shelf life and improving visual appeal by producing a glossy surface36. Previous studies have explored the application of XG coatings on several fresh-cut fruits, such as tomatoes37pears38and apple39with the goal of preserving their quality during storage. Its safety for use in foods was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1969, and is based mainly on its unique rheological properties34. These properties enable the formation of viscous solutions at low concentrations and ensure stability across a broad range of pH and temperature40.

In recent years, innovative food preservation methods have focused on developing coatings with active ingredients that enhance food safety, increase its shelf life, and minimize quality losses41. A number of active ingredients have been used for antimicrobial, antioxidant and antibrowning properties, to inhibit the growth of microorganisms on the surface42. Examples of such active ingredients are essential oils, enzymes, polyamines, plant extracts, spices (natural preservatives) and various others. Research has highlighted several natural preservatives, including cinnamon, clove, rosemary, thyme, basil, lemon leaf, balm, ginger, oregano, basilica, coriander and many of which are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) in the food industry43.

Cinnamon and clove are generally described as powerful antioxidant, antibacterial and antifungal agent44. A previous study has confirmed that cinnamon and clove possess effective free radical scavenging activity as a result of the presence of bioactive compounds45. Cinnamaldehyde is the major constituent responsible for its high antioxidant activity in Cinnamon46. Additionally, Eugenol is the primary bioactive component present in clove, occurring in concentrations ranging from 9381.70 to 14650.00 mg per 100 g of fresh plant material47 and had the most powerful antioxidant activity and radical-scavenging activity48.

In most studies, the application of xanthan gum to fruit stored at low temperatures prevented losses due to spoilage and preserved the overall quality of the fruit. Several researchers have studied the effects of XG-based edible coatings on maintaining postharvest quality and extend the shelf life of various fruits. These studies have focused on fruits such as strawberries49fresh-cut pears38apples50guavas51fresh-cut melons52baby carrots53 and grapes27. These coatings have been shown to maintain physicochemical, sensory and textural properties, preserve color, and slowing the respiration rate and ethylene release during storage.

Despite the availability of similar studies, there is limited data on the effect of xanthan gum on pomegranate fruit quality during cold storage. To the best of our knowledge, no research has yet investigated the impact of Xanthan coating gum enriched with active components such as cinnamon and cloves essential oil on the post-harvest quality of pomegranate fruit during storage. Given the economic importance and health benefits of pomegranate fruits, this study aims to develop Xanthan gum coating enriched with cinnamon and cloves essential oil, evaluate their effectiveness in prolonging shelf life, and preserving the morphological, physicochemical, and biochemical attributes of ‘Sefri Ouled Abdellah’ pomegranates during cold storage.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Pomegranate cultivar ‘Sefri Oulad Abdellah’ was harvested in October 2023 from the Beni Mellal region of central Morocco (23°50′05″ E; 6°48′98″ N) and transported to the Laboratory of Agro-Food Technology and Quality of the Regional Center for Agricultural Research of Beni Mellal-Tadla (CRRAT), Morocco. The selected fruits were fully mature, untreated, and exhibited uniformity in firmness, color, size, and shape.

Experimental design and Preparation of dipping solutions

Four coating solutions were prepared following Sarpong et al.54: XAN (10 g xanthan gum), XANCN (XAN + 1% w/v cinnamon essential oil), XANCL (XAN + 1% w/v clove essential oil), and CON (distilled water + 1% v/v glycerol + Tween-20). The gum coating solutions were prepared by dissolved 10 g the gum powder in 100 mL water with 3% (w/v) calcium chloride to improve wettability. The solution was heated to 40 °C for 60 min, and plasticized with 1% (w/v) glycerol. The pomegranates were brushed with the coating, allowed to air dried for 30 min, stored at 4 ± 1 °C and 85–90% RH for 75 days. Measurements were conducted on day 0 (1 h after coating) and every two weeks until day 75. The main technological, physicochemical, and biochemical properties of nine pomegranate fruits per formulation were analyzed, with each measurement performed in triplicate.

Technological properties

Weight loss

The weight loss was assessed by measuring the whole pomegranate at the end of each storage duration55. This analysis was performed on the same 15 fruits throughout the entire storage period, with measurements taken every two weeks. The formula used to calculate weight loss is represented as a percentage of weight loss relative to the initial weight before storage.

Color attributes

The color of pomegranate peels was determined in CIELAB coordinates (L*, a*, b*) using a Minolta Chroma Meter CR −400 (Minolta Corp., Osaka, Japan)56–58. Measurements were performed in a cold room to avoid temperature fluctuations (4 °C). Peel color measurements were used to monitor changes in the external color of the fruit. These measurements were taken at three designated surface areas located around the middle of the fruit. Physicochemical properties

Fruit per varieties were sampled for physicochemical analysis (pH, titratable acidity (TA), total soluble solids (TSS)). Juice was extracted using a juice extractor (Mellerware, South Africa). The pH of the juice was determined at room temperature using a pH meter (Thermo Orien 3 star, USA). Titratable acidity (TA) was determined by titrating 10 mL of juice with 0.1 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to a pH of 8.1, and results were expressed as g citric acid equivalent/L. Total soluble solids (TSS) were measured using a digital refractometer (Metteler-Toledo GmbH, Switzerland).

Biochemical properties

Total phenolic compounds (TPC)

Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method59. Briefly, 300 µL of juice diluted 1:20 with methanol: water (6:4) was mixed with 1.2 mL of 7.5% sodium carbonate and 1.5 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent diluted in the ratio of 1:10. After 90 min of the reaction at room temperature, absorbance was measured using a UV-Visible spectrophotometer (Schimadzu-UV-2401PC) at 760 nm. Results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalent per 100 mL of juice.

Total anthocyanin content (TAC)

Total anthocyanin content (TAC) was determined according to the method described by Ozgen et al.60 using two buffer systems: Sodium acetate buffer pH 4.5 (0.4 M) and potassium chloride buffer pH 1.0 (25 mM). Briefly, 0.4 mL of pomegranate juice was mixed with 3.6 mL of corresponding buffers, and absorbance was determined at 510 and 700 nm. Water was used as a blank. Total flavonoids content (TFC)

The total flavonoid content of the juices is measured using spectrophotometry, based on the method outlined by Lamaison et al.61. This method based on the formation of a complex flavonoid-aluminium, having the maximum absorbance at 430 nm. To carry out the.

measurement, 1 mL of the diluted sample is combined with 1 mL of a 2% methanolic solution of aluminum chloride. After incubating at room temperature for 15 min in obscurity, the absorbance of the resulting mixture is measured at 430 nm with a UV-Visible spectrophotometer (Schimadzu-UV-2401PC). The flavonoid content is reported as mg of rutin equivalent per 100 mL of juice.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed in triplicate. All variables studied are expressed as mean (± S.E.). Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was performed using SPSS software version 26 (IBM Software, Inc., USA). Statistical analysis, plotting, and treatment comparisons were conducted using Python in Google Colab.

Technological properties

Weight loss

The weight loss is commonly used as a quality index in the postharvest life of fruits62this loss is typically attributed to fruit senescence or desiccation63. In this study, weight loss increased progressively with the advancement of storage time for both coated and uncoated pomegranate fruits (Fig. 1). The uncoated fruits exhibited a significantly (P < 0.001) higher weight loss compared to the coated fruits during the entire storage period. Additionally, the control fruits lost weight faster than the coated fruits, reaching the highest percentage of 12.06% by the end of the storage period. In contrast, the coated fruits showed a lower weight loss, ranging from 11.15 to 9.67%. Among the coatings, XANCN resulted in the lowest weight loss (9.67%) followed by XANCL (9.79%) and XAN gum (11.14%) by the end of the storage.

Changes in morphological parameters of pomegranate fruit during storage. Each point represents mean of three replicates. P < 0.001 difference between the groups.

The weight loss of fruits is a result of the transpiration process, which involves the loss of water vapor through the surface of the fruits10. This natural metabolic process contributes to the shriveling and deterioration of the fruit64. The extent of weight loss depends on the gradient of water vapor pressure between the surrounding atmosphere and the fruit tissue65. Additionally, the increase in weight loss over the storage period may be attributed to an increase in moisture loss from the fruits66.

These results are consistent with previous findings that have demonstrated xanthan gum´s ability to reduce water loss during storage. Subsequently, these results are confirmed in several matrices, such as Strawberry Fruit67rambutan fruit68and grape fruit27. Xanthan gum is frequently used as a food additive and thickening agent, possessing the property of being able to create a thick and semipermeable coating69. This coating acts as a protective barrier against gases including oxygen, carbon dioxide, and moisture, which have the potential to modify the internal atmosphere surrounding fruits and other perishable items. Additionally, it serves as an additional layer that covers the stomata and forms a film on the fruit´s skin66. This barrier serves to prolong the shelf life of the fruit by minimizing moisture loss and decelerating the oxidation process, thereby preserving the product’s quality for an extended duration. Quoc et al.70 reported that xanthan gum could decrease the respiration rate and subsequent weight loss of fruits during storage. Zaharioudakis et al.71 reported that the combination of kappa-carrageenan and 1% xanthan gum has been shown to reduce weight loss in table olives.

Color attributes

Peel color is a crucial quality parameter in the marketing of pomegranate72as it greatly influences market acceptance and consumer preferences73. Throughout the storage period, the average L* value underwent significant changes (P < 0.001) in both uncoated and coated pomegranate fruits (Figs. 1 and 2). Notably, there was a marked decrease in the L* value for CON and XANCN during the storage period, whereas an increase was observed in the L* value for XAN. In contrast, the L* value of XANCL-coated fruits remained relatively constant, and showed no significant differences between samples during storage. Similarly, the a* value for XANCL exhibited a trend similar to that observed for the L* parameter during storage, indicating significant stability in XANCL-coated fruits (Figs. 1 and 2). In contrast, XANCN showed stability until the 60th day of storage, followed by a decrease until the end of the storage period. The non-coated fruits exhibited a notable increase in browning tendency and red hue compared to the various coated fruits. Additionally, significant changes (P < 0.001) in the fruit skin b* value were observed for both CON and XAN coatings during storage (Figs. 1 and 2). However, the XANCL and XANCN coatings demonstrated stability in the b* value throughout the storage period. The C* and h° values exhibited similar changes during the storage period (Figs. 1 and 2). The CON samples showed a significant decrease in both C* and h° during cold storage, while these parameters increased in pomegranate fruits coated with XAN. In contrast, XANCL and XANCN displayed slight changes in C* and h° throughout the storage duration, likely due to the coatings preserving the fruit’s coloration. These results were also reflected in the total color change (ΔE), with the gum coatings showing lower values, following the trend XANCL < XANCN < XAN < Control, corresponding to 2.58, 6.50, 11.18, and 15.21 under cold storage, respectively.

Variation of morphological parameters between D0 and D5.

Previous research has shown that the results of long-term storage of sweet pomegranate fruit are comparable74. The observed color changes are consistent with previous studies by El-wahab et al.75 and Shaarawi et al.76which reported a significant color change of uncoated pomegranate during cold storage. On the other hand, our findings align with several studies indicating that coatings can mitigate color alterations in pomegranates. For instance, Varasteh et al.77 demonstrated that 1% and 2% chitosan coatings effectively maintained the quality of the “Rabbabe Neyriz” pomegranate variety by preserving the rind color, reducing browning, and creating a thin, glossy layer on the fruit’s surface. Additionally, Khedr78 reported that 5% gum arabic is beneficial in reducing husk browning in the ‘Wonderful’ pomegranate variety. A Similar study showed that Xanthan gum reduces color changes in guava and pear fruits during storage36. Likewise, a study carried out on tomatoes demonstrated that the total color difference increased rapidly for non-coated samples but decreased gradually for coated ones79.

The stability in the a* and b* parameters in pomegranate fruit coated with XAN enriched with cinnamon and clove. The results are perhaps explained by the fact that cinnamon is rich in cinnamaldehyde, a compound known for its antioxidant, antifungal and antibacterial properties44. Similarly, clove contains eugenol, a compound with similar properties48. These chemicals can retain the color of the fruit peel by reducing the enzymatic activity responsible for color deterioration80preventing the growth of microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi that contribute to spoilage81and inhibiting degradation caused by oxidation44. However, it is important to note that the effectiveness of these spices can vary depending on several factors, such as the concentration used, the type of fruit, and storage conditions80. Color changes are frequently caused by oxidative reactions that lead to the breakdown of pigments82.

pH

The pH showed an increasing trend in both control and coated pomegranate fruits throughout the storage period (Figs. 3 and 4). The pH for CON and XAN fruits increased rapidly during storage, reaching maximum values of 4.49 ± 0.008 and 4.54 ± 0.042 in CON and XAN, respectively, by the end of the storage period. In contrast, the pH of pomegranate fruit coated with XANCN and XANCL exhibited a slight increase. Notably, XANCL maintained stable pH levels until 75 days of storage, followed by an increase until the end of the storage period. The Xanthan gum enriched with cinnamon and cloves effectively slowed pH changes, thereby delaying fruit senescence.

The application of various coatings can potentially decrease the metabolism of fruits, as these coatings are known to maintain higher levels of carbon dioxide (CO2) and lower levels of oxygen (O2) inside the coated fruits83. This effect may explain the retention of higher acid levels and, consequently, lower pH values compared to the control82.

In this study, the observed effect was likely attributed to the formation of a semi-permeable Xanthum gel on the fruit’s surface, which could have altered the internal atmosphere, specifically affecting the natural concentrations of CO2 and O2 within the fruit during storage84. The increase in pH could be attributed to the decomposition of acids through respiration during storage65. On the other hand, Quoc et al.70 reported that xanthan gum has the potential to reduce respiration rates. This finding suggests a possible explanation for the lower pH value observed in pomegranate fruits coated with xanthan gum compared to uncoated ones. However, Adetunji et al.85 found that the pH of Carica Papaya Linn fruits coated with xanthan gum is higher than that of untreated fruits. Additionally, fresh-cut pears coated with xanthan gum showed variation in pH during storage38.

Our results clearly indicate that fruits coated with XAN gum enriched with clove or cinnamon exhibit significantly lower pH in their juice compared to uncoated fruits or those coated with XAN gum. This could be due to the effect of the added spices (clove and cinnamon), which potentially have a low pH that alters the overall pH of the juice from the coated fruits.

Titratable acidity (TA)

Titratable acidity (TA) is the most important parameter for assessing fruit quality during storage. The flavor of fruits is influenced by acidity, which is determined by the presence of organic acids such as malic, citric, and tartaric acid64. The citric acid being the major organic acid in pomegranate fruit. The amount of TA decreased significantly in all coated and control fruit with increasing storage time.

The results showed that xanthan gum coating slowed the reduction of TA, following the trend Control < XAN < XANCL < XANCN corresponding to 0.105, 0.11, 0.13 and 0.14% citric acid under cold storage, respectively (Figs. 4 and 5). The significant decrease in TA observed in control fruits compared to coated fruits could be attributed to natural ripening processes or the utilization of organic acids essential for respiratory, which accelerates fruit senescence86. This acceleration leads to a reduction in respiration rate30. Additionally, these results suggest that the coating may delay ripening by creating a modified atmosphere around the fruit surface and forming a barrier against oxygen and carbon dioxide. Consequently, the application of coatings to ‘sefri ouled abdellah’ pomegranates prevents the loss of organic acids by inhibiting their oxidative reduction, thereby maintaining a higher concentration of TA87.

Changes in TA of pomegranate fruit juice during storage.

The results are in accordance with Sharma et al.38who reported that fresh-cut pears coated with Xanthan gum showed a declining trend in total acidity (TA) with increasing storage time. Nevertheless, tomatoes coated with Xanthan gum exhibited dissimilar results, with a decrease in TA that was lower than that of non-coated fruits by the end of the storage period79. In a similar study, pomegranates coated with chitosan, carnauba wax, and resin wax exhibited results comparable to our study, where pomegranates coated with Xanthan gum slowed the reduction of TA compared to the control88. In contrast, Ghasemnezhad et al.89 reported a significant increase in TA in chitosan-coated pomegranate arils during storage at 4 °C.

Numerous studies on the application of coatings on various fruits, such as Ziziphus mauritiana, mandarin, and strawberry, have consistently shown a trend similar to that observed in our study. These studies demonstrate that coatings such as starch, carboxymethyl cellulose, chitosan, gum Arabic, and carrageenan significantly delay the decrease in titratable acidity (TA) during storage49,90.

Coating pomegranates with xanthan gum creates a barrier against oxygen, which reduces their oxidative deterioration87. Additionally, clove and cinnamon are recognized for their antimicrobial and antioxidant properties44. When combined with xanthan gum, they can reduce microbial activity and oxidation in coated fruits, potentially slowing the biochemical processes responsible for the decrease in TA.

TSS

The TSS content of pomegranate fruit increased significantly with increasing storage time (Figs. 4 and 6). Coating with xanthan gum delayed this increase in TSS concentration. At the end of the storage period, pomegranate fruits coated with XAN gum enriched with cinnamon showed a higher TSS content in their juice than those treated with XAN gum or XAN gum enriched with clove powder. This effect can be attributed to the sweet aroma and taste of cinnamon91primarily due to the presence of cinnamaldehyde92. The significant increase in TSS results from the hydrolysis of starch during fruit ripening93,94along with the conversion of disaccharides to monosaccharides and cellular respiration95. Additionally, the increase in TSS is attributed to the moisture loss of fruit during storage96.

Changes in TA of pomegranate fruit juice during storage.

The application of postharvest coatings reduces the degradation of complex sugars or starch and slows down the increase in TSS concentration in fruits87. Therefore, XAN gum can probably slow the degradation of sugars, respiration and metabolic activity, thus retarding the ripening process. The effect of xanthan gum coating on TSS of fruit juice depends on fruit types. For instance, strawberry fruits coated with xanthan gum exhibited lower TSS values than control fruits at the end of the storage period49. A similar effect was reported by Kumar and Saini79 for tomatoes coated with xanthan gum. However, the highest TSS was exhibited in mango fruits coated with xanthan gum97. On the other hand, xanthan gum maintained a constant TSS value throughout storage period for papaya fruits85.

These results are in accordance with Ali et al.98who reported that carboxymethyl cellulose coating slowed down the increase in TSS during storage of mandarin fruits. Thakur et al.99also reported that a biopolymer coating slowed the increase in TSS of banana fruits. Additionally, TSS observed a similar trend in strawberry fruits coated with carrageenan gum49. In contrast, TSS content decreased in mango and Ziziphus Mauritiana juice fruits coated with chitosan coating90,100.

Biochemical properties

Total phenolic compounds

The TPC significantly decreased (P < 0.001) for both control and coated pomegranate fruits with extended storage time (Figs. 7 and 8). However, Pomegranate fruits coated with XAN gum enriched with clove or cinnamon exhibited an initial increase in TPC over 45 days of storage, followed by a decline toward the end of the storage period. Furthermore, XAN gum enriched with clove and XAN gum enriched with cinnamon were effective in slowing the degradation of TPC during storage. This effect may be attributed to the fact that clove and cinnamon are rich sources of phenolic compounds101,102 and their antioxidant properties can preserve fruit by delaying the deterioration of bioactive compounds, such as polyphenols, during storage44. The decline in TPC was significantly lower in coated fruits with xanthan gum compared to non-coated fruits. These results are in accordance with Sharma et al.38who reported that XAN gum coating slowed down the decrease in TPC during storage of fresh-cut pears. Furthermore, the XAN gum coating exhibited a remarkable ability to preserve the total phenolic content of ‘Gola’ guava fruits36 and tomatoes79. Prasad et al.97 reported that mango fruits treated with XAN gum exhibited a higher concentration of TPC compared to the control fruits.

In a similar study, pomegranate fruits showed significant degradation of TPC with extended storage time10,103. To address this issue, various edible coatings have been proposed. These coatings have demonstrated their effectiveness in reducing the degradation of TPC during storage. For instance, GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) edible coatings have been shown to preserve the TPC of pomegranate fruit during storage104. Additionally, edible coatings containing gamma-aminobutyric acid and carnauba wax have been effective in slowing the degradation of TPC over storage104. Therefore, this edible coating exhibited a similar effect on pomegranate fruit to those observed with xanthan gum coating.

The degradation of phenolic compounds may result from enzymatic activities within the fruit. Specifically, it could be due to the oxidation of phenolic content by polyphenol oxidase, as well as fluctuations in the activity of the enzyme phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL)105. The decrease in TPC in the control fruit is a result of senescence and the breakdown of cell structure104. Edible coatings can delay senescence by regulating the metabolic rate and preserving TPC for an extended storage period106.

Total anthocyanin content (TAC)

The mean TAC showed a decrease during the storage period for both control fruits and those coated with XAN (Figs. 8 and 9). Pomegranate fruits coated with XAN gum experienced a slight decrease in TAC during the first 15 days of storage, followed by stability throughout the remainder of the storage period. In contrast, the control fruits exhibited significant degradation in TAC after 15 days of storage. However, TAC increased significantly with extended storage time for both fruits coated with XAN gum enriched with clove or cinnamon. At the end of storage period, The XANCL-coated fruits exhibited a higher TAC concentration than the XANCN-coated fruits, with values of 580. 01 ± 0.44 mg.L−1 and 560. 49 ± 0.58 mg.L−1, respectively. This increase can be attributed to the high antioxidant potential of anthocyanins, which continue to be synthesized after harvest and during low temperature of storage107.

Changes in TAC of pomegranate fruit juice during storage.

Several studies in pomegranate fruits showed a significant degradation in TAC during storage period7,10. Several coatings have demonstrated their ability to reduce the degradation of TAC in pomegranate fruit during storage. In our study, XAN gum delays the decrease in TAC and keeps his stability during storage. These results are consistent with the findings of Nazoori et al.104who reported that coating pomegranate fruit with 0.5% carnauba wax plus 5 mM GABA slowed the decrease in TAC. In addition, chitosan and gum arabic coatings effectively prevent the degradation of TAC in pomegranate fruits78. The pomegranate coated with 10 mM GABA exhibited a similar trend to that observed for XANCL and XANCN-coated fruits, where the TAC increased until day 45, followed by a decrease until the end of the storage period108.

Regarding XAN gum coating, it demonstrates efficacy in minimizing the degradation of TAC in strawberry fruit during cold storage49. The efficacy of XAN gum can be further enhanced by enriching or combining it with specific compounds that improve fruit quality protection. For instance, the combination of XAN gum with carboxymethyl starch or maltodextrin has showed remarkable effect in preserving TAC in blueberry anthocyanins109,110. Similarly, Gautam et al.111 reported that XAN gum enriched with sodium nitroprusside effectively preserved the TAC concentration in strawberry during storage. These findings align with our research, where coatings enriched with clove and cinnamon preserve TAC during storage by mitigating its degradation.

Total flavonoids content (TFC)

The TFC exhibited higher degradation during storage in both the control fruit and the fruit coated with XAN gum (Figs. 8 and 10), with degradation rates of 25.43% and 40.88%, respectively. However, pomegranate fruits coated with XAN gum enriched with clove or cinnamon showed an increase in TFC until day 45, followed by a decrease until the end of the storage period, with degradation rates of 20.96% and 10.81%, respectively. Among these coatings, XANCN exhibited the highest TFC concentration (36.87 mg EqR/100 ml), at the end of the storage period. This effect is due to the protective barrier provided by XAN gum27combined with the antioxidant activity of clove and cinnamon44. These factors can reduce flavonoid degradation by preventing oxidative damage and stabilizing the flavonoids. However, the decrease in TFC observed in control and XAN coated pomegranate fruits is consistent with several studies that have reported this as a natural degradation process of flavonoids over the storage period. For instance, Meighani et al.88 reported that the pomegranate control fruits exhibited the lowest TFC after being stored for 120 days. Bajaj et al.112 reported that xanthan gum coatings significantly enhanced the TFC in Kinnow mandarins, with the combination of 1.0% xanthan gum and 1.0% lemongrass oil resulting in a maximum TFC of 0.21 mg g−1 during low temperature storage. In the same study on the effects of chitosan-coated on apples fruits, Wang et al.113 observed a decrease in flavonoid content during cold storage.

on the other hand, the initial increases in flavonoid content in XANCN and XANCL may be attributed to the synthesis of these compounds in pomegranate fruit during storage114. The subsequent decline is associated with the degradation of flavonoids88. In addition, the strawberry coated with XAN gum exhibited a similar trend, with TFC initially increasing, followed by a subsequent decline throughout the storage period49.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that effectively contributed to maintaining the postharvest quality of “Sefri Ouled Abdellah” pomegranates during cold storage. The application of XAN gum enriched with clove or cinnamon essential oil exhibited significant benefits in reducing weight loss, stabilizing color parameters (L*, a*, b*, C*, and h°), and minimizing fluctuations in pH, TSS, and TA. Moreover, the natural antioxidant properties of clove and cinnamon essential oil played a crucial role in preventing the degradation of TPC and TAC. Among the tested coatings, XANCN exhibited the least overall changes from D0 to D5. The findings of this study suggest that xanthan gum enriched with cinnamon essential oil is the most effective coating for preserving the pomegranate fruit quality throughout the storage period, with no perceptible losses.

Despite these promising results, some limitations should be considered. The present study primarily focused on physicochemical and antioxidant attributes, without assessing sensory properties or microbial stability. Further investigations should explore the impact of these coatings on the organoleptic characteristics and microbial safety of pomegranates to ensure consumer acceptability. Additionally, an in-depth understanding of the molecular interactions between the coating components and the fruit surface could provide valuable insights into their preservation mechanisms. Future research should also evaluate the effectiveness of these coatings under commercial storage conditions to assess their feasibility for large-scale applications.

Furthermore, although the present work provided detailed insights into the postharvest evolution of fruit quality, future studies should consider analyzing the internal atmosphere of the coated fruits, including oxygen (O2) and carbon dioxide (CO₂) levels, to better understand the metabolic changes occurring during storage. The monitoring of volatile compounds such as ethanol and acetaldehyde would also be beneficial in ensuring that anaerobic fermentation does not occur. These additional parameters are crucial for accurately predicting the fruit’s shelf-life and marketability.
