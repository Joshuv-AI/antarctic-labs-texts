SIRT3 deficiency drives alveolar epithelial mitochondrial dysfunction in bronchopulmonary dysplasia via PDHA1 hyperacetylation
Authors: Cong Zhao, Hua Peng, Yalan Liu, Yan Chen, Chao Tong, Qianxue Zheng, Ziang Liu, Chunyan Chen, Jiayun Zhang, Wenbo Zhu, Xu Guo, Shiyi Jiang, Lin Wang, Jie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is a prevalent chronic lung disease in extremely preterm infants, characterized by arrested alveolarization and pulmonary vascular impairment. In neonatal mice and A549 cells, hyperoxia exposure significantly downregulated SIRT3 expression and PDH activity, resulting in severe mitochondrial structural and functional damage. Quantitative acetylome analysis revealed this deficiency leads to the hyperacetylation of PDHA1 at lysine 83. This modification inhibits PDH activity, forcing a pathogenic metabolic shift toward lactate accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction. In vitro, a deacetylation-mimetic PDHA1-K83R mutant restored PDH activity and mitigated hyperoxia-induced mitochondrial injury. In vivo, pharmacological activation of SIRT3 with Honokiol prevented PDHA1 hyperacetylation, preserved mitochondrial function, and alleviated alveolar simplification. PDHA1 hyperacetylation at K83 is an important downstream event associated with SIRT3 deficiency and contributes to mitochondrial and metabolic dysfunction in hyperoxia-induced BPD. Targeting the SIRT3/PDHA1 axis provides a promising metabolic intervention strategy for BPD treatment.Subject areas: Health sciences, Biological sciences, Biochemistry, Biochemical mechanism

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) constitutes the most prevalent and debilitating chronic respiratory morbidity in extremely preterm infants. BPD is characterized by arrested alveolarization, characterized morphologically by alveolar simplification, and dysmorphic pulmonary microvasculature.1,2 While advancements in neonatal intensive care have significantly extended the limits of viability, these gains have paradoxically failed to attenuate the rising incidence of BPD.3,4 Epidemiological evidence indicates that BPD affects approximately 40%–50% of infants born at less than 28 weeks of gestation,5 imposing a substantial burden of long-term sequelae, including persistent pulmonary function deficits and neurodevelopmental impairments that extend into adulthood.6 Currently, the clinical management of BPD is largely supportive, relying on mechanical ventilation and non-specific anti-inflammatory agents like corticosteroids, which often carry severe adverse effects.7,8 Therefore, identifying highly specific therapeutic targets is of paramount clinical urgency.

The differentiation and surfactant secretion of alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells in extremely preterm infants occur during a uniquely high metabolic demand window, therefore, mitochondrial homeostasis plays a core role in alveolarization.9,10 As pivotal progenitors for alveolar regeneration, AT2 cells mandate robust bioenergetic support to sustain their differentiation and secretory functions.11 Hyperoxic stress precipitates a deleterious surge in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) both in vivo and in vitro, causing oxidative damage to and depletion of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), thereby impairing mitochondrial biogenesis.9,12 These cumulative insults drive apoptosis and senescence, ultimately arresting pulmonary development and entrapping the lung parenchyma in a simplified, undifferentiated state.13

Beyond structural and oxidative damage, emerging evidence indicates that hyperoxia fundamentally disrupts mitochondrial bioenergetics through pathological metabolic reprogramming.14 During this transition, pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit alpha 1 (PDHA1), a key component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC), functions as a critical metabolic checkpoint governing substrate flux from glycolysis into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS).15 Recent studies indicate that PDHA1 is not merely a metabolic enzyme but a central regulator of mitochondrial integrity.16 Inhibition of PDHA1 activity triggers a metabolic shift from OXPHOS to aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), leading to lactate accumulation and intracellular acidosis, which in turn exacerbates mitochondrial structural damage and dysfunction.17 Notably, the activity of PDHA1 is tightly regulated by post-translational modifications, particularly lysine acetylation.17

Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) resides as the predominant NAD+-dependent deacetylase within the mitochondrial matrix.18,19 Mechanistically, SIRT3 governs core bioenergetic processes, specifically oxidative phosphorylation and the TCA cycle, by recognizing and deacetylating specific lysine residues on PDHA1, thereby potentiating the enzymatic activity of the PDH complex.15,17 The expression of SIRT3 is known to be significantly suppressed in hyperoxia-exposed lung tissues.20,21 Although the SIRT3/PDHA1 axis has been reported in other tissue injuries,22,23 the precise role within the unique high-energy metabolic landscape of the developing immature lung remains entirely unexplored.

In this study, utilizing quantitative acetyl-proteomics in a BPD model, we specifically identified PDHA1 lysine 83 (K83) as a hyperacetylation target. We then investigated the SIRT3/PDHA1 axis in a neonatal mouse model and in hyperoxia-exposed A549 cells, a lung adenocarcinoma-derived cell line commonly used as an AT2-like in vitro model. Our aim was to determine whether altered PDHA1 acetylation contributes to mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction in the setting of SIRT3 deficiency, while recognizing that SIRT3 likely regulates multiple mitochondrial pathways relevant to BPD. The objective was to establish the feasibility of targeting mitochondrial metabolism as a potential therapeutic strategy for BPD.

Result

Hyperoxia disrupts alveolarization and mitochondrial integrity in neonatal lungs with BPD

A neonatal murine model of BPD was established to assess the impact of hyperoxia on lung development (Figure S1A). Exposure to 85% O2 resulted in significant growth retardation, as evidenced by sustained body weight loss starting from postnatal day 7 (P7) compared to normoxic controls (Figure S1B). Histological assessment via H&E staining revealed arrested alveolarization in the hyperoxia group, characterized by simplified alveolar structures, thickened septal walls, and a significantly increased MLI (p < 0.001) (Figures S1C and S1D).

Mitochondrial network morphology was examined by immunofluorescence staining targeting TOM20, a mitochondrial outer membrane marker (Figure 1A). In control A549 cells, mitochondria displayed a tubular, interconnected network. In contrast, hyperoxia exposure induced severe mitochondrial fragmentation, indicated by a reduction in the mitochondrial aspect ratio (AR) and shortened mean branch length (Figure 1B). These data demonstrate that hyperoxic injury compromises both alveolar tissue structure and mitochondrial organelle integrity.

Acetylome profiling identifies PDHA1 hyperacetylation as a candidate metabolic event

An integrated quantitative proteomic and acetylomic analysis was carried out to characterize the molecular landscape underlying this mitochondrial damage. (Figure S1E). While global proteomic profiling highlighted enrichment in metabolic pathways (Figures S1F–S1I), the abundance of key metabolic enzymes remained relatively stable. In contrast, acetylome profiling revealed a profound remodeling of the post-translational modification landscape. A substantial number of proteins showed differential acetylation (Figures 1D and 1E, and Table S1), with a predominant localization to the mitochondria (Figure 1F).

GO and pathway analyses of the differentially acetylated mitochondrial proteins revealed enrichment in pathways related to ADP binding, AMP binding, and nucleosome (Figure 1G). As a hyperacetylated protein, PDHA1 was disproportionately clustered in the TCA cycle, pyruvate metabolism, and acetyl-CoA biosynthetic processes (Figure 1H). Crucially, mass spectrometric fragmentation (MS/MS) specifically pinpointed lysine 83 (K83) of the E1α subunit of PDHA1 as a pivotal hyperacetylation site induced by hyperoxia (Figures 1I and 1J). These omics data suggest that functional inhibition of mitochondrial metabolism via hyperacetylation, rather than protein loss, drives the pathology.

Hyperoxia induces SIRT3 downregulation and PDH inactivation via acetylation

To elucidate the molecular drivers underlying this mitochondrial dysfunction, SIRT3 expression was examined. A significant downregulation of SIRT3 at both mRNA and protein levels was detected in the lung tissues of BPD mice (Figures 2F and 2G). As SIRT3 is a primary mitochondrial deacetylase, its deficiency was postulated to impact the post-translational modification of metabolic enzymes.

Indeed, Co-IP analysis using a pan-acetyl-lysine antibody (Pan-Kac) revealed that PDHA1 acetylation levels were markedly elevated in the BPD group (Figure 2A). Crucially, while the total protein expression of PDHA1 remained unchanged (Figures 2D and 2E) and its histological distribution appeared stable (Figure 2C), the enzymatic activity of PDH was significantly suppressed (p < 0.01) (Figure 2B). This inhibition of PDH activity coincided with a metabolic shift toward glycolysis, evidenced by the accumulation of lactate in lung tissues (Figure 2H). These findings suggest that hyperoxia inhibits PDH activity not through protein degradation, but via SIRT3-mediated hyperacetylation, leading to metabolic reprogramming.

SIRT3-mediated PDHA1 deacetylation at K83 is essential for survival under hyperoxia

To verify the causal involvement of SIRT3 in the observed phenotypes, we utilized A549 cells as a vitro model. Time-course analysis revealed that hyperoxia suppressed SIRT3 and PDHA1 expression in a time-dependent manner (Figures 3A–3C). To clarify whether SIRT3 could regulate PDHA1 acetylation in hyperoxia induced lung injury, we next modulated SIRT3 levels (either overexpression or knockdown), with the modulation efficiency validated by RT-PCR for mRNA expression and immunoblotting for protein levels (Figure S2).

Functional assays revealed that SIRT3 knockdown (si-SIRT3) exacerbated hyperoxia-induced cell death and apoptosis (Figures 3D, 3E, and 3G). Conversely, SIRT3 overexpression (oe-SIRT3) or treatment with dichloroacetate (DCA, a PDH activator) significantly preserved cell viability. Notably, SIRT3 loss further aggravated the metabolic dysregulation caused by hyperoxia, leading to marked accumulation of acetylated PDHA1 (Figure 3F), diminished PDH activity (Figure 3H), and surged lactate production (Figure 3I).

To pinpoint the specific regulation site, we constructed flag-tagged PDHA1 wild-type (PDHA1 WT) and mutant plasmids (PDHA1 K83R). The PDHA1 K83R mutant was designed to mutant the K83th lysine into arginine to mimic the deacetylation of PDHA1 K83. Immunoprecipitation confirmed that the K83R mutation abolished hyperoxia-induced PDHA1 hyperacetylation (Figure 3J). Interestingly, while endogenous PDHA1 hyperacetylation was pronounced under hyperoxia, the immunoprecipitation of exogenous recombinant WT PDHA1 (Figure 3J) showed a less dramatic upregulation of acetylation. This discrepancy may be attributed to saturation of the acetylation machinery when the target protein is overexpressed, or differences in cellular localization and complex assembly between endogenous and exogenous PDHA1. Importantly, the interaction between SIRT3 and PDHA1 was further confirmed by co-IP in A549 cells (Figure 3K). Notably, cells expressing PDHA1 K83R maintained high PDH activity (Figure 3L) and low lactate levels (Figure 3M) even under hyperoxic challenge. These data definitively identify lysine 83 of PDHA1 as the critical substrate for SIRT3-mediated metabolic protection. However, these gain-of-function data were obtained in an in vitro A549 cell. Whether PDHA1-K83 acetylation exerts a comparable causal role in vivo remains to be determined using appropriate genetic models.

SIRT3 preserves mitochondrial bioenergetics and ultrastructural integrity

To further elucidate the impact of PDHA1 hyperacetylation on oxidative stress, SIRT3 expression and PDH activity were modulated, and ROS levels were subsequently assessed. The results demonstrated that hyperoxia triggered a surge in mitochondrial ROS (MitoSOX staining), which was aggravated by SIRT3 knockdown but effectively scavenged by SIRT3 overexpression or DCA treatment (Figures 4A and 4B). Consistently, SIRT3 loss compromised the antioxidant defense system, reducing SOD activity (Figure 4C) and increasing lipid peroxidation (MDA levels) (Figure 4D).

The results demonstrated that hyperoxia collapsed the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), indicated by a shift from red JC-1 aggregates to green monomers (Figure 4E), leading to severe ATP depletion (Figures 5B and 5C). Additionally, alterations in mitochondrial morphology were examined using TEM. At the ultrastructural level, TEM revealed that hyperoxia caused characteristic mitochondrial swelling and cristolysis (Figure 5A, yellow arrows). This structural damage was phenocopied by si-SIRT3 but remarkably rescued by oe-SIRT3 or DCA, with mitochondria exhibiting preserved cristae architecture (Figure 5A). These results indicate that the SIRT3-PDHA1 axis is indispensable for maintaining mitochondrial structural integrity and oxidative phosphorylation efficiency.

Pharmacological activation of SIRT3 alleviates BPD symptoms in vivo

To clarify whether SIRT3 could regulate PDHA1 actylation in BPD, the mice were treated with HKL (a reported SIRT3-activating compound) or 3-TYP (a commonly used SIRT3 inhibitor) from P7 to P14. To evaluate the overall effects and safety of HKL and 3-TYP in the neonatal BPD mouse model, body weight, survival, and histological changes in major organs were assessed. The body weight of neonatal mice increased gradually from P7 to P14 in all groups. Compared with the BPD+vehicle group, treatment with HKL significantly improved body weight gain during the observation period, whereas the 3-TYP group showed a less favorable growth trend (Figure S3A). Mice in the BPD+HKL group exhibited a higher survival probability than those in the BPD+vehicle group during the experimental period, whereas 3-TYP treatment did not improve survival and tended to worsen the outcome (Figure S3B). These results indicate that HKL confers a survival benefit in BPD model. To determine whether HKL or 3-TYP induced systemic toxicity, histopathological examinations of major organs were performed using H&E staining. Representative images of the brain, heart, liver, spleen, kidney, and intestine are shown in Figure S3C. No obvious pathological injury, structural disruption, or overt toxic changes were observed in these organs among the control, BPD, BPD+3-TYP, and BPD+HKL groups.

Consistent with cellular findings, pharmacological activation of SIRT3 significantly mitigated hyperoxia-induced lung injury. HKL treatment improved alveolarization, as evidenced by reduced septal wall thickness and MLI compared to the vehicle group (Figures 5D, 5E, and 5G). Notably, administration of HKL significantly blunted PDHA1 hyperacetylation. Conversely, 3-TYP exacerbated the accumulation of acetylated PDHA1, confirming that PDHA1 serves as a bona fide deacetylation target of SIRT3 during hyperoxic injury (Figure 5F). Metabolically, HKL effectively restored PDH activation (Figure 5H). In contrast, inhibition of SIRT3 with 3-TYP exacerbated the BPD phenotype, leading to more severe alveolar simplification and metabolic dysfunction. In summary, our findings support a model whereby impaired SIRT3-mediated PDHA1 deacetylation promotes mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, ultimately contributing to BPD pathogenesis (Figure 6).

Schematic illustration of the SIRT3-PDHA1 axis governing alveolar epithelial metabolic homeostasis

Under hyperoxic stress, healthy alveolar epithelial cells undergo aberrant metabolic reprogramming. The hyperoxia-induced suppression of SIRT3 impairs the deacetylation of PDHA1, thereby inactivating the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. Consequently, the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA is blocked, and substrate flux is diverted away from the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle toward aerobic glycolysis. This pathological shift leads to excessive lactic acid accumulation, which, coupled with bioenergetic failure, exacerbates mitochondrial damage, characterized by a dramatic surge in ROS and severe ATP depletion. Ultimately, this metabolic and mitochondrial collapse drives alveolar simplification, the hallmark pathology of bronchopulmonary dysplasia.

Discussion

This study is the first to reveal the role of metabolic reprogramming driven by the SIRT3-PDHA1 axis in the BPD model. Hyperoxia exposure significantly suppresses SIRT3 expression in A549 cells. This deficiency leads to the hyperacetylation and subsequent inactivation of PDHA1 and trigger a pathogenic metabolic shift characterized by lactate accumulation and insufficient ATP production, ultimately compromising alveolar epithelial cells survival and differentiation. Pharmacological activation of SIRT3 using HKL was shown to effectively restore PDHA1 activity, re-establish metabolic homeostasis, and attenuate alveolar simplification in neonatal mice. Collectively, these findings support a mechanism wherein hyperoxia-induced SIRT3 downregulation precipitates the accumulation of acetylated PDHA1. This modification suppresses PDH activity and orchestrates a pathological metabolic reprogramming that, coupled with mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately culminates in arrested alveolarization.

Lactate, the terminal metabolite of glycolysis, serves as a pleiotropic signaling molecule that regulates multiple physiological and pathological processes including metabolic reprogramming, immunomodulation, and angiogenic regulation.24,25 Recent advances in metabolomics profiling of extremely preterm infants (gestational age < 29 weeks) demonstrate that elevated serum and urinary lactate may serve as early biomarkers of BPD.26,27,28 In the absence of PDH activity, pyruvate cannot efficiently enter the TCA cycle and is instead reduced to lactate. This metabolic shift, known as the Warburg effect, leads to lactate accumulation and reflects a fundamental reprogramming of cellular energy metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis.29,30 This study revealed that hyperacetylation-induced PDHA1 inactivation may represent a key mechanism driving enhanced glycolysis and suppressed TCA cycle activity in bronchopulmonary dysplasia.

Our findings demonstrate that the mitochondrial fragmentation observed in BPD is a physical consequence of bioenergetic failure rather than a primary morphological defect.29 The electron transport chain (ETC) requires a continuous supply of reducing equivalents (NADH) from the TCA cycle to sustain the proton motive force.14,31 The SIRT3-mediated inhibition of PDH enzymatic activity restricts substrate flux, leading to the dissipation of MMP (ΔΨm) and a subsequent leak of electrons to form superoxide. Under these conditions of ATP depletion and potential loss, the maintenance of cristae architecture and tubular networks becomes thermodynamically unsustainable, resulting in the observed fragmentation and cristolysis.32,33 Notably, the restoration of cristae integrity by metabolic modulators (DCA or SIRT3 overexpression) confirms that organelle ultrastructure is strictly coupled to oxidative phosphorylation efficiency. Thus, metabolic reprogramming, specifically the block in pyruvate oxidation, is the upstream driver that compromises mitochondrial dynamics and cellular recovery.

The arrest of alveolarization is potentially reversible through targeted metabolic reprogramming. The efficacy of HKL in deacetylating PDHA1 and restoring PDH kinetics provides proof-of-concept that the mitochondrial acetylome is a viable therapeutic target.22,34 Indeed, recent pharmacological reviews have highlighted HKL as an activator of SIRT3 that can ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction in various fibrosis and inflammatory models.23,35 Conversely, the exacerbation of alveolar simplification by the SIRT3 inhibitor 3-TYP confirms that endogenous deacetylase activity is rate-limiting for tissue survival under hyperoxic stress, consistent with findings in renal and cardiac injury models.22,36 Furthermore, the observation that DCA directly restores PDH flux and mitigates injury indicates that the inhibition of pyruvate oxidation is the upstream driver of both bioenergetic failure and secondary ROS generation.37 Consequently, therapeutic strategies for BPD should prioritize the restoration of mitochondrial bioenergetic capacity via specific enzymatic modulation,1 rather than relying solely on non-specific antioxidant scavenging. Regarding the timing of pharmacological intervention, Honokiol was administered from P7 to P14 in the present study, representing a therapeutic rescue paradigm. Although earlier prophylactic treatment might theoretically provide greater protection against hyperoxia-induced lung injury, our preliminary attempts to initiate repeated intraperitoneal injections from P1 were associated with substantial technical difficulty and increased mortality in neonatal pups, likely due to their extreme fragility and poor tolerance at this age. Therefore, the P7-P14 regimen was selected to improve feasibility and reduce procedure-related confounding effects. Future studies are warranted to determine whether earlier intervention using less invasive neonatal delivery methods or optimized formulations may offer additional preventive benefit.

However, our data do not imply that PDHA1 is the sole or dominant mediator of all SIRT3-dependent effects in BPD. Rather, PDHA1 K83 acetylation should be considered one mechanistically relevant component within a wider SIRT3-regulated mitochondrial network. SIRT3 regulates the acetylation status of numerous mitochondrial proteins involved in antioxidant defense, fatty acid oxidation, the TCA cycle, electron transport, and cell death signaling. Previous studies have shown that SIRT3 deficiency leads to hyperacetylation of hundreds of mitochondrial proteins and perturbs multiple metabolic and stress-response pathways rather than a single enzyme-centered mechanism.38 Accordingly, although our data support a pathogenic contribution of PDHA1 K83 acetylation, they do not exclude parallel roles of other established SIRT3 targets, including SOD2-dependent redox regulation,39 as well as broader metabolic-oxidative stress crosstalk.40 In addition, the current work primarily focused on pyruvate oxidation and glycolytic rerouting, whereas fatty acid oxidation is also a major determinant of mitochondrial function during postnatal lung development. Because SIRT3 is a well-recognized regulator of fatty acid oxidation enzymes, impaired FAO may represent another important mechanism contributing to bioenergetic insufficiency in hyperoxia-exposed lungs. Future studies should therefore integrate analyses of glucose oxidation, fatty acid oxidation, and redox homeostasis to define how SIRT3 coordinates metabolic flexibility in the developing lung.

Limitations of the study

Despite the promising findings, several limitations of the present study warrant careful consideration. First, the neonatal hyperoxia model used here reproduces an important oxygen toxicity component of BPD and is widely used to study arrested alveolarization. Nevertheless, clinical BPD arises in extremely premature infants exposed to a more complex sequence of prenatal and postnatal insults.41 Hypoxia itself has been reported to suppress SIRT3 expression in other systems,42 raising the possibility that the SIRT3-dependent alterations observed here may also be relevant to preterm hypoxia-hyperoxia transitions. Therefore, it remains to be elucidated whether baseline SIRT3 levels are already altered in the lungs of prematurely born infants compared to term controls. However, this possibility was not directly tested in our study and warrants future evaluation in clinically more representative models. We also did not assess SIRT3 expression in lung tissues from premature human infants or in animal models incorporating prematurity-related hypoxic exposure. These data would strengthen the physiological and translational relevance of the present findings and should be addressed in future studies. Second, our in vivo mechanistic validation predominantly relied on a global systemic exposure model. While A549 cells were utilized for in vitro molecular dissection of the SIRT3/PDHA1K83 axis, the intricate cellular crosstalk within the pulmonary microenvironment remains to be fully elucidated. Future studies employing alveolar epithelial-specific PDHA1-K83 conditional knockout mice are imperatively required to definitively establish the cell-autonomous role of this metabolic reprogramming mechanism in vivo. Lastly, our study focused on the acute phase of neonatal lung injury; thus, longitudinal studies tracking the long-term pulmonary outcomes of these metabolic interventions are essential to comprehensively evaluate their clinical translational potential. In this study, neonatal C57BL/6 mice (postnatal days 1–14) of both sexes were used randomly. Due to the early developmental stage of the pups and the experimental design, the specific sex of each individual was not determined or tracked during the procedures. Consequently, the potential influence of sex on the experimental outcomes could not be evaluated, which represents a limitation of the current study. Future studies are required to investigate whether the observed mechanisms exhibit any sex-dependent differences.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals and study design

C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Shulaibao Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). All animal procedures strictly adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH publication) and received formal approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (IACUC Number: 5160). Mice were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with controlled temperature (22 ± 1°C), humidity (50 ± 10%), and a 12-h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to standard chow and sterile water.

Neonates were pooled and randomized at P0 to circumvent litter bias and subjected to either normoxia or hyperoxia (85% O2) for 14 days. Nursing dams were alternated daily between environments to avoid oxygen toxicity. Therapeutic interventions were performed from P7 to P14 via daily i.p. injection of Honokiol (HKL, 20 mg/kg, S2310, Selleck, Houston, TX, USA) or 3-TYP (5 mg/kg, S8628, Selleck, Houston, TX, USA), with vehicle serving as the control. HKL and 3-TYP were freshly prepared in vehicle (5% DMSO +40% PEG300 + 5% Tween-80 + 50% double distilled water). Vehicle-treated animals received the same volume of vehicle alone. Body weight was monitored throughout the experiment.

Mice were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane (R510-22; RWD, Shenzhen, China) and subsequently euthanized. At postnatal days P14, lungs were aseptically harvested following thoracotomy. The right lung was pressure-fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for histological analysis, while the left lung was immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for molecular analyses.

Cell transfection and treatment

A549 cells were purchased from Wuhan Warner Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in F12K supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C with 5% CO2. The cell line was authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling. The cell line was routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using Mycolor One-Step Mycoplasma Detector Kit (D201-01; Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and was confirmed to be negative. The overexpression plasmids for SIRT3 (constructed in pcDNA3.1 vector) and specific siRNA targeting SIRT3 were synthesized by General Biosystems (Anhui, China). The siRNA-targeted sequences were as follows: Negative control forward: 5′- GCTGAGAATGGG AAGCTGGT-3′, reverse: 5′- CTCGTGGTTCACACCCATCA-3′; SIRT3 siRNA forward: 5′- GCUUGAUGGACCAGACAAA-3′, reverse: 5′- UUUGUCUGGUC CAUCAAGC-3′. A549 cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) upon reaching 70% confluence. Specifically, cells were transfected with either 2.5 μg plasmids or 50 nM siRNA. Plasmids (Flag-PDHA1WT, Flag-PDHA1K83R) were synthesized by Genechem (China). For pharmacological intervention, cells were pre-treated with 5 mM Sodium Dichloroacetate (DCA; S8615, Selleck, Houston, TX, USA) under normoxia for 2 h. Cells were then exposed to hyperoxia (85% O2) or normoxia (21% O2) for 48h prior to collection for subsequent analysis.

Method details

Apoptosis detection by flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-APC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (E-CK-A217; Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Briefly, A549 cells were harvested with EDTA-free trypsin, stained with Annexin V-APC and PI for 20 min in the dark, and analyzed via DxFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The apoptotic rate was quantified using FlowJo software (version 10.8; FlowJo, LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

Mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) assay

The Δψm was assessed using a JC-1 Assay Kit (C2003S; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, treated A549 cells were incubated with JC-1 working solution for 20 min at 37°C, followed by rinsing with JC-1 buffer. Images were captured using an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The decrease in Δψm was determined by the transition of fluorescence from red (JC-1 aggregates) to green (JC-1 monomers).

Mitochondrial ROS measurement

Mitochondrial superoxide levels were detected using MitoSOX Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (S0061S; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, treated A549 cells were incubated with 2 μM MitoSOX reagent for 30 min at 37°C. To visualize mitochondria and nuclei, cells were counterstained with Mito-Tracker Green (C1048; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and Hoechst 33342 (C1022; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Images were captured using a Nikon AX confocal microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and processed with NIS-Elements AR software. For quantitative assessment, the fluorescence intensity was measured using a SpectraMax iD3 multi-mode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) at excitation/emission wavelengths of 396/580 nm.

Intracellular ATP measurement

The intracellular ATP levels were determined using an ATP Assay Kit (S0027; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) based on the firefly luciferase method. Briefly, treated A549 cells were lysed and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was mixed with the ATP detection working solution in a white 96-well plate. The luminescence intensity was measured using a SpectraMax iD3 multi-mode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). ATP concentrations were normalized to the total protein content, as determined by a BCA Protein Assay Kit (KTD3001; Abbkine, Wuhan, China).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

A549 cells were harvested by centrifugation and fixed in electron microscopy fixative (2.5% glutaraldehyde) at 4°C. After three washes in 0.1M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4), the cell pellets were pre-embedded in 1% agarose and trimmed into approximately 1 mm3 blocks. Samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1M PB for 2h at room temperature in the dark, washed three times in PB, and dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 95%, 100%, 100%; 10min each), followed by acetone twice for 10 min each. Samples were infiltrated sequentially with acetone: Epon 812 resin (1:1, 37°C for 2 h), acetone: Epon 812 (1:2, 37°C overnight), and pure Epon 812 (37°C for 5h), embedded in fresh resin, and polymerized at 60°C for 48h. Ultrathin sections (60-80 nm) were cut using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), collected on 200-mesh copper grids, stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 10min and lead citrate for 5min. Finally, mitochondrial ultrastructure was visualized using a JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assessment

Lipid peroxidation was evaluated via the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method using a commercial kit (A003-4; Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). Briefly, cell lysates were incubated with TBA at 95°C to yield MDA-TBA adducts. Absorbance was recorded at 532 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 multi-mode reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Results were normalized to total protein concentrations determined by BCA assay and expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity assay

Total SOD activity was determined using a xanthine oxidase-based colorimetric kit (A001-3-1; Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). Cell lysates were prepared, and the inhibition of nitrite formation was monitored. The absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 multi-mode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to exhibit 50% dismutation of the superoxide radical. All data were normalized to protein concentrations and expressed as U/mg protein.

Quantitative histomorphometry analysis

Right lower lobe lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h at 4°C, then sequentially dehydrated through an ethanol gradient (70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and paraffin-embedded. Tissue sections (5 μm) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Morphological assessment was performed using a Nikon 80i light microscope (Nikon Instruments, Tokyo, Japan). For quantitative analysis, three random non-overlapping fields per section were imaged at 200× magnification. Alveolar development was assessed by measuring mean linear intercept (MLI) and alveolar septal thickness using ImageJ software43 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunofluorescence

A549 cells cultured on glass coverslips were rinsed with warm PBS and fixed immediately with pre-warmed (37°C) 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min to maintain mitochondrial integrity. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 1h at room temperature. For immunolabeling, samples were incubated with anti-Tom20 antibody (1:100; 42406; CST) overnight at 4°C, followed by detection with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500; A21207; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (C0060; Solarbio) prior to mounting with Fluoromount- G (0100-01; Southern Biotech). Images were captured using a Nikon AX confocal microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with identical settings across groups. Mitochondrial morphology in TOM20-stained images was quantified using Fiji software44 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Images were preprocessed via background subtraction and Unsharp Mask, followed by Otsu binarization. To assess fragmentation, the aspect ratio (AR) was calculated using “Analyze Particles” plugin. Network integrity was evaluated by skeletonizing the binarized images and measuring average branch length using the “Analyze Skeleton” plugin.

Western blotting analysis

Lung tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein samples were denatured by boiling at 95°C for 10 min in Laemmli buffer. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE (10% gel) and electrophoretically transferred to 0.45 μm pore size nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-SIRT3 (1:1000; 5490S, CST), rabbit anti-PDHA1 (1:5000; 18068-1-AP, Proteintech), and mouse anti-GAPDH (1:50,000; 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech). After washing with TBST, immunoreactive bands were detected using an Odyssey CLx infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software.

RNA isolation and real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from lung tissues using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and concentration were verified by spectrophotometry (A260/A280 ratio>1.8). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg total RNA using the All-in-One cDNA Synthesis Kit (GeneCopoeia, Rockville, MD, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-PCR) was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system in 10 μL reactions containing: 1 μL cDNA template, 5 μL 2× All-in-One qPCR Mix (GeneCopoeia, Rockville, MD, USA), 0.2 μL each of forward and reverse primers (mouse SIRT3; forward: 5′-GCTACATGCACGGTCTGTCGAA-3′, reverse: 5′-CAATGTCGGGTTTCACAACGCC-3′; mouse GAPDH forward: 5′-AATGTGT CCGTCGTGGATCTGA-3′, reverse: 5′-GATGCCTGCTTCACCACCT TCT-3′), and 3.6 μL nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of: initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min; 39 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s; final extension at 65°C for 5 s; followed by melt curve analysis (95°C for 5 s, 65°C–95°C in 0.5°C increments). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the endogenous control.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry, paraffin sections were deparaffinized twice in 100% xylene for 20 min and rehydrated by incubation in decreasing concentrations of ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, and 60% for 5 min each). Antigen retrieval was then performed using Tris-EDTA (95°C for 20 min), followed by incubation in 0.3% (vol/vol) H2O2 in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) for 30 min at room temperature. The sections were permeabilized in 0.5% (vol/vol) Triton X-100 (in TBS) for 30 min and then blocked in 3% bovine serum albumin (in TBS) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. The following primary antibodies were used for immunohistochemistry: rabbit anti-PDHA1 (1:200; 18068-1-AP, Proteintech). Sections were then washed three times in TBS for 5 min before incubation with goat anti-rabbit/mouse horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled polymer (KIHC-5, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at room temperature. The slices were washed three times in TBS for 5 min and then stained in 3,3 - diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining solution (KIHC-5, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 10 min and then quickly washed three times in PBS for 5 min, followed by counterstaining with a hematoxylin kit (PMK-341, Bioprimacy, Wuhan, China). The stained slices were dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol 60%, 85%, 95%, and 100% for 5 min each, followed by another wash in 100% ethanol for 5 min. The slides were then immersed in 100% xylene for 20 min twice. Finally, the slides were mounted with neutral balsam. Immunohistochemistry images were obtained using a Nikon TE2000U microscope equipped with a QiCam Fast Cooled highresolution charge-coupled device camera (Nikon, Melville, NY) with MetaMorph software (version 6.2r4; Universal Imaging, West Chester, PA).

PDH activity assay

Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) enzymatic activity was quantified using a commercial PDH Activity Assay Kit (BC0385, Solarbio, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The assay measures PDH-catalyzed pyruvate oxidation through the stoichiometric reduction of 2,6-dichlorophenol-indophenol (2,6-DCPIP), monitored spectrophotometrically at 605 nm. One unit of PDH activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to reduce 1 nmol of 2,6-DCPIP per minute per milligram of protein under standard assay conditions.

Lactate quantification

Lactate concentrations were quantified using a commercial Lactate Assay Kit (A019-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, tissue homogenates were deproteinized and reacted with the kit reagents, with absorbance measured at 530 nm.

Immunoprecipitation

For co-immunoprecipitation, lung tissues or A549 cells were lysed in ice-cold IP lysis buffer (KTD105-CN, Abbkine). After pre-clearing with Protein A + G Magnetic Beads (KTD105-CN, Abbkine), equal amounts of protein lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody against PDHA1(18068-1-AP, Proteintech) or pan-acetyl-lysine (AF5632, Beyotime). Normal rabbit IgG (KTD105-CN, Abbkine) was used as a negative control. Immune complexes were captured with Protein A/G magnetic beads for 12 h at 4°C, washed three times in lysis buffer, eluted in SDS loading buffer, and subjected to immunoblotting.

The 4D label-free acetylome and LC-MS/MS analysis

Acetylome profiling was performed using snap-frozen left lung tissues from control and hyperoxia-exposed neonatal mice (n = 3/group). Tissues were ground in liquid nitrogen and lysed in SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (Beyotime). Proteins (5 mg/sample) were digested via filter-aided sample preparation (FASP). Briefly, samples were reduced (100 mM DTT, 100°C, 5 min), transferred to 30-kDa centrifugal filters (Sartorius), alkylated (100 mM IAA in UA buffer, 30 min, dark), and digested overnight with sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega) at 37°C. Peptides were subsequently C18-desalted and quantified. For acetylome analysis, desalted peptides were subjected to immunoaffinity enrichment specifically targeting lysine-acetylated peptides using the Acetyl-Lysine Motif (Ac-K) Kit (13416S, CST) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Enriched peptides were analyzed using a nanoElute UHPLC coupled to a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker) equipped with a CaptiveSpray source. Separation was performed on a 25 cm × 75 μm C18 column (1.6 μm, IonOpticks) with a linear gradient of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (300 nL/min). The mass spectrometer operated in parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode. MS/MS data were searched against the UniProt Mus musculus database using MaxQuant (v1.6.17.0). Carbamidomethylation (C) was set as a fixed modification; oxidation (M), protein N-terminal acetylation, and lysine acetylation were variable modifications. The false discovery rate (FDR) was <1% for both peptide and protein identifications. Differentially acetylated sites were defined by |fold change| > 1.5 and p < 0.05 (two-tailed Student’s t test).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.3.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). For normally distributed data with equal variance, comparisons between two independent groups were performed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test. Comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For the quantitative proteomic and acetylomic data, statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t test, with a fold change (FC) threshold of >1.5 or <0.67 and P-value <0.05 considered as significantly differentially expressed or modified. p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001).
