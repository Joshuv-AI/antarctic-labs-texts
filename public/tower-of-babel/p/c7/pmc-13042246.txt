Infections in Implant-Based Breast Reconstruction: A Systematic Review of Risk Factors, Prevention, and Salvage Strategies
Authors: Elisa Maria Ragaini, Camilla Ferrari, Roberta Comunian, Mattia Federico Cavallero, Stefano Vaccari, Davide Fiore Bavaro, Francesco Klinger, Valeriano Vinci, Riccardo Di Giuli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImplant-based breast reconstruction (IBR) is the most commonly adopted technique following mastectomy because of its relative simplicity and shorter recovery time. However, infection remains one of its most frequent and challenging complications, potentially leading to delayed adjuvant therapy, increased healthcare costs, and implant loss. This systematic review provides an updated and structured appraisal of current evidence on infections in IBR, focusing on the following key outcomes: risk factors associated with postoperative infections; preventive strategies, including systemic and local prophylaxis; microbiological patterns and pathogen distribution; clinical outcomes across different reconstructive stages; and management and salvage approaches, including criteria for implant preservation vs explantation. A systematic search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Google Scholar following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 for studies published between January 2010 and May 2024. Studies were included if they reported original human data on infections after IBR. Twelve studies were analyzed for infection rates, microbiology, prophylaxis, and management. Infection rates ranged from 4.5% to 19.4%. Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci were most common. Main risk factors included obesity, diabetes, radiotherapy, smoking, and acellular dermal matrices. Preventive measures varied widely. Salvage was attempted in up to 80% of cases, with success rates of 42.9% to 90.9%. Biofilm and culture-negative infections were major challenges. Infections in implant-based reconstruction remain multifactorial and clinically demanding, with heterogeneous prevention and management strategies and limited consensus. Standardized definitions, improved microbiology, and validated salvage pathways are needed to enhance outcomes. Level of Evidence: 4 (Therapeutic)

Breast reconstruction following mastectomy is a fundamental component of modern breast cancer care. In recent decades, increasing attention has been given not only to oncologic outcomes but also to the psychosocial well-being and quality of life of breast cancer survivors.1,2 Implant-based breast reconstruction (IBR), particularly with the use of tissue expanders and permanent implants, represents the most commonly used technique because of its technical simplicity, shorter operative times, and faster recovery compared with autologous options.1 Its widespread adoption is also supported by broader availability, lower upfront costs, and compatibility with most hospital settings.1 However, despite these advantages, implant-based approaches carry a significant risk of infectious complications, which can jeopardize aesthetic outcomes, delay adjuvant therapies, prolong hospitalization, and, in some cases, lead to implant loss.

The reported incidence of infection in IBR varies widely in the literature, depending on the definition of infection, patient selection, and perioperative protocols adopted. Pooled data from systematic reviews suggest a rate between 5% and 15%, making it one of the most common complications associated with this type of reconstruction.2-4 Such complications are particularly relevant in the oncologic setting, where even minor surgical setbacks may compromise the timely initiation of chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Several predisposing factors have been identified, including obesity, diabetes mellitus, tobacco use, previous radiotherapy, bilateral procedures, prolonged operative times, and the use of acellular dermal matrices.2,5

This review, conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines, aims to systematically analyze current evidence regarding infections in IBR.6 Specifically, it examines the microbiological patterns of infection, including pathogen distribution and biofilm-related presentations; evaluates patient- and procedure-related risk factors; reviews both systemic and local preventive strategies adopted across institutions; analyzes management approaches with particular focus on the criteria guiding implant salvage vs explantation; and summarizes the clinical outcomes reported across different stages of reconstruction. By synthesizing data from contemporary studies, we aim to provide practical insights to guide clinicians in optimizing care for this complex patient population.

METHODS

A systematic review of the literature was conducted following the guidelines outlined in the PRISMA 2020 statement.6 This review aimed to evaluate infection rates, microbiological characteristics, prevention strategies, and management outcomes in IBR following mastectomy.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive search was performed using the PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases. The search was limited to studies published between January 2010 and May 2024. The following search terms were used in various combinations: “breast reconstruction,” “implant infection,” “prosthetic breast infection,” “tissue expander infection,” “implant-based reconstruction,” “antibiotic prophylaxis,” “biofilm,” and “pocket irrigation.” The literature search was conducted in May 2024. The full electronic search strategy is provided as Appendices A and B.

Study Selection

All identified records were initially screened by 1 reviewer (C.F.) by title and abstract. After removal of duplicates, potentially eligible full-text articles were independently assessed for inclusion by 2 additional reviewers (E.M.R. and R.D.G.). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a fourth reviewer (V.V.).

Any disagreements in data extraction were resolved by discussion and consensus among the reviewers. Review articles were included for qualitative contextualization only. No patient-level data from reviews were aggregated, and quantitative results were derived exclusively from original studies to avoid double counting.

Quality Assessment

A formal validated risk-of-bias tool was not applied. Instead, an author-developed qualitative assessment was performed for descriptive purposes only and should not be interpreted as a standardized risk-of-bias evaluation. All included studies met the predefined inclusion criteria.

Where possible, internal validity was qualitatively evaluated based on clarity of inclusion criteria, sample size justification, microbiological documentation, outcome reporting, and follow-up duration. This systematic review was not prospectively registered in International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) or other registries.

Statistical Analysis

Because of heterogeneity in study design, outcomes, and reporting, no meta-analysis was planned. Data were synthesized descriptively, and results are presented as reported in the original studies.

RESULTS

A total of 165 records were identified through the initial database search. After removal of 35 duplicates, 130 records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 87 were excluded based on relevance, leaving 43 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. Following full-text evaluation, 31 articles were excluded for not meeting the predefined criteria, resulting in 12 studies included in the final qualitative synthesis. Study Characteristics

The included studies were published between 2015 and 2023 and comprised heterogeneous study designs, including retrospective cohort studies, RCTs, and narrative or structured reviews. Sample sizes in original clinical studies ranged from 56 to 239 patients. Reconstruction techniques included both tissue expander–based and direct-to-implant approaches. Detailed study characteristics are reported in Table 1.

Infection Rates and Timing

Reported infection rates across the included clinical studies ranged from ∼4.5% to 19.4%.3,7,9-11,13,16 When specified, infections occurred more frequently during the tissue expander phase compared with the permanent implant stage.9,11 Timing of infection varied across studies and was not uniformly defined. Early infections were generally reported within the first postoperative weeks, whereas delayed infections were described several months after reconstruction, particularly in association with implant exposure or reconstructive stage.3,10-13,16

Microbiological Findings

Microbiological data were reported in several clinical cohorts.9-11 Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci were the most frequently isolated pathogens.10,11 Gram-negative organisms were less commonly reported and were more frequently identified in delayed or chronic infections when microbiological data were available.10 Culture-negative infections were reported in multiple studies, accounting for a variable proportion of cases.10,11 Biofilm-related infection was specifically investigated in experimental or microbiology-focused studies and discussed in relation to early implant colonization and salvage feasibility.10,15

Management and Salvage Outcomes

Management strategies for implant-related infections varied across studies.9,11,16,17 Implant salvage was attempted in a substantial proportion of infected cases, with reported success rates ranging from ∼42.9% to 90.9%, depending on patient selection, timing of intervention, and reconstructive context.9,11,12,16,17 Salvage approaches included systemic antibiotic therapy, surgical washout and pocket irrigation, debridement, and implant exchange.9,11,12,16,17 Explantation was reported in cases of advanced infection, implant exposure, or failure of conservative management.11,12,17

Preventive Strategies

Preventive strategies varied widely across studies. Systemic perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis was universally adopted.7-9,11,13 Studies directly evaluating extended postoperative antibiotic regimens did not demonstrate consistent reductions in infection rates compared with short-duration prophylaxis.8,13 Local preventive measures included intraoperative pocket irrigation with antibiotic or antiseptic solutions, postoperative drain antisepsis protocols, and selective use of local antibiotic delivery systems such as absorbable beads in high-risk patients.3,7,11,13,14 Because of heterogeneity in study design, definitions, and outcome reporting, direct comparison of preventive strategies across studies was limited. DISCUSSION

This systematic review synthesizes contemporary evidence on infections following IBR, highlighting the clinical relevance of this complication and the heterogeneity of prevention and management strategies across institutions. Across the 12 included studies, infection rates remained clinically significant, particularly during the tissue expander phase, with Staphylococcus species identified as the predominant pathogens. Biofilm formation and culture-negative infections emerged as recurrent challenges. Preventive approaches, including systemic antibiotic prophylaxis, pocket irrigation, drain antisepsis, and local antibiotic delivery, varied substantially in formulation and application, reflecting the absence of standardized protocols. Similarly, management strategies differed widely, although several studies supported the feasibility of implant salvage when infections were identified early and treated with structured, patient-specific approaches.

Infection Risk and Pathogenesis

Reported infection rates in IBR varied widely and reached up to 15% in selected high-risk populations.2,18 This variability reflects differences in patient characteristics, reconstructive techniques, perioperative protocols, and definitions of infection across studies. The findings of this review confirm S. aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci as the most frequently isolated pathogens, consistent with previous literature. However, the increasing identification of gram-negative organisms and atypical mycobacteria underscores the importance of comprehensive microbiological evaluation and maintaining a broad diagnostic perspective. Biofilm formation plays a central role in chronic, recurrent, and culture-negative infections.19-21 By limiting antimicrobial penetration and promoting bacterial persistence, biofilms contribute to treatment failure and delayed presentation. Clinically, infections may manifest early with acute inflammatory signs or present later with indolent symptoms, further complicating timely diagnosis. These findings emphasize the need for sustained clinical vigilance throughout the reconstructive course and for management strategies that account for biofilm-related pathophysiology.

Controversies in Prevention

The optimal strategy for infection prevention in IBR remains a subject of ongoing debate. Although perioperative systemic antibiotic prophylaxis is universally adopted, its duration remains controversial. Across the included evidence, extending antibiotic prophylaxis beyond 24 h did not demonstrate consistent reductions in infection rates and may expose patients to unnecessary harms, including antimicrobial resistance and Clostridioides difficile infection.22-24 These findings are aligned with recommendations from the American Society of Plastic Surgeons and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, as well as evidence summarized by Phillips and Halvorson, which advocate limited-duration prophylaxis for clean surgical procedures.22

Pocket irrigation with antibiotic or antiseptic solutions is widely practiced, yet high-level evidence supporting a specific formulation is lacking. Although some studies suggest lower early infection rates with triple-antibiotic solutions, concerns regarding cytotoxicity, solution composition, and lack of standardization persist.3,14 Similarly, drain antisepsis and the use of antibiotic-impregnated beads demonstrated potential benefit in selected cohorts, particularly among high-risk patients, but remain supported primarily by retrospective data.7,13 Larger prospective studies are required to clarify their routine role.

Salvage vs Explantation

Whether an infected implant can be successfully salvaged represents a critical clinical decision point. The evidence synthesized in this review indicates that implant salvage is feasible in selected cases, particularly in early, localized infections without systemic involvement.9,11,12 Factors associated with successful salvage include prompt diagnosis, timely initiation of intravenous antibiotics, surgical debridement, and close postoperative monitoring. In contrast, delayed presentation, virulent pathogens, or advanced infection often necessitate explantation to prevent further morbidity.

The conservative salvage protocol proposed by Marcasciano et al demonstrated encouraging outcomes using pulse lavage and systemic antibiotics in prepectoral reconstructions, even in moderate infections.16 Although based on a limited case series, this approach supports the concept that structured, escalation-based salvage strategies may preserve reconstruction in appropriately selected patients. Algorithms proposed by Rubino et al, Tobias et al, and Khansa et al further emphasize the importance of integrating patient comorbidities, reconstructive stage, and microbial factors into decision making.9,11,12 Nevertheless, the lack of standardized definitions and prospective validation limits the generalizability of these strategies.

Institutional Protocols and Surgical Technique

Institutional protocol standardization was associated with differences in infection outcomes across centers. Several studies highlighted the role of structured perioperative measures, including standardized checklists, “no-touch” techniques, glove changes, and controlled operating room traffic, in reducing contamination risk.25,26 Consistent team adherence to these protocols, coupled with surgeon education and meticulous surgical technique, appears to represent a low-cost, high-impact strategy for infection prevention.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations that must be acknowledged. The restricted publication time window was intentionally selected to focus on contemporary reconstructive techniques and perioperative protocols; however, this choice may have excluded earlier relevant studies and represents a limitation of the review. The included studies are highly heterogeneous in terms of design, population characteristics, definitions of infection, and outcome reporting, which precluded any quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis. However, variability in infection definitions, surgical protocols, and endpoints limits the generalizability and direct comparability of results. Future systematic reviews would benefit from a more uniform methodological approach and prospective data collection to enable formal quality scoring and comparative analyses.

The majority of studies were retrospective, with inherent limitations such as selection bias and incomplete data. Moreover, only a limited number of RCTs were available, and no standardized infection grading systems were consistently used across the literature. Furthermore, most studies focused on early postoperative infections, whereas data on delayed or chronic implant infections remain sparse. There was also considerable variability in microbiological workup and definitions of “successful implant salvage,” which complicates direct comparison between studies.

Although a comprehensive literature search was conducted, some relevant studies may have been missed because of language restrictions, publication bias, or limited database indexing. Additionally, a formal risk of bias or quality assessment tool was not applied to the included studies, which may affect the robustness of the findings. The qualitative quality assessment was author developed and not based on a validated risk-of-bias tool. Finally, the scope of the review was limited to IBR, excluding autologous techniques, which may offer different risk profiles and management strategies.

Future Directions

Despite growing awareness of infection-related complications in IBR, significant gaps remain in the current body of evidence. Future research should prioritize the following areas:

Standardization of definitions and reporting: There is a pressing need for uniform criteria to define and classify implant infections. CONCLUSIONS

Infections after IBR remain frequent and clinically relevant, with reported rates varying widely across studies because of heterogeneity in definitions and perioperative protocols. Staphylococcal species are the most commonly identified pathogens, and biofilm-related, culture-negative infections represent recurrent challenges. Current evidence does not support routine prolonged postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis. Early diagnosis and structured management strategies may allow implant salvage in selected cases. Overall, these findings highlight the need for standardized definitions and more uniform reporting to improve comparability across future studies.
