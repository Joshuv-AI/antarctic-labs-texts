Examination of Gender Role Attitudes and Perceptions of Individuals in Society in the Context of Nursing Profession and Male Nurses
Authors: Canan Uçakcı Asalıoğlu, Şengül Yaman Sözbir, Sultan Özkan Şat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to examine the gender role attitudes and perceptions of individuals in society in the context of the nursing profession and male nurses.DesignThe research is a descriptive cross‐sectional study.MethodTwo hundred eighty‐three individuals were included in the study. The Gender Roles Attitude Scale and introductory information form were used to collect the data.ResultsThe total score obtained from the scale was 143.91 ± 19.55. Being of female gender increased the mean score of the scale by 12.3 times, being a university graduate by 10.5 times, approving to receive service from opposite‐sex nurses by 11.7 times, approving their partner to receive care from a nurse of the opposite‐sex by 6.29 times, having received care from male nurses before by 4.5 times, and having a male nurse close to them by 4.5 times (Adj. R 2: 0.421). Being married increased the mean score of the traditional gender role subscale by 2.7 times while approving male nurses to work in obstetrics and gynaecology decreased it by 3.1 times (Adj. R 2 = 0.280).ConclusionOur study found that the attitude towards the nursing profession and the presence of male nurses in the profession determine the gender attitude of society.Patient or Public ContributionThis research, by revealing the relationship between male nurses' representation in the profession and society's gender role understandings, leads to strategic steps to promote gender equality in the field of nursing. This study contributes to the development of a more open‐minded and accepting attitude towards male nurses among patients and the public, contributing to a more inclusive and equitable health care system.

Introduction

Gender refers to the socially constructed characteristics of women and men, such as norms and roles of femininity and masculinity and their relationships (World Health Organization (WHO) 2020). It means that while biological gender is a physiological process before the individual is born, social gender is a situation that begins to be learned and taught at birth (Kelan 2010). The role of ‘care’ is characterised by women. Caring for family members within the household, caring for children and caring for the elderly are generally perceived as roles and responsibilities characterised by women (Çiçek and Çopur 2018). Gender roles and social identity are constructed by culture (Hollup 2014). Society often categorises a career appropriate for a particular gender (Holmes and Schnurr 2006). In this context, patient care and nursing are among the professions primarily specific to women.

Nursing has been perceived as a female profession for many years. When men choose a female‐dominated profession such as nursing, they are questioned about their sexual orientation and thought to be unsuitable for bedside nursing (O'Connor 2015). However, it is known that men have also been involved in patient care in history. In the past, it was known that soldiers and clergymen provided patient care and wound care. However, this situation has been pushed to the background, and the nursing profession has been associated with ‘women’ (Brown et al. 2000; Achora 2016).

Today, the approach of considering nursing as the effect of changing social conditions is reshaping a profession based on gender discrimination. Nursing is a dynamic process based on professionalism practised by both genders in many developed countries. However, even in developed countries such as the USA, the UK and Canada, the proportion of male nurses is around 10% (USA: 9.1%, Canada: 9% and UK: 10.7%) (Minority Nurse 2024; Canadian Nurses Association 2019; Royal College of Nursing 2019). In Turkey, the Nursing Law enacted in 1954 did not allow men to become nurses. This situation was changed in 2007 with the ‘Law on the Amendment of the Nursing Law’ (Official Gazette of the Turkish Republic 2007). Thus, gender discrimination in nursing was eliminated, and men were legally entitled to become nurses. There is no study reporting the number of male nurses in Turkey. However, Turkey's number of male nurses is low, similar to other countries. Nursing is still a female‐dominated profession. The caring role of nursing is equated with feminine personality traits, and as a result, men in nursing are seen as misplaced and, therefore, face barriers (Achora 2016). Although there are men among them, the profession is still practised mainly by women and continues to be perceived as women's work.

There are many problems related to receiving care from male nurses. In particular, the refusal of female patients or their spouses to receive care from male nurses is an obstacle to providing care and receiving effective care (Cheng et al. 2018). It has also been reported that there is a negative attitude towards male nurses, and patients have some reservations about receiving services from male nurses. A study conducted in Jamaica found that 80% of male patients would not allow a male nurse to give them an enema (Adeyemi‐Adelanwa et al. 2016). In addition, it has been reported that female nurses find the nursing profession appropriate for their gender identity, while male nurses see the nursing profession as a career (Sugiura et al. 2017). Therefore, the areas where male nurses are employed are narrowing. Male nurses are forced to defend their career choices by choosing nursing departments that are more masculine and less feminine. The choice of work area is influenced by the existing social structure (Black 2014). Due to gender perception, men prefer nursing careers less, use nursing education as a stepping stone to other professions (such as police and military service) after graduation, or choose the profession to increase their chances of migrating to industrialised countries such as Australia and the United Kingdom (George and Bhatti 2019; Yılmaz and Karadağ 2011). However, it is reported that male nurses are paid more than female nurses due to gender roles and are more authoritarian than female nurses (Younas and Sundus 2018; Muench and Dietrich 2019).

Changing the established gender perception of society regarding the gender of nursing care and caregivers may pave the way for the change of traditional gender stereotypes in society. This study aimed to examine the gender role attitudes and perceptions of individuals in society in the context of the nursing profession and male nurses.

Method

Study Design

The research is a cross‐sectional descriptive study.

Setting

The study was conducted in Family Life Centers (FLC) in Ankara (the capital city of Turkey). In the selection, all centres in Ankara were divided into ‘high sociocultural’ and ‘low sociocultural’, aligning with Ankara Metropolitan Municipality data. Later on, an FLC was selected randomly from each level.

Participants

The results of Akçınar's (2018) study with Turkish women were used in the sample (R 2: 0.16, p: 0.19) calculation. The sample was calculated (Power (1−β) = 0.99, Type 1 error rate (α) = 0.01) as 247 in the power analysis. All volunteers who participated in the study were included in the sample to increase the study's validity. The sample consisted of 283 individuals who were at least literate (to fill out the questionnaire independently) and agreed to participate. The data was collected between 15 and 30 December 2024.

Data Collection

Researchers created the questionnaire form, and the Gender Roles Attitude Scale was used to collect data.

The researchers created the questionnaire. There were 13 questions in the questionnaire: Six of them were closed‐ended questions for determining the sociodemographic characteristics (such as age, gender, education, marital status, etc.) of participants. Seven of these questions consisted of closed‐ended questions such as ‘Have you ever received care from a male nurse?’, ‘Would you approve of receiving care from a female nurse or a male nurse?’, ‘Would you approve of your spouse receiving care from a nurse of the opposite‐sex?’ to evaluate the participants' views on nurses' gender.

The Gender Roles Attitude Scale was developed by Zeyneloğlu and Terzioğlu (2011) to measure individuals' views on gender roles. The scale is a 5‐Likert type scale (5: Totally agree, 4: Agree, 3: Uncertain, 2: Disagree, 1: Strongly disagree) composed of 38 items. There are five sub‐dimensions of the scale: Egalitarian gender role (sharing daily life roles and responsibilities equally), traditional gender role (not sharing daily life roles and responsibilities equally), female gender roles (roles and responsibilities imposed on women by society), marriage gender roles (roles and responsibilities in marriage life imposed on women and men by society) and male gender roles (roles and responsibilities imposed on men by society). The highest score that can be taken from the scale was 190, and the lowest score was 38. While the high scores show an equitable gender roles attitude, the lower scores show a traditional gender roles attitude (Zeyneloğlu and Terzioğlu 2011). Cronbach's alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.92. In this study, the Cronbach alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.88.

Data were collected by the researchers in the resting rooms of the family life centres. Firstly, the purpose of the research was explained by the researchers. Then, written informed consent was taken from the participants. Data collection tools were distributed and collected after participants were filled. Each individual took approximately 10–15 min.

Data Analysis

The data were evaluated using the SPSS 27.0 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences, version 27.0, for Windows) program. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test tested the convenience of data to a normal distribution. Mean, percentages, standard deviation and linear regression analysis were used to evaluate the data.

Ethical Statement

Ethical permission from the ethics committee of Gazi University (E‐77082166‐604.01‐1123386‐10.12.2024), application permission from the Metropolitan Municipality of Ankara, and written informed consent from the participants were taken before the study application.

According to the regression model, 42% of the change in the mean GRAS score (Adj. R 2 = 0.421) is explained by the variables included in the model (F = 33.545, p < 0.01). Being of female gender increased the mean score of the scale (hence egalitarian attitude) by 12.3 times, being a university graduate by 10.5 times, approving to receive service from opposite‐sex nurses by 11.7 times, approving their partner to receive care from a nurse of the opposite‐sex by 6.29 times, having received care from male nurses before by 4.5 times, and having a male nurse close to them by 4.5 times.

According to the regression model, 17% (Adj. R 2 = 0.1741) of the change in the mean score of the egalitarian gender role attitude subscale is explained by the variables included in the model (F = 15.856, p < 0.01). Being of female gender increased the egalitarian attitude by 3.1 times, being a university graduate by 3.3 times, approving to receive care from a nurse of the opposite‐sex by 1.6 times and having received care from a male nurse before by 1.8 times.

According to the regression model, 28% (Adj. R 2 = 0.280) of the change in the mean score of the traditional gender role attitude subscale was explained by the variables included in the model (F = 37.627, p < 0.01). Being married increased the traditionalist attitude by 2.7 times, while approving male nurses to work in obstetrics and gynaecology decreased it by 3.1 times.

According to the regression model, 35% (Adj. R 2 = 0.350) of the change in the mean score of the sub‐dimension of female gender role attitude (egalitarian view of gender roles attributed to women by society) is explained by the variables included in the model (F = 31.508, p < 0.01). The egalitarian attitude towards the female gender role was increased 3.8‐fold by approving the partner to receive service from the opposite‐sex nurse, 3.8‐fold by being of female gender, 2.4‐fold by being a university graduate, 2.4‐fold by approving male nurses to work in the field of obstetrics and gynaecology, and 2.1‐fold by being married.

According to the regression model, 17% (Adj. R 2 = 0.172) of the change in the mean score of the male gender role attitude (egalitarian view of gender roles attributed to men by society) sub‐dimension is explained by the variables included in the model (F = 20.545, p < 0.01). Being a woman increased the egalitarian attitude towards the male gender role by 2.5 times, being a university graduate increased it by 2.2 times and approving the partner to receive service from the opposite‐sex nurse increased it by 1.6 times.

According to the regression model, 26% (Adj. R 2 = 0.264) of the change in the mean score of the sub‐dimension of marital gender role attitude (egalitarian view in terms of roles and responsibilities imposed on men and women by society in marital life) is explained by the variables included in the model (F = 26.339, p < 0.01). Being female gender increases the egalitarian view of gender roles in marriage by 4.1 times, being a university graduate by 2.8 times, having received care from a male nurse before by 1.3 times and approving male nurses to work in obstetrics and gynaecology by 1.3 times.

Discussion

In Turkish, the word ‘nurse’ means ‘sister’, and identifying the nursing profession with women is one indicator that reflects gender role attitudes. Men started to be admitted to the nursing profession in Turkey, and the Nursing Law was amended in 2007 (Official Gazette of the Turkish Republic 2007). Although nursing is a profession, male nurses may be called ‘nurses’ in society. The word ‘nurse’ is a sexist term and stems from the effort to assign a separate role to men in nursing, which is seen as a female profession (Schmidt 2016; Rajacich et al. 2013). In this context, the presence of male nurses in the nursing profession in Turkey and their acceptance by society may be a milestone in evaluating gender perception.

Although there are different cultures, the perception of gender is similar. Studies conducted in different societies show that individuals prefer to receive care from female nurses more and do not consider the nursing profession suitable for men (LeBlanc et al. 2019; O'Connor 2015). Even studies report that there are social views that the nursing profession is a female profession and that men should take a title other than nurse (LeBlanc et al. 2019; Sasa 2019). A study conducted with male nurses found that male nurses felt different from their female colleagues and thought they were more independent and more efficient in decision‐making (Saleh et al. 2019; Achora 2016). Achora (2016) reported that male nurses' patient care was appreciated more than that of female nurses. This situation is a reflection of the fact that the value given to men in patriarchal societies is higher than that given to women, and men internalise this perception. This situation causes men to have a more traditional gender perspective. As a matter of fact, in our study, it was found that being in the female gender increased the egalitarian attitude 12.3 times. The results show that women's attitudes towards gender roles are egalitarian compared to men's.

The role of education in reducing traditional gender perception has been revealed in many studies (Kantoğlu et al. 2018; Gönenç et al. 2018; Çelebi Çakıoğlu and Harmancı Seren 2022). As the level of education increases, gender perception changes in the egalitarian direction. In our study, it was found that being a university graduate increased the egalitarian attitude 10.5 times. In a study conducted by El Kharouf and Daoud (2019) with nurses, it was found that women's attitudes towards gender roles were more egalitarian than their male counterparts. Nursing is one department that increases the number of women in university education (Higher Education Council 2025). Therefore, content on gender equality in nursing education can effectively reduce traditional gender stereotypes.

Another effect of the nursing profession in reducing the traditional gender perception in society may be male nurses in the angel. The social acceptance of men in nursing, perceived as a female profession, is decisive. The variables that support egalitarian gender perception in our study include ‘having received care from a male nurse’, ‘approving of male nurses working in the fields of gynaecology and obstetrics’ and ‘approving of their partner receiving care from a nurse of the opposite‐sex’. Similarly, Kim et al. (2017) stated that participants who thought that male nurses could contribute to the professional development of nursing had a strong awareness of gender equality.

From another point of view, the fact that male nurses are not adopted by society and female patients are not approved to receive care from male nurses may constitute an obstacle to women's exercise of their right to health, which is one of the most important consequences of gender inequality. In male‐dominated societies, women are often unable to make their own decisions. When men do not give permission, women cannot benefit from health services (family planning, induced abortion, pregnancy follow‐up, birth, etc.), and this situation negatively affects women's health (Cheng et al. 2018; Özvarış and Ertan 2003). Gynaecology and obstetrics are the health areas most restricted by men (Özdemir and Çevik 2019; Yang et al. 2017). Especially in some parts of Turkey, it is known that husbands take their wives to a ‘female’ gynaecologist and refuse to receive care if a ‘female’ gynaecologist is not available (Akın and Mıhçıokur 2003). The literature suggests that for more ‘technical’ care (e.g., taking vital signs), patients do not have a significant gender preference. For ‘intimate’ care (e.g., bathing), both male and female patients have the same gender preference, with women showing a stronger preference than men (Chur‐Hansen 2002). Based on the same view, in our study, egalitarian attitudes were increased by approving receiving care from opposite‐sex nurses, having received care from male nurses before and approving male nurses to work in gynaecology obstetrics. In a study by Chur‐Hansen (2002), 62.4% of the patients stated they were uncomfortable with the male nurse providing care to their partner. Again, a study conducted with male patients in Canada stated that women are more suitable for the nursing profession (LeBlanc et al. 2019). Our results show that having an egalitarian mindset affects the acceptance of care from male nurses.

In our study, only one variable was found to increase the traditional gender role: Being married. We could not find any study results supporting or opposing our findings. One of the gender stereotypes is that individuals get married and become parents. Therefore, it can be thought that married individuals internalise and maintain gender stereotypes more.

Conclusions

In our study, in which the gender role attitudes and perceptions of individuals in the society were examined in the context of the nursing profession and male nurses, it was found that the variables that increased the egalitarian gender role attitude were ‘being a woman’, ‘being a university graduate’, ‘having received care from a male nurse before’, ‘having a male nurse close to her’, ‘approving of her partner receiving care from a nurse of the opposite‐sex’ and ‘approving of male nurses working in the field of gynaecology and obstetrics’, while the variable ‘being married’ increased the traditional gender role attitude. Our study found that the attitude towards the nursing profession and the presence of male nurses in the profession determined the gender attitude. In this direction, it was suggested that education and promotion programmes should be organised to understand that the nursing profession is a profession that can be performed without gender discrimination; the participation of men in the nursing profession should be increased; male nurses should be employed in all areas (all clinics) within the health system; educational systems should be organised to eliminate the perception of gender‐specific profession, the media should be used for this purpose and community education should be provided; the study should be repeated in larger samples and different regions/sociocultural segments.

Limitations

There are some limitations to keep in mind when interpreting the study findings. First, the small sample size of this study may affect the generalisation of the findings. Second, the individuals in this study came from the same country, which may make the findings inapplicable to individuals from another culture.
