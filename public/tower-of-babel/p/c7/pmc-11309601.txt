Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis in Siblings Treated by Surgical Deformity Correction
Authors: Hong Jin Kim, Javier Pizones, Dong-Gune Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) in siblings reflects genetic hypothesis; however, few studies have been published. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no reports in the literature of both siblings with AIS who underwent deformity corrections. A 15-year-old adolescent girl visited our clinic with back pain after recognition of the incidental findings of a scoliotic curve in the spine. Whole spine radiographs detected Lenke classification type 3CN. The patient underwent deformity correction with posterior instrumented fusion from T4 to L3 with thoracoplasty of the right 7th to 10th rib. Four years later, her 16-year-old younger brother also visited our clinic with back pain after recognition of the incidental findings of a scoliotic curve in the spine. Whole spine radiographs detected Lenke classification type 2AN. The patient underwent deformity correction with posterior instrumented fusion from T5 to L2 with thoracoplasty of the right 8th to 10th rib. In conclusion, we report on two siblings with AIS who underwent surgical treatment for different types of curves. They showed favorable outcomes after performing deformity correction with posterior instrumented fusion. Our rare case supports the underlying basis of genetic heterogeneity as a complex polygenic model.

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a three-dimensional structural deformity of the spine, presenting a radiological lateral Cobb angle of more than 10°.1 Although the exact causes of AIS are unknown, several hypotheses have been proposed, including genetic predisposition, disturbances in growth and hormones, and developmental neuromuscular dysfunction.1,2 Especially, the heritability of AIS has been attempted to be explained through a complex polygenic model that suggested considerable genetic heterogeneity.1,3 The pedigree analyses have supported the concept of polygenic inheritance indicating that men with AIS are more likely to have siblings and children with scoliosis compared with women.4,5 The AIS in siblings reflects this genetic hypothesis; however, few studies have been published on this topic.6 Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no reports in the literature of both siblings with AIS treated by surgical deformity corrections.

Case Report

This case report followed the Consensus-based Clinical Case Reporting Guideline Development guidelines.7 Consent for publication was obtained from both patients and parents. A 15-year-old adolescent girl visited our clinic with back pain after recognition of the incidental findings of a scoliotic curve in the spine. Physical examination was notable for 5 cm of thoracic hump without any neurological deficits (Figure 1, A and B). Whole spine radiographs detected Lenke classification type 3CN with 60.3° of the main thoracic curve and 56.3° of the thoracolumbar/lumbar curve (Figure 1, C–F). The patient underwent deformity correction with posterior instrumented fusion from T4 to L3 with thoracoplasty of the right 7th to 10th rib (Figure 1, G and H).

Discussion

One of the suggested hypotheses on AIS etiology is a melatonin alteration or an abnormal bone mineralization in combination with increased bony growth.3 However, it seems clear that a genetic predisposition must play a role in AIS etiopathogenesis. Anderson et al described a 1.05% prevalence in monozygotic twins, indicating evidence of a genetic predisposition for AIS.8 Currently, the complex interplay of genetic, internal, and environmental factors has become an evident etiological theory with wide phenotypic variations.3 There have been advancements in the understanding of etiology for AIS, but there is little information on the inter-relationship of siblings in AIS. Furthermore, we are not aware of any cases where both siblings have undergone deformity correction because of severe deformation of scoliosis.

Kruse et al5 described the polygenic threshold model as the Carter effect in AIS, illustrating that siblings of affected men (55%) also had a markedly higher prevalence of AIS compared with siblings of affected women (45%). Interestingly, both siblings presented severe scoliosis needing surgical correction. Furthermore, the female patient (older sister) presented a more severe curve pattern than the male patient (younger brother), requiring longer fusion levels in deformity correction. Our case provides some insights into the etiology of AIS with general characteristics of AIS including the female-to-male ratio of 1.5-3:1 and higher Cobb angle in women than in men.1 The female patient (older sister in this case) showed a Lenke type 3CN curve, indicating double structural curves. Her brother's curve was classified as a Lenke type 2AN, indicating the double thoracic curves. We then show two different curve magnitudes and patterns, indicating genetical heterogeneity. Our rare case supports the underlying basis of genetic heterogeneity as a complex polygenic model in both siblings.3,5 Meanwhile, in the case of nonidiopathic scoliosis, there are several radiological features including a long collapsing C-shape scoliotic curve, marked pelvic obliquity, and elevation of the iliac crest on the concave side of the curvature.1 By considering the radiological features of nonidiopathic scoliosis, our study presented more like idiopathic scoliosis.

The surgical treatment of AIS was mainly performed to correct three-dimensional deformity and prevent curve progression.1,9 Currently, posterior instrumented fusion with deformity correction using rod derotation provides favorable outcomes regarding radiological and clinical parameters.9 Main goals of surgical treatment in AIS patients with severe scoliotic curves are to prevent curve progression, restore coronal and sagittal balance, and preserve as many motion segments as possible. However, because of cosmetic issues and motion preservation,9-11 new concepts are arising in the treatment of moderate curves that include minimally invasive scoliosis surgery using a posterior approach or vertebral body tethering for the surgical treatment of AIS with moderate curves. Both siblings in this case underwent standard open scoliosis surgery because of severe degrees of scoliotic deformity, which gave favorable outcomes.

In conclusion, we report on two siblings with AIS who underwent surgical treatment for different types of curves. They showed favorable outcomes after performing deformity correction with posterior instrumented fusion. Our rare case supports the underlying basis of genetic heterogeneity as a complex polygenic model.
