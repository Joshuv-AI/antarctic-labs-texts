Evaluating Silicone Gel Efficacy in Reducing Scar Formation Following Microscopic Inguinal Varicocelectomy: A Double‐Blind Prospective Controlled Trial
Authors: Iman Shamohammadi, Sina Jamzad, Shima Jamzad, Abdolreza Haghpanah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis prospective, double‐blind controlled trial was conducted to determine the effect of silicone gel in the reduction of postoperative scar after bilateral inguinal varicocelectomy. Thirty‐four patients were enrolled in this study; the right‐side inguinal scar was treated with silicone gel and petroleum jelly (Vaseline) applied on the left side as a placebo for 60 consecutive days. Scars were evaluated using the Vancouver Scar Scale, pruritus and pain. The result of this study showed that silicone gel was effective in the regression of postoperative scar after microscopic inguinal varicocelectomy in five key parameters, including vascularity, pigmentation, pliability, height and pruritus in comparison to placebo application (p < 0.05), while its effect on pain was insignificant (p > 0.05). The study showed that silicone gel had a considerable effect in the treatment and management of postoperative scar management. Varicocele is a condition that is considered the most common cause of male infertility. While microscopic inguinal varicocelectomy is considered a gold standard in the management of varicocele, its postoperative complications, such as scar formation remain a concern. Recent studies show that silicone gel can be an effective non‐invasive treatment for scar regression. Accordingly, we designed this trial to evaluate the effect of silicone gel after microscopic inguinal surgery. A prospective double‐blind controlled trial was conducted on 34 patients who had microscopic bilateral inguinal varicocelectomy (their age ranged from 19 to 41) at Namazi hospital. Two weeks after surgery, each patient received two identical tubes for application on the scars, the first tube containing silicone gel and the second one dispensed with petroleum jelly (Vaseline). The right side was treated with silicone gel, and the left side with Vaseline twice daily for 60 days. Then, patients were evaluated three times: firstly 2 weeks after surgical repair; second appointment after 2 months and third one after 6 months after surgery, using the Vancouver Scar Scale, pruritus and pain score. The collected data were analysed in SPSS 26, using the chi‐square and Mann–Whitney U tests. The significance level was defined at p < 0.05. This study showed that silicone gel was highly effective in scar parameters, including pliability, height, vascularity pruritic and pigmentation. However, its effect on pain reduction was not significant (p > 0.05). The results of the study indicated that silicone gel effectively improved scar characteristics following microscopic inguinal varicocelectomy, while its efficacy against pain was not significant.Keywords: microscopic inguinal varicocelectomy, scar, silicone gel, varicocele

Introduction

Varicocele is a medical condition, having been evaluated by physicians for centuries. It was first described as ‘the veins are swollen and twisted over the testicle, which becomes smaller’ by Celsus in the early first century AD [1]. In the modern era of medicine, this definition has been refined and understood as an abnormal venous dilatation or tortuosity of the pampiniform plexus within the scrotal area [2]. Epidemiological statistics have shown that varicocele is typically found in 12%–15% of healthy men during routine physical examinations [3, 4, 5]. Although the predominance of left‐sided varicocele has been well established, bilateral involvement is observed in approximately 10% of cases [6]. Furthermore, varicocele has garnered significant clinical attention as one of the most common causes of male infertility, with an estimated prevalence of 40% [4, 7, 8]. Although researchers have stated multiple mechanisms for infertility [9, 10, 11], the precise pathophysiology remains poorly understood. The most widely accepted grading for varicocele was introduced by Sarteschi [12, 13] in 1993 by categorising it into five grades [13]. Surgical management of varicocele depends on established indications such as testicular hypotrophy [14], infertility [15], abnormal semen parameters [16] and testicular pain [14]. Currently, among various surgical techniques described over the years, the gold standard of surgical approaches for varicocele repair is microsurgical subinguinal varicocelectomy due to its efficacy in reducing varicocele recurrence [15, 17]. Although this technique has almost the lowest rate of postoperative complications among other surgical approaches [18], recurrence, hydrocele, testicular atrophy and scar formation at the surgical site have been mentioned as disadvantages of this technique [19, 20]. Among these complications, the formation of postoperative scars remains a clinically important part of treatment since it leads to pain, pruritus, skin deformity and cosmetic concern for patients [21]. To address this specific aspect of treatment, we designed this study to evaluate the effect of silicone gel in minimising scar‐related complications, as a postoperative complication, following microscopic inguinal surgery.

Materials and Methods

This study is a prospective, double‐blind, within‐patient, controlled clinical trial which was designed to assess the efficacy of a topical silicone gel versus a placebo (petroleum jelly) in the management of postoperative scar after microscopic inguinal varicocelectomy. After obtaining informed consent, we performed this study by enrolling the participants who had bilateral microscopic inguinal surgery at Namazi Hospital in Shiraz, (Ethics committee reference number: IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1403.43 and Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials ID: IRCT20170807035551N3). The inclusion criteria were participants (aged 18–65 years) who underwent bilateral inguinal surgery using a microscopic technique after 2 weeks. All wounds were closed using an identical layered technique with absorbable sutures (Vicryl 3‐0) for the subcutaneous tissue and non‐absorbable continuous sutures (Nylon 3‐0) for the skin. The procedure was performed by the same surgical team to minimise variability.

Detailed exclusion criteria were infected wound observed in the postoperative period, pre‐existing diabetes, known hypersensitivity or previous adverse reactions to silicone‐based products or topical creams, and underlying collagen vascular disease. Among 425 patients who had inguinal varicocelectomy, 64 patients had bilateral varicocelectomy in which 30 patients refused to provide informed consent for enrolment in the study (Figure 1). Due to the bilateral nature of the surgery, case and control sites of the experiment were on a single patient, which reduced the chance of interpersonal variety and allowed for within‐patient comparison. The study started 2 weeks after surgery. Silicone gel and petroleum jelly were dispensed in identical, unmarked tubes to ensure blinding. The right‐side scar was considered the case site and treated with commercially available topical silicone gel (Cameos, Allied Biomedical, USA) twice daily, while pure petroleum jelly (Vaseline, Unilever, South Africa Ltd) was applied on the left side of surgery as a control material (twice daily) for 60 days. To reduce bias, the surgeon performing the varicocelectomy was blinded to the postoperative scar management allocation. The application of silicone gel and placebo was carried out postoperatively by an independent nurse who was not involved in the surgical procedure at the second appointment. Then patients were instructed to rub each material with fingertips for 2–3 min at the mentioned site of incisions in a thin layer on the surface of the surgical site. For better evaluation and monitoring, pre‐defined three follow‐up visits were scheduled by a third‐party physician. The first physician visit was 2 weeks after the surgical procedure. In order to evaluate the surgery site for scar formation, the second appointment was 2 months after the study. At this point, evaluation of the scar using the Vancouver Scar Scale and pruritus and pain was performed. The third appointment was 6 months after the application of materials. For standardization of evaluation, all scar assessments were conducted by a team of experienced surgeons who were blinded to the treatment allocation, based on the Vancouver Scar Scale (Table 1), pruritic and pain score (Table 2). For statistical analysis, a p value less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered significant. The collected data were analysed by SPSS‐26 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA), using the chi‐square and Mann–Whitney U tests.

Results

A total of 34 patients (with an age range from 19 to 41 and a mean age of 32 years old) who had bilateral inguinal varicocelectomy entered this study after 2 weeks of operation. The distribution of basic and clinical demographic characteristics of patients with bilateral varicocele is shown in Tables 3 and 4. Collected data of both interventions are also shown in Table 5. Scar characteristics between silicone gel and Vaseline treatments (each with n = 34) revealed statistically significant differences in pigmentation, vascularity, pliability and height in comparison to the Vaseline group (p < 0.05). Regarding pain, 30 subjects (88.2%) in the silicone gel group reported no pain (Score 0) compared to 22 subjects (64.7%) in the Vaseline group. This trial shows that the effect of silicone gel was statistically insignificant (p > 0.05) in minimising scar‐associated pain. In contrast, the results demonstrated the superiority of silicone gel in alleviating pruritus with 27 subjects (81.8%) attaining a score of 0 versus 22 subjects (54.7%) treated with Vaseline, alongside a reduced incidence of pruritus (Score 1: 18.2% vs. 32.4%, with one subject [2.9%] scoring Score 2 in the Vaseline group), so the data showed a clinically significant effect (p = 0.041). Discussion

Consideration of scar treatment can vary according to multiple purposes, especially for aesthetic or functional outcomes. Scar formation is a complex, multifactorial process that immediately begins with the formation of platelet‐fibrin clots, providing effective haemostasis. It is generally considered that the scar formation process can be divided into its phases including inflammation, proliferation and maturation. In recent years, contemporary studies have shown more than 10 distinctive methods for invasive scar treatment regarding scar type, such as microneedling [22], cryotherapy [23], intralesional corticosteroid injection [24] and surgical removal [25]. These invasive procedures may be indicated in some conditions; however, associated complications such as pain have led to a tendency towards non‐invasive medications. The non‐invasive scar formation agents have been demonstrated to be useful in the resolution of scar tissues. For instance, Q10 [21, 26], vitamin A [27, 28], vitamin E [29], allantoin [30, 31] and silicone gel [31, 32, 33, 34] use showed significant improvement for scar tissue resolution. Among these treatments, the one supported by clinical trials and systematic reviews illustrates silicone gel's effectiveness, which has made it an in‐office scar treatment for decades [35]. There are several mechanisms of action regarding the potential therapeutic effects of silicone gel on scar formation, such as inducing a slight increase in skin surface temperature, which may alter metabolic activity and collagen remodelling [36]. Additionally, it has been suggested that silicone gel may maintain skin hydration, which consequently inhibits mast cell activation and reductions in oedema, vasodilation, intracellular inflammatory signals [37]. The modification of collagenase activity [37], cytokine‐mediated signalling and fibroblast modulation [38] that activates fibroblast proliferation and inhibits their excessive synthesis of collagen has also been reported. However, despite these hypotheses, the exact biological mechanism of silicone gel remains incompletely understood. While the application of silicone gel on skin is considered a safe treatment, some adverse effects have been documented. These include persistent pruritus [39], folliculitis arising from alteration in the skin environment, contact dermatitis [39] and, in a few rare cases, toxic shock syndrome (TSS) [40]. The process of scar regression is affected by multiple causes, such as genetic factors [41] and lesion type. It is important to note that Vaseline has consistently been reported as one of the least effective agents for scar modulation compared to other topical treatments. However, due to its safety profile, widespread availability and common use in routine postoperative wound care, we selected it as the most appropriate placebo for this clinical trial [42, 43]. The anatomical site of incision (case and control sites) was on the same patient to mitigate interpersonal variability. A key strength of this study, to the best of our knowledge, is that this is the first trial to employ a within‐subject design aimed at minimising bias in the evaluation of scar reduction outcomes. There are four types of silicone gel forms available for use, such as topical silicone gel, silicone gel sheeting, dressing and patch. In this study, topical silicone gel was used for postoperative scar management following bilateral microscopic inguinal surgery. Results showed silicone gel significantly decreased the scars after microscopic inguinal surgery for 6 weeks. Regarding Table 3, silicone gel acted as an effective agent for the reduction of pigmentation, pliability, height, pruritic and vascularity (p < 0.05) when compared to the control area (left side). However, it is noteworthy that, based on the recorded data analysis, patients did not report a significant difference in pain intensity between the right side and left side. As evidenced statistically, pain reduction was not achieved using topical silicone gel (p < 0.05). This observation implies that silicone gel has a positive influence on the physical parameters of scar, while its effect on pain may require additional therapeutic approaches.

The result of our study is corroborated by earlier trials. Other researchers showed that silicone gel was effective in their study. In 2009, Puri et al. conducted a clinical trial that enrolled 30 patients who were followed for 6 months. Their findings suggested that silicone gel has a supportive role in the healing process of both keloid and hypertrophic scars [44]. Similarly, Kim and colleagues also performed a prospective randomised clinical trial including 33 patients who had hallux valgus deformity on both feet. They illustrated that silicone sheet improved scar formation in a hallux valgus incision scar without adverse effect [45]. Onion extract in silicone derivative gel also showed amelioration of hypertrophic scar in median sternotomy surgery in paediatric patients by a study conducted by Wananukul et al. [46]. It is noteworthy to mention that using placebo can cause modest attenuation in scar regression; however, this degree of improvement is inferior in comparison to other therapeutic interventions [43]. In conclusion, the current body of evidence supports the use and efficacy of silicone gel among non‐invasive approaches for reduction or prevention of scar formation.

Conclusion

Our study provides evidence that topical silicone gel plays a clinically significant role in enhancing key attributes of scars such as pigmentation pliability, vascularity, pruritus and height, while its impact on pain intensity remains inconclusive. This finding suggests that silicone gel acts as an effective non‐invasive intervention for scar regression. Further research is required to optimise outcomes in both cosmetic and functional aspects.

Limitation

Several limitations must be acknowledged about this study. The first limitation is the small sample size, which may affect the results. Although efforts were made to standardise the application process, patients' use of topical silicone gel and the placebo such as variations in the amount of applied product and duration of application could influence the reliability of data and results. It is important to note that while the surgeon tried to ensure that all incisions had the same orientation and length, anatomical variability should be considered as a variation. The last limitation of our study is the lack of scar assessment based on the Vancouver Scar Scale, pruritic and pain 6 months after surgery. Therefore, more studies with larger and more diverse populations are recommended to confirm this conclusion.
