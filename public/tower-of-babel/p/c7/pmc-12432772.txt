Identification of hub genes related to radiosensitivity and prognosis in rectal cancer
Authors: Hui Yang, Yin Liu, Mengdi Hao, Huimin Li, Dajin Yuan, Lei Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiotherapy is one of the main treatment methods for rectal cancer (RC). However, radioresistance often leads to treatment failure and poor prognosis. This study aimed to explore the core molecules associated with radiosensitivity and prognosis of RC.MethodsGSE133057 containing RC radiosensitivity information was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the complete response (CR) group and the incomplete response (iCR) group were identified. Immune genes were obtained from the ImmPort database. Radiosensitivity-related immune genes (RRIGs) were obtained by intersecting DEGs and the immune genes. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed to study the biological functions of RRIGs. Transcriptomic and clinical data of RC were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, and the entire cohort was randomly divided into training and testing sets at a ratio of 7:3. Prognostic genes were selected by univariate and multivariate Cox analyses, and a risk model and nomogram were built subsequently. External dataset validation was performed. The relationship between the risk model and immune cell infiltration was analyzed by single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA).ResultsA total of 76 RRIGs were identified, and they were mainly involved in cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF signaling pathway, cAMP signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathway, and so on. BMP2, COLEC10, MASP2, and GCGR were screened as prognostic genes after Cox regression analysis. The risk score model demonstrated good performance in predicting prognosis as proved by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that the risk score was an independent prognostic factor for RC. Moreover, we found that the immune microenvironment was different between the high and low risk groups, and these four genes were associated with different immune cell infiltration.ConclusionsWe identified four key genes: BMP2, COLEC10, MASP2, and GCGR, which play significant roles in the radiosensitivity, immune microenvironment, and prognosis of RC.Keywords: Rectal cancer (RC), radiosensitivity, immune gene, prognosis

Highlight box

Key findings

• A prognostic model of rectal cancer (RC) was established based on four radiosensitivity-related immune genes (RRIGs).

• Four hub RRIGs (BMP2, COLEC10, MASP2, and GCGR) were identified, which showed associations with both the radiosensitivity and prognosis of RC patients.

What is known and what is new?

• Radioresistance compromises RC treatment efficacy. Immune microenvironment influences radiosensitivity and prognosis, but reliable biomarkers are lacking.

• This study identifies BMP2, COLEC10, MASP2, and GCGR as novel biomarkers linking radiosensitivity, immune infiltration, and prognosis in RC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These genes offer potential targets for radiosensitization. The risk model could guide personalized treatment and warrants clinical validation. Future studies should validate these genes in prospective clinical cohorts and explore therapeutic strategies targeting these biomarkers.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common cancers and ranks third in malignant tumors and second in mortality worldwide (1). Approximately one-third of CRC occurs in the rectum, of which 60% of patients present in a locally advanced stage (cT3/T4 or N+) (2). Currently, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) combined with total mesorectal excision is the standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) (3,4). It has been reported that the pathologic complete response (pCR) rate can be 15–27% after nCRT, and these patients will have lower recurrence rate and higher survival rate (5). However, about one-third of patients are less sensitive or resistant to nCRT, showing little tumor regression or even progressing after treatment (6). For these patients, nCRT not only delays surgical timing and incurs additional costs but also causes radiation-related toxicities. Currently, there are no effective biomarkers to predict radiation response, and molecular markers reported in different studies show little overlap (7-9). The development of radioresistance involves multi-genes, multi-pathways, and multi-factors. The genes capable of predicting the radiosensitivity of rectal cancer (RC) remain unclear (10). It is crucial for improving RC treatment efficacy and advancing personalized medicine to explore core genes involved in RC radiosensitivity and develop radiosensitization strategies.

In recent years, with the development of immunotherapy, the relationship between radiotherapy and immunity has attracted increasing attention. Radiotherapy induces immunogenic cell death, releases tumor-associated antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns, activates dendritic cells (DCs), and promotes T-cell infiltration, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity (11-13). In addition, an increasing number of studies have shown that immune microenvironment can affect radiation response by regulating antitumor immune surveillance, antigen presentation, and so on. El Sissy et al. calculated the biopsy-adapted immunoscore (ISB) based on the infiltration of CD3+ and CD8+ T cells, through which they could predict response to nCRT and better define patients eligible for an organ preservation strategy (Watch-and-Wait) (14). Qian et al. developed a 15-immune gene prediction model to predict the response to nCRT (15). And Lynch et al. systematically reported how radiotherapy modulates immune checkpoint molecules to influence therapeutic outcomes (16). This study aimed to identify hub genes which may play important roles in radiosensitivity, immune microenvironment, and prognosis of RC. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-521/rc).

Methods

Study design

The flowchart of the entire study is shown in Figure 1. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Flow chart of the study. CR, complete response; DEGs, differentially expressed genes; GO, Gene Ontology; iCR, incomplete response; KEGG, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes; RC, rectal cancer; RRIGs, radiosensitivity-related immune genes; ssGSEA, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis; TCGA, The Cancer Genome Atlas.

Data sources

GSE133057 containing RC radiosensitivity information was acquired from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The clinical information and transcriptomic data of RC patients were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Moreover, the immune gene set was obtained from the ImmPort database (https://www.immport.org/).

Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between complete and incomplete response (iCR) groups

First, we performed log2 standardization on the messenger RNA (mRNA) data and took the average value of genes with multiple probes in the GSE133057 dataset. All patients in GSE133057 underwent preoperative radiotherapy based on 5-fluorouracil, with a radiation dose of 50.4 Gy/25 f. The response to nCRT was determined by postoperative pathological staging according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) system. Patients with AJCC 0 were defined as the complete response (CR) group, while patients with AJCC 1–3 were defined as the iCR group. DEGs between the CR and iCR groups were screened using the “limma” package. The selection criteria were |log2fold change (FC)| >0.5 and P<0.05. “Pheatmap” and “ggplot2” packages were used to generate heatmap and volcano plot.

Identification of radiosensitivity-related immune genes (RRIGs)

Following a precise comparison between the immune gene set and the DEGs set, genes present in both sets were selected to generate a new dataset designated as RRIGs. Formally, this dataset corresponds to the intersection of the two sets mentioned above.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment pathway analyses of RRIGs

RRIGs were used for GO and KEGG enrichment pathway analyses. GO analysis included biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). GO and KEGG were determined to be significantly enriched when adjusted P<0.05.

Construction and validation of the prognostic risk model

The prognostic risk model was established and validated using RC samples obtained from the TCGA. After screening for samples with complete clinical information, a total of 145 patients were included in the analysis. These samples were randomly divided into a training set and a testing set in a 7:3 ratio. Univariate Cox and multivariate Cox regression were used to establish the risk model, and the risk score was calculated using the formula: risk score = h0(t) × exp (β1X1 + β2X2 + ... + βnXn). In this formula, h0(t) is a constant, β represents the coefficient of multivariate Cox regression, X represents gene expression. Basing on the median risk score, the cases in the training set were divided into high and low risk groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to calculate the overall survival (OS), and the “survival ROC” package was used to plot receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year. Risk score distribution plots, survival status scatterplots, and heatmaps were used to evaluate the model. Testing set and the entire TCGA set were used to confirm the accuracy of the model.

Independent prognostic analysis and development of a predictive nomogram

Next, we incorporated the risk score, age, gender, stage, and other clinicopathological factors for univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Factors with P<0.05 were determined to be independent prognostic factors. Additionally, we generated a nomogram to predict the survival for the RC patients and Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was calculated to evaluate the prediction accuracy of the nomogram.

Survival analysis of the hub genes

We utilized Kaplan-Meier plotter (https://kmplot.com) to investigate the role of hub genes with respect to the prognosis of CRC patients.

External validation of radiosensitivity of the hub genes

We selected GSE150082 as an external dataset to validate the radiosensitivity of these hub genes. GSE150082 includes 39 samples of LARC who underwent nCRT. The expression levels of these hub genes were compared between the groups with different radiosensitivity, and boxplots were drawn.

Immune infiltration analysis

To further explore the correlation between the immune microenvironment and the risk score, “single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)” was used to analyze the infiltration of 28 immune cells. Furthermore, the correlations between the expressions of the hub genes and the infiltration of 28 immune cells were calculated.

Statistical analysis

R 4.2.0 software was utilized to conduct all bioinformatics analyses. Cox regression analysis was used to screen out prognostic genes. Survival analysis was performed by Kaplan-Meier method. GraphPad Prism 10.3.0 software was applied to analyze the radiosensitivity data of hub genes. Comparison between groups was performed by Wilcoxon test. Spearman’s correlations were used for the correlation analysis. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of DEGs

A total of 1,171 DEGs were identified, among which 698 genes were upregulated and 473 genes were downregulated in the CR group. A volcano plot and heatmap were generated to show the results (Figure 2A,2B).

Identification of DEGs between the CR and iCR groups. (A) Volcano plot for DEGs. (B) Heatmap of the DEGs. CR, complete response; DEGs, differentially expressed genes; iCR, incomplete response.

Identification of RRIGs

Taking intersection of the 698 upregulated genes, 473 downregulated genes, and 1,793 immune genes, we identified 76 RRIGs with 43 genes upregulated and 33 genes downregulated (Figure 3).

Venn diagram. Seventy-six RRIGs were identified with 43 upregulated and 33 downregulated. DEGs, differentially expressed genes; RRIGs, radiosensitivity-related immune genes.

GO/KEGG enrichment pathway analyses

A total of 460 GO terms were identified as significantly enriched, including 426 GO-BP terms and 34 GO-MF terms. The top 5 terms enriched in GO-BP and GO-MF were displayed in Figure 4A. Similarly, 60 KEGG terms were significantly enriched, which mainly included cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, cAMP signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathway, and so on (Figure 4B).

GO/KEGG enrichment pathway analysis. (A) The top 5 terms enriched by GO-BP/MF of RRIGs. (B) The top 10 terms enriched by KEGG of RRIGs. BP, biological process; GO, Gene Ontology; KEGG, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes; MF, molecular function; RRIGs, radiosensitivity-related immune genes.

Construction and validation of the predictive model

After univariate Cox, 6 RRIGs affecting the OS of RC were identified in the training set (Figure 5A). Then, these six genes were included in a multivariate Cox analysis and ultimately four genes, including BMP2, COLEC10, MASP2, and GCGR, were obtained to establish the risk model, as shown in Figure 5B. The risk score of each patient = h0(t) × exp[(−0.4436369 × BMP2 expression) + (−3.2845855 × COLEC10 expression) + (−1.5165470 × MASP2 expression) + (3.0814537 × GCGR expression)]. According to the median risk score, the training set was divided into a high-risk group (n=52) and a low-risk group (n=52). Kaplan-Meier curves for the OS of the two groups were significantly different (P=0.008, Figure 6A). The predictive efficiency of the risk model was studied using the ROC curve, which showed a moderate accuracy. The area under the curve (AUC) values of the risk model at 1-, 3-, and 5-year of OS were 0.736, 0.896, and 0.744, respectively (Figure 6B). The risk score and the survival status of each patient were shown in the prognostic curve and scatter plot, respectively. The gene expression profiles of the four genes were shown in the heatmap (Figure 6C).

Identification of prognostic RRIGs. (A) Univariate forest plot of the correlation between the expression of RRIGs and the OS of RC patients. (B) Multivariate forest plot of the correlation between the expression of RRIGs and the OS of RC patients. CI, confidence interval; HR, hazard ratio; OS, overall survival; RC, rectal cancer; RRIGs, radiosensitivity-related immune genes.

Construction of the risk score model in the training set. (A) Kaplan-Meier curve of OS between the high and low risk groups (P=0.008). (B) ROC curve of the training set. (C) The prognostic curve, scatter plot and heatmap of the training set. AUC, area under the curve; OS, overall survival; ROC, receiver operating characteristic.

We validated the risk model using the testing set and the entire TCGA set. Based on the median risk score, the patients were stratified into high- or low-risk groups in both sets. There were significant differences in OS between the two risk groups in both the testing and the entire TCGA sets (P=0.02, P=0.02) (Figure 7A,7B). The AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year in the testing set were 0.754, 0.799, and 0.937, respectively. And the AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year in the entire set were 0.736, 0.801, and 0.814, respectively (Figure 7C,7D). The risk score distribution plots, survival status scatterplots, and heatmaps were shown in the figure (Figure 7E,7F).

(A,B) Kaplan-Meier curve of OS between the high and low risk groups in (A) the testing set and (B) the entire TCGA set (P=0.02, P=0.02). (C,D) ROC curves of (C) the testing set and (D) the entire TCGA set. (E,F) The prognostic curve, scatter plot and heatmap of (E) the testing set and (F) the entire TCGA set. Independent prognostic analysis of the risk score

Univariate Cox regression analysis indicated that risk score, tumor stage and age were significantly associated with prognosis (Figure 8A). Then, multivariate Cox regression analysis indicated that risk score, tumor stage and age were independent prognostic factors (Figure 8B). Next, the nomogram was designed and the C-index of the nomogram was 0.84, indicating that the nomogram model had a certain predictive value (Figure 8C).

Independent prognostic analysis of the risk score. (A) Univariate Cox regression analysis. (B) Multivariate Cox regression analysis (P<0.05). (C) The nomogram designed with the risk score, stage and age (C-index =0.84). CI, confidence interval; HR, hazard ratio.

Survival analysis

The results of Kaplan-Meier plotter showed that low mRNA expression levels of BMP2, COLEC10, and MASP2 were correlated with shorter post-progression survival (PPS) (Figure 9A-9C). And a high mRNA expression level of GCGR was correlated with shorter OS (Figure 9D).

Radiosensitivity of the hub genes

According to response to radiotherapy, the 39 samples in GSE150082 were divided into 16 good responders and 23 poor responders. The expression levels of BMP2, COLEC10, and GCGR were high in the good response group. And the expression level of MASP2 was low in the good response group (Figure 10A-10D).

Discussion

RC is one of the most common cancers in the world. In recent years, more attention has been paid to the genes related to radiosensitivity of RC, and their effect on prognosis. Immune genes are associated with immune regulation and microenvironment of the tumors, the expression of which can affect patients’ response to radiotherapy (17-19). In this study, we found 76 RRIGs. GO/KEGG enrichment analysis showed that cytokine-mediated signaling pathway, epithelial cell proliferation, TNF signaling pathway, cAMP signaling pathway, and TLR signaling pathway were significantly enriched. Cytokine-mediated signaling pathways have a complex relationship with radiosensitivity, as they can modulate cell apoptosis, regulate immune cell functions, control tumor angiogenesis, and participate in cell cycle regulation, thereby affecting the efficacy of radiotherapy (20). In addition, epithelial cell proliferation has been found to be related to radiosensitivity, cancer cells with a high rate of proliferation tend to be more sensitive to radiation according to the “Bergonie-Tribondeau law” (21). The cAMP signaling pathway is involved in a variety of physiological processes, including metabolism, gene expression, cell growth, and differentiation. This signaling pathway can also regulate tumor immune microenvironment after radiotherapy, affecting the immune response and therapeutic effect (22). TLRs are a type of receptors expressed on the surface of immune cells, especially antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as DCs (23). Radiotherapy can damage tumor cells, releasing some damage-associated molecules, which can activate TLRs, promote the maturation and activation of DCs, increase the infiltration and activation of T cells, and enhance the antibody production of B cells, enhancing the antitumor immune response. These enriched functions and pathways may help us understand the molecular mechanism of RRIGs regulating RC radiotherapy. After the univariate and multivariate Cox analyses, we finally screened four key genes, which were the radiosensitivity up-regulated genes: BMP2, COLEC10, GCGR and the radiosensitivity down-regulated gene: MASP2.

BMP2, located on the 20p12 region of human chromosome 20, encodes bone morphogenetic protein 2 (24). In colon cancer, BMP2 acts as a tumor suppressor. It inhibits cell proliferation, promotes apoptosis and differentiation, as indicated by changes in markers such as proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), cleaved caspase 3, and β-catenin (25). Inactivation of BMP signaling pathway has been shown to be highly prevalent in CRCs (26). Moreover, BMP2 encodes a secreted ligand of the TGF-β protein superfamily, which is involved in a key regulatory pathway that regulates cell growth, differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis in CRC (27). In nCRT of RC, the expression level of BMP2 was significantly lower in the non-responder group than in the responder group (15). Additionally, BMP2 inhibits cancer stem cells in osteosarcoma and renal cancer by inducing osteogenic differentiation (28). However, the role of BMP2 in radiotherapy has not been reported. In our study, we found that BMP2 was highly expressed in the CR group and was associated with a better prognosis in RC patients. In terms of immune infiltration, BMP2 was found to be significantly positively associated with type 17 T helper cell, activated B cell, eosinophil, Th2 cell, activated CD4 T cell, immature B cell, mast cell, and memory B cell. These studies have provided a foundation for BMP2 as a promising target for antitumor therapy.

COLEC10, which encodes for collectin liver 1 (CL-L1), is rarely reported in RC but has been studied extensively in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). COLEC10 serves as a critical tumor suppressor gene in HCC, inhibiting tumor progression by suppressing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and regulating the Hedgehog/PI3K-AKT pathway. Its low expression level is remarkably associated with a poor prognosis (29). Cai et al. have reported that COLEC10 inhibits the stem cell properties of HCC by upregulating WIF1 and promoting β-catenin degradation, and KLHL22-mediated ubiquitination degradation is a key mechanism for its low expression. These findings provide new molecular targets for precision therapy of HCC (30). Other studies have reported that COLEC10 affects tumor immune microenvironment by affecting the infiltration of CD8+ T cells, B cells, and DCs. Low expression of COLEC10 is associated with the high-risk group and it negatively regulates the risk score to indicate favorable prognosis (31). In this study, similar results were obtained with enhanced COLEC10 expression in the CR group, affecting the infiltration of T cells and DCs, which was associated with radiosensitivity and better prognosis.

MASP2, which encodes a member of serine proteases, a plasma protein involved in the lectin pathway of the complement system. In CRC patients, high serum MASP2 levels are associated with high recurrence rate and poor survival, indicating its potential as an independent prognostic biomarker (32). In non-small cell lung cancer, MASP2 is more highly expressed in lung adenocarcinoma than in lung squamous cell carcinoma, and its low expression is associated with poor prognosis, potentially influencing the tumor microenvironment through the complement pathway (33,34). In our study, the results showed that MASP2 was highly expressed in the iCR group and was associated with a better prognosis in RC patients. Moreover, MASP2 was found to be significantly positively associated with plasmacytoid DC, memory B cell, and negatively associated with type 17 T helper cell.

GCGR, which is a gene expressed in various organs including liver, kidney, brain, intestine, pancreas and fat tissue, plays an important role in regulating glucose (35). Glucagon mediates AMPK and MAPK pathways through GCGR, which promotes the progression of CRC (36). Similarly, in this study, GCGR was found to be associated with a worse prognosis. GCGR may be a novel marker to improve the efficacy of RC treatment.

Immune infiltration refers to the infiltration degree of immune cells in tumor tissues which affects tumor microenvironment, thereby affecting radiosensitivity and tumor prognosis, and a good immune status may enhance the efficacy of treatment and improve patients’ survival (37). Therefore, it is likely that immune genes can be used as potential targets for treating RC patients. In this study, we explored the correlation between the risk score and immune status, and found that the immature DC, memory B cell, plasmacytoid DC, and Th2 cell of the low-risk group were higher than those of the high-risk group. DCs are the most functional APCs, which can activate and enhance tumor antigen-specific T cells through delivering tumor-specific antigens and secreting cytokines. Meanwhile, DCs can also inhibit tumor immune evasion by expressing inhibitory molecules and secreting inhibitory cytokines (38). Memory B cells are important immune cells with long-term memory that can kill tumor cells by delivering tumor-specific antibodies. They participate in antitumor immune response and regulate T cell immune response to enhance immunosurveillance and clearance (39). Simson et al. highlighted the potential role of Th2 cell-mediated immune effector cells and their mediators in tumor immune surveillance and clearance (40). Th2 cells may affect the therapeutic efficacy by regulating immune cells and cytokines in tumor microenvironment. The enhanced anti-tumor immune response and better prognosis of the low-risk group confirm that our model is reliable in predicting cancer prognosis.

Our study elucidates the relationship among immune genes, radiosensitivity, and prognosis of RC. The immune status and immune cell infiltration of patients may affect their response to radiotherapy, and meanwhile, radiotherapy can promote the release of tumor-associated antigens, activate the immune system, and enhance the antitumor response, which may affect the prognosis of patients. However, there are some limitations in this study. Firstly, there are few samples of RC in the TCGA database, which results in a relatively small sample size in this study. Secondly, due to the lack of survival information for RC patients in the GEO database, both the training and validation data were derived from TCGA. Thirdly, it is difficult to obtain biopsy tissue samples before nCRT, and thus the study lacks validation from clinical specimens.

Conclusions

We identified four hub RRIGs (BMP2, COLEC10, MASP2, and GCGR), which are associated with the radiosensitivity and prognosis of RC patients. Subsequently, we will validate the functions of these genes through cell and animal experiments.
