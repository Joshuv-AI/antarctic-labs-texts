Harnessing a bispecific αGD2 × αCD3 protein engager to target GD2-overexpressing lung tumors
Authors: Nunghathai Sawasdee, Aussara Panya, Jatuporn Sujjitjoon, Pa-thai Yenchitsomanus, Chutamas Thepmalee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer remains one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide, with persistently poor 5-year survival rates across all stages. The development of novel therapies is therefore crucial for overcoming treatment resistance, improving survival rates, reducing side effects, and enhancing patient outcomes. Disialoganglioside GD2 has emerged as an attractive tumor-associated antigen for immunotherapy against various cancer types. In our study, we confirmed GD2 expression in lung cancer cells and observed differential expression levels, with A549 cells exhibiting markedly higher GD2 expression compared to NCI-H460 cells. To specifically target GD2-expressing lung cancers, we engineered a bispecific protein engager, termed αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE, designed to redirect T cells toward GD2-overexpressing lung cancer cells. The αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE (55 kDa) was successfully produced in a eukaryotic expression system and purified, as confirmed by Western blot analysis. Binding assays demonstrated that the BiPE specifically recognized GD2 on 143B osteosarcoma cells (which exhibit high GD2 expression) and CD3 on T cells. Functionally, treatment with αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE significantly enhanced T-cell activation and proliferation, as indicated by increased proportion of CD25 + CD3+ and CD69 + CD3+ cells after 5 days compared with untreated controls. In co-culture experiments, the BiPE also enhanced T-cell cytotoxicity, resulting in greater killing of A549 cells compared to NCI-H460 cells, consistent with their differential GD2 expression. These findings demonstrate that αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE effectively enhances T-cell activity and anti-tumor responses in a GD2-dependent manner. Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide and remains the leading cause of cancer-related death, with a steadily increasing incidence and mortality rate. Lung cancer is categorized into two major histologic subtypes: small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). NSCLC accounts for approximately 85–90% of all cases, which can be further categorized into adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large-cell carcinoma. According to GLOBOCAN 2022, an estimated 2.48 million (12.4%) new cases of lung cancer were reported globally across all subtypes1. In men, lung cancer accounted for 1.57 million cases, with adenocarcinoma being the most common subtype (45.6%), followed by squamous cell carcinoma (SCC, 29.4%), small-cell carcinoma (11.5%), and large-cell carcinoma (6.5%). Among women (908,630 cases), adenocarcinoma was also the predominant subtype (59.7%), followed by SCC (17.1%), small-cell carcinoma (9.7%), and large-cell carcinoma (6.5%)1.

The incidence and mortality rate of lung cancer are influenced by a range of genetic and environmental factors. Somatic mutations in key oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, such as TP53, EGFR, and KRAS, are frequently implicated in lung tumorigenesis2. Additionally, mutations in CDKN2A, LRP1B, and LRP2 have been linked to acquired resistance to icotinib and gefitinib, epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs), underscoring the role of genetic polymorphisms in modulating therapeutic response2. Apart from the genetic background, environmental and lifestyle factors—including tobacco smoking, air pollution, radon gas exposure, occupational hazards, and household indoor pollution—have been well-documented contributors to lung tumorigenesis and disease progression3–5. Currently, treatment options for lung cancer include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, or a combination of these approaches, depending on cancer type, disease stage, and patient health status. However, delayed diagnosis is common, contributing to lower one- and five-year survival rates compared with other cancers. Disease stage at diagnosis is a critical determinant of treatment outcomes. A study of Thai lung cancer patients reported overall median survival times of 39.72, 21.84, 11.26, and 6.24 months for stages I–IV, respectively6. These data suggest the urgent need for a new effective therapeutic approach for lung cancer, particularly for patients diagnosed at advanced stages (III–IV).

Recently, immunotherapy, including immunomodulators, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), chimeric antigen receptor (CAR-T) cells, and natural killer (NK) cell therapy, has emerged as a promising therapeutic option for harnessing a patient’s immunity to combat cancer, particularly hematologic malignancies such as leukemia, lymphoma, and myeloma7. Bispecific protein engagers (BiPEs) represent a novel class of immunotherapy designed to potentiate T-cell function by facilitating the interaction between T cells and cancer cells, thereby promoting T-cell activation and proliferation. Constructed from single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) that simultaneously target CD3 on T cells and tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) on cancer cells, BiPEs effectively bridge the two cell types, enhancing immune synapse formation and improving anti-tumor activity8. According to the feasibility of BiPE, there are several BiPEs approved for the treatment of hematologic malignancy, i.e., Blinatumomab (targeting CD19), Glofitamab (targeting CD20), Mosunetuzumab (targeting CD20), Epcoritamab (targeting CD20), Talquetamab (targeting GPRC5D), and Tarlatamab (targeting delta-like ligand 3). Unfortunately, no BiPE has received approval for solid tumors, particularly lung cancer. The disialoganglioside GD2 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target owing to its elevated expression in several human malignancies, including neuroblastoma, melanoma, osteosarcoma, glioma, and lung cancer9,10. Its restricted expression to specific tumor types helps minimize the risk of off-target effects. Immunohistochemical analyses of non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) revealed GD2 expression in 72% of lung adenocarcinomas (LUAD, n = 150) and 56% of lung squamous cell carcinomas (LUSC, n = 103), while 39% of small-cell lung cancers (SCLC) were also positive11. These findings support GD2 as a potent target for lung cancer immunotherapy, particularly for BiPEs.

In this study, we designed and constructed a pCDH lentiviral expression system to generate the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE, which enables the simultaneous targeting of lung cancer cells and T cells. The protein was produced in a HEK293T eukaryotic expression system and purified using a His-tag affinity column. The ability of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE to enhance T-cell proliferation and activation was evaluated, along with its capacity to increase T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity against non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell lines. A co-culture system was used to compare its effects on NCI-H460 cells (low GD2 expression) and A549 cells (high GD2 expression).

Results

GD2 expression in lung cancer

The expression of GD2 in lung cancer cells, including NCI-H460 and A549, was determined by flow cytometry using anti-GD2 PE antibody staining. As shown in Fig. 1a, c, and d, the percentages of positive cells and mean fluorescent intensity (MFI) for GD2 expression in NCI-H460 were 7.17 ± 0.49% and 574.3 ± 14.75, respectively, whereas in A549 (Fig. 1b, c and d), they were 42.20 ± 0.85% and 2325 ± 50.61, respectively. Notably, the level of GD2 expression directly influences its efficacy. These results indicated that A549 cells expressed significantly higher levels of GD2 than NCI-H460 cells (p < 0.0001), suggesting that GD2 is a potential target for cancer immunotherapy in lung cancer.

Results are presented as mean ± Standard Error of the Mean (SEM) from three independent experiments (N = 3). Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test, with ****p < 0.0001.

Production and purification of αGD2 × αCD3 protein engager (BiPE) in HEK293T system

The αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE was designed to bring T cells and cancer cells closer together, thereby increasing T-cell cytotoxicity against lung cancer cells. We employed a lentiviral system to produce a lentiviral particle containing genes for an anti-GD2 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) coupled to an anti-CD3 scFv under EF-1α promoter. Additionally, the plasmid construct includes red fluorescent protein (RFP) as a reporter for αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE protein expression (Fig. 2a). The 3D structure of the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE was predicted using the AlphaFold Server and visualized with Discovery Studio Visualizer (Fig. 2b). HEK293T cells were transduced with the lentiviral particle containing the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE plasmid construct to create a stable cell line that expresses and secretes the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE in the cell lysate and culture supernatant, respectively (Fig. 2c and d). The αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE protein was effectively produced and purified using a His GraviTrap column. The molecular weight of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE was approximately 55 kDa (Fig. 2e). These results suggest that the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE protein was successfully produced and purified for the next experiment.

Binding activity of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE to target cells

The specific binding of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE to target cells (GD2 + or CD3 + cells) was investigated by flow cytometry. The binding specificity was assessed in lung cancer cell lines, including NCI-H460 and A549 cells. The results demonstrated that αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE selectively bound to A549 cells (39.87 ± 0.44%; p < 0.0001), whereas only minimal binding was detected in NCI-H460 cells (2.31 ± 0.44%) (Supplementary Fig. S1). These findings are consistent with the differential levels of GD2 expression observed between the two lung cancer cell lines (Fig. 1c). To further validate GD2-dependent binding, two osteosarcoma cell lines were used as representatives of low- and high-GD2-expressing cells, based on their distinct basal GD2 expression levels. The GD2 expressions on the surface of osteosarcoma cells (SaOS-2 and 143B cell lines) were determined using an anti-GD2 PE antibody to assess basal GD2 expression. The results indicated that the positive cells expressing GD2 on SaOS-2 and 143B cell lines were 3.27 ± 0.22% and 99.83 ± 0.06%, respectively (Fig. 3a). For binding activity, the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE bound to SaOS-2 cells at a rate of 9.48 ± 0.66%, significantly lower than the binding to 143B, which was 94.20 ± 0.80% (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, the binding of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE to T cells was significant at 76.37 ± 6.27% (Fig. 3d) (p < 0.001), comparable to the binding observed with an anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody, which showed 65.15 ± 3.87% binding (Fig. 3c). In contrast, Raji cells, which do not express CD3, showed no detectable binding to αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE. These results demonstrate that αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE exhibits selective, target-specific binding to GD2-expressing cancer cells, including 143B and A549, and to CD3 on human T-cell lymphocytes, confirming its dual-specific binding capability.

Cytotoxicity of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE on immune cells

To confirm the specificity and evaluate potential off-target effects of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE, its toxicity toward non-adherent cells (predominantly a lymphocyte-enriched population) was assessed using an apoptosis assay. Cells were treated with αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE (100 pg) or PHA-L for 24 and 48 h, and the number of apoptotic cells was quantified by Annexin V/PI staining and flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 4a and b, apoptosis levels in the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE group (7.53 ± 1.05% at 24 h; 8.67 ± 0.64% at 48 h) were comparable to the untreated control (7.93 ± 1.07% at 24 h; 7.83 ± 0.17% at 48 h). In contrast, PHA-L treatment significantly increased apoptosis (14.57 ± 0.17% at 48 h). These findings indicate that αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE at 100 pg does not induce cytotoxicity in non-adherent immune cells after in vitro exposure.

The effect of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE on the human normal immune cell apoptosis. The non-adherent cells (predominantly a lymphocyte-enriched population) were treated with 100 pg of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE or 5 µM PHA-L per 100,000 cells for 24 h (a) and 48 h (b). Apoptotic cells were determined by Annexin V/PI staining using flow cytometry. Values are presented as mean ± SEM from three independent experiments (N = 3). Statistical significance was determined by using one-way ANOVA with ****p < 0.0001.

Immune cell phenotype and proliferation after treatment with αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE

The immunophenotype of immune cells treated with αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE for 5 days was analyzed by flow cytometry using fluorescent dye-conjugated antibodies to surface markers. The immune cell population was altered, exhibiting a slight increase in CD8 + T cells (CD3 + CD8+) (Fig. 5b) relative to untreated conditions. CD4 + T cells (CD3 + CD4+) and NK cells (CD3 − CD56+) exhibited a propensity for reduction (Fig. 5a and c). The CFSE cell proliferation assay was used to assess the potency of the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE in enhancing the proliferation of immune cells. CFSE-labeled lymphocytes were incubated with 100 pg of the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE for 3 days. Results demonstrated an increase in T cell proliferation following treatment with the bispecific αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE (24.27 ± 2.95%, p < 0.01) and PHA-L as an activator (64.40 ± 2.75%, p < 0.001) compared with the untreated control (4.76 ± 0.48), as illustrated in Fig. 5d. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE enhanced T cell proliferation.

Activation of T-cells by αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE

The T-cell activating potential of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE was evaluated by measuring the expression of the activation markers CD25 and CD69. Lymphocytes were incubated for 5 days with either 100 pg of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE or 5 µM PHA-L. The proportions of CD25 + CD3+ and CD69 + CD3+ cells were determined by flow cytometry using fluorescent dye-conjugated antibodies. As shown in Fig. 6a and c, treatment with αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE resulted in a statistically significant but modest increase in the percentage of CD25 + CD3+ cells (6.11 ± 1.27%, p < 0.0001) compared with the untreated control (0.50 ± 0.11%). In contrast, PHA-L induced a markedly higher level of activation (43.70 ± 5.83%, p < 0.0001). Similarly, αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE (12.73 ± 2.67%, p < 0.0001) and PHA-L (21.13 ± 3.55%, p < 0.0001) treatments significantly elevated the proportion of CD69 + CD3+ cells compared with the control (0.62 ± 0.06%), as illustrated in Fig. 6b and d. These findings suggest that αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE can activate T cells by upregulating CD25 and CD69 expression on CD3 + T cells.

The effect of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE on T-cell activation status. αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE or PHA-L was incubated with non-adherent immune cells for 5 days. Cells were harvested and stained with anti-CD3 FITC, anti-CD25 PerCP-Cy5.5, and anti-CD69 APC antibodies. The proportions of positive cells for CD3 + CD25+ (a and c) and CD3 + CD69+ (b and d) were quantified and analyzed by using FlowJoTM v10 software. Results are presented as mean ± SEM from three independent experiments (N = 3). Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ****p < 0.0001.

αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE improved T cell cytolytic activity

The direct cytotoxic effects of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE on NCI-H460 and A549 lung cancer cells were assessed by crystal violet staining. Under the conditions tested, αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE did not induce detectable cytotoxicity in either lung cancer cell line, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. To enhance the cytolytic activity of T cells against lung cancer cells, we employed the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE–armed T cells (BATs) platform by saturating T cells with αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE, thereby improving their ability to recognize and eliminate target cells. Cytolytic activity was assessed by crystal violet staining following 48 h of co-culture of αGD2 × αCD3 BATs with NCI-H460 (low GD2 expression) or A549 (high GD2 expression) cells. The results revealed that no significant difference in cytotoxicity between BATs and T cells was observed in NCI-H460 cells (Fig. 7a). In contrast, BATs markedly reduced A549 cell viability to 34.00 ± 3.05% (E: T 1:2.5, p < 0.05) and 23.36 ± 2.61% (E: T 1:5, p < 0.05) compared with T cells alone (63.60 ± 5.26% and 48.16 ± 9.34%, respectively) (Fig. 7b). Additionally, the remained lung cancer cells observed by using the MuviCyte Live-cell Imaging system showed the potential activity of αGD2 × αCD3 BATs to kill the specific target cells (Supplementary Fig. S3). These findings demonstrate that αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE enhances T-cell–mediated specific killing by facilitating T-cell engagement with GD2-expressing lung cancer cells.

T cells were armed with an αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE to generate BiPE-armed T cells (BATs). T cells, or BATs, were then co-cultured with lung cancer cell lines at an effector (E)-to-target (T) ratio of 0:1, 1:1, 1:2.5, and 1:5 for 48 h. Statistical significance was determined using two-way ANOVA with *p < 0.05.

Discussion

The incidence of lung cancer continues to increase globally, driven not only by genetic predisposition and lifestyle factors but also by environmental exposures. Among these, the association between air pollution and lung cancer has been increasingly recognized, with studies linking exposure to elevated risks of both morbidity and mortality12. In 2019, an estimated 4,140,971 deaths from all causes and 307,680 lung cancer deaths were attributed to ambient particulate matter with a diameter less than 2.5 micrometers (PM2.5) worldwide13. Beyond initiating and accelerating disease progression, air pollution also appears to influence lung cancer subtypes. Lung adenocarcinoma has recently emerged as the predominant subtype, with the highest burden attributed to ambient PM2.5 pollution, particularly in East Asia, including China1. These findings indicate a projected continued rise in the global burden of PM2.5-related lung cancer in the coming years14. Importantly, due to current standard treatments for lung cancer remaining insufficient in significantly improving patient survival rates, the development of more effective therapeutic approaches and the identification of novel cancer targets are urgently needed to address the rising incidence of lung cancer.

GD2, a disialoganglioside glycolipid, is frequently overexpressed in cancers, where it promotes tumor cell survival and metastasis, induces T-cell dysfunction, and acts as an immune checkpoint. Consequently, GD2 has emerged as a promising target for immunotherapy15. Our results demonstrated that GD2 expression was markedly higher in A549 cells, an adenocarcinoma subtype of NSCLC, compared with NCI-H460 cells, a large-cell carcinoma subtype of NSCLC (Fig. 1). This finding is consistent with previous immunohistochemistry (IHC) data from patients with lung cancer, which reported GD2 expression in 39% of small cell lung cancer (SCLC), 72% of lung adenocarcinoma, and 56% of squamous cell carcinoma cases. In SCLC specimens, cytoplasmic GD2 staining was observed in 38%, 1.3%, and 0.04% of tumor cells exhibiting light (1+), moderate (2+), and strong (3+) intensity, respectively. In NSCLC, 56.6%, 6.1%, and 0.2% of tumor cells demonstrated light (1+), moderate (2+), and strong (3+) cytoplasmic GD2 staining, respectively. In addition, flow cytometry analysis confirmed GD2 expression on the cell surface of freshly isolated tumor cells obtained from biopsy specimens. Although GD2 staining in lung cancer tissues was predominantly cytoplasmic, a subset of tumors exhibiting weak cytoplasmic staining also showed membranous localization11. Collectively, these findings indicate that GD2 expression density varies among different histological subtypes of lung cancer. Importantly, the relatively high GD2 density observed in A549 cells supports their suitability as a clinically relevant high-GD2 model for lung adenocarcinoma studies. The regulation of GD2 expression involves the ST8Sia1 gene (which encodes GD3 synthase) and the B4GalNT1 gene (which encodes GD2 synthase). Both genes can form homo- or heterodimers that transactivate each other to convert GD3 into GD2. Overexpression of these enzymes shifts dimer composition and amplifies this effect, leading to increased surface GD2. Moreover, post-translational modifications such as N-glycosylation can influence glycosyltransferase localization, thereby further modulating GD2 biosynthesis15,16. As a result, GD2 has been validated as a therapeutic target in lung cancer. Consistent with previous reports, GD2 expression in solid tumors, including glioblastoma and melanoma, correlates with responsiveness to GD2-targeted therapies, such as CAR-T cells and bispecific T-cell engagers11,17,18.

In this investigation, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE in enhancing T-cell cytotoxicity against GD2-expressing lung cancer cells, a prevalent cancer type in the Thai population19. A bispecific protein engager, αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE, was developed using a lentiviral system to stably express a humanized anti-GD2 linked to an anti-CD3 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) in HEK293T mammalian cells, the most commonly used cell line for producing therapeutic biopharmaceuticals20. The structural modeling result verified the correct alignment of both binding domains, and the purified fusion protein produced a 55-kDa product (Fig. 2). These results confirm the successful production of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE and support its further evaluation in functional assays targeting GD2-positive lung cancer. The safety of the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE toward normal immune cells was evaluated by assessing cell death following BiPE treatment using Annexin V/PI staining. As shown in Fig. 4, treatment with 100 pgof BiPE did not induce apoptosis in non-adherent cells—predominantly a lymphocyte-enriched population—after 24–48 h of in vitro exposure. These results indicate that αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE does not exert measurable acute cytotoxic effects on normal immune cells under the tested conditions. The binding activity against target molecules, including GD2 and CD3, confirmed that the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE only binds to GD2-expressing cancer cells (A549 and 143B cells) and CD3-positive effector cells (T cells), as shown in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Fig. 3. This supports its use as a T-cell-redirecting therapy. The binding of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE to human T cells is similar to that of a regular anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody. This indicates that it can bind to the CD3 complex, which is part of the T cell receptor (TCR), and initiate a signaling cascade in the T cell, rendering it cytotoxic, similar to blinatumomab, a bispecific CD19-directed CD3 T-cell engager (BiTE) used in the treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL)21. Treatment with αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE resulted in a slight, non-significant increase in the proportion of CD3 + CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) (Fig. 5b). In addition, an increase in T-cell proliferation was observed following αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE exposure. The proportions of CD3 + CD25+ and CD3 + CD69+ T cells were significantly higher than in untreated controls (Fig. 6), indicating that αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE promotes measurable T-cell activation under the tested in vitro conditions. Collectively, these findings support BiPE’s ability to activate T cells.

To enhance cytolytic activity, T cells were armed with αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE, thereby increasing their cytolytic potential and creating αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE-armed T cells (BATs). This approach can overcome several limitations of fusion protein therapy, including high production costs, dosage variability, and potential adverse effects such as cytokine release syndrome, while maintaining potent antitumor activity22–24. In our study, we demonstrated that αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE effectively enhanced the killing ability of BATs, with efficiency dependent on GD2 expression levels, indicating the specificity of BATs for GD2⁺ tumor cells (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. S3). This is consistent with the BiPE’s mechanism of action, which redirects T cells toward GD2⁺ tumor cells and preferentially stimulates CD8+ responses due to their direct cytolytic activity. Moreover, loading T cells with αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE enhances their efficacy against GD2⁺ targets. It offers a safer approach for T-cell-based immunotherapy, particularly in solid tumors that require targeted delivery and immune control. Prior studies using chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell–based immunotherapy have demonstrated that CAR functionality is regulated by both target antigen density and CAR density, with low expression of either limiting anti-tumor efficacy. Moreover, tumor antigen density is a crucial determinant of T-cell activation and cytotoxicity25. Notably, as the level of GD2 expression directly influences the efficacy of GD2-targeted immunotherapy, evaluation of antigen expression prior to treatment is warranted. In this study, A549 cells exhibited higher GD2 expression than NCI-H460 cells, as reflected by higher mean fluorescence intensity (2325 ± 50.61 vs. 574.3 ± 14.75, respectively). Consistent with these observations, the therapeutic efficacy of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE positively correlated with GD2 expression levels. These findings suggest that assessing tumor antigen density may be necessary to predict therapeutic benefit.

Immune checkpoint blockade targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis has become a standard treatment for lung cancer and has demonstrated significant clinical efficacy. However, similar to other solid tumors, the effectiveness of immunotherapy remains limited, mainly due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. In contrast, bispecific protein engagers (BiPEs) have shown promising activity in both hematologic malignancies and solid tumors. Previously, our research group developed a bispecific protein engager, αPD-L1 × αCD3 BiPE, which not only blocks the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction but also redirects T cells to tumor cells through CD3 engagement. Our previous studies revealed that this BiPE exhibits robust anti-tumor activity across a spectrum of malignancies23,24. In cholangiocarcinoma, αPD-L1 × αCD3 BiPE significantly enhanced T cell cytotoxicity and suppressed tumor growth in vitro26. In lung cancer models, the fusion protein promoted T cell activation and cytokine release, leading to potent tumor cell killing24. Furthermore, αPD-L1 × αCD3 BiPE induced effective T-cell-mediated lysis of malignant plasma cells in multiple myeloma, a hematologic malignancy typically resistant to immune checkpoint inhibitors23. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the BiPE platform’s role as an immunotherapeutic agent that bridges immune checkpoint blockades with T cell redirection, offering a promising strategy for treating both solid tumors and hematologic cancers. Furthermore, combining BiPEs that target multiple tumor-associated antigens—such as GD2 and PD-L1—may theoretically produce synergistic effects; however, further investigation is required to validate this approach.

In this study, we used a lentiviral system to transfer the genetic code encoding the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE into HEK293T cells. HEK293T is widely used in early-stage biotherapeutic development due to its exceptionally high protein expression, driven by SV40 large T antigen–mediated episomal replication. This property enables rapid screening, proof-of-concept validation, and optimization of recombinant proteins and viral vectors. Importantly, many recombinant proteins and most therapeutic viral vectors require human-like post-translational modifications that cannot be fully achieved in CHO cells, highlighting the continued relevance of HEK-based expression systems. Since 2015, seven HEK-derived products have received FDA approval, demonstrating the platform’s clinical and regulatory viability27. Moreover, processes established in HEK293T are often transferable to HEK293 or CHO cells for large-scale manufacturing, supporting efficient translation from discovery to commercialization.

Altogether, our findings suggest that arming T cells with αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE enhances their functional capacity against GD2⁺ lung cancer cells, highlighting its potential benefit in NSCLC, particularly in cases with GD2 overexpression. The specificity of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE for GD2 was demonstrated in this study and showed a positive correlation with its efficacy. However, the additional experiments, such as the use of epitope-specific GD2-blocking antibodies or GD2 knockdown approaches, could help further elucidate the epitope-level interactions that contribute to this specificity. Furthermore, further in vivo and clinical studies are required to validate the therapeutic efficacy of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE, as well as to explore the potential of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE.

Materials and methods

Cell cultures

NSCLC cell lines, specifically NCI-H460, A549, and the Burkitt lymphoma cell line Raji (ATCC, Manassas, VA), were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Sigma Aldrich, UK). The osteosarcoma cell lines SaOS-2 and 143B (ATCC, Manassas, VA) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) isolation

The PBMC isolation protocol received approval from the Human Ethics Committee at the University of Phayao, Phayao, Thailand (HREC-UP-HSST 1.2/107/66). The PBMC isolation procedure was conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from the whole blood of healthy volunteers utilizing gradient density centrifugation with Lymphocyte Separation Medium (LSM) (Corning, Inc., Corning, NY, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated PBMCs were cultivated in AIM-V medium (Gibco) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to facilitate monocyte adherence. The non-adherent cells were harvested for use as effector immune cells.

GD2 expression in cancer cells

Cancer cell lines, including NCI-H460, A549, SaOS-2, and 143B, were incubated with an anti-human GD2 PE antibody (mouse IgG2a, clone B289350; BioLegend, CA, USA) at 4 °C for 30 min. A PE-conjugated mouse IgG2a isotype control antibody (clone X5563; Immunotools, Friesoythe, Germany) was used to stain control cells. The cells were rinsed twice with 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% FBS, then resuspended in 1% formaldehyde in 1x PBS with 2% FBS. The percentages and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of GD2 expression were determined using a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA), and the data were processed with FlowJo™ v10 Software (FlowJo, Ashland, OR, USA).

Construct a lentiviral vector and production of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE

The αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE pCDH lentiviral vector was engineered by linking the cDNA sequences of the scFv derived from the humanized anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody (clone name: Hu3F8) and the anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody (clone name: OKT3) utilizing G4S linkers as spacers, with cMyc and 6xHis tags appended at the C-terminus. The gene encoding αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE was incorporated into the pCDH vector, in-frame with red fluorescent protein (RFP) for selection purposes. This design facilitated lentivirus particle generation, which was subsequently transduced into HEK293T cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA) to establish stable cell lines that secreted the recombinant αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE in culture supernatant. The RFP was detected using fluorescence microscopy, whereas the 6xHis-tagged protein was detected using Western blotting with an anti-His antibody to confirm that stable HEK293T cells expressed the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE. The stable HEK293T cells were maintained in DMEM media supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO₂.

Purification of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE protein

The production of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE was conducted in Opti-MEM (Gibco), a serum-reduced medium, utilizing stable cell lines that secrete a recombinant αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE, thereby minimizing background interference from serum. The culture supernatant containing the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE was harvested and purified using a His GraviTrap column (Cytiva, MA, USA). Immunoblotting was employed to analyze the purified protein utilizing an anti-His antibody. The His-tagged protein ELISA kit (GenScript, NJ, USA) was used to quantify the protein concentration. The purified protein was preserved at -20 °C until required.

Cytotoxicity of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE

The non-adherent cells, predominantly a lymphocyte-enriched population, were exposed to 100 pg αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE or 5 µg/mL PHA-L for 24 and 48 h without exogenous cytokines. Apoptotic cells were subsequently assessed by Annexin V APC/PI staining (ImmunoTools, Friesoythe, Germany) using a CytoFLEX S Flow Cytometer, and the proportion of apoptotic immune cells was evaluated with FlowJo™ v10 software (FlowJo, Ashland, OR, USA).

Specific binding of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE protein to target cells

The binding selectivity of the αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE to the target proteins, GD2 and CD3, was assessed using flow cytometry. Purified αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE (2 ng) was incubated with human lymphocytes from a healthy donor (exhibiting high CD3 expression) or with A549 and 143B cells, representing lung cancer and osteosarcoma cell lines with GD2 expression, respectively. Raji cells, which lack CD3 expression, and NCI-H460 and SaOS-2 cells, which are GD2-negative lung cancer and osteosarcoma cell lines, respectively, were included as negative control cell lines. The cells were rinsed twice with 1x PBS supplemented with 2% FBS. Anti-Fab Alexa 647 (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA) and anti-Myc-FITC (Clone ab1394, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) antibodies were administered to the cells and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. Following washes with 1x PBS, the labeled cells were examined using a flow cytometer. The proportion of cells positive for αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE binding, relative to the no-BiPE (− BiPE) condition, was analyzed using FlowJo™ v10 software. In addition, monoclonal antibodies were used to assess target antigen expressions, including an APC-conjugated anti-human CD3 antibody (mouse IgG1, clone HIT3b; Immunotools) to detect CD3 expression on lymphocytes and Raji cells, and a PE-conjugated anti-human GD2 antibody (mouse IgG2a, clone B289350; BioLegend) to evaluate GD2 expression on 143B and SaOS-2 cells. APC-conjugated mouse IgG1 isotype control antibody (clone 1F8; Immunotools) and PE-conjugated mouse IgG2a isotype control antibody (clone X5563; Immunotools) were used to stain control cells.

Immunophenotypes and activation marker staining

Non-adherent immune cells were exposed to 100 pg αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE or 5 µg/mL PHA-L for 5 days. Subsequently, the cells were collected and washed with 1xPBS supplemented with 2% FBS. The samples were subsequently stained fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies, including anti-CD3 FITC (mouse IgG1, clone UCHT-1; Immunotools), anti-CD4 APC (mouse IgG2b, clone OKT-4; Immunotools), anti-CD8 APC (mouse IgG2a, clone UCHT-4; Immunotools), anti-CD25 PerCP-Cy5.5 (mouse IgG1, clone BC96; eBioscience), anti-CD56 PE (mouse IgG1, clone HCD56; BioLegend), and anti-CD69 APC (mouse IgG1, clone FN50; Immunotools). FITC-, PE-, and APC-conjugated mouse IgG1 isotype control antibodies (clone 1F8; Immunotools), APC-conjugated mouse IgG2a isotype control antibody (clone PPV-04; Immunotools), APC-conjugated mouse IgG2b isotype control antibody (clone PLRV219; Immunotools), and PerCP-Cy5.5–conjugated mouse IgG1 isotype control antibody (clone P3.6.2.8.1; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) were used as isotype-matched controls. The stained cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde in 1x PBS supplemented with 2% FBS. The immunophenotypes and activation markers were assessed using a CytoFLEX S Flow Cytometer and FlowJoTM v10 software. The full gating strategy, including: (1) lymphocyte identification by FSC/SSC, (2) exclusion of doublets, (3) total CD3 + T-cell gating, subsequent separation into CD4 + and CD8 + populations, or total CD3 − T-cell gating, subsequent separation into CD56 + population.

Immune cell proliferation

Human immune cells (1 × 106 cells/mL) were labeled with 5 µM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and placed in a 96-well plate. The labeled cells were subjected to 100 pg of αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE or 5 µg/mL PHA-L for 3 days, without exogenous cytokines. Cell proliferation was subsequently assessed with a CytoFLEX S Flow Cytometer, and the proportion of proliferating immune cells was evaluated with FlowJo™ v10 software.

Cytolytic activity assay

The lung cancer cell lines NCI-H460 and A549 were labeled with CellTracker green-fluorescent chloromethyl derivatives of fluorescein diacetate (CMFDA) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) before the experiment, enabling their tracking and analysis using the MuviCyte Live-cell Imaging system (PerkinElmer, Massachusetts, USA). Labeled cancer cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were cultured in a 96-well plate for one day before the experiment. The non-adherent immune cell fraction (predominantly T lymphocytes) was armed with an αGD2 × αCD3 BiPE (100 pg per 100,000 cells) to produce bispecific engager-armed T cells (BATs). T cells or BATs were subsequently co-cultured with effector immune cells at effector (E) to target (T) ratios of 0:1 (control), 1:1, 1:2.5, and 1:5for a duration of 48 h. The culture medium containing effector immune cells and dead target cells was aspirated. The residual target cells in the wells were stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution at ambient temperature for 45 min. The cells were subsequently fixed with cold methanol for a further 45 min. The staining results were documented using a camera and subsequently solubilized with 33% acetic acid. Their absorbance was quantified at 570 nm with a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times independently to ensure the production of reliable datasets. The mean and standard error of the mean (SEM) were computed for each dataset. The experimental data were analyzed utilizing GraphPad version 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test and Student’s t-test were utilized to assess differences among the data. Statistical significance was established at a p-value of less than 0.05.
