Gas station heroin- tianeptine and its impact: a systematic review and exploratory analysis
Authors: Shanli Parnia, Lakshit Jain, Mohsan Ali, Zouina Sarfraz, Muhammad Jamal Nasir, Darshini Shah, Aiman FNU, Noor Atiq, Preet Roshan Shaikh, Rizwan Ahmed, Michael Hower, Surya Karlapati, Muhammad Talha Moazzam, Benjamin Bazin, Saeed Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTianeptine, a drug approved in some countries for the treatment of major depressive disorder, has raised significant public health concerns due to its potential for misuse and addiction. Its activity at the mu-opioid receptor along with its availability as a dietary supplement in certain regions, raises concerns about overdose, toxicity, and its contribution to the opioid crisis. Despite recognition of its addictive potential for over two decades, systematic data on misuse patterns, associated risk factors, and effective treatment strategies remain limited. This study investigates demographic/regional trends in tianeptine misuse, examines the influence of dosage, administration routes, and co-administration with other substances on overdose risk, and evaluates the efficacy of naloxone and other treatments in managing tianeptine toxicity and withdrawal symptoms.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, our systematic review (PROSPERO ID: CRD42024521671) searched PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, and ClinicalTrials.gov. The quality of included studies was assessed using the JBI checklist, and exploratory analysis was conducted in R (v.4.3.3). From 1,562 identified records, 25 studies met inclusion criteria.ResultsTianeptine misuse demonstrated notable regional variations, with North America accounting for 40/52 (76.92%) of cases, followed by Asia 8/52 (15.38%) and Europe 4/52 (7.69%) (X²=44.9231, p < 0.0001). The majority of users were male 29/52 (55.77%) (X²=0.6923, p = 0.4054), and oral ingestion was the predominant route 37/52 (71.15%), while intravenous use accounted for 5/52 (9.62%) (X²=88.3846, p < 0.0001). Habitual misuse was documented in 30/52 (57.69%) of cases (X²=1.2308, p = 0.2673). Overdose occurred in 18/52 (34.62%) of cases (X²=4.9231, p = 0.0265), frequently associated with high doses up to 20 g daily and co-administration with alcohol 6/52 (11.54%) or benzodiazepines 5/52 (9.62%) (X²=68.5932, p < 0.0001). Naloxone reversed symptoms in 9/52 (17.31%) of treated cases (X²=22.2308, p < 0.0001). Withdrawal symptoms were observed in 22/52 (42.31%) of cases (X²=21.3846, p < 0.0001), with management strategies including buprenorphine 4/52 (7.69%) and supportive care 4/52 (7.69%) (X²=44.0000, p < 0.0001).ConclusionTianeptine misuse, characterized by escalating doses, habitual use, and co-administration with other substances, poses significant risks. Its easy availability and lack of regulation further exacerbate its public health impact. Targeted prevention, regulation, and treatment interventions are urgently needed to address these challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24666-0.Keywords: Tianeptine, Opioid misuse, Naloxone, Withdrawal symptoms, Addiction, Legislative action, Systematic review

Introduction

Discovered in the 1960 s, Tianeptine has been prescribed as a novel tricyclic antidepressant for the treatment of patients with major depressive disorder and anxiety in over 25 countries globally [1, 2]. Tianeptine was found to have a unique clinical response, which defied its label as a tricyclic antidepressant given its three-ring chemical structure [3]. However, regulatory authorities soon observed that Tianeptine had a potential for abuse, with a 2005 addiction safety survey conducted in France estimating an incidence of abuse and dependence of 1 to 3 per 1,000 patients treated [4]. These findings led to a warning being added about its potential for abuse in 2007 [4]. Further studies were requested, and in 2011 restrictions on prescribing tianeptine were placed in France after the discovery of a subset of patients (average 40.3 years old) taking high doses of tianeptine (up to 540 mg/d) after obtaining it through doctor and pharmacy shopping (3 doctors and 3 pharmacies in 1 month) [4]. A study from 2014 reported tianeptine’s addictive potential by identifying it as a full agonist of µ-opioid receptors and a partial agonist of δ-opioid receptors [5]. These findings further explained how tianeptine abuse can provide an opioid-like ‘euphoria’ and can lead to addiction and overdose [6]. Since the 2010 s, Tianeptine has become more popular as an opioid-like substance of abuse and has made its way across to the United States (US). Studies have shown that tianeptine also affects neuroplasticity and modulates neurotransmitter concentrations, including glutamate, dopamine, norepinephrine and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which are associated with learning and memory, the reward system, stress and depression [7, 8].

In the United States, tianeptine is easily accessible online and in locations like smoke shops, convenience stores, and gas stations. It is sometimes nicknamed “gas station heroin” due to its misuse and addictive potential and is often sold under brand names like ZaZa, Tia, and Tianaa. Despite the filing of a patent for Tianeptine in the US in 2003 [1], the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not approved tianeptine for medical use. While the US government is making efforts to regulate drugs which may have opioid-like effects to mitigate the public health burden and prevent deaths from opioid overdose, restricting access to Tianeptine remains a challenge [9, 10]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) first identified the increase in Tianeptine exposure as an emerging public health concern during 2014–2017, and the number of Tianeptine exposure cases reported to US poison control rose from 11 cases between 2000 and 2013 to 151 cases in 2020 [11, 12]. Tianeptine appeared to be popular among younger men, with the National Poison Data System reporting that in the period 2000–2017, 82% of Tianeptine calls involved men, and 57% of calls involved people aged 21–40 [13]. With the first Tianeptine associated fatalities noted in 2018 in Texas, 12 states have so far taken steps to classify tianeptine as a controlled substance due to health warnings about its potential for abuse and addiction [14, 15]. In early 2024, bipartisan legislation was introduced in Congress to classify tianeptine as a Schedule III controlled substance. However, as of now, the bill remains under review in committees. Meanwhile, the DEA continues to seize bulk shipments of tianeptine powder [16]. Unfortunately, tianeptine is still accessible in other states and through online platforms, where it is often promoted as a dietary supplement or a cognitive enhancer [11, 12]. Due to its exclusion from common urine drug tests, Tianeptine frequently goes undetected in conventional drug screenings. In fact, during assessments, healthcare providers typically do not screen for this drug, partly due to its lesser-known status compared to more commonly abused opioids. Many individuals also do not report using it, as they remain unaware of its addictive nature and engage in self-medicating for symptoms of opioid addiction, as well alleviating symptoms of anxiety and depression.

Given the fragmented nature of legalization and regulatory actions surrounding tianeptine, we hypothesize significant demographic and regional variations in its misuse patterns. Additionally, we suggest that the dosage and route of tianeptine administration are critical factors influencing the risk of overdose. Considering tianeptine’s activity at the δ-opioid receptor, we propose that naloxone may serve as an effective treatment for overdose, while withdrawal symptoms may be managed using opioid-agonist therapies and supportive treatments targeting the δ-opioid receptor (Fig. 1).

Hypotheses addressing tianeptine abuse and associated risks

In this study, our primary objectives are:

To investigate demographic and regional variations in tianeptine misuse patterns. To evaluate clinical outcomes related to tianeptine misuse, including overdose and withdrawal, with particular attention to dosage and route of administration. To assess treatment strategies for tianeptine intoxication, misuse, and withdrawal, focusing on naloxone’s efficacy and alternative therapeutic approaches.

Methods

Protocol registration and disclosures

The protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (ID: CRD42024521671). While an ethical approval was sought as it was not deemed necessary due to the lack of involvement of human subjects. This study did not receive any funding from a research grant or outside source.

Search strategy and study selection

In alignment with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines, we conducted a systematic review of the literature. We utilized five databases (PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library). The search strategy incorporated a combination of keywords, MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms, and Emtree terms. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied. Ancillary searches were conducted in Google Scholar and ClinicalTrials.gov to identify additional studies and ensure that relevant research was included. An umbrella review approach was employed by hand-searching the reference lists of initially included studies to obtain additional sources. Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

The data extraction process was structured to align precisely with the study objectives, focusing on variables relevant to understanding the addictive potential, withdrawal symptoms, overdose occurrences, and treatment outcomes of tianeptine misuse. Each variable was selected based on predefined criteria and informed by standard guidelines wherever applicable.

Variables extracted

The following variables were extracted for each case, with definitions and thresholds clarified as follows: (1) Author and year; (2) region (Europe, North America, Asia, other); (3) demographics (age and gender); (4) usage regularity - habitual misuse, defined following DSM-5 criteria for substance use disorders. Habitual misuse indicated frequent, repeated use of tianeptine associated with dependency, tolerance, or inability to cease usage despite adverse consequences. DSM-5 symptoms of dependency, such as physical cravings, use in larger amounts over time, or unsuccessful attempts to reduce intake, were applied to cases reporting these patterns. Non-habitual or sporadic usage was noted where cases indicated less consistent patterns without signs of dependency; (5) route of administration, categorized based on specific routes documented, including oral (i.e., use by ingestion), intravenous (IV), and intra-arterial (i.e., injectable routes associated with increased risk of rapid-onset effects and overdose); inhaled (i.e., non-ingestible routes such as smoking); or not specified (i.e., cases where the route was not documented); (6) tianeptine dosage (i.e., in milligrams (mg) and classified based on typical therapeutic and misuse ranges): therapeutic doses were defined as 25–50 mg per day based on commonly prescribed regimens for depression and anxiety. Misuse doses—any dosage significantly exceeding 50 mg daily, with cases commonly reporting up to 20 g daily—were classified as misuse. Dosage escalation patterns, if reported, were noted to identify the risk of dependency; (7) overdose criteria: overdose cases were identified according to toxicology standards, where intake levels caused severe adverse reactions requiring emergency intervention or resulting in significant toxicity symptoms. Cases reported as “overdose” by medical professionals or exhibiting symptoms of life-threatening toxicity were included; (8) co-administration with other substances (i.e., cases involving concurrent use of tianeptine with other substances were noted, including alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids, stimulants, and other psychoactive or non-psychoactive substances); (9) history of other substance misuse (i.e., a past history of other substance misuse was noted if documented in the case, according to DSM-5 criteria for substance use disorders); (10) naloxone usage (i.e., documented when naloxone was used to reverse a tianeptine overdose, to explore naloxone’s efficacy in addressing opioid-like overdose symptoms from tianeptine misuse); 11) withdrawal (i.e., withdrawal symptoms were defined using DSM-5 guidelines, which specify physical and psychological symptoms such as cravings, agitation, nausea, and muscle pain following cessation or reduction after regular misuse) and withdrawal treatment (i.e., treatments for withdrawal symptoms, such as benzodiazepines, opioid-related medications, or supportive care, were documented to identify effective management strategies); and 12) outcome (i.e., categorized as recovered—patients stabilized post-treatment without lasting effects, fatal—cases that resulted in patient death, and not reported—where the outcome was unspecified in the report).

Data extraction process and quality assessment

The data extraction process was carefully designed to minimize bias and increase consistency. Each study was reviewed by two independent reviewers who extracted data separately. The extracted data were then compared, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or, if needed, adjudicated by a third reviewer. To quantify the level of agreement between the two reviewers, a Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated. This inter-rater reliability metric provided an objective measure of consistency, with a high kappa value indicating strong agreement.

The extraction protocol included the following: (1) predefined data fields: each reviewer followed a standardized form to ensure consistency in capturing each variable. (2) Independent extraction: reviewers worked independently to avoid cross-contamination of interpretations. (3) Discrepancy resolution: any differences were resolved through consensus or, when necessary, by consulting a third reviewer.

Risk of bias assessment

Bias assessment utilized the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for case reports, a tool specifically designed for case study reviews. The JBI checklist addresses multiple dimensions of potential bias, including patient demographic clarity, ensuring that basic characteristics such as age and gender are clearly reported; clinical condition, with an adequate description of the patient’s state at the time of the report; diagnostic clarity, through an assessment of diagnostic accuracy and methods; treatment and intervention details, providing specificity to ensure reproducibility; post-intervention outcome, emphasizing transparency in reporting patient outcomes and any follow-up; adverse events reporting, documenting any unanticipated effects during the course of treatment; and clinical takeaways, evaluating whether the case report provides actionable insights.

Each study was rated on the JBI checklist’s eight dimensions, and findings were tabulated. This allowed systematic comparison of study quality across included case reports, offering an overview of methodological rigor and potential biases in the dataset.

Statistical analysis

While our study followed a systematic review approach, we did not conduct a formal meta-analysis due to the heterogeneity of data types, which included case reports and case series with varying outcomes and reporting methods. These study designs typically do not lend themselves to meta-analytic synthesis, given the lack of standardized effect sizes and control groups.

The statistical analysis for this study was conducted using R (V. 4.3.3.), using packages such as `randomForest`, `e1071`, and `ggplot2` for data visualization. In addition to the primary systematic review objectives, an a priori exploratory analysis was conducted to examine associations within the data related to the addictive potential and adverse outcomes of tianeptine misuse. Specifically, this analysis assessed relationships among variables such as regional differences in usage patterns, varying dosages, and the influence of co-administration with other substances on withdrawal severity and overdose risk.

Data were collected on variables including region, patterns of use, route, presence of withdrawal or overdose, co-administration, history of another substance misuse, naloxone usage, and withdrawal treatment. The data comprised both categorical and ordinal variables, ensuring a comprehensive representation of the factors under study.

The preprocessing phase involved handling missing data through exclusion when necessary, given the variability in reporting across case studies. Descriptive analysis was conducted to summarize frequencies, percentages, and distributions for categorical variables.

Inferential analysis focused on examining associations between key categorical variables. The Chi-Square Test for Independence was applied to test relationships between gender and region, history of substance misuse and naloxone administration, region and usage regularity, route of administration and overdose occurrence, and co-administration patterns. These tests identified significant associations in several areas, including overdose risk and withdrawal treatment.

The history of substance misuse other than Tianeptine was present in 17/52 (32.69%) of cases, with only 1/52 (1.92%) reporting no prior substance misuse and a large majority (34/52, 65.38%) not reporting their history. A substantial majority of cases (30/52, 57.69%) were habitual misuse, while 22/52 (42.31%) of the cases did not report habitual use. The chi-square test also showed a statistically significant association between region and usage regularity (X²=31.4231, df = 2, p < 0.0001), suggesting that the patterns of habitual or sporadic use differ notably across regions.

Oral use was the most common route (37/52, 71.15%), with intravenous (5/52, 9.62%), intraarterial (1/52, 1.92%), and smoking (1/52, 1.92%) being less common. Notably, 8/52 (15.38%) of the cases did not specify the route. The reported Tianeptine doses ranged widely, reflecting both therapeutic use and substantial misuse.The frequency of use also varied from once a day to several times a day. At the lower end, patients reported taking 90 mg daily. The higher endwas up to 8–20 g daily for severe misuse. Some patients engaged in gradually increasing their dose over several years (one patient increasing daily dose from 625 mg to 700 mg over 2 years), other patients abruptly increased their dosages (One patient’s use escalated from 12.5 mg three times a day to 400 mg per dose, resulting in the consumption of a 5 g jar within 20–30 days).The dosages that lead to overdose varied among patients, one particular patient overdosed after taking a 50 mg dose. Post-mortem analyses showed concentrations of 2.0 mg/L to 15.50 mg/L in blood and gastric content at 956 mg/mL.

A significant association was found between the route of administration and the occurrence of overdose cases (X²=88.3846, df = 4, p < 0.0001), indicating that the risk of overdose differs based on how the substance was taken. The majority of overdose cases involved oral administration (71.15%), followed by intravenous injection (9.62%), intraarterial injection (1.92%), and smoking (1.92%). In 15.38% of cases, the route of administration was not reported. Co-administration with other substances was varied, with comorbid alcohol use at 6/52 (11.54%), benzodiazepines at 5/52 (9.62%), antidepressants at 4/52 (7.69%), stimulants at 3/52 (5.77%), antipsychotics at 1/52 (1.92%), and a notable 11/52 (21.15%) involving other substances. A small fraction reported no co-administration (2/52, 3.85%), and a significant portion of cases (27/52, 51.92%) did not report co-administration. The chi-square test for co-administration showed a significant association (X²=68.5932, df = 7, p < 0.0001), indicating that tianeptine co-administration patterns vary significantly.

Our analysis of Tianeptine exposures indicates that 18/52 (34.62%) of reported cases involved overdoses. Naloxone was administered in 9/52 (17.31%) of cases, suggesting that opioid receptor antagonism played a role in managing intoxication. A chi-square analysis demonstrated a strong statistical association between naloxone administration and withdrawal symptoms (X² = 21.3846, df = 2, p < 0.0001), indicating that individuals who received naloxone were significantly more likely to experience withdrawal.

Withdrawal symptoms were documented in 22/52 (42.31%) of cases, while2/52 (3.85%) denied withdrawal and 28/52 (53.85%) had no withdrawal status reported. Naloxone was administered in 9/52 (17.31%) of cases, with chi-square analysis showing a strong association between naloxone use and withdrawal symptoms (X² = 21.3846, df = 2, p <0.0001). This suggests that some withdrawal may have been precipitated by naloxone, although the clinical features closely resembled classical opioid withdrawal. It is therefore likely that both spontaneous and naloxone-precipitated withdrawal were represented, but the available data do not allow these to be distinguished with certainty. Overall, the similarity of reported symptoms to classical opioid withdrawal supports the conclusion that tianeptine dependence engages opioid pathways.

Treatment for withdrawal varied, including benzodiazepines (6/52, 11.54%), buprenorphine (4/52, 7.69%), antipsychotics and antidepressants (3/52, 5.77%), and supportive care (4/52, 7.69%), with a fraction using non-benzodiazepine sedatives and other medications (1/52, 1.92%). However, 22/52 (42.31%) did not report receiving any treatment. The chi-square test for withdrawal treatment yielded a highly significant result (X²=44, df = 6, p < 0.0001), indicating that withdrawal management strategies differ notably among cases.

The outcome of Tianeptine exposure showed 43/52 (82.69%) of cases recovering, 7/52 (13.46%) resulting in fatalities, and 2/52 (3.85%) not specified. A significant association was observed between withdrawal treatment and patient outcome (X²=57.7308, df = 2, p < 0.0001), suggesting that treatment strategies may impact recovery rates. Discussion

Tianeptine occupies a unique and controversial space in psychopharmacology. Unlike traditional opioids, it has a long history of medical use as an antidepressant in Europe, Asia, and South America under professional supervision [6]. Its mu-opioid receptor activity raises concerns about its abuse potential, especially when consumed in doses exceeding therapeutic recommendations [22]. In the U.S., tianeptine is not approved for medical use but is marketed as a dietary supplement with unverified claims about its benefits, ranging from treating anxiety and depression to chronic pain and opioid use disorder, some even promote it as a cognitive enhancer [12, 41]. These claims are not supported by strong scientific evidence, and authorities have repeatedly issued warnings to the public about risks related to tianeptine [11, 12]. Our study underscores the urgent need for awareness and regulation, presenting data on 52 cases of Tianeptine exposure. While not all cases resulted in bad outcomes, 7 fatalities were reported, all involving polysubstance use [20, 22, 26, 34, 39]. These findings highlight the potential dangers of unregulated tianeptine use, particularly when combined with other substances.

The rise of tianeptine misuse in the U.S. is yet another layer in the ongoing opioid crisis, highlighting the struggles in managing substance use disorders. One major issue is the lack of access to medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD). The shortage of programs providing MOUD only makes the situation worse, leaving many without proper treatment [42]. In 2021, an estimated 2.5 million adults struggled with opioid use disorder, yet only 22% received MOUD, and just 35.6% received any form of substance use treatment [43]. With such a huge treatment gap, it’s not surprising that some individuals turn to unregulated alternatives like Tianeptine, which is easily accessible despite its risks. Despite being banned in 12 states, including Alabama, Florida, and Ohio, and explicit FDA warnings against its use, tianeptine remains widely available in much of the country [12, 44]. Its accessibility, along with misleading claims about its benefits, makes it a concerning option for those seeking relief outside the healthcare system [45].

Adding to these challenges is the issue of Tianeptine overdose, which accounted for 34.62% of the cases in our study. Tianeptine is rarely used in isolation; co-administration with alcohol, benzodiazepines, and antidepressants is common [46, 47]. Our exploratory analysis showed that alcohol (11.54%), benzodiazepines (9.62%), and antidepressants (7.69%) were the most frequently reported co-administered substances. Additionally, 32.69% of patients had a history of substance misuse beyond tianeptine. Several studies have reported the concomitant use of benzodiazepines (alprazolam), antidepressants (amitriptyline, sertraline), and alcohol [48, 49]. These combinations significantly increase the risk of overdose, particularly due to the potentiation of central nervous system (CNS) depression. Notably, when tianeptine is combined with sedatives or other CNS depressants, the risk of mortality rises due to respiratory depression [21, 22, 39]. Our findings reinforce that overdose risk with tianeptine is magnified when combined with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or antidepressants, underscoring the importance of screening for polysubstance use.

Polysubstance overdoses involving tianeptine present unique treatment challenges. Naloxone, a well-established opioid receptor antagonist, was administered in 17.31% of cases and was significantly associated with withdrawal symptoms, likely reflecting both precipitated withdrawal and underlying dependence. Its effectiveness may be limited in mixed-drug overdoses, particularly with benzodiazepines or alcohol, which depress respiration through non-opioid pathways. These findings highlight the importance of a harm-reduction approach, including wider naloxone availability, comprehensive screening for polysubstance use, and tailored management strategies that account for co-administration effects.

Because tianeptine withdrawal resembles classical opioid withdrawal, opioid agonist therapy—commonly used in opioid use disorder—has shown promise in its management. In addressing the issue of tianeptine withdrawal, we found case reports that demonstrated that opioid agonists like methadone and buprenorphine can be useful in alleviating tianeptine withdrawal symptoms [32, 35, 37, 50]. Opioid agonist therapy, such as methadone or buprenorphine, has shown promise incase reports for managing tianeptine withdrawal. By contrast, naloxone can reverse opioid receptor–mediated effects but does not address tianeptine’s additional actions on glutamatergic and serotonergic pathways. This suggests that while opioid activity is central to tianeptine dependence, non-opioid mechanisms also contribute to its effects and withdrawal profile.

The predominance of young adult males suggests that targeted prevention and awareness campaigns may need to focus on this demographic group. The risks of tianeptine exposure extend beyond adult users, raising concerns about its impact on neonatal health when used during pregnancy. The link between tianeptine’s opioid-like effects and treatment options becomes even more apparent when considering tianeptine misuse during pregnancy. There are cases where opioid-like neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS) has been associated with tianeptine use by the mother [24, 28]. In these cases, newborns showed NAS symptoms and had to be treated with morphine, highlighting the relevance of opioid agonist treatments for symptoms arising from tianeptine exposure.

While its effectiveness in tianeptine overdose cases remains an area for further investigation, when taken at therapeutic doses (25–50 mg/day), tianeptine primarily exerts its effects through glutamatergic and serotonergic modulation [7, 51, 52]. However, at higher doses (75–3000 mg/day), its mu-opioid receptor (MOR) agonist activity becomes more pronounced, leading to opioid-like effects, dependence, and withdrawal [6, 53].

Our study identified two general patterns of Tianeptine use. Some individuals reported consistently high doses, ranging from 90 mg/day to as much as 20 g/day [3, 35, 36, 38]. Others appeared to use tianeptine initially at lower doses, often for self-medication of anxiety or depression, but later escalated their intake, potentially due to tolerance and dependence [27].

Although there are limited studies about the safety of Tianeptine in large doses, data obtained from state surveillance data show potential adverse effects of this substance. For example, Tennessee reported 50 cases of emergency department visits relating to Tianeptine from January 2021 to December 2023 – six of these cases involved fatal overdoses and toxicology reports indicated polysubstance use [54]. Individuals abusing Tianeptine often consume doses significantly higher than the therapeutic range. For instance, some users have been reported to take doses exceeding 4,000 mg per day, which is over 100 times the typical therapeutic dose of 25 to 50 mg per day.

Apart from treating individual patients, primary and secondary prevention strategies should be implemented. In light of our findings, several specific policy actions should be considered to mitigate tianeptine misuse and its associated harms. First, nationwide public awareness campaigns should be launched to educate consumers about the risks of tianeptine and counter the misleading health claims used in its marketing. Second, efforts should focus on improving access to evidence-based mental health and substance use treatment, particularly for individuals who may be self-medicating with tianeptine due to inadequate care. Third, stronger regulatory controls at the federal level are urgently needed to restrict its sale as an unregulated supplement and reduce accessibility. These strategies, in combination with the prevention measures outlined below, could play a critical role in reducing the public health burden posed by this emerging substance. These may include public information campaigns to dispute the various claims advertised by the sellers of tianeptine, and education about the negative consequences of taking high doses of tianeptine or taking tianeptine in combination with alcohol, prescription medications (e.g., opioids, benzodiazepines, etc.) and/or other illicit substances. Moreover, regulatory measures and effective enforcement mechanisms are needed to curb the accessibility of this compound. Key public health recommendations are as follows:

Primary prevention

Education and Awareness: Raising awareness about the risks associated with Tianeptine misuse and educating the public, especially those at higher risk of substance misuse, about safer use practices. This could involve information about how tianeptine interacts with MOR and acting similar to an opioid with a potential for physical dependence and opioid-like withdrawal symptoms if stopped abruptly. There is also a risk of opioid overdose if combined with other substances. People should also check supplement labels carefully and ensure tianeptine doses do not exceed the typical therapeutic range of 25–50 mg per day [8].

Secondary prevention

4.Early Detection and Brief Intervention: Identifying tianeptine misuse early can improve outcomes, particularly for adolescents, as early substance use is linked to continued misuse into adulthood [58, 59]. Standard urine drug screens do not detect tianeptine; therefore, clinicians should be trained to use expanded toxicology panels when appropriate. Although these tests can be costly, under diagnosis may discourage appropriate treatment. Educating insurers and policymakers on their clinical value could improve access and reduce long-term healthcare burdens.

Access to treatment and support services

Many individuals turn to tianeptine to self-medicate for mental health concerns [30]. Expanding access to mental health and substance use treatment, reducing stigma around seeking help, and ensuring culturally competent care can provide individuals with healthier alternatives.

Frameworks like the American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM) criteria help guide clinicians in making these decisions. Research shows that implementing ASAM criteria in residential treatment settings improves patient retention and outcomes [60, 61].

Limitations

There are certain limitations in our review that must be stated. The reliance on case reports and series, while invaluable for gaining in-depth insights into individual experiences and outcomes, may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of findings. These study designs inherently focus on more severe or unusual cases, potentially skewing the perception of tianeptine’s overall impact and its treatment outcomes. Despite a comprehensive search strategy designed to capture a wide array of relevant literature, the potential for missing studies remains. The evolving nature of substance misuse research, coupled with the limitations of database indexing, studies limited to published only in English language which do not reflect wider populations and the publication lag, could mean that recent studies or unpublished data were not included. The assessment of the risk of bias in the included studies revealed a generally high methodological quality, yet variations in reporting and methodological rigor among studies could influence the reliability of the extracted data. The absence of a consistent framework for reporting tianeptine misuse cases, treatment interventions, and outcomes may lead to heterogeneity in the data, complicating the synthesis and interpretation of findings. The JBI checklist, while comprehensive, has limitations for assessing tianeptine misuse case reports, as it may not fully account for nuances like dosage specifics or varied definitions of misuse, impacting the depth of our conclusions.

One drawback of reliance on these case studies and series, however, is that may lead to an increased emphasis on severe cases, possibly skewing the results toward more dramatic misuse cases reported in literature leading to potential publication bias. This is further exemplified by lack of comparative substance, e.g., other opioids, or controls in these studies.

A limitation of our study is the absence of a formal meta-analysis. Due to the heterogeneity in data types—primarily case reports and case series with variable outcomes and reporting methods—meta-analytic synthesis was not feasible, as these designs generally lack standardized effect sizes and control groups. Instead, we conducted an a priori exploratory analysis to identify meaningful patterns and associations within the data. While this approach cannot provide the quantitative synthesis of a meta-analysis, it allowed us to obtain valuable insights into the addictive potential and adverse outcomes of tianeptine misuse, which may inform future research and clinical understanding.

By including only case studies and case series, this study may be subject to selection bias, as these types of reports often emphasize more severe or unusual cases of tianeptine misuse. This focus on extreme outcomes could skew the results, potentially exaggerating the perceived severity and health impacts of misuse. Consequently, the generalizability of our findings is limited, as they may not fully represent the experiences of all tianeptine users, including those with milder or intermittent patterns of misuse. A more diverse set of study designs would likely yield a broader understanding of tianeptine misuse across different levels of severity.

The statistical analyses, while providing valuable insights into tianeptine misuse patterns, routes of administration, and the efficacy of interventions like naloxone, are based on aggregated data from case reports. The nature of such data limits the ability to establish causal relationships and may not fully capture the complex interactions between various factors influencing misuse, withdrawal symptoms, and treatment responses.

These limitations notwithstanding, the current study contributes significantly to the existing body of knowledge on tianeptine misuse and its management. It highlights areas for future research, emphasizing the need for larger, more diverse studies that can overcome these limitations and provide a more comprehensive understanding of this emerging public health concern.

Conclusion

The promotion of tianeptine as a nootropic, supplement, or mental health aid and its subsequent misuse poses a significant public health challenges. Our review shows that tianeptine is primarily misused by adult males, most often taken orally and habitually. Many users escalate their intake, combine it with other substances, and experience overdose events, though the majority recover. Because of its opioid-like mechanism of action, habitual use can lead to withdrawal symptoms, and naloxone may be beneficial in treating overdose in select cases. Tianeptine’s easy accessibility, lack of routine detection in toxicology screens, and rapid tolerance development make it a particularly high-risk substance. Despite repeated FDA warnings and mounting evidence of harm, regulatory oversight remains inconsistent across jurisdictions, and clinical awareness of tianeptine misuse is still limited. Our findings highlight the urgent need for coordinated regulatory action to limit access to tianeptine and recognize it as a substance with significant abuse potential. Clinicians should be trained to identify signs of misuse and withdrawal, particularly in patients presenting with atypical symptoms or polysubstance use. Public awareness campaigns, improved access to mental health and addiction treatment, and expanded naloxone distribution are also critical to addressing this emerging public health issue. Future research should further examine tianeptine’s pharmacologic interactions, especially in the context of other opioid receptor agonists .
