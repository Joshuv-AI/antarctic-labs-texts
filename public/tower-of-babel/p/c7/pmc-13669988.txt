Effects of Ferrostatin‐1 on Vocal Folds in Aging Rats
Authors: Yong‐Il Cheon, Soo‐Young Bang, Ji Min Kim, Ha‐Nee Kwon, Minhyung Lee, Eui‐Suk Sung, Jin‐Choon Lee, Byung‐Joo Lee, Sung‐Chan Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAge‐related vocal fold degeneration is characterized by alterations in the lamina propria, extracellular matrix remodeling, and reduced tissue viscoelasticity, contributing to presbyphonia. Ferroptosis, an iron‐dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is increasingly implicated in tissue aging. This study investigated the association between ferroptosis and vocal fold aging using ferrostatin‐1 (Fer‐1) in a rat model.MethodsThirty‐six male Sprague–Dawley rats were assigned to three groups: Young, Old, and Old–Fer. Fer‐1 (2.5 μmol/kg, 20 μL) was injected unilaterally into the left vocal fold at Weeks 0 and 2. At Week 3, vocal folds were harvested for histological, immunohistochemical, and biochemical analyses. Outcome measures included ferroptosis‐related markers, lamina propria thickness, extracellular matrix morphology, collagen and hyaluronic acid distribution, and inflammatory cytokines.ResultsCompared with the Young group, the Old group showed increased lipid peroxidation, reactive oxygen species, and iron accumulation with reduced glutathione peroxidase 4 activity; these changes were attenuated in the Old–Fer group. The Old group also exhibited increased lamina propria thickness, enhanced collagen deposition, and reduced hyaluronic acid distribution, whereas the Old–Fer group demonstrated reduced collagen accumulation and preserved hyaluronic acid distribution. Transforming growth factor‐β and interleukin‐1β expression levels were elevated in the Old group and reduced following Fer‐1 treatment.ConclusionThe results indicate that ferroptosis is involved in vocal fold aging. In addition, Fer‐1 effectively suppressed ferroptotic pathways, which may be associated with the attenuation of aging‐related extracellular matrix alterations in the vocal folds.Level of EvidenceNA.Keywords: extracellular matrix, ferroptosis, ferrostatin‐1, rat model, vocal fold aging

Introduction

The global population is aging at an unprecedented rate. According to World Health Organization projections cited in a global aging demographics review, the number of people aged 60 years and older is expected to increase from approximately 1.0 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion by 2030 and 2.1 billion by 2050 [1]. Aging is accompanied by various physiological changes, among which age‐related voice changes, also referred to as presbyphonia, have gained clinical importance because of their increasing prevalence in older individuals [2]. Presbyphonia is characterized by hoarseness, reduced phonation time, vocal instability, and increased vocal fatigue, leading to impaired communication and decreased quality of life in older adults [3].

The underlying mechanisms of presbyphonia are closely associated with age‐related histological and functional alterations of the vocal folds. Aging leads to changes in the extracellular matrix (ECM) composition, loss of elasticity and flexibility at the epithelial–lamina propria (LP) junction, decreased glandular secretion with resultant mucosal dryness, and reorganization of collagen and elastic fibers within the LP, all of which adversely affect voice production [4, 5, 6]. In addition, laryngeal muscle atrophy contributes to presbyphonia [7]. Collectively, these structural alterations, including collagen fiber reorganization, loss of elastic fibers, decreased cellularity, and reduced hyaluronic acid (HA) levels, impair the viscoelastic properties of the vocal folds and disrupt normal vibratory behavior. These cumulative laryngeal changes give rise to glottal insufficiency and the characteristic dysphonia in presbyphonia.

Previous investigations into the pathophysiology of vocal fold aging have primarily focused on biomechanical alterations, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress [8, 9]. The accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) has been implicated as a major contributor to age‐related tissue damage and cellular dysfunction [10]. In aged vocal folds, excessive ROS disrupt mitochondrial function, impair ECM integrity, and compromise muscle cell function, contributing to age‐related tissue degeneration [11, 12]. However, the specific cell death pathways that mediate aging‐related degeneration of the vocal folds remain largely unexplored.

Ferroptosis is a recently identified form of regulated cell death characterized by iron‐dependent lipid peroxidation. It is characterized by the intracellular accumulation of ferrous iron, depletion of glutathione, inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and excessive peroxidation of membrane phospholipids. Ferroptosis plays a critical role in aging and age‐related degenerative diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders and musculoskeletal degeneration [13, 14]. Unlike apoptosis and necrosis, ferroptosis is characterized by distinct molecular and biochemical mechanisms driven by oxidative stress and dysregulated iron metabolism, implicating its potential role in age‐related degeneration of vocal folds.

Ferroptosis has been predominantly studied in head and neck squamous cell carcinomas [15]. However, few studies have investigated the relationship between vocal fold aging and ferroptosis. Aging vocal folds exhibit increased oxidative stress and an ECM‐rich LP with low cellularity, features suggestive of susceptibility to lipid peroxidation and potentially ferroptosis‐mediated degeneration. Vocal fold fibroblasts are the primary cells responsible for maintaining extracellular matrix homeostasis within the lamina propria, including the production of hyaluronic acid and collagen. Therefore, ferroptosis‐related injury to these cells may contribute to extracellular matrix depletion and age‐related structural alterations of the vocal folds [16, 17, 18].

Ferrostatin‐1 (Fer‐1) is a well‐established ferroptosis inhibitor that has been shown to suppress lipid peroxidation and protect against tissue injury and age‐associated structural changes in various experimental models [19]. Although antioxidant strategies have been explored in vocal fold research [20], the effects of Fer‐1 on vocal fold tissues remain unknown. Given the vulnerability of aging vocal folds to oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, Fer‐1 may offer a novel therapeutic approach for mitigating age‐related degeneration.

The present study aimed to determine whether ferroptosis occurs in aged vocal folds and to evaluate the effects of locally administered Fer‐1 on vocal fold tissue in an aged rat model.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Experimental Design

Thirty‐six male Sprague–Dawley rats were used in this study. Animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions with ad libitum access to food and water and were allowed a 1‐week acclimatization period prior to experimentation. Following acclimatization, the rats were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 12 per group): Young (13 weeks), Old (60 weeks), and Old treated with Fer‐1 (Old–Fer). The animals in each group (n = 12) were equally divided for downstream analyses. Specifically, half of the animals were used for histological evaluation, including immunohistochemistry, while the remaining half were allocated for mRNA expression analysis using PCR. The sample size was selected to be comparable to those used in previous rat vocal fold studies [21, 22]. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Pusan National University Hospital (IACUC no. PNUH‐2024‐233).

Fer‐1

Based on previous in vivo studies demonstrating the biological efficacy of Fer‐1 at comparable dosing ranges, we selected a dose of 2.5 μmol/kg/day for local laryngeal injection. This dosing regimen was chosen to align with concentrations previously shown to exert protective effects in animal models of iron‐mediated tissue injury, including acute liver injury in mice and iron‐induced neurotoxicity in rats [23, 24].

Vocal Folds Injection Procedure

Rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of a weight‐adjusted combination of alfaxalone (Alfaxan) and xylazine (Rompun). Animals were positioned supine on an intubation platform, and the oral cavity was gently opened using a mouth retractor. The laryngeal inlet was exposed using a small laryngeal retractor. The laryngeal inlet was exposed using a small laryngeal retractor, and the vocal folds were visualized using a 0° rigid endoscope. For local anesthesia, topical lidocaine was applied to the left vocal fold (Figure 1).

Vocal fold injection procedure. (A) Rats positioned supine on a custom‐designed platform. (B) Direct injection of injectate into the left vocal fold using a Hamilton microsyringe under endoscopic guidance.

In the Old–Fer group, ferrostatin‐1 (Fer‐1) was administered by direct injection into the left vocal fold. A total volume of 20 μL was delivered using a Hamilton microsyringe under endoscopic visualization. Young and Old groups received an equivalent volume of physiological saline (20 μL) using the same injection protocol. Rats received two injections at 2‐week intervals (Weeks 0 and 2). All rats were euthanized 1 week after the second injection (Week 3), and vocal fold tissues were harvested for analysis.

Histological Staining and Immunohistochemical Analysis

The harvested laryngeal tissues, including the vocal folds, were fixed overnight in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin, processed, and embedded in paraffin. Serial coronal sections encompassing the membranous vocal folds were cut at a thickness of 4–5 μm. For general histological evaluation, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Additional histochemical staining relevant to ECM remodeling, including Masson's trichrome staining, was performed as indicated. The sections were then stained with 1% Alcian Blue solution (pH 2.5) for 20–30 min to detect acidic mucopolysaccharides, including hyaluronic acid.

Histological evaluation focused on age‐ and treatment‐related changes in the vocal fold epithelium, LP. Particular attention was given to the LP architecture, cellularity, and ECM organization. Quantitative analyses, including measurements of LP thickness, collagen density, and fibrosis‐related indices, were performed by observers blinded to the group allocation using standardized protocols with image analysis software.

LP thickness was quantified in histological sections by measuring the perpendicular distance between the epithelial basement membrane and the underlying thyroarytenoid muscle at predefined locations along the mid‐membranous portion of the vocal fold. Multiple measurements were obtained for each section and averaged to generate the representative LP thickness values for each vocal fold. This standardized approach allowed for a consistent comparison of age‐ and treatment‐related changes in LP morphology across experimental groups.

For immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, paraffin‐embedded sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated using a graded alcohol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using appropriate buffers and methods according to antibody‐specific requirements. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched and nonspecific binding was blocked using a blocking solution. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies targeting collagen I (1:1000; Abcam) at 4°C for 24 h. After washing, the sections were incubated with a goat anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (ENZO Biochem Inc., USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Negative controls were prepared by substituting the primary antibody with phosphate‐buffered saline containing 1% bovine serum albumin.

Representative images were acquired from standardized regions of the LP at 200× magnification using a light microscope (Leica). Quantitative analysis of collagen deposition was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The lamina propria region was manually defined as the region of interest, and the staining intensity of the color signal in staining was measured and compared among groups.

Quantitation of Redox Status and Ferroptosis Response

Immediately following euthanasia, whole blood was collected from rats via abdominal aorta using a sterile syringe. The collected blood samples were transferred into plain tubes without anticoagulants and maintained at room temperature for clot formation (approximately 20–30 min). Subsequently, the clotted blood samples were centrifuged at 2000–3000g for 10–15 min at 4°C to separate the serum fraction. The resulting supernatant (serum) was carefully aspirated without disturbing the cellular components and transferred into clean microcentrifuge tubes. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production was quantified using a fluorescence‐based assay. Briefly, 2′,7′‐dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH‐DA) was introduced into the serum to a final concentration of 25 mM (total volume: 250 mL). Fluorescence signals were measured at 5‐min intervals for 30 min using a microplate reader, with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 485 and 530 nm, respectively. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) enzymatic activity was assessed using a commercially available assay kit (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Levels of lipid peroxidation products, including malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4‐hydroxyalkenals (HAE), were quantified using a Bioxytech LPO‐586 assay kit (OXIS Health Products, Foster, CA, USA). This assay is based on the reaction between MDA/HAE and a specific chromogenic reagent, resulting in the formation of a stable‐colored compound. The intensity of this chromophore was measured spectrophotometrically at a wavelength of 586 nm. Total iron content, including both ferrous (Fe2+) and ferric (Fe3+) forms, in serum was determined using a rat‐specific colorimetric assay kit (Biovision Inc., Spring Valley, CA, USA). Under acidic conditions, ferric ions were liberated from carrier proteins and subsequently reduced to Fe2+. The reduced iron then formed a stable chromogenic complex with Ferene S, which was quantified by measuring absorbance at 593 nm.

Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the dissected vocal fold tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using spectrophotometry based on the A260/A280 ratio. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using a commercial reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems).

Quantitative real‐time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green Master Mix on an ABI PRISM 7900HT Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression analyses have focused on markers related to ferroptosis and ECM remodeling. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons among the three experimental groups (Young, Old, and Old–Fer) were performed using one‐way analysis of variance conducted separately for the left (injected) and right (noninjected) vocal folds. When a significant overall group effect was identified, Tukey's multiple comparison post hoc test was used to assess pairwise differences between groups. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐tailed p < 0.05. Normality of the data distribution was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Bartlett's test. The data points were not excluded from the analysis. All experiments were performed using identical acquisition and quantification settings, and each biological replicate was used as an independent sample.

Results

Ferroptosis‐Related Indicators

Compared with the Young group, the Old group exhibited a significant increase in oxidative stress and ferroptosis‐related indicators in serum and vocal fold tissues, including intracellular ROS levels measured by the DCFH‐DA fluorescence assay, malondialdehyde (MDA), MDA/4‐hydroxyalkenals (HAE), and iron content, accompanied by a marked reduction in GPX4 levels quantified using a commercial assay kit. In contrast, Fer‐1 administration (Old–Fer group) significantly attenuated these age‐related alterations. Specifically, DCFH‐DA fluorescence intensity, lipid peroxidation indicators, and iron content were markedly reduced, whereas GPX4 levels were restored to values comparable to those observed in the Young group (Figure 2).

LP Thickness

In the left (injected) vocal fold, LP thickness was significantly higher in the Old group than in the Young group. This thickening was significantly reduced in the Old–Fer group following the Fer‐1 injection (Table 2). In the right (noninjected) vocal fold, LP thickening was also observed in the Old group compared to the Young group; however, no significant difference was detected between the Old and Old–Fer groups on the contralateral side (Figure 3A,B).

Histological and Histochemical Changes in the Vocal Fold LP

Histological evaluation demonstrated differences in ECM morphology in the rat vocal folds among the groups. As shown in Figure 4, Masson's trichrome staining revealed increased collagen deposition and LP thickening in the Old group compared to the Young group. In the Old–Fer group, collagen deposition was reduced, and LP thickness appeared to decrease relative to that in the Old group.

Immunohistochemical staining for collagen I showed an increased expression and broader distribution throughout the LP in the Old group than in the Young group. In the Old–Fer group, collagen I immunoreactivity was reduced, and its distribution was more limited within the LP than in the Old group (Figure 4).

Histochemical analysis of HA synthase (HAS) demonstrated group‐dependent differences in staining. In the Young group, HAS immunoreactivity was primarily observed in the superficial LP. In the Old group, HAS staining intensity was reduced, and the distribution appeared to be less confined to the superficial layer. In the Old–Fer group, HAS immunoreactivity was higher than that in the Old group, with staining more prominently detected in the superficial LP (Figure 5).

Quantitative Analysis of Collagen and HA

Collagen expression was significantly increased in the Old group compared to that in the Young group and was significantly reduced in the Old–Fer group. In contrast, the expression of HAS2 was significantly upregulated in the Old–Fer group, exceeding that in the Young group. These changes in collagen and HAS2 expression were consistently observed in both left and right vocal folds.

Gene Expression Changes Associated With ECM Remodeling and Inflammation

To evaluate the age‐ and treatment‐related changes in ECM‐associated gene expression in the vocal fold LP, quantitative real‐time PCR analysis was performed using Fer‐1‐injected (left) vocal folds. As shown in Figure 6A, the mRNA expression levels of collagen I, transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β), and interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) were significantly higher in the Old group than in the Young group. In the Old–Fer group, the expression levels of these genes were significantly lower than those observed in the Old group.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate the effects of direct antioxidant injections into the vocal folds of rats. This study demonstrated that ferroptosis contributes to age‐related histological changes in the vocal folds and that local inhibition of ferroptosis with Fer‐1 attenuates the biochemical and structural features of vocal fold aging. The results support the hypothesis that oxidative stress‐ and ferroptosis‐related mechanisms underlie age‐associated alterations.

Ferroptosis is characterized by iron‐dependent lipid peroxidation and depletion of GPX4. In the present study, aged vocal folds exhibited significant increases in ROS, lipid peroxidation products (MDA and MDA/4‐hydroxyalkenals), and cytosolic iron, accompanied by reduced GPX4 activity. These findings suggest the involvement of ferroptosis‐related pathways in age‐related structural alterations of the vocal folds, consistent with previous reports [25, 26]. Furthermore, local administration of Fer‐1 attenuated these changes by restoring GPX4 activity and reducing oxidative injury, supporting the possibility that ferroptosis‐associated mechanisms may contribute to the pathophysiology of vocal fold senescence. However, additional studies are warranted to directly confirm the occurrence of ferroptosis in vocal fold tissue. Structural changes in the LP are well‐known hallmarks of vocal fold aging. In the present study, quantitative histological analysis demonstrated age‐ and treatment‐related differences in LP morphology. LP thickness was increased in the Old group compared to that in the Young group; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Notably, LP thickness was reduced in the Fer‐1–injected vocal folds, whereas no significant change was observed in the contralateral folds, suggesting a side‐specific local effect. These findings appear to contrast with prior descriptions of aging in human vocal folds. Histological evaluations of older human vocal folds have consistently reported epithelial and superficial LP thinning, reduced cellular density, fragmentation of elastic fibers, decreased HA content, and diminished viscoelasticity, all of which contribute to the characteristic aging voice [5, 27]. However, several animal studies have demonstrated that aging in rodents is associated with fibrosis‐dominant ECM remodeling, characterized by increased collagen deposition, reduced elastin density, altered HA synthesis, and changes that promote tissue stiffening, which may result in apparent LP thickening [28, 29]. These findings indicate that age‐related changes in the vocal folds extend beyond the commonly described atrophy‐related morphological changes and may involve heterogeneous patterns of structural remodeling. Although age‐related LP thickening was observed in the present rat model and was partially reduced following Fer‐1 injection, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Given the short observation period, they likely reflect an acute‐phase tissue response to antioxidant modulation rather than established structural remodeling. Therefore, additional evidence is required to clarify the underlying mechanisms and biological significance of these changes.

In the present study, age‐related alterations in the ECM composition were observed in the LP of the vocal folds. Compared with the Young group, the Old group exhibited reduced HA distribution and increased collagen I expression, which is consistent with a previous report [30]. In the Fer‐1‐treated group, these aging‐associated changes were attenuated, with a relative preservation of the HA distribution and a reduction in collagen I accumulation in the injected vocal folds compared with the Old group. These results indicate that the inhibition of ferroptosis is associated with the modulation of age‐related ECM remodeling in the vocal folds. Notably, a prior study by Mizuta et al. demonstrated that long‐term dietary supplementation with the antioxidant astaxanthin mitigated age‐associated structural changes in the vocal folds in a rat model, supporting the role of oxidative stress modulation in vocal fold aging [20]. In contrast to this systemic dietary approach, the present study evaluated the effects of antioxidant therapy through direct injection, allowing for an assessment of the localized effects of Fer‐1 within the vocal fold tissue.

The inflammatory cytokines TGF‐β and IL‐1β are key regulators of aging‐related tissue fibrosis and ECM dysregulation. In this study, compared with the Young group, the Old group exhibited increased expression of TGF‐β and IL‐1β. In the Fer‐1‐treated group, the expression of both cytokines was lower than that in the Old group. These findings indicate that the inhibition of ferroptosis is associated with the attenuation of age‐related inflammatory activation in the vocal folds.

Taken together, the findings indicate that ferroptosis is involved in vocal fold aging and that inhibition of ferroptosis with Fer‐1 is associated with the modulation of key molecular components relevant to vocal fold function. The local administration of Fer‐1 was found to be associated with changes in HA distribution, collagen accumulation, and inflammatory cytokine expression. In addition, clinical evidence supporting the relevance of antioxidant effects on the vocal folds has been reported, with astaxanthin supplementation found to protect against vocal fold injury and inflammation caused by vocal loading in a clinical trial [31]. Collectively, these experimental and clinical observations suggest that antioxidant‐based strategies may hold therapeutic potential for age‐related vocal fold dysfunction. However, their clinical applicability must be established through well‐designed clinical studies. From a clinical perspective, the development of noninvasive or minimally invasive approaches for the localized delivery of antioxidant agents to the vocal folds, such as topical or inhalational administration, may further enhance the feasibility and broaden potential applications beyond direct injection [32].

This study has several limitations. First, to the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to evaluate the effects of the direct administration of an antioxidant targeting ferroptotic pathways in the vocal folds. Although no procedure‐related complications were observed in this experimental model, the safety of Fer‐1 following direct vocal‐fold administration remains unknown and potential adverse effects related to local cytotoxicity, redox imbalance, or injection‐associated tissue injury cannot be excluded. Accordingly, further studies are required to assess potential side effects, long‐term tolerability, and safety. Second, because this study was conducted using a rat model, the observed histological and ECM changes cannot be assumed to translate directly into functional voice improvements. Third, the present study was limited by the fact that the evaluation was conducted only 1 week after ferrostatin‐1 administration. Although the rapid modulation of oxidative stress and ferroptosis‐related pathways is biologically plausible, this relatively short experimental duration may not fully capture the long‐term genetic, proteomic, and histologic effects. Therefore, additional studies with longer observation periods are warranted to verify the persistence and durability of these findings.

As structural remodeling does not necessarily correlate with phonatory outcomes, the incorporation of functional voice assessments would strengthen future investigations. Further studies are required to elucidate the link between ferroptosis and vocal fold aging, including the specific pathways through which ferroptotic processes influence ECM remodeling in the vocal folds.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge this study is the first to demonstrate the effects of direct local antioxidant injection into the vocal folds of aged rats. These findings indicate that ferroptosis is involved in vocal fold aging and that inhibition of ferroptosis with ferrostatin‐1 is associated with modulation of key molecular and histological components relevant to vocal fold structure. Local administration of ferrostatin‐1 was associated with changes in hyaluronic acid distribution, collagen accumulation, lamina propria thickness and inflammatory cytokine expression in aged rat vocal folds.
