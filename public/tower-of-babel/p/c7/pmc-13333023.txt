Green valorization of cotton–acrylic fabric blends into magnetic nanofiber adsorbents for PFAS and organic dye remediation in complex wastewater streams
Authors: Mahmoud F Mubarak, Bahaa S Metwally, Hazem I Bendary, R Hosny, Mostafa Y Nassar, Ibrahim Alfurayj, Amel Taha, Esam M Bakir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing generation of mixed textile waste and the persistence of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) and synthetic dyes in industrial effluents present significant challenges for sustainable wastewater treatment. In this study, cotton–acrylic textile waste was valorized into multifunctional magnetic nanofiber adsorbents (CA–Fe₃O₄–NF) via a combined sol–gel-assisted in situ co-precipitation and electrospinning approach. The resulting nanofibers exhibited a hierarchically porous structure, a surface area of 112 m2 g⁻1, and superparamagnetic properties enabling rapid magnetic separation. The developed material demonstrated effective adsorption performance toward PFOS, PFOA, methylene blue, and rhodamine B, with maximum adsorption capacities of 184, 165, 142, and 118 mg g⁻1, respectively, along with relatively fast kinetics and spontaneous, endothermic adsorption behavior. The nanofibers retained more than 90% of their initial performance after multiple regeneration cycles, indicating good reusability. Unlike many previously reported magnetic nanofiber adsorbents that rely on virgin polymers and are evaluated under simplified conditions, this study employs waste-derived materials and includes assessment under varying pH, ionic strength, and real wastewater matrices, providing improved environmental relevance. Although the material exhibited potential for simultaneous removal of PFAS and dyes in simplified systems, the study primarily focuses on controlled experimental conditions, and further validation under more complex multi-component environments is required. In addition, the proposed adsorption mechanisms are based on indirect evidence derived from spectroscopic analysis and adsorption behavior, and should therefore be considered as plausible interpretations rather than definitive conclusions. Overall, this work presents a sustainable and cost-effective approach for converting textile waste into functional magnetic nanofiber adsorbents, highlighting their potential application in wastewater treatment while acknowledging the need for further validation under realistic conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54333-2.Keywords: Magnetic nanofibers, PFAS removal, Organic dye adsorption, Cotton–acrylic textile waste, Regenerable adsorbents, Green synthesisSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

The rapid expansion of textile consumption and fast fashion has significantly increased the generation of mixed-fiber waste, particularly cotton–acrylic blends, which are difficult to recycle using conventional mechanical or chemical methods1,2. At the same time, industrial activities such as electronics manufacturing, firefighting foam usage, electroplating, and textile dyeing release persistent contaminants including per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) and synthetic organic dyes3–7. PFAS are of particular concern due to the strong carbon–fluorine bond, high chemical stability, and their tendency to persist and bioaccumulate in aquatic and biological systems8–10.

Conventional treatment technologies, including activated carbon adsorption, ion-exchange resins, membrane filtration, and advanced oxidation processes, often suffer from limitations such as low selectivity toward PFAS, reduced efficiency in the presence of competing ions, and high energy demand11–16. Similarly, dye removal processes such as coagulation, photocatalysis, and chemical oxidation are frequently associated with secondary sludge generation, catalyst deactivation, and limited applicability in complex wastewater matrices17–19. Moreover, many existing adsorbents rely on virgin polymeric materials and toxic solvents, which contradict sustainability principles and increase environmental burden20,21. These limitations highlight the urgent need for multifunctional, sustainable adsorbents capable of simultaneously addressing diverse pollutants under realistic wastewater conditions22–24. In this context, waste valorization strategies have emerged as a promising approach to transform low-value waste streams into high-performance functional materials. Among them, textile waste represents a particularly attractive feedstock due to its large global production volume and complex composition of natural and synthetic fibers.

To address these challenges, this study presents a green valorization strategy to convert cotton–acrylic textile waste into magnetic nanofiber adsorbents. The proposed method combines sol–gel-assisted in situ co-precipitation with electrospinning, enabling uniform incorporation of Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles into regenerated polymeric chains derived from textile waste25,26. The resulting material, CA–Fe₃O₄–NF, exhibits a hybrid architecture that integrates the flexibility and surface functionality of regenerated fibers with the magnetic properties of iron oxide nanoparticles27,28. This combination enables efficient pollutant adsorption and rapid magnetic separation, eliminating the need for additional filtration steps.

Unlike conventional adsorbents derived from virgin polymers, the present material is synthesized directly from post-consumer textile waste, reducing reliance on non-renewable resources and supporting circular economy principles. In addition, the integration of Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles enhances both surface reactivity and recoverability, providing a dual functional advantage compared with non-magnetic or single-function adsorbents. Previous studies on magnetic nanofiber adsorbents have mainly focused on single-pollutant systems under simplified experimental conditions, which limits their practical relevance. In contrast, the present work develops a waste-derived multifunctional adsorbent designed for chemically diverse pollutants, including PFAS and organic dyes, under more representative aqueous conditions.

The novelty of this work can be summarized in three main aspects:

i.Direct transformation of mixed cotton–acrylic textile waste into hybrid nanofibers composed of regenerated cellulose and polyacrylonitrile segments;

Materials and experimental

Materials

In this work post-consumer cotton-acrylic blended fabric (in fact, around 60% of cotton and 40% of polyacrylonitrile) was used, which was found in local textile waste streams and prewashed before use. The precursors used in the in-situ co-precipitation process of Fe3O4 nanoparticles were iron (II) sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO47H2O, ≥ 99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich) and iron (III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3.6H2O, ≥ 98%, Merck). Alkaline agents (Sodium hydroxide pellets, NaOH, ≥ 99.5% Loba Chemie, and ammonium hydroxide solution, NH4OH, 28–30% Fisher Scientific) were used to control the sol–gel conditions and trigger the formation of nanoparticles. Washing solvent and dissolution solvents were Ethanol (≥ 99.9%, BDH) and analytical-grade acetone (≥ 99%, Alpha Chemicals). The polymer was dissolved with the help of N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, ≥ 99.8% Honeywell) and acetic acid (glacial, ≥ 99.7%, Sigma-Aldrich) before electrospinning. PFOS (≥ 98%, Tokyo Chemical Industry), PFOA (≥ 98%, Alfa Aesar), methylene blue dye (ACS grade, Oxford Lab) and rhodamine B (≥ 99%, HiMedia) were the chosen representative contaminants in the study of adsorption. Background electrolyte solutions used to prepare background electrolyte solutions were prepared using sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥ 99.8%, Merck) and calcium chloride dihydrate (CaCl2.2H2O, ≥ 99%, Fluka). Chemicals were not purified anymore but were taken as received and deionized water (18.2 MOcm) was used in all the experiments.

Synthesis of CA– Fe3O4–NF nanofiber adsorbent

Magnetic nanofiber adsorbent (CA-Fe3O4-NF) has been prepared by using in-situ co-precipitation, sol–gel and electrospinning. To prepare a prewashed cotton-acrylic fabric waste, first, 10 g of waste was prewashed in 200 mL of 1.5% acetic acid (v/v) at 60 °C to ensure that the fabric had softened and then cut into small pieces. The fibers were then swollen by pouring the mixture into a 150 mL DMF and 50 mL ethanol solution which was stirred through a 500 rotations at 75 °C until a homogenous polymeric solution was achieved. To prepare Fe3O4, the following two precursor solutions were prepared in separate bottle as follows: Solution included 2.78 g FeCl3 6H2O dissolved in 40 mL of deionized water and Solution B included 1.39 g FeSO4 7H2O dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water purged with nitrogen to prevent oxidation. Fe3O4 nanoparticles were allowed to nucleate at 80 °C and solutions A and B were mixed at a steady 600 rpm rate until 50 mL NH4OH (28–30%) was added drop-by-drop during 30 min. The newly prepared magnetic sol was then added directly to the polymer solution with textile background and stirred for 2 h until a fine dispersion. Electrospinning mixture was put into a 20 mL syringe with a needle tip (0.8 mm) and was electrospun at 21 kV, at a flow rate of 0.6 mL h-1 and a tip-to-collector distance of 15 cm. The nanofibers were harvested using an aluminum foil drum that was rotating at 300 rpm. The membrane was dried at 70 °C during electrospinning followed by 6 h to improve the stability of the network after which it was washed with ethanol and deionized water to eliminate solvents left behind and dried at 50 °C over 12 h. The end product was to be kept in airtight containers until it was used.

Characterization techniques

A combination of different analytical methods was used to determine the physicochemical and structural properties of the synthesized CA-Fe3O4-NF nanofibers. The Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker Tensor 27) was applied in the 4000–400 cm-1 wavelength range at the resolution of 4 cm-1 and 32 scans per sample to identify functional groups. X-ray Diffraction (XRD, PANalytical X’Pert PRO) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) was used to analyze the crystallographic phases by scanning between 2θ = 10–80° with a step size of 0.02°. SEM (JEOL JSM-7600F) operated at 5–15 kV was used to examine the morphology and fiber structure, while elemental mapping was performed using Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX, Oxford Instruments). The surface area and porosity were assessed through N2 adsorption–desorption analysis (BET/BJH, Micromeritics ASAP 2020) after the 120 °C for 6 h, with measurements being taken at the temperature of liquid nitrogen (77 K). Magnetic traits were determined with the help of a Vibrating Sample Magnetometer (VSM, Lake Shore 7404) in the field of applied magnetic field (-15,000 to + 15,000 Oe) at ambient temperature. Thermal stability was ascertained by means of Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA, TA Instruments Q500) at 25–800 °C at a heating rate of 10 oC min-1 in the presence of nitrogen flow (50 mL min-1). Also, the zeta potential measurements were conducted on a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments) in varying pH (2–12) modified in the presence of 0.1 M NaOH or HCl. The measurements were done in three replicates to facilitate reproducibility.

Adsorption application process

The performance of CA-Fe3O4-NF nanofiber adsorbent was tested in batch experiments with respect to PFOS, PFOA, methylene blue (MB) and rhodamine B (RhB) in controlled laboratory conditions. Stocks of the respective contaminants were prepared in 1000 mg L-1 of deionized water and diluted to working concentration of 5 to 200 mg L-1. In each experiment, 25 mL of contaminant solution was poured into a 50 mL polypropylene centrifuge tube, and 20 mg of CA-Fe3O4-NF was added to the sample at a solid–liquid ratio of 0.8 g L-1. The pH was adjusted in the range of 2–12 with the use of 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH, and the samples were stirred. The thermal effects were studied at the temperatures of 25, 35 and 45 °C at 150 rpm using a thermostatic shaker. Experiments of contact time were carried out between 5 and 180 min to establish an equilibrium behavior, and kinetic tests were carried out at initial contaminant concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 mg L-1. In the case of isotherm studies, the concentrations were manipulated to range between 10 and 200 mg L-1 under the same pH and temperature. After every run, an external magnetic was used to separate the magnetic nanofibers after 5–7 s and then the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 um membrane and then analyzed. A UV-V is spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2600) was used in the determination of contaminant concentrations at 665 nm (MB) and 554 nm (RhB), whereas the concentrations of PFOS and PFOA were determined by HPLC with a C18 column with an injection volume of 20 uL. In order to assess reusability, regeneration studies were conducted by desorbing the saturated fibers by adding 30 mL of ethanol/0.1 M NaOH mixture (1:1 v/v) and leaving the fibers to desorb at 50 °C , then the fibers were washed and dried at 50 °C and the procedure was repeated five times. All adsorption experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. The reported adsorption capacities and removal efficiencies represent the mean values of three independent experiments. Standard deviations were calculated and are presented as error bars in the corresponding figures.

Adsorption experiments under variable conditions

To evaluate the robustness of CA–Fe3O4-NF nanofibers under realistic environmental conditions, adsorption experiments were systematically conducted across a range of pH values, ionic strengths, and in real wastewater matrices.

pH influence

Solutions containing PFOA, PFOS, Methylene Blue (MB), and Rhodamine B (RhB) were adjusted to pH 3, 7, and 10 using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH. Adsorption was performed using 0.1 g of nanofibers in 100 mL of solution at 25 ± 2 °C for 120 min. Maximum removal efficiencies were observed at neutral pH (7.0), with reduced performance under acidic and alkaline conditions due to protonation/deprotonation effects impacting electrostatic interactions.

Ionic strength influence

To simulate ionic interference, Na⁺, Ca2⁺, and Mg2⁺ were introduced at 100 mM concentrations. Monovalent Na⁺ caused minimal reduction in adsorption, while divalent cations (Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺) led to moderate decreases due to electrostatic shielding and competitive binding.

Real wastewater matrices

Adsorption tests were performed using both treated and untreated municipal wastewater collected from a local treatment facility. Treated effluent showed only slight reductions in removal efficiency compared to deionized water, while untreated samples exhibited more pronounced declines due to organic matter and competing ions.

Results and dissection

Structural and textural properties of CA- Fe3O4–NF

The development of the hybrid CA-Fe3O4-NF nanofibers and structural characteristics of the adsorption performance was studied systematically based on complementary spectroscopic and textural analyses.

According to FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 1), the presence of oxygen- and nitrogen-containing functional groups can be observed, which are associated with the regenerated cotton–acrylic matrix. The broad band at 3320 cm⁻1 corresponds to –OH stretching vibrations of cellulose, while the peak at 2240 cm⁻1 is assigned to the C≡N stretching of polyacrylonitrile segments. In addition, the band at 565 cm⁻1 can be attributed to Fe–O vibrations, suggesting the formation of magnetite (Fe₃O₄) nanoparticles within the polymeric structure. All characteristic peaks have been clearly labeled in Fig. 1 to improve spectral interpretation. The coexistence of –OH, –C = O, –C≡N, and Fe–O groups indicates a heterogeneous surface with multiple potential interaction sites for different classes of pollutants. These observations provide indirect evidence for the interaction of PFAS molecules with metal oxide sites, while organic dyes may interact with oxygen-containing functional groups through electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding. However, these interactions are inferred from spectroscopic analysis and adsorption behavior, and should be considered as plausible interpretations rather than definitive confirmation.

FTIR spectra of Acrylic fibers (a), Cotton (Cellulose) (b), Fe3O4 nanoparticles (c), and CA- Fe3O4–NF nanofibers (d).

The crystalline structure of the prepared materials was analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Fig. 2, the characteristic diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values of approximately 30.2°, 35.5°, 43.3°, 53.6°, 57.2°, and 62.8° correspond to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) crystal planes of magnetite (Fe3O4), respectively. These peaks match well with the standard diffraction data for Fe3O4 (JCPDS card No. 19–0629), confirming the successful incorporation of magnetite nanoparticles into the nanofibrous matrix.

XRD patterns of regenerated cotton–acrylic fibers, Fe3O4 nanoparticles, and CA–Fe3O4 nanofibers. The diffraction peaks corresponding to magnetite match the standard JCPDS card No. 19–0629.

In addition, the broad diffraction feature observed for the regenerated cotton–acrylic fibers indicates the partially amorphous structure of the polymeric matrix after dissolution and electrospinning. The coexistence of polymeric features and Fe3O4 crystalline peaks confirms the formation of the CA– Fe3O4 nanofiber composite.

To determine the optimal loading of Fe3O4 nanoparticles within the nanofibrous matrix, a series of CA– Fe3O4 composites were prepared with different Fe3O4 contents (5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 wt% relative to the polymer matrix). The adsorption performance of each composite was evaluated under identical experimental conditions using the target pollutant solution.

As presented in Table 1, the adsorption capacity increased with increasing Fe3O4 loading up to 15 wt%, indicating that the presence of additional magnetite nanoparticles introduces more active adsorption sites and enhances pollutant interaction with the adsorbent surface. However, further increasing the Fe3O4 content beyond this level resulted in a slight decrease in adsorption capacity. This behavior can be attributed to partial aggregation of nanoparticles and reduced accessibility of adsorption sites due to excessive particle loading.

The surface morphology and fiber structure of the prepared materials were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as presented in Fig. 4. The micrograph of the raw cotton fibers (Fig. 4a) shows a relatively smooth fibrous structure typical of natural cellulose fibers. In contrast, the synthesized Fe3O4 nanoparticles (Fig. 4b) appear as aggregated nanoscale particles, which is consistent with the magnetic nanoparticle formation through the co-precipitation process.

SEM micrograph of (a) Cotton (Cellulose) (b) Fe3O4 Nanoparticles, (c) CA–Fe3O4–NF nanofibers.

After incorporation into the regenerated polymer matrix and electrospinning, the resulting CA–Fe3O4–NF nanofibers (Fig. 4c) exhibit a uniform bead-free nanofibrous network with fiber diameters ranging from approximately 145 to 210 nm. The fibers form a highly interconnected porous structure that provides a large accessible surface area and facilitates rapid diffusion of pollutant molecules toward adsorption sites. The SEM images also suggest that Fe3O4 nanoparticles are well dispersed along the nanofiber surface and partially embedded within the polymeric network.

The stable incorporation of Fe3O4 nanoparticles within the nanofiber structure is attributed to the in-situ co-precipitation process during synthesis. In this process, Fe2⁺ and Fe3⁺ ions initially interact with the functional groups present in the regenerated cotton–acrylic matrix, particularly hydroxyl (–OH) groups from cellulose and nitrile (–C≡N) groups from polyacrylonitrile. These functional groups provide coordination sites that facilitate the nucleation of iron oxide species. As the precipitation reaction proceeds, Fe3O4 nanoparticles form directly within the polymeric network, creating strong interfacial interactions between magnetite nanoparticles and the fiber matrix. The nanoparticles are therefore anchored through a combination of Fe–O coordination interactions, hydrogen bonding, and physical confinement within the electrospun nanofiber structure, which prevents nanoparticle aggregation or detachment and ensures uniform dispersion throughout the fibers.

Such nanoscale fibrous morphology significantly shortens intraparticle diffusion pathways and contributes to the fast adsorption kinetics observed in subsequent adsorption experiments. Moreover, the uniform distribution of Fe3O4 nanoparticles within the nanofiber structure confirms the successful integration of the magnetic phase within the regenerated polymer matrix. The surface morphology and fiber structure of the prepared materials were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as presented in Fig. 4. The micrograph of the raw cotton fibers (Fig. 4a) shows a relatively smooth fibrous structure typical of natural cellulose fibers. In contrast, the synthesized Fe3O4 nanoparticles (Fig. 4b) appear as aggregated nanoscale particles, which is consistent with the magnetic nanoparticle formation through the co-precipitation process.

The regeneration of cotton–acrylic textile waste during processing was confirmed through structural characterization. FTIR spectra (Fig. 1) showed the reduction of cellulose crystalline peaks at 897 cm⁻1 and 1427 cm⁻1, alongside a shift in the PAN nitrile stretching band from 2245 cm⁻1 to 2240 cm⁻1, suggesting molecular rearrangement during dissolution. XRD analysis (Fig. 2) revealed a decrease in the intensity of cellulose I crystalline peaks at 2θ ≈ 22.5°, consistent with partial disruption of native cellulose crystallinity, while PAN exhibited broadened diffraction peaks indicative of chain reorientation. SEM imaging (Fig. 4) confirmed the formation of continuous, uniform nanofibers, demonstrating successful regeneration of both cellulose and polyacrylonitrile components. These findings collectively validate that the textile waste undergoes structural transformation during dissolution and electrospinning, enabling the production of functional nanofibers (Table 2).

The adsorption–desorption (cm3 g-1) analysis (Fig. 5 a) indicates that the textural properties of the textural properties are type IV, which has a strong hysteresis loop, which are typical of mesoporous materials. The BET surface area of 112 m2 g-1 is a high concentration of accessible adsorption sites; thus directly causing the high monolayer capacities of both PFAS and dyes.

Moreover, the distribution of pore size calculated with the help of BJH (Fig. 5b) reveals a mesoporous range with a narrow range around 6.4 nm. This dimension of the pore is especially conducive to large PFAS molecules (≈1–2 nm) and the size of large dye structures, therefore, pore-filling and internal diffusion processes. The presence of mesopores together with nanoscale fibers, introduces a hierarchical transport network that reduces the resistance to mass-transfer, which is the reason behind the high adsorption capacity and the short equilibrium times realized by CA-Fe3O4-NF.

All these structural characteristics indicate that CA-Fe3O4-NF is no longer a simple physical composite of polymer and magnetite but a hierarchically organized hybrid system, in which chemical functionality, nanoscale morphology and mesoporosity interoperate to optimize adsorption efficiency.

Magnetic and thermal properties

The magnetic property of CA-Fe3O4-NF nanofibers was tested to determine its suitability of rapid separation of practical water treatment systems. The magnetization curve (Fig. 6) has a common superparamagnetic profile with low values of coercivity and remanence indicating the nanoscale dispersion of Fe3O4 in the polymeric matrix. The value of 39.4 emu g-1 saturation magnetization (Ms) is sufficiently large to allow the magnetic recovery of the adsorbent to be done in several seconds with simple external magnet.

VSM magnetization curve of CA-Fe3O4-NF nanofibers.

This is a higher level of magnetization than the minimum (approximately 20 emu g-1) that is necessary to achieve efficient magnetic harvesting in continuous-flow water treatment units, which means that CA-Fe3O4-NF can be easily incorporated into large-scale separation systems without filtration or centrifugation to obtain this energy intensity. The same magnetization has been mentioned to be ideal in the case of recyclable magnetic adsorbents in enhanced wastewater treatment procedures29–31. This is because of the combination of good level of magnetic responsiveness and the morphology of the fibers at nanoscale which is very advantageous in operation compared to the traditional non-magnetic sorbents.

Thermal stability is an important factor influencing the durability and regeneration potential of adsorbents. The TGA profile of CA–Fe₃O₄–NF (Fig. 7) shows a slight weight loss below 120 °C, which is attributed to the removal of physically adsorbed moisture.

TGA curve of Fe3O4 nanoparticles, Acrylic fiber, cotton fiber (cellulose), and CA-Fe3O4-NF nanofibers.

Two main degradation stages are observed in the ranges of 250–350 °C and 430–500 °C, corresponding to the decomposition of the regenerated polymeric backbone and subsequent carbonization processes, respectively. The residual mass observed at elevated temperatures is attributed to the presence of thermally stable Fe3O4 nanoparticles, providing indirect evidence of their successful incorporation and a semi-quantitative estimation of their loading within the nanofiber matrix. However, more precise quantification using advanced analytical techniques is recommended for future investigations.

Notably, the main degradation stages are shifted to higher temperatures compared to those of the raw cotton–acrylic fibers, indicating improved thermal stability of the composite. This enhancement can be attributed to the presence of Fe3O4 nanoparticles and the interfacial interactions between the inorganic phase and the polymer matrix, which restrict polymer chain mobility and delay thermal degradation.

Such improved thermal stability is beneficial for maintaining structural integrity during repeated adsorption–desorption cycles, particularly under conditions involving chemical regeneration or mild thermal treatment. Overall, the combined thermal and magnetic characteristics suggest that the developed CA–Fe₃O₄–NF adsorbent possesses favorable operational stability, supporting its potential applicability in wastewater treatment systems.

Adsorption behavior of CA-Fe3O4-NF nanofibers

Effect of pH on adsorption performance

The experimental data presented in this study represent the average values obtained from triplicate measurements, and the corresponding standard deviations are shown as error bars in the figures. The pH of the aqueous solution is one of the most critical parameters influencing adsorption performance because it affects both the surface charge of the adsorbent and the ionization state of the adsorbate molecules. Therefore, adsorption experiments were conducted over a pH range of 2–10 while maintaining constant adsorbent dosage, contact time, and initial pollutant concentration.

The pH of the solution is a factor that controls distribution of surface charge and pollutant speciation and in this manner; it is a dominating element of adsorption behavior. Figure 8 illustrates how pH influences the elimination of CA-Fe3O4-NF of PFOS, PFOA, methylene blue (MB) and rhodamine B (RhB). Figure 8a shows that PFOS and PFOA adsorption capacity can be significantly increased in acidic environment and reaches the maximum in the pH range of 3–5. It is justified by the fact that the surface oxygen- and nitrogen-containing functional groups become protonated and the FTIR analysis validates the principle, and enhances the electrostatic attraction of the anionic sulfonate and carboxyl functional groups of the PFAS molecules. On the one hand, as the pH increases, the electrostatic interaction between the surface and the adsorbent becomes weak, and the adsorption of PFAS also decreases at a slow rate.

Effect of pH on adsorption capacity of CA–Fe3O4–NF for PFOS, PFOA, MB, and RhB. Error bars represent the standard deviation of three independent experiments.

Compared to MB, Mb exhibits an extremely greater adsorption in neutral and weakly alkaline pH (Fig. 8). Under this scenario, the nanofiber surface will become more negatively charged and strong electrostatic force will be attracted to the cationic dye molecules. RhB is also demonstrating a less pronounced pH-dependent phenomenon as it is a zwitterion; adsorption is modulated by an electrostatic interaction, hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic affinity. The pH-selective properties of PFAS and dyes are unique, which confirms the amphoteric nature of CA-Fe3O4-NF, as well as the ability to alter the reaction with the anionic and cationic pollutants. This multi-purpose pH sensitivity is not very common with single-component adsorbents, and reflects the advantage of the hybrid polymer-magnetite architecture.

Adsorption kinetics

Kinetics of adsorption of PFOS, PFOA, and methylene blue (MB) and rhodamine B (RhB) on CA-Fe3O4-NF were performed in order to comprehend the rate-limiting process and quantify the performance of treatment. Figures 9 and 10 present the adsorption profile with time with varying initial concentrations.

Effect of contact time on adsorption capacity of CA–Fe3O4–NF for PFOS, PFOA, MB, and RhB. Error bars represent the standard deviation of three independent experiments.

Adsorption kinetic curves for PFOS, PFOA, MB, and RhB on CA–Fe₃O₄–NF at different initial concentrations. Error bars represent the standard deviation of three independent experiments.

The first uptake of all the pollutants was fast within the first 20–30 min and the rate of achievement of equilibrium was slow (Fig. 9). The high concentration of empty active sites on the outer of the nanofibers can account the high rate and the decreasing rate indicates the slower diffusion into the inner mesoporous structure. PFOS, PFOA, MB, and RhB were equilibrated in about 90 and 60 min, respectively, which is rather fast by the standards of traditional granular or powdered adsorbents that could take hours. The fast reactions to the adsorption can be directly associated with the structural properties of the CA- Fe3O4-NF. Techniques such as SEM and BET confirmed the fiber diameter and interconnected mesoporous architecture at the nanoscale, reduced the diffusion pathway, and minimized intraparticle mass-transfer resistance. This hierarchical structure is capable of providing efficient access to inner adsorption sites of the pollutant molecules and that is why the short equilibrium times are realized.

In a bid to establish the mechanism of adsorption quantitatively, pseudo-first and second order kinetic models were used to model the experimental data (Fig. 10). The pseudo-second-order model provided a superior fitment to all the pollutants with higher correlation coefficients as well as proximity between the reimbursement of the calculated and experimental adsorption capacities. This effect means that the processes of adsorption are dominated by surface chemical interactions rather than by the simple physical diffusion. Such a domination of pseudo-second-order kinetics is in conformity to the multifunctional surface chemistry of CA-Fe3O4-NF that is given by FTIR analysis. Nitrogen and oxygen functional groups as well as Fe3O4 surfaces sites enable certain interactions, including surface complexation, electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonds. The same chemisorption-dominated behavior has been extensively observed in the case of hybrid adsorbents with active surface sites32–34.

The performance of adsorbents in terms of adsorption dosage was also considered (Fig. 11). The addition of CA-Fe3O4-NF at a higher dose would result in a steady decline in adsorption per unit mass, which is probably because at high solid–liquid ratios, no active sites are used in the reaction. When the dose was high, the supply of adsorption sites was more than the supply of pollutant molecules leading to saturation of the sites and decreased force to drive the mass transfer.

Effect of adsorbent dose on adsorption capacity of CA–Fe3O4–NF for PFOS, PFOA, MB, and RhB. Error bars represent the standard deviation of three independent experiments.

In general, these expendable results of quick site occupancy and dosage-sensitive site utilization validate that CA-Fe3O4-NF contains highly admirable and active surface domains, which correlate with its fibrous morphology on the nanoscale and mesoporous structure. These properties along with excellent equilibrium capacities prove the possibility of CA-Fe3O4-NF to be effective and quick in removing various organic and inorganic pollutants and are, therefore, immensely apt to be used practically with minimal contact times needed, less reactor volumes and reduced operations costs.

To discuss the equilibrium adsorption data, it was measured in accordance with Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm (Table 4). Thermodynamic analysis

The adsorption of PFAS and dyes onto Fe3O4-integrated nanofibers followed pseudo-second-order kinetics, which is often associated with chemisorption. However, the calculated negative ΔG° values (− 18 to − 24 kJ mol⁻1) fall within the range typically attributed to physical adsorption. This apparent inconsistency can be reconciled by recognizing that pseudo-second-order kinetics does not exclusively imply chemical bonding, but rather reflects the dependence of adsorption rate on the availability of active sites. In our system, adsorption is primarily driven by physical forces such as electrostatic attraction and hydrophobic interactions, complemented by surface complexation effects. These combined interactions yield kinetic behavior resembling chemisorption while maintaining thermodynamic characteristics consistent with physical adsorption. Thus, the adsorption process can be described as multifaceted, involving strong physical interactions with partial surface complexation contributions, as summarized in Table 7.

The thermodynamic aspects of the adsorption of PFOS, PFOA, methylene blue (MB), and rhodamine B (RhB) on CA–Fe₃O₄–NF nanofibers were investigated to evaluate the feasibility and energetic nature of the adsorption process. Temperature-dependent experiments were conducted at 25, 35, and 45 °C, and the thermodynamic parameters, including standard Gibbs free energy (ΔG°), enthalpy (ΔH°), and entropy (ΔS°), were calculated using the van’t Hoff approach based on the adsorption equilibrium data. The obtained ΔG° values further support the spontaneous and predominantly physical nature of the adsorption process, consistent with the dual-mechanism interpretation presented in Table 7.

Figure 12 shows gradual rise in adsorption capacity as the temperature increased in all the pollutants investigated and this implies that the adsorption process is endothermic. This tendency indicates that high temperatures can increase the mobility of molecules and activate diffusion of contaminant molecules into the mesoporous nanofiber network, increasing the number of sites of adsorption of organic substances.

Computed values of ΔG° of PFOS, PFOA, MB, and RhB were negative within the studied temperature span (Table 8); confirm the spontaneous nature of the adsorption process, with calculations performed according to the equations provided in the Supporting Information. In addition, an increment in temperature made the magnitude of ΔG° to be of negative quantity, which is another positive indication of the positive thermodynamic driving force at higher temperatures. The values of the absolute ΔG° (-17 to -24 kJ mol-1) show that the adsorption process is controlled by the strong physical interactions with the specific surface interactions but not the irreversible chemical bond. Intermediate values of ΔG°, which are typical of adsorption processes, are due to a concerted action of electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic and surface complexation.

All pollutants gave positive values of ΔH° which proved the endothermic nature of adsorption. The values of ΔH° and ΔS° were derived from the slope and intercept of the linear van’t Hoff plots (ln Kc versus 1/T), as described in the Supporting Information. The equilibrium constant (Kc) was calculated from the ratio of adsorbed to remaining pollutant concentrations at equilibrium, following the methodology outlined in the Supporting Information. The comparatively average ΔH° values (10–18 kJ mol-1) indicate that energy is needed to disrupt hydration layers around the particles of pollutants, and to facilitate the penetration of pollutants to mesoporous channels. This is in tandem with the BET analysis that indicated a mesoporous structure with a mean pore size of around 6.4 nm that are helpful in temperature-enhanced pore-filling processes.

The positive values of the ΔS° show that there is an increment in randomness of the solid–liquid interface during adsorption. The gain of this entropy is usually attributed to the movement of the structured water molecules out of the adsorbent surface and pollutant hydration shells during adsorption. When these water molecules are released into the bulk solution it results in the increased degrees of freedom and hence makes an overall contribution to the overall adsorption spontaneity. Overall, the thermodynamic analysis confirms that the adsorption of PFAS and organic dyes onto CA–Fe3O4–NF nanofibers is spontaneous, endothermic, and entropy-driven. These findings are consistent with a mechanism dominated by strong physical interactions, with possible contributions from surface complexation, supporting the dual-mechanism interpretation discussed above.

Reusability and stability

The reusability and stability in the operation of the CA-Fe3O4-NF nanofiber adsorbent were reviewed systematically using repeated adsorption–desorption cycles to determine the viability of the adsorbent in the treatment of wastewater. The regeneration of the saturated nanofibers was performed after every adsorption experiment with the solution of ethanol/0.1 M NaOH (1:1, v/v) then the saturated nanofibers were carefully washed and dried before being used again. The preservation of adsorption capacity and magnetic responsiveness over repeated cycles indicates that the Fe3O4 nanoparticles remain strongly anchored to the nanofiber framework and that the structural integrity of the CA– Fe3O4–NF adsorbent is maintained during adsorption and regeneration processes.

Figure 13 demonstrates that all pollutants under investigation gave an initial adsorption capacity that was still high on the CA-Fe3O4-NF nanofibers, at five consecutive cycles. The adsorption capacity after the fifth cycle was above 85–90% of the initial one, which is a very good reusability. The minor reduction in the adsorption performance of the used material with repeated cycles is explained by the partial blockage of active sites and slight surface fouling as opposed to the structural degradation. Moreover, it was also essential to note that the magnetic responsiveness of the nanofibers was not lost during the regeneration process, thus enabling their quick removal of treated solutions in seconds when an external magnetic field was applied. This aspect removes filtration or centrifugation which uses a lot of energy and greatly improves the efficiency of the adsorption process.

The post-cycle SEM micrographs show that the nanofibrous network remains largely intact, with fiber diameters similar to the fresh material and no significant agglomeration or detachment of Fe3O4 nanoparticles. The embedded Fe3O4 nanoparticles remain uniformly dispersed along the fibers, confirming that the chemical anchoring within the polymeric matrix is effective.

The Effect of pH, ionic strength, and wastewater matrices on adsorption performance

The adsorption performance of CA–Fe₃O₄ nanofibers is influenced by solution chemistry, including pH, ionic strength, and water matrix composition. The effect of pH was evaluated over a range of 3–10. Maximum adsorption efficiency was observed at near-neutral pH (7.0), where electrostatic attraction and hydrophobic interactions are favorably balanced. Under acidic conditions (pH 3), protonation of functional groups reduces the availability of active sites and weakens electrostatic interactions. In contrast, at alkaline pH (10), partial deprotonation may reduce binding affinity toward pollutant molecules.

The influence of ionic strength was assessed using monovalent (Na⁺) and divalent (Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺) ions. The presence of Na⁺ resulted in only a slight decrease in adsorption efficiency, whereas divalent cations caused a more pronounced reduction, likely due to electrostatic shielding and competition for active binding sites.

To further evaluate practical relevance, adsorption experiments were conducted using both treated and untreated wastewater samples. The nanofibers maintained relatively high removal efficiency in treated wastewater, while a moderate decline was observed in untreated samples, which can be attributed to the presence of natural organic matter and competing ions. These observations provide indirect evidence of competitive adsorption effects under more complex conditions, although the system remains less complex than real wastewater environments. These results provide preliminary insight into the material’s behavior under environmentally relevant conditions; however, they should be interpreted with caution due to the simplified nature of the experimental setup. Accordingly, the performance observed in this study may not fully represent behavior under highly complex multi-component systems, where additional interactions may occur.

Overall, these findings suggest that CA–Fe₃O₄ nanofibers exhibit stable adsorption performance under varying environmental conditions, highlighting their potential applicability in wastewater treatment. The detailed results are presented in Table 12.

Potential influence of natural organic matter on adsorption performance

Natural organic matter (NOM), particularly humic substances, is commonly present in real water systems and is known to influence adsorption processes. Although humic acid was not explicitly investigated in the present study, its potential impact can be reasonably inferred based on the observed behavior in real wastewater matrices.

In general, NOM can compete with target pollutants such as PFAS and dyes for active adsorption sites, leading to a reduction in adsorption efficiency. Additionally, humic substances may partially block pores or modify the surface properties of the adsorbent, thereby limiting accessibility to binding sites.

The slight decrease in removal efficiency observed in untreated wastewater (as shown in Table 13) may be partially attributed to the presence of natural organic matter and other competing species. Despite these potential interferences, the CA–Fe3O4–NF nanofibers maintained relatively high adsorption performance, indicating their strong affinity toward target contaminants even in complex environmental conditions.

Adsorption mechanism of CA–Fe₃O₄–NF nanofibers

The adsorption mechanism of PFOS, PFOA, methylene blue (MB), and rhodamine B (RhB) onto CA–Fe₃O₄–NF nanofibers is governed by a combination of surface chemistry, electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic affinity, and intra-particle diffusion within the mesoporous structure, as illustrated in Fig. 15. The proposed interaction pathways and structural changes are further supported by spectroscopic evidence presented in Fig. 16. The hybrid nanofiber architecture, derived from regenerated cotton–acrylic polymer chains with uniformly dispersed Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, provides multiple heterogeneous active sites that may simultaneously participate in pollutant uptake.

The surface functional groups identified by FTIR, including –OH, –C = O, and –C≡N, may undergo protonation or deprotonation depending on solution pH, thereby influencing electrostatic interactions with charged species. Under moderately acidic conditions, protonation of surface sites can enhance interactions with anionic PFAS molecules such as PFOS and PFOA. In addition, hydrophobic domains of the acrylic backbone may contribute to affinity toward fluorinated chains, enhancing non-electrostatic interactions.

The incorporation of Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles introduces additional active sites associated with Fe2⁺/Fe3⁺ species, which may contribute to ion–dipole interactions or surface complexation. For cationic dyes such as methylene blue, adsorption is mainly driven by electrostatic attraction with negatively charged surface sites at near-neutral to slightly alkaline pH conditions. For rhodamine B, adsorption is likely influenced by electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and π–π stacking between aromatic rings and polymeric domains.

The mesoporous structure (average pore diameter ~ 6.4 nm) facilitates diffusion and enhances accessibility of active sites, as summarized in Table 14. In addition, the reduced fiber diameter shortens diffusion pathways, which may contribute to the observed fast adsorption kinetics. Overall, the adsorption process is controlled by synergistic weak interactions rather than a single dominant mechanism.

Conclusion

This study presents a sustainable strategy for the valorization of cotton–acrylic textile waste into multifunctional magnetic nanofiber adsorbents (CA–Fe₃O₄–NF) for the removal of PFAS and organic dyes from aqueous systems. The combined sol–gel-assisted in situ co-precipitation and electrospinning approach enabled the formation of uniform nanofibers with a relatively high surface area (112 m2 g⁻1) and a mesoporous structure that facilitates mass transfer and adsorption efficiency. The developed material demonstrated effective adsorption performance toward PFOS, PFOA, methylene blue, and rhodamine B, with relatively fast kinetics and favorable equilibrium behavior. Thermodynamic analysis indicated that the adsorption process is spontaneous and endothermic, while reusability tests confirmed stable performance over multiple cycles with minimal loss of efficiency, supported by rapid magnetic separation. However, the present study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions using simplified aqueous systems. Although preliminary tests with mixed systems and real wastewater were performed, these conditions still do not fully capture the complexity of real environmental matrices. Therefore, factors such as competitive adsorption, natural organic matter, and variable ionic composition may significantly influence performance in practical applications. In addition, the proposed adsorption mechanisms are based on indirect experimental evidence and should be considered as plausible interpretations rather than definitive conclusions. Further investigation using advanced surface characterization techniques is required to provide deeper mechanistic insight. Long-term stability, including detailed evaluation of potential metal leaching and performance under continuous-flow conditions, also requires further study. These aspects are essential to bridge the gap between laboratory-scale performance and real-world application. Overall, the CA–Fe₃O₄–NF adsorbent offers a promising, cost-effective, and environmentally relevant approach for converting textile waste into functional materials for water treatment. Future work should prioritize system validation under complex multi-component conditions and pilot-scale studies to fully establish its practical applicability.
