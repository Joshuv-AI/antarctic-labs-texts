Neurodegenerative and inflammatory biomarkers are detectable in saliva in multiple sclerosis
Authors: B Castelli, S Shapoori, J M McMahon, K Das, A P Bocanegra-Lopez, S Moosavizadeh, B Counihan, A Pandit, Una FitzGerald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing incidence of multiple sclerosis (MS), a neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative disease, poses challenges for healthcare, as diagnosis and monitoring rely on expensive and/or invasive procedures, including MRI and lumbar puncture. Biomarkers in more accessible biofluids than CSF (serum, saliva, nasal secretion, tears) may aid early diagnosis and monitoring of progression and response to treatment. We investigated MS-associated biomarkers in accessible biofluids collected from 37 people living with MS and 23 healthy controls. Biomarkers analysed were neurofilament light chain (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), free light chains (κFLC and λFLC), chitinase-3-like 1 (CH3L1), chemokine-ligand-13 (CXCL13) and soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells (sTREM2). Linear regression analyses were used to determine mean differences between experimental groups and diagnostic accuracy of biomarkers assessed by generating receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. All biomarkers were measurable in both serum and saliva. All but CXCL13 and sTREM2 were detectable in nasal secretion samples, but none were detectable in tears. Levels of NfL (p = 0.03), κFLC (p = 0.003), and CHI3L1 (p = 0.021) were all significantly higher in the saliva samples of people with MS. Our findings suggest that these biomarkers are detectable in the proposed sample types, and, particularly in saliva, show potential in identifying inflammation and neurodegeneration in MS. Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS), an autoimmune disease with inflammatory and neurodegenerative components1, is diagnosed and monitored by imaging techniques, clinical and neurological evaluation, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers such as oligoclonal bands2. However, due to unpredictable pathology and increased awareness of disease progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA)3, there is an unmet need for non-imaging biomarkers4. Not only could they aid in early diagnosis, known to result in better disease outcomes, but, potentially, provide accessible, inexpensive, and less invasive means of monitoring disease course, progression, and drug response5.

In this preliminary study, we investigated the levels of seven MS-associated biomarkers, five of which were highlighted in a recent review in The Lancet6, in several easily accessible biofluids (saliva, nasal secretions, tears, and serum). Biomarkers were chosen based on research evidence and those whose CSF levels display potential diagnostic and prognostic use7–9, as follows: neurofilament light chain (NfL); glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP); κ free-light chains (κFLC) (whose presence in CSF has been included in the 2024 modifications of the McDonald Criteria10; λ free light chain (λFLC); chitinase-3-like1 (CHI3L1); chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 13 (CXCL13); soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (sTREM2). A summary table of candidate biomarkers can be found in Supplementary Table S1. The liver enzymes aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were also measured to monitor drug-induced hepatotoxicity in individuals receiving disease-modifying treatments (DMTs)11. A cohort of 37 people with MS (and 23 controls) provided samples and were tested for upper limb motor function using the 9-hole peg test (9-HPT).

Of particular interest was whether the biomarkers were detectable in these body fluids and whether their levels corresponded to changes in disease type or response to drug treatments. We were also interested in monitoring biomarker changes relative to one another, and if they had more diagnostic or prognostic use in combination. This preliminary study aimed to establish the feasibility of using accessible and inexpensive testing modalities to monitor people with MS and to add to the growing literature on non-imaging MS biomarkers.

Results

All seven biomarkers tested were detectable in serum and saliva, five out of seven were measurable in nasal secretions, and none were detectable in tears (Table S2 and Fig. S1). From this, we decided that serum and saliva were the most suitable for this study, and all further statistical analyses were performed on data from these body fluids.

Serum and salivary levels of biomarkers in MS and healthy controls

In serum, no biomarker was found to be significantly different between groups (Fig. 1a-e). However, biomarker measurement in saliva showed NfL to be significantly higher both in PMS and total MS groups compared to controls (p = 0.042, p = 0.03, Fig. 1f). Moreover, salivary κFLC was significantly higher in total MS (p = 0.003), RRMS (p = 0.006), and PPMS (p = 0.025) groups (Fig. 1g) and CHI3L1 was significantly higher in both total MS (p = 0.021) and PMS (p = 0.003) groups (Fig. 1j) compared to controls. None of the other biomarkers showed significant differences between the groups (Fig. 1b-e, h and i).

Correlation between different biomarkers in serum and saliva, in both MS and control cohorts

Correlation coefficients were calculated for each biomarker relative to every other biomarker (Figs. 2 and 3) in both serum and saliva. In serum, a positive correlation between GFAP and NfL was observed in control samples (R2 = 0.77, p < 0.0001) as well as in the PMS (R2 = 0.89, p < 0.0001) and total MS groups (R2 = 0.11, p = 0.038). A significant positive correlation was also found between κFLC and λFLC in both RRMS (R2 = 0.62, p < 0.0001) and total MS (R2 = 0.5, p < 0.0001) groups. Finally, NfL was positively correlated with κFLC in the PMS group (R2 = 0.4, p < 0.049).

Single and combined ROC analyses of serum and salivary biomarker levels for MS diagnosis

We employed ROC curves to determine the accuracy of each biomarker in distinguishing patients with MS from controls in both serum and saliva. Area under the curve (AUC) values above 0.7 were considered to indicate good accuracy, and high sensitivity or specificity was established for values above 0.8. Single ROC analysis (Fig. 4, Tables S3 and S4) did not determine any serum markers providing accurate separation between the two groups; however, in saliva, some promising results were obtained: κFLC accuracy was acceptable (AUC: 0.72), but both sensitivity and specificity were low (< 0.8); CHI3L1 accuracy was also considered acceptable (AUC: 0.70) and showed high sensitivity (0.87).

Hepatotoxicity measurement and motor function testing

To investigate liver function, ALT and AST levels were measured in serum and saliva. While there was no significant difference in ALT levels in either fluid, serum AST was significantly higher in the total MS and RRMS groups compared to control (P = 0.033 and p = 0.044, respectively) (Fig. 6a-d). Unexpectedly, there was no significant difference in liver enzyme levels between the DMTs and noDMTs subgroups. Assessment of upper limb motor function using the 9-Hole peg test resulted in significantly better function (lower scores) in controls than in the total MS (p = 0.039) and PMS groups (p = 0.0045), and, unsurprisingly, decreased function correlated with older age (p = 0.03) (Fig. 6g-h). Although 9-HPT scores were generally higher in patients with MS, no correlation was observed between these scores and different biomarker levels in either serum or saliva.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the presence and ease of detection of several MS biomarkers in serum, saliva, nasal secretions, and tear fluid samples. We report, for the first time, raised levels of NfL and CHI3L1 in saliva samples from people with MS compared to healthy controls. We also detected significantly higher levels of κFLC in MS saliva samples (total MS, RRMS and PMS) which concurs with previous studies reporting raised salivary levels of free light chains12,13. Although levels of sTREM2 and CXCL13 in saliva did not differ between MS and control groups, we believe we are the first to report on levels of these molecules in this body fluid in MS.

Our data from MS serum samples, although in agreement with previous studies regarding sTREM214,15, CXCL1316 and CHI3L117, showed some interesting differences in the cases of NfL and the FLCs (both k and λ). Previously, an increase in circulating NfL has been reported in people with MS18,19 but we did not see any significant difference in serum levels between groups. It is of note, however, that we used MSD technology in our measurements, instead of the more often reported SIMOA technology, and differences in sensitivity of NfL detection may account for this result. Furthermore, our findings on serum κFLC and λFLC, which contrast with previous studies20,21, may be due to detection of FLC by ELISA assay, rather than nephelometry. The latter is a widely used technique that has, nonetheless, been shown to be subject to inaccuracies22–25. This, coupled with the short half-life of FLC in serum (≈ 2–6 h) may contribute to the very variable results reported in the literature26.

When comparing individuals taking DMTs to those who were not on treatment at the time of sample collection, we found no differences between the levels of each marker in either serum or saliva. This differs from previously published studies investigating CSF, serum, or salivary levels of NfL, GFAP, FLCs, CXCL13, and CHI3L1 in MS patients on different treatment regimens. Lotan et al. found lower salivary FLCs in a DMT-MS group than in those not receiving treatment12 and other studies have demonstrated lower NfL, CXCL13 and CHI3L1 levels in CSF after treatment with different DMTs27,28. Additionally, DMTs were also found to affect levels of NfL29, GFAP30, and CXCL1331 in serum, and an increase in circulating CHI3L1 was seen after treatment with glatiramer acetate32. It is important to note that our MS cohort was not homogenous in terms of DMT type, which may have confounded the data.

Interestingly, a positive correlation was observed between the levels of serum NfL and GFAP, which is supported by existing literature33,34. This positive correlation was stronger in PMS samples, suggesting a link between these markers and disease progression, supporting the role of astrocytic activation in axonal damage. Moreover, within each biofluid (serum and saliva), κFLC and λFLC levels showed a notable positive correlation in both the total MS cohort and the RRMS group. Specific studies regarding the correlation of FLCs in body fluids are scarce, so more extensive research will be needed to corroborate our findings. NfL was negatively correlated with κFLC in the serum of people with PMS. We could not find any studies investigating the relationship between these two markers in serum. However, when considering κFLC index, Castillo-Villalba et al. found no correlation between it and NfL in CSF samples from an MS cohort35. Further analysis of saliva samples revealed a strong correlation between λFLC and CHI3L1 throughout our cohort, as well as with CXCL13 in samples from patients with PMS. These results possibly underline the relationship between immune activity, inflammation, and B cell recruitment in MS pathology. However, to our knowledge, no previous studies have examined this relationship.

Another finding was the positive correlation between GFAP and λFLC, CHI3L1, and CXCL13 in the saliva of people with PMS. Although specific studies correlating these markers in the saliva of MS patients are lacking, Abdelhak et al. found no correlation between CSF GFAP and CHI3L134. In contrast, our findings suggest that GFAP and CHI3L1 could both be involved in glial activation in MS, although additional analysis is required to corroborate this theory.

ROC analysis results for single and combined biomarkers varied. No single marker in serum was found to have high diagnostic accuracy in differentiating MS patients from controls. These results seem to contradict the findings of Koerbel et al.36, which could partially be explained by the different detection methodologies employed. In saliva, κFLC and CHI3L1 showed acceptable accuracy but low sensitivity and specificity. Overall, although individual markers had limited accuracy, combining markers improved diagnostic performance in specific cases. GFAP/CHI3L1 in serum showed improved accuracy and retained high sensitivity. In saliva, κFLC combined with GFAP, λFLC, sTREM2, CHI3L1 and CXCL13 improved accuracy and showed various degrees of sensitivity and specificity. Salivary NfL/CHI3L1 and GFAP/CHI3L1 also showed good accuracy. However promising, further analysis is required to confirm these results.

A secondary aim of this study was to evaluate whether saliva could serve as a non-invasive alternative for monitoring liver function. Acute liver failure has been recorded in people with MS using beta-interferon, fingolimod, natalizumab, alemtuzumab, and ocrelizumab due to mechanisms such as idiosyncratic reactions, autoimmune hepatitis, or reactivation of Hepatitis B virus (HBV) and Hepatitis C virus (HCV)37. While the levels of saliva AST and ALT tended to be higher in people with MS than in the control, we observed no significant differences. In serum samples, the only significant increase was observed in AST levels in the RRMS and total MS groups compared to the control group. Although there were no significant differences in serum or saliva ALT and AST levels between people with MS taking DMTs and those not on treatment, ALT levels tended to be higher in the DMTs group than in the other groups. Previous research has reported significant differences in the levels of liver enzymes following treatment with different molecules38.

The final objective of this study was to evaluate the motor functions of our MS cohort. Hindered upper limb mobility is a common trait among patients with MS. The 9-HPT has been used for several years for assessing hand dexterity, monitoring disease progression, and response to treatment in MS patients39. Our data showed a significant difference in 9-HPT scores in both the total MS and PMS groups compared to healthy controls. We also found a significant correlation between higher time scores and older age. These results endorse the adoption of the 9-HPT in clinical and research settings as a standard measure of upper limb function in this population. However, age should be considered when performing these tests.

Some limitations of this study should also be acknowledged. A relatively small sample size was used which could limit the power of the statistical analysis in detecting subtle group differences and the generalisability of the findings. Moreover, the study was cross-sectional in design, which prevented analysis of biomarker dynamics over time and full determination of relationship to progression. Another relevant limitation of the present study is the absence of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) measurements. For some molecules, CSF/serum ratios provide important information regarding their site of production and the relative contribution of central versus peripheral sources. Since the easily accessible body fluid samples analysed for this study were donated in a non-clinical setting, it was not possible to include CSF sample and, hence, directly assess the relationship between central and peripheral compartments.

However, all molecules included in our panel have been extensively characterized in CSF, particularly in individuals with MS, where their association with neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative has been well established. The primary objective of the present study was not to determine the intrathecal origin of these biomarkers, but rather to evaluate the feasibility of detecting and quantifying established MS-related biomarkers in minimally invasive body fluids, especially saliva, using commercially available assays. Biomarker levels measured in peripheral fluids are to be interpreted as indicators of a broader disease-associated inflammatory or pathological state that may still allow discrimination between patients and controls. These findings provide a foundation for future studies directly comparing CSF and saliva samples to further explain the relationship between CNS and peripheral biomarker profiles.

The heterogeneity both of patient groups (our cohort was a mix of people with RRMS and PMS) and the DMTs they were receiving may have introduced variability that could affect results and prevent clear comparisons with untreated individuals. Given the exploratory nature of this study, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied; therefore, the results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Finally, adjustment of results to account for confounding factors was possible only in the case of age and gender. Correction based on BMI data would have been useful since it is known that NfL levels, in particular, can be influenced by a number of factors, including BMI, renal insufficiency and neurotoxic drug effects40.

Our preliminary results offer enough evidence to support the exploration of alternative body fluids for biomarker research in the context of MS. Of particular interest, is our detection of raised CHI3L1 in saliva of people with MS, in the absence of any corresponding change in serum levels. This is very reminiscent of the findings of Floro et al.17 who, in a systematic review on MS, reported MS-associated raised CHI3L1 levels in CSF in the absence of changes in serum. This highlights the potential for saliva to be used in place of CSF, as a non-invasive tool for monitoring disease course in MS. Sampling of this body fluid requires no specialised training, can be done with very little expense and can be performed even multiple times daily, without any ill-effects for the patient. Nevertheless, these findings are to be interpreted as exploratory given the cross-sectional design and small subgroups, but they support future longitudinal studies on biomarker roles in prognosis, progression, and treatment response.

Notably, serum NfL and GFAP, are increasingly being investigated as longitudinal blood-based markers of disease activity, prognosis, and therapeutic response in MS, and are expected to enter routine clinical practice as minimally invasive monitoring tools. In this context, exploring their detectability and relevance in saliva is particularly timely, as it may further enhance accessibility and feasibility of repeated monitoring41–44. Having seen NfL, kFLC and CHI3L1 being measurable and all raised in the saliva of people with MS, we suggest their salivary levels could be potentially useful as non-invasive biomarkers for use in monitoring progression and therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, we believe that positive correlations between salivary biomarkers e.g. GFAP and CHI3L1 may help establish a foundation for developing multiplexed, rapid testing approaches. In conclusion, this study introduces a novel battery of biomarkers to investigate MS pathology and assess their potential in monitoring MS progression. The proposed analysis utilises serum and saliva, both readily accessible body fluids that can be sampled by minimally invasive means. Moreover, further longitudinal studies on more homogenous cohorts are warranted to validate the significance of our findings. Such studies will help confirm the ability of the proposed salivary biomarkers in differentiating MS from controls, as well as in monitoring disease progression.

Sample collection

For each participant, all sample collection was carried out in a single session, before midday, as follows:

Blood (5 ml) was collected into BD Vacutainer™ SST™ II Advance Tubes, without anticoagulant, (BD367953, Becton Dickinson, USA). After clotting, tubes were centrifuged at 1500 g for 10 min, and serum aspirated. Saliva, nasal secretions and tears were collected using salivette tubes (51.1534, Sarstedt, Germany), Nasosorption™ synthetic absorptive matrix (SAM) (NSFL-FXI-11, Mucosal Diagnostics, UK) and Schirmer strips (MIPL/A1/98, Madhu Instruments, India), respectively. Further information on collection and storage of saliva and nasal secretions is provided in Supplementary information . All samples were taken according to manufacturers’ instructions, aliquoted and stored at -70 °C.

Biomarker measurements

NfL and GFAP were measured in all (undiluted) samples using ultra-sensitive MesoScale Discovery (MSD) technology (S-PLEX Custom Human Neurology panel, K151ANV-1, MSD, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All other biomarkers were detected in the samples using commercially available ELISA kits [κFLC and λFLC (RD194088100R, Biovendor, Czech Republic); CHI3L1 (KE00239, Proteintech, UK); CXCL13 (DCX130, Bio-techne, USA); sTREM2 (EH464RB0, Invitrogen, USA); ALT (NBP3-18696, Bio-techne USA); AST (MBS700831-96, MyBioSource, USA)]. Sample dilutions were optimised for each body fluid type and assay (Table S2). Assay Limit of detection (LOD), Coefficient of Variability (CV) and Recovery, are summarised in Table S5.

9-Hole Peg Test (9HPT)

Motor-test function testing was carried out during the same session at which samples were collected. Participants were given all necessary instructions on how to perform the 9-HPT (091065259, Physio Needs, Ireland) and each participant given a practice run. Volunteers started the 9-HPT with their dominant hand and repeated it using the non-dominant hand. The scores were based on the time taken to complete each run.

Statistical analyses

Values below the limit of detection (LOD) were excluded from statistical analysis, as these were considered to be unreliable. The measured values of all biomarkers were log-transformed to ensure that the assumption of normality was met. To estimate the mean differences in individual biomarkers between the MS and control groups, linear regression was fit for each biomarker and separate linear regressions were fit to estimate mean differences between control and both MS subgroups (RRMS and PMS). Mean difference in individual biomarkers between DMT and no-DMT subgroups within the MS population, was also estimated using linear regression. All estimates of mean differences were obtained by adjusting for the effects of age and sex. To assess diagnostic accuracy of each biomarker in discriminating MS from control, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated by obtaining the predicted probability of the MS group using logistic regression for each biomarker and a combination of two biomarkers. Cutoff values were determined at the points on the ROC curves where Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1) was the highest to maximise the sensitivity and specificity of the diagnostic tests. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.
