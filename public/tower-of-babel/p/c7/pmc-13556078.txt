Chinese EFL learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy: a structural equation modeling study of L2 selves, foreign language anxiety, and speaking self-efficacy
Authors: Xiaohu Huang, Pan Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLearners’ self-evaluations of pronunciation are closely related to their emotional and motivational experiences in second language learning. However, relatively little is known about how L2 motivational selves, foreign language anxiety, and speaking self-efficacy are jointly associated with learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy.MethodsThis cross-sectional study surveyed first-year English and English-related majors at two universities in western China. Classroom-based foreign language anxiety, ideal and ought-to L2 selves, speaking self-efficacy, and self-perceived pronunciation accuracy were measured using an online questionnaire. Confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modelling were conducted in R. Alternative full and partial mediation models were compared, indirect effects were tested with bootstrapping, and the hypothesized moderation effect of speaking self-efficacy was examined using latent product-indicator models.ResultsThe final structural model showed that the ideal L2 self was negatively associated with foreign language anxiety, whereas the ought-to L2 self was positively associated with anxiety. Foreign language anxiety was negatively associated with self-perceived pronunciation accuracy and statistically mediated the associations between both L2 selves and self-perceived pronunciation accuracy. Speaking self-efficacy was positively associated with self-perceived pronunciation accuracy. However, speaking self-efficacy did not significantly moderate the association between foreign language anxiety and self-perceived pronunciation accuracy. Additional model comparisons showed that direct paths from the L2 selves to self-perceived pronunciation accuracy were not significant.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that Chinese EFL learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy is associated with both classroom-based anxiety and speaking self-efficacy, and that anxiety may represent an important statistical link between L2 self-guides and pronunciation-related self-evaluations. The results also indicate that general speaking self-efficacy may be more directly associated with self-perceived pronunciation accuracy than with weakening the FLA–SPP association. Future research should incorporate pronunciation-specific anxiety and efficacy measures, objective pronunciation assessments, and longitudinal designs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05070-8.Keywords: Foreign language anxiety, Self-perceived pronunciation accuracy, L2 Motivational Self System, Speaking self-efficacy, Chinese EFL learners

Background

Introduction

Affective variables have long been recognized as core issues in second language (L2) learning, especially in performance-based skills such as speaking and pronunciation. Building on Krashen’s affective filter hypothesis, anxiety has been conceptualized as a key factor that may block the intake of comprehensible input and constrain language output when learners feel threatened or evaluated [17, 19]. Meta-analyses consistently show a moderate negative association between foreign language anxiety (FLA) and achievement across different language skills [5, 35]. Within this broader pattern, pronunciation-related tasks appear particularly “vulnerable” because they highlight learners’ “audible identity”. Research on pronunciation and accent anxiety suggests that worries about sounding non-native, fear of negative evaluation, and low pronunciation self-perception may be associated with reduced learners’ willingness to speak and their sense of competence [2, 8, 41]. Empirical studies of oral presentations and classroom speaking tasks have shown that a higher level of speaking or pronunciation anxiety is associated with lower self-perceived pronunciation and reduced teacher-rated speaking proficiency [36]. Additionally, speaking anxiety tends to correlate negatively with both speaking self-efficacy and self-perceived oral proficiency [22]. These findings emphasize that learners’ emotional reactions to pronunciation are closely tied to how they evaluate their own pronunciation competence.

Anxiety, however, is embedded in a broader network of motivational and self-belief constructs. Within Dörnyei’s [11] L2 Motivational Self System (L2MSS), the Ideal L2 Self captures learners’ desired image of themselves as competent L2 users, whereas the Ought-to L2 Self reflects perceived external duties and expectations. Structural equation modelling studies indicate that ideal L2 self guides are typically associated with higher enjoyment and lower anxiety, while certain ought-to L2 self-guides are associated with heightened anxiety and more vigilant, obligation-driven language use [23, 24]. Relatedly, research in positive psychology has shown that foreign language enjoyment and anxiety jointly shape the clarity and strength of learners’ ideal L2 self [14]. Another critical construct is self-efficacy, defined as individuals’ belief in their capabilities to organize and execute actions required to attain specific performances [1]. In L2 contexts, higher self-efficacy has often been linked to lower anxiety, more persistent engagement, and better language outcomes, although evidence for its buffering or moderating role remains limited and sometimes mixed, particularly in speaking-related domains [40].

Taken together, these strands suggest that learners’ pronunciation-related self-evaluations may be associated with a broader network of motivational, affective, and efficacy beliefs. However, little is known about how L2 self-guides, classroom-based foreign language anxiety, and speaking self-efficacy are jointly related to learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy, especially in the Chinese university EFL context. Most existing studies have focused on overall speaking proficiency, global achievement, or pronunciation anxiety, whereas fewer have examined learners’ perceived accuracy of specific segmental and suprasegmental pronunciation features as a distinct outcome.

It should be noted that the present study does not measure pronunciation-specific anxiety or pronunciation-specific self-efficacy. Instead, FLA is operationalized as classroom-based foreign language anxiety, and self-efficacy is operationalized as English speaking self-efficacy. Thus, the study examines whether these broader affective and speaking-related self-beliefs are associated with a narrower pronunciation-specific outcome, namely learners’ self-perceived accuracy of key English pronunciation features. Against this background, the present study draws on Krashen’s affective filter hypothesis, the L2 Motivational Self System, and self-efficacy theory to test a structural model in which L2 selves are associated with FLA, FLA and speaking self-efficacy are associated with self-perceived pronunciation accuracy, and the hypothesized moderating role of speaking self-efficacy in the FLA–SPP association is explored.

Literature review

FLA

FLA, a well-established affective factor in second language learning, is defined by Horwitz et al., [17] as “a distinct complex of self-perceptions, beliefs, feelings, and behaviors related to classroom language learning arising from the uniqueness of the language learning process.” In the present study, it is understood in line with work on foreign language classroom anxiety, that is, anxiety experienced in classroom-based language learning. For simplicity, the term FLA is used in the literature review, while in the empirical part it is operationalized through a classroom-based FLCA measure. As a situation-specific form of anxiety, FLA encompasses communication apprehension, test anxiety, and the fear of negative evaluation in the L2 classroom. Decades of research have consistently shown that FLA has been associated with lower language performance and achievement. Learners with a high level of anxiety often exhibit poorer speaking skills and overall proficiency, as anxiety may interfere with cognitive processing and may be linked to avoidance of communicative opportunities.

Importantly, FLA is related not only to performance outcomes but also to learners’ self-appraisals and confidence. Wang et al., [38] observe that anxiety may be accompanied by nervousness that weakens individuals’ beliefs about their capabilities. Similarly, Phillips [26], found that even if FLA only moderately lowers test scores, it can have a stronger impact on students’ attitudes and self-perceptions related to language learning. Such negative self-perceptions can create a vicious cycle: anxiety may be accompanied by lower self-confidence in pronunciation, which in turn feeds further anxiety about speaking. Baran-Łucarz [2], showed that learners who felt anxious about pronunciation (often due to fear of mispronunciation or negative judgments) had significantly lower self-rated pronunciation competence. In that study, pronunciation anxiety – conceptualized partly as low pronunciation self-perception – correlated strongly with unwillingness to communicate (r = –.60), indicating that those who doubted their pronunciation abilities were far less likely to speak up. Likewise, Clément et al., [7] work on L2 communication confidence demonstrated that low-anxiety learners develop a higher self-perceived communicative competence, whereas more anxious learners have diminished self-perceptions and withdraw from speaking. Overall, current research emphasizes that FLA may be linked to lower confidence in how learners sound in the target language and high-anxiety learners tend to perceive their pronunciation ability pessimistically.

Recent studies have further examined pronunciation- and accent-related anxiety more directly. Baran-Łucarz and Lee [3], identified several correlates of pronunciation anxiety, including willingness to communicate, enjoyment, and self-perceived proficiency, while Erdel [13] found that English-major students reported mild-to-moderate pronunciation anxiety and that beliefs about pronunciation as a communicative skill were especially salient. Accent anxiety research has also shown that learners’ concerns about non-native accents often involve fear of negative evaluation, possible communication problems, and concerns about social positioning [8, 41]. These recent studies support the need to distinguish general classroom-based FLA from pronunciation- or accent-specific anxiety, a distinction that is central to the present study.

L2MSS

Dörnyei’s L2 Motivational Self System provides a contemporary framework connecting language learning motivation with learner self-concept [12]. It posits three components: the ideal L2 self (one’s hoped-for L2 proficient self in the future), the ought-to L2 self (attributes one believes one should have to meet others’ expectations), and the L2 learning experience (situational motives). The ideal L2 self represents personal desires and internal aspirations (e.g. becoming a fluent speaker who can connect with international friends), whereas the ought-to L2 self reflects perceived duties or obligations imposed by external sources such as parents, teachers, or societal norms. Since its introduction [12], this model has been widely applied across cultures and linked to a range of affective variables. Notably, recent studies have explored how these self-guides relate to language learning emotions, marking a shift toward integrating motivation with anxiety and other affective factors.

A robust finding about L2MSS is that the ideal L2 self and ought-to L2 self have opposite relationships with anxiety. Learners with a strong ideal L2 self—who vividly imagine themselves as successful L2 users—tend to experience less anxiety. Papi’s [23] seminal structural equation model was among the first to confirm this pattern: Iranian students’ ideal L2 selves had a significant negative effect on English classroom anxiety, whereas their ought-to selves were positively associated with anxiety. In Papi’s model, the ideal self was linked to a more positive, intrinsically motivated orientation, whereas the ought-to self was associated with concerns about meeting expectations. Subsequent research in diverse contexts has replicated these trends. For example, a large-scale study by Teimouri [34] found that students’ ideal L2 self was associated with positive emotions (e.g. enjoyment) and lower anxiety, whereas the ought-to L2 self correlated with heightened anxiety and a prevention-focused attitude toward learning.

These different emotional profiles have also been linked to different behavioral orientations, with ideal self-guides generally associated with more approach-oriented L2 use and ought-to self-guides more closely related to pressure-driven or defensive orientations [25].

In pronunciation learning, L2 self-guides may also be relevant because learners often imagine not only what they will be able to do in English, but also how they will sound when using the language. An ideal L2 self may include images of becoming an intelligible and confident English speaker, whereas an ought-to L2 self may involve pressure to sound accurate, standard, or acceptable to teachers, peers, or imagined audiences. These pronunciation-related aspects of future self-guides provide a rationale for examining how L2 selves are associated with learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy, particularly through classroom-based anxiety. This link has begun to receive empirical support in L2 speech research. For example, Zhou and Papi [42] found that future L2 selves were related to learners’ development of L2 speech comprehensibility and accentedness in an instructional setting, suggesting that motivational self-guides may be relevant to how learners engage with opportunities for speech and pronunciation development.

Self-efficacy in L2 learning

Unlike global self-esteem, self-efficacy is task-specific and grounded in past experiences of success or failure. In foreign language learning, it is typically reflected in learners’ confidence in carrying out L2 tasks, such as giving a presentation or pronouncing new words. A growing body of research indicates that self-efficacy is a robust predictor of language learning behavior and achievement. Mills et al., [21] reported that French learners’ self-efficacy beliefs significantly predicted their listening and reading proficiency even after controlling for anxiety, and more recent work has similarly reported that students with stronger speaking self-efficacy tend to achieve better speaking performance and participate more actively (e.g., Batu [4], . These findings are consistent with broader educational research showing that high self-efficacy supports engagement and resilience, whereas low self-efficacy is associated with avoidance and withdrawal.

Self-efficacy and anxiety are also closely intertwined. Across various contexts, studies have found a negative association between self-efficacy and FLA: learners who trust their L2 abilities generally report lower anxiety, while highly anxious learners tend to report low confidence (e.g [27, 43]), . This pattern suggests that self-efficacy may play a protective role, potentially weakening the impact of anxiety on learning outcomes. At the same time, self-efficacy is also closely related to learners’ self-perceptions. Learners who believe “I can do this” are more likely to evaluate their performance favorably and to interpret setbacks as manageable. For instance, Campana [6], found that students who felt efficacious about communicating were more likely to believe they could develop good pronunciation and succeed in class, and Batu [4], reported that learners with higher speaking efficacy not only performed better but were also more satisfied with their spoken English progress. Taken together, these findings indicate that self-efficacy and self-perception may be mutually supportive: success builds confidence, and confidence supports a more positive appraisal of one’s L2 skills.

Recent learner-profile and network studies in EFL contexts have also treated self-efficacy, anxiety, engagement, emotions, and self-regulated learning as interconnected learner variables rather than isolated predictors [32, 39]. Although these studies did not focus on pronunciation, they reinforce the rationale for examining anxiety and efficacy beliefs jointly in EFL learning.

Self-perceived pronunciation accuracy

Self-perception of pronunciation (SPP) broadly refers to learners’ subjective evaluations of how they sound in an L2. Previous work has discussed SPP in relation to perceived pronunciation quality, accent acceptability, communicative confidence, and learners’ “audible identity” [2, 8, 41]. In this broader sense, SPP may form part of learners’ phonological self-concept and may be intertwined with their affective responses to speaking, their accent-related beliefs, and their imagined identities as L2 users.

In the present study, however, SPP is operationalized more narrowly as learners’ self-perceived accuracy of key segmental and suprasegmental pronunciation features. Specifically, the self-developed SPP scale asks learners to evaluate the perceived accuracy of their English monophthongs, diphthongs, consonants, word stress, sentence stress, and intonation. This accuracy-based operationalization does not capture all dimensions of pronunciation self-perception, such as accent identity, perceived comprehensibility, social acceptability, or listeners’ evaluations. Rather, it focuses on learners’ own judgments of pronunciation accuracy in features commonly emphasized in English pronunciation instruction for Chinese EFL learners.

This narrower operationalization is pedagogically meaningful because first-year English majors in China often receive pronunciation instruction that foregrounds segmental accuracy and basic suprasegmental control. Learners’ perceived accuracy in these areas may influence how confident they feel when speaking English and how they evaluate their progress in pronunciation learning. At the same time, because the present measure focuses on perceived accuracy rather than actual pronunciation performance, SPP in this study should be interpreted as a self-reported pronunciation-related belief rather than as an objective indicator of pronunciation competence.

Existing research suggests that learners’ pronunciation-related self-evaluations are associated with anxiety, confidence, and motivational self-guides. Learners who feel anxious about speaking or pronunciation often report less favorable views of their pronunciation, whereas those with stronger confidence in speaking tend to evaluate their oral abilities more positively. In L2 motivational terms, learners’ imagined future L2 selves may also be linked to how they evaluate their current pronunciation, although such links may operate indirectly through affective experiences such as anxiety. The present study therefore treats SPP as a pronunciation-specific self-evaluative outcome and examines how it is statistically associated with broader motivational, affective, and efficacy-related constructs.

Relationships among FLA, L2MSS, self-efficacy, and SPP

Although the present study focuses on learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy, the affective and efficacy constructs examined here are broader in scope. FLA is operationalized as classroom-based foreign language anxiety, and self-efficacy is operationalized as English speaking self-efficacy. Thus, the following hypotheses concern how these broader motivational, affective, and speaking-related beliefs are statistically associated with learners’ perceived accuracy of key pronunciation features.

FLA may be relevant to SPP because pronunciation is commonly performed and evaluated in classroom speaking situations. Learners who feel anxious in the L2 classroom may be more sensitive to perceived mistakes, negative evaluation, or their own audible performance, which may be reflected in less favorable pronunciation self-evaluations. Previous research has also linked language anxiety with lower perceived communicative competence and more negative speaking-related self-beliefs. Therefore, the present study proposes:

H1: FLA is negatively associated with learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy.

The L2 Motivational Self System suggests that different future self-guides may be associated with different emotional experiences in language learning. A strong ideal L2 self reflects personally valued aspirations and may be linked to lower anxiety, whereas a strong ought-to L2 self reflects perceived duties or external expectations and may be linked to greater evaluative pressure. Prior studies have generally found that the ideal L2 self is negatively related to language anxiety, while the ought-to L2 self is positively related to anxiety. Accordingly, this study proposes:

H2: The ideal L2 self is negatively associated with FLA, whereas the ought-to L2 self is positively associated with FLA.

H2: The ideal L2 self is negatively associated with FLA, whereas the ought-to L2 self is positively associated with FLA.

FLA may also statistically mediate the associations between L2 selves and SPP. Learners’ future self-guides may be related to how anxious they feel in classroom language learning, and this anxiety may in turn be associated with how they evaluate their pronunciation accuracy. However, because the present study uses cross-sectional data, this mediation is understood as statistical and associational rather than as evidence of a temporal or causal process. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3: FLA statistically mediates the associations between L2 selves and learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy.

H3: FLA statistically mediates the associations between L2 selves and learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy.

Speaking self-efficacy may also be positively associated with SPP. Although the measure used in this study captures confidence in English speaking tasks rather than pronunciation-specific efficacy, pronunciation is embedded in spoken language use. Learners who feel more capable of performing English speaking tasks may therefore evaluate their pronunciation accuracy more favorably. This leads to the fourth hypothesis:

H4: Speaking self-efficacy is positively associated with learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy.

H4: Speaking self-efficacy is positively associated with learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy.

Finally, speaking self-efficacy may moderate the association between FLA and SPP. Learners with stronger speaking self-efficacy may be less likely to evaluate their pronunciation negatively when they experience anxiety, whereas learners with weaker efficacy beliefs may be more vulnerable to anxiety-related negative self-evaluations. Nevertheless, because the efficacy measure is not pronunciation-specific, this moderation hypothesis is tested cautiously as an extension of the main structural model. It was examined as a moderator because self-efficacy theory suggests that efficacy beliefs may condition how learners appraise and respond to anxiety-provoking situations, while its direct association with SPP was already tested in H4.

H5: Speaking self-efficacy moderates the association between FLA and learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy, such that the negative association is weaker among learners with higher speaking self-efficacy.

Methodology

Participants and contexts

This cross-sectional study was conducted at two universities in western China where English is taught as a foreign language. Both universities offer four-year undergraduate programs in English and English-related majors, including English teacher education and translation. The participants were first-year students in these programs. This group was selected because first-year English majors commonly receive formal instruction in English phonetics and pronunciation, making pronunciation-related self-evaluations particularly relevant at this stage of study.

A total of 458 questionnaires were initially returned. After excluding duplicate responses, responses with extremely short completion times, failed attention-check responses, or invariant response patterns, 437 valid cases were retained for analysis. The final sample included 61 male and 376 female students, reflecting the gender distribution commonly observed in English and teacher-education programs in China. On average, participants had studied English for 9.72 years (SD = 2.17). Although participants were English or English-related majors with broadly comparable educational backgrounds, no objective measure of English proficiency or pronunciation competence was collected.

Instruments

The questionnaire included previously published measures of classroom-based foreign language anxiety, L2 motivational selves, and English speaking self-efficacy, together with a self-developed scale of self-perceived pronunciation accuracy. Specifically, FLA was measured using items from Dewaele and MacIntyre’s [10] FLCA scale, L2 motivational selves were measured using items adapted from Papi’s [23] L2MSS questionnaire, and speaking self-efficacy was measured using items from the speaking subscale of the Questionnaire of English Self-Efficacy (QESE; [37]). The SPP scale was developed for the present study. All items were presented in Chinese to ensure clarity and comprehensibility for the participants. Unless otherwise stated, responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

FLCA scale

FLA was measured with eight items from the classroom-based FLCA scale developed by Dewaele and MacIntyre [10]. The items assessed anxiety experienced in foreign language classroom situations, such as feeling anxious even when well prepared for class. Two negatively worded items were reverse coded. Higher scores indicated higher levels of classroom-based foreign language anxiety. It should be noted that this measure assessed general classroom FLA rather than pronunciation-specific anxiety.

L2MSS scale

The ideal L2 self and ought-to L2 self were measured with items adapted from Papi’s [23] L2 Motivational Self System questionnaire. The ideal L2 self scale assessed learners’ desired images of themselves as future L2 users, whereas the ought-to L2 self scale assessed perceived duties and expectations related to English learning. Six items were initially included for each construct. In the CFA, one ought-to L2 self item showed a standardized loading below 0.50 and was removed; therefore, the final analyses used five ought-to L2 self items.

Self-efficacy scale

English speaking self-efficacy was assessed using seven items adapted from the speaking subscale of the Questionnaire of English Self-Efficacy (QESE; [37]). The items measured learners’ confidence in performing English speaking tasks, such as telling a story or expressing ideas orally in English. Higher scores indicated stronger English speaking self-efficacy. This scale assessed speaking self-efficacy rather than pronunciation-specific self-efficacy.

SPP scale

Self-perceived pronunciation accuracy was measured with a six-item scale developed for the present study. The items were designed to assess learners’ perceived accuracy in key segmental and suprasegmental features of English pronunciation: monophthongs, diphthongs, consonants, word stress, sentence stress, and intonation. The initial item pool was developed in Chinese based on prior work on pronunciation self-perception and English pronunciation instruction. Two EFL pronunciation instructors with more than 15 years of teaching experience reviewed the items for clarity and relevance to first-year Chinese university students, and minor wording revisions were made based on their feedback.

In the analyses, the six items were treated as indicators of a single latent SPP factor. This operationalization captures learners’ perceived accuracy of selected pronunciation features rather than broader dimensions of pronunciation self-perception, such as accent identity, social acceptability, comprehensibility, or listener-rated pronunciation quality. Higher scores indicated more positive self-perceived pronunciation accuracy. The English version of the SPP items is provided in the supplementary material.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected through an online questionnaire hosted on Wenjuanxing. Course teachers at the two universities shared the survey link with eligible students and briefly introduced the study as a project on English learning experiences and self-perceptions. Before beginning the questionnaire, participants read an informed consent statement explaining the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the anonymity and confidentiality of responses, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Only those who provided informed consent proceeded to the questionnaire. The survey took 15–20 min to complete. No monetary reward or course credit was provided for participation. The Academic Research Ethics Committee of the host university approved the study.

Data analysis

Data were screened and analyzed in R (R Core Team [9]), using the lavaan [29] package. Descriptive statistics, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, and Pearson correlations were first computed for the composite scores of FLA, ideal L2 self, ought-to L2 self, speaking self-efficacy, and SPP. Because the SPP scale was developed for this study, preliminary item-level diagnostics were conducted, including inter-item correlations and a descriptive one-factor analysis. This exploratory analysis was treated as descriptive rather than as independent confirmation of the scale structure because the same sample was also used in the CFA.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was then conducted to evaluate the five-factor measurement model. Reliability and validity were examined using standardized factor loadings, Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), the Fornell–Larcker criterion [15], and HTMT ratios [16]. Guided by modification indices and item content, four theoretically interpretable within-factor residual covariances were freed in the respecified CFA model: IDL1–IDL2, EFC1–EFC2, EFC6–EFC7, and SPP4–SPP5. These modifications were treated as exploratory and restricted to item pairs within the same construct.

Structural equation modelling was used to test the hypothesized associations among L2 selves, FLA, speaking self-efficacy, and SPP. To examine whether full mediation had been imposed rather than tested, the hypothesized full mediation model was compared with an alternative partial mediation model that added direct paths from the ideal and ought-to L2 selves to SPP. Model fit was evaluated using χ², CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR, AIC, and BIC. Indirect effects were tested using bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples. In the structural models, the ideal and ought-to L2 selves were specified as predictors of FLA, while FLA and speaking self-efficacy were specified as predictors of SPP. The ideal L2 self, ought-to L2 self, and speaking self-efficacy were treated as exogenous latent variables and were allowed to covary. Speaking self-efficacy was not specified as a predictor of FLA in the main model because the hypothesized role of self-efficacy concerned its direct association with SPP and its potential moderation of the FLA–SPP association.

The hypothesized moderating effect of speaking self-efficacy on the FLA–SPP association was tested using latent product-indicator models. The original matched-pair product-indicator approach was supplemented with an all-pair residual-centered product-indicator sensitivity analysis, following Little et al., [20], and a composite-score regression interaction check. Because all constructs were measured using a single self-report questionnaire, common method bias was examined using a one-factor CFA and a common latent factor diagnostic model [28].

Measurement model

A five-factor CFA was conducted for FLA, ideal L2 self, ought-to L2 self, speaking self-efficacy, and SPP. One item from the ought-to L2 self scale showed a standardized factor loading below 0.50 and was removed; the final ought-to L2 self factor was therefore estimated using five items. The initial five-factor model showed marginal fit, χ²(454) = 1394.62, CFI = 0.906, TLI = 0.897, RMSEA = 0.069, SRMR = 0.049. Guided by modification indices and item content, four within-factor residual covariances were freed: IDL1–IDL2, EFC1–EFC2, EFC6–EFC7, and SPP4–SPP5. These residual covariances were restricted to item pairs within the same construct and were treated as exploratory adjustments.

The respecified measurement model showed improved and acceptable fit, χ²(450) = 1034.65, CFI = 0.941, TLI = 0.935, RMSEA = 0.055, SRMR = 0.049. Standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.577 to 0.953 across the five constructs. The largest HTMT value was 0.649, below the 0.85 threshold, supporting discriminant validity. The high reliability and AVE of the SPP factor, together with its high inter-item correlations, suggest that the scale captured a relatively narrow perceived segmental/suprasegmental pronunciation accuracy factor.

Structural model comparison

Before testing the final structural paths, we compared the hypothesized full mediation model with an alternative partial mediation model. The partial mediation model added direct paths from the ideal and ought-to L2 selves to SPP in order to examine whether the mediation structure had been imposed rather than tested. The full mediation model showed acceptable fit, χ²(453) = 1114.31, CFI = 0.934, TLI = 0.927, RMSEA = 0.058, SRMR = 0.078. The partial mediation model showed similar fit, χ²(451) = 1111.53, CFI = 0.934, TLI = 0.927, RMSEA = 0.058, SRMR = 0.077. Adding the two direct paths did not significantly improve model fit, Δχ²(2) = 2.77, p = .250. Moreover, the added direct paths were not significant: ideal L2 self → SPP, β = 0.063, p = .433; ought-to L2 self → SPP, β = 0.057, p = .258. Because the full mediation model was more parsimonious and had lower AIC and BIC values, it was retained as the final structural model. Moderation and sensitivity analyses

The hypothesized moderating role of speaking self-efficacy was tested separately using latent product-indicator models. In the matched-pair product-indicator model, the latent interaction between FLA and speaking self-efficacy was not significant, β = − 0.001, p = .979, 95% CI for b [-0.112, 0.109]. The interaction factor showed acceptable composite reliability (CR = 0.827), although its AVE was below the conventional 0.50 threshold (AVE = 0.413). A corrected all-pair residual-centered product-indicator sensitivity analysis yielded the same conclusion, β = 0.005, p = .903, 95% CI for b [-0.095, 0.107]. In addition, a composite-score regression interaction check also found no significant interaction, b = 0.010, p = .832, 95% CI [-0.081, 0.100]. Therefore, H5 was not supported in the present sample.

Common method bias diagnostic checks

Because all constructs were measured using the same self-report questionnaire, common method bias was examined using two diagnostic checks. First, a one-factor CFA showed poor fit, χ²(464) = 5424.82, CFI = 0.502, TLI = 0.468, RMSEA = 0.156, SRMR = 0.135, suggesting that a single factor could not adequately account for the covariance among the items. Second, adding a common latent factor to the five-factor measurement model did not meaningfully improve model fit, and the average method variance was approximately 1.2%. These results suggest that common method bias was unlikely to fully account for the observed associations, although it cannot be completely ruled out given the single-source self-report design.

Discussion

The present study examined how L2 motivational selves, classroom-based FLA, and speaking self-efficacy were associated with Chinese EFL learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy. Overall, the findings supported a structural mediation model in which the ideal and ought-to L2 selves showed opposite associations with FLA, and FLA was in turn negatively associated with SPP. Speaking self-efficacy was positively associated with SPP, but its hypothesized moderating role in the FLA–SPP association was not supported. Importantly, the alternative partial mediation model showed that direct paths from the ideal and ought-to L2 selves to SPP were not significant and did not improve model fit. This pattern suggests that, in the present sample, the links between L2 self-guides and self-perceived pronunciation accuracy were mainly captured through their indirect associations via classroom-based anxiety.

L2 selves, anxiety, and pronunciation-related self-evaluations

The opposite associations of ideal and ought-to L2 selves with FLA are consistent with previous L2MSS research. Earlier studies have shown that the ideal L2 self tends to be linked to more adaptive motivational and emotional orientations, whereas the ought-to L2 self is often associated with pressure, vigilance, and anxiety [23, 24, 34]. The present findings extend this line of work by showing that these contrasting self-guides are relevant not only to general language learning emotions or achievement-related outcomes, but also to learners’ pronunciation-related self-evaluations. For first-year Chinese English majors, a personally valued image of oneself as a future English user was associated with lower classroom-based anxiety, whereas obligation-oriented self-guides were associated with higher anxiety.

The negative association between FLA and SPP also aligns with research linking anxiety to less favorable language self-evaluations and lower perceived communicative competence [5, 7, 17, 35]. More directly, Szyszka [33], reported a negative association between foreign language anxiety and self-perceived English pronunciation competence, especially for several suprasegmental aspects of pronunciation. Research on pronunciation anxiety has similarly emphasized that learners’ pronunciation-related worries often involve self-assessment, perceived pronunciation efficacy, and fear of negative evaluation [2, 3]. The present study is consistent with this literature in showing that learners who reported higher classroom-based FLA tended to evaluate their pronunciation accuracy less favorably.

At the same time, the present findings should be interpreted in light of the measurement scope of this study. Recent work on accent anxiety has shown that learners’ concerns about accent may involve fear of negative evaluation, fear of communication breakdown, and concerns about how their accent positions them socially [8, 41]. These dimensions are broader than the classroom-based FLA measure used here. Therefore, the significant FLA–SPP association should not be interpreted as evidence about pronunciation anxiety or accent anxiety specifically. Rather, it suggests that general classroom-based anxiety is meaningfully related to learners’ perceived accuracy of pronunciation features.

The significant indirect associations through FLA provide a bridge between L2MSS research and pronunciation-related self-belief research. Previous L2MSS studies have mainly connected L2 self-guides to anxiety, enjoyment, effort, achievement, or willingness to communicate. Pronunciation studies, in contrast, have often examined pronunciation anxiety, accent attitudes, or self-perceived pronunciation competence without explicitly incorporating L2 self-guides. By integrating these strands, the present study suggests that classroom-based anxiety may be an important affective correlate through which L2 self-guides are statistically linked to self-perceived pronunciation accuracy. However, because the data are cross-sectional, these indirect associations should be understood as statistical mediation rather than as evidence of temporal or causal ordering. This interpretation is also consistent with recent L2 speech research showing that motivation, anxiety, and pronunciation-related emotions are relevant to learners’ speech development and pronunciation outcomes, although the specific pathways may differ depending on whether the outcome is self-perceived accuracy, comprehensibility, or accentedness [18, 42].

The non-significant direct paths from L2 selves to SPP also help clarify the nature of the outcome variable. Some accent and identity-oriented research suggests that learners’ imagined L2 identities and accent attitudes may be directly connected to how they evaluate their speech, legitimacy, or identity as English users [30, 31]. The present results do not necessarily contradict this view. Instead, they may reflect the narrower operationalization of SPP in this study. The SPP scale focused on perceived accuracy of monophthongs, diphthongs, consonants, word stress, sentence stress, and intonation, rather than broader dimensions such as accent identity, comprehensibility, social acceptability, or ownership of English. Direct identity-related associations may be more visible when SPP is measured as a broader pronunciation self-concept rather than as a perceived-accuracy factor.

Speaking self-efficacy and the unsupported moderation hypothesis

Speaking self-efficacy was positively associated with SPP. This finding is consistent with self-efficacy theory, which views efficacy beliefs as task-related judgments of capability that are linked to persistence, self-regulation, and self-evaluation [1]. It also resonates with L2 studies showing that learners with stronger self-efficacy tend to report lower anxiety, stronger engagement, and more favorable self-perceptions of language ability [21, 37]. More recently, Okyar [22], found that speaking anxiety was negatively related to speaking self-efficacy and self-perceived oral proficiency, further supporting the close connection among speaking anxiety, efficacy beliefs, and perceived oral ability. The present study extends this pattern to learners’ perceived pronunciation accuracy.

Nevertheless, the self-efficacy finding should be understood within the measurement boundaries of the study. The QESE speaking subscale captures learners’ confidence in performing English speaking tasks, not their confidence in accurately producing specific segmental or suprasegmental pronunciation features. Thus, the observed positive association indicates that broader speaking-related confidence is related to pronunciation-specific self-evaluation, but it does not show that pronunciation-specific self-efficacy was measured. This distinction is important because a learner may feel generally capable of speaking English while still feeling uncertain about particular pronunciation features such as stress placement, vowel quality, or intonation.

The hypothesized moderating role of speaking self-efficacy was not supported. This result qualifies the expectation, often derived from self-efficacy theory, that efficacy beliefs may buffer the negative association between anxiety and learning-related outcomes. In the present data, speaking self-efficacy was better characterized as a direct correlate of SPP than as a moderator of the FLA–SPP association. One possible explanation is the theory–measurement mismatch noted above: a general speaking self-efficacy scale may not be sensitive enough to capture learners’ pronunciation-specific coping confidence under anxiety. Another possibility is that classroom-based anxiety and speaking self-efficacy may contribute additively, rather than interactively, to learners’ pronunciation-related self-evaluations. The strong negative correlation between FLA and speaking self-efficacy may also have reduced the unique variance available for detecting a latent interaction. This possibility further supports a cautious interpretation of the null moderation result.

Accordingly, the non-significant interaction should not be taken as definitive evidence that efficacy beliefs cannot buffer anxiety. Rather, the present data did not support a buffering role for general speaking self-efficacy. Future research could examine whether pronunciation-specific self-efficacy moderates the relationship between pronunciation anxiety or accent anxiety and pronunciation self-perception more strongly than general speaking self-efficacy.

Implications

Theoretically, this study contributes to L2 motivation and emotion research by linking L2MSS constructs with a pronunciation-specific self-evaluative outcome. The findings support previous claims that ideal and ought-to L2 selves have different emotional profiles, while extending this pattern to self-perceived pronunciation accuracy. The study also adds nuance to self-efficacy research by showing that speaking self-efficacy was directly associated with SPP but did not significantly moderate the FLA–SPP association.

Methodologically, the findings highlight the importance of matching theoretical claims with measurement. The broader literature on pronunciation anxiety, accent anxiety, and audible identity provides an important conceptual background, but the present study measured classroom-based FLA and speaking self-efficacy rather than pronunciation-specific anxiety or pronunciation-specific efficacy. Similarly, the SPP scale captured a relatively narrow perceived segmental/suprasegmental pronunciation accuracy factor. This more precise interpretation helps avoid overgeneralizing the findings and points to the need for future instruments that distinguish perceived pronunciation accuracy, comprehensibility, accent identity, listener-based evaluations, and pronunciation-specific efficacy.

Pedagogically, the findings suggest that pronunciation teaching should attend to affective and self-belief factors alongside technical pronunciation practice. Teachers can reduce classroom-based anxiety by creating low-threat opportunities for pronunciation practice, using formative feedback, and helping students view pronunciation development as gradual and improvable. Given the positive association between speaking self-efficacy and SPP, teachers may also support learners through scaffolded speaking tasks, visible progress markers, and feedback that highlights specific improvements in segmental and suprasegmental features. Finally, because ought-to self-guides were associated with higher anxiety, pronunciation teaching should avoid excessive reliance on external pressure, comparison, or native-like standards. Instead, learners may benefit from personally meaningful, intelligibility-oriented pronunciation goals that support both communicative confidence and realistic self-evaluation.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal or temporal interpretation of the mediation model. Although the indirect associations through FLA were significant, longitudinal research is needed to examine whether changes in L2 selves and anxiety precede changes in pronunciation-related self-evaluations.

Second, all constructs were measured through a single self-report questionnaire. Although common-method-bias diagnostics did not suggest that a single method factor accounted for the observed associations, shared-method variance cannot be completely ruled out. Future studies should combine self-report measures with teacher ratings, peer ratings, acoustic analyses, speaking tests, or learner interviews.

Third, the study did not include an objective measure of actual pronunciation competence or general English proficiency. Therefore, it remains unclear how accurately learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy reflected their actual pronunciation performance. Future research should examine the relationship between perceived and externally assessed pronunciation ability.

Fourth, the SPP scale was developed for this study and showed very high internal consistency and strong inter-item correlations. While this supports coherence, it also indicates that the scale captured a relatively narrow perceived pronunciation accuracy factor rather than the full range of pronunciation self-perception. Future studies should validate the scale in independent samples and consider additional dimensions such as perceived comprehensibility, accent acceptability, listener response, pronunciation-specific self-efficacy, and pronunciation identity.

Finally, the sample consisted of first-year English and English-related majors from two universities in western China. These learners had relatively similar educational backgrounds and were studying in contexts where formal pronunciation instruction was relevant. The findings may not generalize to non-English majors, learners in other regions, learners at different proficiency levels, or EFL contexts with different pronunciation teaching practices.

Conclusion

This study examined the associations among L2 motivational selves, classroom-based FLA, speaking self-efficacy, and Chinese EFL learners’ self-perceived pronunciation accuracy. The findings supported a structural mediation model in which the ideal L2 self was negatively associated with FLA, whereas the ought-to L2 self was positively associated with FLA. FLA was negatively associated with self-perceived pronunciation accuracy and statistically mediated the associations between both L2 selves and SPP. Speaking self-efficacy was positively associated with SPP, but its hypothesized moderating role in the FLA–SPP association was not supported.

These findings contribute to L2 motivation and pronunciation research by linking L2 self-guides with a pronunciation-specific self-evaluative outcome. They suggest that learners’ perceived pronunciation accuracy is associated not only with speaking-related confidence but also with the emotional orientations connected to their future L2 selves. At the same time, the findings should be interpreted within the study’s methodological boundaries: FLA and self-efficacy were measured as classroom-based anxiety and speaking self-efficacy rather than pronunciation-specific constructs, and SPP was operationalized as perceived accuracy of selected segmental and suprasegmental features.

For pronunciation teaching, the results highlight the value of addressing anxiety and efficacy beliefs alongside technical pronunciation instruction. Supportive classroom environments, formative feedback, and personally meaningful pronunciation goals may help learners develop more positive and realistic pronunciation-related self-evaluations. Future studies should use longitudinal designs, objective pronunciation measures, and pronunciation-specific anxiety and efficacy scales to further examine how motivational, affective, and efficacy-related variables are connected to learners’ pronunciation-related self-evaluations and actual pronunciation performance.
