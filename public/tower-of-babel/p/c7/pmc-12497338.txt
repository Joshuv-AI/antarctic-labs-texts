Quantitative Evaluation of Lung Perfusion by Dynamic Chest Radiography in Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension: Comparison With Lung Perfusion Scintigraphy
Authors: Megumi Ikeda, Yuzo Yamasaki, Koji Sagiyama, Kazuya Hosokawa, Daisuke Toyomura, Tomoyuki Hida, Takuya Hino, Kosuke Tabata, Takuro Isoda, Noritsugu Matsutani, Hidetake Yabuuchi, Kohtaro Abe, Koichi Akashi, Kousei Ishigami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary perfusion assessment is essential for the management of chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH). Lung perfusion scintigraphy and contrast‐enhanced CT are occasionally limited by the need for radionuclides or allergy to the contrast agents. Dynamic chest radiography (DCR) can evaluate the pulmonary perfusion non‐invasively using conventional X‐ray technology. We validated pulmonary perfusion measurements using DCR by comparing them with those obtained using perfusion scintigraphy in CTEPH. Thirty‐three patients with CTEPH who underwent both DCR and lung perfusion scintigraphy within 1‐month interval from December 2019 to December 2022 were included. DCR was performed with patients in both standing and supine positions. To assess lung perfusion, each lung was divided into six fields (right and left; upper, middle, and lower). The blood flow rates per field were quantified by using analysis software as 100% of the sum of the six sites. The correlation between blood flow rates of each area in DCR and perfusion scintigraphy was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). DCR showed a strong correlation with pulmonary perfusion scintigraphy in both standing (ICC(2,1) = 0.86; confidence interval [CI], 0.81–0.89) and supine (ICC(2,1) = 0.82; CI, 0.77–0.86) positions. When analyzed by region, all regions except the left lower lung showed significant correlations with perfusion scintigraphy findings. Intra‐ and intra‐inspector reliabilities at both positions were excellent. The quantitative assessment of lung perfusion using DCR is reliable in patients with CTEPH. However, perfusion in the left lower lung was underestimated using DCR.Keywords: chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, dynamic chest x‐ray, pulmonary hypertension, x‐ray pulmonary flow imaging

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH, WHO group 4) is characterized by organized thrombotic blockages in major pulmonary vessels, leading to right ventricular (RV) enlargement and dysfunction [1, 2]. CTEPH is a serious but potentially treatable long‐term complication of acute pulmonary embolism (PE) that affects approximately 3.2% of PE survivors [3, 4, 5, 6]. The reduction in pulmonary vascular beds due to multiple organized thrombi in the pulmonary arteries increases the pulmonary arterial pressure, and the extent of pulmonary hypoperfusion is correlated with disease severity [7]. Consequently, the evaluation of pulmonary perfusion is crucial for managing CTEPH.

Lung perfusion scintigraphy is the standard measure to assess the extent of pulmonary perfusion abnormalities in patients with CTEPH. Perfusion scintigraphy has been proven to be more sensitive than CT pulmonary angiography in detecting vascular obstructions [8, 9], besides being useful in evaluating the extent of perfusion abnormalities and changes after treatments such as pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) and balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) [10, 11]. However, the utility of lung scintigraphy is occasionally limited because of its specialty, the need for radionuclides, large size equipment, and long examination times. However, recent progress in CT technology and the establishment of dual‐energy techniques have enabled the evaluation of pulmonary perfusion and lung perfusion scintigraphy [12, 13, 14]. Pulmonary perfusion assessment using dual energy CT requires contrast media and special equipment, resulting in a relatively high dose of radiation exposure. Easier and more readily available methods that use low‐dose radiation to screen for CTEPH are clinically favorable.

Dynamic chest radiography (DCR) is a novel imaging technique that uses a conventional X‐ray system, including a flat‐panel detector, to provide pulmonary perfusion findings non‐invasively [15]. Image acquisition time is 7 s, with additional image analysis for 1 min using a software. Neither contrast media nor radionuclides are required for imaging. Furthermore, DCR is characterized by one‐eighth radiation exposure as lung perfusion scintigraphy (0.13 mSv vs 1.04 mSv) [16, 17]. Pulmonary perfusion images help in the assessment of pulmonary perfusion abnormalities in several pulmonary disease including CTEPH [11, 18, 19, 20]. Moreover, recent technical advancements have enabled DCR to be used to qualify pulmonary perfusion semi‐quantitatively, similar to scintigraphy. This could enable a detailed evaluation of perfusion abnormalities or interval changes as treatment effects. However, the accuracy of quantitative variables in DCR compared with scintigraphy has not yet been investigated.

Therefore, we aimed to compare the measurements of pulmonary perfusion using DCR with those using perfusion scintigraphy and to investigate whether DCR is useful for non‐invasively assessment of pulmonary perfusion abnormalities in CTEPH.

Materials and Methods

The institutional authors, who were not employees of Konica Minolta, Inc. controlled all data. One author (N.M.) was an employee of Konica Minolta, Inc. Japan, and provided technical support in the image analysis software but was not involved in study design or data interpretation. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital (IRB# 2023‐118), and the requirement for informed consent was waived. The study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Population

Consecutive patients newly diagnosed with CTEPH who underwent both DCR and lung perfusion scintigraphy within 1 month from December 2019 to December 2022 were screened for this study. Based on the diagnostic criteria for pulmonary hypertension of mean pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) ≥ 25 mmHg on right heart catheterization (RHC) [21], CTEPH was diagnosed using clinical presentation and imaging tests, including RHC, by a specialized pulmonary circulation team, including interventional cardiologists, surgeons, and radiologists. Consequently, 33 patients diagnosed with CTEPH who underwent DCR and lung ventilation/perfusion scintigraphy were enrolled in this study.

Lung Perfusion Assessment by DCR

All patients underwent DCR with 7 s of inspiratory breath‐holding using a flat‐panel detector (AeroDR fine, Konica Minolta, Inc. Tokyo, Japan) with matrix size 1062 × 1062 pixels, pixel size was 400 × 400 μm, and the whole image area 42.5 × 42.5 cm2 and a conventional X‐ray system (RADspeed Pro, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), along with a conventional chest X‐ray. Imaging was performed with the patient in the standing position in the posteroanterior direction and in the supine position in the anteroposterior direction. Detailed scan parameters were set based on body mass index according to the latest protocol [22].

Pulmonary perfusion images (dynamic images and summarized static images) were created from the DCR using a commercially available workstation (KINOSIS version 1.20R00 or 1.30R00, Konica Minolta, Inc. Japan). The temporal change in the pixel value (X‐ray translucency) in the lung field based on end‐diastole timing during one cardiac cycle was calculated and visualized as lung perfusion [15]. The lung areas were automatically contoured using an edge detection algorithm and manually corrected if needed. To evaluate the intra‐ and inter‐rater errors, image analysis was repeated at least 1 month later by the same primary observer (M.I. with 8 years of clinical experience in cardiology) and an additional reader (D.T. with 10 years of clinical experience in pediatric cardiology) who was blinded to the results of the initial study to determine the reproducibility of measurements.

Lung Perfusion Scintigraphy

A Single Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT)/CT system (Symbia T6; Siemens Medical Solutions, Knoxville, TN, USA) was used for the lung scintigraphy. 99mTc macro aggregated albumin (MAA) was injected intravenously, with the patient in the supine position. The average injection dose of MAA was 222.6 ± 35.7 MBq. Perfusion scintigraphy was performed 5 min after the MAA injection. In this study, we used only the anterior and posterior views to adjust the DCR.

Image Analysis

For both imaging techniques, each lung was divided into three lung fields (upper, middle, and lower), and six areas were assessed for each case. The blood flow rates per field were quantified using analysis software as 100% of the sum of the six sites, referred to as the “percentage measurement.” The percentage measurements were calculated for each case using both DCR and lung perfusion scintigraphy (Figure 1). We compared the percentage measurements in each lung in DCR with those obtained by planar perfusion scintigraphy and assessed their correlations.

Statistical Analysis

JMP Pro version 17.0.0 (SAS Institute Inc., NC, USA), GraphPad Prism version 10.2.2 (GraphPad Software, Inc., CA, USA), and BellCurve for Excel 4.06 (Social Survey Research Information Co. Ltd.) were used for statistical analysis. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to measure the linear correlation between the percentage measurements made with DCR in the standing/supine position and planar ventilation/perfusion scintigraphy. The level of agreement was graded as follows: excellent, ICC > 0.74; good, ICC 0.60–0.74; fair, ICC 0.40–0.59; and poor, ICC < 0.40. All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 198 regions from 33 patients were evaluated. Patient characteristics, including hemodynamic data from RHC, are shown in Table 1. The average mean PAP was 37.7 ± 9.3 mmHg, and the average of pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) was 8.0 ± 6.3 Wood Units. The median interval between DCR and perfusion scintigraphy was 4 (0–30) days. DCR was performed first in 22 cases, perfusion scintigraphy first in 4 cases, and both examinations were performed on the same day in 5 cases. No patient underwent additional treatment, including BPA or PEA, or required drug changes within 1 month. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that percentage measurements of pulmonary perfusion images using DCR in both the standing (ICC = 0.86) and supine (ICC = 0.82) positions were highly correlated with those of pulmonary perfusion scintigraphy; ICC in the left lower lungs was low, with no correlation between DCR in standing and supine positions and pulmonary perfusion scintigraphy, unlike those for other lung areas; and inter‐ and intra‐rater reliabilities were excellent in standing and supine positions.

DCR demonstrated a reasonable correlation with perfusion scintigraphy, indicating the possibility of a new method for lung perfusion assessment. These results are consistent with those of a previous study investigating the correlations between standing DCR and perfusion scintigraphy values in patients with pulmonary disease [20]. In contrast, underestimation and poor correlation were observed on scintigraphy in the left lower lung tissue. Considering that the shadow of the heart masked the lower left lung, these results are reasonable. Additionally, differences in diseases among the study cohorts might have influenced the results. This should be noted when DCR is applied to evaluate pulmonary perfusion and should be addressed in future studies.

We also found that the distribution of lung perfusion in the supine position did not differ from that in the standing position. DCR can be performed in both standing and supine positions. Our study clarified not only supine positional DCR but also standing DCR have significant correlation with perfusion scintigraphy and could be used for the evaluation of pulmonary perfusion. This leads to the reduction of radiation exposure and improving clinical workflow. In healthy individuals, the distribution of pulmonary blood flow is strongly influenced by gravity. However, gravity‐dependent perfusion redistribution relies on a low pulmonary arterial pressure and the ability to distend and/or recruit previously underperfused vessels [23]. The loss of vascular reserves in pulmonary hypertension diminishes the ability for vessel recruitment and, hence, flow redistribution. Therefore, we assume that this contributed to the similarity between the variables in the standing and supine positions, resulting in similarly high correlations with perfusion, although perfusion scintigraphy was performed in the supine position. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies using perfusion scintigraphy [23, 24].

In recent decades, the treatment of CTEPH has advanced markedly owing to the emergence of pulmonary vasodilators and refined BPA. Lung perfusion must be evaluated in the current diagnostic and treatment flow. Therefore, an easy and quick assessment of pulmonary perfusion with a low radiation dose using DCR can play an adjunctive role to perfusion scintigraphy. In addition, DCR could also play an important role in the follow‐up of patients with acute PEs as 3.4% of them develop CTEPH [25]. In this study, we excluded patients who had undergone PEA or BPA from the analysis because postsurgical changes on DCR images following PEA could affect image interpretation and potentially compromise accurate assessment of pulmonary blood flow. Similarly, after BPA, complications such as pulmonary hemorrhage or pleural effusion may occur, resulting in lung opacities that could interfere with blood flow evaluation. However, the ability to evaluate posttreatment changes remains an important area for further investigation. If this becomes feasible, it would significantly expand the clinical utility of DCR.

DCR is a simple and readily available technique that requires only a conventional X‐ray system, including a flat‐panel detector, and perfusion assessment can be applied only in the posteroanterior or anteroposterior directions. However, the heart and diaphragm shadows cover the lung areas behind them, interfering with the evaluation of the lower lungs, particularly the left lower lung, as shown in this study. Further technological advancements, such as the use of oblique‐view images, may compensate for this problem and enable more accurate assessments in the future.

This study has certain limitations. First, the sample size was small. Second, it was difficult to accurately determine the diagnostic performance of CTEPH using DCR because of the retrospective nature of this study. Further prospective studies are required to confirm this hypothesis. Additionally, the proportion of male patients was relatively high in our cohort. In contrast, previous Japanese studies have reported a female predominance in CTEPH, often associated with a higher prevalence of peripheral‐type disease, possibly due to genetic factors [26]. This difference may be attributable to the retrospective nature of our study and the limited sample size. Notably, European registry data have reported no sex‐related differences in CTEPH, including lesion distribution [27]. Although our study population demonstrated a sex imbalance, we believe this did not substantially influence our findings. Third, we compared DCR with only planar images and not all images from lung perfusion scintigraphy. Furthermore, in this study, we evaluated pulmonary perfusion using DCR and investigated its correlation with perfusion scintigraphy. However, the influence of ventilation abnormalities was not considered, as we did not evaluate ventilation/perfusion mismatch. The inability to assess ventilation represents a limitation of DCR compared to V/Q scanning. Combining DCR with chest radiography findings might partially address this issue, although radiographic abnormalities cannot be considered equivalent to ventilation abnormalities. Fourth, we recruited patients with mean PAP ≥ 25 mmHg, in accordance with Japanese guidelines for diagnosis and treatment for pulmonary hypertension (PH) [21]. This definition differs from the European guidelines, which define PH as mean PAP ≥ 20 mmHg [28].

In conclusion, the quantitative assessment of pulmonary perfusion using DCR demonstrated reasonable correlations with perfusion scintigraphy. However, it should be noted that there was no significant correlation in the left lower lung because of cardiac shadow interference. DCR is expected to be an easy, low‐dose, readily available, and noninvasive method for the evaluation of pulmonary perfusion in CTEPH, although further technological advancements are required.
