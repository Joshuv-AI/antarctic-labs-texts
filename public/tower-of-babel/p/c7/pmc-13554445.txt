Total saponins from Panax ginseng delay aging and cellular senescence via PI3K-AKT pathway in a rapid dual-system screening model of aging
Authors: Yu-jing Tan, Wen-qi Jin, Jing Wang, Hui Wang, Ting-ting Lou, Li-wei Sun, Fang-bing Liu, Shuai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAging, as an inevitable biological process, is an inevitable process of multisystem functional decline, with cellular senescence being one of its hallmarks. Although total saponins from Panax ginseng (TSG) offer health benefits, its precise anti-aging mechanisms have not been fully elucidated.MethodsA rapid dual-system screening model of aging was established using tert-butyl hydrogen peroxide (tBHP) to induce senescence in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans). Anti-aging effects and key components of TSG were evaluated via fluorescent staining, Western blotting (WB), and biochemical and behavioral analysis. Bioinformatics analysis, WB, AKT activator and loss-of-function mutants in C. elegans were used to clarify TSG's anti-aging and anti-cellular senescence effects via PI3K-AKT signaling.ResultsTSG alleviated oxidative stress, enhanced antioxidant capacity and reduced β-galactosidase activity, indicating delayed aging. It also modulated senescence-related markers and increased Ki-67, supporting its role in promoting proliferation and suppressing cellular senescence. Among 20 ginsenosides screened, PPD, F1 and Rh1 were identified as the principal active components. Network pharmacology and experimental validation revealed the PI3K-AKT pathway as central to TSG's effects. AKT1 and PI3K were confirmed as the targets, with decreased phosphorylation levels observed. TSG's anti-aging effects were partly abolished in loss-of-function mutant worms akt-1;akt-2 and age-1, substantiating the essential role of PI3K-AKT signaling in vivo.ConclusionThis study establishes a rapid dual-system model for aging research, and demonstrates that TSG and its principal ginsenosides (PPD, F1 and Rh1) mitigate aging and cellular senescence through the PI3K-AKT pathway by downregulating PI3K/AKT phosphorylation.Keywords: Aging, Cellular senescence, Total saponins from Panax ginseng, Ginsenoside, Tert-butyl hydrogen peroxide, PI3K-AKT pathway, Phosphorylation

Introduction

Aging is a universal process caused by the accumulation of biological alterations that progressively impair the functions of different organs over time [1]. Human aging is associated with cognitive and physical decline as well as an elevated risk of various diseases, such as cancer, cardiovascular disorders and neurodegenerative diseases [2]. Studies suggest that age-related morbidities adversely affect the quality of life and are linked with an increased risk of mortality, which has serious ramifications for both individuals and society [3]. Therefore, the development of effective anti-aging therapies is urgent.

Cellular senescence is a hallmark of aging, defined as an irreversible and stable state of proliferative arrest, which is characterized by functional, structural and morphological alterations [4]. Key factors underlying cellular senescence include mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, oxidative stress and repetitive cell division. The primary characteristic of aged organisms is the accumulation of cellular senescence, a state of permanent cell cycle arrest caused by different damaging stimuli [5]. Senescent cells (SCs) accumulation accelerates aging and promotes age-related tissue dysfunction. Therefore, targeting cellular senescence is a promising approach for the development of anti-aging interventions.

Replicative senescence closely mimics physiological aging; however, it is time-consuming and costly [6]. Since oxidative stress is a primary factor in senescence, oxidants such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) have been used to model aging, though their instability limits reproducibility [7]. Tert-butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP) is a stable organic peroxide that can generate sustained intracellular oxidative stress [8]. Because of its stable and consistent action, this study selected tBHP for establishing a rapid aging model for both in vivo and in vitro analyses. Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) is a valuable model for aging research due to its short lifespan of 2-3 weeks [9]. tBHP exposure induces aging phenotypes in C. elegans within hours to days, enabling rapid in vivo screening [10]. In aging humans, senescent endothelial cells contribute to vascular dysfunction and related diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and neurodegenerative disorders [11]. Therefore, tBHP-treated human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were chosen as the in vitro aging model for this study. By combining these rapid in vivo and in vitro models, this study aims to facilitate the discovery of novel candidate compounds with anti-aging potential and explore their mechanisms.

In Asia, Panax ginseng as a traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) and is both edible and medicinal [12]. Previous literature has indicated that the total saponins from Panax ginseng (TSG), have various health benefits, including anti-cancer, anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory effects [13]. However, due to the multi-target and multi-pathway properties of natural extracts, the precise anti-aging mechanisms, effects and active components of TSG remain unclear. This study established a rapid dual-system screening model of aging to validate TSG's anti-aging and anti-cellular senescence effects, and active components. It was observed that TSG exerts its effects by modulating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway through PI3K/AKT phosphorylation, as supported by in silico, in vitro and in vivo analyses. These results provide fundamental evidence supporting the potential of TSG as an anti-aging supplement.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ginsenoside monomers, including PPD, Ra2, Rh1, F1, Rg1, Rd, PPT, Rb3, Rf, Rg3, Rc, Ra1, Ra3, F2, Rh2, Re, Rb2, CK, Rg2 and Rb1, were acquired from Yuanye Bio-Technology (Shanghai, China). Staurosporine, tBHP, and 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) were provided by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)/F12 and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Gibco (Carlsbad, CA, USA). The superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA) detection kits were sourced from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Primary antibodies, including Lamin B1 (AF5161), p53 (AF0879), p21 (AF6290), γ-H2A.X (AF3187), GAPDH (AF7012), Tubulin (AF4351), Phospho-PI3K p85 alpha (AF3241), PI3K p85 alpha (AF6241), and secondary antibodies were acquired from Affinity (Nanjing, China). The Ki-67 antibody (PB0526) was brought from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). SC79 (HY-154848) and acridine orange (AO, HY-D0952) were provided by MCE (New Jersey, USA). Rapamycin (1292/1) was provided by Bio-Techne (Minnesota, USA). Antibodies for Hsp90 (13171-1-AP), p-AKT (66444-1-Ig), AKT (10176-2-AP) and PI3Kp110A (27921-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

Extraction and chemical characterization of TSG

This study employed dried roots of five-year-old Panax ginseng. The roots were harvested from Fusong (Jilin Province, China, in May 2025), crushed and used for subsequent extraction. TSG was prepared using the ethanol reflux extraction method (70% ethanol, 70 °C, 40 min, solid-to-liquid ratio of 1: 30 g/mL, repeated 2-3 times) [14]. The combined extracts were filtered, purified using D101 macroporous resin, and eluted first with distilled water (until colorless) and then with 60-70% ethanol. After collecting the TSG-containing eluate, ethanol was recovered, concentrated, and dried to acquire the final TSG product. The saponin content was evaluated using the vanillin-perchloric acid colorimetric method. Briefly, the TSG sample was mixed with 8% vanillin and 72% perchloric acid, heated at 60 °C for 15 min, cooled and assessed at 540 nm [15]. Furthermore, saponin content was measured using a standard curve for ginsenoside Re, yielding a total saponin purity of about 82.5%. To prevent hydrolysis and photodegradation, the TSG powder was stored in a sealed and light-proof container at -20 °C.

Relative TSG content analysis

High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) (Shimadzu, Japan) was employed to analyze the TSG and ginsenoside monomers, including PPD, Ra2, Rh1, F1, Rg1, Rd, PPT, Rb3, Rf, Rg3, Rc, Ra1, Ra3, F2, Rh2, Re, Rb2, CK, Rg2 and Rb1 [16]. The compounds were separated using an Agilent ZORBAX SB C18 column (1.8 μm, 4.6 × 50 mm) via a gradient elution system. The mobile phase comprised (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) Acetonitrile. The column temperature was maintained at 35 °C, with a flow rate of 1 mL/min and an injection volume of 10 μL. The gradient elution conditions were as follows:

Elution parameters (1): 33% B for 0-15 min, 33-35% B for 15-20 min, 35-40% B for 20-30 min, 40-45% B for 30-35 min, 45-50% B for 35-50 min, 50-70% B for 50-51 min, and 70% B for 51-75 min. Elution parameters (2): 27.3% B for 0-55 min, 27.3-37% B for 55-70 min, 37% B for 70-75 min, 37-50% B for 75-76 min, and 50% B for 76-90 min. Elution parameters (3): 18.8% B for 0-45 min, 18.8-50% B for 45-47 min, and 50% B for 47-50 min. Elution parameters (4): 33% B for 0-10min, 33-51% B for 10-25 min, 51% B for 25-60 min, 51-80% B for 60-75 min.

Cell culture, treatment and study design

HUVECs were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, VA, USA) and cultured in DMEM/F12 augmented with 10% FBS and penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The cells were categorized into control (Ctrl), Model, and TSG (10, 25 and 50 μg/mL) and their active ingredients (10, 25 and 50 μM) treatment groups. Each group consisted of three replicates.

For analyses, cells were cultured in 96- or 6-well plates until 80% confluent. Except for the Ctrl and Model groups, the cells in the treatment groups were pretreated with the respective compounds for 24 h, followed by 20 μM tBHP treatment for 5 h.

Cytotoxicity assays

The cytotoxicity of TSG was evaluated in HUVECs using an MTT assay. Briefly, cells were treated with TSG for 24 h, followed by the addition of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) into each well, and incubation at 37 °C for 4 h. After adding DMSO (150 μL/well) to dissolve the formazan crystals, the absorbance was then measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN A-5082, Magellan, Austria). The results were expressed as the percentage of viable cells relative to the vehicle control treatment.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

The activity of SA-β-gal in HUVECs was assessed via the SA-β-gal kit (Dojindo, Shanghai, China) per the manufacturer's instructions [17]. For each experimental group, three independent biological replicates were used, where each replicate comprised three culture wells. For counting, five non-overlapping fields of view (60×) were randomly selected in each well, and in each field, 100-200 cells were counted. In total, about 6750 cells were counted per group (calculated as: 5 fields × 3 wells × 3 biological replicates × an average of 150 cells per field = 6750 cells). SA-β-gal-positive cells were depicted by distinct green fluorescence in the cytoplasm. Images of random fields were acquired using a LionHeart digital camera (BioTek, Vermont, USA). The percentage of positive cells was calculated as: (number of positive cells/total number of cells) × 100%. Counting was carried out via a double-blind method, and ImageJ software was employed for quantification.

Ki-67 nuclear staining

After treatment, HUVECs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, washed thrice with PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min, washed again with PBS (thrice), blocked using 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min, and treated with Ki-67 primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The next day, cells were treated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and observed using a LionHeart digital camera (BioTek, Vermont, USA). For each experimental group, three independent biological replicates were used, where each replicate comprised three culture wells. For counting, five non-overlapping fields of view (60×) were randomly selected in each well, and in each field, 50-100 cells were counted, resulting in a total of approximately 3000 cells counted per group. Cells with distinct green fluorescence in the nucleus were selected as Ki-67 positive [18]. The percentage of positive cells (Ki-67 labeling index) was calculated as: (number of Ki-67-positive nuclei/total number of nuclei) × 100%. Counting was carried out via a double-blind method, and ImageJ software was employed for quantification.

Assessment of SOD, GSH and MDA levels

HUVECs (5 × 105/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with various TSG concentrations for 24 h. Then, cells were treated with 20 μM tBHP for 5 h, and the medium was removed. The GSH, SOD and MDA activities were measured per the instructions provided with the respective kits.

Assessment of ROS levels

The ROS levels were evaluated using the CM-H2DCFDA fluorescent probe. Briefly, after treatment, cells in each group were processed according to the kit's instructions. Fluorescence intensity was observed using a fluorescence microscope, while quantitative analysis was conducted via a fluorescence microplate reader (excitation wavelength: 488 nm; maximum emission wavelength: 525 nm).

Western blotting (WB)

Total cellular protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) for 30 min on ice. Then, the protein was quantified with the BCA kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, equal amounts of protein were isolated by 12.5% Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and transferred onto PVDF membranes, which were then blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, and treated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by three 10-min washes with TBST. Then, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, washed three times with TBST, and analyzed for immunoreactive bands using ECL reagent. Images were acquired using an Image Quant LAS 4000 system (GE Healthcare, Australia). Band intensities were normalized to GAPDH or Tubulin and quantified using ImageJ software. The supporting data for the Western blot results are shown in Fig. S7–8 of the Supplementary Materials.

C. elegans strains and culture

The strains utilized in this study were procured from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and included N2/Bristol, WT strain; CF1553, muIs84 [(pAD76) sod-3p::GFP + rol-6(su1006)]; CL2166, dvIs19[(pAF15)gst-4p::GFP::NLS] III; RB759, akt-1(ok525) V; VC204, akt-2(ok393) X and TJ1052, age-1(hx546) II. Briefly, C. elegans strains were cultured at 20 °C by following standard methods. According to the Wormbook protocol (https://wormbook.org), the double mutant akt-1(ok525);akt-2(ok393) was generated by crossing the respective single mutants [19]. Homozygous double mutants were isolated from the F2 progeny and confirmed by PCR genotyping and DNA sequencing.

The previous literature has shown that C. elegans primarily feeds on E. coli OP50 under laboratory conditions, and is considered the standard food source for laboratory nematode cultivation [20]. Briefly, synchronized worms at the same developmental stage were placed on Nematode Growth Medium (NGM) plates augmented with TSG (100, 200 and 400 μg/mL) [21]. Worms were allowed to develop from eggs and maintained under these conditions until completion of the assays.

Lifespan assays of C. elegans

Before experimentation, all worms were maintained following standard culturing protocols. Synchronized worms at the same developmental stage were placed on NGM plates supplemented with TSG at concentrations of 100, 200 and 400 μg/mL. Worms were allowed to develop from eggs and maintained under these conditions until completion of the assays. For the lifespan analysis, approximately 45 worms were allocated to each group and monitored daily. Death was confirmed by the absence of response to gentle probing with a platinum wire. Worms were transferred to fresh plates every day, and mortality was recorded throughout the experiment. Survival was tracked until all worms had died, and the resulting data were used to construct survival curves. Each lifespan experiment was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Lifespan assays of tBHP-induced C. elegans

Synchronized worms at the same developmental stage were cultured on NGM plates supplemented with TSG (100, 200 and 400 μg/mL) or individual ginsenoside monomers (200 μM) from the L4 stage until day 1 of adulthood. For lifespan assays, day 1 adult worms from each treatment group were transferred to NGM plates containing 9.125 mM tBHP, and mortality was recorded at hourly intervals until all worms had died. Survival curves were then generated based on the collected data. Each lifespan assay was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Bacterial avoidance and pumping rate assays

The feeding preference assay was performed by placing OP50 and TSG-supplemented OP50 on opposite ends of NGM plates. Approximately 50 worms were positioned centrally, and distribution was recorded after 3 h and 6 h. Trials were repeated three times independently. Pharyngeal pumping was assessed on day 1 of adulthood by counting the number of pharyngeal contractions over a 30-s interval. A total of 20 worms were evaluated for each group.

SOD-3:GFP and GST-4:GFP expression quantitation

Expression levels of GST-4 and SOD-3 were assessed using the CL2166 and CF1553 reporter strains, respectively. Synchronized L4 larvae were maintained on NGM agar plates seeded with OP50 and supplemented with either 200 μg/mL TSG or DMSO as a control. After three days of incubation, worms were transferred to NGM plates containing 9.125 mM tBHP and exposed for 90 min. Subsequently, 20 worms from each group were immobilized on 2% agarose pads using PBS containing 10 μM levamisole. GFP fluorescence intensity, reflecting the expression of GST-4 and SOD-3, was quantified using ImageJ software.

RT-qPCR

Synchronized N2 worms were cultured from eggs to adulthood. On day 1, the worms were given 100, 200 or 400 μg/mL TSG or a DMSO control. Then, the worms were treated with 9.125 mM tBHP for 90 min on NGM plates. Total RNA was extracted from approximately 200 worms using Trizol Reagent (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) and quantified with a NanoDrop 2000/c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RT-qPCR was carried out using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system, with act-1 serving as the endogenous control. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method and is presented as the mean fold change from three independent experiments. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Network pharmacology

The Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) molecular formulas of ginsenoside monomers were acquired from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The Swiss Target Prediction platform was employed to identify target proteins for each ginsenoside monomer. Aging-related target genes were retrieved from GeneCards and OMIM databases and corrected using the UniProt database. The resulting “Network.xlsx” and “type.xlsx” files were imported into Cytoscape 3.7.2 for network mapping. Then, the overlapping target genes associated with ginsenoside anti-aging activity were imported into the STRING database to predict protein-protein interactions. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted via the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/), and the results were visualized using Cytoscape (https://cytoscape.org/).

Molecular docking

For semi-flexible molecular docking, highly relevant anti-aging targets of ginsenoside monomers were selected to assess the binding affinity of ginsenosides. The three-dimensional structures of ginsenoside monomers and proteins were acquired from the PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and PDB (https://www.rcsb.org) databases, respectively. Proteins were processed with PyMOL by removing ligands, water molecules, and small molecular compounds. Furthermore, polar hydrogen atoms were added, and docking analysis was carried out using AutoDock. Docking results were screened based on the principle of minimum energy and visualized with the help of PyMOL software.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were carried out in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA) was employed for all statistical tests. Survival data were compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. The inter-group statistical comparisons were analyzed using a two-tailed Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

TSG delayed aging in tBHP-induced HUVECs

The in vitro senescence model was established by treating HUVECs with tBHP to assess the anti-aging efficacy of TSG [22]. MTT assays were performed to determine the optimal conditions; based on cell viability results, 20 μM tBHP with a 5-h exposure was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 1A-B). To confirm the successful establishment of the HUVECs aging model induced by tBHP, SA-β-gal staining was performed to evaluate the degree of senescence [23]. As shown in Fig. 1D, SA-β-gal activity was significantly elevated in HUVECs treated with tBHP. Similarly, tBHP exposure led to reduced SOD and GSH levels and increased MDA levels (Fig. 1E–G). Given that previous studies have reported a strong association between ROS accumulation and senescence, ROS levels were further assessed using the CM-H2DCFDA fluorescent probe, which passively diffuses into cells [24]. Fluorescence intensity measurements revealed a significant elevation of intracellular ROS in tBHP-treated HUVECs (Fig. 1H). These findings validate tBHP-induced HUVECs as a reliable in vitro model of senescence.

MTT assay determined suitable TSG concentrations by cytotoxicity analysis. As shown in Fig. 1C, TSG concentration of ≤ 200 μg/mL was not cytotoxic within 24 hours. Therefore, low, medium, and high doses (10, 25 and 50 μg/mL) were selected for anti-aging evaluation. The data revealed that TSG treatment dose-dependently reduced SA-β-gal activity (Fig. 1D). Medium and high doses markedly increased SOD and GSH levels while decreasing MDA (Fig. 1E–G). All tested concentrations significantly reduced intracellular ROS accumulation (Fig. 1H). These results indicate that TSG mitigates oxidative stress, boosts antioxidant enzyme activity, and delays aging in tBHP-induced HUVECs.

TSG inhibits cellular senescence in tBHP-induced HUVECs

Cellular senescence involves permanent cell-cycle arrest induced by stress. Ki-67 staining, a marker of cell cycle progression, was used to assess the proportion of SCs [25]. As shown in Fig. 2A, TSG treatment reversed the tBHP-induced decrease in Ki-67 expression, indicating increased cell proliferation. It is well established that levels of p53 and p21 rise during cellular senescence [26]. Phosphorylated histone H2A.X (γ-H2A.X) is recognized as a marker of DNA damage, while Lamin B1, which maintains nuclear membrane integrity, declines in SCs [27]. Consistent with these reports, tBHP exposure elevated the expression of p21, p53 and γ-H2A.X while reducing Lamin B1 expression, confirming the induction of senescence in HUVECs (Fig. 2B). High-dose TSG treatment significantly downregulated p53 and p21 expression and restored Lamin B1 levels (Fig. 2B). Moreover, TSG treatment decreased γ-H2A.X expression in tBHP-induced HUVECs, indicating a reduction in DNA damage (Fig. 2B). In summary, these results demonstrate that TSG alleviates cellular senescence by increasing cell proliferation and modulating key senescence-associated markers in tBHP-induced HUVECs.

TSG prolongs lifespan and delays aging induced by tBHP in C. elegans

Consistent with the in vitro aging model, a rapid-aging nematode model was established by exposing worms to 9.125 mM tBHP to evaluate the in vivo anti-aging efficacy of TSG at concentrations of 100, 200 and 400 μg/mL, based on previous studies [28]. TSG treatment significantly improved the survival of tBHP-induced N2 worms, extending survival rates by 18.85%, 27.70% and 21.88% at concentrations of 100, 200 and 400 μg/mL, respectively (Fig. 3A-B). To further assess the antioxidant capacity of TSG, the mRNA expression of antioxidant enzyme genes gst-4, gst-7, sod-3 and hsp-16.2 was measured in tBHP-treated worms. Compared with the Ctrl group, the expression of these genes was significantly downregulated following exposure to tBHP. However, TSG treatment restored their expression in a dose-dependent manner, with the strongest effect observed at 200 μg/mL (Fig. 3C). The GFP reporter strains revealed that 200 μg/mL TSG increased SOD-3 and GST-4 protein levels (Fig. 3D), which aligns with mRNA results and indicates elevated antioxidant enzyme activity. These results demonstrate that TSG exerts a dose-dependent protective effect against aging in tBHP-induced C. elegans.

To evaluate the impact of TSG on organismal lifespan, the wild-type C. elegans was used as the model. Chronic TSG treatment (100, 200 and 400 μg/mL) during the lifespan resulted in a dose-dependent and significant extension of longevity by 10.95%, 27.64% and 20.59%, respectively (Fig. 3E-F). To exclude potential dietary effects, behavioral assays were performed, which indicated no worm preference for TSG-supplemented OP50 (Fig. 3G-H) and no change in pharyngeal pumping rate (Fig. 3I). This suggests that the observed lifespan extension is not attributed to altered feeding behavior or intake, but rather a direct anti-aging effect of TSG in C. elegans.

TSG-derived ginsenosides PPD, Rh1 and F1 delay aging and inhibit cellular senescence in tBHP-induced HUVECs and C. elegans

It has been observed that TSG typically contains around 20 ginsenoside monomers, mainly classified as dammarane tetracyclic triterpenoids [29]. This study quantified the relative abundances of these ginsenoside monomers by HPLC (Figs. S1–2; Table S2). To rapidly screen bioactive anti-aging components. In total, 20 monomers were tested in a tBHP-induced C. elegans model. Treatment with 200 μM of ginsenosides PPD, Ra2, Rh1, Rg1 and F1 significantly extended worm lifespan under oxidative stress by 18.35%, 15.20%, 13.62%, 13.09% and 9.88%, respectively (Fig. 4A–F). These five ginsenosides were thus identified as the primary anti-aging constituents of TSG for further study.

To verify these effects, SA-β-gal staining in tBHP-induced HUVECs treated with 50 μM of PPD, Ra2, Rh1, Rg1 or F1 showed that all five monomers inhibited senescence (Fig. 5A). At lower doses (10 and 25 μM), only PPD, Rh1 and F1 significantly reduced SA-β-gal activity (Fig. 5B-C). Under the same conditions (10 μM), these three ginsenosides also increased SOD and GSH levels, but decreased MDA and intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 5D–G). Moreover, MTT assay results demonstrated that none of the three ginsenoside monomers significantly affected HUVEC viability at a concentration of 10 μM (Fig. S3). Taken together, ginsenoside PPD, Rh1 and F1 were identified as the primary active constituents of TSG that counteract tBHP-induced aging in both HUVECs and C. elegans.

The inhibitory effects of ginsenoside PPD, Rh1 and F1 on cellular senescence were confirmed. Ki-67 fluorescence staining showed increased expression levels following treatment with these ginsenosides, indicating reduced proportions of SCs (Fig. 6A). Western blotting results further revealed that ginsenoside PPD, Rh1 and F1 suppressed the expression of senescence markers p53, p21 and γ-H2A.X while restoring Lamin B1 levels in tBHP-induced HUVECs (Fig. 6B). These results suggest that ginsenoside PPD, Rh1 and F1 serve as active components in TSG, effectively delaying cellular senescence induced by tBHP.

Potential TSG targets against aging and cellular senescence were identified in silico

Network pharmacology was employed to explore the anti-aging mechanism of TSG. The Venn diagram identified 106 overlapping targets shared by TSG and aging-related genes (Fig. 7A; Table S3). Ginsenosides have been classified into protopanaxadiol (PPD) and protopanaxatriol (PPT) types based on their C-6 hydroxylation [30]. Here, the results revealed 83 aging-related targets for PPD-type saponins and 43 for PPT-type (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, a “ginsenosides-aging targets” network mapping ginsenosides to aging targets was constructed, detailing the specific targets for each monomer (Fig. 7C-D; Table S4). Moreover, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was established to identify core anti-aging targets of TSG. The analysis of 106 potential targets using STRING and Cytoscape 3.9.1 revealed 10 key targets (association degree ≥ 28, top 15%), including STAT3, AKT1, HSP90AA1, PIK3CA, ESR1, VEGFA, KDR, MTOR, IL2 and AR (Fig. 7E). Similarly, STAT3, VEGFA, IL2, HSP90AA1 and ESR1 had the highest interaction potential with ginsenoside monomers (Table S5). These results indicate the key targets through which TSG and its active ingredients, particularly ginsenoside PPD, Rh1 and F1, may exert their anti-aging and senescence-inhibiting effects.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the intersecting targets were performed using Metascape. Based on adjusted p< 0.05, 167 biological processes (BPs), 54 cellular components (CCs) and 82 molecular functions (MFs) were identified. The top GO 10 terms in each category are presented in Fig. 7F. Core targets enriched in these terms included AKT1, MTOR, AR, HSP90AA1, STAT3 and ESR1 (Table S6). KEGG analysis revealed 123 pathways, with the top 20 displayed in Fig. 7G. The primarily enriched pathways included the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Tables S7–8), a conserved aging-related pathway [31]. These results demonstrate that the PI3K-AKT pathway, mediated by key targets AKT1, HSP90AA1 and PIK3CA, is a critical mechanism by which TSG delays aging.

TSG and ginsenosides PPD, Rh1 and F1 regulate the PI3K/AKT phosphorylation in tBHP-induced HUVECs

The PI3K/AKT pathway is a central regulator of aging. Its dysregulation accelerates aging, whereas controlled inhibition extends lifespan [32]. Targeting its core components, PI3K and AKT, is therefore a key anti-aging strategy, as demonstrated by lifespan extension with the AKT inhibitor MK-2206 [33]. Furthermore, the molecular chaperone HSP90 promotes aging by stabilizing AKT [34]. Based on the relevance of these proteins in aging and predictions from network pharmacology, molecular docking was conducted to evaluate the binding potential of TSG-derived ginsenosides F1, Rh1 and PPD with the identified targets. It was observed that all three ginsenosides could potentially form stable complexes with AKT1, with binding affinities of -9.3, -9.0 and -7.6 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 8A). Moreover, they also had a binding potential to PI3K, with affinities of -8.7 (F1), -9.3 (Rh1) and -8.9 (PPD) kcal/mol (Fig. 8A). However, their interaction was weaker for HSP90. This study then evaluated whether TSG affects the PI3K-AKT pathway in tBHP-induced HUVECs. TSG treatment significantly reduced the phosphorylation of both AKT and PI3K, but did not substantially affect HSP90 protein levels (Fig. 8B). The key ginsenoside constituents PPD, Rh1 and F1 inhibited AKT and PI3K phosphorylation, while also leaving HSP90 expression unchanged. These results indicate that TSG and its active ginsenosides inhibit the PI3K-AKT pathway, potentially by direct binding that reduces kinase phosphorylation; however, further validation is required.

TSG and ginsenoside PPD, Rh1 and F1 delays aging induced by tBHP via the PI3K-AKT pathway

To investigate the dependency of TSG on the PI3K-AKT pathway, SC79 was selected as an AKT activator. SC79 specifically binds the PH domain of the AKT protein, and induces a conformational change to make AKT more favorable for phosphorylation and activation by upstream kinases [35]. Firstly, the optimal SC79 concentration for activating AKT was determined by assessing p-AKT (Ser473) levels via WB [36]. Results indicated that SC79 induced a concentration-dependent increase in p-AKT, and a concentration of 2 μM was sufficient to achieve stable and robust activation (Fig. 9A). SA-β-gal staining revealed that 2 μM SC79 significantly inhibited SA-β-gal activity, highlighting its anti-aging effect (Fig. 9B). Furthermore, TSG treatment effectively reversed SC79-induced SA-β-gal inhibition (Fig. 9B). IF staining of Ki-67 showed that SC79 mono-treatment significantly reduced the percentage of Ki-67 positive cells compared with the Ctrl group, indicating decreased cell proliferation and elevated cellular senescence, which could effectively be reversed by TSG (Fig. 9C). Moreover, WB was performed to detect the protein expression levels of senescence-related markers. The results showed that, compared with the Ctrl group, the SC79 treatment group had a significant senescent phenotype, with increased p21, p53 and γ-H2A.X protein expression and decreased Lamin B1 levels via p-AKT activation (Fig. 9D). However, co-treatment with TSG and SC79 could effectively reverse these changes by partially restoring AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 9D). These results indicate that TSG exerts anti-aging and anti-cellular senescence effects by inhibiting AKT phosphorylation.

Research on C. elegans, a widely accepted model of aging, has demonstrated that the mechanisms modulating lifespan are evolutionarily conserved [37]. In C. elegans, age-1 and akt-1/2 are significantly homologous to PI3K and AKT, and have broad effects on longevity and aging induced by multiple factors [38]. Loss-of-function mutants age-1(hx546) and akt-1(ok525);akt-2(ok393), were employed to identify whether the anti-aging effects of TSG and its main active monomers depend on the AKT-PI3K pathway in vivo. The loss-of-function mutation of age-1 and akt-1/2 genes significantly prolonged the lifespan of worms exposed to tBHP to 52.84% and 48.74%. (Fig. 9E-F). Compared with the Ctrl, the age-1 mutant indicated a significant increase in average lifespan of 8.21% when exposed to TSG (Fig. 9E). Due to the 27.70% average increase in the lifespan of wild-type worms exposed to TSG compared to the Ctrl, it was inferred that the lifespan extension by TSG is partially dependent on age-1. This data was consistent with the akt-1;akt-2 double mutants' lifespan assays (Fig. 9F). Furthermore, by utilizing 100 μM rapamycin, which directly targets the TOR kinase to extend lifespan [39], we have eliminated the ceiling effect in lifespan assay of tBHP-induced age-1 worms (Fig. S4). These in vivo results indicated that TSG's anti-aging effect is partly dependent on the PI3K-AKT pathway.

The mutants exposed to TSG's main active monomers, PPD, Rh1 and F1, indicated no significant difference in the lifespan extension compared with the Ctrl group (Fig. 9E-F), suggesting that ginsenoside PPD, Rh1 and F1 delay aging induced by tBHP via the PI3K-AKT pathway in C. elegans.

Discussion

Traditional Chinese medicine has a rich history of anti-aging practices, with historical texts highlighting ginseng as a key longevity promoter [40]. Modern research supports ginseng's anti-aging potential, and its primary active components TSG, demonstrate broad protective effects across multiple organ systems [41]. However, the precise mechanisms of TSG remain partially determined, motivating the current mechanistic investigation. This study developed a rapid dual-system model for aging research and showed that TSG and its major ginsenosides (PPD, F1 and Rh1) attenuate aging phenotypes and cellular senescence by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT pathway by suppressing PI3K and AKT phosphorylation, thus providing basic mechanistic evidence supporting TSG's potential as an anti-aging intervention.

Aging is a biological process that causes age-related diseases such as diabetes and hypertension, impacting longevity [42]. Although extending lifespan is a key goal of anti-aging research, delaying aging does not always increase longevity [43]. Therefore, recent research focuses on healthspan, prolonging healthy living, and improving life quality in later years [44]. Our previous studies showed that TSG extends both lifespan and healthspan in C. elegans by activating NRF2/SKN-1, SIRT1/SIR2.1 and FOXO/DAF-16 pathways, and also delays aging in human cells. However, whether TSG can extend lifespan in mice or humans remains elusive due to limited samples and ethical constraints. During aging, SCs accumulate in various tissues, thus progressively losing their physiological functions and adversely affecting tissue integrity and function [45]. The excessive accumulation of SCs in tissues promotes chronic diseases and organ aging, playing a central role in the human aging process [46]. Here, it was observed that TSG delays senescence in aging models; however, further research is required to determine whether it benefits by reducing senescent cell numbers or directly inhibits senescence mechanisms.

TSG and its major ginsenosides (PPD, F1 and Rh1) inhibit PI3K and AKT phosphorylation, thus inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and attenuating aging-associated phenotypes and cellular senescence. AKT1 and PIK3CA are central components of the PI3K-AKT pathway, a key regulator of apoptosis, proliferation, and metabolism that is strongly implicated in aging and related diseases [47]. Moreover, AKT1 activity is stabilized by the chaperone HSP90AA1, while oncogenic mutations in PIK3CA drive tumor progression through pathway activation [48]. Other than its canonical role, PI3K-AKT signaling interacts with other effectors such as STAT3 [49], forming an integrated network. This indicates that the anti-aging targets of TSG cannot act in isolation but via multi-target cascades, consistent with ginseng's characteristic multi-components and multi-pathway bioactivities; however, in vivo confirmation warrants further analyses.

In this study, 9.125 mM and 20 μM tBHP were used to induce oxidative stress in both C. elegans and HUVECs, respectively, to establish a rapid dual-system model for aging research. tBHP is a potent oxidant that induces acute cellular senescence via oxidative stress at low concentrations, whereas its acute toxicity directly stimulates necrosis at high concentrations [50]. Unlike disordered and passive cell death, acute cellular senescence triggers a highly ordered form of apoptosis [51]. In this study, the results of SA-β-gal staining indicate that 20 μM tBHP treatment significantly increases cellular senescence. Moreover, to determine whether the selected tBHP concentration successfully induced cellular senescence rather than acute toxicity-induced necrosis, cell membrane integrity was evaluated using AO/PI staining to directly quantify the proportion of viable cells [52]. Staurosporine (20 μM) was used as a positive control, and the percentage of viable cells after its treatment was < 50%, with red fluorescence (membrane-compromised cells) predominating, consistent with a typical acute cytotoxicity phenotype (Fig. S5A). AO/PI double-staining revealed that treatment with 20 μM tBHP yielded > 80% viable cells, with the majority displaying green fluorescence (indicating live cells), only a small fraction of PI-positive (red) dead cells (Fig. S5A). There were no significant differences in viability compared with the Ctrl group, suggesting that 20 μM tBHP does not cause widespread acute plasma membrane damage leading to cell death. This confirms the feasibility of establishing an in vitro accelerated senescence model in HUVECs using 20 μM tBHP.

In C. elegans, we employed AO single staining to distinguish whether the lifespan reduction induced by tBHP was due to acute toxicity or rapid aging. Healthy nematodes actively expel AO, yielding minimal fluorescence, while damaged cells with impaired efflux pumps and DNA fragmentation trap AO, resulting in stronger green fluorescence indicative of more severe injury [53]. Similar to the previous studies, fluorescence intensity results indicated that control group had less than 20% green fluorescence, while the staurosporine exposure group (positive control) exhibited intense green fluorescence (Fig. S5B). 9.125 mM tBHP-treated worms exhibited less than 50% green fluorescence intensity, significantly lower than that of the positive control group, confirming that this concentration of tBHP does not induce acute damage in C. elegans. These findings support the feasibility of establishing a chemically induced accelerated aging model in C. elegans using 9.125 mM tBHP.

The tBHP-induced rapid senescence model closely recapitulates key features of physiological aging in vivo while also exhibiting distinct characteristics. Based on previous reports and the results of this study, tBHP-induced rapid oxidative stress-induced senescence and physiological aging share multiple core cellular and molecular features [54]. Here, first, both indicated p53/p21 pathway upregulation; treatment with 20 μM tBHP substantially increased the expression of p53 and p21, consistent with the molecular alterations characteristic of replicative senescence [55]. Second, increased SA-β-gal activity is a well-recognized hallmark of senescence, and can be reliably detected in both replicative senescence resulting from long-term in vitro passaging and in senescence induced by acute tBHP stimulation [56]. Moreover, ROS accumulation is a key driver of aging, and tBHP rapidly mimics this process via exogenous oxidative stress, reflecting, at least to some extent, the consequences of endogenous oxidative damage during physiological aging [57]. However, tBHP-induced rapid senescence and physiological aging have certain differences. Physiological aging takes place within a complex organism, involving multiple factors such as immune surveillance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and metabolic changes, whereas the tBHP-treated cell model is developed by inducing simplified oxidative stress conditions [58]; therefore, it cannot fully recapitulate the multi-factorial interactions of in vivo aging. Future studies should more closely recapitulate physiological aging, perform cross-validation using replicative senescence models or naturally aged animal models for more comprehensive and robust conclusions.

Based on traditional efficacy and pharmacological research on the neuroprotective effects of TSG [59], in addition to HUVECs, this study also evaluated the anti-senescence effects of TSG in tBHP-induced SH-SY5Y neuronal cells using phenotypic assays. Consistent with observations in endothelial cells, TSG treatment significantly reduced SA-β-gal positivity, intracellular ROS levels and MDA content in neuronal cells, while elevating SOD and GSH activities (Fig. S6). These results indicate that TSG effectively ameliorates oxidative stress and inhibits senescence-associated phenotype not only in vascular endothelial cells but also in neuron-derived cells. Although this study does not include mechanistic pathway validation in neurons, the significant consistency of the phenotypic readouts across two distinct cell types suggests that the anti-senescence effect of TSG may not be cell-type-restricted. Based on the conserved roles of PI3K-AKT signaling across various cell lineages [60], it can be hypothesized that the key mechanisms identified in HUVECs are shared by neuronal cells. Further studies based on targeted inhibitors or gene knockdown in neuronal models are required to confirm the pathway dependence. However, the cross-cellular consistency of the phenotypic improvements partially supports the generalizability of the acquired results and highlights the translational potential of TSG for combating senescence in diverse tissues.

Before assessing the anti-aging effects of the 20 ginsenoside monomers selected in this investigation, HPLC analysis was conducted to elucidate their relative abundances in TSG. Based on their relative contents in TSG, the ginsenoside monomers were divided into three categories (Table S2): major (content > 10%), medium (content of 1%-10%), and minor (content < 1%). The five ginsenoside monomers identified as the primary ginsenosides included PPD (16.16%), Rh1 (15.00%), F1 (12.04%), Rg1 (11.17%) and Ra2 (10.76%), which together account for 65.13% of the TSG content. However, the conclusion drawn in this study based solely on the HPLC results has limitations. Based on the existing literature, 20 monomers were selected, which may have omitted certain unidentified bioactive components. Moreover, some identified ginsenoside monomers, though less extensively studied, were not included in the analysis. To further confirm these results, future research should use metabolomics techniques and Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS). The compositional profile of the enrolled components in high-level models (such as the aging mouse model) should be systematically analyzed to help identify the active ingredients and underlying mechanisms of TSG's anti-aging effects.

This study developed a tBHP-induced C. elegans model to rapidly assess the longevity-promoting properties of 20 ginsenoside monomers from TSG. The ginsenosides PPD, Ra2, Rh1, Rg1 and F1 considerably increased the lifetime of the rapidly aging C. elegans model after administration at the same dose (Fig. 4B–F). Furthermore, these five compounds are also the main saponins detected by HPLC in TSG. This result suggests that major saponins may contribute to TSG's ability to prolong lifespan in the fast-aging C. elegans model. Moreover, several previous studies suggest that these saponins have lifespan-extending potential in various model systems [61], indicating the feasibility of the rapidly aging C. elegans model for lifespan screening used in this study, as well as the definitive lifespan-extending effects of these ginsenoside monomers. Our previous study showed that ginsenosides Re and Rb1 also positively extend the lifespan of wild-type C. elegans [28], which contrasts with our negative results. This contradiction necessitates incorporating additional aging models (such as rapid-replication-induced senescence) in future studies to enable a systematic evaluation of lifespan-extending efficacy.

SA-β-gal staining results in the rapid aging cell model revealed that although all 5 ginsenosides had anti-aging activity at 50 μM (Fig. 5A), only PPD, F1 and Rh1 indicated significant anti-aging effects at lower concentrations (10 and 25 μM) (Fig. 5B-C), suggesting that these three compounds possess superior anti-aging potency. Therefore, PPD, F1 and Rh1 were identified as the representative ginsenosides among the five major saponins, and thus selected for subsequent efficacy evaluation and mechanistic investigation in the rapid-aging cell model. The relatively potent anti-aging effects of PPD, F1 and Rh1 may modulate aging-associated molecular targets and signaling pathways [62]. For instance, PPD, as the most abundant monomer, possesses a PPD-type skeleton that may more readily enter the nucleus to regulate the p53 and p21. Rh1 and F1, as PPT-type saponins, may rapidly initiate antioxidant responses through membrane receptors [63]. Moreover, Rg1 has been identified to exert anti-aging effects [64]; however, its lack of significant efficacy in our experimental model may be attributable to the inherent characteristics of the tBHP-induced acute oxidative injury model. Due to the multi-target and multi-efficacy properties of ginsenosides, the anti-aging effects of ginsenoside monomers warrant further investigation across diverse experimental models.

The translational significance of this study would be improved by pharmacokinetic (PK) data, particularly systemic exposure levels after oral ginseng administration. The existing PK results show that, oral administration of total ginsenosides in rats (200-400 mg/kg) of 30 days yields detectable plasma levels of prototypes like Rb1 and Rg1 [65], with Rg1 absorption being significantly better from the total formulation than from the monomer alone [66], indicating that other components in TSG may facilitate absorption; thus the overall in vivo TSG exposure, reflected by the combined AUC of multiple components, may be higher than single-monomer reports, and even low individual concentrations could produce synergistic tissue-level effects. In terms of the active ginsenoside monomers, the plasma AUC of its metabolites Rh1 and F1 were 4185.5 and 3774.3 ng·mL-1·h, respectively, following oral administration of ginsenoside Rg1 to rats. The corresponding blood drug concentration levels peaked at 3-5 hours after oral administration; PPD steady state concentrations are also in the 2-6 μM range after oral ginsenoside PPD (75 mg/kg) (AUC0-24: 3-8 μg·mL-1·h). Here, ginsenoside monomers were administered to the senescent cell model at a dose of 10 μM, which is marginally higher than the observed plasma levels. According to published research, PPD is detectable in both tissues and plasma, exhibits high plasma protein binding, and is distributed relatively evenly in vivo [67]. Since plasma protein binding was absent in vitro and there was no tissue- or organ-mediated local accumulation, a concentration marginally higher than the plasma exposure level was selected for the in vitro anti-aging efficacy assays. In the future, in vivo mouse studies should be conducted within the PK concentration range.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates how TSG may counteract the senogenic effects of tBHP, delaying aging and preventing cellular senescence through the PI3K-AKT pathway. It also developed a rapid dual-system screening approach for aging research. AKT1 and PIK3CA emerged as potential targets, and the key bioactive ginsenosides PPD, Rh1 and F1 were shown to contribute to these anti-aging effects. These results promote the development of treatments for aging-related diseases, providing pharmacological and molecular insights into ginseng's anti-aging effects.
