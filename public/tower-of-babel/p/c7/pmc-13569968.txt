Helical Folding of Abiotic Chiral Poly(phosphodiester)s
Authors: Ranajit Barman, Brice Kauffmann, Jean‐François Lutz, Victor Maurizot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFunctions of biomolecules are intimately linked to their structures which result from the folding of a linear sequence of building blocks, amino‐acids for proteins and nucleotides for DNA. In the quest to achieve functions with similar efficiency and selectivity that of biomolecules, several synthetic mimics of secondary structures of proteins have been developed but, on the other hand, only scares examples of de novo design of DNA mimics are available. Most of described DNA inspired molecular systems are based on modifications of either the sugar/phosphate backbone or nucleobases. Here are presented the design, the synthesis and the structural characterization of a new class of synthetic phosphodiester oligomers, composed of an aromatic chiral building block, that fold into single helical architectures. Folding pattern diverge from its natural counterpart (DNA) but through intramolecular H‐bonds and aromatic stacking these oligomers show similar cooperative temperature dependence stability.

Introduction

Biomacromolecules, such as proteins and DNA, are linear monodisperse polymers composed of a perfectly defined sequence of building blocks (22 amino acids for proteins and 4 nucleobases for DNA). Folding of these molecules into well‐defined molecular architectures is one of the key features that define their functions. The second aspect of these biopolymers is there specific sequence that not only drive their specific folding and induce functions but can also be use as information storage. From these two observations, chemists have devoted efforts to produce synthetic oligomeric mimics of these biopolymers to either, copy their functions or, since it is closely related to their structure, to mimic their folding. From there, two different approaches can be depicted [1]: (a) the top‐down approach that take inspiration from natural building blocks that constitute the biopolymers, developing units resembling their natural parents to understand key features of the folding principles and to produce new architectures with enhance biological properties. (b) The bottom‐up approach that try to produce oligomers with predicted folded molecular architectures resembling the secondary structures of biopolymers using building block that are chemically remote from the natural monomeric units. Two distinct research lines, depending on the nature of the parent model biomolecules, can be drawn: the protein inspired model and the DNA inspired model. Due to the large diversity of functions and structures observed for proteins, and their major roles in the different biological processes, it is not surprising that proposing peptides mimics have attracted the greatest attention. A large number of peptide synthetic mimics have been proposed through the top‐down approach such as the replacement of the α‐aminoacids by β‐ or γ‐aminoacids, replacement of the amide connection by urea, triazine or N‐alkylated amide (peptoids) [2]. The bottom‐up approach is not left out, and many aromatic oligomers have been developed where aromatic units can be connected directly through single C─C bonds [3], alkyne linkers [4, 5], amine functions [6] or like natural peptides using amide bonds [7, 8, 9]. Aromatic oligoamides have been designed to fold in helical and sheet‐like structures where their stepwise synthesis allows sequence modulation and fine tuning of properties of each oligomer. For example, functionalization of these rigid helices at specific positions has allowed to produce higher order assemblies [10, 11, 12], and bioactive compounds that can interact with proteins to modulate protein‐protein and protein‐DNA interactions [13, 14, 15]. On the DNA side modifications of the backbone, following the top‐down approach, have also been implemented with the objective to modulate complementary strands recognition [9, 16]. The most studied case is the preparation of PNA which outcompete duplex formation with native DNA than DNA itself but many other modifications of the phosphodiester linkage have been proposed. However, in the majority of these researches, base pairing was the predominant objective or the driving force of the structuration of the oligomer. Looking at the DNAs bottom up approach, few research groups have tried to developed artificial oligomers that adopt controlled folded architectures based on oligophosphodiester backbone, most of them rely on the solvophobicity and the electronic complementarity of aromatic units to specifically aggregate in water. Serpell and Morvan have developed oligomers with electron rich and electron deficient aromatic building block that stacked to produce compact structures [17, 18, 19]. Häner has proposed synthetic building blocks composed of large aromatic panels such as pyrene derived oligophosphodiester (pyrenotides) for which, by playing with salt concentration and temperature, the formation of a continuous stack of aromatic units with a helix shape architecture has been demonstrated [20]. Similar arrangement has been observed for phenantrene and anthracene building blocks [21, 22, 23]. In some cases double helix formation, similar to DNA, has been described for these arenotides.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Solution Studies

In order to have a better understanding of intramolecular forces driving the self‐organization of these oligomers and to decipher design principle for the de novo construction of folded oligophosphodiester, we have proposed to study the folding behavior of an oligomeric model composed of a small electron poor pyridinedicarboxylic acid units connected with short chiral linkers and phosphate connectors. The design of the monomeric unit relies on the use of 2,6‐pyridinedicarboxamide units, for which conformational rotation of aryl‐carbonyl bonds are restricted by intramolecular hydrogen bonds that rigidify the system. Short chiral alkyl chains (methyl‐ethanolamine) connecting aromatic ring to the phosphate group have been implemented to limit flexibility and the chiral center introduced to monitor the folding ability of the oligomers. It is important to note that the two amide‐NH of this monomeric unit are pointing in the same direction and are available for interacting with H‐bond acceptors. The oligomers were all prepared by automated phosphoramidite chemistry [24]. Originally developed for oligonucleotide synthesis [25, 26], this chemistry also enables the creation of abiotic macromolecules [27, 28, 29]. For instance, our group has extensively applied it to produce synthetic informational polymers [30, 31], stereocontrolled polymers [32] and bio‐hybrid macromolecules [33, 34]. Phosphoramidite monomer 1 was prepared in three steps from pyridinedicarboxylic acid (Scheme S1). Amide coupling with the chiral methyl‐ethanolamine results in the dihydroxy compound that is subsequently reacted with dimetoxytritylchloride to statisticaly afford the disymetrized mono‐alcohol. The hydroxyl function is then converted to the phosphoramidite precursor to be engaged in oligomerization reaction on solid support. For practical reasons, commercially available solid support was purchase with a preloaded thymidine nucleobase, resin for which synthetic protocols were optimized in previous studies [30, 31, 32]. As a consequence, thymidine unit was present at the extremity of all oligomers but was shown to have no influence on structuration. Folding propensity of these oligomers was investigated using Circular Dichroism measurement. Oligomers P1 and P6‐P8 (5, 10, 15, and 20 mer) were first dissolved in distilled water in an approximate 0.5 mg∙mL−1 concentration and UV‐CD spectra were recorded. CD signal was normalized according to the absorbance of oligomers at 250 nm at 20°C in order to have an apparent constant concentration per monomer for each compound and to be compared. CD spectra showed a length dependence behavior as illustrated in Figure 1b. The 5‐mer P1 showed two positive bands at 210 and 245 nm and two negative bands at 222 and 270–275 nm while 20‐mer P8 only shows one positive band at 220 nm and two negative bands, at different wavelength compared to P1, at 240 and 278 nm. These shifts operating with the increase of the oligomers length indicate difference in the chiral organization of chromophores. Oligomers solution were then subjected to temperature variation from 60°C to 5°C (Figures 2 and S1). For the shortest oligomer P1, no significant changes of their UV‐CD spectra, nor in intensity nor in wavelength maxima (Figure 2a), can be observed while for longer oligomers dramatic changes appears at low temperature. For example, at temperatures between 60°C and 30°C, P8 shows spectra similar to P1 but upon cooling bellow 30°C several modifications of the spectrum are observed: an increase of the intensity at 220 nm, a pseudo‐isobestic point at 226 nm, a new negative maximum at 240 nm and an increase in intensity of the negative band centered at 278 nm (Figure 2b). To monitor the influence of the temperature for each oligomer P1–P8, melting curves of the CD signal at 240 were plotted (Figure 2c). From this graph, we can observe that at high temperature (T>30°C) all oligomers show similar constant relative intensity which can be attribute to a nonstructured architecture with signals reflecting only the presence of chiral centers in the oligomer. When cooling down solutions, it appears that oligomers longer than five units are subject to an increase of their CD signal at 240 nm. This observation is in agreement with a chiral structuration of the oligomers at low temperature presumably in a helical structure. The intensity of this change increase with the length of the oligomer. For the 20mer P8 after a sharp increase of the absorption between 30°C and 10°C further cooling to 5°C shows leveling of the intensity and the appearance of a plateau which indicate that a stable structuration of the oligomer has been reached. For P8 a melting temperature of about 18°C can be determined. It is interesting to see that, for every oligomer, intensity changes start at similar temperature, around 30°C in distilled water, indicating that the initial driving force—interaction—for the folding is similar for all compounds. What differ, then, is the slope and the intensity of the change which shows a sharper transition for longer oligomers and indicate a cooperative behavior. This cooperativity is in agreement with the stabilization of the folded architecture through the aromatic stacking of monomeric units. In order to see if intermolecular interactions were involved, the 10mer solution (P6) was diluted five time to an approximate concentration of 30 µM and the melting curve was reproduced (Figure S2). No significant change was observed upon this dilution which allowed us to exclude intermolecular phenomenon. Ionic strength of a DNA solution is an important parameter of duplex stability. Indeed, the presence of cation minimize the self‐repulsive forces of the phosphate anion by shielding their charges and stabilize the duplex. In order to evaluate the influence of salts on folding propensity, two solutions of P8 respectively in PBS (Phosphate‐buffered saline containing 137 mM NaCl) and in a 1 M ammonium acetate solution were prepared and CD measurement was reproduced (Figure S3). Melting curves obtained for these solutions showed similar sigmoidal shape that of distilled water but melting temperature was increased from 18°C to 25°C in PBS and to 30°C in 1 M ammonium acetate solution, indicative of a stabilization of the folded architecture and a close proximity of phosphate ions in the folded state. In order to evaluate the role of intramolecular hydrogen bonds between the amide NH and the endocyclic nitrogen of the pyridine units that rigidify the scaffold of the monomeric block, an oligomer composed of ten benzene units replacing pyridine units, homologous to P6 was synthesized (PH6 in SI). This oligomer, for which no stabilizing intramolecular hydrogen bonds are present, is expected to offer more conformational freedom around the amide‐aryl bond. Folding propensity of this oligomer was evaluated through temperature dependence CD analysis similarly to the previous pyridine series (Figure S2c). For this compound no temperature dependence behavior was observed, CD spectra remain similar at every temperature which lead us to the conclusion that no folding could be induce by lowering the temperature. This result emphasize the importance of the pre‐organization imposed by the pyridine unit and the presence of intramolecular hydrogen bonds despite the expected weakening of such bonds in aqueous media.

Solid State Study

To get structural information of the folded architecture, attempts to get single crystals for Xray diffraction analysis of the different oligomers were investigated but remained unsuccessful. In order to maximize the chance to obtain crystals, a simpler symmetrical oligomer P9 composed of three pyridine units and two phosphodiester linker was prepared (Figure 3a). This trimer was prepared in solution from the phosphoramidite precursor 1 used for solid phase synthesis (Scheme S2). Two equivalents of 1 were reacted with the pyridinedicarboxamide dialcohol to produce the diphosphate. After oxidation with iodine in pyridine the trialkylphosphate was purified using recycling GPC. The resulting protected compound was then deprotected first with trichloroacetic acid to remove DMT groups then with ammonium hydroxide solution to afford the dialkylphosphate as the ammonium salt. This compound was then dissolved in distilled water and subject to crystallization screens using commercially available sets. Single crystals suitable for x‐ray diffraction analysis were obtained from ammonium sulfate solution (1 M) [35]. The structure of the oligomer reveals a folded left‐handed helix with a symmetrical C2 axis crossing the central pyridine unit, making both extremities equivalent. All amide‐NH are involved in hydrogen bond with the endocyclic nitrogen of the neighboring pyridine unit as initially design. At the two ended units, these hydrogens are also bound to the phosphate moieties which stabilize the folded architecture. From the x‐ray analysis it is not possible to distinguish the saturation degree of P‐O linkages (single or double bond). The bond length between the two non‐ester oxygens and the phosphorus atom of the phosphate are equivalent (1.47 and 1.52 Å) which indicate a delocalization of the negative charge on the two oxygen atoms. Amide protons of the central unit appear not to be involved in H‐bond with phosphate moieties, one phosphate can only be bound to one pyridine unit and the oligomer is composed of three pyridine units for two phosphates. However, it is easy to imagine that, upon few bond rotations, one phosphate can navigate between two positions to form H‐bond with units that are in position n and n+1. This switch may be facilitated by the charge delocalization that prevent the need of chirality inversion of the phosphorus center. In the obtained crystal structure, no intramolecular aromatic stacking is observed, the distance between pyridine rings is about 7Å which allows, in the packing, intercalation of aromatic units of the neighbor molecules creating an infinite 1D assembly of helices. This assembly is presumably induced by the crystal packing during crystal grows since no spectroscopic concentration dependence could be observed in solution. In order to confirm the prevalence of the helical structure of the oligomer in solution, molecular mechanic was performed using the crystal structure as the starting point of the minimization. The calculated structure shows a compact helical architecture with a shifted stack of pyridine units. H‐bonds observed in the crystal are maintained and no obvious steric hindrance is observed. The switch in the position of the phosphate discuss earlier—H‐bond with the n or n+1 pyridine units—was also investigated and the molecular model produced showed only a slight change in the orientation of the pyridine units but did not disturb the overall shape of the helix in agreement with the two possible positions of the phosphate moiety. Longer oligomers were also modeled and showed a stable continuous network of H‐bond resulting in a single helix architecture.

Conclusion

This study shows that simple chiral building blocks can be used to produce a new type of single helical foldamer using phosphodiester linkage. Phosphate connectors can be design to be involved in H‐bond and induce the folding of the oligomers. The versatility of the phophoramidite chemistry used to prepare these oligomers can be applied to produce longer polymers with controlled size and sequence. The modularity of the sequence, by the use of several different monomers, will allow to fine tune the molecular structure and the stability of the oligomer and consequently its properties. Efforts done to produce a library of building blocks to be implemented in oligomers are in progress and will be described in due course. The presence of a charged phosphate backbone confers a great water solubility to these objects, despite the presence of aromatic units, that can be valuable to produce compound with biological activities. These helices can be decorated with proteogenic sidechains in order to interact with protein or DNA, as already demonstrated for other abiotic foldamers.
