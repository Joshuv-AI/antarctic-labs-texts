Self-Management Strategies for Treating Pediatric and Adolescent Headaches and Migraines with Over-the-Counter Molecules: Expert Opinion and Literature Review
Authors: Christopher Oakley, Preeti Kachroo, Ashoke Mitra, John Bell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute pain associated with headaches and migraines in children and adolescents may be managed with over-the-counter (OTC) treatments (e.g., paracetamol and ibuprofen); however, there are few studies on their safety and effectiveness in these patients. This narrative review evaluates the use of OTC treatments in children and adolescents with headaches and migraines.MethodsA literature search of Embase, MEDLINE, ToxFile, and Derwent Drug File was performed for reviews of clinical studies and meta-analyses (January 2010–October 2023) related to the acute treatment of headaches and migraines in children (1 month–11 years) and adolescents (12–18 years) with OTC analgesics (paracetamol, ibuprofen, aspirin, and naproxen). The results of the literature search were interpreted alongside expert recommendations per real-world clinical experience.ResultsTwenty-eight articles were identified, of which half included recommendations that either paracetamol or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs; typically, ibuprofen) were appropriate first-line OTC treatments for headache and/or migraine in children and adolescents. The remaining studies recommended ibuprofen and/or other NSAIDs (particularly naproxen) exclusively or preferentially over paracetamol. Four studies noted that aspirin was appropriate for adolescent patients > 16 years of age. An overall lack of clinical evidence on children and adolescents was noted, particularly regarding combinations of paracetamol and NSAIDs, and adjuvants such as caffeine.ConclusionParacetamol appears to be effective for treating headaches and migraines in children and adolescents; however, evidence is inconclusive regarding the equivalence or superiority of NSAIDs for the same indication. Clinical judgment and patient or caregiver preferences should guide medication selection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40122-025-00783-x.Keywords: Acetaminophen, Analgesics, Headache disorders, Migraine disorders, Non-prescription drugs, Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, Paracetamol, Pediatrics, Self-care

Key Summary Points

Over-the-counter (OTC) treatments are important in management of headaches and migraines in children and adolescents.This narrative review examines the literature on use of OTC analgesics (i.e., paracetamol, ibuprofen, aspirin, and naproxen) for this indication.Both paracetamol and ibuprofen may be appropriate with inconclusive evidence regarding the equivalence or superiority of either drug.

Introduction

Headaches and migraines in children and adolescents can cause distress to both the patient and their caregivers. These health conditions are recognized as among the most prevalent and debilitating in the world [1, 2]. Notably, the overall mean prevalence of headaches among children and adolescents ranges from 43% to 66%, while for migraines it ranges from 7% to 11% [3], with variations based on age, sex, and region [4, 5]. The conditions can be severely disabling, with drastic reductions in quality of life and significant socioeconomic impacts that can be comparable to those in arthritis or patients with cancer [6–10]. Furthermore, these conditions tend to persist over time, with approximately 70% of patients continuing to experience symptoms even after 30 years [11].

Caregivers are typically able to provide acute abortive care for headaches and migraines without recourse to medical professionals. Interventions appropriate for the self-care space include a range of non-pharmacological therapies [12–14] and pharmacological medications [15–20]. Specific pharmacological treatments such as triptans, gepants, and dihydroergotamine are usually only available through prescription, but non-specific analgesics (i.e., paracetamol/acetaminophen) and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as ibuprofen, are widely available over-the-counter (OTC).

The use of non-specific OTC medications for acute abortive treatment of headaches and migraines is common and encouraged in the self-care setting, even for younger patients. At least two-thirds of children appear to respond well to currently available therapies [18]. However, there appears to be a critical knowledge gap among primary healthcare providers regarding the use of these medications, leading to uncertainty about their efficacy and safety for children and adolescents. This lack of confidence may result in unnecessary medical consultations and strain on the public health system. Complicating matters is the difficulty for many children to adequately articulate their complaints, as well as the potential for non-neurological conditions such as infections, allergies, and gastrointestinal disorders to mimic the symptoms of headaches and migraines [21, 22]. Moreover, OTC treatments may be misused as a result of limited knowledge regarding their side effects and contraindications [23]. Clearly, there is a need for improved awareness of OTC treatments for headaches and migraines among caregivers.

Primary care providers (PCPs; including general practitioners, pediatricians, and pharmacists) play a pivotal role in guiding and reassuring caregivers on the use of OTC therapies. However, the knowledge gap concerning first-line abortive treatment with OTC molecules may even extend to the realm of clinical evidence. The issue is compounded at an expert level because of a limited number of studies and trials on the safety and efficacy of OTC treatments in younger age groups compared with adults, with data being scarce and often contradictory [24, 25]. Furthermore, there are difficulties arising from distinguishing treatment effects from high placebo response rates, as well as a lack of standardized criteria for diagnosis and treatment response in non-adult groups [21]. Therefore, it is crucial to address these knowledge gaps and promote a better understanding of OTC treatments for pediatric and adolescent headaches and migraines even at the expert level.

The primary objective of this narrative review is to increase clinical awareness and promote evidence-based use of OTC abortive therapies for acute headache and migraine-related pain in children and adolescent populations. By synthesizing current guidelines, product labeling, clinical trial outcomes, and expert consensus, the review seeks to inform PCPs, thereby enabling them to guide caregivers in effective self-management strategies for children and adolescents experiencing headache disorders.

Methodology

This review employed a targeted literature search strategy using keywords including “headache,” “migraine,” “children,” and “adolescents.” To ensure a high standard of evidence, only peer-reviewed publications and publicly available product monographs or summary of product characteristics were considered. The focus was restricted to studies addressing the acute treatment of headaches and migraines in pediatric populations, specifically children (1 month–11 years) and adolescents (12–18 years), using oral OTC analgesics such as paracetamol, ibuprofen, aspirin, and naproxen.

An initial search was conducted in February 2022 via the ProQuest platform, encompassing databases such as Embase, MEDLINE, ToxFile, and Derwent Drug File, covering the period from 2010 to 2022. Inclusion criteria were limited to clinical studies, reviews, meta-analysis, and clinical guidelines involving individuals up to 18 years of age treated with oral OTC analgesics for headache or migraine. Emphasis was placed on evaluating efficacy, age-appropriate dosing strategies, and therapeutic suitability across headache subtypes. Adjuvant compounds were considered only in the context of fixed-dose combinations with paracetamol and/or NSAIDs. Studies were excluded if they addressed non-OTC indications, were published in languages other than English, focused solely on adult populations, or originated from unpublished or gray literature sources. Prescription medications, including triptans, gepants, and ergot derivatives, were also excluded.

Of the 260 records identified, 24 original research articles and eight clinical guidelines met the inclusion criteria. Subsequently, nine additional studies were incorporated through ad hoc searches using PubMed and Elicit.org between February 2022 and October 2023. These supplementary records were not constrained by the original timeframe and included adolescent populations, while maintaining a focus on oral administration of paracetamol or NSAIDs for acute headache or migraine management. Following full-text review, a total of 28 unique records were selected for inclusion (see Table S1).

Expert input was incorporated through a structured selection process to ensure balanced representation of both clinical and subject matter expertise. Panel members were selected on the basis of their recognized contributions to pediatric headache research, including peer-reviewed publications and professional standing. The panel comprised six experts including four internationally renowned neurologists, one global expert in complementary approaches to headache and migraine management, and one PCP with specialization in headache and migraine care. The expert panel convened virtually on November 10, 2022. Consensus on pediatric migraine self-management strategies was developed through a structured process informed by pre-meeting survey data based on assessment of available evidence. The insight guided the thematic focus of expert discussions. As a synthesis of clinical experience, available literature, and safety considerations relevant to pediatric care, the resulting recommendations were presented in this review. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Classifying Headaches and Migraines

Headaches and migraines are complex neurological events with a variety of causes and symptoms. Yet despite their prevalence and far-ranging impacts, little is understood about their etiology and timing, and there is still more to ascertain about their pathophysiology. Thus, they are chiefly understood by categorization into symptomatic types that can be diagnosed through medical examination and history according to conventional criteria [26] (Fig. 1). Primary headaches originate without cause and are not attributed to another medical condition; they include migraines, tension-type headaches (TTHs), trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias, and diverse other forms. These mechanisms are complex and involve neurological processes in the central and peripheral nervous systems [27–30]. Secondary headaches have an exogenous cause such as trauma, infection, anatomical disorders, or foreign substances. Patients can experience multiple types of headaches simultaneously or separately.

Simplified criteria for headaches and migraines with typical presenting symptoms, and guidance for when to seek medical aid from primary care or emergency providers [26, 31, 33]

Most headaches in children and adolescents are due to a primary headache disorder, chiefly migraines and TTHs, or a benign secondary cause, such as an infection [31]. From a self-management perspective, caregivers can recognize headaches as falling into one of four different temporal patterns [31, 32]. Acute headaches have a short-term onset and dissipation of symptoms (minutes to hours) with no return, while acute recurrent (or episodic) headaches show the same short-term onset and dissipation but occur repeatedly (i.e., multiple times per week or month). Chronic progressive headaches persist over longer timescales (occurring on multiple days per week) and are characterized by increasingly worse symptoms, whereas chronic non-progressive headaches are persistent but show no clear change in the severity of symptoms over time. Acute recurrent and chronic non-progressive headaches are typically related to a primary headache disorder, while singular acute headaches are often triggered by a secondary cause or a benign form of primary disorder. In most cases, acute and chronic non-progressive headache patterns can be treated at home, although certain red flags can indicate if medical attention is recommended (Fig. 1). Chronic progressive headaches, however, warrant immediate and thorough medical examination and neuroimaging once recognized.

A proper diagnosis for a headache disorder can only be achieved through medical examination, but it helps when caregivers recognize the temporal pattern of headaches being experienced and identify whether the headache is primary or secondary. Caregivers may also benefit from ascertaining whether the headache is likely to be a migraine or not, because this information can help both them and the patient know what to expect and decide whether medical intervention is required. A basic awareness of the condition can also facilitate communication with healthcare providers.

Different age groups present different challenges for classifying headaches [21, 32]. Infants and children may be unable to articulate their headache episodes; thus, caregivers may need to rely on behavioral symptoms or vague descriptions. Younger children may react to acute pain by crying, rocking, or hiding. If the pain is chronic, they may display developmental regression or general psychological disorders such as anxiety, depression, or behavioral problems. Everyday activities such as eating, sleeping, and playing may also become difficult. Older children and adolescents will be more articulate but may choose to hide information from their healthcare providers. OTC Treatments for Immediate or Early Relief of Headaches and Migraines

Overview of OTC Treatments

This review covers the most common OTC molecules for the symptomatic treatment of acute pain associated with headache and migraine, namely, paracetamol (acetaminophen) and NSAIDs (including ibuprofen, aspirin, and naproxen). These molecules are generally well tolerated and have established safety profiles, although the potential for overuse does exist. Their efficacy can be idiosyncratic to the patient and the type of pain, so practice typically follows the guideline of “use whatever works for you.” That being said, a survey on OTC analgesics among patients aged 14–20 years suggests that most users have inadequate knowledge around the appropriate use, side effects, and contraindications of OTC analgesics [23]. Thus, there exists an unmet need to summarize the appropriate use of OTC analgesics for treating headaches and migraines in the pediatric and adolescent populations.

Notably, NSAIDs and other OTC analgesics carry a risk of medication overuse headache (MOH) if taken on more than 15 days per month. However, evidence suggests that short-term, on-label use of these medications may offer protection against the progression from episodic to chronic migraine [34]. The International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) (2018) specifically recognizes MOH as occurring in susceptible patients with pre-existing primary headaches (typically migraines or TTHs) who use acute pain medication. Furthermore, OTC analgesics can lose their effectiveness over time. Therefore, if a patient is regularly using OTC products to treat headache or migraine (i.e., more than 3 days per week), then they are recommended to consult with a PCP and/or seek preventative therapy.

Paracetamol/Acetaminophen

Paracetamol is an extremely popular OTC analgesic and antipyretic. It is indicated for various acute pain conditions, including headache, although the indication for migraine may differ by geographic region. The mode of action is not completely understood, but research suggests that it involves (i) a peripheral component that reduces transduction of the sensory nerves and consequent nociceptive impulse transmission, as well as (ii) a central component that inhibits the pain-mediating prostaglandins induced from peripheral transmission [35]. Specifically, these mechanisms may involve inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis through indirect effects on the cyclooxygenase (COX) pathway and the activation of cannabinoid receptors that modulate signaling pathways [36–40].

The efficacy of paracetamol varies between individuals and the source of pain, according to a systematic review [41]. There is evidence for pain relief in episodic TTHs, acute migraines, and cold-related headaches. However, some evidence suggests that paracetamol is not efficacious for migraines in children and adolescents. Paracetamol is generally considered safe [42]. It is mostly metabolized to inactive compounds, which are readily excreted; however, reactive metabolites can damage the liver with excessive dosing or in susceptible patients [43]. At-risk groups among children and adolescents include those that are malnourished or have eating disorders, and those with cystic fibrosis, AIDS, or cachexia. Paracetamol may also not be appropriate for patients with renal or hepatic impairments. Overdoses, although rare, can be fatal as a result of complete liver failure or acute pancreatitis with liver dysfunction. Furthermore, the principal excipient in most formulations is sodium, which can induce adverse symptoms (e.g., nausea) in high doses. Overall, paracetamol is most likely to be safe for children and adolescents when used as directed. However, the general recommendation is to seek medical advice if paracetamol is being used to treat headaches or migraines for more than 3 days [42].

NSAIDs

NSAIDs are a broad, heterogeneous group of chemicals with anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, and analgesic properties. This group includes OTC forms of ibuprofen, aspirin, and naproxen, which are all indicated for acute relief of headache and migraine pain. The principal mode of action for NSAIDs is the direct inhibition of inflammatory COX enzymes, which contrasts with the indirect inhibitory effect of paracetamol on this pathway [35]. The selection and degree of COX inhibition varies with the molecule: aspirin is COX-1 selective, whereas ibuprofen and naproxen are non-selective and block both COX-1 and COX-2 [44]. The analgesic effects of NSAIDs are partly attributed to their anti-inflammatory actions on the peripheral nervous system, but ongoing research indicates that wide-ranging effects on the central nervous system (CNS) may also be responsible for pain-relief effects [35, 44–46].

The mode of action for NSAIDs makes them particularly effective for pain resulting from local stimulation of nociceptive fibers and hyperalgesia; this includes many forms of headache [44]. Furthermore, by directly blocking COX enzymes, NSAIDs can inhibit the synthesis of prostaglandins involved in the pathophysiology of migraines [47]. However, COX inhibition is also the main cause of adverse events related to NSAIDs use, as this action can damage the gastrointestinal and cardiovascular systems [35, 48, 49]. As such, NSAIDs may not be appropriate for patients with gastrointestinal, coronary, renal, or bleeding disorders. Furthermore, when overused, NSAIDs increase the risk of gastrointestinal side effects and are highly associated with avoidable drug-related hospital admissions [50]; when used as directed, their use for acute pain treatment is generally considered to be safe. One notable exception is a contraindication for aspirin in children under 12, owing to a potential link with the rare but fatal Reye syndrome. However, the evidence for a cause–effect link between aspirin and Reye syndrome is disputed [51]; anecdotally, aspirin is provided to younger patients. As with paracetamol, it is recommended to seek medical advice if NSAIDs are being used to treat acute headache or migraine pain for more than 3 days in a row.

Combinations

Some OTC products include combinations of paracetamol and NSAIDs (e.g., paracetamol + ibuprofen), which may improve therapeutic benefits due to additive effects that extend duration [52, 53]. A potential benefit of combinations is that lower and fewer doses are needed, which can reduce overall exposure to the drugs. Although there does not appear to be any major additional risks of adverse events, research on combined OTC analgesics is scarce, especially in the pediatric population.

Caffeine is a psychoactive stimulant of CNS and a common adjuvant to paracetamol and/or NSAIDs in OTC analgesic products [54–56]. There is evidence to suggest that caffeine is especially effective for hypnic headaches and for headaches associated with excessive muscle tension/contraction [54]. Meanwhile, the evidence related to migraines is limited, with very few studies in non-adult patients [54]. Numerous hypotheses have been proposed to explain the analgesic action of caffeine adjuvants, including increased distribution and absorption of active analgesic molecules, antagonism of adenosine receptors, inhibition of COX enzymes, and emotional changes [56, 57]. Caffeine is generally regarded as a safe adjuvant to paracetamol and aspirin [58]. Associations with nervousness and dizziness are rare and seen only in a minority of patients, although there is some concern for toxicity to the kidneys [56] and the potential for overuse headaches and withdrawal symptoms in children and adolescents when appropriate directions for use are not followed and/or when used without guidance from a healthcare provider [54, 59, 60]. Consequently, the general recommendation is to avoid caffeine combinations in patients under 12 years of age and to always use caffeine in fixed-dose combinations alone.

Antiemetics may be administered in combination with OTC migraine medications for treating nausea associated with migraine. A popular antiemetic adjuvant and treatment for gastroparesis is metoclopramide, which appears to reduce nausea and photophobia, while stimulating gastric emptying, which may improve absorption of the analgesic while posing little increased safety risk; however, this adjuvant has not been thoroughly studied in non-adult groups [61–64]. Commonly used pediatric antiemetic medications include ondansetron and prochlorperazine [65, 66].

Paracetamol

Dosages and Administrations Suitable for Pediatrics and/or Adolescents

The dosage by weight for paracetamol is 10–15 mg per kg body weight for a single dose, not exceeding 60 mg/kg/day, with a minimum duration of 4 h between doses and a maximum of 4 doses per day. No paracetamol products are suitable for infants under 1 month old in a self-care setting. Liquid formulations (e.g., suspensions, solutions, syrups) and suppositories are appropriate from 1 month onwards. Tablet formulations are more appropriate from the age of 6 years; dosages must remain small, not exceeding 250 mg, from the ages 6 to 9 years and not exceeding 500 mg from ages 9 to 12 years. A 1000 mg single dose is considered suitable for adolescents over 12 years old.

Important Contraindications, Warnings, and Precautions

To avoid overdosing, patients should not use paracetamol products with any other medicines that contain paracetamol and never exceed the recommended dose (up to 4 g from 12 years old and more). To avoid complications related to organ damage or metabolic acidosis, parents or caregivers should seek medical advice before use if patient (i) has known liver or kidney impairments; (ii) is malnourished, anorexic, or underweight; or (iii) is suffering from a severe infection. Paracetamol should not be used if the patient has ever had an allergic response to it. Some formulations may not be suitable for patients with phenylketonuria or patients on sodium-controlled diets.

Important Interactions with Other Drugs

Patients using anticoagulants may be at increased bleeding risk if using paracetamol regularly but not with temporary paracetamol use. Medical professionals will advise on any important interactions with prescription medications.

Symptoms/Signs/Treatments for Adverse Events

Parents or caregivers should contact local poison control or emergency health services if paracetamol overdose or adverse reaction (even without overdosing) is suspected, even if no symptoms are observed.

Initial signs of overdose include nausea and vomiting, which then escalate to signs of lactic acidosis and liver failure: strange-smelling breath, confusion and impaired consciousness, jaundice (yellowing skin and eyes), shallow and rapid breathing, tender abdomen, or hand tremors.

Ibuprofen

Dosages and Administrations Suitable for Pediatrics and/or Adolescents

Ibuprofen products are not suitable for infants under 3 months old, but suspensions and solutions are appropriate from 3 months onwards as per the World Health Organization (WHO) Model List of Essential Medicines [67]. Some tablet formulations (e.g., junior strength) may become appropriate from the age of 6 years; dosages of 200–300 mg may be administered up to 4 times per day, with 6–8 h between doses, with consideration of the patient’s weight (10 mg/kg dose).

Important Contraindications, Warnings, and Precautions

To avoid overdosing, do not use ibuprofen products with any other medicines that contain NSAIDs and never exceed the recommended dose. Parents or caretakers should be urged to seek medical advice before using ibuprofen if the patient has (i) previously experienced an allergic reaction to any other pain reliever/fever reducer; (ii) a history of stomach problems; (iii) high blood pressure, heart disease, liver cirrhosis, kidney disease, asthma, or has had a stroke; (iv) not been drinking fluids or has lost fluids owing to vomiting or diarrhea. Be aware of a history of hypersensitivity to any NSAIDs or other compounds in the formulation (e.g., polysorbate 80). Unless prescribed by a doctor, ibuprofen should not be used during pregnancy.

Important Interactions with Other Drugs

Patients should avoid taking ibuprofen with any other NSAIDs. Ibuprofen may affect or be affected by anticoagulants and high blood pressure medications.

Symptoms/Signs/Treatments for Adverse Events

Initial signs of overdose include nausea, stomachache, headache, vomiting, diarrhea, ringing in the ears, vomiting blood, and blood in stools. More serious poisoning can lead to dizziness, drowsiness, excitation, disorientation, low blood pressure, reduced breathing, blue discoloration of the skin and mucosa, loss of consciousness, convulsions, and coma. Parents and caregivers should urgently contact local poison control or emergency services if an overdose is suspected, even if no symptoms are observed.

Aspirin Dosages and Administrations Suitable for Pediatrics and/or Adolescents

Aspirin products are not suitable for children under 12 years old, owing to the risk of developing Reye syndrome. Dosages for children and adolescents follow those for adults and vary regionally in the range of 300–1000 mg at once, with a maximum of 3900–4000 mg per day and with at least 4 to 6 h between doses.

Important Contraindications, Warnings, and Precautions

Children and teenagers who have or are recovering from chicken pox or flu-like symptoms should not use aspirin. Aspirin should not be used if the patient is suffering from hemophilia or has or has had a stomach ulcer. Unless prescribed by a doctor, aspirin should be avoided during pregnancy or if breastfeeding.

Important Interactions with Other Drugs

Patients should not use aspirin if they are allergic to aspirin components or other pain relievers/fever reducers. Aspirin should not be used concurrently with medicines that reduce blood clotting (warfarin) or for treatment of gout (sulfinpyrazone or probenecid).

Symptoms/Signs/Treatments for Adverse Events

Parents or caregivers should seek immediate medical advice if an allergic reaction, ringing in the ears, or loss of hearing occurs; or if there are any signs of stomach bleeding, pain gets worse or lasts more than 10 days, or a fever lasts more than 3 days; or if any new symptoms arise. Medical attention should be sought immediately if more tablets are taken than required.

Naproxen

Dosages and Administrations Suitable for Pediatrics and/or Adolescents

Naproxen products are not suitable for children under 12 years old. Dosages for children and adolescents follow those for adults: typically, 220–550 mg at one time, with at least 8 to 12 h between doses and a maximum of 825–1375 mg per day.

Important Contraindications, Warnings, and Precautions

Do not use if patient is allergic or hypersensitive to naproxen sodium, or if patient has a history of asthma, urticaria, or allergic-type reaction to acetylsalicylic acid or NSAIDs. Also avoid if a patient has active peptide ulcers, history of recurrent ulceration, active gastrointestinal bleeding, inflammatory bowel disease, severe liver impairment or active liver disease, severe renal impairment or deteriorating renal disease. Unless naproxen is prescribed by a doctor, patients should avoid using it during pregnancy or if they are breastfeeding.

Important Interactions with Other Drugs

Interactions with cyclosporine, lithium, methotrexate, low-dose aspirin, anticoagulants, and glucocorticoids should be adequately monitored. Naproxen in combination with other NSAIDs adds to the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and should be avoided. Concomitant use with diuretics may increase the risk of congestive heart failure.

Symptoms/Signs/Treatments for Adverse Events

Seek medical attention immediately for the management of overdose. A significant overdose may be characterized by drowsiness, indigestion, nausea, and vomiting. Some cases with acute renal failure have been described.

Results of Literature Survey

The identified literature largely reflected studies from Europe (n = 17) [17, 24, 88–102] and North America (n = 8) [18–20, 103–107], with one study each from China [108], India [109], South Korea [110], and Israel [111].

Half of the articles (14/28) included recommendations that either paracetamol or NSAIDs (typically ibuprofen) were appropriate first-line OTC treatments for headache and/or migraine in children and adolescents [20, 24, 90, 92, 94–96, 99, 101–103, 109–111].

The remaining studies (14/28) recommended use of ibuprofen and/or other NSAIDs (particularly naproxen) exclusively or preferentially over paracetamol as first-line pharmacological treatments for headache and/or migraine in children and adolescents [17–19, 88, 89, 91, 93, 97, 100, 104–108]. Paracetamol is commonly preferred for its favorable safety profile, particularly in younger children, and demonstrates efficacy in managing TTHs and mild migraine episodes [112]. Similarly, ibuprofen has shown greater effectiveness in clinical studies, especially for moderate to severe migraine attacks. Selection between the two should be guided by patient-specific factors, including age, headache severity, and individual tolerability [40, 111]. One study highlighted the relative effectiveness of paracetamol over ibuprofen [111] and may be better tolerated by patients [113]. Overall, paracetamol appears to be effective at treating TTHs and migraine, but evidence is inconclusive on the equivalence or superiority of NSAIDs for the same indication. Four studies noted that aspirin was an option for adolescent patients > 16 years of age [20, 89, 100, 101]. None of the articles identified here recommended the combination of paracetamol and NSAIDs. Similarly, the use of adjuvant caffeine was discouraged among children and adolescents in some studies, owing to a lack of clinical evidence and concerns for development of MOH [89, 100, 101, 113]; however, the NSAID indomethacin, alone or together with prochlorperazine and caffeine, was proposed to be effective in adolescents with primary headaches > 12 years [24]. Two studies noted the benefits of magnesium supplementation alongside paracetamol or ibuprofen [90, 104].

Recommendations for pharmacological treatment of headache and migraine in children derive mainly from studies in adults [103, 113]. Limitations were noted in terms of the small number of trials and the high placebo rates observed [93]. In particular, there is a prominent knowledge gap with regard to the small number of studies on children aged < 5 years [88]. Although young children are often excluded from clinical trials because of ethical and practical challenges [114], they represent a vulnerable group with unique treatment needs. Future research should prioritize this age group in primary care centers to develop safe and effective interventions for headache or migraine [24].

With respect to non-pharmacological interventions for migraine, overall evidence was noted to be limited [101, 113], but many studies encouraged low-risk common sense approaches [88, 91, 94, 100]. For instance, promoting healthy lifestyle habits is a key component of pediatric headache and migraine management. Children and adolescents should be encouraged to maintain regular sleep patterns, engage in physical activity between headache episodes, stay well-hydrated with non-caffeinated fluids, and consume consistent, balanced meals—particularly breakfast. Incorporating periods of rest and relaxation, along with fostering an understanding of primary headache disorders, can further support self-management. Conversely, unnecessary restrictions on normal activities, foods, or beverages should be avoided unless there is clear, individualized evidence of harm, to prevent undue burden and maintain quality of life [94]. In addition, physiotherapy may be appropriate for headache associated with musculoskeletal symptoms or post-traumatic injury [100]. Cold therapy—for example, with a cold compress or ice pack applied to the base of the skull or area of pain for up to 20 min—may offer some immediate relief from migraine by reducing inflammation and promoting relaxation.

Clinical Experience with OTC Interventions in Children and Adolescents

Official global- and national-level recommendations for treatment of migraine in children and adolescents are limited, with few exceptions [18]. NSAIDs, particularly ibuprofen and primarily in the USA, have become the first choice for acute migraine treatment in children per the latest available guidance and data. However, paracetamol is also often used as a first-line OTC treatment in practice, for reasons including (i) the patients’ or caregivers’ preference for milder analgesic effects and better tolerance than ibuprofen or other NSAIDs; (ii) previous positive experience(s) of effectiveness of paracetamol; and (iii) local differences in expert recommendations and clinical practice.

Experts generally make recommendations based on drug label information and solid evidence, given safety concerns and parental involvement in administration. Owing to a lack of evidence for OTC analgesics with respect to younger patients, some additional recommendations, largely based on clinical experience, include (i) fixed-dose combinations of paracetamol and NSAIDs with adjuvants, such as caffeine and magnesium, may be effective; and (ii) the reasoning for these combinations is that inclusion of adjuvants may allow for lower dosages of the key analgesic ingredients than with monotherapy. Such combinations may be suitable for caregivers, including school nurses, to dispense as needed.

Clinicians typically recommend paracetamol or other non-NSAIDs for patients with renal disease or marked gastrointestinal concerns (e.g., reflux), whereas NSAIDs may be recommended for patients who may have hepatic disease. As there is no definitive negative evidence or comparative studies that show one medication to be superior to the other, sound clinical judgment and patient preferences should prevail regarding the medication choice.

Strengths and Limitations

This review presents a comprehensive synthesis of current literature and expert opinion on self-management strategies for pediatric and adolescent headache and migraine treatment using OTC analgesics. A key strength lies in its integration of pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches into a unified, patient-centered framework, an area that remains underexplored in existing literature. While individual aspects such as OTC medications [104, 115] and behavioral strategies [116, 117] for migraine management have been previously addressed, this manuscript uniquely combines these elements to support practical, evidence-informed self-care strategies especially in pediatric and adolescent populations. Furthermore, by incorporating expert recommendations grounded in real-world clinical experience, the review bridges the gap between academic research and everyday practice, particularly in a field where high-quality pediatric data are limited.

Despite its strengths, certain methodological limitations are acknowledged. The review was conducted in a narrative format rather than following a systematic review protocol. This approach, while allowing for broader clinical interpretation and flexibility, does not employ standardized procedures for literature selection or critical appraisal, which may introduce selection and reporting biases. Additionally, the absence of a formal risk of bias assessment for the included studies is a recognized limitation. While narrative reviews typically do not apply structured tools, efforts were made to mitigate potential bias by selecting peer-reviewed sources, applying clear inclusion criteria, and triangulating findings with expert consensus.

Conclusion

This narrative review provides a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence and expert clinical insights regarding the use of OTC analgesics for acute headache and migraine management in children and adolescents. While paracetamol remains a well-tolerated and effective option for tension-type headaches and mild migraines, NSAIDs such as ibuprofen may offer superior efficacy in moderate to severe cases. However, the current literature reveals a notable paucity of pediatric data, particularly for younger age groups and combination therapies, including naproxen.

From a clinician’s perspective, the findings of this review reinforce the importance of individualized, age-appropriate treatment strategies and highlight the ongoing challenges in clinical practice. Despite the familiarity of these medications, issues related to dosing accuracy, adherence, and caregiver education remain critical barriers to optimal outcomes. The integration of expert opinion with available evidence offers a guide for primary care providers, who play a pivotal role in educating families with adolescent children and supporting subsequent self-management.

Furthermore, the review underscores the need for more inclusive, high-quality pediatric research to establish clearer treatment algorithms and safety profiles. Non-pharmacological interventions and adjunctive therapies, though underexplored, may offer additional benefits and should be considered as part of a holistic, patient-centered approach. Future studies should aim to address these gaps, explore combination strategies, and evaluate long-term outcomes to better inform clinical decision-making and improve care for this vulnerable population.
