Altered pain-related attention engagement in individuals with chronic musculoskeletal pain: Evidence from EM-ERP
Authors: Zhou Yang, Jiehong Liao, Xingyu Zhang, Yong Liu, Xing Kang, Todd Jackson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe investigated neurocognitive mechanisms of pain-related attentional control in individuals with chronic musculoskeletal pain.MethodsBy simultaneously recording eye movements and event-related potentials (ERPs), we examined pain-related attentional control in a sample with chronic musculoskeletal pain (N = 26) and a matched pain-free control group (N = 26). Using an adapted go/no-go paradigm, the go condition required participants to focus on pain or neutral images to measure attention engagement while the no-go condition required inhibiting attention toward these images to measure attention inhibition.ResultsIn the go condition, participants with chronic musculoskeletal pain exhibited shorter first fixation latencies and longer dwell times on pain images than neutral images, indicating enhanced attentional vigilance and maintained attention engagement. These behavioral effects were fully mediated by enhanced pain image-evoked N2 amplitude biases and partially mediated by pain image-evoked P3 amplitude biases, respectively. N2 amplitudes reflected initial top-down attentional capture, and P3 amplitudes reflected maintained attention engagement regulation. No differences in eye movements or ERPs were observed during the no-go condition in either group.DiscussionPeople with chronic musculoskeletal pain display enhanced pain-related attention engagement that suggests pain-related attention control deficits. Future research that aims to improve pain-related attentional control by targeting maladaptive patterns of attention engagement may lead to better chronic pain outcomes.Keywords: Chronic musculoskeletal pain, Attention control, Go/no-go paradigm, Eye movements, Event-related potentials

Introduction

Chronic musculoskeletal pain (CMSP) affects between 13% and 47% of the adult population, with a global growth rate of 20.3% within the last 15 years (Ader et al., 2023). Chronic pain is strongly linked to cognitive impairment (Godfrey et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2025). Attentional control reflects the ability to inhibit dominant responses, habitual behaviors, and irrelevant stimuli, allowing individuals to maintain sustained attentional focus on task goals amid dynamic environments (Diamond, 2013). Among individuals with CMSP, attention control deficits may contribute to heightened attention biases toward pain-related cues, diverting cognitive resources from the pursuit of daily life goals (Franklin et al., 2019; Liossi et al., 2014; Schoth et al., 2015a, 2015b). However, neurocognitive mechanisms underlying pain-related attention control in individuals with CMSP have been explored only fleetingly.

Over the past decade, research utilizing eye movement indices has documented distinct attention biases toward pain-related information in both healthy individuals and chronic pain patients (Franklin et al., 2019; Jackson et al., 2018; Liossi et al., 2014; Schoth et al., 2015a; Yang et al., 2013). For example, chronic headache patients exhibit stronger initial direction biases toward pain faces compared to healthy controls (Liossi et al., 2014; Schoth et al., 2015b), and chronic back pain patients demonstrate faster first fixations and prolonged mean fixations on pain-related movement images (Franklin et al., 2019). Furthermore, chronic pain patients with longer gaze durations on injury images report significantly greater pain intensity and interference at follow-up (Jackson et al., 2019). However, a meta-analysis by Chan et al. (2020) involving 24 eye-tracking studies found no significant differences in attentional allocation to pain-related cues between chronic pain groups and healthy controls, a result consistent with Mazidi et al. (2021), who observed no group differences in orientation, first fixation duration (FFD), or fixation counts on pain stimuli although a vigilance-avoidance pattern for pain faces was identified across groups. Further supporting this pattern, Priebe et al. (2021) reported no CMSP versus control group differences in first fixation probabilities, fixation durations, or bias scores towards pain faces; instead, vigilant-avoidant processing of pain faces was observed in both groups. Methodologically, the capacity to assess attentional control in chronic pain patients should involve stimuli that are salient to specific clinical conditions as well as control of chronic pain versus control group differences in potential moderating factors including pain catastrophizing (Mazidi et al., 2021) and fear of pain/(re)injury (Khatibi et al., 2009; Ranjbar et al., 2020). Stimuli such as injury images (Jackson et al., 2019), pain-related words (Yang et al., 2012), and painful facial expressions (Wang et al., 2020) may be suitable in some samples; to examine pain-related attention consistent with CMSP, we developed and standardized multiple images depicting pain at various musculoskeletal sites in the present study.

Recent research investigating neural bases of pain-related stimulus processing in chronic pain patients has revealed both generalized attentional deficits and condition-specific biases. Using event-related potentials (ERPs) during an Attentional Network Test for Interactions and Vigilance–Executive and Arousal Components paradigm, Chen et al. (2024) identified magnified N1 amplitudes during phasic alerting trials, prolonged P2 latencies and increased orienting-evoked P1 amplitudes alongside impaired executive vigilance (reduced hit rate improvement) and arousal vigilance (longer reaction times with higher variability) as relevant responses in chronic migraine patients. These electrophysiological and behavioral deficits distinguished patients from healthy controls and predicted headache frequency, underscoring the possible clinical relevance of attentional dysfunction in migraine management. Regarding pain-relevant processing, Fernandes-Magalhaes et al. (2022) observed enhanced fronto-central P2 amplitudes, indicating early attentional capture, to pain-related faces in fibromyalgia, in tandem with impaired resource allocation (reduced N2a and slower reaction times during incongruent trials). In contrast, Wang et al. (2020) found adults with CMSP exhibited enhanced N2pc amplitudes to emotional faces (both painful and happy) compared to controls, suggesting heightened early attentional allocation toward general affective salience cues. Studies across chronic pain conditions also consistently report impaired resource allocation, as evidenced by reduced P3a/P3b amplitudes during voluntary and involuntary attention tasks (Gubler et al., 2022), attenuated P300 responses to rare stimuli (Cardoso et al., 2024), and disrupted habituation to repetitive pain cues (Vossen et al., 2018). However, Coll (2018) performed a meta-analysis on 40 ERP studies tapping empathy for pain and concluded that while the centro-parietal P3 and LPC components are sensitive to vicarious pain observation, early N1 and N2 component associations are not reliable. These findings suggest that although chronic pain is associated with general attentional impairment, manifested by reduced P3 responses, the direction of change (enhancement or reduction) in late ERP components during attentional processing of pain-specific stimuli remains unclear.

In the current study, we investigated neurocognitive mechanisms of attentional control among individuals with CMSP and matched pain-free controls using simultaneous eye movement (EM) and ERP recordings within an adapted go/no-go paradigm. This paradigm integrates elements of a classic anti-saccade task (Dafoe et al., 2007; Munoz & Everling, 2004) and traditional go/no-go paradigms (Chelazzi et al., 2013; Padmala & Pessoa, 2010). Participants were first presented with a go or no-go signal. Subsequently, a pain-related or neutral image appeared on one side of the screen, opposed by a same-sized black rectangle on the other side. Following a go signal, participants were instructed to immediately fixate on the pain/neutral image and sustain attention there, allowing assessment of attentional engagement. Following a no-go signal, they were instructed to immediately fixate on the black rectangle and sustain attention there, enabling measurement of attentional inhibition. To effectively elicit salient pain-related attentional allocation, we developed novel pain-related images depicting pain in body regions commonly affected in CMSP patients (e.g., low back, neck, shoulder).

We hypothesized that participants with CMSP would demonstrate heightened pain-related attentional engagement and greater attentional inhibition difficulties. Specifically, we expected that, in both go and no-go conditions, participants with CMSP would show increased initial direction towards pain images, shorter first fixation latency (FFL), longer FFD, and longer dwell time (DT) on pain images compared to neutral images. This behavioral pattern would correspond to comparatively larger pain image-evoked N2 and P2 amplitudes in the CMSP group. As the modified paradigm required participants to initiate gaze from a central point between two lateral stimuli, we did not anticipate group differences in the early N1 component. Differences in P3 and the late potential component (LPC) were also examined. Finally, we explored EM and ERP differences between stimulus types in the pain-free group.

Methods

Participants

We recruited 26 participants with CMSP (22 women, 4 men) from the Department of Rehabilitation, Southwest University Hospital, Chongqing, China, and 26 matched pain-free controls (17 women, 9 men) from Southwest University, Chongqing, China. All participants were unmarried, aged from 18 to 24 years (M = 20.08, SD = 1.53), with an average of 2.52 years of university education (SD = 1.20). CMSP inclusion criteria required musculoskeletal pain occurring most days or daily for at least 3 months, with an average pain duration of 27.96 months (SD = 11.98; range = 6–60 months). Primary pain complaints included low back pain (57.69%), cervical pain (23.08%), and limb pain (19.23%). All participants had normal or corrected to normal vision, no self-reported color vision deficiency, no current opioid prescriptions for pain, and no history of neurological or major psychiatric disorders (such as schizophrenia, psychotic disorder, bipolar disorder or dementia) that might affect comprehension.

Measures

Chronic pain grade (CPG)

The CPG (Smith et al., 1997) was used to measure pain intensity and pain interference in individuals with chronic pain. Pain intensity was measured using three items, i.e., current pain, worst pain, and average pain over the past three months. Pain interference was assessed using three items related to impairment in daily activities, social and family activities, and ability to study or work. Each pain intensity and interference item was rated from 0 (no pain/interference) to 10 (extreme pain/unable to carry on any activities). Those CPG dimensions have satisfactory psychometric properties in samples from China (Wong & Fielding, 2011). In the current study, alphas were 0.77 for pain intensity, and 0.74 for pain interference. In this study, participants’ average pain intensity was 16.54 (SD = 3.89) and average pain interference was 15.81 (SD = 4.80).

Fear of pain questionnaire-III (FPQ-III)

The Chinese version of FPQ-III includes 25 of the original FPQ-Ⅲ items (McNeil & Rainwater, 1998) based on exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses on Chinese young adult samples and has the same components as the original questionnaire, i.e., minor pain, severe pain and medical pain (Yang et al., 2013). Each item was rated on a 5-point scale, with from 1 (no fear at all) to 5 (extreme fear). Higher total FPQ-III scores represent a higher level of pain-related fear. The alpha coefficient was α = 0.94 in the current study.

Pain catastrophizing scale (PCS)

The 13-item PCS (Sullivan et al., 1995) assesses features of pain catastrophizing including rumination, magnification, and helplessness with items rated from 0 (never like that) to 4 (always like that) and summed to calculate total scores. Higher scores on the questionnaire represent a greater level of pain catastrophizing. The scale has been used previously and found to have satisfactory psychometrics in Chinese samples (Yap et al., 2008). In this study, the total PCS had an alpha of α = 0.90.

Attention control scale (ACS)

The 20-item ACS measures attention control ability and comprises two subscales, attention focusing and attention shifting (Derryberry & Reed, 2002). Items are rated on a 4-point scale of agreement, with 1 (never) to 4 (always) as anchors. . Eleven items need to be reverse-scored. Higher total ACS scores indicate better attentional control ability. The ACS factor structure, reliability, and validity have been supported in Chinese samples (Yu et al., 2016). Cronbach's alpha coefficients for the current study sample were α = 0.87 (ACS-total), α = 0.86 (ACS-focusing), and α = 0.62 (ACS-shifting).

Center for epidemiologic studies depression scale (CES-D)

The 20-item CES-D assessed the frequency of specific depressive symptoms experienced during the past week on a 4-point scale anchored by 0 = rarely or none of the time (<1 day) and 3 = most of all the times (5–7 days) (Radloff, 1977). The CES-D factor structure, reliability, and validity have been supported in Chinese samples (Cheng et al., 2012). In this sample, the CES-D alpha was α = 0.80.

Demographics

Age, gender, height, weight, years of education in university, primary pain site, pain duration (months), and current opioid medication use were assessed.

Image sets and apparatus

Images sets. Images consisted of body posture pictures commonly seen in daily life scenarios, including standing and sitting positions. Pain pictures depicted pain in body parts from the side or back while avoiding facial expressions (e.g., holding the neck with the hand). Neutral posture photos displayed body parts in natural postures from the back or side (e.g., hands naturally hanging down). The images were obtained by recruiting three male and three female models from Southwest University on a voluntary and paid basis. Photos were all taken under consistent environmental and lighting conditions. Resulting images were processed into uniform black and white pictures of the same size and brightness using Photoshop, with pain body parts highlighted in red. Each image measured 11 cm × 10 cm (width × height) with a resolution of 100 pixels per inch. From the captured photos, 306 images were selected. Then, 30 healthy university student volunteers were recruited to rate the images on four dimensions, including pain intensity, arousal, and controllability using a 9-point scale ('1′ = not at all, '5′ = moderate, '9′ = extreme), and emotional valence using a 9-point scale ('1′ = extreme negative, '5′ = neutral, '9′ = extreme positive). Finally, 60 pain images and 60 neutral images were selected for the formal experiment in this study, with an additional 4 neutral images selected for practice trials. As expected, pain images elicited significantly higher pain intensity (M = 5.39, SD = 0.46), negative valence (M = 3.74, SD = 0.24), arousal (M = 4.03, SD = 0.42), and controllability (M = 3.80, SD = 0.42) ratings than neutral images did (M pain intensity = 1.38, SD = 0.18; M valence = 4.66, SD = 0.30; M arousal = 1.94, SD = 0.19; M controllability = 1.90, SD = 0.21; all F1, 119 > 357.63, p < .001, ηp2> .75).

EM apparatus. The presentation of images occurred on a 19-inch monitor with a 85 Hz refresh rate, connected to a Pentium IV 3.2 GHz computer. The EyeLink 1000 tracker from SR Research in Mississauga, Canada was connected to a Pentium IV 2.8 GHz host computer to track and record eye movements. The eye-tracker had a sampling rate of 500 Hz with a spatial accuracy of >0.5° and a resolution of 0.01° in the pupil-tracking mode. Eye movements were tracked using an infrared motion system. To maintain a consistent viewing distance and minimize head movements, participants rested their forehead and chin on a support. They were positioned 60 cm away from the monitor screen, resulting in a visual field of approximately 29° horizontally and 22° vertically. At this standardized viewing distance, each stimulus image subtended at a visual angle of approximately 10.46° horizontally and 9.53° vertically to ensure sufficient visual detail for participants to clearly discern image content while minimizing head movements. Before the task, a standardized calibration procedure was conducted to assess eye movements. Participants were instructed to focus on 9 randomly appearing white dots arranged in a 3 × 3 array from the top left to the bottom right of the black display space.

EEG apparatus. Electroencephalography (EEG) data were recorded from 64 scalp sites using tin electrodes mounted in an elastic cap (Brain Products, Munich, Germany). Averages of the right and left mastoid were used as a reference while an electrode in the medial-frontal region was used as a ground. Vertical electrooculograms (EOG) were recorded by an electrode below the right eye. Horizontal EOG were recorded by an electrode placed lateral to the external canthi of the right eye. EEG and EOG activity were amplified with a DC∼100 Hz band-pass and continuously sampled at 500 Hz. All electrode impedances were maintained below 5 kΩ.

The adapted go/no-go paradigm

The adapted go/no-go paradigm assessed attentional focus by instructing participants to attend to pain/neutral images rather than the black block and measured attention inhibition by instructing participants to focus on the black block rather than on corresponding pain or neutral images. The paradigm procedure is shown in Fig. 1. First, a "+" fixation point was presented in the center of the computer screen, randomly lasting 800–1200 ms. Following a 100 ms blank screen, a red (no-go) or green (go) dot cue was presented in the center for 1000 ms. After another 100 ms blank screen, an image and an equally sized black block were presented simultaneously onscreen for 1500 ms. Positions of the image and black block appearing on left or right sides of the screen were balanced. Participants were required to focus their attention on the image or the black block according to the previous red or green cue. Finally, an 800–1200 ms blank screen ended the current trial.

Schematic illustration of the adapted go/no-go paradigm. A red circle represented the no-go signal, indicating a need to focus and maintain attention on the black block as soon as possible rather than focusing on the body image. A green circle represented the go signal, indicating a need to focus and maintain attention on the body image as soon as possible rather than the black block. The sequence of a single trial was as follows: first, a cross appeared in the center of the screen for 800–1200 ms, followed by a blank screen for 100 ms. Subsequently, a red or green circle appeared in the center of the screen for 1000 ms, followed by a blank screen for 100 ms. Then, a pain or neutral body image appeared on the left or right side of the screen, paired with a black block in its symmetrical position. Positions of each body image and black block were balanced. All task trials were pseudo-randomized to ensure that the same image-pair type did not appear consecutively more than two times.

Participants first completed 20 practice trials with neutral images that did not appear in the formal task. The formal task consisted of 60 pain body images and 60 neutral body images. There were 4 blocks, of 120 trials each: 60 trials each for go and no-go conditions. Each block included 15 pain images and 15 neutral images, each appearing 4 times. After completing each block, participants were required to rest for at least 1 min before proceeding to the next block.

Experimental design

The study employed a 2 pain status (CMSP, pain-free) × 2 cue type (go, no-go) × 2 image type (pain, neutral) three-factor mixed design. Pain status served as the between-participants factor, while cue type and image type were within-subjects factors. Dependent variables included EM indices (initial direction, FFL, FFD, and DT) toward images, and ERP components (i.e., N2, P2, P3, LPC) elicited by images.

Procedure

Ethics approval (H21068) for the study was provided by the Human Research Ethics Committee at Southwest University in Chongqing, China. Participants with CMSP were recruited from the campus hospital, while pain-free volunteers were recruited online through campus platforms. The two groups were closely matched for demographic characteristics (e.g., age, education level) and emotional status. Each participant arrived at the laboratory at their scheduled appointment time. Participants read and signed an informed consent form for the study followed by the registration of demographic information and completion of questionnaires. Subsequently, participants were seated 60 cm away from the computer screen, and EEG caps were placed on their heads, followed by the application of electrode gel. Prior to the start of the study tasks, a nine-point calibration was performed on each participant to ensure accurate recording of eye movements. Participants were then informed that the task required them to view or not view picture pairs based on the cues. Specifically, when the cue was a green circle, they were required to fixate on the body image as soon as possible and maintain their focus on that image rather than corresponding black block (go condition). Conversely, red circle cues indicated the no-go condition as described above). After reading task instructions and demonstrating their understanding the task requirements, participants began the practice trials. Following the practice session, the formal task commenced. EMs and ERPs were recorded during the presentation of image pairs. Completion of the study typically took about 50 min. Upon completion, participants were given monetary compensation and debriefed regarding the purposes of the research.

Data preparation and analysis

Preparation of EM data. EM data processing involved creating areas of interest centered on the position of body images and black blocks respectively. Fixations were defined as the gaze remaining at the same location in an image for at least 100 ms. The initial direction, FFL, FFD and DT for images in each trial were calculated based on previous research (Yang et al., 2012; 2013). After removing invalid data (i.e., when there were no fixation points in either area of interest for the trial), data were imported into IBM SPSS 26.0 for analysis. Biases in the initial direction toward pain and neutral images was obtained by calculating the percentage of trials in which EMs initially fixated upon these images rather than complementary black block as a percentage of total trials with EMs. FFL for pain and neutral images were defined as the time interval from the onset of body and black block image pairs to the first fixation located on the images. FFDs for pain and neutral images were defined as the duration of first fixations located on the images. DTs for pain and neutral images were defined as the total durations of all fixations located on the body images rather than black block during each trial.

Preparation of ERPs data. ERPs data processing involved the use of Matlab 2014a and EEGlab 14.0 toolboxes for analysis. When analyzing these data, the bilateral mastoid was used as the reference point, and independent component analysis (ICA) was used to automatically correct horizontal and vertical eye movements. A high-pass filter of 0.1 Hz, a low-pass filter of 30 Hz, and a notch filter of 50 Hz were applied. EEG epochs that were time-locked to image onset were extracted using an 1100 ms window-analysis time (100 ms pre-stimulus and 1000 ms post-stimulus) that was baseline corrected using the pre-stimulus interval signal. The EEG data for each particicipant were overlaid according to pain status, cue type and image type to obtain waveforms for the eight conditions within a 2 (CMSP, pain-free) × 2 (go, no-go) × 2 (pain, neutral) design. Based on theoretical and actual waveforms elicited in the current study, N2 was defined bynegative deflections over parietal-occipital electrodes (Po7, Poz, and Po8) 240–310 ms after image pair presentation. P2 and P3 were defined by positive-going detection over occipital electrodes (O1, Oz, and O2) 160–240 ms and 320–420 ms after image onset, respectively. LPC was identified based on positive-going detections over parietal electrodes (P3, Pz, and P4) 600–1000 ms after image pair onsets.

Statistics analyses of EMs and ERPs data. Preliminary χ2 tests and analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted to ensure that nonsignificant effects of pain status were retained for demographics and self-report measures of adjustment. Based on previous studies (Wang et al., 2014; Yang & Yan, 2013; Zhang et al., 2019), we conducted separate repeated measures ANOVAs with a 2 (pain status: CMSP, pain-free) × 2 (image type: pain, neutral) design in the go condition to assess effects of pain status on differences in EMs and ERPs, thereby evaluating attention engagement. Similarly, for attention inhibition, repeated measures ANOVAs based on the same 2 × 2 design were performed for no-go condition trials to assesseffects of pain status and image type on differences in EMs and ERPs. When a main effect or interaction was significant (p < .05), we performed post-hoc comparisons with the Bonferroni correction method to control for multiple comparisons.

Bivariate correlations and mediation analyses. We conducted bivariate correlation analyses to evaluate relationships between pain status and demographics (gender, age, education level), psychological measures (CES-D, ACS-total, ACS-focusing, ACS-shifting, FPQ-III, and PCS), pain-related EM (initial direction, FFL, FFD, and DT) biases and pain-related ERPs (N2, P2, P3, and LPC) biases. Pain-related EM biases were obtained by subtracting EM indice values toward pain images from EM index values toward neutral images in go and no-go conditions, respectively. Similarly, pain-related ERPs amplitude biases were obtained by subtracting ERPs amplitude elicited by pain images from those amplitude elicited by neutral images in go and no-go conditions, respectively. Next, we conducted mediation analyses to test whether pain status affected attentional control via neural activity. Mediation analyses were performed using Process v3.5, Model 4 for SPSS. In mediation models, the independent variable (X) was pain status, the dependent variable (Y) was each pain-related EM bias. Mediators (M) were pain-related ERPs amplitude biases. Bias-corrected bootstrap estimation analyses with 5000 boot-strapped samples were performed to calculate each mediation effect. These analyses elucidated indirect effects of pain status on behavioral (EM) performance via brain activity. The analyses yielded the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for indirect effects that were considered statistically significant at p < .05 when the CIs did not include zero.

Given that pain status influenced both brain activity and attentional control toward pain images compared to neutral images in the go condition, we assessed the extent to which pain status affected attentional control via changes in brain activity (ERPs). Mediation analysis revealed that pain status indirectly affected go condition pain-related FFL biases via pain image-evoked N2 amplitude biases (a*b = –3.62, SE = 2.17, 95%CI = [–.43, –8.72]; see Fig. 4A) as well as pain image-evoked P3 amplitude biases (a*b = –3.19, SE = 2.00, 95%CI = [–.28, –7.88]; see Fig. 4B), respectively. Because LPC latency occurs later than FFL, we did not further analyze LPC as a potential mediator between pain status and FFL.

Discussion

This study may be the first to employ simultaneous EM and ERP recordings to investigate pain-related attentional control mechanisms in a CMSP sample based on an adapted go/no-go paradigm. Partially consistent with our hypotheses, participants with CMSP exhibited significantly accelerated initial orientation (shorter FFL) and prolonged attentional maintenance (longer DT) specifically toward CMSP-pain-related images compared to neutral images during go trials, whereas no such biases were observed in a matched pain-free control group. This pattern aligns with prior evidence showing that chronic headache patients display stronger initial attentional biases toward pain faces than healthy controls do (Liossi et al., 2014; Schoth et al., 2015a, 2015b), while chronic back pain patients demonstrate faster first fixations and prolonged mean fixations on pain-related movement stimuli (Franklin et al., 2019). The consistency of these effects across studies contrasts with results of an earlier meta-analyses that reported no consistent attentional biases across heterogeneous chronic pain samples (Chan et al., 2020). Crucially, the emergence of these biases specifically with our CMSP-targeted stimuli aligns with the proposition that ecologically valid materials, which are tailored to patients' specific pain conditions, may be essential for effectively capturing potentially pathological attention-related engagement. The complete absence of these effects during no-go trials also suggests that people with CMSP retain a fundamental inhibitory capacity when explicitly instructed to suppress attention toward pain stimuli.

Neurophysiological findings further elucidated temporal dynamics of attentional engagement in patients with CMSP. Participants exhibited significantly larger N2, P2, P3, and LPC amplitudes to pain images versus neutral images during go trials. Critically, the absence of N1 differences was consistent with our hypothesis, as our paradigm required central fixation to be initiated before lateral gaze shifts toward target stimuli. As such, this design minimized early spatial-orienting conflicts that typically modulate N1. This finding aligns with Tabira et al. (2020) who found N1 amplitudes correlated with psychological factors (e.g., catastrophizing) but not stimulus valences within a chronic low back pain sample. Together, these results suggest preserved early sensory encoding when tasks avoid spatial competition between stimuli.

While the finding of an enhanced P2 component—reflecting automatic bottom-up hypervigilance—converges with Fernandes-Magalhaes et al. (2022) observations in fibromyalgia, the larger N2 amplitude we observed diverges from the N2 reduction reported in that study. This critical discrepancy underscores how attentional mechanisms may be modulated by laboratory task designs. Whereas dot-probe tasks capture implicit orienting, go/no-go paradigms explicitly recruits top-down regulatory networks (Chelazzi et al., 2013; Padmala & Pessoa, 2010). Supporting this contention, Chen et al. (2024) found that migraine patients exhibit prolonged P2 latencies during phasic alerting, reflecting delayed early threat evaluation—a contrast to our P2 results, possibly due in part to differing task goals (passive viewing vs. active response preparation). The interretation of paradigm-driven effects is further reinforced the established role of N2 responses in conflict monitoring during goal-directed behavior (Folstein & Van Petten, 2008). Our observation of a larger N2 component signifies heightened early attentional capture when patients intentionally engage with pain stimuli and contrasts, with evidence of reduced N2a in fibromyalgia during implicit tasks (Fernandes-Magalhaes et al., 2022) and diminished P3b during voluntary attention in generalized chronic pain (Cardoso et al., 2024; Gubler et al., 2022). Subsequently, amplified P3/LPC components may reflect sustained cognitive evaluation of pain salience—consistent with meta-analytic evidence linking late potentials to motivational relevance (Coll, 2018). This pattern diverges from studies showing reduced P3 in a chronic pain sanple during oddball tasks (Cardoso et al., 2024) or failed P3 modulation under a cognitive load (Veldhuijzen, Kenemans, de Bruin et al., 2006) and suggests that explicit pain-processing demands unmask heightened engagement otherwise obscured in passive paradigms. Crucially, Vossen et al. (2018) also demonstrated effects of pain hypervigilance in altering late ERP habituation, directly paralleling our LPC effects and confirming their role in the maintenance of pain processing.

Mediation analyses established precise neurocognitive pathways through which ERP components govern attentional dynamics. Enhanced N2 amplitudes to pain stimuli fully mediated the relationship between CMSP status and accelerated attentional orientation, intimating early top-down control as a mechanism driving initial hypervigilance. Conversely, larger P3 amplitudes partially mediated the association of pain status with prolonged attentional maintenance, suggesting that later cognitive resource allocations underly potential difficulties in attentional disengagement. This temporal cascade—early top-down capture (N2) preceding sustained resource allocation (P3) may aid in explaining the "sticky attention" phenomenon in CMSP and reconciling seemingly contradictory findings in the literature. While P2 amplification supports bottom-up hypervigilance models (Eccleston & Crombez, 1999), N2/P3 mediation highlights the top-down control mechanisms involving sustained engagement with pain stimuli. As such, this pattern aligns with evidence that executive attention systems modulate threat processing (Fu et al., 2017) and further contextualizes why general attention tasks can reveal impairments in chronic pain samples (Cardoso et al., 2024; Gubler et al., 2022).

Highly consistent null findings across EM and ERP measures during no-go trials also provide possible insights into the neural architecture of pain processing. While chronic pain broadly disrupts attentional networks (Veldhuijzen et al., 2006; Vossen et al., 2018), our findings supported relatively intact inhibitory capabilities among people with CMSP when task goals explicitly require suppression. This dissociation suggests CMSP patients retain the capacity to implement top-down control strategies that redirect attention from pain, consistent with evidence that working memory engagement can block nociceptive processing at early stages (Legrain et al., 2013). Such preserved functionality offers promise for interventions targeting cognitive control systems, particularly given neuroimaging evidence that fronto-parietal network modulation reduces threat bias (Fu et al., 2017) and that connectivity changes in executive networks correlate with pain reduction (Loggia et al., 2013; Napadow et al., 2012). The observed dissociation between impaired engagement and preserved inhibition aligns with emerging models of chronic pain as a disorder of disrupted network dynamics (Hemington et al., 2016), where our findings specifically implicate dysregulation of the cingulo-frontal-parietal cognitive control network during pain-relevant engagement (Mao et al., 2014). Recent research on attentional bias modification (ABM) training from Fernandes-Magalhaes et al. (2024) exemplifies this paradigm dependency, as their work indicates dot-probe training reduces P2 amplitudes (reflecting attenuated early threat detection) while enhancing N2a (indicating improved conflict monitoring)—a pattern diametrically opposed to the larger N2 elicted during active pain engagement in our study. Given that Fernandes-Magalhaes and colleagues' intervention improved mood but not pain symptoms, it can be hypothesized that modifying early components alone is insufficient for pain control.

The current study underscores significant methodological innovations that help to address critical limitations in attentional research on pain. Our go/no-go paradigm may have successfully dissociated attentional engagement from inhibition—a fundamental distinction often obscured in passive viewing or implicit attention tasks (Fernandes-Magalhaes et al., 2022). Developing ecologically valid, CMSP-specific stimuli also aided in overcoming limitations with the use of generic affective images that fail to activate condition-relevant neural circuits (Wang et al., 2020). Moreover, synchronized eye-tracking in tandem with ERP measurements facilitated capture of the dynamic attentional cascade from early neural responses to behavioral outcomes and directly addresses calls for multimodal assessment in pain research (Vossen et al., 2018). These advances hold substantial translational potential for optimizing laboratory research on pain-related attention and ABM training, particularly in light of recent null findings regarding gamified ABM training for chronic pain (Vermeir et al., 2025). Our paradigm provided a granular methodology that is useful in identifying specific attentional components (engagement vs. inhibition) that could serve as precise therapeutic targets for future ABM training protocols. By quantifying early neurobehavioral markers of attentional dysfunction through synchronized EM/ERP recordings, this approach provides foundations that may guide more precise assessments of attentional deficits and personalized intervention strategies.

Despite these methodological strengths, several limitations should be considered. First, age and gender as determinants of chronic musculoskeletal pain were not systematically examined; our patient and pain‑free control samples were drawn from a narrow age range and consisted predominantly of women. Hence, generalizability across age, gender, and chronic pain conditions may be limited. Individual differences in chronic musculoskeletal pain may relate, in part, to gender differences in pain sensitivity (Ellermeier & Westphal, 1995; Han et al., 2008; Osborne & Davis, 2022), and advancing age increases risk for musculoskeletal pain (El-Tallawy et al., 2021). Cardiovascular factors such as hypertension might also modulate pain sensitivity, particularly in women (Nyklíček et al., 1999). Future research should therefore enhance representativeness through broader demographic sampling that includes older adults, balanced gender representation, and consideration of cardiovascular and related individual‑difference factors. Second, while static images provide experimental control, dynamic multimodal stimuli could better capture clinical pain experiences and warrant consideration in future work. Third, although all participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and passed the 9-point eye-tracker calibration, we did not conduct objective clinical visual acuity testing (e.g., Snellen chart) prior to testing; thus, at least in theory, subtle individual differences in visual acuity could have contributed to variance in early visual ERP components. Fourth, future studies should examine how clinical variables (e.g., pain severity) moderate identified pathways, particularly given evidence linking prefrontal dysfunction to symptom severity (Mao et al., 2014). Finally, generalizability requires validation in diverse chronic pain populations whose attentional systems may be compromised.

In conclusion, this research most fundamentally advanced our understanding of attentional pathology involved in CMSP by establishing maladaptive engagement—not impaired inhibition—as the core attentional deficit. Identified neurocognitive mechanisms (N2-mediated capture and P3-mediated maintenance) provide empirically-grounded evidence for plausible mechanisms involved in pain-related hypervigilance, based on the integration of early bottom-up capture and top-down control processes. By documenting preserved inhibitory function during explicit suppression tasks, this study identified a promising target for interventions harnessing cognitive control systems to disrupt potentially pathological engagement patterns. As such, our main findings provide foundations for testing attention modification therapies that normalize maladaptive engagement by modulating identified neurocognitive pathways.
