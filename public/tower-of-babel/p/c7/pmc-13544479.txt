Extent of Sinus Surgery Is Associated With Disease Control in Biologic Treated Type 2 Dominant CRS
Authors: Nicholas J. Campion, Peta‐Lee Sacks, Lu Hui Png, Ruoxin Hua, Rhea Darbari Kaul, Raquel Alvarado, Christine Choy, Raymond Sacks, Peter Earls, Raewyn G. Campbell, Larry Kalish, Richard J. Harvey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundA greater benefit of biologics is observed after surgery in Type 2 chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP). However, the extent of surgery remains undefined in many studies. This study evaluated the extent of surgery on disease control in patients receiving biologics for refractory Type 2 dominant CRSwNP.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was performed on patients with CRSwNP treated with biologics for ≥ 6 months at a tertiary center. All patients had undergone prior sinus surgery, which included a sphenoethmoidectomy and then extension via Draf I, IIa, IIb, IIc, or III. Disease control was defined as having a SNOT‐22 ≤ 30, endoscopic modified Lund–Mackay ≤ 4, and the absence of rescue therapy within 6 months. Comparisons were made across surgical and biologic groups, and logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with disease control.ResultsA total of 159 patients were assessed (age 50.9 ± 12.8 years, 45.3% female). Disease control was achieved in 49.1% of patients. Disease control was greater with extended surgery (Draf III 60.8% [49.7%–70.8%] vs. Draf IIc 68.3% [53.0%–80.4%] vs. Draf IIa 8.7% [2.4%–26.8%] vs. Draf I 0% [0.0%–19.4%]; p < 0.001). The control was similar across biologic types (anti‐IL‐4αR 63.3% [45.5%–78.1%] vs. anti‐IL‐5/5R 45.7% [37.3%–54.3%]; p = 0.083). On regression, extended surgery (Draf III + IIc) was associated with disease control (Draf III/IIc vs. Draf IIa/I OR: 110.2 [12.7–14,682.5]) but not biologic type (anti‐IL‐4αR vs. anti‐IL‐5/5R OR: 2.2 [0.7–7.2]). However, class switching had already occurred in 15.1% of patients initially treated with anti‐IL‐5/5R.ConclusionsDisease control was greatest in CRSwNP patients who had both extended sinus surgery and optimized biologic treatment.

Background

Chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) represents an inflammatory phenotype affecting approximately 0.5%–4% of the adult population and is characterized by persistent Type 2 immune responses [1, 2]. The economic impact of CRS is significant, with direct healthcare costs in the United States exceeding $8–$10 billion annually [3, 4]. Traditional management has relied on a combination of topical and systemic corticosteroids and endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS). However, historically, some patients with severe disease experienced a “revolving door” of care due to high recurrence rates despite surgery and compliance with local therapy [5]. The emergence of biologic therapies for CRSwNP in the last 10 years has revolutionized the treatment of uncontrolled disease with monoclonal antibodies such as dupilumab, benralizumab, mepolizumab, and, more recently tezepelumab. These antibodies target key Type 2 inflammatory mediators, including interleukin‐4 (IL‐4), IL‐5, IL‐13 and TSLP, and they are highly effective but there are still nonresponders and not all patients achieve remission [6, 7, 8, 9]. In this regard, a critical debate remains regarding the optimal integration of surgical intervention and biologic therapy. Traditional concepts of ESS often focus on sinus ventilation, yet evidence suggests that the “completeness” of surgery, including anatomically complex regions such as the frontal sinus, is likely to be an important determinant of long‐term disease control [10, 11]. Inadequate frontal sinus access is often a surrogate for poor upper ethmoid cavity dissection, and leads to persistent remodeled tissue, areas for mucociliary dysfunction, and poor penetration of topical therapies [12, 13]. Recent research on the completeness of surgery in creating a neo‐sinus cavity, such as the Amsterdam Classification of Completeness of Endoscopic Sinus Surgery (ACCESS) and the Completion of Surgery Index (CoSI), has demonstrated that low surgical completeness scores are strong predictors of the subsequent need for biologic “rescue” [14, 15, 16].

Current guidelines, including the European Position Paper on Rhinosinusitis and Nasal Polyps (EPOS)/The European Forum for Research and Education in Allergy and Airway Diseases (EUFOREA) 2025 statement on biologics and surgery, acknowledge the benefit of complete surgery but have yet to incorporate specific surgical mandates due to limited prospective evidence [17, 18]. Data on extensive sinus procedures, such as complete sphenoethmoidectomy with Draf III, suggest a superior clinical baseline that enhances the effectiveness of biologics or perhaps even obviates the need for them in select patients [19]. However, the extent of surgery and the resultant therapeutic response to biologics remain poorly defined. This study aims to explore the extent of sinus surgery on disease control in previously operated patients with severe diffuse Type 2 dominant CRSwNP undergoing biologic therapy.

Methods

Study Design

A cross‐sectional study was performed on patients with Type 2 dominant (> 10 eosinophils/high‐power field [HPF]) CRS who had commenced and continued treatment with either anti‐IL‐5Rα (benralizumab), anti‐IL‐4Rα (dupilumab), or anti‐IL‐5 (mepolizumab) for at least 6 months or longer before study inclusion. All patients had symptomatic upper airway disease, had previously undergone ESS and were started on biologic therapy by the authors, or had symptomatic upper airway disease and were referred for further care after commencing a biologic elsewhere. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Study Population

The study population comprised adult patients (≥ 18 years) treated at a tertiary referral rhinology center with Type 2 dominant chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) diagnosed according to EPOS 2020 criteria [20]. All included patients were receiving biologic therapy for either uncontrolled severe asthma or severe refractory CRS in accordance with Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) guidelines.

All patients had previously undergone ESS before the initiation of biologic therapy, which included at least sphenoethmoidectomy as part of their management. The extent of sinus surgery was recorded from operative reports, confirmed by postoperative nasal endoscopy, and classified by anatomical modification of frontoethmoid anatomy as defined in Section 2.4. All patients received standardized postoperative medical therapy consisting of daily corticosteroid nasal irrigation (mometasone 2 mg or budesonide 1 mg in 240 mL saline). Patients with eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, allergic fungal rhinosinusitis, cystic fibrosis, or known immunodeficiency were excluded.

Demographic Characteristics

Demographic data, including sex, age, atopy status, asthma status, aspirin‐exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD) status, smoking status, and number of previous ESS procedures at baseline, were collected. Asthma was defined by the current use of a bronchodilator or inhaled corticosteroid and/or evidence of airway reversibility (15% change post bronchodilator/post challenge or peak flow rate variability). Atopy was determined by a positive result in either skin prick allergy testing or an automated immunoassay (ImmunoCap) to detect serum‐specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies to the following four aeroallergen mixes (1) grass mix, (2) dust mite, (3) mold, and (4) animal epithelium. A serum‐specific IgE level of greater than 0.35 kU/L for any of these aeroallergen mixes was considered a positive result and the patient was classified as atopic. AERD was defined as patients with asthma as defined above, documented hypersensitivity to nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDS), and evidence of CRS. Active smokers were defined as patients who smoked at least weekly and were actively smoking or had ceased smoking in the past 12 months. Ex‐smokers were defined as patients who smoked at least weekly for 12 months and had ceased more than 12 months ago. Never smokers were defined as patients who denied ever smoking. Furthermore, the 22‐Item Sinonasal Outcome Test (SNOT‐22) score (SNOT‐22, 0–110) and modified Lund–Mackay (mLM) endoscopy scores were collected for all patients [21, 22].

Definitions of the Extent of Sinus Surgery

The extent of sinus surgery was determined from operative reports and classified by the anatomical modification of frontoethmoid anatomy by a modified Draf classification based on the healed postoperative result, as Draf I, IIa, IIb, IIc, or III as previously described [23]. In summary, the sphenoethmoidectomy or neo‐sinus cavities were classified as Draf I if ethmoid cells were still visible in the frontal recess, IIa if all the ethmoid cells had been removed and the posterior table of the frontal sinus was hidden by the axilla of the middle turbinate and therefore not visible on 0° endoscopy. Draf IIb, if any part of a IIa cavity was extended medial to the middle turbinate. Cavities with a clear endoscopic view into the frontal sinus, posterior table, and intersinus septum using a 0° endoscope were classified as Draf IIc and Draf III for cavities with a clear view of the frontal sinus apex, a septal window, and lateral extension present. Serum and Histological Profile

Baseline blood samples were taken from patients before initiation of biologic therapy and assessed for serum eosinophil count (×109 cells/L) and serum IgE (IU/mL). The eosinophil proportion of the total white cell count was also collected. Baseline tissue samples were obtained before biologic initiation, either intraoperatively, in the outpatient clinic, or from archived histopathology specimens where surgery had been performed externally, and were assessed for eosinophil density (< 10, 10–100, or > 100 cells/HPF) [24].

Biologic Therapy

For benralizumab, all patients were started at 30 mg every 4 weeks for three cycles and then every 8 weeks thereafter. Dupilumab patients were started at 400 mg for one cycle and then 200 mg every 2 weeks thereafter. For mepolizumab patients were started and continued at 100 mg every 4 weeks.

Eligibility for benralizumab and dupilumab was based on PBS criteria for severe asthma, defined as persistent uncontrolled disease for ≥ 12 months despite optimized therapy. Optimized therapy required treatment with high‐dose inhaled corticosteroids for ≥ 12 months and prior exposure to systemic corticosteroids (cumulative dose ≥ 500 mg). Evidence of Type 2 inflammation was mandatory, defined by a blood eosinophil count ≥ 300 cells/µL, or for dupilumab, fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) ≥ 25 parts per billion (ppb) within the preceding 12 months.

Eligibility for mepolizumab required a diagnosis of CRSwNP with prior ESS and persistent disease despite adherence to intranasal corticosteroid therapy for ≥ 2 months. Patients were required to meet at least two of the following criteria: bilateral endoscopic nasal polyp score ≥ 5/8 (minimum score ≥ 2 per nasal cavity), nasal obstruction Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) > 5/10, or overall symptom VAS > 7/10. In addition, a blood eosinophil count ≥ 300 cells/µL within the preceding 12 months was needed.

Defining Disease Control in CRS

Disease control was defined as described previously [25]. Patients with “controlled” disease were defined as (1) absent or non‐bothersome symptom burden, as represented by a “normal to mild” SNOT‐22 score of 30 or less as per the EUFOREA criteria of “adequate response” to therapy [26]; (2) healthy or almost healthy mucosa on nasoendoscopy, defined as a total mLM score of 4 or less [21]; and (3) not having need for rescue treatment, defined as no use of systemic corticosteroid or antibiotics within 6 months and no need for revision surgery during biologic therapy. Patients not fulfilling the above criteria for controlled disease were defined as having “poorly controlled” disease. This definition was used to closely align with modern concepts of clinical remission in CRSwNP [27]. Endoscopic examples of controlled and poorly controlled disease are shown in Figure 2.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp.) and in R (v4.5.1; R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https//www.R‐project.org). Parametric continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical results are presented as percentages. Comparisons between patients in the controlled disease groups by Draf type and biologic type were performed. Age was compared across groups using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐square test. To evaluate factors associated with disease control, penalized Firth logistic regression was performed to generate odds ratios (ORs [95% CI]) for sinus surgery extent (Draf classification), biologic class (anti‐IL‐4Rα vs. anti IL‐5/5R), asthma status, AERD status, serum eosinophils (per 0.1 × 109/L increase), and tissue eosinophilia (> 100 cells/HPF vs. < 100 cells/HPF) as independent variables to assess their relative contributions to disease control. Confidence intervals for reported rates of disease control outcome were calculated using Wilson's interval. All tests were two‐tailed, and a p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Overview of Study Population

A total of 159 patients with diagnosed severe diffuse Type 2 dominant CRS receiving benralizumab (22.6%), mepolizumab (58.5%), or dupilumab (18.9%) were included (age 50.9 ± 12.8 years, and 45.3% female). Active and previous smoking were reported by 5.4% and 19.6% of patients, respectively. Asthma, atopy, and AERD were present in 78.9%, 56.9%, and 18.1% of patients, respectively. The population demonstrated a high Type 2 inflammatory burden, with a total IgE of 269.7 ± 518.6 U/L, serum eosinophil count of 0.4 ± 0.4 × 109/L, and 59.5% of patients exhibiting > 100 eosinophils per HPF on representative tissue biopsy. Patients had undergone 1.3 ± 1.5 prior ESS procedures before referral to tertiary rhinology care. The extent of surgery was defined as Draf I (10.1%) Draf IIa (14.5%), Draf IIb (0%), Draf IIc (25.8%), and Draf III (49.7%) (Table 1). Disease control or clinical remission was achieved in 49.1% of the total population.

Patients Undergoing More Extensive Sinus Surgery Exhibit a Higher Burden of Comorbid Inflammatory Disease

Baseline characteristics differed across surgical subtypes. As expected, patients who had had more extensive surgery had more Type 2 comorbidities with higher rates of asthma (Draf I [62.5%] vs. Draf IIa [76.2%] vs. Draf IIc [69.2%] vs. Draf III [88.2%]; p = 0.030) and AERD (Draf I [7.7%] vs. Draf IIa [0.0%] vs. Draf IIc [17.9%] vs. Draf III [25.0%]; p = 0.045). The serum eosinophil and eosinophil proportion of the total white blood cell count was highest in the Draf I group (serum eosinophils 0.6 × 109/L; 9.5% eosinophils/WCC), already suggesting poor control before biologics despite less comorbid Type 2 disease. No other baseline characteristics differed significantly between groups (Table 2).

More Extensive Sinus Surgery Is Associated With Improved Disease Control

The extent of sinus surgery was associated with differences in disease control. Patients who had undergone more extensive sinus surgery had greater “controlled” disease (Draf III 60.8% [49.7%–70.8%] vs. Draf IIc 68.3% [53.0%–80.4%] vs. Draf IIa 8.7% [2.4%–26.8%] vs. Draf I 0.0% [0.0–19.4]; p < 0.001) (Table 3). In the Draf I group (n = 16), failure to achieve disease control was driven by persistent endoscopic disease (mLMS > 4) in 93.8%, elevated SNOT‐22 (> 30) in 46.7% and the need for systemic therapy or revision surgery in 50.0%, with only 25.0% classified as uncontrolled based on endoscopic findings alone. Multiple comparisons found no significant difference in the rates of disease control between Draf I and IIa (0.0% vs. 8.7%; p = 1.000) and Draf IIc and Draf III (68.3% vs. 60.8%; p = 1.000) (Table S1).

Disease Control Did Not Differ Significantly by Biologic Type But Class Switching Had Already Occurred

Although no statistically significant differences were observed across mepolizumab, benralizumab, and dupilumab or when grouped by biologic class (Table 4 and Table S2), 15.1% of all patients commenced on anti‐IL‐5/5R therapy had been switched to anti‐IL‐4Rα therapy due to poor response, suggesting a predisposing phenotype for effect. However, when compared across biologic classes (anti‐IL‐5/5R vs. anti‐IL‐4Rα), patients treated with dupilumab demonstrated a higher proportion of patients displaying healthy or near‐healthy nasal mucosa (mLKS < 4) on endoscopy (80.0% vs. 60.5%; p = 0.045), data not shown.

Extent of Sinus Surgery Is the Dominant Determinant of Disease Control

In the Firth penalized logistic regression analysis, the extent of sinus surgery emerged as the strongest factor associated with disease control. Compared with limited surgery (Draf I + IIa), patients who had undergone an extended procedure (Draf IIc + Draf III) demonstrated higher odds of achieving disease control (OR: 110.2 [12.7–14,682.5]; p < 0.001) (Figure 3 and Table S3). The distribution of biologic class was similar across surgical extent groups, with anti‐IL‐5 therapy comprising the majority in all cohorts (Draf III [74.7%], Draf IIc [85.4%], Draf IIa [91.3%], Draf I [87.5%]; p = 0.204, data not shown), showing no significant association between biologic class and extent of surgery. Biologic class was not significantly associated with disease control, but class switching had already occurred in 15.1% of patients initially treated with anti‐IL‐5/5R. Marked tissue eosinophilia (> 100 eosinophils per HPF) was associated with reduced disease control (OR: 0.3 [0.1–0.8]; p = 0.020) (Figure 3 and Table S3).

Discussion

This study determined that the extent of sinus surgery is a strong factor in determining disease control in patients receiving optimized biologic therapy for severe diffuse Type 2 dominant CRSwNP. Patients who had undergone extended procedures (Draf IIc/III) achieved substantially higher rates of composite disease control than those who had limited surgery (Draf I/IIa), despite a greater baseline burden of asthma and AERD. These findings suggest that surgical completeness remains a dominant determinant of outcome in the biologic era.

The historical focus of ESS was functional ventilation of the paranasal sinuses. However, in the context of Type 2‐driven disease, ventilation is not what drives polyp recurrence [11]. These results support the shift toward “anatomical completeness,” where a simple surgical cavity is prioritized not for ventilation, but to facilitate the delivery of topical pharmacotherapy, avoid mucociliary dysfunction, and maximally remove remodeled tissue from the prior active disease. This concept is particularly relevant in the frontal sinus as Draf IIa procedures achieve frontal sinus lavage in only 12.5% of nasal irrigations, whereas this rate is much higher after extended surgery (Draf III) [12]. As Type 2 inflammation is not limited to polypoid mucosa but is equally present in non‐polypoid mucosa, residual diseased compartments may perpetuate inflammation and contribute to therapeutic failure; thus the concept of “incomplete” surgery as a driver of therapeutic failure is increasingly supported by objective radiologic scoring systems. The ACCESS has been validated as a predictor of clinical response [15]. Patients who achieve disease control after revision surgery without the need for biologics have significantly higher mean preoperative ACCESS scores (10.1) than those who remain uncontrolled (3.7) [10], and patients with lower ACCESS scores do subjectively better on biologic therapy [28]. In assessing multiple biomarker and objective scoring parameters, in CRSwNP, the ACCESS score was the only confirmed independent predictive factor of response to monoclonal antibody therapy [16]. Furthermore, this is supported by data demonstrating that patients undergoing revision surgery immediately before dupilumab initiation exhibit more favorable biological responses, with sustained suppression of Type 2 inflammatory signaling compared to those without [29]. Similarly, the CoSI demonstrates that patients with a preoperative score of < 70 (indicating incomplete previous surgery) experience the greatest quality‐of‐life (SNOT‐22) and asthma control test improvements after a thorough revision surgery [14, 30]. In addition, recent work has shown that biologics were necessary in only 14.9% of patients who had undergone a Draf III procedure at 2 years post‐surgery [19]. This study's findings align with these data, as patients with extended surgical baselines (Draf IIc/III) had better disease control after optimized biologic therapy. For a subset of patients, “refractory” disease may actually represent a failure of complete surgery, or lack of a neo‐sinus cavity, with ineffective delivery of topical therapy and mucociliary dysfunction, rather than a failure of systemic or biologic therapy.

The increasing emphasis on remission or disease control in CRSwNP reflects a shift toward defining meaningful disease stability. We have chosen a clinically pragmatic definition recognizing that many patients with long‐standing Type 2 CRSwNP demonstrate residual tissue remodeling despite effective suppression of active disease.

Symptom burden remains central to disease control. While a SNOT‐22 score below 20 approximates normative population values reported in validation studies of healthy controls [31], this threshold may be overly stringent in severe CRSwNP populations who have accumulated comorbid asthma, sleep disturbance, and irreversible olfactory impairment. The threshold of SNOT‐22 < 30, as incorporated into biologic monitoring, represents substantial improvement while allowing for noninflammatory residual morbidity [26]. This level corresponds to low residual symptom burden and is often associated with patient‐perceived control/remission.

Endoscopic assessment should likewise avoid dichotomous definitions. Absolute mucosal normality (e.g., zero edema or discharge) may be unrealistic in postsurgical cavities where scarring and mild or stable mucosal thickening reflect prior remodeling rather than active Type 2 inflammation. A more defensible remission construct would require minimal, nonprogressive findings without features of active eosinophilic disease, rather than a complete absence of visible change. This aligns with broader CRS consensus statements emphasizing inflammatory control rather than anatomical perfection [20]. Treatment burden provides an important anchor. Sustained remission should require the absence of systemic corticosteroid need and no surgical indication over a defined period, commonly ≥ 6–12 months. We chose a clinically balanced remission framework in CRSwNP (1) SNOT‐22< 30, (2) minimal, stable endoscopic inflammatory findings, (3) no systemic corticosteroid requirement, and (4) stability over 6–12 months. Such criteria reflect real‐world disease biology while maintaining conceptual rigor.

Another important factor in determining disease control is strategies to ensure optimal postoperative healing and appropriate biological selection. As demonstrated in this study, patients who underwent a Carolyn's window procedure but whose healed postoperative cavity was ultimately classified as Draf IIa rather than Draf IIc exhibited lower rates of disease control. To ensure long‐term surgical success in extended surgery, postoperative healing can be optimized through the use of free mucosal grafts or vascularized flaps to cover exposed bone [32, 33]. Adjuvant strategies, including silicone splints, steroid‐impregnated stents, reshaping the middle turbinate, and delayed irrigation protocols to facilitate graft integration, have been shown to minimize scarring and adhesions [34, 35].

Optimal biological agent selection is key to achieving disease control considering comorbid asthma, allergies, and markers of Type 2 inflammation. Although we did not detect any difference in disease control rates by biologic type, a large proportion of patients had already undergone optimization of their biologic therapy and had already been class switched [25]. While remission rates could be achieved with both anti‐IL‐5/5R and anti‐IL‐4Rα therapies, there is a clear nonresponse rate with anti‐IL‐5/5R that requires switching to dupilumab and is consistent with data comparing biologics [36, 37]. Future analysis of disease control and the extent of surgery with other monoclonal antibodies targeting upstream cytokines, such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), warrants investigation [38].

The integration of biologics and surgery necessitates a strategic approach to patient selection and timing. While biologics provide rapid symptomatic relief and olfactory recovery [6], they impose a substantial economic burden. A long‐term cost‐utility analysis estimated that 36 years of dupilumab therapy costs approximately $536,420, compared to $50,436 for ESS [39]. Recent budget impact analyses further indicate that utilizing biologics as a first‐line therapy (no prior surgery) would increase direct costs by 184%–1048%, depending on the scenario [40].

This study has limitations. The retrospective design and single‐center setting may introduce selection bias. Specifically, patients in the Draf IIc/III group were more likely to have asthma and AERD, representing a “more difficult” baseline population. However, this limitation is somewhat negated by demonstrating that this group achieved the best outcomes despite their higher inflammatory burden, which reinforces the potency of the surgical intervention. Furthermore, differences in group sizes may have influenced comparisons; thus, the small number of patients in the Draf I subgroup and the tertiary referral nature of our cohort may have limited the representation of patients with milder disease or remission following limited surgery. However, this was addressed as far as possible through outcome‐specific denominators, mechanism‐based grouping, and multivariable modeling using Firth‐penalized logistic regression to reduce small‐sample bias and separation. In addition, the magnitude of the OR associated with extended surgery reflects near‐complete separation between limited and extended groups rather than a precise biological effect size. While Firth's approach mitigates small‐sample bias, residual confounding cannot be excluded. The primary strength of this work lies in its real‐world evaluation of the interaction between surgical extent and biologic efficacy, providing practical evidence for surgical planning in the biologic era.

Prospective research is needed to define the optimal timeframe for surgical intervention relative to biologic initiation. Ongoing initiatives such as the RELIBIO trial [41] and the international INVENT registry [42] will be vital in providing real‐world validation of these findings across larger, multicenter populations and the optimal timing for biologic therapy in Type 2 dominant CRS. In addition, future studies should investigate whether adjuvant perioperative biologics can improve wound healing and long‐term optimal cavity shape after extended sinus procedures.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the extent of sinus surgery significantly influences disease control in patients receiving optimized biologic therapy. A proportion of anti‐IL‐5/5R‐treated patients (15.3%) had already demonstrated a suboptimal response and had been class switched to anti‐IL4/13, but after this had occurred, the extent of surgery was a significant factor in both groups. Given the substantial cost of biologics, achieving surgical completeness should remain a fundamental pillar of care, enhancing therapeutic success and ensuring the maximal patient benefit imparted by biologic therapies.
