From Behavior to Dynamics: Decoding Carrier Roles in Cu‐based Photocathodes for Solar‐Driven CO2 Reduction
Authors: Yi‐Cheng Wang, Chenyu Xu, Peng‐Fei Sui, Yi Liu, Xiaolei Wang, Jing‐Li Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCu‐based photocathodes offer unique advantages for photoelectrochemical CO2 reduction (PEC CO2RR) due to their earth abundance, tunable electronic structures and capacity to efficiently produce multi‐carbon (C2+) products. However, their practical performance is fundamentally governed by the dynamics of photogenerated charge carriers, whose generation, separation, accumulation, transport, and extraction directly determine both catalytic efficiency and stability. This review examines how carrier behavior governs key reaction steps, evaluates material design strategies for tuning interfacial charge dynamics, and summarizes emerging techniques for probing these dynamics and interfacial transformations in operando. Looking ahead, integrating precise carrier control with stability and selectivity will be critical, requiring deeper insights into the coupling between carrier physics and catalytic function to guide the development of next‐generation Cu‐based photocathodes for PEC CO2RR.Keywords: carrier dynamics, Cu‐based photocathodes, energy‐time‐space scale, photoelectrochemical CO2 reduction

Introduction

Fossil fuels have long served as the dominant energy source in modern society. With the accelerating pace of population growth and industrialization, alongside the rapid consumption of fossil fuels, the balance of the carbon‐neutral cycle has been severely disrupted, leading to a significant increase in carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions and posing a severe threat to global climate stability [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. As a result, achieving carbon sustainability and neutrality have become one of the central concerns for governments and scientists alike. Although CO2 is the primary greenhouse gas responsible for the severe global climate change, it is also a readily available carbon source [6]. Several approaches such as biotransformation, thermochemical conversion, and electrocatalytic reduction have been developed for the waste‐to‐value utilization of CO2 [7, 8, 9]. Among these, the photoelectrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (PEC CO2RR) is considered as a promising strategy that can simultaneously utilize clean solar energy and enable carbon‐neutral sustainability [10, 11, 12]. Generally, PEC CO2RR refers to a solar‐driven process that converts CO2 into value‐added chemical fuels such as CO and formic acid (HCOOH) through redox reactions occurring at photoactive semiconductor electrodes. To clarify it precisely, light harvesting by the semiconductor generates photogenerated electron‐hole pairs (EHPs), which are separated and directed toward the photoelectrode/electrolyte interface under an applied bias or built‐in potential. The photogenerated electrons drive the multi‐electron CO2 reduction process at the photocathode, while the photogenerated holes are consumed in the oxidation reaction at the anode. Recently, various materials have been explored as the photocathodes for PEC CO2RR, such as ZnTe [13, 14, 15], GaP [16], InP [17, 18, 19], p‐Si [20, 21, 22], and perovskites [23, 24, 25, 26]. Among them, Cu‐based semiconductors and catalysts have emerged as prominent candidate due to their cost‐effectiveness, broad visible‐light absorption, and intrinsically active sites for CO2 activation [27, 28, 29, 30]. More importantly, Cu has unique catalytic capability toward not only one‐carbon (C1) production but also multi‐carbon (C2+) production depending on their surface coordination environment, oxidation state, and electronic structure [31, 32]. Therefore, Cu‐based photocathodes represent a vital research direction for PEC CO2RR, especially offering significant advantages in high‐value hydrocarbons or C2+ production.

Despite the rapid development of Cu‐based photocathodes and their excellent capability toward CO2RR, the conceptual framework used to illustrate and optimize their performance remains largely inherited from electrocatalysis. Most existing studies and reviews on PEC CO2RR still adopt a surface‐centric paradigm, in which catalytic activity and selectivity are interpreted almost exclusively in terms of the chemical nature of surface active sites and reaction intermediates. Within this framework, performance is regarded as a direct consequence of adsorption energetics and interfacial reaction pathways. However, a PEC system is fundamentally different from a purely electrochemical one, in which carrier dynamics play non‐negligible roles. For example, a kinetic framework for metal oxide photoanodes in PEC water oxidation has been established, highlighting how short‐lived photogenerated carriers must survive long enough to drive slow interfacial water oxidation catalysis [33], and emphasizing the energetic cost required to achieve carrier lifetime gain for charge extraction [34]. Similarly, in PEC CO2RR, the reactive interface is not fed by an external circuit, but by photogenerated carriers that originate in the semiconductor bulk and must undergo generation, separation, transport, and extraction before participating in surface chemistry. Consequently, the semiconductor bulk is not a passive background, and carrier behavior is not a secondary detail. Rather, it constitutes an indispensable physical foundation that governs whether, where, and when electrons become available for catalysis.

Specifically, Cu‐based photocathodes in PEC system are always immobilized and designed as multi‐layer thin‐film architectures on conductive substrates [35]. In such configurations, light can only be harvested from one direction, and photogenerated EHPs must travel through distinct material interfaces and along opposite directions [36, 37]. These immobilized thin‐film architectures can also result in longer carrier diffusion paths and increased recombination risks. Moreover, the interfacial contact between multiple layers introduces additional charge transfer resistance and localized defect states. The situation is further complicated by the fact that many Cu‐based semiconductors, such as Cu2O, possess intrinsically low carrier mobility and high densities of surface trap states, which aggravate bulk recombination and hinder effective charge extraction [38, 39, 40]. Another crucial challenge arises from the complex interplay between carriers and electrolyte species at the solid‐liquid interface. Cu‐based PEC systems could involve transient chemical states and spatially heterogeneous process, further complicates the charge extraction [29, 41]. As a result, the material designing and structure optimization of Cu‐based photocathodes with modulated carrier dynamics are critical in view of the light harvesting, charge separation, and catalytic activity. More fundamentally, the selectivity and durability of CO2 reduction of Cu‐based materials are strongly governed by the dynamics of photogenerated charge carriers. The generation, separation, transport, and accumulation of electrons and holes along with their interaction with surface adsorbates and electrolyte species, collectively determine the local reaction environment and the eventual catalytic pathway. While several excellent reviews have addressed the development of PEC CO2RR systems from the perspective of semiconductor or cocatalyst classification, most of them focus on material composition without explicitly considering how photocarrier behavior governs device performance. For example, several works have summarized the development of Cu‐based photocathodes by emphasizing material fabrication [42, 43], modification strategies [27], and product‐dependent reaction mechanisms [44, 45]. Others have focused on photocathode interface engineering, where interfacial architectures are discussed as key factors for enhancing charge transfer and product selectivity [46, 47]. These studies have greatly advanced the understanding of PEC CO2RR from the perspectives of material composition and catalytic reaction chemistry. However, the discussion of photogenerated carriers in these reviews is often embedded within individual material systems or engineering strategies, rather than being treated as the central thread that connects semiconductor physics, carrier dynamics, and catalytic performance. In contrast, this review aims to provide a comprehensive overview centered on the carrier dynamics of Cu‐based photocathodes and corresponding strategies to modulate them, and how their dynamic evolution impacts CO2 reduction selectivity, efficiency, and stability.

In this review, we first summarize the fundamental principles of PEC CO2RR, followed by an overview of Cu‐based photocathode materials and charge carrier dynamics. We then analyze key strategies that have been developed to modulate carrier dynamics, and highlight emerging experimental and computational approaches for probing photocarrier behaviors. This mechanistic and function‐oriented framework offers valuable guidance for the rational design of high‐performance Cu‐based photocathodes, and accelerate progress toward practical PEC CO2RR applications.

Fundamentals of PEC CO2RR at Photocathodes

Solar energy, originating from nuclear fusion in the sun, can be regarded as an electromagnetic wave [48, 49, 50]. From the perspective of terminal energy output, it can be harvested and converted into electrical and chemical energy via photoelectric methods. Since solar energy is widely considered as a clean and infinite energy source, PEC CO2RR as “artificial photosynthesis” has been studied since the late 1970s [51, 52]. In a typical PEC CO2RR system, there are three principal processes during the whole process: (1) solar light harvesting and generation of charge carriers, that is, electrons (e−) and holes (h+), (2) separation and transport of these charge carriers, and (3) interfacial redox reaction of charge carriers [35, 53, 54]. Apparently, the generation, separation, and utilization of charge carriers play a decisive role, as any inefficiency in carrier dynamics will directly translate into energy losses and reduced catalytic activity or selectivity. To understand the carrier dynamics that govern the efficiency and activity of PEC CO2RR, it is essential to examine the two fundamental components that define the process: the catalytic reaction mechanism of CO2 reduction which determines the thermodynamic and kinetic feasibility of various reaction pathways, and the semiconductor properties of the photocathode which directly affect the availability of photogenerated electrons for surface reactions. These two aspects are intricately coupled at the semiconductor/electrolyte interface, where the local energetics, band alignment, and adsorption behavior co‐determine the fate of intermediates and the overall catalytic outcome. Therefore, this section first outlines the thermodynamic landscape of PEC CO2RR, followed by a discussion of the semiconductor physics relevant to photocathodes, laying the foundation for the subsequent analysis of carrier dynamics and material design strategies.

Thermodynamics and Pathway Diversity in Cu‐based Photocathodes for CO2RR

The overall PEC CO2RR process comprises simultaneous cathodic and anodic reactions occurring in two separate compartments separated by a membrane. In most cases of PEC CO2RR, the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) occurs at the anode. Under different conditions, the cathodic CO2RR on Cu‐based photocathode may proceed through several multiple‐electron‐transfer processes xCO2(g) + y(H+ + e−) → C x H y −2 n O2 x ‐ n + nH2O, forming various reduced products like carbon monoxide (CO), methane (CH4), formate (HCOO−), formaldehyde (CH2O), methanol (CH3OH), acetic acid (CH3COOH), ethylene (C2H4), ethanol (C2H5OH), ethane (C2H6) [55]. The similar reduction potentials of different products pose challenges in controlling the target product selectivity. Additionally, the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) usually occurs concurrently, further complicating selective conversion.

The descriptors of reaction pathways in PEC CO2RR and the competitive HER at molecular level are depicted in Figure 1a. Initially, CO2 molecule is adsorbed onto the Cu‐based photocathode surface and activated via one‐electron transfer with the thermodynamic potential of −1.90 Vvs. SHE, which is energetically demanding and thermodynamically unfavorable. Then the reaction intermediate *OCHO or *COOH (depending on which atom binds to the surface) is formed by the addition of a proton, followed by further proton‐coupled electron transfer (PCET) steps. A number of different intermediates are involved in the process, and the relative binding strength of these key intermediates and the efficiency of charge transfer are essential metrics that dictate the reaction pathways and determine the final products.

If the two oxygen atoms are bonded to the surface and the first protonation step takes place on the carbon atom, the *OCHO intermediate forms, followed by the second PCET step to form HCOOH/formate [56]. On contrary, if the carbon atom is bonded to the surface instead, one oxygen atom is protonated to form *COOH. The key intermediate *CO forms in the next step, which is not only the final intermediate for CO desorption, but also the necessary intermediate for the hydrocarbon or C2+ product pathways [57]. More specifically, on Cu‐based photocathodes, *CO can undergo various reaction steps such as *CO coupling, *CH2 dimerization, and CO insertion to produce CH4, C2H4, C2H5OH, etc [32]. The formation of C2+ products strongly depends on the reaction kinetics and binding affinity of *CO‐*CO coupling step. Based on these pathways, the general design principle of photoelectrocatalysts for CO2RR lies in optimizing binding energy for the corresponding intermediates to favor the aimed products.

Since all elementary steps in CO2RR proceed via PCET, the behavior of photogenerated electrons as charge carriers play an important role in determining both activity and selectivity. While thermodynamics defines the minimum driving force required for each pathway, the actual possibility of a given product strongly depends on whether electrons can be supplied to intermediates with appropriate energy and timing. Specifically, the real electrochemical potential accessible at the photocathode surface is determined by the energy distribution and density of photogenerated carriers, which are strongly affected by light absorption, recombination, and transport dynamics within the semiconductor, leading to the shift of the practical onset potential and different required overpotential do drive CO2RR. Beyond this shift in apparent energetics, carrier dynamics can also influence product selectivity. The competition between the multi‐electron CO2RR and the two‐electron HER is highly sensitive to the local electron density and lifetime at the surface. When carrier extraction is sluggish or recombination dominates, the remaining electrons may preferentially follow kinetically facile proton reduction, leading to suppressed CO2RR selectivity. Conversely, rapid delivery of electrons with sufficient energy can lower the energy barrier for C‐O bond cleavage or C‐C coupling, thereby favoring CO or even C2+ products. Moreover, interfacial carrier accumulation can locally modify band bending and surface energetics, altering the relative stabilization of key intermediates such as *COOH or *OCHO. Such effects can shift the effective free‐energy landscape away from its idealized thermodynamic values, making certain pathways more favorable than predicted by bulk reduction potentials alone. Therefore, the interplay between thermodynamic driving forces and carrier behavior must be considered. Even if a reaction pathway appears feasible from a purely energetic standpoint, its realization in PEC CO2RR critically depends on how efficiently carriers are generated, separated, transported, and delivered to the Cu active sites. In this sense, carrier dynamics do not simply affect the reaction rate, but redefine the practical thermodynamic accessibility and selectivity of PEC CO2RR.

Overview of Semiconductor Physics in Photocathodes of PEC CO2RR

Semiconductor photoelectrodes are indispensable for utilizing solar energy to supplement or even replace electrical energy inputs during PEC CO2RR, while the applied potential does not directly govern the electrochemical reactions [54]. Based on the band theory utilizing delocalized orbitals that overlap to form a low energy band of mainly filled orbitals (the valence band, VB) and a set of higher energy bands of mainly unoccupied orbitals (the conduction band, CB), semiconductors have significant energy spacings called the bandgap (E g) between the VB maximum (VBM, the highest energy of filled electronic state in VB) and the CB minimum (CBM, the lowest energy of unoccupied electronic state in CB). Upon illumination with photons of energy (h ν) equal to or greater than E g, photogenerated electrons are excited from VB to CB, leaving empty states in VB, which act as photogenerated holes. Then the photogenerated electrons and holes migrate independently to the surface of photocathode and anode to conduct CO2RR/HER and OER, respectively. To overcome the overpotential for CO2RR and drive the reaction with sufficient electrical potential, CBM should lie considerably more negative than the potential listed in Table 1. This requirement is typically met by using p‐type semiconductors with appropriate band alignments. Figure 1b–d depict the band diagrams of p‐type semiconductors prior to and after soaking in the electrolyte. Upon contact with the aqueous electrolyte containing redox couples (A/A−) with an effective Fermi level of charge carriers in solution (EA/A−), the equilibration between the Fermi level of the semiconductor (E F) and EA/A− induces downward band bending at the interface (direction: semiconductor → electrolyte), forming a depletion region. Notably, the “bent” band represents the electrostatic attraction that favors movement of the minority carriers (electrons for p‐type semiconductor) toward the surface [58]. Upon illumination, the system is perturbed away from the equilibrium. Both electrons and holes accumulate at and near the surface since they are generated faster than they can be removed by charge‐transfer into solution or electrostatic drift into the bulk of the semiconductor. The new net electrons replenish the depletion region and unbending the bands. At steady state, a single E F is no longer sufficient since it is only meaningful at equilibrium, while two quasi‐Fermi levels, E F,n and E F,p, can be ascribed to electrons and holes, respectively. For a p‐type photocathode, CO2RR can be thermodynamically driven when E F,n is located at a sufficiently negative electrochemical potential relative to the relevant CO2RR redox couple, enabling interfacial electron transfer from the photocathode to adsorbed CO2 or reaction intermediates.

As described above, upon photoexcitation, semiconductors generate EHPs as electrons are excited from the VB to the CB, leaving behind holes in the VB. These photogenerated carriers are initially created near the surface, within the absorption depth of incident photons. Once generated, electrons and holes begin to migrate within the semiconductor under the influence of concentration gradients and the built‐in field. In p‐type photocathodes, electrons are driven toward the semiconductor/electrolyte interface by the downward bending to drive CO2RR, while holes drift in the opposite direction into the bulk. During the migration, the spatial separation of electrons and holes is a prerequisite for suppressing recombination and ensuring charge availability at reactive interfaces. However, both bulk and surface recombination can occur. The probability of recombination strongly depends on the carrier lifetime and mobility, both of which are material‐dependent parameters. If electrons successfully reach the photocathode surface before recombination, they may undergo interfacial charge extraction by transferring to adsorbed *CO2 or electrolyte species to initiate reduction reactions. Apparently, the semiconductor physics of photocathodes provides the fundamental framework for understanding how light absorption and electronic structure translate into photovoltage and carrier availability. However, these intrinsic properties must be viewed through the lens of carrier dynamics. The E g not only dictates the spectral range of light harvesting but also defines the excess energy of carriers, which influences their driving force for surface reactions. Band alignment at heterointerfaces determines whether carriers are efficiently separated or prone to recombination. Defect states and trap densities control the carrier lifetime and mobility, thereby modulating how many electrons can actually reach the reactive sites. As a result, semiconductor parameters such as band positions, defect densities, and transport characteristics do not merely describe material properties, but also critically govern the carrier behavior that affects catalytic performance.

Charge Carrier Dynamics in Cu‐Based Photocathodes

Among various candidates for photocathodes, Cu‐based photocathodes have emerged as a leading class of materials and have achieved rapid development evolving from pure oxides to polynary oxides and Cu‐based composites, owing to their unparalleled catalytic capability toward not only C1 products (e.g., CO, HCOOH, CH4) but also C2+ products (e.g., C2H4, C2H5OH) due to the moderate binding affinity toward key reaction intermediates in Figure 1a, which enables further proton‐electron coupling and even C‐C coupling [43]. This unique behavior distinguishes Cu‐based materials from other materials. In contrast, elements like Au and Ag bind *CO too weakly, limiting products to CO, while transition metals such as Fe or Ni bind too strongly, favoring HER or catalyst poisoning. Moreover, the coexistence of multiple oxidation states and the dynamic reconstruction of Cu surface contribute to a wide spectrum of active sites geometries and electronic properties, offering further tunability for CO2RR selectivity, making Cu‐based materials fundamentally distinct from more inert or less selective alternatives such as bare Si or TiO2. However, despite their catalytic advantages, the overall performance of Cu‐based photocathodes remains far from optimal. One of the central reasons lies in the complex behavior of photogenerated carriers. The generation, separation, transport, recombination, accumulation, and extraction dynamics of carriers critically determine the local potential landscape, the population of reactive sites, and the activation of CO2 molecules and intermediates. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of carrier behavior in Cu‐based photocathodes is essential to explain performance limitations and guiding rational design. To establish a process‐oriented framework, carrier dynamics in Cu‐based photocathodes can be divided into several sequential but strongly coupled steps. First, carrier generation determines the initial population and energetic distribution of electrons and holes, and is governed by the Eg, absorption coefficient, film thickness, and incident photon flux. Second, carrier separation depends on band bending and built‐in electric fields, which determine whether photogenerated electrons can be directed toward the photocathode/electrolyte interface to participate in CO2RR. Third, carrier transport through the semiconductor bulk and across the interface is controlled by mobility, lifetime, diffusion length, crystallinity, and contact resistance. Fourth, recombination, including bulk recombination, surface recombination, and back electron transfer, compete with productive carrier utilization and directly limits photocurrent density and the incident photon‐to‐current efficiency (IPCE). Finally, interfacial extraction and accumulation determine whether electrons are delivered to adsorbed CO2/intermediates with appropriate density, energy, and timing, thereby influencing product selectivity. Apparently, carrier dynamics are not independent of material identity, but are strongly governed by the intrinsic semiconductor properties of each Cu‐based photocathode. Different Cu‐based materials exhibit distinct carrier‐generation efficiencies, transport pathways, recombination tendencies, and interfacial extraction behaviors. Therefore, before discussing carrier‐governed performance limitations, it is necessary to first summarize the major classes of Cu‐based photocathodes and their intrinsic optoelectronic characteristics. The following sections detail recent developments in Cu‐based photocathodes for PEC CO2RR, then systematically identify how carrier dynamics underlie key limitations related to activity, selectivity, and durability.

Development of Cu‐Based Photocathodes

The number of studies using photoelectrocatalysis for CO2 reduction has been increasing since 2013 [59]. Cu‐based photocathodes have undergone rapid development over the past decades, evolving from early pure Cu oxides to Cu‐based ternary oxides, as well as Cu‐based cocatalysts (Figure 2a). In a pioneering work by Ghadimkhani et al., CuO‐Cu2O electrodes were employed under visible light irradiation to convert CO2 into CH3OH [60]. Since then, various targeted designs have been proposed to harness the unique catalytic properties of Cu for the production of a broad range of CO2 reduction products. In these systems, Cu serves either as a light‐absorbing semiconductor or as active sites when integrated with other materials. To clarify it precisely, Cu‐based photocathodes discussed in this review can be organized according to the functional role of Cu in the PEC CO2RR architecture rather than by chemical composition alone. In Cu‐based semiconductor photocathodes including Cu‐based oxides and Cu‐based chalcogenides, the Cu‐containing material acts as the light absorber, carrier‐generation medium, and sometimes the catalytic surface. Therefore, carrier generation, bulk transport, recombination and interfacial extraction are all strongly governed by the intrinsic properties of the Cu‐based semiconductor. In Cu‐based cocatalyst/non‐Cu light absorber systems, carrier generation and transport mainly occur in the non‐Cu semiconductor, while Cu species serve as catalytic electron‐accepting sites that determine CO2 activation and product selectivity. In this case, Cu does not primarily determine carrier generation in the bulk semiconductor, but controls interfacial charge extraction and surface accumulation.

Cu‐Based Semiconductors

Cuprous Oxide

Cu2O is a promising p‐type semiconductor with a direct E g of 1.9–2.2 eV, enabling effective light absorption. Besides, its CBM is sufficiently negative to thermodynamically favor CO2RR. Cu2O itself has been widely reported to exhibit inherent photoelectrocatalytic activity toward CO2RR, with CO as the dominant product [70], accompanied by trace amounts of CH4, CH3OH, and other carbonaceous species [71, 72]. However, its poor product selectivity and the strong competition from HER significantly limit its overall CO2RR catalytic performance. From the viewpoint of carrier dynamics, the benchmark role of Cu2O originates from the balance between favorable carrier generation and problematic carrier utilization. Its moderate E g and CBM allow Cu2O to generate sufficient electrons with appropriate energy for surface reduction reactions. However, the practical utilization of these carriers is strongly limited by short diffusion lengths, relatively low carrier mobility in many polycrystalline films, and abundant surface trap states. As a result, a large fraction of photogenerated electrons may recombine before participating in CO2RR [73].

To address these limitations, various cocatalysts have been integrated with Cu2O to modulate the reaction pathway, enhance product selectivity, and suppress HER. For example, Xia et al. [28] modified the Cu2O/Ga2O3/TiO2 photocathode with Sn/SnOx catalysts, achieving a remarkable half‐cell solar‐to‐fuel (STF) efficiency of ∼0.31% (Figure 3a). Beyond improving efficiency, product selectivity can also be tuned. The incorporation of a Bi2Se3 layer was found to greatly promote CH3OH accumulation during PEC CO2RR [74]. Ag nanoparticles (NPs) exhibiting localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) have been used to enhance both CO (Figure 3b) [75] and acetate [76] production. In addition to metallic cocatalysts, certain inorganic modifiers have also demonstrated beneficial effects in promoting the performance of Cu2O‐based photocathodes, such as amine‐functionalized polysiloxane for formate production [77], adenine‐functionalized GO for methanol production (Figure 3c) [78], and Cu3(BTC)2 for CH4 production [66]. Consequently, Cu2O has been widely recognized and employed as a benchmark photocathode material for PEC CO2RR.

Cupric Oxide

Theoretically, CuO possesses a relatively narrow E g of 1.3–1.6 eV and exhibits an even broader photoresponse range compared to Cu2O, thus it should be capable of generating a higher photocurrent [60]. However, in terms of carrier behavior, CuO illustrates a typical case in which enhanced light absorption does not necessarily lead to improved PEC CO2RR performance. Specifically, CuO consistently suffers from poor charge‐transport properties [79], primarily due to its large effective masses of electrons and holes (0.58m 0 for Cu2O and 7.9m 0 for CuO, where m 0 is the mass of a free electron) [80, 81, 82], as well as its intrinsically low charge mobility arising from the highly localized nature of the CB formed by Cu 3d and O 2p hybridized orbitals [83]. More importantly, the CBM of CuO lies at a relatively high potential (less negative), making it less favorable for driving CO2RR compared with Cu2O, thereby further limiting its applicability as an efficient substrate. Therefore, CuO is primarily utilized in PEC CO2RR as a complementary component, typically through the construction of heterojunctions with other semiconductors, rather than as the main photoactive material. For instance, the hybrid CuO‐Cu2O composites have been synthesized by different methods to drive CO2RR into methanol [60], formate (Figure 3d) [61, 84], and acetate [85]. The photocurrent response and IPCE of g‐C3N4 have also been successfully enhanced by coupling with CuO [86].

Cu‐Based Ternary Oxides

In addition to simple binary oxides (Cu2O and CuO), complex Cu‐based ternary oxides like CuFeO2 (Figure 3e) [87, 88], CuFe2O4 [89, 90, 91], CuBi2O4 [92, 93], Cu3Nb2O8 [62], and CuGaO2 [94], etc., have attracted increasing attention due to their narrow E g and improved charge separation properties. However, similar to CuO, most of them still suffer from poor charge transport abilities, low carrier mobilities, and relatively high CBMs. For Cu‐based ternary oxides, carrier dynamics are strongly affected by their multi‐cation lattice structures. The incorporation of additional metal cations provides opportunities to tune band positions, E g, and internal electric fields, which can improve carrier separation and broaden the design space beyond binary Cu oxides. However, the same structural complexity can also introduce antisite defects, oxygen vacancies, grain boundaries, and phase inhomogeneity, which can act as recombination centers or trap states. In many cases, the carrier mobility remains insufficient, and the transport pathway becomes more tortuous because photogenerated carriers must move through chemically heterogeneous domains. Moreover, the synthesis of these multi‐cation oxides is always more complex and less controllable. As such, further materials engineering efforts are required to fully exploit their potential in PEC CO2RR systems.

Cu‐Based Chalcogenide

Apart from Cu‐based oxides discussed above, there are also plenty of p‐type Cu‐based chalcogenides (CuxE, where E represents S, Se, Te, 1 ≤ x ≤ 2) have also been employed in the area of PEC CO2RR. From a carrier‐dynamics perspective, Cu‐based chalcogenides represent systems with strong carrier generation but limited carrier energy utilization. For instance, the E g of CuxS (1.2–2.0 eV) varies depending on the stoichiometry, with a high light absorption below 550 nm [95, 96]. However, the shallow CBM reduces the energetic offset between photogenerated electrons and the CO2RR redox levels, leading to sluggish interfacial electron injection. Besides, bare Cu‐based chalcogenides generally exhibit low catalytic activity for CO2RR [27, 97]. Therefore, the applications of Cu‐based chalcogenides are always coupled with cocatalysts to improve performance in CO2RR. For example, the multifunctional CuS/GaN/Si photocathode was reported to exhibit the Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 70.2% and a partial current density of 7.07 mA·cm−2 for HCOOH production [98]. Similarly, Guo et al. [99] fabricated Cu2O/Cu2S NPs immobilized in TiO2 nanocavity arrays, which showed long‐term stability with FECO of 81%. Some ternary chalcogenides such as Cu2ZnSnS4 [97, 100], CuInS2 [101, 102], and Cu(In,Ga)Se2 [103, 104], also shows narrow E g and good catalytic activity toward CO, HCOOH, and CH3OH. Compared to Cu‐based oxides, Cu‐based chalcogenides are less commonly used in PEC CO2RR primarily due to their relatively narrower E g and shallower CBM that lack sufficient thermodynamic driving force.

Conclusively, in Cu‐based semiconductor photocathodes, Cu participates directly in the semiconductor electronic structure and therefore strongly affects photogenerated‐carrier behavior. The Cu 3d‐derived states hybridized with O 2p or chalcogen p orbitals contribute to the VB structure, Eg formation, effective mass, and defect tolerance of many Cu‐based semiconductors. As a result, Cu‐containing semiconductors often exhibit suitable visible‐light absorption and relatively favorable band alignment for cathodic CO2RR. In this category, Cu always plays a dual role: it provides both the photoactive semiconductor framework and the chemically active interface, while simultaneously introducing carrier‐transport and stability challenges.

Cu‐Based Cocatalyst Integrated With Semiconductor Substrates

Beyond the application of Cu‐based semiconductors themselves, a widely adopted strategy involves utilizing Cu species as catalytically active sites, selectively deposited or anchored onto other photoactive semiconductor substrates. In such architectures, the semiconductor serves as the primary light absorber and charge separator, while the Cu‐based species act as catalytically active sites at the interface, facilitating CO2RR [105]. This spatial separation of functions enables independent optimization of photophysical and catalytic properties, thereby improving overall system flexibility and performance. In recent years, this cocatalyst engineering approach has attracted increasing attention due to its potential to improve catalytic efficiency, expand material compatibility, and address the limitations of single‐component photocathodes.

Since the Cu‐based cocatalysts do not directly participate in light absorption, they can be rationally designed and tailored for specific target products [106]. For example, Yang et al. [107] utilized an intrinsic Au‐doped TiO2 nanotube photonic crystal as the light absorber, and integrated Cu NPs as the catalyst, achieving formic acid production with a remarkable FE of 98% (Figure 4a). Similarly, CO production was significantly enhanced by anchoring single‐atom Cu‐modified polymeric carbon nitride (CN), resulting in a 3.2‐fold increase over pristine CN (Figure 4b) [108]. More importantly, compared to other elements, Cu uniquely catalyzes CO2RR toward multi‐carbon and hydrocarbon products with wide industrial applications due to its moderate binding strength for the *CO intermediate [109, 110, 111, 112]. The cocatalyst design strategy provides a versatile platform to fully exploit this intrinsic advantage, enabling greater selectivity and diversity in product formation. For instance, by introducing Cu+ species into the TiO2 overlayer, the methanol FE reached 56.5%, over twice that of bare Cu2O. Besides, as reported by Mi et al. [67, 113], while the n+p‐Si/GaN produced only CO, the growth of Cu NPs or a CuFe binary cocatalyst on the surface triggered CH4 formation. Likewise, Lewis et al. [114] observed the evolution of C2H4 and C2H6 on n+p‐Si/Cu surface with a high loading of Cu NPs (Figure 4c). In addition, Cu‐based cocatalysts have also enabled PEC CO2RR to ethanol on a variety of photoactive substrates, such as graphene/Cu2O [64], Cu2ZnSnS4/CdS [115], and CNT/Cu2O (Figure 4d) [116]. Though considerable progress has been devoted to the design of Cu‐based cocatalyst in PEC CO2RR, this approach is still in its early stages for practical applications.

In contrast to Cu‐based semiconductor photocathodes, Cu‐based cocatalyst systems decouple light absorption from catalytic turnover. The non‐Cu semiconductor substrate is responsible for photon absorption, carrier generation, and initial charge separation, while Cu species act as electron‐accepting catalytic sites. Therefore, the following discussions on carrier dynamics primarily focus on Cu‐based semiconductor photocathodes, whereas Cu‐based cocatalyst systems discussed here are used as complementary examples to illustrate interfacial electron extraction and catalytic utilization.

Carrier‐Governed Performance Limitations in Cu‐Based Photocathodes

Despite the promising properties of Cu‐based photocathodes in PEC CO2RR, including favorable band alignment and inherent CO2RR catalytic activity, several intrinsic limitations remain. Key challenges such as low photocurrent densities, poor product selectivity, short operational lifetimes, and susceptibility to degradation continue to hinder their practical deployment. These limitations arise from a combination of electronic, structural, and chemical instabilities intrinsic to Cu‐based photocathodes. While these shortcomings are often attributed to material quality or fabrication techniques, deeper mechanistic understanding reveals that most of these limitations fundamentally originate from unfavorable carrier behavior, including insufficient energetic alignment, short lifetimes and poor transport, and inefficient interfacial transfer. Thus, this section frames the performance in terms of activity (photocurrent and onset potential), selectivity (Faradaic distribution, especially C2+ products), and stability. By mapping energy‐, time‐, and space‐domain descriptors of carrier dynamics, shortcomings in carrier generation, separation/transport, surface extraction and accumulation collectively define the operative limits of PEC CO2RR, beyond what static thermodynamics alone would suggest.

Limited Photovoltage and Light‐Harvesting Efficiency From Carrier Generation

The attainable photovoltage and light‐harvesting efficiency of Cu‐based photocathodes are fundamentally limited at the stage of photocarrier generation, which sets the upper bound for overall carrier availability. As mentioned above, the CBM should lie above (higher in energy) the redox potential of the target products (i.e., CO and H2) [133]. Meanwhile, an appropriate E g is also required to balance sufficient photovoltage with efficient solar absorption. As shown in Figure 2b, the band alignments of some common Cu‐based semiconductors are summarized. Theoretically, the minimum E g is constrained by the energy demands of CO2RR, thermodynamic losses, and overpotentials throughout the PEC system, and generally falls within the range of 1.7–2.3 eV, while the upper limit is around 3.2 eV to allow effective light harvesting, as illustrated in Figure 6a [117]. An excessively narrow E g (in some Cu‐based chalcogenides) allows the absorption of more infrared photons and thus generates a large number of charge carriers under solar illumination. However, it often results in insufficient photovoltage, as the energy difference between E F,n and CBM becomes small, limiting the driving force for the interfacial reduction reactions. In contrast, a E g that is too wide suffers from poor photon utilization. Since most of solar spectrum wavelength (λ) lies in the visible region (400–700 nm) [134, 135], wide E g semiconductors only respond to a short range even ultraviolet light (<380 nm), which accounts for less than 5% of the total solar flux. As a result, although the carriers generated are highly energetic, their overall quantity is severely limited. This leads to low current densities and inefficient solar‐to‐fuel conversion, especially in pristine CuO and certain Cu‐based ternary oxides.

In addition to energy alignment, the quality of photogenerated carriers is governed by both the spectral overlap between the solar spectrum and the material's absorption profile, as well as the material's absorption coefficient (α) and thickness (d) [136]. Many Cu‐based semiconductors possess relatively high absorption coefficients [137, 138, 139], enabling strong light absorption in thin films for photocathode. However, this also means that EHPs are generated very close to the surface, within the minority carrier diffusion length. Consequently, the fate of these carriers is highly sensitive to surface and near‐surface properties, including defect density, trap states, and band bending. If photogenerated carriers recombine before reaching the interface, or fail to separate due to a weak internal electric field, their catalytic contribution is lost. Therefore, the carrier generation is inherently coupled to the subsequent processes of transport and interfacial dynamics.

Low Photocurrent Density and Inefficient Charge Utilization due to Recombination and Transport Losses

The photocurrent density of Cu‐based photocathodes is not only determined by the quantity of photogenerated carriers but also strongly constrained by their ability to survive and reach the reactive interface. Following photogeneration, the transport, lifetime, and utilization of charge carriers are critically influenced by their recombination and mobility within the Cu‐based semiconductor matrix. For most Cu‐based photocathodes, particularly those composed of polycrystalline oxides or ternary compounds the recombination of photogenerated EHPs remains one of the most prominent loss pathways, which extinguishes the generated charge carriers by releasing the absorbed energy, thereby drastically shortening their lifetimes and suppressing effective charge utilization for catalysis [144, 145, 146]. This recombination occurs through multiple pathways, including bulk recombination within the semiconductor and surface/interface recombination at the semiconductor/electrolyte interface as illustrated in Figure 6b. Bulk recombination is always induced by unavoidable intrinsic structural defects such as Cu or O vacancies [147, 148], grain boundaries [149, 150], or disordered regions [151, 152], which act as recombination centers [153] and significantly limit the diffusion length of charge carriers, thereby limiting the probability of carriers reaching the semiconductor/electrolyte interface.

Surface or interfacial recombination is also a critical factor. Specifically, carrier transfer at the interface is strongly influenced by semiconductor surface states, which are localized electronic states that arise exclusively at the surface of a semiconductor. In particular, those surface states originate from the disruption of the crystal lattice where dangling bonds (unsaturated atomic orbitals) interact with species in the electrolyte, and are also located within E g [154, 155]. In such cases, photogenerated EHPs are likely to be trapped at surface states and recombine before participating in interfacial charge transfer [156]. More importantly, these trap sites can also reduce the population of active carriers available for catalytic reactions. Additionally, the built‐in electric fields in the Cu‐based photocathodes are often insufficient to overcome the sluggish charge transport and limited charge extraction resulted from these defects, especially in the absence of engineered band structures. At such defective interfaces, photogenerated carriers are more likely to be trapped and annihilated before participating in CO2RR. As a result, both light absorption efficiency and catalytic selectivity are significantly impaired. Besides, if the extraction of photogenerated electrons is hindered, the back‐transfer recombination with photogenerated holes that are not effectively swept away by the internal field also occurs. This phenomenon is frequently observed under insufficient band bending, especially when the E fb is poorly aligned with the redox level of the electrolyte.

Another critical but always underappreciated factor is the mobility of charge carriers (µ), which determines how quickly electrons and holes travel under an electric field. For p‐type Cu‐based semiconductors such as Cu2O, the mobility of Cu2O synthesized from different methods varies over a wide range of 0.004 to 100 cm2·V−1·s−1 [157], leading to unbalanced transport and preferential accumulation near the illuminated surface [158, 159]. Higher mobility means longer diffusion lengths, allowing carriers more time to reach reactive interfaces before recombination and lower ohmic losses. Besides, low mobility materials are always more susceptible to accumulation of charge at interfaces or grain boundaries, which increases surface recombination and reduces catalytic turnover. Recent advances using single‐crystal Cu2O thin films with (111) orientation have significantly improved electron mobility by an order of magnitude, along with enhanced photocurrent densities (−8.5 mA·cm−2 at 0 Vvs. RHE) and long‐term stability over 120 h [160]. Therefore, severe recombination and poor carrier mobility do not merely represent microscopic material flaws but directly manifest as suppressed photocurrent density, low IPCE, and diminished FE for CO2RR. Engineering strategies aimed at enhancing crystallinity, reducing defects, and inducing preferential orientation present effective pathways are thus essential to enhance carrier mobility and, in turn, improve PEC CO2RR performance.

Inefficient Interfacial Reaction Charge Injection and Deteriorated Product Selectivity From Poor Electron Extraction

Beyond recombination and bulk transport losses, the low ability to efficiently extract photogenerated electrons at the semiconductor/electrolyte interface represents another critical performance bottleneck in Cu‐based photocathodes (Figure 6c). Even when photogenerated electrons are successfully separated, the failure of electrons to reach and react at the catalytic surface can suppress photocurrent output, reduce CO2RR activity and selectivity, and trigger deleterious side reactions. The injection efficiency (η inj) directly governs the fraction of photogenerated electrons that participate in productive catalysis. A low η inj manifests as reduced photocurrent output and a lower FE for CO2RR. Carrier behavior at the interface is governed by a complex interplay of multiple factors. Surface states can serve not only as loci for EHPs recombination but also as trapping sites for charge carriers, delaying or even preventing their participation in interfacial redox reactions. Besides, photogenerated electrons may also trigger undesired side reactions. One prominent process is HER due to its lower equilibrium potential, which not only consumes valuable charge carriers but also reduces the overall FE for CO2RR. Furthermore, the extracted electrons may also induce the oxidation states or phase structure of the Cu‐based photocathode itself, which will be further discussed in detail in Section 3.2.4.

In addition, band alignment between the semiconductor and the electrolyte redox potential plays a decisive role in electron extraction. Energetically, when the CBM is insufficiently negative relative to the CO2RR redox couples, or when the E fb does not allow for strong band bending, the driving force for electron injection into the electrolyte is weak. Such conditions also open side channels for HER, which has a lower equilibrium potential and is kinetically more favorable, leading to the expense of CO2RR selectivity. Moreover, electron accumulation occurs at the surface under illumination due to low electron utilization efficiency, which not only limits the rate of productive CO2RR but can also lead to competing reactions or induce local structural transformations of the semiconductor. Moreover, interfacial electric fields, which are critical to driving electrons toward the reaction sites, can be severely weakened in defective or poorly engineered Cu‐based photocathodes [69]. The lack of sufficient band bending fails to maintain the space charge region necessary to support efficient charge separation and extraction. This is particularly problematic in unmodified Cu‐based materials where native surface oxidation, residual stress, or passivation layer disruption may adversely affect interfacial energetics.

Inappropriate Carrier‐Electrolyte Coupling at the Photocathode/Electrolyte Interface

Although carrier dynamics are often discussed from the perspective of semiconductor properties, the local environment also plays an essential role in determining the behavior of photogenerated carriers at the photocathode/electrolyte interface. In PEC CO2RR, the electrolyte is not only an ionic conductor, but also a dynamic medium that regulates interfacial electric fields [163]. Therefore, the coupling between carriers and the local reaction environment should also be considered during carrier extraction process. First of all, the electrolyte affects the interfacial electric field and band bending through the structure of electrical double layer. Upon contact between semiconductor and electrolyte, the distribution of ions in the Helmholtz layer and diffuse layer contributes to the local potential drop across the interface, which is influenced by electrolyte ionic strength, specific adsorption, and surface charge compensation [164]. As a result, the same semiconductor can exhibit different interfacial electron densities, surface recombination rates, and injection efficiencies in different electrolytes. Local pH and buffer capacity are also critical. During CO2RR and HER, proton consumption near the photocathode can increase the local pH, shifting the CO2/HCO3 –/CO3 2– equilibrium, and decreasing the local concentration of dissolved CO2. Zanoni et al. obtained different pH‐dependent products and CO2 conversion efficiency when conducting PEC CO2RR in a buffer solution of 0.1 M Na2CO3/NaHCO3 with different pH value range of 8–11 [59]. With increasing pH, the conversion efficiency and selectivity were found to be lower and higher, respectively. This is because a locally alkaline environment can affect PCET steps depending on the surface chemistry. The identity and concentration of cations provide another level of electrolyte control [165]. Alkali‐metal cations such as K+ and Cs+ can accumulate near negatively polarized photocathode surfaces and modify the local electric field. These cations can stabilize polar or charged intermediates, thereby influencing the kinetics of interfacial electron transfer and the competition between CO2RR and HER [166]. In PEC CO2RR systems, such effects are coupled with carrier accumulation. A higher local electron density can strengthen cation accumulation in the double layer, while cation‐induced field modulation can in turn affect electron extraction and intermediate stabilization. Therefore, cation effects should be viewed as part of the carrier‐extraction environment, not only as classical electrocatalytic electrolyte effects.

Overall, carrier‐electrolyte coupling can be understood as a feedback loop. Photogenerated carriers change the local reaction environment by consuming CO2/protons and modifying interfacial charge distribution. Conversely, the electrolyte environment controls how efficiently carriers are extracted by regulating double‐layer fields and surface blocking. Therefore, Φ inj, charge‐transfer resistance (R ct), and product selectivity are all strongly affected by electrolyte composition and mass transport. Inappropriate carrier‐electrolyte coupling can directly translate into activity, selectivity, and stability losses. Poor ionic compensation or unfavorable double‐layer fields promote surface electron accumulation, recombination, and delayed electron injection, leading to lower photocurrent density and more negative onset potentials. Insufficient CO2 mass transport or weak bicarbonate buffering causes local CO2 depletion and pH elevation, diverting photogenerated electrons toward HER or Cu self‐reduction. Meanwhile, carbonate accumulation and specific ion adsorption can block Cu active sites and modify the adsorption of intermediates, thereby altering product distribution. These effects are especially detrimental to C2+ formation, which requires sustained *CO coverage and adjacent accessible Cu sites.

Loss of Operational Stability From Carrier‐Induced Photocorrosion and Structural Degradation

In addition to the above issues related to photoresponse and catalytic activity, Cu‐based photocathodes also face stability challenges during PEC CO2RR operation (Figure 6d). Among the most widely observed degradation pathways is photocorrosion, especially in oxide materials such as Cu2O [167, 168]. The stability of the semiconductor under PEC conditions is primarily determined by the alignment of its self‐oxidation potential (Φ ox) and self‐reduction potential (Φ red) relative to the equilibrium potential of CO2RR/HER/OER (ΦA/A−), respectively [169, 170]. Thermodynamically, when Φ red is located between the CBM and ΦA/A−, the stability of the semiconductor is determined by the branching ratio (relative competition between CO2RR/HER and self‐reduction) [27]. Similarly, there is also a competitive reaction between the self‐oxidation and OER [171]. Based on this, Cu‐based photocathodes are always thermodynamically unstable during PEC operation since the corresponding Φ ox are above ΦO2/H2O and Φ red are below ΦCO2 and ΦH2O/H2, respectively. The degradation severely alters the semiconductor's original properties and disrupts the balance between light absorption and catalytic performance. Beyond intrinsic photocorrosion, electrochemical degradation may arise under inappropriate applied potentials. When the operating potential is excessively negative, Cu species can be over reduced, which not only alters the material phase and conductivity but also introduces structural inhomogeneity, especially at defect‐rich regions or grain boundaries. Mechanical degradation also plays a non‐negligible role. The generation of gas bubbles, volumetric changes during redox cycling, the presence of soft or poorly anchored interfacial layers due to the limitation in photocathode fabrication methods [43], and local photothermal effect caused by concentrated light absorption, may induce thermal expansion, interfacial stress, or even microcrack formation, further exacerbating mechanical delamination and detachment from the substrate over prolonged operation. Last, chemical poisoning of the Cu‐based photocathode surface may occur. During CO2RR, accumulation of carbonate species, or irreversible adsorption of intermediate species may hinder charge transfer, block active sites and lead to performance loss [32, 172]. These diverse degradation pathways underscore the necessity of comprehensive interfacial and structural engineering strategies to stabilize Cu‐based photocathodes.

Suppressed C2+ Products Selectivity due to Timescale Mismatch of Carrier Supply and *CO Coverage

A defining advantage of Cu‐based catalysts lies in their unique ability to promote C‐C coupling and form C2+ products. However, despite this intrinsic catalytic capability, Cu‐based photocathodes in PEC CO2RR typically exhibit much lower Faradaic efficiencies toward C2+ species than their electrocatalytic counterparts. This gap cannot be fully rationalized by surface energetics or adsorption thermodynamics alone. Instead, it reflects a fundamental distinction between electrochemical and photoelectrochemical systems: the temporal nature of electron supply. Recent PEC studies have shown that the reaction selectivity can be drastically altered by modifying the charge‐transport layers in the photocathode architecture. For example, replacing the electron transport layer (ETL) in p‐Si/ETL/Cu photocathodes (e.g., TiO2 vs. TaOx) can switch the system from CO2RR‐dominant behavior to nearly exclusive HER under otherwise similar conditions, highlighting that interfacial carrier extraction critically governs the reaction pathway rather than surface energetics alone [173]. Similarly, MIS‐type p‐Si/TiO2/Cu photocathodes demonstrated that optimizing interfacial transport and device configuration can markedly enhance ethylene formation, even when the Cu catalytic layer is unchanged [174]. Recent plasmon‐assisted CO2RR studies further support the importance of dynamic electron delivery in C2+ formation [175]. These observations indicate that in PEC systems, the ability of Cu to access C2+ pathways is constrained not only by catalytic thermodynamics, but also by how efficiently and rapidly photogenerated electrons are delivered to the surface. A key performance limitation arises from the mismatch between the temporal dynamics of carrier supply and the interfacial lifetime of key intermediates, particularly *CO. It has been widely accepted that *CO‐*CO coupling occurs during C2+ formation, and the surface *CO coverage is a decisive parameter [176, 177, 178, 179]. During electrochemical CO2 reduction, electrons are supplied directly from an external circuit into the conductive catalyst with effectively instantaneous and continuous availability. As a result, no explicit electron transport time needs to be considered, and the reaction kinetics are mainly governed by intermediate lifetimes, adsorption energetics, and mass transport of reactants. In contrast, in PEC, electrons must first be photogenerated inside a semiconductor and then migrate through the bulk and across the space‐charge region before reaching the surface [180]. while the mobility in the semiconductor photocathode is also significantly limited compared with conductor electrocathode. This introduces a finite and distributed electron arrival time (τ arr), which is determined by the photogeneration rate, carrier mobility, recombination probability, and interfacial transport kinetics. The probability of *CO‐*CO coupling depends strongly on the surface coverage of CO and on the residence time of this intermediate (τ *CO). In electrochemical systems, continuous electron supply readily sustains high CO coverage, enabling coupling events. In PEC systems, however, electrons arrive at the surface in a temporally dispersed manner. In order for *CO‐*CO coupling to occur, multiple photogenerated electrons must be delivered within a narrow time window to stabilize and accumulate sufficient *CO coverage at the catalytic surface. In other words, it is reasonable to assume that this energetic requirement must be matched with the average τ arr and τ *CO. When τ arr becomes comparable to or longer than τ *CO, the surface fails to maintain a sufficiently high and synchronized population of *CO. Under such conditions, *CO intermediates are more likely to undergo hydrogenation, desorption, or surface rearrangement before a second *CO can be stabilized, causing the reaction pathway to collapse toward CO formation or the kinetically facile HER. This temporal mismatch provides a mechanistic explanation for the long‐standing difficulty in achieving high C2+ selectivity in PEC CO2RR, even on intrinsically Cu‐active surfaces. Recent demonstrations of highly selective PEC CO2RR under optimized device architectures further support this view, showing that when carrier transport and extraction are sufficiently enhanced, PEC systems can approach the selectivity of purely electrochemical counterparts [181]. It indicates that C2+ formation in PEC systems is not limited solely by catalytic energetics, but by the synchronization between carrier delivery and intermediate lifetime. In other words, PEC CO2RR introduces a time‐domain constraint that is absent in purely electrochemical systems. From this perspective, carrier dynamics redefine the selectivity landscape of PEC CO2RR. Strategies that merely optimize adsorption energies or surface composition may be insufficient if the cadence of carrier supply remains mismatched with reaction timescales.

Heterojunction Engineering

Constructing heterojunctions represents a widely adopted and effective strategy to regulate carrier behavior in PEC CO2RR systems [43]. By coupling two or more semiconductors with staggered band structures, heterojunctions enable efficient separation and directional transport of photogenerated charge carriers, thereby suppressing recombination and enhancing surface reaction kinetics [185, 186, 187]. In Cu‐based systems, common heterojunction configurations include type‐II band alignment, Z‐scheme architectures, and Schottky junctions with conductive substrates or cocatalysts. Depending on the relative alignment of CBs and VBs, heterojunctions for photocathodes in PEC CO2RR system are typically categorized into Type‐II heterojunction, Z‐scheme heterojunction, and Schottky junction structures as illustrated in Figure 8.

Different types of heterojunctions used for Cu‐based photocathodes: (a) Type‐II heterojunction; (b) direct Z‐scheme heterojunction; (c) indirect Z‐scheme heterojunction; (d) Schottky heterojunction.

In type‐II heterojunctions, both the CBM and VBM of the p‐type Cu‐based semiconductor lie at lower energy levels than those of the other semiconductor. Upon illumination, both semiconductors generate EHPs. Due to the band offsets, photogenerated electrons transfer from the CBM of the secondary semiconductor to that of Cu‐based semiconductor to drive CO2RR, while holes migrate in the opposite direction, effectively promoting spatial separation of charge carriers and suppressing recombination [188]. Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of type‐II heterojunctions in enhancing PEC CO2RR performance. In this case, both p‐p junction and n‐p junction can be utilized. For instance, Zhu et al. [189] reported that a p‐p Fe2O3/Cu2O photocathode effectively extended charge carrier lifetimes and boosted a photocurrent density of 3 mA·cm−2 with stability exceeding 5.6 h for PEC CO2RR (Figure 9a). Similarly, a Cu3(BTC)2/Cu2O p‐p junction has been developed, resulting in a 0.83% solar‐to‐CO (STC) efficiency [66]. To enhance CH3OH generation, Pan et al. [190] combined n‐type CeO2 NPs with p‐type CuO NPs, increasing the carrier concentration by ∼108 times.

In Z‐scheme heterojunctions, the VBM of Cu‐based semiconductor should be higher than the CBM of the secondary semiconductor to permit electron transfer from the CBM of the secondary semiconductor to the VBM of the Cu‐based semiconductor [191]. The direct Z‐scheme heterojunction facilitates directional charge recombination at the interface. In indirect Z‐scheme heterojunctions, external energy carrier transfer occurs via the solid mediators (e.g., Au [192], carbon layers [193], and graphene [194]), enabling electrons to recombine with holes at the contact surface. Based on this rationale, Bi et al. [195] engineered a Z‐scheme heterojunction of ZnIn2S4/Cu2O, successfully suppressing the recombination of EHPs and consequently enhancing the efficiency of PEC CO2RR (Figure 9b). Moreover, a three‐dimensional BiVO4/carbon‐coated Cu2O Z‐scheme heterojunction has been constructed by Kim et al. [196], in which the interfacial recombination of photogenerated carriers effectively prolonged carrier lifetime. As a result, enhanced CO2‐to‐CO selectivity and improved photocurrent output were achieved. Similarly, Wang et al. [197] employed Pt‐bridged Z‐scheme heterojunctions to achieve directional charge flow, enabling cooperation of CO2RR and OER (Figure 9c). Time‐resolved photoluminescence spectra further indicated that the Au mediate could improve interfacial charge transfer in the TiO2/Au/Cu2O heterojunction [198].

Another important class involves Schottky junctions, where metallic or conductive components form rectifying contacts with Cu‐based semiconductors [199]. These junctions induce a built‐in electric field that promotes electron transfer from the semiconductor to the conductive phase while blocking hole backflow, thus acting as electron sinks and suppressing recombination. Furthermore, such heterojunctions often provide additional active sites or improved conductivity, leading to synergistic improvements in PEC performance. For example, Xu et al. [75] integrated Ag NPs onto a Mo/Cu2O photocathode to construct a Schottky junction, significantly improving the CO production rate. In addition to pure metal, Hou et al. [200] achieved a high‐efficiency and stable PEC CO2RR to methanol using the 3D Au‐Cu/graphene/Cu2O system (Figure 9d).

Overall, the incorporation of heterojunctions not only addresses key performance limitations of Cu‐based photocathodes but also introduces new opportunities for tailoring interfacial energetics, promoting directional carrier transport, and enhancing operational durability. Heterojunction engineering improves Cu‐based photocathodes through three common carrier‐dynamic mechanisms: strengthening the built‐in electric fields, spatially separating electrons and holes, and providing directional transport pathways toward catalytic interfaces. Type‐II heterojunctions are effective for suppressing recombination by driving electrons and holes into different components, but they may sacrifice redox driving force if electrons accumulate in a less negative conduction band. Z‐scheme heterojunctions can preserve strong redox ability by retaining high‐energy electrons in the reduction component, but their performance depends critically on selective interfacial recombination between low‐energy electrons and holes. Schottky junctions facilitate electron extraction into conductive phases and suppress back recombination, yet excessive metal coverage or poorly controlled contact can block light absorption or active sites. Therefore, the key design principle is not simply to construct a band offset, but to ensure that the heterointerface simultaneously provides favorable energetic alignment, low‐resistance carrier transfer, suppressed interfacial recombination, and preserved catalytic accessibility.

Doping and Defect Engineering

Doping is an effective strategy to tailor the electronic structure, carrier behavior, and surface properties of Cu‐based semiconductors, thereby enhancing their performance in PEC CO2RR [201, 202, 203]. In particular, n‐type doping with elements such as Ga3+ [204] and Mg2+ [205, 206] has been reported to increase the concentration of free carriers and improve conductivity, leading to enhanced charge transport and reduced bulk recombination (Figure 10a). Meanwhile, p‐type doping can adjust the position of E F and improve interfacial charge transfer efficiency. For example, Guo et al. [30] and Yu et al. [207] demonstrated that Cl doping in Cu2O not only stabilized the photoelectrode under PEC conditions but also facilitated selective CH4 evolution due to enhanced electron mobility and suppressed charge recombination (Figure 10b). Moreover, doping can shift band edge positions, enabling fine‐tuning of the thermodynamic driving force for CO2RR. For instance, Sun et al. [208] investigated the doping effects of various alkali and transition metal atoms and observed an upward shift of the VBM in Cu2ZnGeS4, while Fe and Cr dopants effectively suppressed HER activity (Figure 10c). In summary, rational doping strategies provide a versatile toolbox for optimizing the optoelectronic and catalytic properties of Cu‐based photocathodes, offering promising opportunities to address their intrinsic limitations in PEC CO2RR systems.

Aside from dopant‐induced defects, defect engineering itself has emerged as a powerful approach to modulate the physicochemical properties of Cu‐based photocathodes. Depending on their dimensionality, defects can be classified as point defects, dislocations, grain boundaries, or bulk/volume defects [211]. Unlike unintentional intrinsic native defects, which typically serve as recombination centers and deteriorate performance, deliberately introduced or well‐controlled defects can generate beneficial states to facilitate charge separation or promote surface catalysis. The roles of defects can be broadly categorized as either geometric effects providing direct adsorption and activation sites [212, 213], or electronic effects not only altering adsorption energies [214, 215, 216] but also extending the light absorption range [217, 218, 219]. As discussed in Section 3.2, some defects may act as recombination centers, thus, effective defect engineering should aim to maintain a high surface‐to‐bulk defect ratio and introduce shallow‐level trap states [220, 221]. Sequeda et al. [209] demonstrated that Cu2O with controlled Cu vacancy exhibited higher charge carrier density and photocurrent than pristine Cu2O (Figure 10d). Beyond tuning defect density and adsorption sites, recent work has shown that defect engineering can directly regulate carrier mobility. Wang et al. demonstrated that metal‐vacancy management in semiconductor oxides reduces the effective mass of holes and markedly enhances hole mobility [222]. In addition to cation vacancies, as shown in Figure 10e, Kan et al. [210] constructed a p‐Si/n‐ZnOV/p‐CuxO heterojunction, where the shallow ZnOV defect level allowed electrons to escape and reach the CuxO for selective PEC CO2RR to C2H5OH under low bias. An anion vacancy was also been introduced successfully through heat treatment strategy in a Cu2ZnSnS4/CdS photocathode, achieving simultaneous optimization of surface S vacancy engineering and interface charge transfer (Figure 10f) [115]. Similar to doping, defect engineering provides a flexible and scalable route to modulate both the electronic structure and surface reactivity of Cu‐based photocathodes for PEC CO2RR.

Taking together, doping and defect engineering regulate carrier dynamics by modifying both the electronic structure and the defect landscape of Cu‐based photocathodes. Beneficial dopants can increase carrier density, shift band edges, enhance conductivity, and improve interfacial charge transfer. Similarly, well‐controlled shallow defects or surface‐enriched vacancies can provide additional adsorption/activation sites and promote carrier separation. However, these strategies involve an inherent trade‐off. While shallow states and moderate defect concentrations can facilitate carrier extraction or catalytic activation, excessive defects or deep trap states can act as recombination centers, shorten carrier lifetimes, and accelerate photocorrosion. Therefore, the design principle for doping and defect engineering is to maximize electronically beneficial shallow states and surface‐active defects while minimizing bulk deep‐level traps and structural disorder. Future progress requires more quantitative control over defect location, energy depth, and concentration, rather than simply increasing dopant or defect density.

Protective Layer Construction

Given the thermodynamic instability of Cu‐based semiconductors under aqueous PEC conditions, protective layers can serve as physical barriers to isolate the semiconductor from direct contact with the electrolyte, while still allowing charge transfer and light absorption. An ideal protective layer must meet several critical requirements: chemical/electrochemical stability in PEC environments, high transparency to the light and a thickness of less than 100 nm to avoid photon losses [117], excellent electrical tunneling properties, and conformal coverage to prevent local exposure of the active layer. As early as 2011, Paracchino et al. [223] introduced a TiO2 layer onto the Cu2O photocathode as the protective layer in the field of PEC water splitting for the first time. Since then, various metal oxides with classically low CB and VB, such as TiO2 [93, 139, 182], Ga2O3 [224, 225], WO3 [218], and NiOx [226, 227], which can be deposited via atomic layer deposition (ALD), sputtering, or solution processes, have been explored as protective coatings for Cu‐based photocathodes. The long‐term stability of photocathodes can be greatly enhanced due to the conformal coverage, high transparency, and strong chemical stability (Figure 11a). In Cu‐based photocathodes, these metal oxide overlayers can suppress direct electrolyte contact and photocorrosion, while buried junctions can improve photovoltage and bulk charge separation. However, these metal oxides such as TiO2 are not intrinsically a highly conductive catalytic layer, so efficient CO2RR generally requires suitable cocatalysts or defect/metal incorporation to reduce interfacial resistance and provide active sites. For example, Cu+‐incorporated TiO2 overlayers have been shown to improve both methanol selectivity and photocurrent retention by combining protection with catalytic Cu species at the surface [65]. Beyond metal oxides, carbon‐based materials like carbon layer [228], oligoaniline [229], and polyimide [230] have also emerged as effective protective layers, since their conductivity can facilitate lateral electron transport and reduce recombination. However, it is important to note that these protective layers may simultaneously block catalytically active Cu sites from direct interaction with CO2 and reaction intermediates. As a result, they may restrict the formation of C2+ products, which strongly rely on the accessibility and availability of Cu surface atoms for C‐C coupling. Furthermore, carbon layers are often less conformal than ALD oxides, and incomplete coverage may leave localized corrosion sites. Thus, some Cu‐based layers such as Cu3(BTC)2 [66, 73] and Cu(BDC‐NH2) [231] provide a third strategy by integrating protection, molecular transport, and catalytic active sites (Figure 11b). Their porous structures can maintain access for CO2 and intermediates, while Cu nodes can participate directly in CO2RR. Nevertheless, their electronic conductivity, interfacial adhesion, and long‐term chemical stability must be carefully optimized. Protective layers should therefore be understood as functional carrier‐regulated interfaces rather than just passive physical barriers. Their effectiveness is determined by a balance among three requirements: suppressing electrolyte‐induced degradation, allowing photogenerated electrons to be extracted efficiently, and preserving access to catalytic active sites.

To clarify it precisely, the protective‐layer construction can be guided by the stability‐extraction‐accessibility balance rules. First, the thickness of the protective layer should be optimized within a protection‐transport window. A discontinuous or ultrathin layer cannot fully prevent electrolyte penetration and local photocorrosion, whereas an overly thick or electronically insulating layer increases the tunneling distance and interfacial charge‐transfer resistance. For wide‐E g oxide overlayers, the interfacial electron‐transfer rate can decrease approximately exponentially with d: k ET ∝ e− κd , where κ is the tunneling decay constant [232]. Therefore, the protective layer should be sufficiently conformal to suppress pinhole‐induced corrosion, but thin or conductive enough to maintain efficient electron extraction [171, 233]. Second, the protective layer must provide a defined electron‐transport pathway. This pathway may arise from direct tunneling through an ultrathin layer, favorable CB alignment, defect‐assisted hopping, conductive carbon networks, or embedded catalytic active sites. Without such a pathway, photogenerated carriers may accumulate at the buried semiconductor/protective‐layer interface, increasing recombination and even triggering self‐reduction of Cu‐based semiconductors. Third, protective layers should be evaluated using carrier‐extraction metrics, not stability alone. An effective layer should suppress corrosion‐related charge loss and trap‐mediated recombination while maintaining fast CO2RR electron transfer. Experimentally, this requires low R ct, high Φ inj, sustained partial photocurrent density, and retained FE. If a protective layer increases R ct or decreases Φ inj, it may improve apparent durability while reducing the number of photogenerated electrons that participate in CO2RR. Finally, active sites accessibility must be preserved, and the outer surface of the protective layer should be compatible with the targeted reaction pathway. A recent study by Wu et al. further demonstrated that protective layers could reshape not only stability but also product selectivity [234]. It showed that when Au or Cu cocatalysts were deposited on TiO2‐protected Cu2O photocathode, the expected CO or C2+ pathways were strongly suppressed and HER became dominant, whereas Bi‐, In‐, and Sn‐decorated photocathodes retained high formate selectivity. This behavior was attributed to the interaction between TiO2 and the cocatalyst, which altered the adsorption energies of key intermediates. This result indicates that protective layers can be further modified with the outer cocatalyst.

Conclusively, protective layers regulate carrier dynamics in a dual manner. On one hand, they suppress direct contact between unstable Cu‐based semiconductors and the electrolyte, thereby reducing carrier‐induced self‐reduction, self‐oxidation, dissolution, and phase transformation. On the other hand, they introduce an additional interfacial barrier that photogenerated electrons must cross before participating in CO2RR. If the protective layer is too thick, poorly conductive, or energetically mismatched, it may increase charge‐transfer resistance, promote interfacial accumulation, and reduce electron injection efficiency. In addition, chemically inert protective layers may block Cu active sites and suppress C‐C coupling pathways that require direct access to Cu surface. Thus, the design of protective layers should balance three requirements: conformal chemical protection, efficient electron tunneling or transport, and preservation or reconstruction of catalytically active Cu sites. Emerging strategies such as ultrathin conductive oxides, carbon‐based protection, and catalytically active MOF‐derived or Cu‐containing protective layers may offer promising routes to couple stability enhancement with selective CO2RR.

In summary, several general design principles for optimizing Cu‐based photocathodes from the perspective of carrier dynamics can be given. First, enhanced light absorption must be accompanied by sufficient electron driving force and efficient carrier separation. Otherwise, increased carrier generation may simply intensify recombination or photocorrosion. Second, strategies that improve carrier transport should be combined with defect control to avoid introducing deep recombination centers. Third, interfacial engineering should promote directional electron extraction while minimizing back recombination and preserving Cu active‐site accessibility. Fourth, stability protection should be designed as an electronically active interface rather than as a passive barrier, because excessive insulation or unfavorable band alignment can suppress CO2RR despite improved durability. Finally, the most effective Cu‐based photocathodes are unlikely arise from a single modification strategy. Instead, integrated designs that simultaneously optimize carrier generation, separation, transport, extraction, and stability are required to achieve high activity, selectivity, and long‐term operation.

The above sensitivity analysis provides a practical criterion for identifying which carrier parameter should be prioritized in photocathode design. Before selecting a carrier‐modulation strategy, several effective carrier‐separation parameters can first be evaluated experimentally, including τ e, µ, t n, and S. Here, µ should represent the mobility of photogenerated electrons involved in transport within the actual photocathode architecture, which can be obtained from photo‐induced charge extraction by linearly increasing voltage (photo‐CELIV). The τ e should correspond to the carrier population relevant to diffusion and recombination, and can be estimated from the slow free‐carrier decay component in time‐resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) or transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS). The parameter t n can be approximate as d—W SC, where W SC is the depletion width estimated from Mott‐Schottky analysis. And S can be extracted from thickness‐dependent or surface‐passivation‐dependent TRPL fitting or from drift‐diffusion modeling. With these parameters, two practical criteria can be calculated before the modification of photocathodes:

The two criteria can be illustrated using typical values of S (1000 cm·s−1) and t n (300 nm) for Cu‐based photocathodes as a case as shown in Figure 12e,f. The first parameter, τ e,0, defines an approximate lifetime threshold under a given transport thickness and surface recombination velocity, which separates the lifetime‐limited regime from the interface‐limited regime. When τ e < τ e,0, the photocathode is strongly lifetime‐sensitive, meaning that photogenerated carriers are more likely to recombine before effective collection. In this regime, lifetime engineering through defect passivation, crystallinity improvement, or recombination suppression should be prioritized. When τ e ≥ τ e,0, further extension of carrier lifetime is expected to provide diminishing returns, indicating that the dominant limitation may shift toward carrier mobility, surface recombination, or interfacial injection. The second parameter, ητe/μ, compares the effectiveness of lifetime enhancement and mobility enhancement under the same relative degree of improvement. When ητe/μ approaches unity, performance remains highly sensitive to lifetime, and recombination suppression is expected to be effective. When ητe/μ approaches zero, the benefit of further lifetime extension becomes comparable to or weaker than mobility enhancement, suggesting that transport optimization and surface recombination suppression should be considered together. When ητe/μ < 0, prolonging lifetime alone is no longer an effective strategy, and optimization should instead focus on improving µ, reducing S, or enhancing interfacial injection efficiency. Therefore, the proposed model enables a parameter‐guided design strategy for Cu‐based photocathodes. Rather than empirically applying heterojunctions, dopants, defects, or protective layers without distinguishing the dominant bottleneck, researchers can first evaluate τ e, µ, t n, and S, then use τ e,0 and ητe/μ to identify whether the system is lifetime‐sensitive, transport‐sensitive, or surface‐sensitive. This analysis also explains why some reported systems show weak correlation between lifetime enhancement and PEC performance: once carrier survival is no longer the limiting factor, additional improvements must arise from interfacial charge extraction, catalytic utilization, or stability enhancement. This approach provides a semi‐quantitative guideline for selecting appropriate modulation strategies, although the criteria should be regarded as approximate rather than absolute because t n, and S are effective parameters that depend on film architecture, surface states, applied bias, and electrolyte environment. Heterojunction engineering is most useful when spatial carrier separation and directional transport are insufficient. Doping and defect engineering are effective when carrier density, mobility, or trap‐state distribution must be regulated. Protective layers are essential when carrier accumulation induces surface degradation and photocorrosion. Table 5 gives a detailed parameter‐guided strategy selection.

Technologies for Probing Carrier Behavior and Interface Dynamics

Understanding the behavior of photogenerated carriers in the bulk and at the semiconductor/electrolyte interfaces is central to elucidating and ultimately overcoming the performance limitations of Cu‐based photocathodes in PEC CO2RR. However, these dynamics, that iaccessed: June 2026s, generation, separation, transport, extraction, and accumulation, typically occur on ultrafast timescales, making them difficult to probe. To this end, a variety of experimental and theoretical tools have been developed, which enable researchers to extract quantitative parameters such as carrier lifetime, mobility, interfacial charge‐transfer resistance, and recombination rates, etc., thereby establishing mechanistic connections between Cu‐based photocathode properties and PEC CO2RR performance. Based on the frequency and time‐domain analysis, the degree of carrier recombination, and the transmission rate/efficiency, several characterization methods have been successfully utilized [263].

Photophysical Spectroscopic Techniques

Photophysical spectroscopic techniques are widely employed to analyze the band structure of Cu‐based photocathodes, providing critical insights into their electronic and energetic configurations. For instance, ultraviolet‐visible (UV–vis.) absorption spectroscopy and valence band X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (VB‐XPS) are commonly applied to determine E g, E F, and VBM relative to the vacuum level, enabling the construction of complete band alignment diagrams, which are essential for assessing the thermodynamic feasibility of PEC CO2RR [30, 75]. More importantly, spectroscopic techniques also serve as key tools for investigating the nature, dynamics, and energetics of photogenerated charge carriers, as well as their interaction with surface states and adsorbed intermediates in Cu‐based photocathodes. These methods enable both qualitative and quantitative characterization of charge carrier lifetimes and recombination pathways during PEC CO2RR.

Transient Absorption Spectroscopy

As a well‐known “pump‐probe” technique, TAS provides time‐resolved insight into the relaxation process of excited‐state carriers, where an ultrafast laser pulse (pump) excites the sample to generate excited states (e.g., electron transition from VB to CB), and a time‐delayed broadband probe pulse monitors the subsequent changes in light absorption. By monitoring the changes of the transient substances at a specific wavelength and establishing the relationship between the absorbance and relaxation time, TAS can directly track the evolution of photogenerated EHPs (changes in the excited state absorption) on femtosecond to nanosecond timescales, thereby revealing carrier lifetimes, separation efficiency, and recombination dynamics at intrinsic conditions or driven by external bias driven [267, 268]. TAS measurements have been successfully employed to monitor interfacial carrier dynamics in Cu‐based photocathodes. Pastor et al. [269] designed a Cu2O/RuOx heterojunction for CO2RR, which effectively suppressed recombination loss compared with bare Cu2O, proven by in situ TAS study as shown in Figure 14a. Similarly, by comparing the concentrations of the excited states CuPS (CuPS•−) and one‐electron‐reduced CuPS (*CuPS) derived from TAS, Takeda et al. [270] demonstrated that CuPS exhibited a long excited‐state life time, and the product selectivity between CO and HCOOH could be controlled by changing the Mn(4OMe) cocatalyst (Figure 14b).

Time‐Resolved Photoluminescence Spectroscopy

TRPL spectroscopy is known as transient fluorescence analysis technique, which serves as a complementary probe by monitoring radiative recombination process of photocarriers, providing in‐depth information about minority carrier (electrons in p‐type Cu‐based photocathodes) lifetime [271]. As illustrated in Figure 13, the photogenerated electrons can occupy the first/second excited singlet states and relax in CB to reach their unoccupied lowest excited states. Except for extracted electrons, most electrons transfer from excited state back to ground state to recombine with holes and emit fluorescence [272]. After switching off irradiation, the emitted fluorescence gradually decays and finally vanishes. The time window ranges from picoseconds to milliseconds and beyond. The longer the fluorescence lifetime, the longer travel distances of the EHPs, and thus the higher carrier separation efficiency [263]. TRPL measurements have also been widely used to study the carrier dynamics during PEC CO2RR [191, 273, 274]. Wang et al. [275] fabricated P and Cu dual sites on graphitic carbon nitride (P/Cu SAs@CN) as the photocathode. The constructed dual sites structure showed much higher C2H6 selectivity compared with CN, P@CN, and Cu Sas@CN, with the evolution rate of 616.6 µmol·g−1·h−1. The lifetime of electrons over P/Cu SAs@CN (7.13 ns) was remarkably longer than that of Cu SAs@CN (2.84 ns), demonstrating the highly efficient carrier transfer capability (Figure 14c). Deng et al. [276] also constructed a type II heterojunction Cu‐In‐Zn‐S/Ni metal‐organic framework (CIZS/Ni‐MOF), which exhibited an extended lifetime due to its suitable platform compared with CIZS (Figure 14d).

Photoelectrical Transient Measurements

In addition to photophysical spectroscopic techniques, photoelectrical transient measurements have also become indispensable tools for investigating the charge carrier dynamics in Cu‐based photocathodes, offering valuable time‐resolved insights beyond what steady‐state techniques can provide. By probing the temporal response of photocurrent or photovoltage following a pulsed or modulated light excitation, these methods reveal crucial information about carrier lifetime, mobility, transport efficiency, and interfacial behavior under operationally relevant conditions. Moreover, by adjusting the light intensity or bias potential, these techniques enable the quantitative evaluation of transport limitations and charge accumulation phenomena that critically affect the efficiency and stability Cu‐based photocathodes.

Time‐Domain Techniques

Transient photovoltage (TPV), transient photocurrent (TPC), and photo‐CELIV have been widely applied in PEC CO2RR systems. TPV measures the decay of the open‐circuit photovoltage after pulsed illumination, and the decay profile reflects the recombination dynamics of electrons in Cu‐based photocathodes [277]. After normalization, the electron extraction efficiency (Ae ) can be calculated as the integrated area under the curve, and the recombination time (τ) of the photogenerated EHPs can be defined as the time interval over which the photovoltage decays to 1/e of its initial value immediately after excitation [278]. For example, Zhang et al. [279] designed Cu5FeS4 quantum dots on nickel foam, which showed a stronger ability to extract the photogenerated electrons compared to Cu2−xS (Figure 15a). Besides, by comparing the TPV plots under different atmosphere, Xue et al. [280] confirmed that CO2 atmosphere promoted hot carrier separation, resulting in better plasmon‐assisted CO2RR to CO (Figure 15b).

TPC, in contrast, tracks the transient photocurrent response under short‐circuit conditions upon light on/off perturbation. The rising and falling edges of the current transient provide information on carrier extraction efficiency and charge transport dynamics [281, 282]. A sluggish rise or overshoot during TPC may indicate trap‐limited extraction, surface accumulation, or poor interface contact. For example, Jiang et al. [283] designed copper vanadate samples with various element ratios and studied the recombination process by TPC measurement, finding that a higher Cu:V ratio led to more competitive surface recombination (Figure 15c).

Photo‐CELIV measurements extend insights further by combining photogeneration and field‐assisted extraction. Upon pulsed illumination and subsequent application of a linearly increasing voltage, the extracted current transient is recorded. From this, both mobility and diffusion behavior of photocarriers can be quantified based on the voltage increase rate and transient time [284, 285]. As shown in Figure 15d, the photo‐CELIV mobility for the carriers in Ag/CuxO@rGO/Mo was found the be the highest (2.86 × 10−2 cm2·V−1·s−1) among the prepared samples, demonstrating that rGO could act as a highway for enhanced charge transfer [75]. Similarly, Bi et al. [235] found that the carrier mobility in Cu2S‐In2S3 was apparently much higher than those in Cu2S and In2S3, proving that the interfacial engineering treatment successfully shortened the distance between the heterostructures and decreased the trapping state density among the catalysts.

Frequency‐Domain Techniques

While time‐domain methods provide transient insights into carrier kinetics, frequency‐domain techniques including intensity‐modulated photocurrent spectroscopy (IMPS) and intensity‐modulated photovoltage spectroscopy (IMVS) offer complementary frequency‐resolved characterization of charge carrier dynamics in Cu‐based photocathode systems [288, 289]. Generally, the input signal contains a beam of stable background light and a beam of sinusoidally modulated light with small disturbance, while the output signal is the corresponding steady‐state photocurrent/photovoltage with modulation across a range of frequencies [290]. IMPS/IMVS can be used to determine the rate constants of charge recombination, transfer, and extraction, while the electron transport time (τ d), electron lifetime (τ n) and the carrier collection efficiency (η cc) can be calculated by τ d = 1/(2πf IMPS), τ n = 1/(2πf IMVS) and η cc = 1 − τ d/τ n, where f IMPS and f IMVS are the frequency values at the minimum in the IMPS and IMVS plot, respectively [291]. For example, the fabricated CuBi2O4/APTES sample [286] showed a higher charge transfer efficiency compared with bare CuBi2O4 based on the corresponding IMPS plots (Figure 15e). Similarly, Xia et al. [28] presented a Cu2O/Ga2O3/ TiO2 photocathode modified with Sn/SnOx catalysts, where the deposited Sn/SnOx increased the τ n from 18.0 to 41.3 µs. The surface recombination semicircle response also disappeared upon Sn deposition, further demonstrating that the catalysts can effectively suppress interfacial charge recombination (Figure 15f). Moreover, Liu et al. [287] further expanded IMPS to determine the rate constants for charge transfer (k tran) and recombination (k rec) as a function of the applied potential to study the PEC CO2RR performance of CuInGaS2, revealing the occurrence of E F pinning in a wide potential range extending about 1 V into the E g from the VBM (Figure 15g).

Scanning Probe Techniques

Scanning electrochemical techniques can also study the interfacial behavior of Cu‐based photocathodes during PEC CO2RR, offering a unique ability to visualize charge carrier dynamics from electrochemical signal like surface potential distributions. These methods bridge the gap between macroscale performance metrics and nanoscale phenomena, making them applicable for understanding structure‐activity relationships and identifying degradation pathways at the microscale.

Surface photovoltage (SPV) spectroscopy provides complementary information by detecting changes in surface potential upon illumination, probing the nature of band bending, trap state density, and the energy distribution of photoactive states, helping to clarify how interfacial electric fields and surface states affect charge separation and collection. Recently, electrochemical SPV [292], XPS‐based SPV [293], macroscopic SPV [294, 295], Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) based SPV [296, 297] have been used for Cu‐based photocathodes. Among them, KPFM is a powerful technique to measure surface potential changes and local work function variations with nanometer resolution. The local contact potential difference (CPD) between Kelvin probe and photocathode surface can be quantified by detecting the capacitive electrostatic forces. Under light irradiation, KPFM can track SPV generated by charge separation and visualize how band bending evolves over different regions of the photocathode [298]. By monitoring photovoltage decay across time or under different atmospheres, illuminated KPFM‐SPV images can offer clues about the localized carrier separation and the built‐in potential at the surface or within the interface of photocathodes, helping to clarify how interfacial electric fields and surface states affect charge separation and extraction [299, 300]. By detecting SPV images, Chen et al. [297] demonstrated that the directional charge separation in Cu2O is driven by charge mobility differences and asymmetric light illumination, while the controllable charge separation via external light manipulation can be used to initiate a spatially controlled asymmetric cocatalyst assembly (Figure 16a). Similarly, Liao et al. [301] used KPFM‐SPV to verify that the generation of electrons can be greatly enhanced by the cascade excitation of CuInS2, thus promoting the following CO2 reduction. Moreover, time‐resolved photoemission electron microscopy (TR‐PEEM) measurement was performed for the first time by Chen et al. [302] on Cu2O to monitor the charge transfer processes on the femtosecond to second time‐scale as shown in Figure 16b. Due to the high sensitivity and spatial resolution of this method, it can directly observe the carrier separation process under illumination, making it a powerful tool to visualize and guide the carrier separation process, and provide experimental evidence for some speculated mechanisms.

Theoretical and Computational Methods

Beyond experimental techniques described above, theoretical modeling and first‐principles calculations also provide theoretical mechanistic insights into PEC CO2RR processes at the atomic and electronic levels, offering predictive capabilities for material design and interfacial optimization. Fundamentally, density functional theory (DFT) calculation has been widely used to study the key electronic properties of Cu‐based photocathode such as band structure, density of states, work function, charge density distribution, and absorption energies of intermediates [305, 306]. These parameters are crucial for evaluating the driving force for charge separation, the alignment of CB and VB with redox potentials, and the binding strength of reaction intermediates that influence product selectivity. It should be noted that DFT is predominantly used to calculate the electronic structure of systems in their ground state, which does not account for actual photo‐excitation conditions. As a representative, time‐dependent DFT (TDDFT) was proposed in 1984 [307], which can simulate how electrons in a material respond, transitioning from ground to excited states, through applying an external time‐dependent perturbation term representing a photon field. Recently, by using real time TDDFT calculation, the distribution and relaxation process of photogenerated carriers at different energy levels have been analyzed [51, 308, 309, 310]. Zhang et al. [303] studied the product selectivity toward CH4 production by LSPR under illumination. It was found that in photocatalytic reactions, hot electrons selectivity transfer to the anti‐bonding orbitals of CHO intermediates to weaken the chemical bonds and drive the reaction on a charge‐state reaction (Figure 16c). By simulating the charge trapping and recombination dynamics, Cheng et al. [304] found that control over charge carrier dynamics in the Cu/TiO2 could be achieved by spin selection because the second trap state responsible for the majority of carrier losses appeared only in one spin channel, thereby advancing understanding of excited‐state dynamics in photocatalytic and photovoltaic systems, and suggested that spin selectivity can be used to design better single‐atom photoelectrocatalysts (Figure 16d).

Outlook and Future Perspectives

In this review, we systematically examined the photogenerated carrier dynamics within the bulk and at the semiconductor/electrolyte interfaces, highlighting their pivotal role in determining light‐harvesting efficiency, charge separation, and catalytic selectivity of Cu‐based photocathodes in PEC CO2RR. However, despite the promising advances, several challenges remain before efficient and stable Cu‐based photocathodes for PEC CO2RR can be realized for practical applications.

First, the relationship between nanoscale carrier‐dynamics and catalytic function must be more clearly established. While considerable progress has been made to probe the lifetime and extraction behavior of photogenerated carriers, capturing the real‐time evolution of carrier processes especially the sequential steps from carrier separation to the carrier transport with intermediate formation demands more studies. How local carrier accumulation, extraction barriers, surface states, and intermediate formation are coupled under operating conditions are important to be determined. Future research should therefore focus on correlating spatially resolved and time‐resolved carrier information with catalytic selectivity and stability. Rather than simply identifying longer carrier lifetimes or higher charge densities, the key goal should be to determine where carriers accumulate, how long they remain available, and whether they are consumed by CO2RR, HER, or degradation pathways.

Second, rational design of Cu‐based semiconductor architectures through precise modulation and prediction of carrier distribution and dynamics remains a significant challenge. Specifically, the regulation of photocarrier dynamics at the semiconductor/electrolyte interface, where charge extraction, accumulation, and catalytic conversion occur, is still insufficiently understood and controlled. Future research should aim to rationally tailor photocathode surfaces to direct carrier transport and spatial distribution, which enables more deterministic control over where and how photogenerated carriers migrate, accumulate, and participate in surface reaction, ultimately improving the efficiency of Cu‐based photocathode. Moreover, achieving high selectivity in PEC CO2RR remains elusive due to the complex nature of Cu‐based active sites. However, the interplay between charge carrier dynamics and electronic structure of active sites is still poorly understood, making it difficult to predict or engineer systems with optimal carrier transport and catalytic activity. Future research could explore adaptive Cu‐based photocathode systems in which photocarrier behavior and site structure evolve in concert. Besides, understanding how interfacial carrier behavior governs catalytic site evolution will also pave the way toward selectivity‐tailored Cu‐based photocathode in PEC CO2RR system. Simultaneously, computational screening and machine learning‐assisted design could be integrated with experimental synthesis to establish predictive correlations between material structure, carrier dynamics, and catalytic selectivity. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of how carrier dynamics govern local surface reactivity will be essential for the next generation of high‐efficiency and high‐selectivity Cu‐based photocathodes.

Third, the inherent trade‐off between CO2RR activity and photostability of the photocathode remains a persistent bottleneck. While Cu‐based photocathodes possess favorable CO2 binding characteristics and electronic structures for multi‐electron reduction pathways, their susceptibility to photocorrosion and electrochemical degradation under operational bias and illumination severely limits their durability, especially for oxide‐based Cu semiconductor such as Cu2O. Although protective layers have been widely applied to suppress photocorrosion, the fundamental mechanism underlying this process remains poorly understood. In particular, the spatial distribution and temporal dynamics of photogenerated carriers at the photocathode/electrolyte interface and their direct involvement in triggering corrosion reactions have not been systematically clarified. A deep understanding of how interfacial charge accumulation, asymmetric extraction, or local electric fields trigger redox‐induced degradation will be essential for shifting from damage control to damage prevention.

Finally, despite the progress in material design and mechanistic understanding, the overall performance of Cu‐based photocathodes for PEC CO2RR remains far from satisfactory. In particular, achieving high selectivity toward C2+ products remains elusive. This limitation arises from the insufficient photovoltage, unstable surface intermediates, and poor control over multielectron transfer pathways, which collectively restrict C‐C coupling efficiency on illuminated Cu‐based surfaces. Moreover, system‐level development tailored for PEC CO2RR has lagged significantly behind developments in electrocatalytic CO2RR. Unlike in electrocatalysis, where membrane electrode assemblies (MEA), gas‐diffusion electrodes, and flow cells have been extensively optimized to achieve industrial‐level current densities and product separation, the lack of scalable and stable PEC architectures continues to hinder practical applications. Key challenges include light attenuation in thick electrodes, unstable solid‐liquid‐gas interfaces, and incompatibility between light absorption layers and gas‐diffusion systems. Furthermore, data‐driven materials discovery empowered by machine learning and high‐throughput computation is anticipated to accelerate the identification of optimal Cu‐based architectures with tailored carrier dynamics. The success of such approaches will rely on the development of standardized databases and reliable descriptors, particularly those related to charge dynamics and degradation signatures. The synergy between artificial intelligence and experimental validation could dramatically accelerate the discovery‐to‐deployment cycle, enabling a new generation of Cu‐based photocathodes that bridge the gap between fundamental advances and practical CO2 conversion technologies.
