Microfluidic-enabled stretchable thermoelectric device array for multimodal haptic interfaces
Authors: Dong Cheng, Zhenlong Huang, Tao Chen, Hongwei Xie, Longpeng Yang, Yizhuo Wang, Junjie Ji, Jing Liu, Yan Jiang, Tailong Wu, Tianyu Yan, Mingrui Chen, Hao Li, Binbin Jiang, Taisong Pan, Min Gao, Yuan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stretchable thermoelectric devices hold significant promise for thermal haptic interfaces. However, their development has been constrained by limited cooling capacity arising from inadequate thermal dissipation, restricting operation to low-power conditions. In addition, single stimulus units fail to evoke complex multimodal haptic perceptions. This work introduces a three-dimensional manufacturing strategy with embedded microfluidic architectures, which markedly enhance thermal flux and endow the resulting thermoelectric device array (TEDA) with superior cooling performance surpassing conventional designs. Integration of the TEDA with temperature sensors and control circuitry enables a wearable closed-loop platform capable of rapid and precise skin temperature regulation. Furthermore, diverse temperature modulation strategies, including variations in range, frequency, spatial distribution, and coordinated multi-device operation, are demonstrated to stimulate subcutaneous neural networks and elicit multimodal tactile sensations such as pressure, pain, and sliding. These advances establish an effective route for fabricating high-performance wearable thermoelectric devices and elucidating the mechanistic basis of sensory illusion regulation, underscoring their significance for next-generation haptic interfaces.

Introduction

Multidimensional sensory stimulation can significantly enhance immersion in virtual reality (VR), yet current visual and auditory stimulation modalities fall short of meeting increasingly sophisticated user expectations1,2. Human skin constitutes a natural multisensory interface for haptics, leveraging its dense network of subdermal thermoreceptors and mechanoreceptors as innate biological gateways3–5. Sensory feedback in virtual interactions includes touch, pain, and thermal sensations6–8. While electrical and vibrotactile stimulation are commonly employed to simulate touch sensations, they often produce limited perceptual diversity and exhibit reduced spatial acuity9–11. A skin-integrated haptic system that integrates mechanical vibration with thermal stimulation demonstrates notable functionality, yet still exhibits limitations in heat dissipation efficiency and adaptability to complex environmental conditions12,13. Capsaicin-based stimulation has been employed to induce pain sensations but is limited by the narrow range of pain modalities it can elicit, potential adverse effects on the skin, VR-related side effects, and high device costs14,15.

Thermal stimulation is a key modality for enriching multidimensional perception and enhancing the realism of haptic feedback. Benefiting from their precise temperature control and simple structural design, thermoelectric devices (TEDs) show great promise for wearable thermal stimulation and immersive interaction16–19. Through dynamic temperature modulation, TEDs can not only reproduce thermal sensations associated with real-world physical contact but also elicit multimodal perceptual experiences, including touch and pain-related sensations. For example, rapid temperature fluctuations have been shown to evoke tactile sensations, including perceptions of pressure and postural responses, when applied to the skin20,21. Moreover, fast yet safe temperature changes can lower the threshold for thermal pain perception, enabling the simulation of non-damaging pain sensations through controlled heating and cooling22. Furthermore, when spatially alternating hot and cold stimuli are applied simultaneously, the resulting interaction produces more intense thermal and pain sensations, known as the thermal grill illusion (TGI)23. Although the concept of achieving multimodal perception through spatiotemporal temperature modulation has been previously explored, existing implementations predominantly rely on conventional rigid TEDs with poor skin conformity, which substantially limits stimulation fidelity. In addition, the use of heat sinks and fans to ensure sufficient cooling capacity leads to bulky configurations that compromise wearability24. Consequently, the precise control of perceived thermal intensity remains unachieved, and the underlying mechanisms linking spatial distribution, temperature differential, and transition rate to the strength of thermal illusions are not yet fully understood. These limitations hinder the advancement of TED-based technologies for targeted applications in VR and haptic feedback systems.

Stretchable TEDs have emerged as an ideal solution, seamlessly combining wearability with precise temperature control. However, conventional stretchable TEDs suffer from poor heat dissipation efficiency, making them susceptible to Joule heat accumulation during continuous operation25. Multiple strategies have been investigated to enhance the cooling efficiency of stretchable TEDs in recent decades, such as enhancing thermal conductivity of elastic encapsulation layers or integrating advanced heat dissipation architectures26. Although incorporating high-thermal-conductivity nanomaterials or liquid metals into elastic materials can reduce the thermal resistance between high-power thermoelectric components and the environment, the improvement is relatively limited. While integrating larger heat sinks enhances convective heat transfer, their substantial volume and mass critically compromise wearer comfort and mobility. Microfluidic systems that employ high-specific heat capacity fluids in conjunction with miniaturized channel architectures enable efficient operation within compact device footprints, rendering them particularly well suited for wearable applications27,28.

To address the shortcomings of current research, this study presents a 3D integrated fabrication strategy that combines elastomer modification, 3D printing, and laser etching to embed microfluidic structures29. The resulting stretchable TEDs achieve seamless co-integration of microfluidics and thermoelectric units, where efficient heat dissipation markedly enhances cooling performance and enables stable operation under high-power conditions with minimal Joule heating. Under high-power operation with microfluidic cooling, the TED achieves a temperature switching rate exceeding 1.5 °C/s and a maximum temperature reduction of 6 °C. By employing advanced interconnection technology to integrate multiple TEDs into a ribbon-like array with independently addressable units, this study enables coordinated operation that broadens the range of achievable tactile sensations. Furthermore, through a combination of theoretical analysis and experimental validation, this study reveals the relationship between dynamic thermal parameters including amplitude, frequency, and spatial distribution and human tactile perception. Leveraging these mechanistic insights, a wearable closed-loop system integrating a TEDA, sensors, and control circuitry is demonstrated as a proof of concept, achieving spatiotemporal temperature modulation to deliver multimodal tactile stimulations, including touch, hot and cold sensations, and multiple pain modalities, within virtual interaction scenarios.

Results

Architecture of the microfluidic-enabled stretchable thermoelectric device array

Stretchable electronics are ideal for wearable and interactive systems due to their ability to conform closely to skin and provide effective stimulation. In virtual tactile applications, compared with vibrotactile, electrotactile, and pneumatic/mechanical systems, TEDs offer key advantages including silent operation, higher safety, direct and highly controllable physical stimulation, and superior multimodal capability via temperature modulation in a compact, highly integrated, skin-conformal form factor (Supplementary Table 1). Their compact, soft nature enables precise, programmable thermal feedback, making them well-suited for realistic and responsive haptic interfaces. By precisely modulating temperature variations in both time and space at the skin-device interface, stretchable thermoelectric systems can elicit rich multimodal perceptual responses. Temporal modulation of temperature at the skin interface using a TEDA can create the illusion of pressure (Fig. 1a). This perceptual effect is attributed to temperature-induced sweat duct expansion, which mechanically stimulates Merkel cells (tactile mechanoreceptors)30. Within a safe temperature range, rapid elevation of skin-surface temperature by a TEDA activates nerve endings through TRPV1 ion-channel opening, producing harmless pain sensations by lowering the heat-induced pain threshold (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, spatially arranging multiple thermoelectric units in alternating hot and cold patterns can induce the TGI, which manifests as a burning pain sensation. The induction of multimodal haptic interfaces depends on both the rate of temperature change and the maximum temperature differential, necessitating high-power currents for spatiotemporal thermal control. The stretchable TED array developed in this study is specifically engineered to meet these stringent thermal regulation requirements. The stretchable TEDA adopts a stacked 3D architecture with embedded microfluidic integration and interconnected array design, balancing system integration and enhanced thermal dissipation (Fig. 1b). TEDA consists of six individual TED units, each capable of independently providing heating or cooling. The internal microfluidic channels are connected in series using copper tubing, allowing liquid to flow sequentially from TED1 to TED6 to effectively remove Joule heat generated during device operation. The AlN/PDMS composite is used for bottom-layer encapsulation and microfluidic channel construction, thereby improving in-plane thermal transport and enhancing the overall thermal modulation performance of the TED. Although AlN doping moderately increases the modulus of PDMS, the composite maintains sufficient softness to ensure conformal skin contact, enabling effective thermal stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 2)33. The stretchable TEDA achieves outstanding cooling performance through highly efficient microfluidic heat exchange. By optimizing microfluidic flow rate and input power, the TEDA achieves a stable cooling reduction of approximately 6 °C under ambient conditions. The device exhibits excellent stretchability due to its island-bridge architecture and elastomer encapsulation, enabling controlled bending and twisting (Fig. 1c, d) while maintaining normal operation (Fig. 1e, f). The fabrication of 3D microfluidic devices primarily relies on laser etching and 3D printing technologies. This advanced design and fabrication approach is applicable not only to high-thermal design power (TDP) TEDs, but also to various types of high-power stretchable electronics.

Although the cooling capacity of the proposed stretchable TED is lower than that of commercial rigid thermoelectric modules, it eliminates the need for bulky external forced-air cooling systems. Compared with rigid devices, it is significantly thinner and lighter while retaining excellent bendability and stretchability for skin-conformal applications. Relative to previously reported flexible TEDs, the achieved minimum cooling temperature is comparable, but most existing systems rely on natural convection or bulky external heat sinks. Natural convection designs typically require air-gap insulation layers that increase thickness and exhibit strong dependence on ambient airflow, leading to unstable performance, whereas heat sink-based systems improve cooling at the expense of added weight and reduced wearing comfort (Supplementary Table 2). Overall, the proposed microfluidic-integrated stretchable TED achieves a favorable balance between portability and stable cooling performance, addressing a key limitation of flexible thermoelectric haptic devices.

Temperature regulation performance of the thermoelectric device

Each stretchable TED is rendered bendable by its elastomeric encapsulation and serpentine interconnects (Fig. 2a). The device adopts a stacked 3D architecture, in which embedded microfluidic channels within the central thermal regulation layer enhance heat dissipation (Fig. 2b). Each temperature regulation network contains 53 pairs of series-connected P- and N-type Bi₂Te₃ pillars, with each thermoelectric unit measuring 1 mm × 1 mm × 2.5 mm (Supplementary Fig. 3). These units are organized in an island–bridge configuration, where thermoelectric units serve as islands linked by serpentine copper/polyimide (Cu/PI) bridges (Fig. 2c). Upon stretching, the serpentine interconnects deform to accommodate strain, thereby imparting system-level stretchability (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). The microfluidic channels feature a leaf-vein-inspired wavy structure, designed to align with the thermoelectric unit arrangement and encase them for efficient heat exchange. The serpentine channel has a cross-sectional dimension of 1 mm (width) × 1 mm (height), with slight width variations due to its meandering design (Supplementary Fig. 6). The embedded microfluidic system demonstrates exceptional heat exchange capability, significantly enhancing the device’s thermal management efficiency. Under a 0.3 A driving current, the microfluidic-integrated device achieves rapid heat dissipation with a maximum hot-side temperature of 30 °C while maintaining a stable cold-side cooling temperature of 24 °C. In contrast, the device without microfluidic integration exhibits poor heat dissipation capability, resulting in a hot-side temperature exceeding 45 °C. This leads to a transient cold-side temperature of 26.0 °C, which then rapidly rises above ambient temperature (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, by adjusting the fluid flow rate, the stretchable device can meet its TDP requirements across varying current inputs. Increasing the flow velocity enhances the convective heat transfer coefficient and reduces the thermal boundary layer thickness between the fluid and heat source34. A thinner boundary layer decreases the thermal resistance for heat transfer from the source to the fluid, thereby significantly improving heat exchange efficiency. Higher flow velocities enhance Joule heat dissipation, enabling the TED to achieve lower minimum cooling temperatures while improving temperature maintenance capability (Fig. 2f). Under ambient temperature of 28 °C and a fixed driving current of 0.5 A, the microfluidic flow rate demonstrates significant influence on the TED’s cooling performance. In the absence of microfluidic cooling, the device exhibits impaired heat exchange with the ambient environment. Consequently, the device temperature initially decreases rapidly, but subsequently rises above ambient temperature, failing to achieve effective cooling. The device without microfluidics is only suitable for low-power cooling (0.1 A current), achieving a mere 27.1 °C as its stable cooling temperature. With increasing microfluidic flow rates, the enhanced heat transfer capability between the device and ambient environment leads to a progressive decrease in the minimum achievable cooling temperature. The system achieves a cooling temperature of 20 °C at microfluidic flow rate of 100 mL/min. Further increase in microfluidic flow rate did not significantly improve cooling performance, as the heat exchange efficiency had already reached the required level for effective cooling.

In practical applications, high power is required to drive the TED for rapid temperature inversion, which is essential to induce more pronounced pressure sensation and thermal pain effects. The temperature modulation rate of the TED is influenced by factors such as the thermal conductivity of the encapsulation material, the thermoelectric figure of merit (ZT) of the P–N pillars, and the driving power. Thanks to the modification with PDMS/AlN composite material, the thermal conductivity of the encapsulation layer is significantly improved. The selected thermoelectric materials exhibit high ZT values of 1.16 (p-type) and 1.04 (n-type) at room temperature (25 °C). More importantly, the integration of microfluidic cooling allows the TED to operate under high-power conditions without concern for Joule heating. When maintaining an initial temperature of 25 °C, the TED can achieve a heating rate exceeding 1.7 °C/s under a 0.5 A driving current in active heating mode (Fig. 2g). In contrast, the TED exhibits a passive temperature recovery rate of 0.8 °C/s. When initially maintained at 38 °C, the TED achieves an active cooling rate exceeding 1.6 °C/s under a 0.5 A driving current, compared to a passive cooling rate of only 0.7 °C/s (Fig. 2h). Precise regulation of device temperature via power tuning is crucial for realizing accurate haptic interfaces. Benefiting from the heat dissipation efficiency of embedded microfluidics, the TED achieves stable heating and cooling control across a wide current range (Fig. 2i). In contrast, devices without microfluidic remain stable only at low current (0.1 A) and exhibit progressive temperature drift under higher driving currents. There are certain performance variations among devices from different batches, but these differences are generally within an acceptable range (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Additionally, in the aforementioned performance characterizations, fluid circulation was achieved using an external reservoir combined with a controllable peristaltic pump, which enabled precise regulation of fluid flow rate during performance testing. For future on-body wearable applications, commercially available miniature micropumps and a custom-designed compact micro-reservoir will be integrated to establish a self-contained fluidic circulation loop. Spiral flexible tubing will be employed to accommodate body motion while extending the flow path, enabling sufficient convective heat exchange with ambient air and preventing heat accumulation. This integrated design removes bulky external components and ensures stable, long-term thermal regulation of the wearable haptic system.

Single thermoelectric unit enabled temporal temperature modulation for pain and pressure sensation

A single TED unit can elicit both pressure and thermal pain sensations through temporal modulation of skin temperature. To systematically investigate these perceptual responses, four stimulation modes with identical frequency (0.2 Hz) but distinct temperature ranges were applied to five participants (Fig. 3a, b). Psychophysical experiments were conducted with five participants, where perceptual intensity and qualitative characteristics were quantitatively assessed using visual analog scale (VAS) questionnaires (Fig. 3c). Modes I–III consistently evoked pressure sensations without inducing pain. Among these, Mode II generated the strongest response, indicating that the magnitude of pressure perception can be effectively tuned by adjusting the thermal fluctuations. This virtual pressure is hypothesized to originate from transient heating and cooling that dilates sweat ducts, thereby mechanically stimulating Merkel cells, which serve as key mechanoreceptors for tactile perception. In contrast, Mode IV produced a distinct shift in perceptual outcome. While it did not broaden the virtual pressure response, the higher temperature range (36–40 °C) elicited pronounced pain sensations. This pain perception is attributed to the activation of TRPV1 ion channels in cutaneous nerve endings, which function as molecular transducers of heat and nociception35. Afferent nerve impulse frequency is a key determinant of subjective sensation intensity. Perceived intensity depends on the rate at which stimulus energy accumulates, with faster accumulation eliciting higher neuronal firing frequencies. Consequently, within the same thermal range, greater temperature modulation frequencies are expected to produce stronger perceptual responses36. Thermal pain intensity was evaluated in five participants using VAS questionnaires to quantify perceived pain levels (Fig. 3d). Pain intensity was recorded in all participants under three stimulation conditions with temperature modulation ranges of 35–40 °C and frequencies of 0.2 Hz, 0.15 Hz, and 0 Hz (Fig. 3e). Analysis indicated that within this thermal nociceptive range, higher modulation frequencies consistently elicited stronger pain perception (Fig. 3f). Under the 0 Hz condition, participants reported minimal pain, experiencing only thermal sensation (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Besides, pressure perception induced by temperature changes was evaluated in five participants using a VAS questionnaire (Fig. 3g). Pressure sensation intensities were recorded under three stimulation conditions with temperature modulation ranges of 27.5–32.5 °C and frequencies of 0.1 Hz, 0.15 Hz, and 0.2 Hz (Fig. 3h). Analysis indicated that within this temperature range associated with pressure sensation, faster temperature changes elicited stronger perceived pressure (Supplementary Fig. 9c). At the lowest stimulation frequency of 0.1 Hz, participants reported minimal pressure perception while still felt clearly thermal changes (Fig. 3i). The thickness of human skin and the density of sensory receptors vary across different body regions. Thermal pain and pressure sensitivity were evaluated on three areas, the volar wrist, palm, and back of the hand, of five participants using a consistent stimulation pattern (Supplementary Fig. 10)37,38. The results revealed notable inter-subject variability, with inconsistent sensitivity rankings among the tested regions across participants. Despite the intrinsic complexity and inter-individual variability of human perception, consistent response patterns were observed across all participants. Within the designed operating range, the device reliably modulates graded thermal pain and pressure sensations, indicating robust generalizability across users. Although the current performance is adequate for typical haptic interaction scenarios, broader applicability, particularly across diverse individuals and anatomical sites, requires further development. Specifically, a pre-use calibration framework should be established to quantify individual- and site-specific thermal perception thresholds and to enable adaptive tuning of stimulation parameters. In parallel, modular and scalable array architectures are needed to accommodate variations in body size and wearable configurations.

Thermoelectric array enabled spatial temperature modulation for multimodal haptic interfaces

Multiple thermoelectric units enable spatial modulation of skin temperature, supporting dynamic tactile sensations such as sliding and enhancing perceptual dimensionality, whereas a single TED, constrained to temporal modulation at a single site, is insufficient to elicit such dynamic or sustained sensations. A 1 × 6 TEDA was fabricated, in which each unit can be independently addressed and controlled (Fig. 4a). The fabricated TEDA was mounted on the forearm, with each unit periodically driven by alternating current inputs. In the experimental protocol, a forward current of 0.6 A was applied to each TED for 2 s, followed by a reverse current of 0.6 A for another 2 s. Under this driving scheme, each unit initially stabilized at 28 °C, then sequentially increased to 32 °C and subsequently returned to 28 °C. Distinct activation times were assigned to different units, generating phase-shifted thermal responses that together produced a sliding touch sensation along the arm (Fig. 4b, c). By modulating the phase differences of the driving currents applied to individual units in the TEDA, the dynamic spatial distribution of skin temperature can be effectively regulated, thereby eliciting distinct perceptual experiences in the user. To assess the effectiveness of phase-controlled thermal modulation in evoking sliding sensations, questionnaires were administered to evaluate perceptual intensity and sensation type, and response rates were analyzed across five stimulation modes with varying phase differences between adjacent driving currents (Mode I: 0°, Mode II: 60°, Mode III: 90°, Mode IV: 120°, Mode V: 180°; Fig. 4d). Under synchronous operation (0° phase difference), participants predominantly reported pressure sensations (87.5%), whereas sliding perception was limited (25%). Introducing phase differences of 60° or greater substantially increased reports of sliding sensations, with more than 60% of participants perceiving sliding, while the incidence of vertical pressure sensations declined accordingly (Fig. 4e–g). Although contact temperatures below 45 °C are generally considered safe for human skin, rapidly heating a single TED to induce thermal pain remains risky. Moreover, rapidly heating a single TED produces only brief, transient pain sensations at the temperature peak. To enable sustained and controllable pain stimulation without tissue damage, the device was designed based on the thermal grill illusion (TGI, Fig. 4h). TGI is a perceptual phenomenon in which spatially alternating warm and cold stimuli evoke persistent burning and painful sensations. Leveraging this principle, the device integrates alternating thermal units to amplify sensory input via multimodal pathways, generating controllable and continuous pain perception. Larger temperature contrasts between adjacent warm and cold units further enhance the perceived intensity, allowing safe and effective simulation of nociceptive sensations39. Based on this theoretical framework, the TEDA was configured to apply spatially alternating cold (20 °C) and warm (40 °C) stimuli to evoke a TGI-like burning sensation, while as a control, additional tests were conducted using uniform all-cold or all-hot modes (Fig. 4i). Eight participants were tested, and their perceived cold, heat, and pain intensities were quantified using VAS questionnaires under three stimulation conditions (Mode I–III), with mean scores computed for analysis (Fig. 4j). The spatially alternating thermal arrangement enhanced thermal perception and effectively elicited pain without causing tissue damage. These results support the convergence principle underlying the TGI, demonstrating that TEDA can safely and controllably activate multimodal thermonociceptive pathways to induce sustained pain sensations, providing a practical approach for VR applications.

Wearable closed-loop system for virtual haptic interaction

The excellent thermal modulation capability and wearable form factor of the TEDA make it particularly well suited for VR haptic interfaces, providing a more immersive user experience. To enable precise thermal modulation on the skin, a closed-loop control system was developed and optimized for wearable integration. The system consists of a stretchable TEDA, flexible printed circuit board (FPC), integrated temperature sensors, and a VR headset (Fig. 5a). Temperature sensors embedded at the skin-device interface provide continuous feedback on local skin temperature. This feedback is processed by control circuitry that implements a PID algorithm to dynamically regulate both the polarity and magnitude of the TEDA drive voltage in response to deviations from the target setpoint. The control circuitry includes a microcontroller unit (MCU), H-bridge drivers, temperature sensors, a Bluetooth module, and a power management unit (Fig. 5b). During VR interactions, collision events detected in the virtual environment are translated by the host computer into control commands, which are transmitted via Bluetooth to the embedded controller. The controller then actuates the TEDA in real time to deliver corresponding thermal or other haptic simulation. Human thermal perception is highly sensitive to small temperature variations, making precise and stable temperature control essential for high-quality haptic feedback. Unlike open-loop systems that rely on fixed pre-calibrated power and cannot compensate for environmental fluctuations, the proposed closed-loop architecture dynamically regulates output power in real time (Supplementary Fig. 11). The wearable TEDA system enables multimodal tactile feedback in virtual environments, providing controllable sensations of pressure (Supplementary Video 1), warmth (Supplementary Video 2), cooling (Supplementary Video 3), stinging pain (Supplementary Video 4), and continuous burning pain (Supplementary Video 5). For instance, when a virtual caterpillar crawls across the user’s arm, the host computer translates the detected contact into control commands, and the embedded controller drives the corresponding TED unit to rapidly heat from its baseline cooling state (Fig. 5c). This rapid temperature increase expands subcutaneous sweat ducts, mechanically stimulating Merkel cells and evoking a pressure sensation. When a virtual raccoon hugs the user’s arm, the closed-loop control system maintains a uniform warm temperature across the skin via TEDA, reproducing the gentle warmth associated with an embrace (Fig. 5d). Conversely, when a virtual faucet pours cold water, the system sets TEDA to a global cooling state, simulating the refreshing sensation of flowing water (Fig. 5e). For sharp pain, such as a virtual ant bite, the controller rapidly and periodically raises the temperature of the corresponding TED unit to approximately 40 °C. This transient thermal change lowers the heat-pain threshold, safely activates cutaneous TRPV1 ion channels, and reproduces the characteristic stinging sensation of an ant bite (Fig. 5f). Finally, in a virtual fire scenario, the host system converts the flame’s motion across the arm into TGI control commands. The TEDA then maintains spatially alternating cold (20–23 °C) and warm (37–40 °C) regions, inducing a thermo-pain illusion that mimics the continuous burning pain sensation of skin contact with fire (Fig. 5g). The microfluidic design and advanced control strategy of TEDA enabled highly realistic haptic feedback across these virtual scenarios, demonstrating its potential for immersive and multimodal tactile interaction in both virtual and augmented reality environments. Beyond wearable thermal haptic systems, the proposed microfluidic-integrated technology demonstrates strong potential for other high-power wearable applications. The fabrication strategy is compatible with scalable manufacturing approaches such as 3D printing and laser etching, enabling future large-area and high-throughput production. Benefiting from low-cost silicone materials and scalable processing routes, the overall system cost can be effectively controlled. In addition, the device shows excellent compatibility with existing wearable electronic platforms and can be seamlessly integrated into various wearable systems without substantial architectural modifications, highlighting its broad applicability and translational potential in wearable electronics.

Discussion

Stretchable TEDs offer great potential for wearable haptic interfaces, but their limited heat dissipation restricts operation under high-power conditions. Moreover, single-point thermal modulation on the skin is insufficient to meet the requirements of dynamic and multimodal tactile stimulation in complex scenarios. This work overcomes these challenges through a three-dimensional microfluidic integration strategy that substantially enhances cooling performance. Leveraging this advance, a TEDA was fabricated to enable diverse tactile stimulations, including pressure, pain, and sliding. A wearable closed-loop system was further developed to achieve dynamic skin temperature regulation for realistic haptic simulation. When coupled with virtual environments, the system delivers real-time multimodal feedback across a variety of scenarios. By bridging thermodynamics, neuroscience, and flexible electronics, this study establishes a new framework for multimodal thermal–haptic interfaces and expands the design space for next-generation wearable technologies.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of the microchannel

The microchannel was fabricated using a molding process comprising mold preparation, substrate formation, encapsulation layer fabrication, and final assembly. A mold incorporating microchannel structures (1 mm in height and width) was first designed using 3D modeling software and then printed with a rigid photosensitive resin via 3D printing (Supplementary Fig. 12). After printing, the mold was cleaned with 75% ethanol to remove residual resin and exposed to ultraviolet light for 15 min to ensure complete curing. To form the microchannel, PDMS (SYLGARD™ 184, Dow Corning), aluminum nitride (AlN, YT-ALN-01, Zhenhan New Materials (Suzhou) Co., Ltd.) powder, and Sylgard 184 curing agent were mixed at a mass ratio of 10:2:1 to obtain a homogeneous sol. The mixture was poured into the mold and degassed under vacuum for 30 min to remove trapped air. The mold was then cured at 60 °C for 1 h, and the microchannel was demolded. A release agent was applied to the mold surface before casting to facilitate demolding. The resulting substrate measured 46 mm × 39 mm, with a channel bottom film thickness of approximately 0.2 mm. The incorporation of AlN notably enhanced in-plane thermal conductivity while maintaining the inherent flexibility and skin conformability of PDMS. The top encapsulation layer was prepared using a second PDMS-based mixture composed of PDMS, Sylgard 184 curing agent, and platinum catalyst in a mass ratio of 10:1:0.2. The mixture was spin-coated onto a 50 mm × 50 mm glass substrate and partially cured on a 120 °C hot plate for 2–3 min to form a transparent encapsulation layer. A thin layer of the same mixture was then spin-coated onto the encapsulation surface as an adhesive. The substrate with embedded channels was aligned and laminated onto the encapsulation layer, followed by additional heating at 120 °C for 2–3 min to complete bonding. Since the encapsulation layer was slightly larger than the substrate, the edges were trimmed after bonding to achieve dimensional conformity in the final stretchable structure (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Fabrication of the stretchable TED unit

The geometry of the stretchable serpentine electrodes for electrical interconnection was first designed using computer-aided design (CAD) software (Supplementary Fig. 14). The desired electrode patterns were then fabricated on PI/Cu composite films via precision laser ablation. Square holes were fabricated at predefined positions on the fabricated PDMS/AlN substrate using laser micromachining to anchor the thermoelectric units. Each hole had a side length of 0.8 mm, slightly smaller than the thermoelectric unit dimensions (1 mm), ensuring a tight and stable fit after insertion. The p-type and n-type thermoelectric units were individually embedded into their corresponding holes, followed by soldering of the serpentine electrodes to achieve reliable electrical interconnections between adjacent thermoelectric units. A mixed sol-gel precursor (PDMS/AlN/curing agent, mass ratio = 10:2:1) was uniformly coated onto the bottom surface of the device and cured in an oven at 60 °C for 45 min (Supplementary Fig. 15). This encapsulation layer served not only to mechanically secure the structure but also to enhance the thermal management performance of the device. Upon curing, the stretchable TED unit was successfully fabricated (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Fabrication of the stretchable TEDA

After fabricating six individual microfluidic TEDs, a series-integrated TEDA was constructed using a sequential assembly process. First, TED #1 was fixed in place, and a copper tube (4 mm in length, 1 mm in diameter) was half-inserted into its side outlet (Supplementary Fig. 17). Uncured PDMS was then applied around the connection site. The remaining half of the copper tube was inserted into the side inlet of TED #2, aligning the two devices closely side by side. The assembly was placed on a heating platform and cured at 120 °C to form a sealed connection. Due to the cushioning effect of the elastomeric substrate encapsulation, the small size of the copper tubes, and the relatively low bending curvature of the forearm during actual wearing, the strain and stress generated at the interface between the copper tubes and the substrate can be maintained at a low level without significant stress concentration. This procedure was repeated sequentially to connect all TED units in series, resulting in a fully integrated stretchable TEDA (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Construction and measurement of the microfluidic system

A microfluidic system was developed in this study to evaluate the thermal management performance of the stretchable TED. The system consisted of a peristaltic pump with precise flow-rate control, a water reservoir serving as the heat dissipation medium, and a series of connecting tubes linking all components. The inlet and outlet interfaces of the fluidic loop were sealed using uncured PDMS, effectively preventing fluid leakage during continuous operation and ensuring long-term stability of the testing system. During testing, the input current to the TED was regulated using a programmable DC power supply, while two high-precision thermocouples were employed to measure the ambient temperature and the surface temperature of the TED, respectively. The temperature data were digitized via a data acquisition card and transmitted to a LabVIEW-based software interface for real-time monitoring and visualization. To investigate the relationship between microfluidic cooling performance and flow rate, the system enabled fine adjustment of the peristaltic pump speed to match the TED’s operating conditions under different driving currents (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Control system for TEDA

For the control and drive circuitry, we selected the STM32F103RCT6 microcontroller (MCU), which is based on the ARM Cortex-M3 architecture. This MCU offers robust computational performance, ensuring reliable execution of control algorithms and task scheduling. To meet the precise temperature control requirements of the PID algorithm, real-time temperature acquisition is essential. We used the LMT70 analog temperature sensor in a miniature package, which ensures accurate temperature detection and good thermal contact at the skin-device interface. The analog output from the sensor is digitized using the MCU’s onboard ADC (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). TEDA functions as a thermal system with inertia, and both its power and voltage polarity are controlled using PWM signals. Specifically, the MCU’s PWM output is connected to an H-bridge circuit, which modulates both the duty cycle and polarity to adjust TEDA’s driving power and select between heating and cooling modes. For thermal regulation, the MCU receives real-time data from the temperature sensor and uses a PID control algorithm to compute the required PWM duty cycle and voltage direction. These outputs are then used by the H-bridge circuit to control TEDA’s power and operation mode (Supplementary Fig. 22). The PID control algorithm uses feedback to continuously monitor the temperature, compare it with the target value, and dynamically adjust the output to achieve the desired setpoint. It comprises three components: proportional, integral, and derivative. The system compares the current temperature with the target, calculates the error, processes it through the three terms, and sums the results to generate the next control output. Temperature is sampled and adjusted in real time to ensure system stability. Accurate tuning of PID parameters is critical for effective system control. Development of Unity-based virtual environment

We employed the PICO Neo 3 head-mounted display as the core VR presentation device. This standalone headset is widely adopted in the market and offers a high cost-performance ratio. Powered by the Qualcomm Snapdragon XR2 platform, it supports six degrees of freedom positional tracking and delivers robust graphics rendering and real-time computing capabilities. Its 4 K high-resolution display produces detailed and realistic visual effects, providing users with a highly immersive virtual experience.

For the construction of the virtual environment, we used 3ds Max for 3D modeling and animation, specifically designing architectural scenes, prop models, and dynamic interactive elements (such as mechanical and organic movements). All models were exported in FBX format after completing UV unwrapping, texture mapping, and rigging.

Subsequently, the model assets were imported into the Unity3D engine, where the built-in renderer was used to configure scene lighting, shadows, and post-processing effects, significantly enhancing visual realism. Scene interaction logic was implemented through C# scripting, including user input handling, Bluetooth instruction transmission during scene transitions, and integration with the PICO SDK to ensure natural and real-time VR operation. Finally, all functional modules were integrated within Unity into a complete VR application. An Android APK was generated via Unity Build Settings and deployed on the headset for real-world operation and performance debugging.

VAS experiment protocol

The VAS is a validated psychometric instrument widely used to quantify subjective experiences such as pain and tactile perception. To minimize subjective bias, a single-blind experimental design was adopted, and participants were not informed of the specific testing conditions or underlying hypotheses. During each trial, the device was securely attached to the participant’s skin, with an integrated temperature sensor at the device-skin interface for real-time monitoring and recording of local temperature. For safety, all thermal stimuli were maintained strictly within the range of 20 °C to 45 °C. To minimize sensory adaptation and fatigue, an interstimulus rest interval of at least 3 min was provided between different stimulation modes.

After each stimulation mode, participants completed a VAS questionnaire to report the type and intensity of their perceived sensations. In this study, participants performed a two-step evaluation: (1) selecting descriptive anchor words corresponding to their perceived sensation (for pain: “no pain,” “mild pain,” “moderate pain,” and “severe pain”; for pressure: “no pressure,” “slight pressure,” “moderate pressure,” and “strong pressure”); (2) participants rated the intensity using a digital VAS slider, consisting of a 10 cm continuous scale annotated with the same descriptive anchors. The endpoints represented the minimum (“no pain”/“no sensation”) and maximum (“worst imaginable pain”/“extremely strong sensation”) perceptual intensities. Participants were instructed to drag the slider to the position that best reflected their subjective perception. The distance from the scale’s origin to the marked point was measured and converted to a numerical score from 0 to 100 (0 = no sensation, 100 = maximum intensity).

Healthy participants were recruited to systematically evaluate subjective sensations of pain, touch, and thermal perception under controlled thermal stimulation. Prior to testing, all participants were thoroughly instructed on the meaning of the scale anchors to ensure accurate and consistent self-reporting. In the TGI experiments involving the TEDA system, the same procedure was followed. During each 3-min interval, participants independently rated their sensations of cold, warmth, and pain using three separate VAS assessments (Supplementary Fig. 24).
