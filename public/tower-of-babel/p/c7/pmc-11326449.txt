COVID-19 vaccination and concerns regarding vaccine hesitancy after the termination of the zero-COVID policy in China: A nationwide cross-sectional study
Authors: Min Yang, Wenjuan Ma, Jingfeng Jiang, Zhen Lu, Xuwen Wang, Yuan Shen, Huachun Zou, Xiaojun Meng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCOVID-19 vaccine hesitancy remains prevalent globally. However, national data on this issue in the general population after the termination of the zero-COVID policy in China are limited. In March 2023, we conducted a nationwide cross-sectional survey among Chinese adults using a self-administered questionnaire. Descriptive statistics and multivariate logistic regressions were employed. Among 4,966 participants, 43.8% reported COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy following the end of the zero-COVID policy in China. Higher rates of vaccine hesitancy were associated with being married (married: OR 1.36, 95%CI 1.17–1.57; other marital status: OR 1.86, 95%CI 1.36–2.55), working in healthcare (OR 1.64, 95%CI 1.38–1.96), having both minors and older adults in the household (OR 1.45, 95%CI 1.20–1.75), having no minors and older adults in the household (OR 1.44, 95%CI 1.17–1.77), having chronic diseases (OR 1.42, 95%CI 1.23–1.64), experiencing adverse events post-vaccination (OR 1.39, 95%CI 1.19–1.61), and uncertainty about previous COVID-19 infection (OR 1.45, 95%CI 1.13–1.86). Conversely, participants who had received the influenza vaccine in the past three years (OR 0.62, 95%CI 0.54–0.72), had previously taken the COVID-19 vaccine (OR 0.44, 95%CI 0.32–0.59), and had higher confidence in vaccines (OR 0.63, 95%CI 0.60–0.67) were less likely to exhibit hesitancy. Our findings indicate a significant level of vaccine hesitancy, underscoring the urgent need for tailored public health strategies to address vaccine hesitancy and improve uptake post-zero-COVID policy in China. A comprehensive understanding of public concerns and related factors is essential for developing effective vaccine communication strategies.

Introduction

Since the outbreak of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), the World Health Organization (WHO) has identified over 775.6 million confirmed cases across more than 200 countries and regions, with over 7.1 million deaths reported as of the end of May 2024.1 Now that vaccines are available and the Omicron variant did not cause much severe disease and hospitalization, most countries have lifted mandatory nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) such as face masks, social distancing, lockdowns, and travel restrictions to coexist with the virus. Despite this, the WHO continues to promote increased access to COVID-19 vaccines and support countries in accelerating vaccine delivery to combat the pandemic. By the end of May 2023, 70.8% of the global population had received at least one dose of the COVID-19 vaccine, whereas only 32.8% of individuals in low-income countries had received at least one dose.2

High coverage of COVID-19 vaccination is crucial for controlling the pandemic and restoring social and economic activities to pre-pandemic levels.3 Despite the global distribution of COVID-19 vaccines to achieve herd immunity, studies have reported significant vaccine hesitancy among various populations.4,5 Prior to the start of the COVID-19 vaccination program, systematic reviews indicated that overall COVID-19 vaccination intention during the first year of the pandemic ranged from 27.7% to 93.3%, with associated factors such as sociodemographic differences, perception of risk, and susceptibility to COVID-19.6,7 During the initial rollout of COVID-19 vaccines, concerns about the effectiveness and safety of COVID-19 vaccines, along with inadequate information, were primary factors contributing to vaccine hesitancy.8 As billions of vaccine doses have been administered, many countries encouraged booster doses to enhance herd immunity. However, COVID-19 booster doses face higher levels of public hesitancy compared to initial vaccinations.9

The zero-COVID policy, characterized by strict movement restrictions and lockdowns, was implemented by the Chinese government to curb the spread of COVID-19. This policy, in place for nearly three years until November 2022, significantly influenced public perceptions and behaviors regarding COVID-19 vaccination.10,11 Considering vaccine supply and updated evidence from clinical trials of newly designed vaccines, China adopted a two-step strategy for COVID-19 vaccination during the early stages of the campaign. Initially, individuals at high risk of occupational exposure and essential workers were prioritized for emergency use. From February 2021 onwards, the vaccination program was gradually extended to the general public.12 From October 2020 to December 2022, 34.5 billion doses of COVID-19 vaccines were administered in China, with 92.9% of the population receiving their first dose and 90.1% completing primary vaccination before the end of the zero-COVID policy.2

Although extensive government campaigns have promoted vaccinations and vaccine availability, some individuals were hesitant to receive them before the end of the zero-COVID policy in China. A national, multicenter, online survey reported an overall hesitancy rate of 15.6% in China, including 23.9% among students, 21.2% among the general population, 13.1% among medical workers, and 10.4% among public health professionals.13 Additionally, a cross-sectional questionnaire survey among older adults aged ≥60 years reported that 29.9% of participants refused to receive vaccines in China.14 A systematic review summarized that the safety and effectiveness of approved domestic COVID-19 vaccines, infection risk perception, and gender were factors affecting COVID-19 vaccination among Chinese community residents during the zero-COVID policy period.15

On December 7, 2022, China implemented the “Ten New Measures” to optimize COVID-19 prevention and control;16 thereafter, China experienced its first national wave of widespread infections.17 The Chinese government updated COVID-19 vaccination guidance in July 2023 to increase immunity levels in specific groups and reduce the risks of severe infection and death; priority groups include minors over 3 years old who have not been infected and have not completed primary immunization, infected adults who have not received booster vaccination, infected adults who have not completed primary immunization.18 Since the termination of the zero-COVID policy, there has been growing concern among the general population in China regarding the intention to continue receiving COVID-19 vaccines. A vaccine hesitancy rate of 29.7% was reported among the general population in 4 provinces in China at the beginning of 2023.19 Of people who have recovered from COVID-19 infection, 42.2% were hesitant to receive the next vaccine dose of COVID-19 vaccines in February 2023 in China.20 However, there was no national studies conducted in the general population to investigate the updated overall vaccine hesitancy since the termination of the zero-COVID-19 policy in China.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a nationwide cross-sectional online survey among Chinese adults in March 2023. Participants were recruited using the snowball sampling method via WeChat, a popular social media app with over 1 billion monthly active users in China. Recruitment was initiated simultaneously in four cities: Chengde, Shenzhen, Luzhou, and Wuxi, representing northern, southern, western, and eastern China, respectively. Ten individual seeds in each city were purposely selected to recruit participants from their social networks. Participants recruited by these seeds who were willing to promote the study were then encouraged to enroll more respondents from their social networks until the target sample size was reached. A survey QR code with a link to the structured online questionnaire was forwarded or shared by the participants with their friends, family members, and colleagues, allowing them to access and complete the survey.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Chinese residents aged ≥18 years without vaccination contraindications; (2) owning a smartphone to access the questionnaire; (3) being literate in Chinese to complete the survey; and (4) willingness to participate. Participation in this survey was voluntary, and an electronic informed consent form was signed before answering the questionnaire. Each WeChat account was allowed to access the online questionnaire only once to avoid duplicate responses. The questions in the questionnaire were closed-ended and mandatory, utilizing automatic logical jumps. Records with logically contradictory answers were excluded from the final analysis.

The minimum sample size was calculated using the following formula:21 N=Zα/22×p×1−pd2

A preliminary online survey was conducted to elucidate the prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, revealing that 40.1% of the 277 participants expressed hesitancy. The minimum sample size required for the main study was calculated to be 4925. This calculation was based on a standard normal variate of 1.96 for a 5% type I error, an observed prevalence of vaccine hesitancy of 40.1%, and an allowable error of 1.5%, accounting for an estimated ineligibility rate of 20%.

Survey items

The original survey questionnaire was adapted from previous studies on similar topics.13 Then, the questionnaire was revised based on suggestions provided by a panel of epidemiologists. The revised questionnaire was used in the preliminary survey and has been tested to show good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.732) and validity (Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin = 0.925). The final questionnaire included questions related to sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge of COVID-19 and its vaccine, risk perception of COVID-19, trust in COVID-19 vaccines, the experience with COVID-19, vaccination and related reasons for getting vaccinated vaccination and being non-vaccinated vaccination before ending the zero-COVID-19 policy, willingness to be vaccinated against COVID-19 after ending the zero-COVID-19 policy ended, related reasons for the vaccination intention to be vaccinated and vaccine hesitancy, and other potential factors associated with vaccine hesitancy. We assessed participants’ knowledge of COVID-19 and its vaccines using 10 items (Table S1) covering infection sources, transmission routes, susceptible populations, immunity persistence, contraindications, and adverse events related to vaccination. One point was awarded for each correct answer, and the final score evaluated the participant’s knowledge level. Participants who scored above the median were considered to have a high level of knowledge about COVID-19 and its vaccines.

The questionnaire included four questions to assess participants’ risk perception of COVID-19, addressing concerns about the possibility of an outbreak, self-susceptibility, reinfection likelihood, and the health impact of infection (Table S2). Respondents received one point for selecting “Yes” to a specific question. Those with higher final scores were considered to have a stronger risk perception of COVID-19. Similarly, four variables were designed to analyze individual confidence in domestically manufactured vaccines, focusing on the safety and effectiveness of vaccines, their ability to alleviate infection symptoms, and prevent severe disease and death (Table S3). Higher final scores indicated stronger confidence in the COVID-19 vaccines.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among Chinese adults after the end of the zero-COVID policy. Since no standard COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy scale has been developed for Chinese adults, we designed a specific item in the questionnaire to evaluate individuals’ attitudes toward vaccination based on the Oxford COVID-19 Vaccine Hesitancy Scale.22 The WHO SAGE vaccine hesitancy working group defines vaccine hesitancy as “a delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccination despite the availability of vaccination services.” Some published studies often counted vaccinated people as not hesitant, conflating vaccination behavior with vaccine attitudes.23,24 In fact, vaccine hesitancy refers to a broad continuum of attitudes and does not perfectly align with vaccination status.25,26 In other words, even if individuals have already been vaccinated, they may still hold hesitant attitudes toward the vaccine or subsequent doses, and these people are considered hesitant adopters.27–29

In our study, we distinguished vaccine hesitancy from vaccination status. The prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy was evaluated using the following question: “Since the zero-COVID policy ended in early December 2022 in China, are you willing to receive COVID-19 vaccines?” The response options were: (1) having received a COVID-19 vaccine after the zero-COVID policy, (2) willing to get the COVID-19 vaccine, (3) delaying COVID-19 vaccination, (4) unwilling to get the COVID-19 vaccine, and (5) anti-vaccination. Participants who selected any of the latter three options were defined as hesitant to get vaccinated, whereas others were considered acceptant of vaccination.30

Statistical analysis

Using descriptive statistics, we described COVID-19 vaccine coverage overall and within predefined subgroups based on doses (unvaccinated, at least one dose, primary vaccination completed, and booster completed). Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages (%). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to explore the factors (sociodemographic characteristics, chronic disease, experience with COVID-19, previous influenza and COVID-19 vaccination, knowledge of COVID-19, risk perception of infection, and confidence in vaccines) significantly associated with COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among Chinese adults after the end of the zero-COVID policy. Variables with a p-value of <0.10 in univariate analysis were selected for constructing the multiple logistic regression model. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to determine associations between potential significant factors and COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. Significance tests for variables were performed using the Wald test. McFadden’s R2 was used to evaluate the goodness-of-fit of the logistic regression model. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p-value of < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Wuxi Municipal Centre for Disease Control and Prevention. Participants were notified of the purpose and potential use of the collected data in the informed consent Only those who signed the electronic informed consent were allowed to proceed to the questionnaire response page.

Results

Multivariate analysis (Figure 3) revealed that marital status was a predictor of higher COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, with an OR of 1.36 (95% CI 1.17–1.57) for married participants and 1.86 (95% CI 1.36–2.55) for other marital statuses compared with unmarried participants. Healthcare workers were significantly more hesitant to COVID-19 vaccination, with an OR of 1.64 (95% CI 1.38–1.96). Compared with participants with minors in their household, the OR for hesitancy was 1.60 (95% CI 1.31–1.95) for those with older adults, 1.45 (95% CI 1.20–1.75) for those with both minors and older adults, and 1.44 (95% CI 1.17–1.77) for those with no minors or older adults. Participants with chronic diseases were more likely to be hesitant about COVID-19 vaccination compared to those without chronic diseases, with an OR of 1.42 (95% CI 1.23–1.64). Individuals who experienced adverse events after vaccination were more hesitant to receive the COVID-19 vaccine (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.19–1.61). The time interval since the last dose was also a factor associated with COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, with an OR of 1.42 (95% CI 1.20–1.67) for 6–12 months and 1.44 (95% CI 1.20–1.72) for over 12 months compared to less than 6 months. Participants who were unsure about their COVID-19 infection status had higher vaccine hesitancy (OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.13–1.86) compared to those who were certain about their infection status. In contrast, participants who were less likely to be hesitant about COVID-19 vaccination included those who had received an influenza vaccine in the last three years (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.54–0.72), those who had received the COVID-19 vaccine (OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.32–0.59), and those with more confidence in the vaccines (OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.60–0.67). This association may reflect the influence of prior positive vaccination experiences on reducing hesitancy toward COVID-19 vaccines. However, sex, age, severity of symptoms after COVID-19, and risk perception did not have statistically significant effects on COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy.

Reasons for accepting COVID-19 vaccination

The distributions of the categorized reasons for accepting COVID-19 vaccination before and after the end of the zero-COVID policy were similar (Figure 4), with the same top four reasons. Specifically, 66.9% (1869/2793) of participants considered relieving infection symptoms as their primary reason to get vaccinated after the end of the zero-COVID policy, whereas preventing COVID-19 was the top reason before the policy ended. In contrast, the requirement to get vaccinated by an employer decreased from 26.4% (1153/4735) of participants before the end of the zero-COVID policy to 10.3% (289/2793) after its conclusion, owing to the adjustment of mandatory vaccination strategies. A similar pattern was observed for the reasons for unwillingness to receive COVID-19 vaccines before and after the end of the zero-COVID policy in China (Figure 5). Discussion

While this study provides the first nationwide assessment of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy post-zero-COVID policy in China, comparative analyses with studies from diverse cultural backgrounds could offer additional insights into the global patterns of vaccine hesitancy and their underlying drivers. In this survey, we observed an increased COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy rate of 43.8% among participants, which is higher than the rate of less than 20.0% for the general population before the termination of the zero-COVID policy.13,30,31 Even in some countries where NPIs were canceled earlier, COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy rates remained below 40.0%: 7.5% in Singapore, 19.8% in the United States, 21.9% in Germany, and 35.9% in Poland.32 Although the WHO declared that COVID-19 is no longer a public health emergency of international concern on May 5, 2023, they emphasized the need for COVID-19 vaccination in life-course vaccination programs due to the continuous mutation of SARS-CoV-2 and the ongoing nature of the epidemic.33 Therefore, addressing public concerns about vaccination and continuously improving herd immunity levels are critical issues that the Chinese government must decisively tackle, given the significantly high vaccine hesitancy rate.

In this study, univariate analysis revealed that the perception of the risk of COVID-19 was strongly and significantly associated with vaccine hesitancy, consistent with findings from previous reviews.34 After lifting strict NPIs, many countries experienced several waves of the COVID-19 epidemic, each with significant cases case burdens.35,36 Although the first nationwide COVID-19 wave, driven by the Omicron variant, has largely subsided, subsequent epidemic waves are unavoidable due to waning immunity and ongoing virus evolution.37 Until the end of 2023, several people may be vulnerable to COVID-19, particularly those who are not vaccinated, those who escaped waves of infection, and infected individuals with waning immunity. To foster a comprehensive perception of infection risk among the public, the government should strengthen the monitoring of epidemic trends, virus mutations, and waning immunity and share these data transparently and promptly. Additionally, vaccine communication campaigns aimed at promoting vaccination can enhance public awareness of susceptibility and severity, thereby ensuring higher uptake of COVID-19 vaccines.38

Since the emergence of the Omicron variant, the effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines developed based on the original strain has greatly waned in preventing infection.39 Globally, the promotion strategy for COVID-19 vaccines has shifted from preventing infection to preventing severe disease and death. However, in this study, we observed that the top two reasons for unwillingness to get vaccinated among the general population were doubts about the vaccine’s efficacy in preventing infection and concerns about vaccine safety, both before and after the termination of the zero-COVID policy in China. This implies that public confidence in the safety and effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines remains a major factor affecting vaccination decisions corroborating previous studies.32,40 The most direct way to increase public willingness to get vaccinated is by accelerating the development of new, safe vaccines, particularly multivalent vaccines that can effectively prevent Omicron variant infection.

Multivariate analysis revealed that participants who had received the influenza vaccine in the past three years were less hesitant to receive COVID-19 vaccines. With the continuous mutation of the virus, COVID-19 may become a long-term problem for humans, similar to seasonal influenza.41 Another nationwide cross-sectional study reported that the simultaneous administration of influenza and COVID-19 vaccines as a single injection might decrease rejection of the COVID-19 vaccine.42 Therefore, promoting the simultaneous administration of COVID-19 and seasonal influenza vaccines in the future has potential benefits. Encouragingly, Chinese scientists have developed a combined novel mRNA vaccine and a live attenuated virus-based intranasal vaccine, both of which have achieved positive results in preclinical experiments.43,44 However, further studies in this field should be substantially supported.

Real-world evidence from an Omicron outbreak in early 2022 in Hong Kong, China, revealed that 90% of COVID-19-related deaths among older adults were in those who had not received the full vaccination course.45 Considering the impact of the Omicron variant, the WHO revised the roadmap in March 2023 for prioritizing the use of COVID-19 vaccines, reemphasizing the importance of vaccinating those still at risk of severe disease, primarily older adults and those with underlying conditions.46 However, in some countries, COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy is common among older people.47 Similarly, our study results suggest that older age groups in univariate analysis and those with chronic diseases in multivariate analysis are more hesitant to receive COVID-19 vaccination. This is consistent with a previous study in China conducted just before the end of the zero-COVID policy.48 The main reasons for hesitancy among older people include the refusal to vaccinate older individuals with potentially life-threatening conditions and misunderstandings of COVID-related contraindications among both local vaccinators and older people.49 Common chronic diseases among older people, such as hypertension, heart disease, stroke, and diabetes,50 are often mistakenly perceived as vaccine contraindications. Moreover, some older individuals are hesitant about vaccination, considering that the side effects of the COVID-19 vaccine may exacerbate their chronic diseases.48 Therefore, scientific guidelines and transparent communication based on real-world evidence about vaccine safety are expected to decrease hesitancy among older people.

When China began implementing COVID-19 vaccines in late 2020, healthcare workers, a group at high risk of infection, were recommended to prioritize vaccination. Three-year period before ending the zero-COVID policy, COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy rates among Chinese healthcare workers were 12%-23%.51 In our study, we noted that the highest hesitancy rate, over 50.0%, was reported among healthcare workers after the termination of the zero-COVID policy. A systematic review found that healthcare workers displayed more hesitancy toward the COVID-19 vaccine in other countries.52 Similar to the general population, concerns about the efficacy and side effects of the COVID-19 vaccine are common reasons for hesitancy among healthcare workers. Notably, despite their medical education background, some healthcare workers doubt that the speed of COVID-19 vaccine development was too fast to guarantee its safety and suspect that the large-scale promotion of vaccination may be driven by political factors.53 Furthermore, some hesitant healthcare workers even believe that COVID-19 does not exist or is not a serious disease, and some of them are more inclined to believe in community myths, including conspiracy theories, injection of microchips, genetic mutation, or infection from the vaccine.54,55 Improving vaccine acceptance among healthcare workers and disseminating scientific COVID-19 information through healthcare workers will have potential benefits for decreasing hesitancy among the general population. Therefore, healthcare workers should be considered a key population for vaccination campaigns in China.

Limitations

Although our study provided evidence of a high rate of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among Chinese people after the end of the zero-COVID policy, the following limitations should be considered with caution. Firstly, despite including 4966 participants, it was sufficient to meet the estimated minimum sample size of 4925, the sample was too small to represent the entire country, which has a population of more than 1.4 billion. Secondly, the snowball sampling method was used to recruit participants rather than a random sampling method, especially these initial 10 seeds were not randomly selected. Since personal network structures do not grow randomly and mostly have a preferential attachment, it may result in involving more people who have social media usage habits and live in more economically developed areas. This methodological choice may have introduced a selection bias, potentially overestimating hesitancy rates among certain demographic groups that are more active on social media platforms. The relationship between factors and vaccine hesitancy may be overstated due to the limited types of data collected. Thirdly, we used an online survey to collect participants’ self-reported intentions to receive COVID-19 vaccination, making it difficult to avoid bias caused by social expectations. Fourthly, for simplicity and efficiency, we used a single item with 5 response options referring to a published large-scale national Chinese study30 to evaluate hesitancy due to the absence of a valid COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy scale for adults in China. A standard scale should be developed and adopted to explore hesitancy in future studies. Fifthly, our focus was on hesitancy toward current domestic vaccines in China, limiting our ability to assess hesitancy toward more effective vaccines against the Omicron variant in the future or those manufactured in other countries. Finally, as public attitudes dynamically change with the development of the COVID-19 epidemic and continuous optimization of vaccination strategies, our cross-sectional study could not capture the trend of participants’ intentions to receive COVID-19 vaccines.

Conclusion

In summary, the alarmingly high prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among Chinese adults calls for immediate action to develop and implement effective communication and intervention strategies after the termination of the zero-COVID policy. After experiencing a surge of infections within a short period, public perceptions of COVID-19 and its vaccines have drastically changed. To develop reasonable and scientific vaccine communication strategies, we should not only summarize vaccination experiences before the end of the zero-COVID policy but also gain a comprehensive understanding of public opinion afterward.
