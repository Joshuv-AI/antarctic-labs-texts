Strategies and challenges for immunisation of children under 5 years in primary health care in different countries: a scoping review
Authors: Camila Amaral Moreno Freitas, Ana Luiza Queiroz Vilasbôas, Nília Maria de Brito Lima Prado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study mapped the strategies used by PHC to immunise children under 5 years of age, as well as identifying challenges in achieving vaccine coverage in accordance with goals set by countries’ national immunisation programmes. This scoping review followed PRISMA-Scr (Scoping review) recommendations. The databases searched for information were PubMed, Science Direct, Embase and VHL (SciELO), from which 352 publications were retrieved and appraised for eligibility. Ultimately, 66 articles were selected for review. The results point to a variety of vaccination strategies used in different countries. Challenges facing PHC in improving vaccination coverage include lack of vaccines in low- and middle-income countries, such as Brazil. In high-income countries, such as the United Kingdom and Canada, remind-recall systems are particularly important. Challenges in the United States include the use of alternative vaccination calendars. This study highlighted important strategies adopted by PHC and the need to meet challenges, especially in middle- and low-income countries, in order to broaden access and improve vaccination coverage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24846-y.

Limitations and potential of this study

The limitations of this study included the large number of countries studied, which makes in-depth, comparative analysis ambitious and difficult. The local scope and a small sample of a few studies may not wholly reflect the immunisation policy in place in a given country. Some studies returned low CASP scores, revealing poor methodological rigour and calling for more careful, judicious analysis of those articles. On the other hand, one potential of the study is the methodological focus on PHC and the analysis of countries’ vaccination-related normative documents, which made it possible to discuss differences and similarities in primary health care vaccination recommendations in each territory.

Background

Immunisation has been acknowledged worldwide to be the main strategy for preventing and controlling immune-preventable diseases [1]. Nonetheless, in recent decades, a series of structural and individual factors, including fragmented health care systems, humanitarian crises, outbreaks and epidemics, vaccine hesitation and missed opportunities for vaccination (MOV1), have contributed to a decline in vaccination coverage in some countries [2, 3].

At the global level, national vaccination coverage estimates are evaluated by comparing estimates from administrative data with those based on population studies [4]. The present scenario shows that the mean levels of vaccination coverage are still far below expectations (the hope is that all vaccines achieve at least 90% coverage), although studies show that global coverage by the third dose of the diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis (DTP) vaccine stands at around 81.6% [4].

A study by Vaccine Coverage Collaborators (2020) found that 94 countries and territories had recorded a decrease in third-dose DTP coverage in children under 1 year old since 2010 [4, 5]. Also, only 11 countries and territories had achieved the national target of the Global Vaccine Action Plan (GVAP), of at least 90% coverage by all vaccines evaluated in 2019 [4, 5].

The confidence in vaccines has changed over the years, among both health care practitioners and the general public [6]. Studies have shown that trust-related attitudes to vaccines suffer from various influences, including cultural, religious, personal and professions beliefs. Other authors [7, 8] have described confidence-related factors among individuals with differing sociodemographic and economic profiles, including vaccine lag among children living in the same district, resulting from the neighbourhood effect [7, 8].

Achieving high, equitable vaccination adhesion in all population groups is not a quick solution; it requires long-term investment in multifaceted interventions informed by target-group research. International agendas, such as the Gavi strategy up to 2025 and the new 2030 Immunisation Agenda concentrate on indicators for vaccination of specific individuals and contexts [9]. Examples of individual determinants are perceptions of risk and mistrust of immunisation. Contextual determinants, meanwhile, include social norms, socioeconomic status and level of education, as well as how health systems are structured and funded [10].

Vaccine hesitation, a growing phenomenon worldwide, has been identified by the World Health Organisation (WHO) as one of the ten greatest threats to public health [11] and plays a critical role in adhesion to the vaccination calendars of national immunisation programmes [12]. As this phenomenon has complex causes, Primary Health Care (PHC) strategies must provide for awareness building as to the benefits of vaccination, perception of the risk of disease, issues of vaccine safety and, most importantly, combating fake news and disinformation [2, 13].

Since Alma-Ata, PHC has become established as national health systems’ main strategy capable, together with other levels of care, of addressing the social and environmental determinants of health. The various factors that affect child vaccination coverage, such as MOV, demonstrate the clear need to strengthen PHC and implement vaccination strategies tailored to territories in order to improve vaccine coverages, with a view to meeting globally-agreed immunisation goals [9, 14]. In that regard, PHC has been the main level at which immunisation activities are carried out in diverse countries around the world [1, 3, 15–18].

Interventions based in PHC contexts can afford better understanding of sociocultural behaviour, as well as helping reinforce communication and social mobilisation and informed, voluntary participation, thus yielding greater public adhesion to vaccination strategies [15, 17].

Examples of adapted national vaccination strategies can be seen in India, where flexible, innovative approaches are used to organise an appropriate monitoring system (with geospatial mapping), which has led to a substantial increase in equitable coverage country-wide [19].

Another operating approach to increasing equitable vaccination coverage is to reduce missed opportunities for vaccination. This can be addressed by integrating actions through PHC staffs in order to monitor all users’ vaccination status continuously. Brazil has applied a strategy titled Rapid Monitoring of Vaccination Coverage (RMVC), which is designed to identify children in the catchment territory with vaccines overdue and to apply them at home. This has proven effective in reducing missed opportunities [20, 21].

In view of the foregoing – and given that, at present, most low- and middle-income countries’ health systems are to a large extent fragmented, meaning a lack of coordination and collaboration among various health programmes that often have different funding and accountability flows – it is necessary to identify to identify immunisation strategies adopted by PHC teams and the challenges for achieving vaccine coverage [22, 23] and for efforts to be made to integrate, by country and by context [22].

In that light, this study mapped the strategies used by PHC to immunise children under 5 years of age, as well as identifying challenges in achieving vaccine coverage in accordance with the goals set by countries’ national immunisation programmes.

Materials and methods

This scoping review regarding the role played by primary health care in national child immunisation programmes followed the recommendations of the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) Checklist and Explanation [24]. The study was registered on the Open Science Framework platform (protocol: https://osf.io/rmepu/).

Eligibility criteria

There was no restriction by geographical location or country where the study was conducted. This review included studies published between 2013 and 2025. The review included qualitative and quantitative, mixed method studies addressing relations between primary health care and the role it plays in child vaccination coverage and national immunisation programmes. Only studies written in English and Brazilian Portuguese were included. Not included were opinion articles, theoretical essays, interventions studies, impact assessment studies, clinical trial studies, vaccine immunogenicity studies, observational vaccine effectiveness studies or studies that did not address the relation between child vaccine coverage and PHC (see Fig. 1).

Information search

The PubMed, VHL (SciELO), Science Direct and Embase electronic databases were searched for information in May 2022. The search terms, identified in MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) and/or DeCS (Health Sciences Descriptors), were: (“primary health care” OR “primary care” OR “primary healthcare”) AND (“vaccinal coverage” OR “Immunization Programs” OR “Immunization Schedule”) AND (Child OR children OR “child health”). The search produced a total of 2638 articles: 521 in Pubmed, 199 in VHL (Scielo), 828 in Science Direct and 1090 in Embase.

Study selection and data extraction

The references were managed in End Note Web. Duplicates were excluded electronically (88) and manually (57), leaving a total of 2493 articles. Of these, 2141 were excluded after titles and abstracts were read; 352 were evaluated for eligibility. In the end, 66 publications (original articles) were selected to form the corpus of this review (Fig. 1).

The selected studies were exported to the Rayyan Qatar Computing Research Institute software to be assessed for eligibility. Two of the authors, working independently, identified and checked the study titles and abstracts. These two authors then assessed the article texts. Any disagreements were resolved by a third specialist author.

Data were extracted from the articles to an Excel spreadsheet (Supplementary Material – Annex 1) contemplating the following variables: number (DOI), study type, author, year, study country and town, objectives, methodology, population, vaccines examined, vaccination barriers and facilitating factors, impact of vaccination on PHC burden of disease and admissions, PHC characteristics in countries studied, strategies to improve vaccination coverage, main findings and study abstract.

Methodological quality assessment

Study methodological quality was appraised using the criteria of the Critical Appraisal Skills Program (CASP) [25] instruments specific to each type of quantitative or qualitative study. Studies were classified into two categories: those meeting at least 9 of the 10 criteria were considered methodologically sound; and those meeting at least 5 of the 10 were considered methodologically moderately sound [25].

Data analysis

After data extraction and organisation by topic, data were analysed on the basis of three analytical dimensions: immunisation strategies contemplating each country’s national immunisation policy guidelines; health education and communication addressing vaccine-related beliefs and perceptions, immunisation-related communication strategies, vaccination recall and reminder delivery systems; and challenges to be met by immunisation programmes, considering the main problems posed for effective immunisation measures and permanent maintenance of vaccination coverage in PHC.

Results

General characterisation of articles included for review

By geographical distribution, the 66 articles originated from 5 continents: 19 from South America (Brazil), 13 from Africa (Nigeria, Kenya, Angola), 7 from Asia (Singapore, China, Japan), 13 from Europe (United Kingdom, France, Spain, Portugal, Greece), 6 from Oceania (Australia and New Zealand) and 8 from North America (USA, Canada), as in Fig. 2. Countries with Universal, public health care systems

This category included Brazil, Spain, Portugal, United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, France and Angola.

Public systems: immunisation strategies

In Brazil, mapped strategies are planned and agreed jointly by the Ministry of Health and state and municipal governments [33]. They consist in routine immunisation for newborns and children in PHC services, rapid monitoring of vaccination coverage [21], specific vaccination campaigns (influenza, poliomyelitis, measles, multi-vaccination), National Vaccination D-Day [15, 34] and home visits by community health workers (CHWs) to provide specific guidance and ascertain current vaccination status [35, 36].

In Brazil, a study of regional health management arrangements, based on Carlos Matus’ conception of planning and World Health Organisation documents, showed that municipal health planning led to better PHC immunisation indicators, including improved training and awareness of immunisation personnel, increased numbers of up-to-date vaccine cards, improved rates of “community health worker visits to search actively for children missing vaccines”, as well as improvement in the indicator “monthly meetings between PHC and health surveillance”, reflecting a significant increase in coverage by vaccines, such as varicella and triple viral, in children under 2 years of age [37].

On the other hand there are specific strategies: in Brazil’s north region, Operation Droplet (Operação Gota) provides immunisation for indigenous and riverside people in hard-to-access geographical areas [33]. A qualitative study [38] show that, in the state of Minas Gerais, the Vaccination in the Palm of Your Hand application for personal use was an effective way of sending out reminders and alerts about vaccine scheduling and/or vaccines behind schedule.

Similarly, in London, the Celebration Card (an intervention that consists in a card celebrating vaccination, sent out by GPs to members of the family before routine vaccination), assists in improving vaccination coverage. That study showed that the Celebration Card strategy was easy to implement and did not call for extra funding, and was effective in strengthening immunisation recall systems [39].

In France, a national study [40] found that the compulsory immunisation strategy and reimbursement of payment for some vaccines fostered adhesion to the country’s vaccination calendar. On the other hand, the study revealed that frequent alterations in the vaccination calendar and the introduction of new vaccines into the child vaccination calendar can influence patient acceptance [40]. In Australia, a study [41] noted that parents consider vaccinating the children against influenza if this is recommended by the family doctor and if the vaccine forms part of the basic immunisation calendar.

As regards immunisation records, a study in New Zealand showed the use of both “Health books” and “National Immunisation Register (NIR)” methods in PHC facilities. The study comparing health book records and the NIR showed high sensitivity and specificity in highlighting the importance of correct records [42].

A study in the United Kingdom using data from Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum, a primary care dataset of electronic health records, showed the importance of CPRD Aurum in supporting immunisation research with reliable data and vaccine coverage estimates similar to official national coverage estimates. The study also showed that immunisation is delivered in different places (PHC, schools) and recommended a “more centralised system for collecting vaccination data in order to bridge the gap between vaccines administered in different setting of care.” [43].

Public systems: health education and communication

In the United Kingdom, a study in three cities with gypsy and Romany populations found mistaken perceptions on the part of parents as to the severity of measles in children: to some members of the community, contracting measles was a “rite of passage” [44]. The study found language barriers, because a translator was needed in order for gypsy and Romany communities in the United Kingdom to access the health system’s immunisation services [44].

In Melbourne, Australia, although parents considered vaccinating their children against influenza, they were found to hold mistaken perceptions such as “thinking that the vaccine may cause flu” or that it was unnecessary, because children needed to build up their own “immunity” [41]. In New Zealand, a study [45] reported low rates of immunisation were associated with families whose natural lifestyles or religious beliefs were at odds with the national immunisation programme.

Studies have also shown mistaken beliefs among health professionals, such as doctors and nurses, as well as a lack of vaccination guidance [46]. Two studies in Brazil found that a lack of appropriate guidance for parents in the vaccination room, when the children are being vaccinated, or a lack of knowledge about vaccines and the reasons for applying them, contributed to vaccine hesitation [35, 47].

In France, a study [40] found that bonding and trust between health professionals and family facilitated adhesion to vaccination, as did wide circulation of information on vaccines, by way of large-scale media campaigns [40]. In Brazil, two studies [35, 36] also stressed the importance of CHW visits and bonding in giving vaccination guidance to parents.

As regards communication about immunisation through the media, a study in Portugal that mapped journalistic coverage of vaccines, found that most of the articles examined were positive in tone (n = 162) or neutral (n = 117) on the subject of vaccines. Nonetheless, 21 negative texts were identified highlighting possible adverse reactions to vaccines, such as: “meningitis vaccine associated with child’s death” [48].

Public systems: challenges facing immunisation programmes

The studies describe a variety of challenges in the countries studied, ranging from parents’ and health professionals’ individual beliefs through to operating problems in immunisation programmes. In Melbourne, Australia, barriers to accepting the influenza vaccine were found to result from concerns over iatrogenic effects of vaccination, the administration timetable and knowledge of the severity of influenza [41].

In Australia, the limited bond between aboriginal communities and PHC services has an impact on vaccination coverage of children, because the parents approach the services only in an emergency. Among the barriers, the study highlighted costs, transport, location, language barriers and waiting time for care. The study also found a deficit of trained vaccination staff [49].

Similarly, the low demand for primary health care by gypsy and Romany communities in rural areas of the United Kingdom was found to pose a challenge for immunisation of children The study described the communities as “coping with immediate stressors”, using emergency services when necessary. It also found that communication with these communities (by letter or telephone) was difficult, because they are highly transitory and change address [44].

In New Zealand, caregivers to refugees reported barriers to accessing immunisation services, including lack of transportation, bureaucratic difficulties, communication challenges (learning English), adjusting to a new country and culture and other challenges [50]. A similar study, also in New Zealand, exploring the perceptions and experiences of ethnically diverse parents accessing health services for their children, found a number of barriers to accessing immunisation and PHC services [51].

A study in Barcelona found that 32.1% of health professionals reported doubts as to whether to delay the administration of certain vaccines or avoid vaccinating their children with at least one of the vaccines of the national basic calendar. The HPV vaccine gave rise to most doubt (21.9%) [46]. Also in Barcelona, another study [52] found that health personnel had doubts about at least one vaccine in the basic calendar: again, it was the HPV vaccine that raised most doubts (40%).

In 2025, an intervention study in Barcelona and Central Catalonia, using the CONFIVAC protocol, was designed to empower and improve primary health care personnel’s knowledge and communication to address vaccine hesitancy in children and to promote immunisation, leading to higher vaccination coverage [53].

In France, potentially dangerous vaccination delays occurred in 47% of children from 0 to 24 months old; risk factors were the child’s increasing age and its having a working mother [54]. Delayed vaccination was also evaluated by the BRISA birth cohort in São Luís do Maranhão, Brazil. The study found that vaccination delays with new and older vaccines were associated with mothers’ lack of education, children belonging to the classes D and E, lack of outpatient and/or hospital care for the child and lack of vaccines in health services [55].

Especially prominent in Brazil are difficulties in using the immunisation programme information system to record the vaccine doses applied. These included inadequate Internet access, lack of qualified personnel and vaccination room staffs’ difficulties in using the system, leading to under-recording of doses applied in the information system [56–58].

In addition, studies of the BRISA cohort, in Ribeirão Preto and São Luís do Maranhão, Brazil, found that delayed vaccination in children was associated with socioeconomic factors, poor maternal schooling, long distances between the residence and health care facility, family with two or more children and not receiving a CHW visit in the prior 30 days [36, 59]. The same factors associated with low vaccination coverage of children under 5 were also identified in the study by Oliveira et al. (2014) [7], in Bom Jesus, Angola.

In Brazil, a study [60] showed that, it took up to 14 visits to family health units in order for some children under 2 years of age to complete the vaccination calendar, far beyond the number of visits recommended by the PNI [60].

Delays were found in multi-dose vaccine coverage in Luís do Maranhão, in Brazil: practitioners’ reluctance to open multi-dose vials for only one or two children had contributed to failure to vaccinate on time [61]. Mello et al. (2020) [34] found low rates of adhesion by parents to vaccination campaigns and to National Vaccination Day, primarily among parents of children in socioeconomic stratum A, who went to the private sector for vaccination. More than 20% of parents who failed to frequent the National Vaccination Day offered rationales that disparaged the vaccination campaign. Two studies also found vaccine shortages at health facilities to be a challenge [35, 47].

Lastly, this review found the Covid-19 pandemic to have had an impact on vaccine coverages in several countries. In Brazil, in comparison with the pre-pandemic period, all vaccines for children under 2 years old fell around 14% short of their vaccination targets [62, 63]. In Canada, the current vaccination coverage rate declined 5.7% early in the pandemic period, with vaccination coverage against chickenpox falling furthest, to 72.9%, while other vaccines dropped to about 85% [64].

A study in Canada found racial disparities in childhood immunisation coverage: overall immunisation coverage was lower than in the USA, while black and indigenous children showed lowest coverage. The study identified factors in the Canadian health care system, such as limited accessibility, barriers impacting immunisation and health care system structure that contribute to these disparities [65]. The study also mentioned challenges in monitoring racial disparities due to limitations in race and ethnicity data.

A nationwide Brazilian study of childhood vaccination against COVID-19 found vaccine hesitation and difficulty in achieving vaccination goals, due to vaccine-related fear and fake news. The study also highlighted the role of PHC personnel in increasing vaccination coverage, given their proximity to people in their territory and the possibility of reverting vaccine-related misinformation [66].

Countries with mixed health care systems

This category included health care systems classified as mixed, that is, featuring a mix of public and private services, in which access to some health services is free of charge and, to others, on direct payment or via health insurance. It comprised the following countries: Greece, Nigeria, Kenya and China.

Mixed systems: vaccination strategies

The vaccination strategies mapped in the studies of Nigeria and Kenya consisted of routine vaccination of children under five years old in clinics and primary care centres, while offering care to the mothers in order to build a bond to the vaccination centres for the children, using remind recall systems to prevent MOV of children, surveillance of acute flaccid paralysis in the under-15s and supplementary poliomyelitis vaccination measures by the Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI) [16, 67].

In states of northern Nigeria, integration of the Global Polio Eradication Initiative with primary health care services, jointly by local government areas, WHO and universities, constitutes an effort to improve child vaccine coverages [68]. Also high-resolution satellites were used for geospatial location of villages inaccessible to health care services [69], as well as household-based micro-planning, by mapping households and enumerating families, so as to optimise resource use and improve the effectiveness of immunisation measures [68].

A study in Patras, Greece, found little vaccine hesitancy; with the main place of immunisation being PHC services and the key figure mentioned as a trusted source of information, the paediatrician [3].

In China, an EPI application proved effective in managing child vaccination by the community medical team of rural Sichuan province. The study found that practitioners saved time by finding information on caregivers and entering into contact to inform them of missed vaccines, record the reason for the delay and plan future doses [70].

Mixed systems: health education and communication

As regards health education and communication, the studies suggested that greater likelihood of children not being vaccinated was associated with lack of schooling among mothers and other relatives [16, 71, 72] and, in northern Nigeria, insufficient knowledge on the part of health care practitioners in carrying out vaccination and, in Kenya, inappropriate guidance for families on the vaccination calendar and 47% erroneous vaccination records [16, 71].

In Nigeria, a study showed that using Mobile Reminders was effective in maintaining timely vaccination in children under 1 year old. The study showed that the proportion of children immunised on time with vaccines administered at 6, 10, and 14 weeks after birth was significantly higher in the intervention group [73].

A Hong Kong study of parents’ perceptions of the chickenpox vaccine found that positive parental attitudes, high economic position and discussion with the family doctor were associated with higher vaccination rates. In China, health practitioners and the government play important roles in promoting vaccination against varicella [74].

A study of primary health care professionals and paediatricians in China showed a need to improve practitioners’ awareness of timely vaccination in children with special healthcare needs (CSHCN) [75].

Mixed systems: challenges facing immunisation programmes

The challenges facing immunisation programmes in the states of northern Nigeria include high percentages of children not having had poliomyelitis or pentavalent vaccinations [64, 76, 77], in addition to difficulties in accessing specific territories under the influence of extremist groups, such as Boko Haram [78], which hinder continuity in the provision of immunisation services. Bawa et al. (2019) [1] signalled difficulties in reaching nomad communities in areas where access was difficult.

The studies also demonstrated high rates of missed opportunities for vaccination (MOV) and challenges in reducing them [16, 71, 78]. Fatiregun et al. (2021) [78] showed that 86.6% of children attending a health facility for growth follow-up appointments had MOV. The causes for MOV included lack of refrigerators for storing vaccines at the health care facility, overloading of facilities and insufficient numbers of health personnel [78]. Health care facilities on state borders distant from state capitals and less well provided with basic health services were also listed as posing operating challenges [76].

In Ibadan, southwest Nigeria, a study comparing the trend in infant vaccination uptake a year before and a year after the onset of COVID- 19, showed a substantial decrease in vaccine coverage, specifically as regards timeliness and completeness, for all vaccines except birth dose vaccines and IPV [79].

In Kenya a study showed lower pentavalent vaccine coverage in children under one year old, because of health facilities’ geographical inaccessibility. Pentavalent coverage was 82.6% among children who lived 30 min away on foot, bicycle or motorised transport, as compared with 62.1% among those who travelled more than 2 h to reach health facilities [80].

One Chinese study in rural areas of Hubei province noted that children living with their parents were more likely to receive complete vaccination (92.7%) than children living with grandparents or other relatives (79.9%) [81].

Countries with private health care systems

This category included countries with private health care systems in which access to health care services occurs by way of out-of-pocket payments or health insurance. The countries included here were Japan, Singapore and United States of America.

Private systems: vaccination strategies

In Japan, vaccination against measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) does not form part of the basic routine calendar, but is provided in private clinics. One study that assessed acceptance of MMR vaccine found that 35.9% of parents did not vaccinate their children with MMR, because it is not a routine vaccine, although 58.8% of parents revealed that they vaccinated their children if the vaccine was provided free of charge [82].

In Washington, in the United States, a cross-sectional study found that about 10% of parents used non-conventional vaccination timetables based on “The vaccine book” by Dr Robert Sears and recommended by some doctors. As a result, they delayed application of vaccines such as chickenpox, hepatitis A and hepatitis B until their child was 18 months old [83].

In New York, the philanthropic intervention Keeping Families Healthy, operated by Stony Brook Children’s Hospital since 2011, in which community health workers’ make home visits to children under two years old to provide clarifications on vaccines, increased the likelihood of complete vaccination in 20.9% of the families visited [84].

Private systems: health education and communication

As regards health education and communication, studies by Hara et al. (2021) [82] pointed to a need to educate parents more about vaccines and demystify mistaken associations about the MMR vaccine in Japan [82].

A study [85] assessed the efficacy of using a vaccination remind-recall system centralised in the public health department in PHC in 15 counties of Colorado, USA. The study polled the primary health teams’ preferences as to vaccination reminders: 79% felt these would be effective if sent to parents by the health department, but 26% of the offices preferred to send them themselves, reasoning that a recall reminder sent by the health department would not be as effective as one sent by the team itself.

A survey of first-time mothers in the state of Georgia, USA, found that although mothers generally have positive perceptions and attitudes regarding vaccination, 63.5% reported not receiving information on child immunisation during antenatal follow-up and that 36% of the mothers reported seeking information on vaccination on the Internet (Google, Yahoo). Among mothers who tended not to vaccinate their children, more than 58% looked for information on immunisation on the Internet [86].

In the USA, a national study by Mergler et al. (2013) [87] found three times greater likelihood of children being vaccinated if their parents and health caregivers held the same positive beliefs with regard to vaccination [87]. On the other hand, the study revealed that parents who believed that a child’s immune system might be weakened by excessive vaccination were 4.6 times more likely to have a primary care provider who shared the same belief. One of the strongest associations involved the belief that healthy children do not need vaccines: it was approximately 12 times more likely parents would hold that belief if the service provider believed the same, as compared with parents whose service provider did not share the same belief [87].

Private systems: challenges facing immunisation programmes

Numerous challenges persist for appropriate vaccine coverage to be achieved. Among these, the COVID-19 pandemic had a significant effect on vaccination coverage against measles, mumps and rubella in children under two years old during 2020, in Singapore [88].

The beliefs of child health care providers and parents, including those regarding vaccine safety, are similar and health care providers’ beliefs can contribute to parents’ decisions to accept, delay or refuse vaccines. Parents may choose to select providers who hold beliefs similar to their own [87].

Kempe et al. (2017) [89] found that family doctors and PHC paediatricians had difficulty using computerised immunisation information systems. Although the paediatricians showed better understanding of how to use the system, both categories reported barriers to use, including lack of time, difficulty in entering information and concern over the system’s not capturing data on immunisations outside the region or state [89].

In Japan the costs of vaccines and the need for fees constitute a barrier to access some childhood vaccines. Public submissions for vaccines against haemphilus influenzae type B, meningitis and HPV have already existed since 2011, but some APS professionals still do not know [90].

Discusssion

Examination of the selected studies revealed countries’ differing immunisation policies, as well as the numerous challenges facing PHC in the context of child vaccination. Generally speaking, in the group of countries with universal, public health care systems, the articles reported a basic vaccination calendar recommended by the WHO, Ministries of Health and PHC health care practitioners. In these countries, vaccines are offered universally and free of charge [15, 41, 44, 46, 54].

With the exception of Angola, the official websites of the countries’ health ministries provided national and institutional normative documents guiding the immunisation policy. In addition, the studies showed that, in the countries with universal health care systems, children under 5 years of age were vaccinated primarily in public PHC services [15, 41, 44, 46, 54].

In the countries with universal health care systems, vaccination strategies are connected with PHC, with vaccines being offered regularly by primary care services, recommended by practitioners and applied as part of PHC. However, studies in Brazil and Angola found difficulties in achieving vaccination coverage of children under 5 years old [7, 47]. Also, a qualitative study [41] showed that gypsy and Romany communities met language barriers to accessing PHC services in the United Kingdom. In New Zealand, authors [49] found a need for PHC to be made more flexible and better suited to the country’s needs. Another three studies from New Zealand and Canada found that children of ethnic and racial minorities and refugees encountered barriers to accessing immunisation services, pointing to the need for more equitable access and care [50, 51, 65, 91].

In countries with mixed or private health care systems, the studies showed heterogeneous vaccination strategies and recommendations: in Washington, USA, for example, alternative vaccination calendars are recommended, entailing delays or omissions in children’s vaccination [83]. In Japan, access to vaccination is gained by subscribing to health insurance. By law, all residents in Japan (including foreigners with a residence card) must be registered with a health insurance programme in order to gain access to vaccination services [82]. In Greece, despite the high level of child vaccination coverage and some vaccines being provided free of charge, vaccination is not mandatory, only recommended [3].

Vaccination strategies in countries with mixed or private systems were not totally connected into PHC: in Nigeria, for example, in spite of efforts at connected, some immunisation activities occurred in isolation [68]. In Japan, vaccination strategies were distributed between secondary services, such as hospitals, and primary care clinics [82]. In the USA, the heterogeneity of immunisation strategies and recommendations pointed to a lack of immunisation activities with PHC.

One issue that has to be examined here is that some middle- and low-income countries, such as Angola, Brazil and Nigeria, share some common problems. The studies showed that, in these three countries, lack of follow-up of children in mother-and-child programmes, the lack of vaccines in some health care facilities in Brazil, precarious infrastructure (lack of refrigerators for storing vaccines), socioeconomic factors, such as mothers’ lack of schooling, and neighbourhood effects, in addition to long distances to health care facilities, all pose important challenges for efforts to reduce missed opportunities for vaccination [7, 16, 36, 47, 67, 80].

In Brazil, for example, although the country’s health care system is equal to those of high-income countries, such as the United Kingdom, “Brazil operates in a context of significant socioeconomic and geographical inequalities, which pose an additional challenge in comparing the Brazilian model of PHC with those of other countries”.

In Nigeria, specifically, the studies found weak health care service coordination among the three levels of the health system, duplication of responsibilities and weakening of primary health care. Authors [93] argued that, in several countries, lack of good governance of national immunisation programmes constitutes an important barrier to their implementation. A study that assessed the impact of PHC in child mortality among the under-fives, indicated that the availability and quality of infrastructure and human resources at vaccination services have direct impact on mortality of children under five years of age.

Also, remaining gaps in service coverage reflect a multiplicity of underlying reasons that, in some contexts, may be attributed to a weakened PHC model and a deficit in primary care service integration [22].

It is important to discuss the need for immunisation programmes to take an connected, horizontal approach based on the recommendations of international organisations such as the WHO and Immunisation Agenda 2030 [22]. The IA 2030 stresses the importance of adapting immunisation strategies to the context in each country, while noting four key points: community involvement in planning and adapting community-focussed immunisation activities; a comprehensive, inclusive approach; improved coordination, management and inter-sector partnering on immunisation programmes; as well as the incorporation of all immunisation strategies with PHC services, by means of professional qualification with a view to expanding access to connected vaccination services [22].

It is important to achieve the recommended horizontality in order to strengthen immunisation programmes, especially in the current context of growing vaccine hesitation and vaccine disinformation, particularly after implementation of the COVID-19 vaccine. The document guiding IA Agenda 2030 also signals the importance of strengthened, robust immunisation programmes in order to reach all children and guarantee equitable vaccination for all children and individuals over their life course [94].

Final remarks

This review showed that, although all the countries considered had a calendar of recommended child vaccination, disparities were found among countries in their vaccination recommendations and access and the health care systems examined, as well as, in some contexts, a lack of integration of immunisation with PHC services. This study highlights the importance of guaranteeing structured, effective national immunisation programmes, with investments in quality PHC.
