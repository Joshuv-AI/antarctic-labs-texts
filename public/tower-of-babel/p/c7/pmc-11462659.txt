Genome-wide analysis of the WRKY gene family and their response to low-temperature stress in elephant grass
Authors: Chunli Mao, Jian Zhang, Yaning Zhang, Bixian Wang, Weihang Li, Xiaoshan Wang, Linkai Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroudElephant grass (Cenchrus purpureus) is a perennial forage grass characterized by tall plants, high biomass and wide adaptability. Low-temperature stress severely limits elephant grass biomass and geographic distribution. WRKY is one of the largest families of plant-specific transcription factors and plays important roles in plant resistance to low-temperature. However, the understanding of the WRKY family in grasses is limited. Background

Elephant grass (Cenchrus purpureus), also known as napier grass, is the well-known C4 perennial forage grass as a forage crop, which grows rapidly (in a warm climate it can be harvested four times a year) and has an upright height of about 3–5 m [1, 2]. Elephant grass is the most productive perennial warm season grass, with annual biomass ranging from 12 to 150 Mg ha− 1, which is generally grown as a high quality, high yielding forage for beef and dairy cattle [3, 4]. It was reported that dry matter accumulation of elephant grass from the first cut ranges from 20.3 to 32 Mg ha− 1, even when planted on soils with low fertility and low plant nitrogen availability [5]. Elephant grass is characterized by high biomass, low nutrient requirements and strong resistance to drought [6] and heat stress [7], making it a promising option for high-quality forage. However, it is sensitive to low temperatures, which severely limiting its application and distribution. Currently research on elephant grass focuses on energy grass utilization [8–10], anthocyanin synthesis [11] and interspecific elongation [12]. The molecular mechanism and key genes involved in its response to low temperature stress remain largely unexplored.

Low temperature stress severely limits plant growth and geographic distribution [13]. To deal with low temperature stress, plants have produced a number of adaptive mechanisms at molecular, cellular, physiological and biochemical levels [14]. Here, transcription factors (TFs), including the WRKY family, are also involved in these regulatory mechanisms. The WRKY family is the largest class of TFs unique to higher plants [15]. WRKY TFs are a class of DNA-binding proteins that regulate plant growth, development and stress response through cooperative interactions with different genes and other TFs. The N-terminus of WRKY has a highly conserved WRKYGQK sequence. Based on the number of WRKY structural domains and the characteristics of the zinc-finger structure (CX4-5CX22-23HXH or CX7CX23HXH) of WRKY, WRKY TFs are classified into three major groups [16, 17]. Many studies have found that WRKY TFs are involved in the regulation of low temperature response in plants. It has been found that CsWRKY46 in cucumber (Cucumis sativus) [18], OsWRKY71 in rice (Oryza sativa) [19], VvWRKY28 in grape (Vitis Vinifera) [20], PmWRKY57 in Prunus mume [21], SlWRKY50 in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) [22] and ShWRKY55 in wild tomato (Solanum habrochaites) [23] are associated with enhanced cold tolerance. Therefore, we hypothesize that WRKY genes regulating cold tolerance may also be present in elephant grass.

The publication of the elephant grass genome established the foundation for exploring low-temperature-tolerant gene resources and analyzing the regulatory mechanism of key low-temperature-tolerant genes. This is of great significance for cultivating low-temperature-tolerant elephant grass and other plants. The WRKY gene family has been analyzed in many species, including red clover (Trifolium pratense) [24], white clover (Trifolium repens) [25], alfalfa (Medicago sativa) [26] and pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum) [27]. These studies have noted that WRKY genes plays an important role in abiotic stresses, yet little is known about the function of WRKY genes in elephant grass, particularly in response to low temperature stress. In this study, we identified the WRKY family members in elephant grass at the genomic level and analyzed their physicochemical properties, chromosomal distribution, cis-acting elements, gene structures, conserved motifs and phylogenetic relations. Additionally, we investigated the expression levels of CpWRKY family members in different tissues and under low-temperature stress. This revealed key WRKY genes involved in the root system’s response to low-temperature stress and provides a theoretical basis for futher in-depth studies on the molecular mechanism of cold tolerance of WRKYs of elephant grass.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and cold stress treatment

The elephant grass (C. purpureus. ‘CN002’) accessions were collected from Chongzhou, Sichuan, China. To analyze the expression of CpWRKY genes in different tissues, we collected roots, stems, leaves, and stem tips from non-flowering elephant grass growing in the Chongzhou field. Three replicates were taken for each sample. The stems of the accessions were grown hydroponically in a growth chamber at 26 °C (12 h light)/23°C (12 h night) for 10 d with nutrient solution. The uniformly grown material was then divided into two equal parts: one part was still kept in the growth chamber as a control (CK), while the other part was transferred to a 4℃/4℃ (12 h light/12 h night) incubator for cold stress (CS) treatment. Roots of elephant grass were collected at 3, 6, 12, 24, 36 and 48 h from both CK and CS treatments. Each treatment collected triplicate samples at each time point.

Identification of WRKY in Cenchrus purpureus

The Cenchrus purpureus genome was used to identify WRKY genes [11]. The elephant grass genome sequence was download from the Milletdb [28]. Download the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) file of WRKY (PF03106) and use the HMMER3.0 program from TBtools (v2.069) to identify CpWRKYs [27]. The identified genes were then verified using NCBI CD-search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) to confirm their conserved structural domains (E-value < 0.01). Plant-mPLoc (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/, accessed on March 5, 2024) was used to predict the subcellular localization of CpWRKYs.

Phylogenetic analysis of the CpWRKYs

The amino acid sequence of Arabidopsis WRKY was accessed from the plantTFDB online database (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/index.php) ClusterW was used to analyze the multiprotein sequence alignment of WRKYs from elephant grass, rice and Arabidopsis. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using the neighbor-joining method with a bootstrap value of 1000, employing TreeBeST software. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and refined using iTOL online tool (https://itol.embl.de/)

Chromosome location, synteny and gene duplication analysis

The chromosomal locations of CpWRKY genes were analyzed by downloading the genome annotation file (gff file) for Cenchrus purpureus from the Milletdb website [28]. All CpWRKYs were localized to the chromosomes of elephant grass using Tbtools software, then, to determine the collinearity of CpWRKY genes, we used TBtools with MCScanX to analyze their duplication events [29]. We used Dual Systeny Plotter software to elucidate Cenchrus purpureus synteny relationship with Arabidopsis thaliana, O. sativa, sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), JUJUNCAO (Cenchrus fungigraminus) and six pearl millet (P. glaucum). To assess the evolutionary selection pressure on the CpWRKY gene family, the ratio of synonymous to non-synonymous mutations (Ka/Ks) between gene pairs was calculated using TBtools [29].

Gene structure, conserved motif and cis- regulatory elements analysis of CpWRKYs

CpWRKYs motifs were identified using the Simple MEME Wrapper in TBtools. Cis-regulatory elements within the 2000 bp promoter region upstream of the start codon of all CpWRKY genes were analyzed using the PlantCARE website. The exon-intron structure, conserved motifs and cis-acting elements were visualized using TBtools [29].

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

The HiPure Plant RNA Mini Kit (Magen, China) was used to extract the total RNA from different tissues of elephant grass and samples under low temperature stress. The cDNA was obtained by using HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer’s instruction. The expression of CpWRKY genes in three biological replicates and three technical replicates of the samples was analyzed using elongation factor-1-alpha (EF1α) as the internal reference gene [7]. RT-qPCR was performed using the CFX96 RT-qPCR system and 2X M5 HiPer SYBR Premix EsTaq (with Tli RNaseH), following the standard protocol provided with the kit. The expression levels of CpWRKY were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method. RT-qPCR primers were designed using Primer Premier 6.0 software, and the sequences of these primers are provided in Table S7.

Expression of genes in different tissues and under low temperature stress

The expression levels of CpWRKY genes in different tissues (stem, flower, root, leaf and stem tip) were obtained from the Milletdb database [28]. Meanwhile, the expression of CpWRKYs genes was analyzed in the root system of elephant grasses (‘Chuanxu NO.4’) under low-temperature stress (accession numbers PRJNA1007572, data unpublished). The log2 transformed Transcripts Per Kilobase Million fragments (TPM) mapped values of the WRKY genes were calculated. We used the genome of purple elephant grass as a reference for the analysis [11]. A new RNA sequencing quantification method was established using kallisto software [30]. Additionally, we used an R package named sleuth in conjunction with kallisto to identify differentially expressed genes [31]. Heatmaps were generated using the TBtools [29].

Amplification of CpWRKY81

Roots are essential for the overwintering of perennial forage grasses. CpWRKY81 was significantly upregulated by low temperatures in the root of elephant grass and contains a root-specific motif in its promoter region. Additionally, CpWRKY81 expression in the roots was found to be higher than in the leaves. Therefore, we are interested in investigating the role of this gene in response to low-temperature stress. The coding sequences of CpWRKY81 were amplified from cDNA using the following primers (forward 5’-ATGACGATGGACACCCCGG-3’, reverse 5’-CTACTGAAACGTAGCAGTAGCAATGTGATGG-3’). The sequences were cloned into the vector of pYES2.

Low-temperature tolerance assays of yeast transformants

The transgenic yeast low-temperature tolerance function was verified with the method of Xiang et al. [32], in briefly, the recombinant pYES2-CpWRKY81 and pYES2 empty plasmid were used for transformation of Saccharomyces cerevisiae INVSc1 (Weidi, China) with manufacturer’s instructions. The yeast cells of INVSc1 (pYES2-CpWRKY81) and INVSc1 (pYES2) were independently cultured in SG-Ura (0.67% Yeast nitrogen base containing ammonia sulfate, 0.077% DO Supplement-Ura and 2% galactose) liquid medium at 30℃ for 36 h with shaking (200 rpm/s). The solution was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, and then both yeast cells were adjusted to the same density (OD600 = 0.8) with SG-Ura liquid medium. To assess the low-temperature stress tolerance of the yeast transformants, the samples were incubated at 4 °C for 24 h and then subjected to serial dilution. Next, 5 μl of yeast cell dilution was spotted on SG-Ura medium and incubated at 30 °C to observe the yeast growth rate.

Results

CpWRKYs gene identification

A total of 176 putative CpWRKYs were identified using HMM characterization of WRKY genes in the elephant grass genome. CpWRKYs were named CpWRKY1 to CpWRKY176 based on their location on the chromosome. Additionally, the main properties of CpWRKY proteins including protein size, isoelectric point (pI), molecular weight (MW) and subcellular localization were summarized in Table S1. The protein size ranged from 99 amino acids (CpWRKY6) to 1602 amino acids (CpWRKY29). The molecular weights of these proteins vary between 11.32 kDa (CpWRKY140) and 178.27 kDa (CpWRKY142). Moreover, the CpWRKY proteins exhibited a theoretical isoelectric point ranging from 4.58 (CpWRKY21) to 11.38 (CpWRKY121), with 53% CpWRKY being acidic (pI < 7.0). The average number of amino acids, molecular weight, and pI of the CpWRKY proteins in elephant grass were 355, 38.20 kDa, and 7.46, respectively. The median amino acid number, molecular weight and pI of elephant grass CpWRKY were 321, 34 kDa and 6.75, respectively. It was found that almost all CpWRKYs proteins were localized in the nucleus, except for CpWRKY30 (Cell membrane), CpWRKY97 (Cytoplasm) and CpWRKY109 (Chloroplast) (Table S1).

Chromosomal distribution, duplication and synteny analysis of CpWRKYs

The chromosomal localization of CpWRKY genes was mapped using data from the elephant grass genome. It was found that 172 genes were unevenly distributed across 14 chromosomes, while 4 genes could not be anchored to any chromosome (Fig. 1A). The largest number of CpWRKYs (25) was found on chromosome 9, whereas the smallest number of CpWRKYs (3) was found on chromosome 14. A correlation was found between chromosome length and the number of CpWRKY genes, with longer chromosomes appearing to have a higher number of CpWRKY genes.

Chromosomal locations (A) and duplication events (B) of elephant grass WRKY genes

MCScanX was used to analyze gene duplication patterns (tandem and segmental duplication). In the CpWRKY gene family 169 pairs of segmental duplication events involving 138 CpWRKYs were observed (Fig. 1B). However, no tandem duplicated were found, suggesting that these gene clusters were formed as a result of gene segmental duplication. To investigate the selective constraints on CpWRKY duplicated genes, we calculated the nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka) and the synonymous substitution rate (Ks) for the duplicated genes, and determined their ratio (Ka/Ks) for analysis. The Ka/Ks ratio of CpWRKY segmental duplication ranged from 0.07 to 1.07. Six CpWRKY codominant pairs (CpWRKY18;CpWRKY83), (CpWRKY45;CpWRKY46), (CpWRKY33;CpWRKY85); (CpWRKY34;CpWRKY83); (CpWRKY32;CpWRKY117) and (CpWRKY33;CpWRKY137) had unavailable Ka/Ks values, and the remaining codominant pairs of CpWRKYs showed Ka/Ks ratios of less than 1, except for the pair (CpWRKY12;CpWRKY113), which had a Ka/Ks ratio of 1.07. This suggests that most CpWRKY genes were subjected to purifying selection (Table S2).

To further understand the evolutionary relationships among WRKY members in different plant species, we characterized the homologous relationship between CpWRKY and homologs from other species, including A. thaliana, O. sativa, S. bicolor, C. fungigraminus and six P. glaucum. The smallest number of co-linear gene pairs (17 pairs) was found between C. purpureus and A. thaliana, while the largest number of gene pairs (411 pairs) was found between C. purpureus and C. fungigraminus, with 197, 196, 180, 200, 195, 191, 190 and 213 colinear pairs with O. sativa, S. bicolor, P. glaucum (PI537069), P. glaucum (PI521612), P. glaucum (PI526529), P. glaucum (PI583800), P. glaucum (PI587025) and P. glaucum (Tifleaf3 ), respectively (Fig. 2, Table S6). It was observed that co-linear genes clustered into the same subgroups, indicating sequence similarity among them (Fig. S1).

Phlogenetic analysis of CpWRKY genes

To explore the classification and evolution of CpWRKY proteins, a phylogenetic tree was constructed 346 WRKYs protein sequences, including 176 elephant grass CpWRKYs, 98 rice OsWRKYs and 72 A.thaliana AtWRKYs. These CpWRKY proteins were classified into three major groups, of which group II was further divided into five subgroups (Fig. 3). Group II was divided into five subgroups, among which IIa and IIb were grouped in the same clade, IId and IIe were grouped in the same clade, and IIc was divided into three subclades. Group IIc was very close to Group I, in which CpWRKY43 and CpWRKY66 is even classified in Group I (Fig. 3). The same pattern was observed in the phylogenetic tree constructed from WRKY proteins of 10 species (Fig. S1). Interestingly, we observed complex variants with the group IIc subgroup of CpWRKY. Some CpWRKY proteins (CpWRKY10, CpWRKY94, CpWRKY109, CpWRKY110, CpWRKY14, CpWRKY79, CpWRKY52, CpWRKY119, CpWRKY135 and CpWRKY 87) lacked the C2H2 zinc finger protein, while others (CpWRKY41, CpWRKY99, CpWRKY163, CpWRKY115 and CpWRKY133) contained incomplete WRKY domains. In total, there were 12 CpWRKYs in group I, 80 CpWRKYs in group III, and 8, 19, 27, 12, and 18 CpWRKYs in group IIa, Group IIb, Group IIc, Group IId and Group IIe, respectively (Fig. 3).

Conserved motifs and gene structural analysis of CpWRKYs

The diversity of gene structures can indicate the evolution of gene families. By combining phylogenetic tree analysis and motifs analysis, we classified the CpWRKY genes into four major groups (group I, II, III and IV). Notably, group IV included members from both group IIc and group III (Fig. 4). The motifs in the CpWRKY genes were consistent within the same groups, with the exception of Group IV, which lacked distinct motif characteristics. This variability may be attributed to the presence of incomplete WRKY domains or zinc finger proteins among some members of Group IIc and Group III. Motif 1, 2, 3 and 4 was found in almost all CpWRKYs proteins. The group I included motif 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7, a number of group I CpWRKYs proteins contained motif 6 (Fig. 4). The group IIa had the same motif 1, 5 and 9 (Fig. 4). The group IIb had the same motif 1, 2 and 10, where motif 10 was unique to group IIb (Fig. 4). Group IIc is characterized by complex motifs, with some group IIc CpWRKYs (CpWRKY10, CpWRKY94, CpWRKY109, CpWRKY110, CpWRKY14, CpWRKY79, CpWRKY52, CpWRKY119, CpWRKY87 and CpWRKY135) only contained motif 7, some CpWRKYs (CpWRKY42, CpWRKY66, CpWRKY100, CpWRKY101 and CpWRKY146) only contained motif 6 and motif 7, and the remainder contained common motifs 2, 3 and 4 (Fig. 4). The group IId had the same motif 2, 3, 4 and 8 (Fig. 4). The group IIe had the same motif 1, 2, 3, 4 and 8 (Fig. 4). The group III had the same motif 1, 2, 3 and 4 (Fig. 4). The CpWRKYs gene family contain 1–9 exons and 0–8 introns (Fig. 4). There were 41% (72/176) CpWRKYs gene have 3 exons and 2 introns.

Cis-acting elements analysis of CpWRKYs

The PlantCARE website was used to analyze the cis-acting elements of the 2.0 kb sequence upstream of each CpWRKY gene start codon (ATG) to derive the functions of CpWRKYs. From which 10 representative cis-acting elements were selected for functional analysis (Fig. 5 and Table S3). Cis-acting elements can be categorized into three main classes based on their regulatory functions: plant growth/development, phytohormonal responses, and biotic/abiotic stress responses. A class of plant growth and development cis-acting elements, motif I (GGTACGTGGCG), is present in the promoter region and belongs to the root-specific regulatory elements. There were five CpWRKYs (CpWRKY14, CpWRKY81, CpWRKY113, CpWRKY114 and CpWRKY145) containing motif I (Fig. 5). Phytohormone-responsive cis-acting elements including ABRE, AuxRR-core, CGTCA-motif, CARE-motif, O2-site, P-box, SARE, TATC-box, TCA-element, TGA-element and TGACG-motif (Fig. 5). It was found that 91% (160/176) of CpWRKYs involved in MeJA-responsive (CGTCA-motif and TGACG-motif). In the elephant grass CpWRKY gene family, there were 80% (157 /176) of the genes contain the ABRE motif, which is involved in the response to ABA (Fig. 5). Biotic/abiotic stress response elements such as ARE, GC-motif, LTR and TC-rich repeat sequences, which are related to anaerobic induction, cold stress, defense and stress response, respectively (Fig. 5). Based on the types of cis-acting elements, we found that 157, 89, and 51 CpWRKYs in the elephant grass were involved in anaerobic stress, low-temperature stress, and defense and stress responses, respectively. As a whole, CpWRKYs may be involved in root growth, hormone response, and stress response in elephant grass.

Gene expression analysis of CpWRKYs

Expression analysis of the CpWRKYs under low-temperature stress

Using Illumina RNA sequencing data (unpublished data), we analyzed the expression levels of CpWRKY genes in the root systems of (CX NO.4) under low-temperature stress. It was found that 63 CpWRKY genes were differentially expressed at least at one time point elephant grass under low-temperature conditions (Table S4), and more than half (34/63) of these genes belonged to group III. It indicated that the CpWRKY genes in group III may play an important role in low-temperature stress.

Expression analysis of the CpWRKYs in different tissues and under cold stress

Expression patterns of CpWRKYs in different tissues were analyzed using transcriptomics data of elephant grass (Cenchrus purpureus cv. Purple) in the Milletdb database. Almost all genes were expressed in leaves, except CpWRKY6, CpWRKY9, CpWRK68, CpWRKY83, CpWRKY124, CpWRKY132, CpWRKY139, CpWRKY142, CpWRKY148, and CpWRKY160 (Table S5). Since the roots system is critical for overwintering of perennial forage grasses, we focused on analyzing the expression of CpWRKY genes in the roots of elephant grasses. We found that 25 CpWRKY genes were expressed at higher levels in roots compare to leaves (Table S5). These genes might be the potential key genes for root response to low-temperature stress. To further characterize the tissue-specific gene expression patterns of CpWRKY genes, we analyzed their expression in four tissues (root, stem, leaf and stem tip) of ‘CN002’ elephant grass using RT-qPCR. The results showed that the expression of most of these 10 genes was higher in the roots compared to the leaves, with the exceptions of CpWRKY100 and CpWRKY115 (Fig. 6A and J). However, RNA-seq data revealed that the expression of these genes was generally higher in leaves than in roots, with only CpWRKY100, CpWRKY114 and CpWRKY81 showing higher expression in roots (Table S4). This discrepancy between the RT-qPCR and RNA-seq data might be attributed to differences in the elephant grass varieties used in the experiments. Combined with the expression data of CpWRKY genes in low-temperature stress, we identified six CpWRKY genes (CpWRKY51, CpWRKY81, CpWRKY100, CpWRKY101, CpWRKY140 and CpWRKY143) that exhibited higher expression in roots compare to leaves and were induced by low-temperature stress (Fig. 7, Table S4 and Table S5). We conducted RT-qPCR in ‘CN002’ elephant grass, selecting 10 CpWRKY genes that had shown induction at least at one time point during low-temperature stress. Consistent with the RNA-seq data, the RT-qPCR results also demonstrated that these genes were induced by low temperatures (Fig. 6K and T). Among them, the expression level of CpWRKY81 in the roots was consistently higher than in the leaves, and it was induced under cold stress. Additionally, the CpWRKY81 promoter contains root-specific cis-elements motif I (Fig. 5). We hypothesize that CpWRKY81 might be an important gene for the perennial elephant grass roots response to cold stress.

Ectopic expression of CpWRKY81 in yeast enhanced cold tolerance

To characterize the role of CpWRKY81 in low-temperature tolerance. We generated CpWRKY81-overexpressing yeast cells. INVSc1 (pYES2-CpWRKY81) and control INVSc1 (pYES2) were grown similarly under unstressed conditions (Fig. 8A), showing no significant difference in growth. However, under low-temperature stress, yeast INVSc1 (pYES2-CpWRKY81) exhibited better growth compare to the control INVSc1 (pYES2). Notably, as the dilution rations increased, the growth difference between CpWRKY81-overexpressing yeast cells and the control became more pronounced (Fig. 8B). It was demonstrated that overexpression of CpWRKY81 significantly increased the ability of yeast cells to resist low-temperature stress (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Elephant grass is an important C4 forage grass, which is now mainly cultivated in southern china and under-resourced barren land due to its high biomass and strong heat-resistant ability [33–35]. Although it is a promising forage, the regulatory roles of TF families under stress conditions have not been well understood, especially those TFs in response to low-temperature stress. WRKY is one of the largest families of TFs in plants, playing a crucial role in plant growth and development, as well as defense and stress responses [36]. A total of 176 CpWRKY genes were identified in this study (Table S1), which is more than the number found in Arabidopsis (72), rice (101) [37], white clover (145) [25], red clover (59) [24], alfalfa (91) [26] and pear millet (97) [27]. This reflects that CpWRKY genes have undergone expansion in elephant grass. with segmental duplication and tandem duplication contributing to the emergence of novel genes that likely assume new functions during evolution [29, 38, 39]. A total of 169 pairs of segmental duplication events involving 138 CpWRKY genes were identified in elephant grass, whereas no tandem duplication events were observed (Fig. 1B). This suggests that the expansion of CpWRKY genes stems mainly from segmental duplication. Ka/Ks analysis of the 169 identified homologous gene pairs revealed that nearly all (168/169) members of the CpWRKY gene family have undergone purifying selection, suggesting that the CpWRKY gene family has evolved to be highly conserved. However, the Ka/Ks ratio for 1 homologous genes pair (CpWRKY12;CpWRKY113) was greater than 1 (Table S2), indicating that this pair has been subjected to positive selection. In addition, we analyzed the co-linear relationships between elephant grass WRKYs and four monocotyledonous plants (O. sativa, S. bicolor, C. fungigraminus and P. glaucum) and one dicotyledonous plant (A. thaliana). These results indicate that C. purpureus shares more genomic linkages with other monocotyledonous plants compared to Arabidopsis thaliana, suggesting a possible evolutionary relationship among them (Fig. 2). The elephant grass WRKY family was found to have stronger co-linearity with monocotyledonous plants and weaker co-linearity with dicotyledonous plants. This phenomenon has also been observed in Miscanthus sinensis [40]. In contrast, analysis of the MADS gene family in the dicotyledonous plant sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides ssp. sinensis) revealed that its MADS genes exhibited stronger collinearity with those of other dicotyledonous plants, such as jujube (Ziziphus jujuba) and large-fruited jujube (Elaeagnus moorcroftii wall) [41]. These results suggested evolutionary differences between dicotyledons and monocotyledons. This may be related to the long process of natural selection and evolution in angiosperms that produced the classification of monocotyledons and dicotyledons.

WRKY TFs were classified into three groups, group I contained two WRKY conserved structural domains and Cx4-5Cx22-23HxH zinc-finger motif, group II contained one WRKY conserved structural domain and one Cx4-5Cx22-23HxH zinc-finger motif, and group III contained one WRKY conserved structural domain and the Cx7Cx23HxC zinc-finger motifs, where group II can be further classified into five subgroups based on their phylogenetic branching and assembly (IIa-IIe) [42]. Group I was further divided into two subgroups, a subgroup Ia containing C2H2 zinc fingers and a subgroup Ib containing C2HC zinc fingers [43]. In the elephant grass WRKY gene family, all group I proteins contained only the C2H2 zinc fingers (Table S1). In pear millet, WRKY TFs were divided into three groups, and there were 9, 47 and 29 WRKYs in groups I, II and III, respectively, of which group II has the largest number of WRKY genes [27]. The most abundant WRKY genes in Group II were also observed in zucchini [44], apricot (Prunus sibirica) [45] and pea (Pisum sativum) [46]. In elephant grass, CpWRKY genes were similarly categorized into 3 groups in elephant grass (Fig. 3), with group II containing the highest number of CpWRKY proteins (84), while the number of genes in Group III (80) was slightly less than that in Group II. We hypothesized that the expansion of the CpWRKY genes might be due to segmental duplication of the genes in Groups II and III, where 65 and 59 genes in Groups II and III, respectively, were segmental duplicated.

Similar to other plants, most of the CpWRKY proteins were characterized by WRKYGQK domain. However, variants were found in elephant grass CpWRKY genes such as WRKYGEK, WKKYGQK, WRKYGKK, WLKYGEK and WRKYGEK (Table S1). WRKYGEK was present in 19 group III CpWRKY proteins (CpWRKY6, 7. 8, 19, 20, 32, 33, 34, 39, 72, 106, 107, 7, 121, 123, 127, 128, 129 and 161). WKKYGQK was present in seven group II CpWRKY proteins (CpWRKY42, 57, 58, 100, 101,153 and 154) and four group III CpWRKY proteins (CpWRKY9, 81, 108 and 140). WRKYGKK was found in group IIc CpWRKY protein. It has been reported that in soybean, WRKYGKK has lost its binding ability to the W-box [47]. Whether this also applied to CpWRKY94 in elephant grass requires further invistigation. Interestingly, we identified the WLKYGEK domian in the CpWRKY29 protein of group III. This domain has not been reported previously, and it is unknown whether this variant affects the function of the CpWRKY29 gene.

The diversification of WRKY genes into three groups is the result of long-term evolution. Our results found that group IIa and IIb clustered together in a clade, and group IId and IIe formed a clade. This observation consistent with previous suggestion to combine subgroup IIa and IIb into a subgroup, and subgroups IId and IIe into another [48]. Group IIc was further divided into three clades, two of which were close to Group I and another closed to group IIa (Fig. 3). The WRKY genes in group IIc were the most diverse, with 5 IIc CpWRKY genes (CpWRKY41, CpWRKY99, CpWRKY163, CpWRKY115 and CpWRKY133) contained incomplete WRKY domain and 10 IIc CpWRKY genes (CpWRKY10, CpWRKY94, CpWRKY109, CpWRKY110, CpWRKY14, CpWRKY79, CpWRKY52, CpWRKY119, CpWRKY135 and CpWRKY 87) did not contain C2H2 zinc finger proteins domain, each of which were clustered in a subclade, the remaining group IIc CpWRKY genes were clustered into the third subclade (Fig. 3). This divergence supported the hypothesis that the ancient IIc WRKY genes are the ancestor of all WRKY genes in wild rice (Oryza nivara) [43]. This hypothesis indicated that the ancient subgroup IIc WRKY gene was the ancestor of all WRKY genes, and the WRKY gene can be derived from group IV by loss of part of the WRKY domain [43]. Some CpWRKY genes lost part of their WRKY domain during evolution were also observed in elephant grass, but they were not clustered in a subgroup and were distributed in groups IIa, IIc, IId, IIe and III (Table S1). It is necessary to carry out further studies to verify the evolutionary relationship between them.

TFs activate or repress the transcription of downstream genes by specifically binding to their promoter regions of genes, thus playing a key role in signal transduction and the induction of downstream functional gene expression [49]. The promoter regions of CpWRKYs contain many cis-elements with a variety of potential regulatory functions, including plant growth, hormone response, and stress response (Table S3). Plant roots are crucial for maintaining the water and nutrient up-taken, as well as providing structural support essential for plant growth and survival. Root systems make an important contribution to the successful overwintering of perennial forages. We found that five CpWRKYs (CpWRKY14, CpWRKY81, CpWRKY113, CpWRKY114 and CpWRKY145) contained the root-specific response element motif I, and these genes may be important for root growth and development. It has been shown that the expression of WRKY in specific tissues could have a significant impact on plant growth and development [50]. In this study, many CpWRKYs were expressed in leaves and roots of elephant grass, of which 25 CpWRKYs were found to be more abundant in roots compare to leaves (Table S5). To investigate the response of CpWRKY genes to low temperature, we analyzed the expression of CpWRKY genes under low temperature stress. It was found that low-temperature stress resulted in 63 CpWRKYgenes being differentially expressed (Table S4). Integrating the cis-acting elements with the tissue-specific and low-temperature stress expression profiles of CpWRKY genes, we found that the CpWRKY81 promoter contains root-specific cis-acting elements and exhibits high expression in the roots and under low-temperature stress. It has been reported that insertion of the GGTACGTGGCG sequence of motif I upstream of the truncated cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35s promoter of the X-GUS-90 vector results in stronger GUS activity in transgenic tobacco roots than in GUS vectors that do not contain this sequence [51]. Therefore, we hypothesized that CpWRKY81 is a key TF in the response of elephant grass root system to low-temperature stress. Transgenic yeast experiment demonstrates that CpWRKY81 enhanced cold tolerance in the yeast (Fig. 8). While CpWRKY81 shows a cold tolerance function, its regulatory mechanism needs to be further investigated.

Conclusions

In this study, 176 CpWRKY genes were characterized from the elephant grass genome and classified into seven subfamilies (I, IIa, IIb, IIc, IId, IIe, and III). Similarities in gene structure and conserved motifs were observed within the same subgroups. Based on phylogenetic analysis, we hypothesized that the ancient subgroup IIc WRKY gene is the ancestor of all WRKY genes in elephangt grass. Segmental duplication has contributed significantly to the expansion of the elephant grass WRKY gene family. Ka/Ks analyses indicated that almost all CpWRKYs have been subjected to strong purification and selection during evolution, with the exception of (CpWRKY12; CpWRKY113). Gene expression analysis revealed that the CpWRKY81 was highly expressed in root tissues and under low-temperature stress. Additionally, over-expression of CpWRKY81 improved the cold tolerance in yeast. The results of the present study established a foundation for understanding the role of WRKY genes and their molecular mechanisms in enhancing cold tolerance in plants This is of great significance for breeding low-temperature-tolerant elephant grass or other plants.
