Efficacy of Reverse Metal vs Glass Fiber and Polyethylene Fiber Posts in Esthetic Rehabilitation of Severely Decayed Primary Anterior Teeth: A Randomized-controlled Clinical Trial
Authors: Bolla Deepthi, Ghanashyam P Madu, Naga R Ambati, Saujanya Kanithi, Annam A George, Rajkumar Manoharan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe goal of restoring severely mutilated primary anterior teeth is to restore the lost tooth structure to maintain function and prevent psychological problems that will affect a child's self-esteem. The present study aimed to compare clinical retentiveness and marginal integrity of the reverse metal, glass fiber, and polyethylene fiber posts (PFP) in esthetic rehabilitation of severely decayed primary anterior teeth.Materials and methodsA total of 60 grossly decayed maxillary primary anterior teeth meeting inclusion criteria were selected and equally divided into three groups based on the type of post used; group I: reverse metal, group II: glass fiber, and group III: polyethylene fiber. The restorations were assessed at every 3-month interval until 24 months clinically for retention of post and intact marginal integrity and radiographically for post/root fracture. The obtained data were analyzed statistically using Cochran's Q test for intragroup and Chi-squared test for intergroup comparisons using SPSS software version 20. p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe loss was statistically significant at the 3rd-month review in groups II and III, during the 6th and 9th month reviews (p-value 0.019), and at the 18th, 21st, and 24th month reviews (p-value 0.045). At different time intervals the difference in retention rates was statistically insignificant in group I and significant in groups II and III. Clinically, no marginal adaptation failures and, radiographically, no root/post fractures were observed.ConclusionAs reverse metal and glass fiber posts (GFP) were found to be retentive, both can be recommended for restoring severely decayed primary anterior teeth that require long-term survival.How to cite this articleDeepthi B, Madu GP, Ambati NR, et al. Introduction

The esthetically restoring severely decayed primary anterior teeth has been a challenge for pediatric dentists, not only due to the scarcity of materials but also because the children who require such restorations are typically the youngest and least manageable group of children.1 The objective of restoring lost tooth structure is to maintain function, prevent psychological difficulties that influence the child's self-esteem, and ensure the child's positive attitude, lowering anxiety.2

Many types of root canal posts are there to restore severely damaged primary teeth, including priory manufactured orthodontic wire in alpha/gamma/omega forms, metallic posts with macro retention, composite resin in the form of short post, and biologic posts using direct or indirect techniques.2 However, the optimal post and core in pediatric dentistry should provide appropriate retention and resistance without impeding the eruption course. The adaptation of the post and core to the inner dentinal wall, which is dictated by adhesive and cohesive forces,3 has resulted in the development of newer fiber reinforced composites.4

Glass fiber posts (GFP) are made up of unidirectional glass fibers embedded in a resin matrix that reinforces the dowels without reducing their modulus of elasticity.5 GFP has stronger flexural strength, fracture resistance, and cement interfacial adhesion, but it is more difficult to utilize for an inexperienced clinician because of fibers propensity to separation. Furthermore, bonding to intraradicular dentin is challenging due to the complexity and sensitivity of the technique.6

A new fiber system called Ribbond Triaxial has been introduced as a result of further advancements in these systems. The benefits of Ribbond fiber include its visual appeal,7 higher strength, and improved flexural properties of the composite resin, which offer high fatigue resistance, maintain fiber orientation during application, and preserve architectural shape.8 However, as compared to glass fiber reinforced composite posts, their flexural strength is lower.7

Metal posts have two major drawbacks: they impede root resorption and are unsightly. Eshghi et al. proposed the reverse metal post insertion technique (RMPT), which is a new, easy, and effective approach for reconstructing and reestablishing function in severely deteriorated primary anterior teeth.9 Few clinical investigations, including those by Ayna et al.10 and Mehra et al.,5 have indicated acceptable results after using polyethylene fiber and GFPs in primary anterior teeth. However, the majority of the studies were not longitudinal but rather case reports or short-term assessments. The reverse metal post (RMP) insertion approach lacks both short- and long-term clinical evaluation.

The current study aimed to assess and compare the long-term clinical post retentiveness of RMP, GFP, and polyethylene fiber posts (PFP) in badly decayed maxillary primary front teeth.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A randomized, single-blinded clinical controlled trial was designed in 20 children (12 girls and 8 boys) aged 3–5 years who attended the Outpatient Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, requiring pulpectomy followed by post and core in 60 grossly decayed primary anterior teeth having minimal crown above the gingival margin with no signs of exfoliation and healthy development of the underlying permanent successor were included in the study. Children with root caries, pathologic root resorption, bruxism, deep bite, poor oral hygiene, and history of systemic illness were excluded from the study. The parents/guardians of the selected children provided written informed consent. The Institutional Ethical Committee approved the study (Protocol/IRB/45/2013–16).

The primary anterior teeth were randomly separated into three groups of 20 dependent on the type of post utilized.

Group I—RMP (Mani®, Japan)

Group II—GFP (3M ESPE, USA)

Group III—Polyethylene fiber (RIBBOND®, USA)

During the initial visit, the teeth were isolated with a rubber dam and then access opening and biomechanical preparation were done using conventional methods followed by obturation with Endoflas® (Sanlor Labs, Colombia). In the second appointment the post space was prepared by removing 4 mm of Endoflas and placing a 1 mm base of glass ionomer cement to isolate the obturated material. After try-in of respective posts, excess length of posts beyond 3 mm of cementoenamel junction were trimmed using a wheel bur to prevent premature occlusion and to avoid stress on the roots.

Reverse Metal Post Placement Technique

As the selected metal post is placed reversely into the root canal, the outline of the post space was modified to be round with rounded line angles to match the round core of the metal post. Since the prefabricated metal post has a 3 mm core, it will not interfere with the eruption process, and a 3 mm post would be sufficient to hold the composite core material. Angulations of RMPs were checked and then cemented into the post space using zinc phosphate cement (Zinc Cem, Medicept, USA). After the cement had set, the tooth was etched with 37% phosphoric acid, then a thin coat of bonding agent was applied and allowed to cure for 20 seconds. A coating of flowable composite resin (Filtek™ Z 350 XT, 3M ESPE, USA) was applied over the RMP to hide the metallic appearance and cured for 60 seconds.

Glass fiber Post Placement Technique

The prepared post space was saline cleaned, air dried, and then acid etched with 37% phosphoric acid (3M ESPE, St Paul, USA) for 15 seconds. Bonding agent (Adper Single Bond, 3M ESPE, St Paul, USA) was applied on the etched surface and light cured for 20 seconds. Dual-cure resin cement (Rely X ARC, 3M ESPE, St Paul, USA) was injected into the canal, and the GFP was inserted into the root canal with the help of a plugger and then cured for 40 seconds.

Polyethylene Fiber Post Placement Technique

A 3 mm thick PFP was selected and cut to a length of 6 mm so that 3 mm could be inserted into the canal and the remaining 3 mm could be used for core build-up. After determining the PFP length, it was wet with bonding agent (Adper Single Bond, 3M ESPE, St Paul, USA) and cured for 20 seconds. The post space was etched for 15 seconds, then cleaned and gently dried. A bonding compound was brushed over the carved post space and allowed to cure for 20 seconds. Flowable composite (Filtek Z350 XT, 3M ESPE) was injected into the canal, followed by the implantation of PFP using a plugger and curing for 40 seconds to achieve complete polymerization of the composite.

After respective posts were cemented, etching and bonding agent application were done in all groups, an appropriate size strip crown (3M ESPE) was selected, loaded with an appropriate shade of composite (Filtek™ Z 350 XT, 3M ESPE) matching the adjacent tooth in natural light, and then cured from all the surfaces for 120 seconds. Strip crowns were removed, and occlusion was adjusted using articulating paper to check for any occlusal interference, finishing and polishing was carried out using fine diamond burs.

One pediatric dentist performed the treatment procedures in all three groups. Two examiners who were blindfolded and calibrated assessed each restoration. One skilled clinician calibrated the operators. The observer agreement was assessed using Cohen's Kappa coefficient. Every 3 months for 24 months, the restorations were assessed radiographically for any post/root fractures and clinically for post retention and intact marginal integrity without explorer catching. Using SPSS software version 20, the collected data were statistically evaluated using the Cochran's Q test for intragroup comparisons and the Chi-squared test for intergroup comparisons. p ≤ 0.05 was deemed statistically significant (Figs 1 to 6).

The retention rate was significantly higher for group I compared to groups II and III at different time intervals during the study duration except at the 12th- and 15th-month review. Intragroup comparison revealed that the difference in retention rates was insignificant in group I with 90% intact posts and significant in groups II (65%) and 3 (55%) at different time intervals. Clinically, there were no marginal adaptation failures and, radiographically, no root or post fractures were observed.

Discussion

Early childhood caries can be a particularly aggressive kind of caries, starting soon after the eruption of teeth, forming on smooth surfaces, advancing quickly, and having a long-term negative impact on the dentition. Early loss of carious primary incisors can impair speech, interfering with the pronunciation of consonants and labial sounds.11 Behavior management, the cost-effectiveness of the treatment, and morphological variations present challenges in restoring the teeth.12

Rifkin's13 post and crown approach for primary anterior teeth was not widely accepted because the wire could interfere with natural root resorption if it extended too far into the root. Furthermore, if the post is forced into a restricted canal, it might increase internal tensions within the root, potentially leading to fracture.1 Rodrigues et al. described the use of nickel-chromium cast posts with macro elements to improve the endurance of restorations,14 which were expensive and necessitated an extra lab stage.15 Mortada and King's Omega loop had mechanical adhesion between the Omega wire and the dentinal wall,3 which is inadequate, resulting in wire dislodgement and radicular fracture due to high masticatory forces.16 So, a cost-effective treatment that could be accomplished in a single appointment is needed as this sort of treatment is required for young children where it is difficult to get cooperation for extended times.

Eshghi et al. described the technique of metal post cementation reversely with zinc phosphate cement in primary maxillary canine.9 However, it was a case report lacking comparison to commonly used posts for primary teeth. The present study was the first to clinically evaluate and compare the long-term retention of RMPs with GFP and PFP as cost-effective restorative treatment for grossly decayed primary anterior teeth. In the present study, among group I (RMP), 18 posts were retained with loss of 2 posts throughout the 24 months. Retention loss of the RMP along with the crown could be due to zinc phosphate contamination with oral fluids during cementation, which might have resulted in microleakage and dissolution of cement between the tooth and post interfaces. A study conducted by Geetha et al. reported 59.8% solubility of zinc phosphate cement immersed in artificial saliva for 3–9 minutes.17 The high retention of RMP posts can be attributed to the short chair-side time required for performing the RMP technique, which eliminated both etching and bonding steps for cementation.

Glass fiber posts are available in a variety of diameters, making them ideal for handling and use in high stress regions. It attaches to any kind of composite with improved light transmission due to the interpenetrating polymer network, which improves resin polymerization at the apical area during cementation.15 In the present study for group II, initial failure of 20% posts at the 3rd-month recall may be due to bond failure between the post, resin cement, and teeth because of salivary contamination during operatory as the children requiring these treatments fall under a younger age. In long-term review, 35% of posts loss was recorded at the 24th-month review. Factors that may have attributed to the loss are poor oral hygiene, improper biting forces, and frequent snacking of sticky foods. In contrast to our study result, Mehra et al. reported 93.33% success rate for GFPs after 12 months follow-up.5

Reinforced polyethylene compared to metal posts have clinical advantages since their flexibility is closer to dentin. The use of reinforced fiber, such as Ribbond, as an intracanal post provides an esthetic and simple solution because restorations may be finished in only one session with no laboratory work.18 In the current study, the retention loss for PFP postings was 30% after 3 months. Island G compared the fracture resistance of nonimpregnated and impregnated resin fibers and discovered that nonimpregnated resin fibers had more adhesion failures between the fiber and its core to the internal wall of the root canal.19 Ribbond employed in the current investigation was also a nonpreimpregnated resin; therefore, considerable loss over the first 3 months might be attributed to adhesion failure.

In the present study, retention loss of PFP posts was 35% at 6-, 9-, and 12-months, 40% at 15 months, and 45% at 24 months review. Contrasting results of 86.7% complete retention of the PFPs (Ribbond) were observed in a 1-year follow-up study conducted by Mehra et al.5 The reasons for the PFP loss can be attributed to the individual fiber quantity, composition of resin matrix, adequate adhesion of the fibers to the polymer matrix, and low retention strength of PFP in load-bearing areas like primary anterior teeth.20 Similar reasons might have been responsible for the loss of 9 PFPs in our study.

In the current study, after 24 months follow-up, there was a drop in post retention with time for groups II and III. The decline in retention was significant for groups II and III compared to group I. The complete loss of both glass fiber and polyethylene posts may have been due to trauma, dietary habits, and technique sensitivity, thereby influencing the retention of posts. In the overall period of 24 months, group I was found to be more retentive even in patients with less compliance as it involved a simple post placement procedure.

Metal posts often cause internal strains, resulting in tooth fracture. Because metallic materials have substantially greater moduli of elasticity than the supporting dentin, this mismatch in the moduli could lead to stress concentration in the cement lute, resulting in its failure.21 In the present study, similar to the other two groups, none of the teeth in the RMP group displayed either fracture of root or fracture of post as the extension of the RMP was limited to 3 mm within the root canal, thereby reducing the internal stress on the root. However, trimming of RMPs was a tedious process as they were >6 mm in length. If prefabricated metal posts of 6 mm for primary teeth are available, it reduces the chair-side time, and child cooperation can be gained even better.

Post retention is influenced by various factors clinically, which include frequent snacking of hard substances, sports activity, trauma, oral hygiene, eruption process, bruxism, operator efficiency, and parental interests. Kemoli et al. reported that poor oral hygiene resulted in lower survival rates of restoration. Preschoolers lack motor coordination resulting in falls and trauma to the anterior teeth.22 In our study too, similar factors might have influenced post retention. The ideal requirement of post and core is nonhindrance of eruption path3; all the posts utilized in the present study were successful in this regard.

Further long-term studies with larger sample sizes and various factors such as oral hygiene, masticatory impact, dietary intake, and lack of proper isolation responsible for the loss of reverse metal, glass fiber, and PFPs should be identified and evaluated for excellent clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Reverse metal post is more retentive compared to glass fiber and PFPs throughout 24 months. Both RMPT and GFP demonstrated good retentiveness at the end of the research, making them suitable for replacing badly decaying primary anterior teeth that require long-term survival.
