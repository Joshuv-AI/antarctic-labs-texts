Impact of Foliar Spray of Salicylic Acid on Morpho-Physiological and Biochemical Responses of Pea (Pisum sativum L.) under Drought Stress
Authors: Iqra Anwar, Safura Bibi, Athar Mahmood, Muhammad Anjum Zia, Muhammad Mansoor Javaid, Liaqat Ali, Muhammad Ather Nadeem, Zunaira Naeem, Ibrahim Al-Ashkar, Kifah Gharzeddin, El Sabagh Ayman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrought stress reduces growth, yield, and photosynthetic efficiency in pea plants, limiting nutrient uptake and requiring mitigation strategies. Salicylic acid plays a key role in plant development, and pea (Pisum sativum L.) ranks fourth in global production. A pot experiment was conducted to evaluate the effect of salicylic acid on two pea cultivars viz: Super Classic and Madarna, under water stress conditions at the Botanical Garden Research Area, University of Agriculture Faisalabad PARS, in 2021. Salicylic acid was applied as a foliar spray at concentrations of 0 (control), 50 ppm, 100 ppm, and 150 ppm. Drought stress was imposed at two levels: 100% field capacity (FC) and 75% FC. The experiment was conducted in completely randomized design (CRD) with a factorial arrangement and each treatment was replicated three times. The analysis revealed that drought intervals significantly affected various growth indicators, including fresh and dry weights of both shoots and roots, their lengths, physiological pigments such as chlorophyll and carotenoids, ionic contents of Na + , K + , and Ca2 + , and the overall yield. Notably, the application of 150 ppm salicylic acid effectively mitigated the effects of drought stress in pea plants. In conclusion, salicylic acid demonstrated beneficial effects against drought in pea plants by enhancing growth and yield while protecting photosynthetic pigments. Additionally, the Super Classic variety exhibited greater growth compared to Madarna when treated with salicylic acid at the 150 ppm concentration under drought stress.Keywords: Carotenoids, Drought stress, Field capacity, Madarna, Protein, Salicylic acid

Introduction

Pea (Pisum sativum L.) is a winter season vegetable native to central or Southeast Asia belongs to Leguminosae family. One of the most significant bean plants, pea is grown in several nations across the earth planet. It has a high nutritional content, including vitamins, iron, calcium, protein, and carbs [1]. One of the most important vegetables crop is the pea (Pisum sativum L.), which is cultivated on 0.035 million hectares (Mha) and and yields an average of 1.57 million tons (Mt) annually in Pakistan [2]. Due to a number of biotic and abiotic variables, the average yield per acre of the crops in Pakistan is quite poor. The pea plant holds considerable agronomic importance and is a common vegetable in Pakistan. Pea plant serves as forage, while the seeds (pods) of the pea are consumed as food; however, after the aboveground crop is harvested, the underground crop enhances the soil's nutrient availability [3]. Pea provides a plentiful supply of dietary fiber, important minerals, and protein, thus establishing it as a nutritious and economically sound dietary choice for disadvantaged groups. As a result, peas are widely grown in Pakistan and around the world to take advantage of these beneficial attributes [4].

Globally, peas are cultivated extensively, with major producers including countries like India, China, and the United States. Production varies significantly due to diverse climatic conditions, ranging from arid to semi-arid regions [5]. Monsoon rains, which are critical in some areas, contribute significantly to annual rainfall. Climate risk indices reveal that climate change has severely affected many nations, with notable impacts on agricultural production, emphasizing the need for adaptive strategies worldwide [6].

Drought is a significant abiotic stressor that adversely affects crop productivity and quality, posing a major challenge to global food security. It impacts various physiological, biochemical, and ecological processes in plants, leading to reduced growth, yield, and quality of crops [7]. It causes reduced stomatal conductance, grain filling, leaf expansion, growth rate and grain production that is both of lower quality and quantity [8]. Drought stress can cause changes in gene expression that affect protein synthesis in physiological, metabolic and morphological processes in plants. Drought causes reduction in root and shoots biomass and length. Similarly, carotenoids, total chlorophyll like physiological traits also decrease with water stress. Drought also has a negative impact on ionic traits and decrease Na+, P− and K+ of pea plant [9]. Yield is also affected by the drought conditions. Drought stress greatly hinders photosynthesis in essential food crops by influencing both light-dependent and light-independent processes. It harms photosystems, obstructs the electron transport chain, and limits CO2 availability, resulting in a decline in photosynthetic efficiency [10]. Although plants have an antioxidant defense system and produce more antioxidants like ascorbate peroxidase, glutathione reductase, peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and catalase, but drought stress still causes oxidative damage to plants through increased ROS production [11]. External applications of plant growth regulators, micronutrients, or osmo-protectants throughout various plant growth phases may greatly enhance drought resistance.

Salicylic acid (SA) plays a significant role in mitigating drought stress in plants by enhancing their physiological and biochemical responses. This plant growth regulator (PGR) is crucial in improving plant resilience to water deficit conditions, thereby supporting crop productivity and soil health. By enhancing seedling development, leaf gases exchange properties, the activity of APX, CAT and SOD enzymes, while lowering the Malondialdehyde and hydrogen peroxide contents, salicylic acid has been found to be helpful in reducing detrimental impacts of drought stress. SA might be utilized to boost and stabilize crop production under stress circumstances and significantly boosted drought-tolerance [12]. Studying drought-induced responses of pea cultivars give a valuable tool for investigating basic mechanisms involved in drought tolerance. Salicylic acid has been shown to improve physiological and biochemical characteristics in peas under drought stress. For instance, the application of SA significantly enhanced relative water content, light transmission ratio, energy interception, and total soluble solids in peas, indicating improved water retention and photosynthetic efficiency [13].

SA also enhances the antioxidant defense mechanism in plants, which is crucial for mitigating oxidative stress caused by drought. This includes the activation of enzymes like superoxide dismutase, catalase, and ascorbate peroxidase, which help in reducing oxidative damage [13].

While the role of salicylic acid (SA) in reducing drought stress has been studied in various crops, little research has thoroughly examined its effects on pea plants (Pisum sativum L.), especially when it comes to anatomical adaptations. The proposed research is novel, as most studies focus on physiological and biochemical responses, while the anatomical changes that support drought resilience remain largely unexplored. This research combines morpho-physiological, biochemical, and anatomical analyses to provide a comprehensive understanding of how SA improves drought tolerance in pea plants. By addressing this gap, the study offers valuable insights into the mechanisms behind SA’s effects, contributing to the development of better drought management strategies for legume crops. The objectives of this study are follows: 1) To evaluate the effects of drought stress on the morpho-physiological, biochemical, and anatomical attributes of pea (Pisum sativum L.) plants. 2) To determine the impact of foliar application of salicylic acid (SA) on enhancing drought tolerance in pea plants. 3) To explore potential anatomical adaptations in pea plants triggered by salicylic acid under drought stress.

Materials and Methods

Experimental designs and planting material

To evaluate the impact of salicylic acid on pea cultivars under water stress conditions, an experiment was conducted in the Botanical Garden of the Postgraduate Research Station, Department of Botany, University of Agriculture Faisalabad. Seeds of two pea cultivars (Super classic and Madarna) were obtained from the Ayub Agriculture Research Institute (AARI) in Faisalabad and planted in plastic pots. Pots were filled with 7 kg of soil each and arranged in a completely randomized design with three replications. Each replication consisted of a set of pots treated under identical experimental conditions to ensure consistency and accuracy in the assessment of the treatments. Seven seeds were planted in each pot, and three plants per pot were kept following germination. One week after germination, plants were maintained at a field capacity of 75% for the water-stressed treatment group, while the control group was maintained at 100% field capacity. This differential irrigation strategy was employed to simulate water stress conditions and assess the impact on plant growth and physiological responses. Drought stress was kept constant by weighing the pots daily and adding the necessary amount of water to each pot.

These conditions were followed: day length 10/14 h, with an average temperature of 14 ± 3 °C and relative humidity of 49.7 ± 7%. Water-stressed pots were wrapped with plastic sheets to prevent inadvertent moisture from rain or dew. The following formula was implemented to calculate field capacity.

Soil analysis

Soil analysis of the potting medium showed that it consisted of sandy soil with a soil saturation level of 60%. The soil contained sodium ions at a concentration of 177 ppm, potassium ions at 102 ppm, and calcium ions at 101 ppm. Every plastic pot was loaded up with soil and at the hour of planting the water content in the soil was determined. Moisture content in the soil equivalent to control (100%FC) and 75% field limit were determined for drought conditions. Soil samples were gathered at a depth of 6 inches from the soil surface, and its moisture content was determined by given formula.

Morphological traits

The following morphological characters such as fresh and dry biomass of root and shoot, shoot length (cm), root length (cm), plant height (cm) and leaf area were measured with weighing balance and measuring tape.

Physiological traits

One fresh leaf from each replicate was collected and grind 0.1 g of leaf. After this, add 5 mL of 80% acetone to the extract and kept for one night. On the next day, the extract was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min. Supernatant absorbance was measured by using a spectrophotometer (InnoTech, Inno-UV2000, China) at three wavelengths 480 nm, 645 nm, and 663 nm for various physiological characters like Chlorophyll a, Chlorophyll b, carotenoid, ratio of Chlorophyll a to Chlorophyll b, total Chlorophyll, protein, leaf area index and specific leaf area using formula devised by [14].

Ionic traits

The ionic content in the plant shoots, including sodium ions (Na+), potassium ions (K+), and calcium ions (Ca2+), was measured using a flame photometer (Jenway, PFP-7, Staffordshire, UK) by digesting the 0.1 g shoot samples with 2.5 ml of concentration sulphuric acid and 5 ml of 35% hydrogen peroxide [15, 16].

Total soluble protein

According to Dey et al. [17] methodology, the total soluble protein was determined. Fresh 0.25 g leaf samples were homogenized in 5 mL of potassium phosphate buffer and centrifuged at 4,000 rpm. At 595 nm, a spectrophotometer (Peak Instruments, C7200S, USA) was used to measure the absorbances of the supernatants.

Yield traits

Growth and yield parameters including pod length (cm), total number of tendrils per plant, pod weight (g), biological yield, harvest index, weight of 100 seeds per plant, and leaf area were measured using standard procedures.

Anatomical traits

Fresh stem tissue from both pea cultivars was preserved using 70% alcohol. The stems were then sectioned into precise slices and subjected to double staining to facilitate detailed examination using digital microscope. A formalin acetic alcohol (v/v formalin 10%, acetic acid 5%, ethyl alcohol 50% and distilled water 35%) was used for fixation and acetic alcohol (v/v acetic acid 25%, and ethanol 75%) solution for preservation. Micrographs of stained slides were taken on a stereo—microscope (Nikon 104) equipped with a digital camera (Nikon FDX-35) [18].

Statistical analysis

Data for all parameters were analyzed using a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications, employing COSTAT software. The means of treatments were compared using the least significant difference (LSD) test at a 5% level of significance. Pearson correlation analysis and heatmaps were performed using R Studio Version 4.1.2.

Results

Morphological traits

The experiment showed that salicylic acid application improved the growth of both pea cultivars under normal (100% FC) and drought (75% FC) conditions. Growth parameters such as shoot and root weight, length, and dry matter increased with rising salicylic acid concentrations, with the best results at 150 ppm.

Normal irrigation conditions, Super Classic variety performed better than Madarna, showing higher values in all traits. For example, at 150 ppm, Super Classic recorded the highest shoot fresh weight (7.37 g), shoot length (36 cm), and shoot dry weight (1.36 g).

Under drought stress conditions, the growth of both cultivars was reduced compared to normal irrigation condition. However, the application of salicylic acid still helped improve plant growth. In Madarna, shoot fresh weight increased from 2.20 g (0 ppm) to 4.18 g at 150 ppm. In Super Classic, shoot fresh weight rose from 3.12 g (0 ppm) to 4.46 g at 150 ppm. Growth traits

The results clearly showed that salicylic acid improved plant growth in both pea cultivars, but the response was more pronounced in Super Classic compared to Madarna. As the salicylic acid concentration increased, both cultivars showed better plant height and leaf area under both well-watered and drought conditions. However, Super Classic consistently outperformed Madarna across all parameters. For instance, under full irrigation at 150 ppm salicylic acid, Super Classic reached a height of 43.67 cm compared to 38.67 cm in Madarna, and its leaf area was also larger (25,383.33 mm2 vs. 21,609 mm2). Even under drought stress, Super Classic maintained higher growth, with a plant height of 35.67 cm compared to 29.67 cm in Madarna. This suggests that Super Classic is more responsive to salicylic acid and more tolerant to water stress than Madarna, making it the better-performing variety under both irrigated and drought conditions Fig. 2.

Ionic traits

Application of SA increased sodium, potassium, and calcium ion levels in the shoots of both Madarna and Super classic pea cultivars under 100% FC and 75% FC conditions, with Super classic consistently outperforming than Madarna in ion accumulation across all treatments. Higher SA concentrations led to progressively greater ion uptake, with Super classic reaching 28 ppm sodium, 38.83 ppm potassium, and 20.17 ppm calcium under normal irrigation at 150 ppm SA, compared to Madarna that recorded 24.33 ppm sodium, 35.33 ppm potassium, and 18.67 ppm calcium. Drought reduced ion levels in both cultivars, but SA still enhanced absorption and increased the sodium from 10.67 to 17.33 ppm in Madarna, while in Super classic it increased from 15.67 to 23.17 ppm where 150 ppm SA was applied, with similar trends for potassium and calcium. These results demonstrate that SA mitigates drought stress by improving ion uptake, with Super classic exhibiting greater resilience and higher nutrient efficiency than Madarna Fig. 3.

Physiological traits

The study revealed that SA application significantly improved photosynthetic pigments and protein content in both Madarna and Super classic pea cultivars, with Super classic consistently outperforming than Madarna. Under 100% FC, increasing SA concentrations from 0 to 150 ppm enhanced chlorophyll a content by 163% in Madarna (from 2.06 to 5.43) and 55% in Super classic (from 3.99 to 6.18), while total chlorophyll increased by 126% and 69%, respectively. Protein content also increased substantially, with Madarna showing a 59% increase (0.35 g to 0.55 g) and Super classic a 44% increase (0.65 g to 0.93 g). Under drought conditions (75% field capacity), SA application remained effective, though absolute values were lower. Chlorophyll a increased by 225% in Madarna (0.67 to 2.19) and 122% in Super classic (1.20 to 2.67), while protein content rose by 36% and 23%, respectively under drought conditions. The chlorophyll a/b ratio, a key indicator of photosynthetic efficiency, was consistently higher in Super classic, reaching 3699 under drought at 150 ppm SA compared to Madarna that recorded 3192 ppm. Yield traits

The results show that SA application improved the performance of both pea cultivars (Madarna and Super Classic) under both normal irrigation (100% field capacity) and drought stress (75% field capacity). In general, increasing SA concentration led to better outcomes across all measured traits: pod length, number of tendrils per plant, pod weight, 100-seed weight, biological yield, and harvest index. Under full irrigation, the highest SA dose (150 ppm) produced the most significant improvements in both cultivars. For example, in Madarna, pod length increased from 5.33 cm at 0 ppm to 10.17 cm at 150 ppm, and harvest index increased from 11.86% to 16.17%. Similar trends were observed under drought conditions, although values were lower compared to the irrigated condition. Super Classic consistently outperformed Madarna, especially at higher SA levels, reaching the highest pod length (11.67 cm), seed weight (48.47 g per 100 seeds), and harvest index (19.21%) at 150 ppm under irrigation Fig. 5.

Anatomical studies

At 50 ppm of salicylic acid under 100% field capacity, both Madarna and Super Classic cultivars showed greater metaxylem and vascular bundle sizes, along with increased cortical and phloem cell areas, compared to the untreated control. 100% FC. Additionally, at a concentration of 100 ppm salicylic acid at 100% FC, the Super Classic variety exhibited a reduction in the number of metaxylem elements and vascular bundles compared to the 50 ppm treatment. However, it showed an increase in the cortical and phloem cell areas, indicating enhanced development in these tissues. Under drought conditions 75% FC, the application of 50 ppm salicylic acid led to notable improvements in anatomical features in both Madarna and Super Classic variety. Specifically, this treatment resulted in larger metaxylem and vascular bundle sizes, as well as expanded cortical and phloem cell areas, compared to the untreated control under the same water-limited conditions. Anatomical analysis showed that the stems of peas treated with 150 ppm of salicylic acid at 100% FC demonstrated maximum metaxylem, vascular bundles, cortical cell area, and phloem cell area, while these measurements were at their lowest with 50 ppm application at 75% FC (Table 1, Fig. 6). These findings suggest that salicylic acid, particularly at moderate concentrations, can positively influence both structural and physiological adaptations in pea plants under varying irrigation levels.

Discussion

This study evaluated the morphological, physiological, ionic, yield, and overall responses of pea plants to drought stress under varying concentrations and applications of salicylic acid (SA). The findings demonstrate that SA application can alleviate plant stress during drought conditions. Recent studies have confirmed that salicylic acid enhances drought tolerance in plants by improving growth, physiological balance, and antioxidant activity, supporting our findings that SA application mitigates drought stress in pea plants [19]. Results revealed significant changes in the defense systems of both SA-treated and untreated pea plants under drought stress, emphasizing the important role of SA concentration in modulating plant responses. Foliar application of salicylic acid at 150 ppm was found to be the most effective concentration for mitigating the adverse effects of drought on pea cultivars. These findings are in agreement with previous studies that reported dose-dependent effects of SA, where moderate concentrations, typically between 100 and 150 ppm, were most beneficial in enhancing plant tolerance to abiotic stress [20, 21]. The effectiveness of 150 ppm SA can be attributed to its ability to regulate antioxidant enzyme activity, maintain cell membrane stability, and modulate stomatal conductance, thereby improving water use efficiency and reducing oxidative damage under drought conditions [22, 23]. These mechanisms collectively contribute to improved growth, physiological balance, and yield stability during water-limited conditions, supporting the observed dose–response relationship in our study. Overall, drought stress hinders plant growth and disrupts antioxidant activity in crops by promoting the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [24]. Foliar application of salicylic acid significantly promoted nearly all growth metrics compared to the control, both under drought and non-drought conditions. Other studies have also found that topical application of salicylic acid enhances plant growth during stress [25]. Many plants, such as peach, chickpea, and milk thistle, have shown positive benefits from the exogenous application of salicylic acid, enhancing both morphology and productivity during periods of water scarcity [26]. Total plant height, leaf area, and the number of leaves per plant decreased due to drought. This detrimental effect of reduced moisture is linked to a decline in water uptake by the xylem and phloem tissues of the pea plants, leading to a reduction in cell elongation and division, as well as decreases in height, leaf count, and leaf area [27]. The limited water availability leads to reduced cell elongation and division, ultimately decreasing plant height, leaf count, and leaf area. [28]. In pea plants, for instance, drought stress resulted in a 30% reduction in plant height, 40% in leaf area, and a 25% decrease in the number of leaves [29]. The application of SA has been shown to counteract the reduction in plant height and leaf area caused by water stress. For example, in a study on pea, foliar application of SA increased plant height by 22%, leaf area by 25% [19]. The seed yield was significantly affected by drought conditions. Drought-stressed pea plants exhibited notable decreases in all measured parameters, including the number of pods per plant, pod length, number of seeds per pod, and the weight of 100 seeds [30]. These characteristics may have declined due to the negative effects of drought on cell division and expansion, as well as on leaf and stem size, and the balance between water and nutrients [31]. Rai et al. [32] depicted that SA application, particularly at concentrations of 200–250 mg L−1, plays a significant role in alleviating drought stress in tomato plants. Naz et al. [33] concluded that SA treatment at 250 mg L−1 effectively enhances antioxidant defense mechanisms by boosting the activity of enzymes like SOD, APX, and CAT, which reduces oxidative stress and promotes plant health. This optimal concentration not only improves gene expression and the accumulation of bioactive compounds but also supports shoot and root growth without negatively impacting yield, making it a promising strategy for sustainable crop improvement. SA can effectively boost drought tolerance in crops like tomatoes, providing a sustainable approach to enhancing crop productivity under challenging environmental conditions [34]. It is observed that photosynthetic pigments are essential factors that influence the photosynthetic machinery, ultimately affecting the morphology and productivity of plants [35]. It has been reported in Chinese cabbage and lemon grass plants by Iamba et al. [36] that exogenous SA application enhances stress tolerance and stimulates the production of secondary metabolites, improving overall plant growth and resilience. In the present study, drought stress markedly decreased the content of photosynthetic pigments in both cultivars. However, the foliar spray of salicylic acid (SA) enhanced pigment levels under water stress. Research indicates that SA reduces pigment degradation during stress by diminishing the negative impact on the photosynthetic mechanism through the accumulation of compatible solutes, decreasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and increasing the activity of chlorophyll-forming enzymes [37]. This negative effect of dryness may result from its role in chlorophyll degradation, stomatal closure, and reduced CO2 fixation, leading to significant changes in photosynthesis [10] which in turn results in a decrease in chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b concentration, and decreases the amount of oxygen available for photosynthesis. Additionally, SA enhanced drought tolerance in plants by improving photosynthetic efficiency, biomass production, and water uptake through modulation of stomatal conductance, chlorophyll biosynthesis, and root hydraulic conductivity [38]. Higher salt level affects not only development but also the physiological and leaf characteristics. Ion toxicity and osmotic impacts on water intake, disruption with nutrient uptake [39]. In the current study, the shoot exhibited reduced levels of potassium, sodium, and calcium compared to the control group at 100% field capacity. However, SA enhanced the absorption of these ions. According to Chowdhury et al. [40]this improvement is particularly significant for K+, as drought stress typically restricts its uptake due to decreased soil mobility and competitive inhibition by Na+ under osmotic stress conditions. Furthermore, Rodríguez-Ruiz et al. [41] also depicted that ionic imbalance negatively impacts enzyme activities, cellular turgor, and protein production. SA has been shown to maintain photosynthetic efficiency under drought stress. In radish, SA treatment reversed growth limitations and upheld photosynthetic efficiency, despite a decrease in total photosynthesis due to lower shoot dry mass [42]. In wheat, the combined application of SA and potassium increased chlorophyll content and photosynthetic pigments. This enhancement contributed to improved photosynthetic efficiency and higher grain yield under drought conditions [19]. Anatomical traits are more adaptive to environmental conditions, allowing plants to survive under water stress. An increase in stem storage parenchyma helps conserve water in dry environments. Our results are in line with those of Bibi et al. [43] who suggested that the presence of sclerified cells around the vascular and cortical regions supports growth and facilitates water absorption during water deficit conditions. Pea plants respond to drought stress by altering their stem structure to enhance survival. The cross-sectional area of the vascular bundles in the stems decreases, resulting in a significant reduction in the conductivity of both phloem and xylem due to water stress [44]. The current study showed that the stems of pea cultivars treated with 150 ppm under 100% field capacity exhibited increased metaxylem, larger vascular bundles, and expanded cortical and phloem cell areas compared to those treated with 50 ppm under 75% field capacity.

Conclusion

Water deficiency is one of the most significant abiotic factors affecting plant growth and productivity. The exogenous application of SA has been shown to enhance drought stress tolerance by improving pigment levels, specifically carotenoids, which ultimately lead to increased pea yield. Morphological traits, including the fresh and dry weights of roots and shoots, as well as root and shoot lengths, were significantly improved following SA treatment. Similarly, physiological traits such as chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids were elevated with the foliar application of SA at 150 ppm. Ionic traits, including Na+, K+, and Ca2+ levels in pea plants, were also enhanced. Yield, however, decreased with reduced water levels. When pea plants were treated with salicylic acid, various yield-related factors such as pod length, pod weight, biological yield, harvest index, 100-seed weight, and leaf area showed significant increases. Foliar spray of salicylic acid at 150 ppm was the most effective treatment against drought stress. This study also found that the Super Classic variety exhibited greater growth compared to the Madarna variety when treated with salicylic acid at 150 ppm under drought stress conditions.
