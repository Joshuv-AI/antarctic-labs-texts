Addressing laboratory challenges in clinical trials: approaches from the Clinical Diagnostic Laboratory Services at MRC/UVRI and LSHTM Uganda Research Unit
Authors: Gloria Kakoba Ayebazibwe, Charline Clarissa Kakweera Katurole, Geofrey Kimbugwe, Zephyrian Kamushaaga Member, Dora Jocelyn Mulondo, Priscilla Balungi, Juliet Nsimire, Esther Nabanoba, Joanita Nassali, Stephen Musemeza, Moses Egesa, Florence Nambaziira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaboratories can play a critical role in the conduct of clinical trials. Management of different aspects of the clinical laboratory processes is essential and can determine the success of a trial. Activities within the laboratory process encompassing pre-analytical, analytical, and post-analytical processes ought to be thoroughly monitored. In this paper, we present an outline of approaches for sample collection, sample transport, sample storage, sample analysis, and data management, in a laboratory setting. The paper highlights that clinical trials are more likely to be completed successfully if there is rigorous adherence to organized quality management approaches in the laboratory. We also discuss the challenges encountered that include sample quality issues, equipment downtime, absence of reference ranges generated from the local population among others, and approaches implemented to overcome these. If implemented, the approaches described here are expected to improve the quality and integrity of the data produced in laboratories which in turn will contribute to successful clinical trial conduct.Keywords: Clinical trials, Laboratory, Quality

Background

Clinical trials are essential in advancing medical interventions, and the reliability of their outcomes can depend on the accuracy of laboratory data. The importance of clinical trials is recognized worldwide with a significant increase in the number of trials conducted over the last 2 decades globally [1, 2]. As of November 2024, there were more than 515,000 clinical trials registered worldwide [3] with Africa estimated to account for about 4% of the clinical trials started in 2023 [4]. Evidently, the significance of clinical trials has become increasingly recognized across the African continent over the years with the historical backdrop of clinical trials in Uganda tracing back to the 90s [5, 6]. An analysis by Edem et al. listed Uganda among the top ten countries registering clinical trials within the sub-Saharan region, with randomized trials accounting for at least 80% of trials registered generally [7]. In Uganda, clinical trials were initially majorly concentrated in the field of HIV, a pattern that was also observed across much of the African continent. In the context of HIV, approximately 6236 HIV-related trials were registered worldwide between 2000 and 2019, of which 8.1% were conducted in South Africa, 4% in Uganda, 3% in Kenya, and 2% in Tanzania [8]. A separate earlier study reported 77 HIV clinical trials conducted across 18 of the 48 sub-Saharan countries [9]. Over time, however, the scope of clinical research in Uganda has broadened to include a wider range of diseases.

Similarly, clinical trials at the MRC/UVRI & LSHTM Uganda Research Unit expanded overtime beyond the scope of HIV to include trials on neglected tropical diseases, endemic, emerging, and re-emerging infections [10]. Consequentially, the Clinical Diagnostic Laboratory Services (CDLS) was established at the research unit to undertake mainly safety laboratory testing for participants in clinical trials. Over the years, CDLS has been involved in safety testing for a number of different disease trials [11–20]. The scope of the laboratory has, however, evolved over time to support different research initiatives within the unit beyond safety testing for clinical trials.

A rather important aspect of laboratory testing in clinical trials is the principal outcome of product efficacy and safety. Agreeably, Chuang-Stein et al. in their paper on Laboratory data in clinical trials stated that, “such laboratory data provide the best indicators for systemic toxicities in clinical trials of investigational medication with dire consequences for both the participants and trial if errored, not managed well and responded to promptly” [21]. Clinical trials can fail due to the absence of a structured, pragmatic approach towards laboratory quality management aimed at reducing errors and managing toxicities as they occur. Moreover, a trial could fail due to efficacy and safety issues, as highlighted by Hwang et al. in their paper assessing 640 phase 3 trials, and this could potentially arise from the laboratory data not accurately reflecting the true effects of the intervention [22]. This paper showed that 57% of the trial products failed due to efficacy issues and 17% due to safety issues with laboratory test abnormalities featuring as a direct cause. In their paper, Crucitti et al. wrote about a failed multi-site clinical trial examining the effectiveness of the candidate microbicide cellulose sulfate in the prevention of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections [23]. The authors report the major cause of failure in this trial as discordant test results of HIV and Amplicor CT/NG polymerase chain reaction (PCR) between the reference and the site laboratories as a result of sample contamination in the preanalytical phase. Further emphasis on the importance of quality in research laboratories is made in a paper by Schultze and Irizarry stating the importance of adherence to standard operating procedures (SOPs), good equipment maintenance and proper sample identification for trial success [24]. These examples portray that a strict adherence to quality coupled with maximizing the benefits of laboratory testing for participants [25, 26] reverberates a positive outcome for clinical trials.

In this paper, we present approaches for good clinical trial management from a laboratory perspective. In particular, we emphasize the importance of structured and practical laboratory quality management approaches during different pre-analytical, analytical and post-analytical processes. We discuss the sample collection, sample transport, sample storage, sample analysis and data aspects of the laboratory workflow in detail below with a focus on approaches for improving quality outcomes.

Sample collection

Decentralized sample collection by multiple sites detached from the testing laboratory, a common feature in multicenter trials, poses certain complexities. Notably, a disconnect in the coordination of sample collection between the collection sites and the testing laboratory can introduce common challenges, including poor collection techniques, use of improper containers leading to disparities in sample quality across sites, pre-analytical biases, insufficient volumes particularly in young participants, labeling discrepancies, and incomplete participant forms [27]. Poor collection techniques, more especially during blood draws such as prolonged use of tourniquets, can increase the risk of specimen hemolysis [28], leading to inaccurate test results or even rendering samples unanalyzable. Different authors have shown that hemolysis alters results of various chemistry analytes including liver enzymes [29, 30], with one study demonstrating altering Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) results up to 3 times above the upper reference limit at 10.3 g/l of cell-free hemoglobin [31]. The impact of hemolysis is even more pronounced on blood electrolytes such as potassium [32] where slight alterations in results have a more significant impact on participant health interpretation. These substantial disparities can result in inaccurate clinical diagnoses and potential participant drug withdrawal. Furthermore, prolonged tourniquet use can result in the development of platelet clamps, hence giving falsely low platelet counts during complete blood count (CBC) analysis. Improper mixing of samples with anticoagulants can induce microthrombin formation, and overfilling of blood vacutainers can result in pseudothrombocytopenia, both of which can alter participant results [33]. The impact of collection technique extends beyond blood draws, as urine tests like urinalysis require specific participant instructions for mid-stream sample collection to yield the most accurate results [34].

In addition to poor sample collection techniques, compromises made with sample collection containers affect sample quality and inevitably the participant results [35, 36]. Another crucial factor to consider when obtaining blood samples for various analytical purposes is the sequence in which the different vacutainers are filled during phlebotomy from the same participant. Additives in the initial blood tubes, such as anticoagulants, have the potential to contaminate succeeding blood tubes and lead to inaccurate analyte measurements. Serum tubes without additives should be collected before those containing anticoagulants, and then followed by tubes containing glycolysis inhibitors [37]. The interaction of blood with anticoagulants used to preserve blood may affect the accuracy of other analyte determinations [38].

To minimize differences across sample collection sites in a trial, investigators need to be guided by the laboratory for example through provision of a clinical handbook and constant meetings with the laboratory team prior to the start and during the trial. Such guidance can be used as a platform to inform investigators of appropriate collection containers, and general best practices. Additionally, this sets ground for investigators to work with the laboratory to procure supplies that align with the specific requirements of the study protocol yet still allow for good sample integrity. Constant communication between the laboratory and other trial teams allows for continuous process improvement both on the laboratory and clinic side.

Automating data collection and sample reception processes with automated quality control checks for the biodata provided on request forms could be worthwhile and beneficial for identifying any errors that could potentially mislead the testing laboratory [39]. Errors like inserting the wrong gender and age could affect result interpretation as different reference ranges will appear on the participant’s result report based on the information provided. The use of automated processes minimizes the occurrence of transcription errors.

Research site laboratories need to adhere strictly to participant management in line with Good Clinical Practice (GCP), Good Clinical Laboratory Practice (GCLP), and Human Subject Protection (HSP) guidelines. In addition to this, diagnostic laboratories need to adhere to additional international standards such as the ISO 15189 [34]. This should be done whilst prioritizing the well-being of participants and considering any risks associated with trial participation. Best practices for sample collection techniques need to be considered as this directly impacts sample quality [40–42]. Aside from the general guidelines, specific participant categories may require strict adherence to additional guidelines in line with safety during sample collection. An example is the US NIH guidelines for blood draws in children that recommend blood draw of ≤ 5% of total blood volume per day (i.e., 5 mL/kg body weight) [43].

It is rather important that the laboratory take the lead in training sessions on sample collection, more especially if the collection is done in non-laboratory field sites. In addition to this, the laboratory should offer audit support for such sites to ensure that best collection practices are being followed [34]. This will ensure that phlebotomy guidelines are followed, and phlebotomists are trained prior to the start of the clinical trial. Additionally, the use of uniform collection procedures documented in collection SOPs and bench aids also supports adherence to best practices. Needless to say, in order to support clinical trials successfully, a laboratory needs to be able to support the different sites and should have robust sample collection processes as this could directly affect the quality of laboratory results. The specific processes that laboratories need to be critical about include ensuring proper collection techniques are followed, standardized collection containers are used even across different sites for multicenter trials, participants are well instructed in preparation for collection and adequate documentation is maintained.

Sample transport and storage

In multicenter clinical trials, sample aliquoting and storage may be necessary before specimens can be transported to the clinical trial laboratory responsible for testing different analytes. Samples may be temporarily stored at the collection sites before being transported for testing or shipped to the final testing laboratory. It is rather important that validated criteria for transport and storage of samples are fulfilled for laboratories supporting clinical trials.

Suboptimal storage practices, such as multiple freeze-thaw cycles, have a direct impact on laboratory results. Different studies have shown the effects of multiple freeze thaw cycles [44–46] and long-term storage at higher temperatures on the accuracy of results of various biomarkers [47]. Additionally, a long distance between the sample collection site and testing laboratory may introduce the risk of samples being exposed to inappropriate temperatures for long time periods and samples being tested outside the recommended time frame such as within 4–6 h from sample collection for complete blood count testing, 2 h for urinalysis testing hence increasing the likelihood of erroneous results [48]. This is even more critical with certain tests, such as the measurement of electrolytes and glucose for biochemistry samples, where even small time lags can lead to significantly varied outcomes [49] and could lead to wrong diagnoses of trial participants.

To address the challenge of long-distance transportation at inappropriate temperatures, it is ideal for sample collection to occur in centralized collection points that are in proximity to the laboratory where the samples will be analyzed and stored. Consideration should also be given to establishing and strengthening quality testing at laboratory extensions based in the field sites to minimize any potential delays in testing caused by distance. When long transport times cannot be avoided, investigators should consider implementing proper handling procedures at the collection sites in order to maintain the integrity of the samples. Some of the measures to minimize result variance due to transportation time include the use of appropriate transport containers such as cool boxes, the use of data loggers to monitor temperatures during transit, centrifuging samples in the field to increase stability during transport (as achieved with serum/plasma rather than whole blood), and temporary storage at the field site. Study protocols need to define recommended time frames clearly to minimize variance in results that may be introduced by differences in the time of testing. If sample collection occurs at external field sites, standardized storage, transport, and processing procedures become more critical, particularly with respect to time and temperature.

Clinical trial laboratories need to consider working with established Biobanks for sample storage. This allows for research to be furthered whilst minimizing resources involved for clinical trials [50] by allowing for maximum efficiency of collected samples. Biobanks provide a range of services tailored to suit different sample needs, including storage in frost freezers at different temperatures, availing nitrogen plants and nitrogen tanks for sample cryopreservation. Additionally, proactive temperature monitoring with system alerts, as is observed by biobanks with a 24-h surveillance system to keep track of temperatures at all times with automated alerts in case of temperature excursions, allows for good integrity of samples. System alerts and freezer alarms have proven to be important tools in monitoring temperature fluctuations real-time and allow for prompt action to be taken to prevent sample deterioration. Key to note is the importance of accompanying samples for storage for further research with appropriate documentation and consent forms. This should be done in keeping with different research guidelines. Without following stringent storage guidelines, research undertaken on these samples may not be publishable and hence it defeats the purpose of impacting health policy change.

Additionally, it is crucial that sample storage and transportation be done according to standardized international guidelines such as International Air Transport Association (IATA) regulations. In keeping with good laboratory practice, laboratory staff involved in storage and shipping need to be trained on such guidelines. Additionally, consideration needs to be made regarding the suitable volumes for storage based on the assays to be conducted and the study protocol. Other considerations during storage include appropriate capping and material for storage cryovials to prevent contamination and breaking, efficient aliquoting with volumes that will minimize freeze-thaw cycles. Good storage practices will allow for good sample stability; hence, more accurate results when tested.

Sample analysis

Adherence to best practices during sample analysis is fundamental to safeguarding data integrity during clinical trials. Laboratory personnel need to comply with Good Clinical Laboratory Practice while performing bench work [51]. Additionally, simple checks, such as verifying request forms and sample labels before analysis, are critical, as seemingly minor errors can result in serious consequences for the participants, the laboratory and the trial. In one study, mislabeling and unlabeled samples accounted for 10% and 36% of their laboratory errors respectively [52]. Given that human error is inevitable, it is imperative that laboratories establish quality management systems for recognizing, documenting, and rectifying errors, as well as implementing measures to prevent their recurrence. Testing conditions specific to individual sample types also need to be considered during sample analysis. Seemingly minor environmental changes such as prolonged exposure of whole blood samples for complete blood count (CBC) analysis to direct sunlight or warmer temperatures can alter results significantly. This consideration is particularly important for analytes that are light or temperature sensitive, necessitating additional measures, such as shielding samples from sunlight exposure through the use of blinds or opaque sample containers. One systematic review showed that white blood cell counts were less stable when samples were exposed to warmer temperatures for more than 24 h, and that total bilirubin concentrations decreased with extended sunlight exposure [53]. Similarly, another study reported that temperature variations led to fluctuations in laboratory results, which in turn influenced clinical decision making [54].

In addition to managing environmental conditions, the accuracy of results produced after sample analysis can be improved through adherence to standardized protocols and the use of validated analytical methods. New procedures and test kits should be verified against manufacturer specifications to prevent externally introduced errors, while equipment should be assessed for analytical sensitivity, specificity, accuracy and linearity prior to use in quantitative testing [55]. In addition, routine calibration and maintenance of laboratory equipment are necessary to safeguard precision and accuracy in measurements. Proficiency testing also plays a central role in quality assurance by comparing results across analytical platforms, thus enhancing confidence in data integrity. A major challenge at this stage is the potential unavailability of critical reagents at the start of trials, which can delay or compromise essential verification experiments for new methods to be used for trial samples. Reagent shortages can lead to incomplete verification resulting in unreliable results that may affect data integrity and patient safety. Evidence from low- and middle-income countries underscores how supply chain disruptions significantly affect diagnostic workflows, reinforcing the need for contingency planning to prevent delays in verification and trial sample analysis [56]. To address these challenges, laboratories should establish robust procurement strategies, maintain buffer stocks and identify alternative suppliers to ensure the continuous availability of reagents [57].

Given the diversity of clinical trials, ranging from randomized and non-randomized to blinded and unblinded designs, laboratories need to remain adaptable and well informed. This requires continuous staff training and regular updates on study protocols to minimize deviations, such as accidental unblinding. Continuous professional developments through study protocol trainings, journal clubs and different training sessions further equip laboratory personnel to meet evolving research demands. Such initiatives have been shown to improve the quality of sample analysis and strengthen the robustness of findings across different research settings [58, 59].

Result review and data management

Result review and good data management are important in ensuring data integrity and participant safety in clinical trials. This importance has long been recognized, with early work such as Chuang-Stein et al. (1998) highlighting the critical role of rigorous documentation, standard operating procedures, and correct interpretation of laboratory reference ranges in ensuring data integrity within clinical trials [21]. The interpretation of final results by investigators greatly depends on how trial laboratory data is presented, underscoring the importance of clarity and accuracy in result reporting. To support this, it is advantageous for laboratories to employ dedicated data managers, allowing laboratory data from multiple trials to be centrally coordinated and handled efficiently. In addition to this benefit, working with staff who possess a basic understanding of test result interpretation facilitates the presentation of data in ways that support clinical decision making. Providing accurate and representative reference ranges is also essential, more especially for non-laboratory investigators. Where resources allow, laboratories should establish locally derived reference ranges rather than relying solely on those reported in the literature, as these reflect the characteristics of local populations and take into account the common analytical methods used within the region [60, 61].

Automated result and data systems offer further advantages, particularly when equipped with automated result review checks that minimize transcription errors and enhance data reliability. To maximize their benefits, such systems should be fully integrated across all equipment and processes. Furthermore, an onsite data system administrator is valuable for configuring new equipment and procedures, troubleshooting issues in real time, and implementing system improvements. Robust data security measures should be in place to protect participant confidentiality and data managed by the laboratory. Automated results and data systems should also be designed to identify and promptly flag critical results, ensuring timely clinical intervention and enhancing safety monitoring within the trials. In addition, result review processes should incorporate age and sex-specific reference ranges, as values considered normal in one subgroup may represent clinically significant findings in another [62]. Together, these measures ensure that laboratory data is accurate, easy to interpret and clinically meaningful within the context of clinical trials.

Conclusions

In this article, we have put together approaches to address different challenges encountered in the laboratory during the conduct of clinical trials. Crucial considerations for quality control in sample collection, transport, storage and analysis, as well as data management for laboratories supporting clinical trials are discussed in detail. A well-structured laboratory system with standardized processes, continuous training, and automated data management is essential for reducing errors, improving trial reliability, and supporting evidence-based policy decisions.
