Menstrual product preference and factors associated with use of freely-distributed reusable products among secondary students in Uganda: a longitudinal study
Authors: Levicatus Mugenyi, Ronald Kyasanku, Robert Bakanoma, Denis Ndekezi, Kate Andrews Nelson, Katherine A Thomas, Stephen Lagony, Aggrey Tumuhimbise, Jackline Namara, Norah Kembabazi, Andrew Mpaji, Shamirah Nakalema, Clare Tanton, Mandikudza Tembo, Nambusi Kyegombe, Helen A Weiss, Belen Torondel-Lopez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAccess to menstrual products is a key factor for improving menstrual health, but there is limited research on the facilitators and barriers to their uptake and use. We describe menstrual product use, and factors associated with use of freely-distributed reusable pads and menstrual cups among female students in Ugandan secondary schools.MethodsWe analysed one-year follow-up data nested within a cluster-randomised trial evaluating the effectiveness of a multi-component menstrual health intervention among female students in 60 schools. The intervention included provision of five reusable pads and an optional menstrual cup, with training. We used random-effects logistic regression to identify factors associated with reported use of each product type at last menstrual period (LMP) at endline. We conducted focus group discussions and in-depth interviews among students and caregivers to explore product preferences, using thematic analysis.ResultsWe analysed data from 2622 post-menarche participants present at both baseline and endline (mean age 15.6 years). Of these, 1683 (64.2%) used more than one type of menstrual product at LMP and 2203 (84.0%) used disposable pads at LMP, at endline. Among 1221/1302 (93.8%) intervention arm participants who received menstrual pads, and 613 (47.1%) who received a menstrual cup, 845 (69.2%) and 156 (25.4%) respectively reported using these products at LMP at endline. Reusable pad use was associated with older age, lower socioeconomic status, being a day scholar, prior use of reusable pads, and having higher menstrual self-efficacy at baseline. Use of a menstrual cup was associated with older age and being of non-Muganda ethnicity. Qualitative findings showed that knowledge, perceptions, affordability, prior familiarity, ease of use, comfort, peer influence, social stigma, and access to private washing facilities played key roles in product preference among the students.ConclusionIn this setting, participants used more than one product during a single menstrual period and preferred disposable pads when provided with free reusable products, due to challenges including washing and drying, social stigma, and discomfort. Provision of menstrual products needs to address both uptake and sustained use by considering individual preferences, cultural acceptability, sustainability, comfort, and environmental factors.Trial registrationISRCTN 45461276; 16/09/2021.

Background

Menstrual health (MH) is a state of physical, mental, and social wellbeing in relation to the menstrual cycle [1]. Menstruation, especially among adolescents, often comes with fear and discomfort due to the social, psychological, and physical challenges associated with menstrual management [2, 3]. In low and middle-income countries (LMICs), there can be additional challenges around access to adequate menstrual products, water and soap to clean reusable products, and facilities to change, wash, and dispose products; poor psychosocial support, and socio-cultural restrictions [3]. These barriers can lead to social exclusion, school absenteeism, and adverse health outcomes, underscoring the urgent need for effective and sustainable solutions to menstrual health challenges [4, 5].

A key requirement for achieving good menstrual health is access to sufficient, safe, and comfortable menstrual products, along with the knowledge necessary to make informed decisions about their use [1, 6]. Menstrual products include disposable products (e.g. disposable pads and tampons), and reusable products (e.g. manufactured reusable pads, period pants, and the menstrual cup). Individuals may also use non-manufactured products including cloth, toilet paper or old underwear [7, 8]. Inadequate access to sufficient, safe and preferred menstrual products is a widespread issue, particularly for those in LMICs [3].

Access to menstrual products in LMICs is hindered by several factors including lack of information and education about available products, cultural stigma and fear preventing use of certain products, limited product availability in local shops and markets, and economic constraints [3, 8, 9]. These challenges often lead to girls and women using unhygienic alternatives to manage their menstruation that can cause embarrassment by leaking or smelling, or urogenital health risks such as reproductive and urinary tract infections (RTI and UTIs) [10]. This limited access to menstrual products is evidenced in a systematic review and qualitative meta-synthesis looking at women’s and girls’ experiences of menstruation which reported that most struggled to afford their preferred product and found products such as disposable pads to be a luxury [3]. If menstrual health is to improve among girls and women in LMICs, addressing access to menstrual products should be a priority.

Initiatives aimed at addressing menstrual health inequities have often included the provision of free or subsidized menstrual products. Reusable manufactured pads and menstrual cups are favoured in MH interventions for their cost-effectiveness and environmental sustainability. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 44 studies (78% from LMICs) among women and girls showed that in 12 studies from LMICs where manufactured reusable pads were distributed for free, the median reported uptake at follow-up (median 6 months, range: 2–18 months) was 90% (range 22–100%) [11]. Though uptake of reusable pads was high in this systematic review, it is not clear if the participants exclusively used freely-distributed products or used them in combination with other products. Understanding the different product combinations used will inform interventions aiming to improve adequate access to menstrual products.

Most research on factors influencing the use of reusable menstrual products has been descriptive or qualitative, identifying social, cultural, and practical factors that shape women’s and girls’ decisions regarding menstrual product preference [11, 12]. These factors often include product accessibility, cost, environmental concerns, cultural beliefs, and personal preferences. For example, a systematic review including quantitative and qualitative studies focusing on commercially-available reusable products indicated that challenges with washing, changing reusable products, and cost were some of the factors influencing their use [11]. Few quantitative studies have estimated factors associated with reusable product use [13, 15]. While a systematic review [14] provided detailed data on use of reusable pads and menstrual cups, it did not include an analysis of factors associated with use of any of these products. We aim to address this evidence gap, to inform sensitization programs aiming at improving informed menstrual product choice.

A situational analysis on menstrual health management (MHM) in 14 districts in Uganda showed that access to menstrual products was a challenge as about half of 250 participants (52%) used disposable pads, 32% used cloth, 6% used reusable pads, 5% cotton wool, 3% used toilet paper, while 2% improvised other materials [15]. While a systematic review and meta-analysis of freely-distributed reusable pads (including 6 school-based studies in Uganda) estimated a pooled uptake of reusable pads of 72% [11], we observe from this systematic review and meta-analysis that the pooled uptake of free reusable pads is much higher compared to the situational analysis of MHM in the 14 Ugandan districts. This highlights the need to understand barriers and facilitators to menstrual product uptake, especially when products are provided for free.

We recently completed a cluster-randomised trial of a multi-component menstrual health intervention on education, health, and wellbeing among secondary schoolgirls in Uganda (“MENISCUS”) [16, 17]. The intervention included free provision of reusable manufactured menstrual pads and an optional menstrual cup, and found an impact on use of adequate menstrual products, and a reduction in unmet menstrual practice needs [17]. In this paper, we use quantitative and qualitative methods to describe the different menstrual products used by in-school female adolescents, understand reasons for using different types of menstrual products, and the factors associated with use of free reusable pads and menstrual cups, respectively.

Methods

Study design and setting

This mixed-methods study is nested in the MENISCUS trial among secondary schoolgirls in 60 schools in Wakiso and Kalungu districts in Uganda [16]. Wakiso district includes both rural and urban areas bordering with Kampala, the Uganda’s capital city, and Kalungu district is in the southwest of Uganda and is largely rural. Details about the design and intervention effect of the trial are published elsewhere [16, 17]. For this paper, we use quantitative data collected at baseline and endline (one-year post-baseline) to describe the use of menstrual products and to determine factors associated with use of free reusable pads and menstrual cups. We collected qualitative data at midline (immediately after intervention delivery) and endline (at the end of the intervention) to have an in-depth understanding of participants’ preferences for menstrual products.

Study participants

Participants were female students in one academic year (secondary two; S2) at baseline (March-July 2022) who were in S3 at endline (June-August 2023). The quantitative analyses are restricted to female students who participated in both surveys (baseline and endline) in the same school, who had passed menarche at baseline, and who reported to have menstruated in the past 6 months prior to the endline survey. The qualitative data was collected using a purposive sample of female students (n = 60) and female caregivers (n = 7) in four purposively selected intervention schools based on context diversity (urban/rural, rate of menstrual cup uptake, school size and previous experience with similar interventions). Participants’ selection was based on their degree of involvement in intervention activities and whether they opted to receiving a menstrual cup.

Consenting and recruitment

We sought consent from parents or guardians of the female students (if aged less than 18 years) followed by assent from female students whose parents had consented to the trial, and consent from female students who were aged ≥ 18 years. Students and parents who assented/consented to trial were then asked for separate consent to receive a menstrual cup. Information about the trial (including the menstrual cup) was provided to students using videos on tablets and headphones, with a choice of English or Luganda (the local dialect). Students who stated they understood the video and assented/consented to participate were asked to provide an electronic signature and were given a signed paper copy to retain. For the qualitative data collection, we sought additional assent/consent from students and caregivers before conducting the interviews.

Data collection

For the quantitative data collection, participants were asked to complete a self-administered questionnaire on tablets using Open Data Kit (ODK) software. Qualitative interviews were conducted by social science researchers using a semi-structured topic guide in a private location at school that was comfortable for the interviewer and the interviewee. The qualitative interview guide was developed by the study team based on the study objectives and existing literature on adolescent menstrual health. The guide was informed by relevant conceptual frameworks and refined through iterative discussions within the qualitative research team. It was pilot tested in the study setting to ensure cultural appropriateness, clarity, and age-appropriate language, and minor revisions were made prior to data collection. The qualitative interview guides were designed to elicit in-depth information on participants’ views, experiences and decision-making processes regarding menstrual product use at midline and endline. Topics included utilization of the menstrual kit (components preferred and reasons for preference; components not used and reasons), experiences with reusable pads and menstrual cups, menstrual management practices before and after receiving the kit, reasons for non-receipt or non-use of the menstrual cup and, household norms and practices related to menstrual product use. The domains informed the analysis of individual, social and contextual factors influencing menstrual product preferences. Data collection was conducted in a language chosen by the participant (English or Luganda), and audio recorded. We conducted a total of 8 focus groups discussions (FGDs) (4 at midline, and 4 at endline) each with 6 female students; 12 in-depth interviews (IDIs) with female students (6 at midline, and 6 at endline); and 7 IDIs with caregivers at endline. The IDIs lasted about 35–50 min and the FGDs lasted about 120–140 min. Due to the sensitive nature of the topic, moderators spent some time at the start of the discussions building a rapport with the participants.

Intervention

All schools were given copies of the current Government guidance on MHM [18, 19]. In addition, the intervention schools received the MENISCUS intervention which included teacher training on puberty education, a student-led drama skit, a menstrual health kit and training on how to use the menstrual products, pain management support, basic improvements to the school water, sanitation, and hygiene facilities (WASH), and creation of a Menstrual Health Action Group to oversee the intervention. The kit consisted of the AFRIpads “schoolgirl kit” of five reusable pads, a towel, soap, two pairs of underwear, and menstrual health booklet with a menstrual tracker. Participants who consented to the cup also received a menstrual cup and a metallic storage container. The intervention was delivered by WoMena Uganda, a non-governmental organization (NGO). Girls were trained on how to insert and remove the cup, as well as how to wash and store the cup. Details of the intervention have been published elsewhere [16, 17]. The schools that received only the copies of the current Government guidance on MHM without the above intervention activities are referred to as control arm schools.

Quantitative data management

Research assistants synced the data to an ODK Central Server daily, and the data were exported to Stata version 18 for cleaning and analysis.

Definition of study outcomes

For this paper, we defined two endline study outcomes: (1) use of reusable menstrual pads (AFRIpads), estimated as the proportion of female participants who reported using reusable pads at LMP, among those who reported to have received reusable pads from MENISCUS; and (2) use of a menstrual cup, estimated as the proportion of female participants who reported using the cup at LMP among those who reported to have received a cup from MENISCUS.

Statistical analysis

We developed a conceptual framework showing our hypothesised relationships among baseline exposures and their relationships to the outcomes, among the intervention arm participants (Fig. 1). The most distal factors (Level 1) included individual, household and school-level socio-demographic factors: age, religion, ethnicity, type of caregiver, district, schooling category (day/boarding), social economic status (SES), school ownership (government vs. private), and schooling category (day vs. boarding). Level 2 factors were menstrual-related baseline exposures including type of menstrual product used at LMP, and menstrual knowledge and attitudes. Level 3 included factors related to menstrual experiences and confidence as measured by Menstrual Practice Needs Scale (MPNS) score [20], the Self-efficacy in Addressing Menstrual Needs Score (SAMNS) [21] respectively, and reported experience of menstrual pain at LMP. The MPNS score was derived from 36 items on the participants’ menstrual experience during LMP and it was calculated as the average of (i) the core items and school-specific items, and (ii) the relevant material-specific items [20].

Conceptual framework showing relationships between exposure and outcome variables

SES was derived from self-reported survey items on household assets, source of water, and toilet facilities using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Using the derived index (based on the first component), individuals were classified in quintiles with the lowest quintile corresponding to the poorest and the highest quintile the richest. Participants with higher values of the MPNS score [20] had fewer unmet menstrual needs, and those with higher values of the SAMNS-26 score [21] were more confident in carrying out menstrual-related tasks. We categorised the MPNS and SAMNS scores into tertiles. Type of menstrual product used at LMP was categorised as disposable only, reusable only, or both disposable and reusable.

We summarised baseline characteristics of participants using frequencies and proportions for categorical data and mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous data. For endline data, we first described the different combinations of menstrual products that participants reported using during LMP, stratified by arm. We then restricted analyses to intervention arm participants, to explore participants’ preferences for reusable menstrual products when they are provided for free, and to understand the factors associated with the use of the provided products during their LMP. We described the reported experiences using the cup, and the reasons for not using the provided reusable pads and menstrual cup at LMP.

We used random-effects logistic regression to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for associations of each outcome with exposures. We fitted separate models for each level, with each model including all variables at that level, adjusted for each other, and all variables on more distal levels as potential confounders. For example, to estimate the effect of the Level 2 exposures on the outcomes, we included in the model all the Level 1 and Level 2 variables. P-values for each exposure variable were estimated using Wald tests.

Qualitative data management and analysis

Multiple strategies were employed to enhance the trustworthiness of this study. Given the sensitivity of adolescent menstrual health, interviews were conducted in a confidential and supportive environment by trained female interviewers using age-appropriate, culturally sensitive topic guides. Interviews were conducted in English and Luganda, audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim, and Luganda interviews translated into English, with translations reviewed for accuracy by the lead social scientist. Each transcript was assigned a unique identification code to ensure confidentiality and facilitate data management. Codes reflected participant type and interview sequence (e.g. MEN-CS-FGD-FS-K10-(2)-5 ) reflects the study, school category, interview type, type of participant, school code, phase of data collection and participant’s number. Identifiers were removed from transcripts prior to analysis. Anonymized quotations were used in the results section to illustrate themes, representing the range of perspectives and grounding interpretations in participants’ actual words, thereby enhancing credibility and confirmability.

Data were analysed using a hybrid inductive–deductive thematic analysis. The deductive component was informed by study objectives and existing literature, enabling exploration of predefined domains relevant to programme implementation and lived experiences. The inductive component allowed identification of unanticipated themes emerging directly from participants’ accounts. Initial familiarisation involved repeated reading of transcripts to identify emerging patterns, which informed development of a preliminary coding framework. This framework was reviewed and refined by the qualitative team and organised into a matrix of codes, descriptive sub-themes, and overarching themes. Analysis was conducted in NVivo 14. Two social scientists (the team leader, not involved in programme implementation, and a senior social scientist) independently coded transcripts, iteratively comparing coding to ensure consistency and refine interpretations. Regular analytic discussions with the wider research team were held to resolve discrepancies, enhance dependability, and maintain rigor. An audit trail documenting coding decisions and framework revisions was maintained throughout.

This hybrid approach is well-established in applied qualitative health research, providing analytic flexibility while maintaining conceptual focus, and aligns with guidance on reflexive thematic analysis [22] and hybrid coding strategies [23]. This methodology allowed systematic examination of anticipated constructs while capturing context-specific and emergent insights.

Results

Study participants characteristics

A total of 3878 female students were recruited at baseline (n = 864 Kalungu, n = 3014 Wakiso; and n = 1937 control arm, n = 1941 intervention arm). Of these, 2932 (75.6%) were enrolled in a trial school at endline and completed the endline survey (1466 (75.7%) control arm participants, 1466 (75.5%) intervention arm participants). Thirteen participants (0.4%) had switched to a school in a different arm and were excluded from this analysis. At endline, 2622 (89.8%) participants reported having reached menarche and had a menstrual period in the past 6 months and were included in the analysis (1320 (90.6%) control arm, 1302 (89.1%) intervention arm).

The mean age of participants at baseline was 15.6 (standard deviation [SD] = 0.94). The majority (n = 1811, 69.1%) were of Muganda ethnicity, 1420 (54.2%) were day students, and 1683 (64.2%) attended privately owned schools (Table 2). The 946 baseline participants who did not complete the endline survey were older than those seen at endline (mean age: 15.7 (SD 0.99) vs. 15.6 (SD 0.94) years), more likely to be day students (58.1% vs. 54.2%) and in privately-owned schools (67.9% vs. 64.2%).

Menstrual product distribution and reported use in the intervention arm

Among the 1302 participants in the intervention arm, 1221 (93.8%) reported receiving reusable pads of whom 611 (50.0%) also received a menstrual cup (Fig. 2). A further two participants reported receiving only a menstrual cup, and 79 (6.1%) received neither product, likely due to their absence at school on kit distribution days. Of the 1221 participants who reported to have received reusable pads, 1079 (88.4%) reported ever using the pads and 845 (69.2%) reported using the pads at their LMP at the endline survey (Fig. 2). Of 613 participants who received a menstrual cup, only 226 (36.9%) reported ever using the cup and 156 (25.4%) reported using the cup at LMP at endline. Of 611 participants who received both reusable pads and a menstrual cup, 134 (21.9%) used both products at LMP at endline.

Table 1 shows the reported experiences with using reusable products provided by MENISCUS. Of the 226 participants who reported ever using the cup, 148 (65.5%) said it was comfortable (i.e. they did not feel it), and about half of participants reported it was easy to insert and remove. Similarly, of 156 participants who reported using the cup at their LMP at endline, 109 (69.9%) said it was comfortable. Among the 457 participants who received a menstrual cup but reported not using it at LMP at endline, almost half (N = 221; 48.4%) said they preferred another product, and 29.1% said they did not like using the cup. Of the 376 participants who received reusable pads but reported not using them at LMP at endline, about half (N = 196; 52.1%) said they preferredanother product and 23.7% reported that they did not like using them (Table 1).

Menstrual product use at endline in control and intervention arm participants

At endline, the proportion of participants reporting having used each product at LMP in control and intervention (irrespective of whether the participant received a free product or not) arms respectively were 89.9% and 78.0% for disposable pads; 23.9% and 59.2% for reusable pads; 3.7% and 14.4% for the menstrual cup; 10.3% and 10.1% for homemade pads; 4.1% and 5.5% for tampons; 26.4% and 21.0% for underwear alone; and 14.3% and 13.9% for toilet paper or cotton wool. Most participants, 64.2% (control arm 58.9%, intervention arm 69.6%) used more than one product at LMP. The most frequently-reported combinations of menstrual products used at LMP were disposable pads with a product other than reusable pads or a menstrual cup (33.3%) among control arm participants, and disposable pads with reusable pads (16.6%) among intervention arm participants (Fig. 3). As expected, due to the distribution of reusable pads in the intervention arm, the proportion using reusable pads was lower in control than intervention arm, either alone (2.0% vs. 7.6%) or in combination with disposable pads (7.5% vs. 16.6%) (Fig. 3).

Factors associated with use of reusable products provided by MENISCUS at LMP

Quantitative findings

In adjusted analyses, socio-demographic factors associated with use of reusable pads provided by the trial at LMP were older age (aOR = 1.70, 95%CI 1.10–2.62 for those aged ≥ 17 vs. 15 years; and aOR = 1.56, 95%CI 1.15–2.11 for those aged 16 vs. 15 years), lower SES (aOR = 1.78, 95%CI 1.13–2.81 for lowest vs. highest quintile), and being a day vs. boarding student (aOR = 2.47, 95%CI 1.81–3.37) (Table 2). Baseline menstrual-related factors associated with using reusable pads at endline LMP were using reusable pads only compared to using disposable pads only at baseline (aOR = 2.75, 95%CI 1.72–4.42), having many unmet menstrual practice needs (aOR = 1.51, 95%CI 1.05–2.18 for low vs. high MPNS score), and better menstrual self-efficacy (OR = 1.86, 95%CI 1.30–2.66 for high vs. low SAMNS score).

Individual factors

Choice and use of menstrual products relied on the participants’ knowledge and perceptions about products, how to use them, and ability to afford them. Most participants were unfamiliar with menstrual cups, which led to some hesitation in trying them. Knowledge of how to use the products also determined their preference. Issues around using products included difficulties in properly inserting cups, properly positioning pads on their underwear and properly washing and drying reusable pads.

Regarding menstrual cups, some participants reported discomfort and pain during their initial use, which they attributed to the size of the cups. Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

Several findings were consistent across data sources. Older age was associated with use of both reusable pads and menstrual cups in adjusted analyses, and qualitative accounts described how increased confidence, skills, and menstrual self-efficacy facilitated product uptake over time. Lower socioeconomic status and higher unmet menstrual practice needs were associated with use of reusable products quantitatively, aligning with narratives that affordability and cost savings were central drivers of product choice. Baseline use of reusable pads predicted subsequent use of both reusable pads and cups, corresponding with qualitative descriptions of familiarity with reusable materials easing transition to new products.

Qualitative findings clarified mechanisms underlying statistical associations. The higher likelihood of reusable pad use among day students reflected environmental factors described in interviews, including greater access to water, privacy, and time for washing and drying at home. Likewise, the association between menstrual self-efficacy and product use was illuminated by accounts of training, practice, and gradual development of competence in inserting cups and managing reusable pads.

Although quantitative analyses demonstrated menstrual cup uptake among older students and certain socio-demographic groups, qualitative data revealed persistent barriers not fully captured in survey measures, including fear of insertion, concerns about virginity loss, menstrual pain, stigma, and parental opposition. These findings highlight variability in experiences and social constraints that may limit cup use despite statistical associations.

Discussion

Menstrual product preferences

In this population of Ugandan secondary school adolescents, the majority (64.2%) used more than one menstrual product during their last period. In most cases, they preferred to use disposable pads in combination with other products regardless of whether they received free reusable products. When provided with free reusable pads and a menstrual cup, the participants’ tended to use these in combination with disposable pads. For example, the proportion using the free reusable pads in combination with disposable pads (alone or with other products) was about twice as high in the intervention compared to the control arm participants (37.6% vs. 19.1%). No participants used a combination of only the reusable pads and menstrual cup, even in the intervention schools where these products were freely provided. These findings suggest that providing adolescents with only free reusable pads and a menstrual cup may not improve their menstrual health as they may still prefer other products like the disposable pads.

Previous studies have found a preference for disposable pads over other products. For example, among secondary schoolgirls aged 10–19 years in rural communities in Nigeria [24], most participants (60.4%) preferred disposable pads over other products [24]. The preference for disposable pads when participants are provided with reusable products supports results from a systematic review and meta-analysis of menstrual product use among ~ 14,800 women and girls in 44 studies (with most coming from studies among schoolgirls in LMICs, including 6 studies from Uganda) [11]. In this review, in which reusable pads were distributed for free, one Ugandan study among secondary schoolgirls reported that the use of reusable pads ranged from 23 to 44% with most (93%) reporting regular use of disposable pads [11]. However, these studies did not show that girls use a combination of several products in a single period, and that in most cases they prefer to combine other products with disposable pads.

There was low uptake of menstrual cups in our study, and low usage among those who received a cup. Among participants who received a cup, the proportion who ever used the cup, and who used the cup at LMP, were low (36.9% and 25.4%, respectively). These findings align with a study among 1414 participants aged 16–24 years in Zimbabwe asked to choose between receiving free reusable pads or the cup where 88.0% chose reusable pads [8]. The low use of menstrual cups can be attributed to girls being unfamiliar with the cup, poor perceptions, attitudes, and fear for loss of virginity [8, 25]. However, other studies have reported higher usage of menstrual cups. A systematic review which identified three studies where menstrual cups were distributed for free together with reusable pads, reported 61.0% usage of menstrual cups in a Ugandan refugee settlement [11]. This high uptake could be due to fewer other available options among refugees compared with our study. However, a Kenyan study where one group of adolescent schoolgirls was provided with free menstrual cups (but not with reusable pads), reported a much higher usage of menstrual cups at 79.3% [26].

Factors associated with menstrual products preference

Our findings showed that adolescents frequently combine disposable pads with other products or alternate between various combinations throughout their menstrual periods. Key determinants of product preference included menstrual pain, location (home vs. school), access to essential resources (e.g., products, water, soap), availability of private and comfortable spaces, and the convenience of product management (e.g., day vs. night). Additionally, comfort, ease of use, prior training, and product familiarity played significant roles in decision-making. These findings are consistent with other studies, which identify comfort, leak protection, affordability, and knowledge of menstruation and menstrual product use as common reasons for selecting specific products [11, 12, 27]. Notably, adolescents’ preference for disposable pads was driven by their ease of disposal, familiarity, comfort, accessibility, and effective absorbency, with minimal need for maintenance. These findings are consistent with existing literature that highlights similar factors, including comfort, leak protection, affordability, and product knowledge, as key determinants of menstrual product preference [27–34]. For example, among students aged 14–18 in rural schools of Zambia, convenience and ease of use of disposable pads were key factors driving preference [32]. Similarly, a study conducted among schoolgirls in Ethiopia found that familiarity and accessibility of disposable products influenced their use [33]. Another study in Ethiopia on menstrual management and school absenteeism among female adolescents found that absorbency and maintenance were key requirements for products preferences [34].

In contrast, menstrual cups were less favoured by participants in our study. Similar to a systematic review on the acceptability of menstrual cups which indicated that up to 45% of women struggled with cup usage [35]. Our qualitative findings align with the Zimbabwean study that found barriers to cup use included perceptions that the cup could lead to loss of virginity, and poor knowledge or awareness about the cup [8]. This contrasts with the Kenyan study and a study conducted among medical students in South Africa which reported that majority of the students perceived the menstrual cup as convenient in terms of use, comfort, hygiene, and safety [36].

In our study, quantitative analysis revealed that the use of reusable pads and cups were associated with multiple factors including older age and having more unmet menstrual needs. The use of reusable products associated with increasing age could be explained by the increased menstrual knowledge, positive experiences with reusables, confidence in their bodies (for the cup), and reduced challenges for use such as cleaning and changing them outside the home [31]. The higher use of the reusable pads and the cup among students with many unmet menstrual needs, and with lower SES may be because these students have fewer menstrual product choices or are uncomfortable with their current choice. For example, a study conducted in Ghana among women of reproductive age found that use of reusable pads was associated with poorest SES compared to those in middle or higher statuses [37]. Also, a study in Nepal among women and adolescents showed that use of reusable pads was associated with increasing age, non-school going, and previous use of reusable pads [29]. The higher use of reusable pads among students with higher menstrual self-efficacy measured by the SAMNS score could be due to their confidence and positive perception of menstrual challenges when managing their periods [38–40]. For example, a study that explored young school women’s attitude and experiences with menstruation in rural Kenya links confidence with menstrual product management [40].

The qualitative findings complemented and helped explain the quantitative results. For example, older students were more likely to use reusable pads and menstrual cups, which aligns with qualitative reports that older participants had greater knowledge, skills, and confidence in managing products. Similarly, lower SES predicted higher use of reusable pads, consistent with participants describing affordability as a key factor in their product choices. Environmental constraints, such as privacy and water access in schools, explained differential use among day and boarding students.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this research include its large sample size, longitudinal design following in-school adolescents over a year, the use of mixed-methods of data collection and analysis, and its distinction as one of the few extensive school-based studies conducted in a setting with diverse sociocultural perceptions of menstrual cups. The qualitative data illuminated additional contextual and psychosocial factors, including beliefs about virginity, peer stigma, and time management of menstrual products, providing a richer understanding of product uptake and use. Together, the mixed-methods findings triangulate and complement one another, reinforcing the robustness and interpretive depth of the study. A limitation of our study is selection bias at endline, as participants who did not complete the endline survey were older, and more likely to be day students and in privately-owned school than those seen at endline. For example, we may be under-estimating the use of reusable products in our population, as these products are most often used by older students [37]. Another limitation is that this research was only conducted within two districts in Uganda, and given that ethnicity influenced product choice (especially around use of a menstrual cup), these results may not be generalizable to other settings whereby the sociocultural environment may be very different. It is therefore important that further studies are conducted to look at these factors in different settings such as remote rural areas, out of schoolgirls, and among girls with disabilities.

Conclusion

About two-thirds of in-school adolescents in this Ugandan setting used more than one menstrual product during their LMP, and the majority preferred to use disposable pads in combination with other products. This was similar whether adolescents were provided or not provided with free reusable products. Use of freely provided reusable pads and menstrual cup was affected by factors such as challenges with washing and drying, social stigma, and discomfort. This study suggests that interventions aiming to increase access to menstrual products among adolescent girls should include more than one product, including disposable pads. Further, this study underscores the need for integrated interventions to promote both uptake and sustained use of new reusable products. Reusable pads, given their higher acceptability, should be prioritized and ideally bundled with a supply of disposable pads to support product combinations commonly used by adolescents. Menstrual cups may be more appropriate for older adolescents with higher menstrual self-efficacy, and should be introduced with comprehensive training and support. All product distribution efforts must be accompanied by adequate WASH facilities to ensure safe use, as well as community engagement to address cultural norms and misconceptions particularly around virginity and internal product use. Programs should prioritize adolescents with greater unmet menstrual needs and lower socioeconomic status. Furthermore, menstrual health should be incorporated into national health and education policies, accompanied by financial support mechanisms, such as subsidies, to enhance accessibility.
