A scoping review of person-centred maternity care service in humanitarian and fragile settings
Authors: Wubshet D Negash, Asmamaw Atnafu, Robera Olana Fite, Kamalini Lokuge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWomen who live in humanitarian settings are three times more likely to die than those who live in peaceful environments. Person-centred maternity care (PCMC) is a fundamental human right for every woman, yet it is often overlooked in humanitarian contexts. This scoping review aims to summarise person-centred maternity healthcare in fragile and humanitarian contexts.MethodsA scoping review of quantitative and/or qualitative approach was conducted to assess person-centred maternity care in humanitarian settings. Research that assessed dimensions of PCMC such as autonomy, dignity, privacy, communication, confidentiality and supportive care was included. Electronic database searches of PubMed, MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Scopus and generic web searches (Google Scholar) were used to search for available evidence. We used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses criteria for scoping review statement. The data from the final selected articles were extracted into an Excel spreadsheet. Finally, we described the study characteristics and summarised the concept of person-centred care.ResultA total of 889 articles were identified. After exclusion by title and abstract, 71 articles were eligible for full-text review, and finally 16 articles were eligible for data extraction. Our findings revealed low respect and supportive care, poor communication and autonomy, and breach of privacy. Resource constraints, protracted insecurity, cultural and language barriers were attributed to poor person-centred sexual and reproductive health. The review identified several implemented interventions, including training for healthcare providers, access to language translators, social and cultural support programmes, free healthcare services and community engagement initiatives.ConclusionThere are significantly more reports of negative experiences of maternity healthcare services than positive ones. Prioritising culturally appropriate approach, simulation-based training for healthcare providers on person-centred care, community engagement and the integration of PCMC domains into existing health services are essential to improve quality maternal health.Keywords: Maternal health, Public Health, Health services research, Health systems evaluation

Introduction

Health services are inequitably distributed around the world and are often least accessible where they are most needed, which threatens people’s lives and well-being.1 More than 25% of the world population resides in humanitarian and fragile settings (HFS).2 Of this population, one-fourth are reproductive age women.3 The reproductive age women who live near high-intensity conflicts are three times more likely to die than those who live in peaceful environments.4 There is a high level of unmet need for sexual and reproductive health (SRH) information and services among internally displaced people and those living in humanitarian crisis sites,1 contributed by limited access to healthcare services, damaged health facilities and worn-out human resources.5 6 Although it is imperative to increase access to this care in such settings.7 Addressing SRH services often takes lower priority to other services in humanitarian setting8 and may also need a different approach than those health programmes implemented in stable settings.9

Humanitarian responses to crisis situations operate with limited resources, under potential security concerns and often under pressure to alleviate acute suffering and need.10 11 In response to the SRH problems outlined, the Inter-Agency Working Group on Reproductive Health in Crisis, which is comprised of non-governmental organisations (NGOs), donors, governments and UN agencies, developed a technical and programmatic guide for use during emergency situations. At the onset of every humanitarian emergency, the guide provides information about the Minimum Initial Service Package (MISP) such as prevention of sexual violence, reducing excess maternal and newborn mortality, and averting unintended pregnancy.12 As an international standard, providing MISP for SRH should be implemented in all crisis situations.12 Quality healthcare service is the core aspect of maternal and child health response in HFSs.13 There is a WHO call to take action for high quality of care in fragile, conflict and vulnerable settings.14 Limited data about maternal and child health in emergencies are also an obstacle for the design and implementation of effective interventions.7 Hence, similar to other stable settings, there is a need for assessment of how services are delivered in humanitarian contexts.15

For this scoping review, the concept of humanitarian is defined as ‘In which an event or series of events has resulted in a critical threat to the health, safety, security or well-being of a community or other large group of people. The coping capacity of the affected community is overwhelmed, the country’s infrastructure is disrupted, and external assistance is required. This can be the result of man-made events such as armed conflicts, natural disasters, epidemics or famine and often involves population displacement’.16 Fragility, which is interconnected with humanitarian crises, can be defined as exposure to multiple risks that result in an inadequate coping capability of the state or the population to mitigate the risks. Violence, poverty, inequality and displacement are frequent consequences of fragilities.17

A person-centred approach to healthcare means ensuring responsive and respectful care to the preferences, needs and values of patients. In the context of maternity services, person-centred care (PCC) emphasises dignity, respect, effective communication and emotional support throughout the childbirth experience.18 PCC is essential to improving the quality of maternal and child health.19 Studies have found that despite an increase in maternal health services, the reduction in maternal mortality is limited, revealing a fundamental gap in effectiveness.20 21

While proponents have argued that this gap can be addressed through person-centred models of respectful and dignified maternity care,21 22 studies reveal that abuse, mistreatment and obstetric violence are still common.23 24 A growing body of evidence suggests that excess maternal mortality associated with healthcare is largely caused by poor quality care rather than lack of access.20 25 Strengthening the quality of health services with efforts to comprehend context-specific challenges and developing PCC are required for improving the quality of care in humanitarian and fragile areas.26 Care that is person-centred ensures the rights, dignity and privacy of affected populations and provides compassionate, survivor-centred care. The policy also takes into account the needs of vulnerable groups, such as children, girls, women and marginalised communities as well as how they would like their care delivered.27 28

Healthcare providers and women accessing these services argue that just as in safe and stable settings, person-centred maternity care (PCMC) is crucial and requires to be implemented in humanitarian contexts.29 A person-centred approach to maternity care is a fundamental human right, and the same domains, focuses and characteristics apply for every woman in every place.20 27 Recently, the assessment of PCMC has made good progress from the patient and healthcare provider’s perspective.30 31 Moreover, interventions to improve PCMC among healthcare providers have been piloted in peaceful settings.31 These studies showed that patient-centred care can reduce the risk of maternal and neonatal complications. Similarly, communication and autonomy can increase postpartum family planning utilisation and neonatal health.32,34 On the other hand, pregnant mothers experience exploitation such as non-consented care, loss of decision-making and poor communication between mother and provider, which may lead to reduced adherence to care, psychosocial distress and negative effects on the health of the mother and child.35,37

Mistreatment of women, such as slapping, hitting, shouting at and abandoning, has a significant impact on the quality of care, particularly its PCC dimension.18 Delivering maternal health services with the approach of PCC ensures that the healthcare is not just clinically effective but also respectful, responsive and empowering the woman receiving it.18 Despite calls to improve the culture of care and/or to end disrespect, discrimination and detainment of women and newborns everywhere, the assessment of how person-centred maternal health services are delivered still lags behind.38 Between the years 2004 and 2013, studies tried to systematically evaluate service availability, utilisation and general programme effectiveness. The review did not specifically address how care is experienced by women, nor did it analyse key PCMC domains such as dignity, communication, autonomy or respect during maternity care.1 A systematic review of literature from 1990 to 2018 was also conducted to assess maternal and neonatal health interventions in conflict settings.10 Although valuable, this review focused on delivery mechanisms and intervention coverage, without detailed attention to the quality of care or the experiences of women receiving maternity care in these fragile settings. Another systematic review from the year 1980 to 2017 was more concerned with access and barriers to service use, rather than examining how maternity services are provided in a respectful, responsive and woman-centred manner. Further review was aimed to investigate the utilisation of SRH services based on the MISP guide during humanitarian crises and suggested that evidence-based SRH services are still very limited and more research is needed in such humanitarian contexts.39 However, this review was more concerned with access and barriers to service use, rather than examining how maternity services are provided in a respectful, responsive and woman-centred manner.

Because neither of these reviews explicitly focuses on PCMC nor do they synthesise evidence on how respectful, responsive and dignified care is provided in maternity services in humanitarian contexts, this leaves a substantial gap in the available evidence. Although the concept of person-centred care (PCC) is useful for maternal and child health outcomes, it is overlooked in HFs where conflict, vulnerabilities and displacement worsen mistreatment during labour and delivery.29 40 41 Therefore, this review aims to utilise available evidence on SRH programmes in humanitarian settings to assess the degree to which they explicitly meet the requirements of PCC. The findings will shed light on the facilitators and barriers of person-centred SRH care to adapt the concept to other unreached HFSs.

Methods and materials

This study utilised the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses criteria for scoping review statement in reporting findings (online supplemental file 1). The protocol was registered in open science framework, https://osf.io/qezdn/. The scoping review follows the scoping (familiarity with the existing studies about PCC), searching, screening, data extraction and finally data analysis steps42 43 put forward by WDN. To guide the mapping and assessment of the existing literatures, our PCMC operational definition centred on key dimensions such as communication, autonomy, dignity, respect and supportive care, which are consistent with the WHO maternal quality of care framework,18 44 and statement on avoiding disrespect and abuse during facility-based childbirth.45 These include ensuring that all women receive respectful, and dignified care, where their privacy is safeguarded, confidentiality is maintained, and all forms of mistreatment, discrimination or denial of services are closely avoided. Supportive care involves providing emotional support that is responsive to each woman’s needs, strengthens her confidence and allows for the presence of a birth companion of her choice. Effective communication and autonomy are also equally vital that every woman and her family be engaged through clear, respectful and timely interaction that empowers them to actively participate in their care. In addition, we expanded this definition to include other critical elements highlighted by healthcare providers as integral to respectful care, such as cultural sensitivity, trust and the health facility environment. These elements were also identified by women as essential to feeling respected and well cared for during maternity services.29 We conducted an initial search in PubMed using the terms humanitarian, refugee, PCC, maternal, newborn and child to identify relevant literature on PCMC in HFSs. The study was written following Arksey and O’Malley’s scoping review framework for writing a scoping review.

Step 1: identifying the research question

In this scoping review, we searched for PCC in the literature reporting maternal, newborn and child health (MNCH) programmes in the context of HFSs. We identified the existing literature, types of evidence and research gaps and provided a broad overview of the field. We synthesised literature to describe PCC, critically analyse the delivery of programmes and conceptualise PCC in humanitarian settings. The study questions are as follows. (1) What evidence exists regarding person-centred maternal and child health programmes in HFS? (2) What are the barriers and facilitators of person-centred SRH? (3) What are the implemented interventions towards improving person-centred SRH?

Step 2: identifying relevant studies

Different electronic databases of PubMed, MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Scopus and generic web searches (Google Scholar) were used to search for available evidence. The search strategy was developed by finding synonyms, keywords and medical headings and created in consultation with a librarian from Australian National University. Moreover, we added other searching strategies used in the previous systematic reviews on similar concepts1 2 15 (online supplemental file 2).

Step 3: selecting studies to be included in the review

We used the population, issues/concept, comparison and outcome (PICO) framework approach. The population was all reproductive age women, newborns and children, adolescents, physicians delivering any MNCH healthcare issue in HFSs. The concept was health service programmes and PCC (concepts, PCC domains such as autonomy, dignity, privacy, communication, confidentiality, social support and supportive care) in HFS. For this scoping review, the comparator element was considered optional rather than essential and studies were eligible regardless of whether they had a control or comparison group. Evidence of MNCH-related research in HFS was included. All available prereviewed literature conducted from 2010 were included. We selected the year 2010 because the idea of PCC on SRH has been prominent since that period.46

Based on the WHO quality of care framework, dignity, communication, autonomy, privacy, confidentiality, supportive care and essential physical resource availability were used for the presentation of PCC findings.18 44 Available evidence written in English was included. Scoping review allows us to use any type of article and summarise studies; hence, we used all types of articles.47 We used maternal obstetrics, newborn and child health programmes to assess PCMC as a primary outcome of interest. Then, we highlighted the best practices and innovative approaches that facilitate PCC in humanitarian settings. To be eligible, the available articles should hold the term ‘humanitarian and/or fragile’ or refugee or other synonym words and variant spellings of ‘patient-centred care’ and women and/or child health. Furthermore, the study excluded brief reports, brief communications, preprints and presentations (table 1).

Step 4: charting the data

All studies collected from different electronic databases were exported, merged and managed using EndNote V.21 software. Using Covidence and the manual method, duplicated studies were removed. WDN screened the eligibility of articles. Although formal quality assessment is not a mandatory requirement for scoping review, a basic appraisal was conducted to provide an overview of methodological rigour of included articles. The Critical Appraisal Skills Program checklist was used to appraise the 14 studies, focusing on key components such as clarity of aims, suitability of methods, research design, recruitment, ethical considerations, analysis and value of findings. For the two studies employing mixed methods design, the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool was used to assess the quality of both the qualitative and quantitative components as well as the integration of the data. Overall, the studies demonstrated good methodological quality. While most studies met the criteria, some lacked detailed reporting on researcher-participant relationships or reflexivity (online supplemental file 3).

The data from the final selected articles were extracted into an Excel spreadsheet. The data were extracted by WDN and ROF. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and with KL. The following data were extracted; The basic characteristics of the included articles (author and publication year, country, type of study, study participants (women, clinicians, other NGOs), target population, type of programme or service, aim of the programme, evaluation design/methods, key findings that relate to the review questions (indicators of PCC), contextual factors that might influence PCC and major findings of each study were recorded in Excel.

Step 5: collating, summarising and reporting the results

We described and presented the study characteristics in tabular summary. We summarised the concept of PCC based on dimensions of dignity and supportive care, autonomy and communication, privacy and confidentiality, social support and health facility environment.27 28 We also reported facilitators, barriers and interventions to improve person-centred SRH healthcare.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in this study.

Results

Search results

A total of 889 articles were identified using electronic databases and citation chaining. All the identified articles were exported into EndNote V.21 and then to Covidence. We excluded 41 duplicated articles by Covidence and manually. Among 848 articles, 777 articles were excluded based on title and abstract screening because the articles did not report information for PCC. A total of 71 articles were eligible for full-text review. Among the 71 articles, 34, 15 and 6 articles were excluded because of unavailable outcome of interest, wrong population and wrong setting, respectively. Finally, a total of 16 articles were eligible for data extraction (online supplemental file 4).

Characteristics of articles

Five articles were published in 2024, one in 2016, one in 2017, one in 2018, one in 2019, two in 2020, three in 2021 and two in 2022. The included studies were conducted in various countries including, Chad,29 Bangladesh,48 Lebanon,11 Afghanistan,40 49 Nigeria and Central African Republic,50 Uganda,51 Australia,52 Somalia,53 Iran,54 Colombia,55 South Sudan,56 Poland,57 USA,58 Syria59 and Canada.60 Fifteen articles involved qualitative data collection or mixed methods1129 40 49,60 that followed focus group discussion40 48 51 53 57 59 60 and in-depth interviews.11 29 40 48 51 53 54 56 59 Of the 16 articles, six articles were conducted in a refugee context.11 29 40 48 51 58 Three articles were conducted among migrants from humanitarian settings. Six studies were conducted about childbirth healthcare services. The detailed characteristics of all the identified articles are available in online supplemental file 5.

Person-centred maternity care

Dignity and supportive care

Among the 16 articles, 11 articles reported about dignity and supportive care.1129 40 48,51 54 Women reported both negative and positive healthcare experiences. Three articles reported on the positive experience of PCC: well treatment, feeling supported, good dignity and respect. Six articles, however, described women’s experience of mistreatment in the form of disrespect, discrimination, poor support, inequitable care and verbal abuse.

Communication and autonomy

A total of 12 articles discussed the communication and autonomy component of PCC.1129 40 48 50 52,58 Almost all studies reported the absence of good communication between women and healthcare providers. Similarly, negative experiences of decision-making power were reported in all studies.

Privacy and confidentiality

Privacy and confidentiality were reported in eight articles.1129 48,51 53 57 Of the eight studies, six studies reported breach of privacy either during childbirth or in the maternity room. Confidentiality is also violated due to the presence of more than one woman and/or provider in the room.53

Birth companion and health facility

Six articles mentioned social support and health facilities.11 29 52 56 59 60 Although two articles reported clean and convenient health facilities, all the findings reported negative experiences of women with respect to social support. Women were not allowed to be supported by birth companion and/or social support and were held responsible for their own health (table 2).

Facilitators and barriers of PCC

As described in table 3, 7 articles reported facilitators1129 48 52 57,59 and 12 articles dealt with barriers towards PCC.1129 40 48,51 53 With respect to facilitators, availability of language translators, good collaborations and mutual support among staff, staff training, client capacity building and community engagement were mentioned. On the other hand, lack of understanding of patients’ culture, persistent shortage of supplies and medication, frequent staff turnover, community stigma and discrimination, poor functional services were mentioned as barriers to PCMC.

Discussion

This scoping review identified 16 studies on PCC of women while accessing maternity healthcare services in HFSs. PCC is a key component for quality health services that considers the values, preferences and respect of the patients with the emphasis on dignity and supportive care, autonomy, clear and effective communication, confidentiality, privacy and social support dimensions.18 29 44 All the identified studies included at least one of the PCC dimensions. Despite a few studies reported incidents of positive PCC, the overall findings uncovered that person-centred maternal care was practised poorly, with women reflecting different ways of mistreatment: disrespect, less support, poor communication, breach of privacy and confidentiality, and no social support.

Regarding dignity and supportive care, our research found that women often experienced negligence, prejudice, rudeness, discrimination and verbal abuse by healthcare providers. Only a few studies suggest otherwise. The finding aligns with other systematic reviews, which reports negative attitudes and behaviours reflected as verbal and physical abuse, rudeness and neglect.61 62 Lack of dignified and respectful care from the healthcare providers is likely to cause negative perception by women and partners and families and consequently affects the well-being of clients.63 Therefore, in humanitarian settings, where women’s dignity may be particularly vulnerable, healthcare providers must be intentional about establishing rapport by consistently demonstrating respect, offering friendly support and creating a welcoming environment during antenatal care, childbirth and postnatal care.

Elsewhere reviews indicated that effective provider–patient communication and involvement of patients in care planning are important for informed decision-making and may contribute to PCC.64 65 Findings in this review highlighted that almost all the studies largely reported negative experiences of women with maternal health services in humanitarian settings. Women faced significant SRH challenges. They reported a lack of decision-making power regarding medication, poor communication with providers and limited choice of both providers and health facilities. Furthermore, they had infrequent opportunities to ask questions or seek clarification on their treatment plans, hindering their sense of autonomy. Consequently, it results in suffering for women and an obstacle for accessing skilled birth attendance.62 Therefore, it is mandatory to counsel women to create awareness about patient rights and the way to communicate between refugee women from humanitarian settings and host country healthcare providers. Additionally, it is important to consider patients’ preferences of either family members or other skilled interpreters to assist their communication with the healthcare provider.

The review highlighted that despite some women reporting sufficient privacy and satisfaction with the healthcare in refugee camps,29 48 others reported breaches of privacy through removing all clothes of the woman, non-consented vaginal examination, presence of more than one man and/or patients in the delivery room and shameful childbirth experiences. These findings are similar to another study where lack of confidential care, discrimination and non-consented care were common ways of mistreatment.62 Hence, intervention, such as training and onsite monitoring of healthcare providers about patient privacy and confidentiality, is needed to consider the concept as a fundamental attribute of human rights and good PCMC.

Every woman should be offered the opportunity to get social support and have a birth companion with her during labour and delivery.66 Birth companionship has been shown to enhance both emotional and physical support, resulting in a good birth outcome.67 Our study revealed that no family or social support was available nearby during labour and delivery, left women to take responsibility for their own healthcare and resulted in women suffering anxiety and depression. The probable reason for lack of family and/or other social support might be due to limited space, privacy issues for other women, overcrowding and presence of other emergency cases. Regarding the health facility and service environment, our findings showed that only a few studies reported on the condition of health facility and refugee camps. Among those, women generally expressed no complaints, with some describing the facilities and camps as clean and well-organised.11 29

Facilitators and barriers towards PCC

The use of professional language translators and online language translation applications in health facilities was utilised, this was reported to have facilitated PCMC for refugee and migrant women. This is because good communication leads to desirable health outcomes and minimises potential health risks and promotes good compliance with medication. However, the use of language translation by host country healthcare providers to understand refugee and migrant women’s health issues may raise privacy and ethical concerns.57 68

Good collaboration and mutual support among staff members29 as well as cooperation between mainstream and multicultural healthcare providers52 and mobilised personal relationships57 were all noted as facilitators towards PCMC. Moreover, healthcare providers’ capacity, commitment and dedication were also identified as facilitators. The finding is similar with another scoping review conducted among migrant and refugee women, where collaboration and integration between health facilities is a facilitator to PCMC.69

Community participation is important for health service utilisation, to understand sociocultural dynamics and programme sustainability.70 Similarly, this scoping review also revealed that community mobilisation, participation and engagement at the early stage of programme implementation are found to be facilitators towards person-centred SRH. This is because community engagement helps to identify and address barriers to obtaining health services, builds trust in health system, ensures that health services are personalised to individual needs and offers people a sense of ownership.

This review supports the findings of previous review that identifies factors affecting PCC among adult immigrants and refugees based on 16 studies from 2010 to 2019.69 The review highlighted lack of training or professional development, and language-based challenges that prohibit progress towards PCC. Consistently, the current review also identified common barriers such as linguistic and cultural barriers between healthcare providers and women for effective communication and cultural understandings. The finding is similar with another scoping review69 where lack of cultural competency and language-based challenges were barriers for immigrant and refugee women. The finding is an indicator for further research or interventions such as language and culture trained or family interpreters.71

Resource constraints, such as inadequate supplies and equipment, community stigma and discrimination for adolescent females, healthcare providers overwork, turnover and stress, limited trained healthcare staff on how to deliver culturally competent care, burnout, lack of clear policies and the devaluing of respectful maternity care are reported as barriers in the identified studies. The review also revealed lack of knowledge to use guidelines to treat women fleeing war and women trust to traditional birth attendants than healthcare providers. Other deterrents such as protracted insecurity, transportation problems and poor functional services and no organisational support to healthcare providers are identified from the identified articles.

Interventions to improve PCC

Regarding implemented interventions towards improving PCMC were trainings and onsite monitoring of healthcare providers, client capacity-building, available language translators and grassroot initiatives such as online language translation to overcome language barrier to improve understanding and satisfaction between healthcare providers and women. The training improved providers’ attitudes and communication with patients and improved patient trust. This is because the training and provision of onsite technical support help the healthcare provider to be initiative, responsive and updated.

Socioeconomic status (ie, unable to cover health costs) and cultural background (ie, different customs, religious beliefs) are common phenomena that influence health-seeking behaviour.72 To overcome these challenges, our review identified some implemented interventions such as social and culture supporting programmes of community-based multicultural health brokers, the Protection, Gender and Health (ProGeSan) programme to improve SRH and beyond health concerns were the identified interventions towards PCMC.29 60

Free healthcare services for refugee women increased utilisation where available, as per patient reports, inclusion of local and community liaisons, maternity social work team are reported as key to increasing awareness and trust in the health system, contributing to better service uptake.52 Other interventions such as persistent advocacy, policy level communication and integration of services with the existing and non-governmental organisations were reported interventions to improve maternal health services in humanitarian settings.

Study strengths and weaknesses

To the investigators’ knowledge, this is the first review to synthesise a timely and underexplored issue of PCMC in humanitarian settings, taking a comprehensive approach that examines key care domains such as autonomy, dignity, communication, privacy, confidentiality and supportive care, in contrast to previous reviews that were limited to non-humanitarian settings. We expanded our search to include all studies that reported any aspects of PCC, not just those explicitly reviewing the concept of PCC as previous reviews have done. Our study was not only limited to PCMC but also highlighted enabling, barriers and interventions that support PCC. Moreover, the study provides action-oriented recommendations to integrate PCMC into existing SRH services and provider training that can inform policy and programme development aimed at improving maternal health outcomes in these humanitarian settings. This scoping review has limitations to be noted. Due to limited evidence and richness of information in humanitarian settings, only two studies were able to explore the whole component of PCC, otherwise, most of the studies did not include all the PCC dimensions. Additionally, we considered studies that tried to assess PCC in both humanitarian and non-humanitarian contexts. Although we used the PICO framework to guide our search strategy and study selection, we acknowledge that many of the included studies did not have an explicit comparator element. However, PICO still supported a structured approach to study selection and data extraction, even in the absence of formal comparison groups in some studies. Our study included only English language publications, which obviously limited our results. Due to strict search strategy, we excluded some SRH services, such as gender-based violence, sexually transmitted infections and HIV/AIDS. Inclusion of these themes might be useful in future reviews.

Conclusion

Our review found that women in humanitarian settings experienced negative PCC, especially frequent instances of women being mistreated, abused, disrespected, breached of privacy and confidentiality, lack of decision-making power, poor communication and not being supported during childbirth by birth companions. Improvements in patient experience and patient-provider interaction should therefore be made by integrating the concept of PCC with the existing international, national and local health systems. Given the number of limited available studies and not fully assessed, our review demonstrates the need for further primary studies with the inclusion of all PCC core dimensions (ie, dignity, autonomy, communication, respect, supportive care, confidentiality and privacy) in HFSs comparable to stable contexts.
