Effect of genotype × environment interactions on starch physicochemical properties in diverse sorghum germplasm
Authors: Muhammad Khubaib Zahid, Jing Yu, Yaqi Hu, Huabing Lu, Jinsong Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genotypic diversity and genotype-environment (G × E) interactions affecting starch quality parameters were investigated in 28 sorghum genotypes over two growing seasons. Starch granule diameter ranged from 3.22 to 29.20 μm. Amylose content effectively distinguished waxy genotypes (∼5%) from non-waxy (∼20–33%) genotypes. Starch phosphorous content ranged widely from 109.26 to 618.06 ppm in environment 1 (E1) and 106.94 to 1753.24 ppm in environment 2 (E2). Starches in E1 had higher gelatinization temperatures but lower enthalpy than those in E2. Dynamic rheological analysis indicated that all starches displayed elasticity-dominated, weak-gel behavior, while steady-shear analysis showed consistent shear-thinning behavior with a flow index <1. Principal component analysis of starch quality parameters revealed 60.0% and 61.9% of total variance for E1 and E2, respectively. In addition, correlation analysis further highlighted a significant negative association between amylose content and crystallinity, thermal/retrogradation properties, and shear-thinning behavior across both environments. G × E results also showed significant impact of growing conditions on sorghum starch phosphorus levels, pasting and thermal properties. Together, these findings provide a foundation for developing improved sorghum cultivars tailored to food and industrial applications.

Introduction

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench) is a climate-hardy C4 cereal. Its low irrigation demand and stable yields reduce production risk and support resilient food systems in semiarid regions (Gardi et al., 2025). China annually produces almost 3 million tons of sorghum on 0.64 million hectares (FAO, 2023), highlighting the crop's economic relevance. Sorghum also has diverse applications, including use as human food, fodder, and in industrial products such as bioethanol and biodegradable materials (Khoddami et al., 2023; Zahid et al., 2025; Zhu, 2014).

Sorghum starch consists of two glucose polymers: amylose and amylopectin which are organized into discrete granules (Pérez & Bertoft, 2010). Amylose content (AC) predominantly determines the physicochemical and structural properties of starches. For example, higher AC typically lowers peak viscosity, increases setback and gel firmness, and often reduces relative crystallinity and reheating stability (Gao et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2023). Furthermore, AC influences digestibility by promoting resistant-starch formation and restricting enzymatic access (Yang et al., 2024). One study reported that waxy or low-amylose sorghum starches have higher gelatinization temperatures than normal AC starches (Sang et al., 2008). Collectively, these studies underscore the important role of AC in shaping the physicochemical characteristics of sorghum starch, thereby influencing specific end-use applications.

Sorghum starch physicochemical and structural traits are affected by growing conditions. For instance, sorghum starch AC, pasting, texture, and thermal parameters showed significant genotype by environment (G × E) interaction effects, even environmental variance exceeded genotypic differences in some traits (CPV, Tp and ΔH) (Beta & Corke, 2001). In contrast, a study showed that AC only had a strong genotypic effect, whereas environment and G × E interaction were non-significant (Wirnas et al., 2024). Similarly, Malathi et al. (2024) stated that genetic factors contributed >96% of total variance in amylopectin content, with modest environmental effects (2.8%) across genotypes. Also, warmer growing season can significantly increase sorghum starch gelatinization temperatures relative to cooler season (Kaufman et al., 2017). Similarly, at increased growth temperatures, sorghum starch yield per grain, granule size, and amylopectin branching significantly reduced, but no effect was observed on AC (Li et al., 2013). Additionally, delayed harvesting raises gelatinization enthalpy due to a gradual increase in crystalline order (Kang et al., 2023). Thus, multi-environment trials are essential in determining the potential of sorghum starches for food and industrial applications. However, previous studies lack a comprehensive assessment of sorghum starch physicochemical and structural parameters under varying environmental conditions.

The main objective of this study was to quantify genetic diversity in sorghum for starch quality traits including granule morphology, crystallinity and lamellar structure, amylose and phosphorus content, pasting properties, thermal and retrogradation properties, and rheological properties across two environments, and to assess the effect of environment on starch properties among sorghum genotypes. The ultimate goal was to dissect the genotypic, environmental, and G × E variance that shapes starch structure and properties. The findings from this study are expected to provide food processors with information on stable starch properties and offer sorghum breeders insights to improve starch quality for food and industrial applications.

Material and methods

Plant materials

Thirty-two sorghum genotypes (SG01–SG32) were grown at Huajiachi Campus, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China, during 2021 (E1) and 2024 (E2). Trials used a randomized complete block design with two replications. All genotypes were sown from early-July to early-August and harvested in mid-October to late November, following standard culturing practices. Of the initial 32, 28 genotypes (18 waxy and 10 non-waxy) produced enough starch for physicochemical and structural analysis. Detailed environmental conditions for both growing seasons, including temperature, precipitation, and sunshine duration data (July–November for E1 and E2), are provided in Table S1.

Starch isolation

Sorghum starch was isolated using a modified protocol after Beta and Corke (2001). Dehulled seeds (100 g) were soaked overnight at 4 °C in 0.25% (w/v) NaOH (1:3 seed to solution). The seeds were rinsed five times with distilled water and blended for 3 min (Joyoung C020E, China). The slurry was treated again with 0.25% NaOH and left overnight at 4 °C to settle and remove protein. After discarding the top liquid, the starch was mixed with distilled water and repeatedly filtered through 100–200 mesh sieves. Filtrates were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min, and the top liquid and brown protein layer were removed. Washing and centrifuging with distilled water and 95% methanol were repeated until the starch was white, with little protein and lipid left. Starch was then dried at 37 °C for 24 h, milled, sieved through a 200 mesh, and stored at 4 °C until use.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Granule morphology was examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (Gemini 300, ZEISS, Germany) operated at 3 kV following Gao et al. (2022). Starch was dusted onto stubs lined with conductive carbon tape, sputter-coated with gold, and imaged at 1000× magnification, using identical settings across samples. Mean diameter of all individual starch granules was measured by ImageJ software (v 1.54).

X-ray diffraction

X-ray diffraction was used to assess long-range order in starch with a D8 Advance diffractometer (Bruker, Germany) set to 40 kV and 40 mA. Diffraction patterns were recorded from 2θ = 5 to 40° at 0.05° increments. Relative crystallinity (RC) was determined from the crystalline and amorphous regions using Origin software (Zhang et al., 2022).

Small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)

The synchrotron SAXS patterns for sorghum starches were determined as described by Kuang et al. (2017). Additionally, lamellar parameters were derived from SAXS 1D correlation functions including crystalline thickness (dc), amorphous thickness (da), long period distance (dac = da + dc), and the Bragg repeat distance (D = 2π/q).

Apparent amylose content (AAC)

Sorghum starch AAC was measured based on iodine colorimetry method (Ahmad et al., 2025; Hovenkamp-Hermelink et al., 1988; Wickramasinghe et al., 2009).

Phosphorous content (PC)

Sorghum starch PC was determined based on dry-ash method as described by Ahmad et al. (2025) and final values were determined in ppm units.

Pasting properties

Pasting behavior was measured on an RVA (Model 4500, Perten, Hägersten, Sweden) using the Standard-1 program in TCW3 v3.1, following Zhou et al. (2018). The software recorded peak (PV), hot-paste (HPV), cool-paste (CPV), breakdown (BD) and setback (SB) viscosities (in RVU units), also pasting temperature (Ptemp), and peak time (PeT).

Thermal and retrogradation properties

Sorghum starch thermal (Gelatinization) properties were measured as described by Ahmed et al. (2018) using Differential Scanning Calorimeter (DSC: Q20, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE). Retrogradation parameters were determined after 7 days (stored at 4 °C) using same DSC profile.

Rheology

A 6% (w/w) starch suspension was gelatinized at 100 °C, cooled to room temperature, and tested on a rheometer (DHR-2, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) with parallel plates (40 mm, 0.5 mm gap) at 25 °C. The linear viscoelastic range was verified by an amplitude sweep at 1 Hz over 0.01 to 100% strain. Frequency sweeps (1 to 100 rad/s) were then run to obtain storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), complex modulus (G*), and loss tangent (tan δ). Steady-shear tests characterized flow behavior across 0.1 to 100 1/s. A power-law model was fitted to shear data;(i)τ=Kγnwhere τ is shear stress (Pa), K the consistency coefficient (Pa.sn), n the flow behavior index, and γ the shear rate (1/s).

Statistical analysis

All measurements were run in duplicate. Descriptive statistics (mean, minimum, maximum) were calculated, and Tukey's range test, boxplots and Pearson correlation coefficients were computed in R software. Correlation heatmaps were generated with the ggcorrplot package. PCA was conducted using the prcomp package and visualized with the factoextra package. Genotype × environment effects were assessed in OriginPro software through multivariate ANOVA, with genotype, environment, and their interaction included as fixed factors.

Results and discussion

Starch granule

Sorghum starch granules were irregularly spherical to polygonal, with surface pores and dents evident on all genotypes across both environments (Fig. 1A–B). Granule diameter ranged 4.00 to 27.83 μm in E1 and 3.22 to 29.20 μm in E2 with no prominent difference between waxy and non-waxy groups. Crystallinity and lamellar order

X-ray analysis revealed consistent A-type pattern for all sorghum genotypes at diffraction angles (2θ) of 15°, 17°, 18°, and 23° (Fig. S2A–B), characteristics of sorghum starch (Kang et al., 2023). Relative crystallinity (RC) varied significantly among genotypes across both environments (Fig. 2B), In E1 waxy genotypes ranged 22.50% to 27.64% and non-waxy 19.70% to 21.58%; whereas in E2, waxy ranged 20.97% to 27.32% and non-waxy 18.45% to 21.02%. E1 also showed slightly higher mean RC (23.47%) than E2 (22.54%) (Table S2). Similar sorghum starch RC ranges of 18.6% to 31.2% were reported previously (Dos Santos et al., 2023; Sandhu et al., 2021; Zhong et al., 2020).

SAXS profiles showed well-defined peak intensities (q) at 0.05 to 0.09 Å−1 for all genotypes which aligns with previous reports (Kang et al., 2022; Kang et al., 2023; Kaufman et al., 2017). The derived lamellar parameters (D, da, dc, dac) in E1 for waxy genotypes ranged 8.90 to 9.21 nm, 2.39 to 2.59 nm, 6.12 to 6.49 nm, and 8.61 to 9.02 nm, respectively and non-waxy ranged 9.21 to 9.63 nm, 2.15 to 2.44 nm, 6.57 to 7.33 nm, and 9.02 to 9.52 nm for non-waxy genotypes, respectively. Similarly, in E2 the D, da, dc and dac ranged 8.80 to 9.10 nm, 2.37 to 2.55 nm, 6.11 to 6.43 nm, and 8.61 to 8.81 nm for waxy genotypes, and 9.18 to 9.42 nm, 2.06 to 2.23 nm, 6.87 to 7.36 nm, and 9.07 to 9.42 nm for non-waxy genotypes, respectively (Table S2). (2020) reported ranges of D, da, dc and dac for waxy sorghum as 9.10 to 9.28 nm, 2.42 to 2.49 nm, 6.56 to 6.80 nm and 9.01 to 9.22 nm, respectively. Another study reported range of D (9.4–10.7 nm), da (1.2–2.6 nm), dc (3.6–6.1 nm), and dac (6.1–7.5 nm) (Kang et al., 2023). Furthermore, lamellar peak intensity decreased with increasing AAC but increased with increase in RC across both environments, also evident by XRD results. AAC

AAC is a primary determinant of sorghum starch functionality. AAC ranged 4.01% to 7.30% for waxy and 20.20% to 32.16% for non-waxy genotypes in E1; whereas in E2, waxy ranged 2.67% to 6.32% and non-waxy 24.92% to 32.49% AAC (Fig. 2C). Although, no significant difference was found between mean AAC values for E1 (13.73%) and E2 (13.57%) (Table S2). Our results aligns with reported values of 5.5% to 7.3% AAC for waxy sorghum genotypes (Yan et al., 2011) and 25.7% to 39.1% for non-waxy genotypes (Hill et al., 2012). Another study reported AAC range of 20% to 25% for non-waxy genotypes (Ali & Hasnain, 2014). A broader panel of sorghum genotypes also showed AAC range of 0.2% to 28.2% (Ping et al., 2019). Sorghum starch with higher AAC can produce stronger gels than those of lower AAC starch, suitable for food applications (Sang et al., 2008).

PC

Phosphorus content of sorghum starch exhibited significant genotypic divergence and clear environmental modulation (Fig. 2D). The values reported here represent total starch-bound phosphorus, and therefore include both amylose-associated phospholipids and phosphate monoesters esterified to amylopectin (Morrison, 1964). In cereals, non-waxy starches typically contain higher levels of lysophospholipids complexed with amylose, whereas waxy starches may contain only trace amounts of covalently bound phosphate groups (Kasemsuwan & Jane, 1996). PC ranged widely with mean values significantly higher in E2 (592.97 ppm) than in E1 (270.76 ppm) (Table S2), indicating substantial environmental influence on phosphorus deposition in the developing endosperm. These values fall within the previously reported range for sorghum starch from 83 ppm to 1006 ppm (Subrahmanyam & Hoseney, 1995).

Functionally, starch-bound phosphorus can influence molecular organization and pasting behavior. Phospholipids complexed with amylose can weaken amylose–amylose interactions, increasing chain mobility during heating and promoting shear-thinning behavior in starch pastes (Blennow et al., 2001; Subrahmanyam & Hoseney, 1995). In contrast, phosphate monoesters introduce localized negative charges along amylopectin chains, enhancing electrostatic repulsion and granule hydration, which may stabilize semi-crystalline lamellae and shift thermal transition temperatures (Blennow et al., 2000).

It ranged 287.09 to 456.00 RVU for waxy genotypes and 275.83 to 336.71 RVU for non-waxy genotypes in E1; whereas in E2, waxy ranged 245.83 to 363.67 RVU and non-waxy 303.08 to 415.92 RVU. HPV represents swollen starch granule disintegration. Thermal and retrogradation properties

DSC results showed wide variation in thermal properties of sorghum starch across environments (Fig. 5A–D). In E1, waxy genotypes showed To (64.53–74.28 °C), Tp (74.26–78.03 °C), Tc (79.60–83.93 °C), and ΔHg (11.59–16.49 J/g) and non-waxy genotypes showed To (69.12–72.13 °C), Tp (72.40–75.82 °C), Tc (77.33–81.37 °C), and ΔHg (11.27–14.77 J/g). In comparison, under E2 conditions, waxy genotypes had To (63.46–73.47 °C), Tp (70.38–78.50 °C), Tc (76.68–85.46 °C), and ΔHg (14.00–18.97 J/g), while non-waxy genotypes had To (64.78–68.73 °C), Tp (70.32–74.92 °C), Tc (75.18–81.50 °C), and ΔHg (12.87–16.25 J/g). These results are consistent with previous reports on sorghum starch thermal data. For instance, waxy starch generally gelatinizes at higher temperatures and with greater enthalpy than non-waxy starch (Yang et al., 2024, Singh, Chang, Lin, Singh and Singh, 2011). Our data also showed that mean values for thermal indices (To, Tp and Tc) for E1 were significantly higher than those for E2 (Table S4). Given that starch biosynthesis and crystalline organization occur primarily during the late reproductive and grain filling stages, these differences likely reflect variation in growing conditions during endosperm development prior to harvest. Rheological properties

Rheology defines gel performance. Steady shear behavior was modeled with the Power Law parameters (K and n) (Fig. 2E–F) and moduli (G′, G″, G* and tan δ) were determined from small-amplitude oscillation across both environments. All gels were shear-thinning (n < 1); viscosity decreased and shear stress increased with shear rate (Fig. 6C, 6F [E1]; 7C, 7F [E2]). In E1, K ranged 3.84 to 22.74 Pa.sn for waxy, and 10.56 to 42.97 Pa.sn for non-waxy. In E2, waxy K ranged 1.23 to 21.61 Pa.sn, and non-waxy 24.32 to 55.98 Pa.sn. Furthermore, n ranged 0.314 to 0.685 for waxy and 0.172 to 0.448 for non-waxy in E1, and 0.332 to 0.785 for waxy and 0.185 to 0.309 for non-waxy in E2 (Table S2). Frequency curves across both environments showed that G′ and G″ increased with angular frequency (Fig. 6A–B, 6D–E; 7A–B, 7D–E). For all sorghum starches, G′ exceeded G″, indicating an elasticity-dominant and weak-gel response (Jafari & Koocheki, 2024). Notably, non-waxy starches consistently displayed higher G′, G″, and G* than waxy starches in both environments. This trend supports the observation that non-waxy genotypes undergo faster amylose retrogradation and form a denser gel network (Sang et al., 2008). In addition, tan δ remained less than 1 in all samples, confirming solid-like properties throughout. However, tan δ was higher in waxy genotypes than in non-waxy genotypes, reflecting that waxy starches are less elastic and more viscous (Cabrera-Ramírez et al., 2021).

Principle component analysis

PCA clarified covariation among sorghum starch traits. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 60% of the total variance for E1 and 61.9% for E2 (Fig. 8A–B). In E1, PC1 (47.9%) captured variation mainly in AAC, pasting traits (CPV, SB), RC, thermal traits (Tc, Tp), lamellar traits, and rheological parameters. PC2 (12.1%) was associated with PV, BD, Ptemp, PeT, Tor, ΔHr and R%. In E2, PC1 (45.2%) mainly represented AAC, pasting traits (PV, CPV, BD), RC, and thermal (To, Tp, Tc), lamellar, and rheological traits. PC2 (16.7%) captured variability in SB, PeT, Tcr, Tpr, and MD. Correlation analysis

Correlation analysis revealed that AAC was positively correlated with pasting traits (CPV, SB), rheological trait (K), and lamellar traits (D, dc, dac) across both environments (Fig. 9). Additionally, AAC showed negative correlation with Ptemp, n, RC, MD, da, thermal traits (To, Tp, Tc, ΔHg), retrogradation traits (Tor, Tpr, ΔHr). These observations align with previous multi-genotype sorghum starch studies (Beta & Corke, 2001; Kaufman et al., 2017; Yan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). In parallel, RC opposed AAC and showed significant positive correlation with thermal traits, indicating that short-range order raises thermal transitions and aligns with waxy genotypes across both environments. Similarly, thickness of amorphous region (da) showed significant negative correlation with other lamellar traits (D, dc, dac) and positive correlation with thermal transitions which highlights the potential role of lamellar periodicity in determining sorghum starch physicochemical properties. Also, trait stability along above observations was highly significant based on diagonal correlation coefficients (Fig. 9) between E1 and E2 for AAC, CPV, RC, lamellar and rheological parameters. Additionally, thermal traits (To, Tp, Tc), SB, Ptemp, PeT, Tor, Tpr and ΔHr showed moderate stability. However, few pasting traits (PV, BD, HPV), PC, MD, Tcr, R% and ΔHg showed environment-sensitive behavior, matching reports that growing conditions can disproportionately affects starch properties (Beta & Corke, 2001; Kaufman et al., 2017; Li et al., 2013). RC was strongly affected by both genotype and environment (p < 0.001), with a smaller G × E interaction. Thus, genotype and environment contributed comparably, but waxy showed higher RC values than non-waxy in both environments (Table S2).

For lamellar parameters, D, da and dac were mainly influenced by the environment, seasonal conditions expanded or contracted the semicrystalline stack. Dc was primarily genotype-dependent, reflecting allelic control over amylopectin packing and crystalline lamella formation (Zhong et al., 2020). G × E effects on these parameters were minimal, so waxy/non-waxy rankings persisted throughout seasons.

Genotypic effects were dominant for determining AAC with a smaller G × E component exerting secondary impacts while environment showed non-significant effect (Table 1). This observation aligns with previous reports of small AAC shifts under varying growing conditions (Beta & Corke, 2001; Endalamaw et al., 2025). A study observed a modest seasonal shift, with mean AAC increasing from 21.4% in the cooler season to 22.2% in the warmer season (Kaufman et al., 2017).

Sorghum starch PC levels were strongly affected by growing conditions (p < 0.001) and genotype. This shows that genotypes responded differently to environmental factors like temperature, soil, and field management practices.

Sorghum starch pasting properties were affected by both genotype and environmental variability (Table 1). For instance, PV was highly affected by genotype, environment and G × E effects. CPV was mostly affected by genotypic effect with a smaller environmental component. HPV and BD were found highly environment-sensitive (p < 0.001). SB was primarily affected by genotypic effects and showed non-significant environmental effects. Ptemp was highly affected by environment, whereas PeT was not affected by effects.

Gelatinization parameters (To and Tp) were mainly influenced by environment, although genotype also played a role (Table 1). In contrast, Tc was mainly affected by both genotype and environmental effects. ΔHg was impacted by environment and genotype. Previous studies indicated that increased growing temperatures can influence thermal parameters in sorghum starches (Beta & Corke, 2001; Kaufman et al., 2017).

For retrogradation properties, Tor was modestly affected by the environment (p < 0.05); whereas Tpr and Tcr were strongly affected by the environment. Tpr was slightly affected by genotype, and Tcr was not affected by genotype and G × E interaction effects. ΔHr was strongly affected by both genotype and G × E interaction, with only a minor environment effect (p < 0.05). This shows role of amylose levels in determining retrogradation enthalpy and its seasonal modulation (Kaufman et al., 2017).

Rheological parameter K was primarily affected by genotype and environmental effects and a smaller interaction effect, whereas n was mainly affected by genotype with smaller yet significant environment effect and G × E interaction effect (Table 1).

Conclusion

This study resolved trait-specific G × E effects on sorghum starches' structure–function relationships. AAC differentiated waxy/non-waxy genotypes with small seasonal fluctuations across environments. PC was wide-ranging and strongly environment-responsive. Granule diameter showed limited seasonal shifts. For structure, XRD confirmed A-type crystalline pattern with RC differing by genotype and season. SAXS analysis showed environment-driven spacing parameters (D, dac), genotype dependent crystalline thickness (dc), and da was affected by both genotype and environment. For functionality, pasting properties PV, HPV and BD were affected by genotype and environment, CPV, SB and PeT were mainly affected by genotype, and Ptemp was mainly affected by environment. Thermal traits (To, Tp) were mainly affected by environment, and Tc and ΔHg by both genotypic and environmental effects. ΔHr was strongly affected by genotype while R% was strongly affected by environment, with modest interaction effects. Static rheology showed weak-gel profile across both seasons, K and n was mainly affected by genotype and environment. This study clarifies how starch physicochemical and structural traits vary among genotype and across environments, guiding the development of sorghum varieties tailored to diverse food and industrial applications.
