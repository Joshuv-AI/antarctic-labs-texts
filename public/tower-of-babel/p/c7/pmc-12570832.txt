Hybridization of Lycium qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense revealed using morphological, SSR markers, and chloroplast genomic data
Authors: Lei Zhang, Chaopan Zhang, Erdong Zhang, Yuqing Wei, Guoqi Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLycium has economic and ecological significance, but its natural hybridization has received little attention. Lycium qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense exhibit intermediate morphological traits between or blended with L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum, inferring their potential hybrid origin. To clarify this hypothesis, the present study investigated their hybridization using morphological traits, nuclear simple sequence repeats (SSRs) markers, and chloroplast genomic data.ResultsComparative morphological analysis clearly distinguished L. barbarum from L. ruthenicum based on petiole length, leaf morphology and fruit color. In contrast, most L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense individuals exhibited intermediate traits between L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum, although some L. ningxiaense individuals overlapped morphologically with L. ruthenicum. The genetic composition of all L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense individuals represented a genetic mix of the two parent species, all of which are hybrids. The maximum likelihood (ML) and bayesian inference (BI) analyses based on the chloroplast genome revealed nearly congruent topologies, providing a strongly supported (BS = 100%; PP = 1) for a shared clade comprising L. qingshuiheense, L. ningxiaense, and L. ruthenicum.ConclusionBased on analyses of morphological traits, SSR markers, and cp. genome data, L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense are likely hybrids resulting from out-crossing between L. ruthenicum and L. barbarum. However, genetic composition of L. qingshuiheense is derived from approximately 85% L. ruthenicum and 15% L. barbarum, Similarly, the variant of L. ningxiaense shows a genetic composition consisting of 40% derived from L. ruthenicum and 60% from Lycium barbarum. The present study elucidated the origin of two Lycium species, which enhances our understanding of the evolutionary mechanisms underlying interspecific hybridization in Lycium. These findings clarify the evolutionary history of these taxa, as well as provide molecular tools and genetic insights for developing improved Lycium varieties with desirable agronomic traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12150-3.Keywords: Lycium, Morphological analyses, Simple sequence repeats (SSRs), Chloroplast genome, Hybridization identification

Background

Species of Lycium L. (Solanaceae) are shrubs with high ecological adaptability commonly used to prevent wind and sand erosion and combat soil salinization [1, 2]. Lycium consists of more than 80 species, primarily distributed across South America, southwest North America, and Africa, with a limited number of species found in the temperate regions of Eurasia [3, 4]. In China, the recorded number of Lycium species ranges from 8 to 15, containing four subspecies, primarily growing in the arid and semi-arid regions of Ningxia, Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, and other provinces [5–7]. For centuries, the fruits of certain species of Lycium, including L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum, have been used in China both as traditional herbal remedies and dietary staples [8–10].

Lycium species exhibit significant genetic diversity [11]. However, species delimitation of Lycium has remained problematic [12, 13]. For example, species such as L. ningxiaense R. J. Wang and Q. Liao [5, 14] and L. qingshuiheense X. L. Jiang and J. N. Li [6], which were described based on morphological characteristics, exhibit intermediate or blended morphological traits of L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum. L. barbarum has lanceolate or long-elliptic leaves and red or orange-yellow, oblong or ovoid fruit (Fig. 1A), whereas L. ruthenicum has linear or subcylindric leaves and purple-black, globose fruit (Fig. 1B). The leaves of L. qingshuiheense are nearly lanceolate, and fruit are dark reddish-brown, oblate (Fig. 1C). In L. ningxiaense, leaves are small, strip or strip-oblanceolate and orange-yellow, with oblong or ovoid fruit (Fig. 1D). Geographically, L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum are widespread in northwest China. L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense are found in central Ningxia, China, as an overlapping geographical area of L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum [1, 5, 6]. Thus, it is speculated that the L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense may be the result of hybridization based on morphological characteristics and geographical distribution. However, species often exhibit morphological variability across different geographical distribution [15, 16]. Therefore, identifying hybrid species based on traditional morphology-based approaches and geographical distribution remains challenging. With ongoing advancements in molecular biology, the application of molecular techniques in species delimitation research has become increasingly effective for detecting hybrids [17–19]. In Lycium, nuclear simple sequence repeats (SSRs or microsatellites) are particularly valuable for distinguishing hybrids from parental species due to their codominant inheritance and high polymorphism [20–25]. Meanwhile, chloroplast genomes, which are maternally inherited in most angiosperms, can clarify maternal lineages in suspected hybrids [26–32].

Plant morphology of four Lycium species investigated, indicating leaf, flower, and fruit. A L. barbarum; B L. ningxiaense; C L. qingshuiheense; D L. ruthenicum. To clarify the relationship among these species, this study used nuclear SSRs, cp. genome, and morphological data to assess the genetic composition and structure of L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense in central Ningxia, China. This study aimed to address the following questions: (1) Are L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense the products of hybridization of L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum? (2) What is the genetic composition resulting from such hybridization?

Results

Morphological data analysis of four Lycium species

We conducted separate analysis of variance (ANOVA) on 12 observed and measured morphological traits of 90 Lycium individuals representing the four species. The results revealed that L. ruthenicum typically lacks petioles, whereas L. barbarum, L. qingshuiheense, and L. ningxiaense possess petioles (Fig. S1A, Table S1). Petiole lengths decreased in the following order: 0.42 cm (L. barbarum) > 0.17 cm (L. qingshuiheense) > 0.08 cm (L. ningxiaense) (Table S1). In terms of leaf shape, L. barbarum and L. qingshuiheense both belong to lanceolate leaves. L. ruthenicum has typical linear leaves (L/W: 6.43), while L. ningxiaense exhibits unique strip-shaped leaves (L/W: 6.05) (Figs. 2B; S1B, Table S1). Significant differences in floral morphology were observed among the four Lycium species. L. barbarum possessed a significantly larger corolla (0.851 cm) and prominently larger corolla lobes (0.36 cm) compared to the other species (Figs. S1C; E, Table S1). In contrast, L. ruthenicum was characterized by a significantly longer floral tube (0.59 cm), a trait that clearly distinguishes it from L. barbarum, L. qingshuiheense, and L. ningxiaense (Fig. 2C, Table S1). However, no significant differences were found among the species in the angle between the corolla and tube (Fig. S1I, Table S1). Regarding fruit color, each species displayed distinct characteristics: L. barbarum produces red fruits, L. ruthenicum black fruits, L. qingshuiheense reddish-brown fruits, and L. ningxiaense orange-yellow fruits (Figs. 2D; S1F-G, Table S1).

Among the 12 morphological traits, there were no significant differences in 3 traits (Fig. S1; Table S1). We conducted principal coordinate analysis (PCA) analysis on the 9 traits with significant differences. The result indicated that four PCs (PC1 to PC4), each accounting for > 5% of the variance in the data, collectively explained 84.99% of the variance in the data (PC1 = 51.22%; PC2 = 16.63%; PC3 = 10.24%; PC4 = 7.95%). All other PCs individually accounted for < 5% of the variance in the data. The highest proportion of morphological variance was observed across PC1 and PC2 (Fig. 2A). PC1 represented leaf morphology, with high-scoring species exhibiting larger leaf areas. PC2 reflected reproductive trait divergence, where L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum exhibiting relatively high score. The arrangement of the scatterplot was based on (PC1) and (PC2), individuals of L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum formed two clear groups, whereas most individuals of L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense were distributed between L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum, with some samples distributed in the L. ruthenicum group. Similar distribution patterns were observed across other significant principal components (e.g., PC3 and PC4). Notably, PCA on wild morphological traits revealed clustering patterns consistent with those from the common garden. The relative positions of the four species in PC1-PC2 space were concordant between both datasets, indicating that interspecific morphological divergence was driven by genetic factors rather than environmental factors. (Fig. 2A, S2).

Suitable distribution area of four Lycium species in Ningxia

MaxEnt simulation results indicate a partial geographic overlap in the potential suitable habitats of L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum over the past 50 years, with core overlapping zones concentrated in the Yellow River Basin of the central and northern of Ningxia. This spatial overlap indicates sympatric distribution, which facilitates natural hybridization (Fig. S3). Furthermore, the suitable habitats of L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense are located within this area of parental overlap and their marginal zones. Consequently, these results support the hypothesis that hybrid formation is intrinsically associated with the spatial coexistence of the parent species.

Nuclear DNA genomic size of four Lycium species

We estimate the DNA C-values of four Lycium species by flow cytometric analysis (Table S2). The result shows that four species are diploid (2n = 2x = 24), with significant genome size variation: L. ruthenicum showed the largest 1 C DNA content (1.88–2.08 pg) and L. barbarum had the smallest genome (1.51–1.55 pg). Putative hybrids L. ningxiaense (1.70–1.77 pg) and L. qingshuiheense (1.75–1.83 pg) exhibited intermediate values (Table S2).

Clustering analyses of nuclear SSR (nSSR) microsatellite data

Structural analysis of the 10 nSSR loci for 67 individuals from the four Lycium species showed that the data comprised two genetic clusters (K = 2), based on both Pritchard and Evanno methods (Fig. 3A, B). When K = 2, The first cluster was composed primarily of pure L. barbarum, and the second cluster included all L. ruthenicum pure individuals (Fig. 3C). The genetic composition of L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense represented a genetic mix of the two clusters. The genetic composition of L. ningxiaense includes 40% L. ruthenicum and 60% L. barbarum. In contrast, L. qingshuiheense genetic composition dominated by L. ruthenicum (85%), with a minor contribution from L. barbarum (15%) (Fig. 3C).The PCA of the nSSR data showed that L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum formed distinct clusters, with all the sampled individuals of both L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense dispersed between them, indicating mixed origins (Fig. 3D).

Characteristics of cp. genomes of four Lycium species

The cp. genomes from the four Lycium species contained a total of 132 common genes, which comprised 87 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 37 tRNA genes, and 8 RNA genes (Table 1). The size of the chloroplast genomes varied from 154,830 to 155,148 bp (Fig. 4; Table S3). All the cp. genomes exhibited characteristic quadripartite circular structures, consisting of a large single copy (LSC) region and a small single copy (SSC) region, which were separated by two inverted repeat (IR) regions (Fig. 4). This cp. genome result was consistent with Zhang et al. [33], suggesting that the cp. genomes of Lycium were conserved in structure. In addition, the lengths of the LSC regions varied from 85,831 to 85,991 bp, while the lengths of the SSC and IR regions ranged from 18,190 to 18,209 bp and from 25,395 to 25,458 bp, respectively (Table S3). The GC content of the cp. genomes was found to be 37.9%, whereas the GC content within the IR regions ranged between 43.14% and 43.19%. This is notably higher than that observed in the LSC and SSC regions, which were 35.92%–35.98% and 32.31%–32.35%, respectively (Table S3). The total length, LSC length, SSC length, IR length, and GC content of the entire genome and that of individual regions of L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense were identical (closer to L. ruthenicum than to L. barbarum).

Phylogenetic analysis of the four Lycium species based on cp. genome

The final concatenated dataset included 79 PCGs with 60,564 sites after trimming poorly aligned regions and gaps with missing genes (Table S4). We utilized Lycium chinense as an outgroup to elucidate the phylogenetic relationships among the four species based on their cp. genomes. In the phylogenetic trees, most nodes generated using both maximum likelihood (ML) and bayesian inference (BI) analyses exhibited congruent topologies (Fig. 5; Figs. S4-S6). A total of 27 individuals (ten L. barbarum, seven L. ruthenicum, and five each for putative hybrids) from four Lycium species were divided into two typical branches. Clade 1 includes all ten individuals of L. barbarum, and clade 2 includes all individuals sampled of L. qingshuiheense, L. ningxiaense, and L. ruthenicum (BS = 100%; PP = 1) (Fig. 5; Figs. S4-S6).

Discussion

Interspecific hybridization is a common phenomenon in nature [34, 35], and hybrid swarms provide valuable insights into interspecific gene flow and hybrid fitness [36–38]. Although the origins of hybrids have been extensively studied in many plant groups, such as Populus [39], Rhododendron [40], and Mimulus [41], natural hybrids occurring in Lycium has received limited attention. The current study fills this gap by providing evidence of hybridizations between the distantly related species L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum in the origin of L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense.

The observed morphological patterns provide evidence supporting the hybrid origin of L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense. Most L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense individuals displayed leaf size and shape, corolla size, and fruit color that fall between those of L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum. This primary morphological signature — intermediacy characters — strongly aligns with classic expectations for hybrid taxa, wherein hybrid species often exhibit characteristics that are intermediate between or a blend of those of their parent species [16, 17, 42–47]. However, due to adaptation to a similar arid environment, the morphology of some L. ningxiaense individuals overlapped with that of L. ruthenicum, especially in features potentially associated with environmental adaptation. This convergence is likely attributable to strong selective pressures imposed by the shared arid habitat. Traits conferring advantages in water conservation or heat tolerance under xeric conditions.

SSR marker data showed clear genetic admixture, with alleles form both putative parental species (L. ruthenicum and L. barbarum) present in every individual of both hybrid taxa. Complementing this, the DNA C-values analysis positioned both L. ningxiaense and L. qingshuiheense between the two parental species, further corroborating their hybrid status. Finally, comparative analysis of the cp. genome provided definitive evidence for the hybrid origin and enabled identification of the maternal parent: all L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense individuals clustered phylogenetically with L. ruthenicum, confirming it as the maternal parent, consistent with the maternal inheritance of the chloroplast.

Although both species are of hybrid origin, they exhibit distinct genetic composition. L. ningxiaense populations are predominantly composed of F1 hybrids, with genetic composition of 40% L. ruthenicum and 60% L. barbarum. In contrast, L. qingshuiheense shows evidence of backcrossing with L. ruthenicum and possesses an asymmetrical genetic composition dominated by L. ruthenicum (85%), with a minor contribution from L. barbarum (15%). This predominance of F1 hybrids is rare, as hybrid swarms typically contain a mix of F1s, F2s, and backcrosses [48–51]. Field observations provide context for this phenomenon: populations of both L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense are relatively small, produce few seeds, and crucially, no young seedlings were observed in their habitats. The presence of these hybrid populations consisting mainly of F1s suggests that the new and recent hybridizations between two parental species may have continuously produced more F1s to repopulate the L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense hybrid zones and have maintained the unique F1 hybrid zones. This pattern is consistent with the presence of strong reproductive isolation barriers that restrict gene flow beyond the F1 generation.

The sympatric distribution of L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense within the geographic overlap zone of their putative parental species, provides a crucial ecological environment for their hybrid origin. This spatial coexistence is a fundamental prerequisite for interspecific hybridization, facilitating pollen transfer and cross-pollination events. In addition, the geographic overlap serves not merely as a passive setting for hybridization but actively influences the evolutionary dynamics. The coexistence of parental species maintains the potential for recurrent gene flow, while the specific ecological pressures of the arid environment and the strength of reproductive barriers act as key determinants of hybrid outcomes—whether hybrids persist primarily as ephemeral F1 swarms (L. ningxiaense), exhibit limited introgression (L. qingshuiheense), or potentially evolve towards distinct hybrid species.

Conclusions

This study examined four Lycium species using a combination of morphological traits, SSR markers, and cp. genome sequences. These findings suggest that L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense likely originated through hybridization events between L. barbarum and L. ruthenicum. The genetic composition L. qingshuiheense is derived from approximately 85% L. ruthenicum and 15% L. barbarum, Similarly, the variant of L. ningxiaense shows a genetic composition consisting of 40% derived from L. ruthenicum and 60% from Lycium barbarum. This study elucidates the evolutionary origins of these two Lycium species, advancing our understanding of interspecific hybridization mechanisms within the genus. Furthermore, these findings resolve the taxa’s evolutionary history while providing molecular tools and genetic insights for breeding improved Lycium cultivars with enhanced agronomic traits.

Materials and methods

Field survey and sample collection

Before the field investigations, we scrutinized 198 specimens of the four species in the primary herbaria: PE (Herbarium, Institute of Botany, CAS, Beijing, China), KUN (Herbarium, Kunming Institute of Botany, CAS, Kunming, China), WUK (Herbarium, College of Life Sciences, Northwest A&F University, Yangling, China), and IBSC (South China Botanical Garden Herbarium, Guangzhou, China), along with the digital images of the specimens from the Chinese Virtual Herbarium database (http://www.cvh.ac.cn/), including that of L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense held at PE. To avoid misidentification, we conducted a thorough verification of the typical morphological characteristics of leaf, flower, and fruit when collecting samples. For all accessible populations of these four species in the field, healthy fresh leaves were collected from mature plants that were situated at least 50 m apart and promptly dried them in silica gel for DNA extraction. In addition, for each population, branchlets with flowers or fruits were collected from each mature plant for the preparation of voucher specimens. Subsequently, these specimens were identified by Dr. Lei Zhang (zhangsanshi-0319@163.com) and stored in the Herbarium of North Minzu University, Yinchuan, China. We recorded the geographic coordinates, including latitude and longitude, as well as the elevation for each sampled population. As L. qingshuiheense and L. ningxiaense are exclusively found in central Ningxia, a total of 67 individuals and 31 specimens were collected from 10 representative populations of four species distributed in Ningxia for population genetic and morphological analyses (Fig. S7; Table S5).

Morphological analysis

To obtain comprehensive information on the four species, 90 specimens from 90 individuals, comprising 31 specimens collected from the wild field and 59 specimens from herbaria collections were analyzed. The sampling process involved the selection of specimens and accounted for the distributional ranges of the four species found in northern China. To eliminate the influence of the environment on morphology, we also collected samples from 12 individuals representing the four species in the Qixin plantation (Zhongning, Ningxia, China) (Table S6). The 12 morphological traits related to leavf, flower, and fruit were measured, with a focus on those traits previously used to identify species in taxonomic studies [4, 5, 52]. Two or three mature leaves, flowers, and fruits from each of the 90 specimens and 12 common garden individuals were selected for measurement using ImageJ version v1.53t [53]. Final morphometric analyses were conducted on four leaf traits (petiole length and leaf length, width, and area), five flower traits (flower length, corolla radius, tube length, corolla lobe length, and angle between the corolla and tube), and fruit color. Subsequently, PCA was conducted using the R package ggrepel version 0.9.5 (https://ggrepel.slowkow.com), in conjunction with Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT), and the results were visualized using ggplot2 [54]. The variability in each quantitative trait is shown in boxplots generated by the R program [55]. ANOVA analysis was performed for each quantitative trait using SPSS version 19 [56].

Geographical distribution simulation

Contemporary climate data (1970–2000), consisting of 19 bioclimatic variables (Bio1 - Bio19), were obtained from WorldClim (https://www.worldclim.org/). To mitigate potential model overfitting and its detrimental effects on prediction accuracy resulting from spatial collinearity, a key environmental factor selection procedure was implemented. Pre-modeling experiments were then conducted using the Maximum Entropy software MaxEnt version 3.4.4 [57], with four Lycium species distribution point data and the current climate dataset serving as inputs.

Flow cytometry and genetic analysis

The ploidy and genomic size of the four Lycium species were determined using fresh leaves of Solanum lycopersicum (0.9 pg/1 C) leaves as an internal standard through BDFACScalibur flow cytometry analysis [58]. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the dried leaves using the hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide method [59]. The 10 nSSR primers (Table S7) were used to genotype the samples [60, 61]. Polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) was performed using a 25 µL reaction mix comprising 2.5 µL of 10× Taq buffer, 0.5 mM of each dNTP, 50–100 ng of diluted genomic DNA, 0.5 µL of each primer, and 0.5 units of Taq polymerase (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). The cycling conditions were as follows: a single cycle at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 36 cycles of 95 °C for 45 s, 55 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 80 s, with a final extension cycle at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified products were analyzed on an ABI 3830xl DNA analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Inc., Foster City, CA, USA) at Nuohe Biological Technology (Tianjin, China).

The genetic composition and structure of the four Lycium species populations (Table S7) were analyzed using STRUCTURE v2.3.4 [62] with Bayesian hybrid mixture computation. Ten replicates were performed for each genetic cluster, with K values spanning from 1 to 10 [63]. Each run comprised 1,000,000 Markov chain Monte Carlo repetitions after a burn-in period of 500,000 iterations. The most likely number of clusters was determined using a combination of the Evanno [64] and Pritchard [65] methods performed using Structure Harvester software [66]. For the SSR data, we conducted a PCA using the ape package in R [67].

Cp. genome assembly and annotation

At least two gigabases of 2 × 150 bp short read data were generated for each sample. After filtering out reads with quality scores < 7 and > 10% ambiguous nucleotides, clean reads were generated to ensure high-quality data for subsequent analysis. All of the remaining reads were assembled using NOVOPlasty version 2.7.2 [68] software with k-mer = 39, read length = 150, and insert size = 350. The contigs were aligned into sequences in Geneious version 9.1.8 software [69]. Finally, the CPGs were annotated using Plann version 1.1 [70] software based on the L. chinense cp. genome as a reference. Additionally, the circular gene map images of the cp. genome were created using OGDRAW version 1.2 [71], and all cp. genomes annotated for the first time were submitted to GenBank [72].

Phylogenetic analysis

In our study, two datasets of 27 cp. genomes (Table S3) were used to reconstruct phylogenetic relationships: the whole chloroplast genome sequences (WPs) and the aligned protein-coding sequences (CDSs). Using Perl scripts to extract PCGs from GenBank formatted files and exclude possible pseudogenes, 79 PCGs were retained across all species. Each PCG was aligned using MAFFT v.7 [73], and the aligned sequences were assembled into a supermatrix. Independent phylogenetic analyses were conducted for each dataset (CDSs and WPs) utilizing both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methodologies. RAxML version 8.1.24 [74] was employed to perform ML analyses was obtained using the rapid hill-climbing algorithm (i.e., the option “-f d”) with 1,000 bootstrap replicates under the gamma distribution (GTR + Γ) model. The optimal model (GTR + I + G) was identified using jModeltest software, and BI analysis was performed using MrBayes version 3.2.6 [75]. Furthermore, FigTree version 1.4.2 [76] software was used to visualize all phylogeny topology results.
