Genome‐wide association studies for identifying SNPs and candidate genes associated with grain quality traits in rice (Oryza sativa L.)
Authors: Shivani Darmagaru, Abdul Fiyaz Rahaman, Farzana Jabeen, Aravind Kumar Jukanti, Chaithanya Kasarla, Dileep Kumar Gowdru Dhananjaya, Arivelarasan Tamilarasu, LellaVenkata Subba Rao, Supriya Kallakuri, Shoba Venkatanagappa, Raman Meenakshi Sundaram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsuring superior quality is of paramount objective in modern rice (Oryza sativa L.) breeding program and investigating quality traits is crucial for developing superior rice varieties. The current study aimed for identifying single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and potential candidate genes for eleven quality traits, namely, hulling %, milling %, kernel length, kernel breadth, kernel length to breadth ratio, grain type, head rice recovery, chalkiness, amylose content, alkali spreading value, and gel consistency, using genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) on diverse germplasm sourced from various rice‐growing regions around the world. Phenotypic evaluation revealed significant genetic diversity among germplasm, with substantial natural variation observed across 10 quality‐related traits. GWAS using 7K SNP chip identified 31 SNPs significantly linked to grain quality traits, of which 20 were novel and 11 had been previously reported, including two co‐localized SNPs (GS3 and SNP‐6.6652023) associated with multiple traits. In addition, 121 candidate genes related with these traits were identified and categorized into seven molecular functions, four cellular components, and nine biological processes. Notably, 17 putative grain quality‐related genes were identified, viz., GS3 (Grain size regulator), Wx (granule‐bound starch synthase), OsZIP7 (ZIP family protein), OsRING4 (RING‐type E3 ubiquitin ligase), OsMKK3 (Mitogen‐activated protein kinase), OsSSIV‐2, OsSSIIa/SSII‐3 (ALK; soluble starch synthase), and OsbZIP58 (bZIP transcription factor). The identified SNPs and potential candidate genes represent a valuable genomic resource for accelerating rice breeding programs focused on the enhancement of key quality traits.

INTRODUCTION

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a staple food crop for significant portion of the global population and is widely cultivated across Asia and Africa. Rice occupies a position of immense significance on a global scale, functioning as a cornerstone in the realms of food security, livelihoods, and cultural identity. The cultivation of rice plays a crucial role in supporting livelihoods of millions of farmers, particularly in the developing countries where agriculture forms the backbone of the economy (Fiyaz et al., 2022). Its versatility in cooking, adaptability to diverse environments, and resilience make rice a key player in addressing the nutritional needs of a growing population worldwide.

The quality parameters of rice are very important and have a key role in its value and importance in global agriculture (Prodhan et al., 2024). The quality parameters of rice, appearance, cooking and eating, nutritional, and milling, collectively influence the market value and export potential of rice (Ren et al., 2023). The grain appearance quality encompassing traits such as kernel length (KL), width, shape, and overall visual appeal plays a vital role in determining consumer acceptance, market value, and commercial demand, making it a critical target in rice breeding programs. Simultaneously, the increasing health consciousness of consumers on nutritional content, opens avenues for higher export potential in regions prioritizing wellness. Certain varieties of rice, stand special worldwide due to their exceptional cooking and eating qualities, like amylose content (AC) and gelatinization temperature. Additionally, efficient milling processes that ensure high recovery rates enhance the economic viability of rice production (Alkahtani, 2022).

The global demand for high‐quality rice continues to increase and the integration of advanced genomic technologies has become of paramount importance in empowering researchers and breeders with effective tools to unravel the genetic basis for grain quality parameters. Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) and molecular markers have become important tools, allowing researchers to identify specific regions in the rice genome associated with preferred grain quality traits (Abhijith et al., 2022). The precision in genomic analysis enables a thorough understanding of the genetic factors that affect these characters, offering invaluable insights for breeders. Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers, in specific, have become essential tools in GWAS, playing a key role in deciphering the genetic base of complex traits (Zheng et al., 2021). Their significance is underscored by their high density and genome‐wide coverage, allowing researchers to explore the entire genome. In rice research, GWAS had substantial progress over the past decade. For instance, GWAS has been used to identify the potential candidate genes linked with quality parameters in various rice germplasm (Verma et al., 2022), while GWAS was applied to uncover SNPs associated with grain quality parameters in hybrid lines of rice (P. Yu et al., 2022).

Although some potential genes associated with quality traits have been identified in earlier studies, comprehensive genetic analysis related to these traits remain limited. In the current study, 11 key grain quality traits, including hulling %, milling %, KL, kernel breadth (KB), kernel length to breadth ratio (KLBR), grain type (GT), head rice recovery (HRR), chalkiness (CH), AC, alkali spreading value (ASV), and gel consistency (GC), were phenotyped in 72 diverse rice germplasm and 7K SNP chip was employed to conduct GWAS. The study aimed to (1) conduct GWAS for identification of SNPs that are linked to various quality parameters in rice, (2) to compare the results of the study with earlier gene mapping studies, and (3) predict and identify potential candidate genes associated with the quality for future studies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material

Seventy‐two diverse rice germplasm from across India are used and their details are given in Table S1. The selected germplasm are grown in the fields of the Indian Council of Agricultural Research—Indian Institute of Rice Research, Rajendra Nagar, Hyderabad, over two different seasons from 2019 to 2020 and 2020 to 2021. Seeds were sown in nursery beds, and seedlings aged 25–30 days were manually transplanted into puddled plots. The rows were spaced 20 cm apart, while the plants within each row were spaced 15 cm apart with two replications in randomized block design. Care was taken to ensure a consistent plant population in each replication.

Phenotyping for quality traits

For phenotyping of quality traits, the seeds were dried to an approximate moisture content of ∼12% and stored for 3 months. Approximately 200 g of sample per genotype was dehusked using SATAKE dehusker to obtain brown rice and later milled through SATAKE mill to produce milled rice. The hulling and milling percentage was computed by using the following formula.

Milled rice is repeatedly passed through a rice grader with 5 mm grooves to separate the broken grains from whole kernels. Kernels retaining ≥75% of their original length were classified as head rice as per IRRI (2013) standards. Ten whole milled rice kernels were randomly selected from each germplasm for the measurement of KL and KB using a dial micrometer. The KLBR was estimated using the formula described by Murthy and Govindaswamy (1967). GT was estimated based on the KLBR of polished rice kernels, following the classification standards described by Murthy and Govindaswamy (1967) and the standard evaluation system (SES) for rice (IRRI, 2013).

Grain CH percentage was determined by visual assessment of 100 polished kernels per genotype following International Rice Research Institute (IRRI)’s SES (2013). ASV was estimated using the method as described by Little et al. (1958). Six whole milled kernels per each germplasm were placed in petri dishes containing 10 mL of 1.7% KOH (potassium hydroxide) and incubated at 23–30°C for 23 h. Kernel disintegration was scored on a 7‐point scale. Based on ASV scores, germplasm were classified into three categories: low (<70°C; scores 6–7), intermediate (70–74°C; scores 4–5), and high (75–79°C; scores 1–3). AC was determined via iodine‐binding colorimetry using a spectrophotometer at 620 nm (B. O. Juliano, 1971).

For estimation of AC, 100 mg of rice flour was mixed with 1 mL ethanol and 9 mL of 1N NaOH (sodium hydroxide), heated for 15 min in water bath, and diluted to 100 mL. A 5 mL aliquot (in triplicate) was reacted with 1 mL acetic acid and 2 mL iodine reagent, and volume was made up to 100 mL. After 20 min of color development in the dark, absorbance was measured at 620 nm. Amylose percentage of each sample was calculated using a standard curve prepared with potato amylose. GC was determined following Cagampang et al. (1973) with classification according to the International Network for Genetic Evaluation of Rice (IRRI, 1996). Note that 100 mg of rice samples were mixed with 0.2 mL ethanol containing 0.025% thymol blue and 2 mL of 0.2 N KOH. After vortexing, tubes were then heated in water bath for 8 min, cooled for 5 min, and vortexed again. The tubes were then placed in an ice bath (1–6°C) for 20 min and laid horizontally for 1 h. The length of the gel from the bottom of the tube to the front was measured in mm as GC.

DNA isolation and SNP genotyping

Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) methods were used to isolate DNA from young leaves (Murray & Thompson, 1980). Initially, the DNA quality was checked on 0.8% agarose gel followed by quantification using NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000/2000c, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples are then genotyped using the Illumina Rice 7K chip provided by IRRI.

Plant material and DNA extraction

The CTAB method is used to isolate DNA from the leaves of 21‐day‐old seedlings (Murray & Thompson, 1980). The quality of DNA was assessed on 0.8% agarose gel followed by quantification using NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000/2000c, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

SSR genotyping and diversity analysis

For initial diversity analysis, the germplasm was genotyped using 36 simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers covering all the 12 chromosomes of rice. The information of SSR markers was given in Table S4. The polymorphic information content (PIC) of each marker was calculated as per Anderson and Sullivan, 1993.

The pairwise Jaccard's dissimilarity coefficients were calculated from the binary data matrix (presence = 1, absence = 0) obtained from polymorphic SSR profiles using the SIMQUAL module of NTSYS‐pc v2.11 W (Rohlf, 1998). Using unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean, the cluster analysis was performed in the SAHN module and dendrograms were constructed to assess genetic relationship among the germplasm.

SNP genotyping using Illumina Rice 7K SNP chip

A total of 72 diverse rice germplasm, representing a wide range of diversity, were used for genotyping. The Illumina Rice 7K SNP chip platform provided by IRRI was used to carry out the high‐throughput genotyping of the samples. For the association study, the SNP data obtained from this platform were filtered using TASSEL v5.2.81 (Bradbury et al., 2007) by a couple of filters, that is, markers with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 5% and those with more than 10% of missing reads were excluded to ensure data quality. This process resulted in a final dataset of 3944 SNPs.

Population structure, linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay studies

For the association study, the data were filtered using TASSEL 5.2.81 (Bradbury et al., 2007) by a couple of filters, that is, markers of MAF cutoff of 5%, with 10% of missing reads. This process resulted in a final dataset of 3944 SNPs. The obtained markers were then used for analysis with STRUCTURE v2.3.4 (Pritchard et al., 2000). The optimal number of subpopulations (SPs) was determined by testing values of K, 1–10, using independent runs with 50,000 burn‐ins. The optimal number of K was determined using the method outlined by Evanno et al. (2005) and implemented through the online tool “Structure Harvester” (Earl & VonHoldt, 2012). This was done by plotting the ad hoc statistic (ΔK) against the natural logarithm of the probability data [LnP(K)]. The resulting Q matrix was incorporated as a fixed effect in the mixed linear model (MLM) to account for population structure during GWAS.

The pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) among markers was predicted using TASSEL 5.2.81 software by calculating r 2 values. Intrachromosomal marker pairs were retained selectively for subsequent LD decay analysis on the basis of significant threshold of p < 0.05. The retained marker pairs, located within 5 kb distances, were grouped into distinct bins. An LD decay curve was then constructed by plotting the average r 2 values of the bins against their respective distances. The rate of LD decay was determined as the distance at which the average r 2 value decreased to half of its maximum average value (Huang et al., 2012).

Association mapping

The marker data comprising of 3994 SNPs were analyzed by implementing a single‐locus model, MLM executed in GAPIT v.3 (J. Wang & Zhang, 2021). The fixed significance threshold of −log10(p) ≥ 3, corresponding to p ≤ 0.001 was used to identify marker‐trait associations (MTAs). The novelty of identified SNPs was assessed by comparing with earlier reported quantitative trait loci (QTLs).

Prediction of candidate genes

Candidate genes are identified on the basis of significant SNPs and their extended regions, ranging from 100 kb upstream to 100 kb downstream (LD decay). This information was retrieved from the Gramene QTL database (https://www.gramene.org/) and the QTL annotation database (http://qtaro.abr.affrc.go.jp/), supplemented by a thorough review of relevant literature. To investigate potential candidate genes situated near the identified SNPs, the Rice Annotation Project Database (https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/) was utilized for reference and information retrieval. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed on these identified candidate genes using the GO website (http://www.geneontology.org/).

RESULTS

Variation for phenotypic traits for quality parameters in the association panel of rice

The phenotypic variation for grain quality traits is shown in Table 1, Figure 1, Table S2, and Figures S1 and S2. The results revealed existence of significant variation among germplasm studied and all the quality traits followed normal distribution. High heritability coupled with high genetic advance was recorded for KL, KB, KLBR, AC, GC, and ASV across two seasons. In contrast, HRR and CH exhibited moderate heritability coupled with low genetic advance. The correlation study indicated that, hulling % showed a positive significant correlation with milling %, HRR, KB, and ASV and a significant negative association with AC and GT. Milling % exhibited a positive significant correlation with HRR, KB, and GT. A significant positive correlation of KL with KLBR, ASV, GC, and CH was observed, whereas, KB showed a positive significant correlation with GT and a negative significant association with KLBR and CH. HRR exhibited a positive significant correlation with KB and GT and a significant negative association with KLBR. AC displayed a positive correlation with GT and is negatively associated with ASV and GC. Similarly, ASV presented a positive correlation with GC, whereas GT showed a negative correlation with CH (Figure 1). The analysis of variance (ANOVA) conducted for the data across two different seasons revealed substantial variability for all traits among the studied germplasm (Table S2).

Genotyping and diversity analysis

The ANOVA showed that mean sum of square was significant for all the traits indicating presence of variability among the germplasm. PIC values ranged between 0.0721 (RM280) and 0.6400 (RM20698). The alleles per locus varied from two to three, with the highest number of alleles observed for 10 markers (RM 11033, RM 10162, RM 15404, RM 18516, RM 20098, RM 22586, RM 21309, JGT 11–15.3, RM 25066, and RM 206). Among the 30 polymorphic SSR markers, 10 are tri‐allelic and 20 are bi‐allelic. The highest PIC values are detected for the markers RM 495 (0.607), RM 11033 (0.63), RM 15404 (0.61), RM 22586 (0.64), RM 25066 (0.61), and RM 206 (0.621) having a mean PIC value of 0.32 (Table S3). The diversity analysis grouped the panel into five main clusters, with each cluster having a varying number of germplasm (Figure S3; Table S4). On the whole, the germplasm exhibited considerable genetic variation, indicating their suitability for association mapping studies.

Population structure

Population structure based on Evanno plot showed a sharp ∆K when k was 5 (Figure 2A) indicating the presence of five SPs, which are denoted as SP1, SP2, SP3, SP4, and SP5. SPs are assigned to genotypes with a likelihood of ≥80%, while those with lower likelihoods are classified as admixtures (Famoso et al., 2011). SPs each contained a nearly equal number of germplasm, with Fst values of 0.52, 0.76, 0.66, 0.51, and 0.52, respectively, indicating their stratification. SP1 comprised 12 individuals, SP2 included 18 individuals, SP3 had 16 individuals, SP4 consisted of 12 individuals, and SP5 comprised 12 individuals (Figure 2B). The plot of average r 2 values for marker pairs versus their physical distance showed a nearly flat curve, suggesting a relatively slow decline in LD within the population. The maximum r 2 value (rmax) was approximately 0.85, and this value reduced to half at a distance of ∼1 Mb (Figure 2B).

Hulling %

In case of hulling %, one SNP was detected on chromosome 5 at position 5,294,341 cM and is named as qH5.1. The phenotypic variation exhibited by the marker (id5002699) was 17.9%. The favorable and the alternate alleles of the SNP are T & C.

Milling %

In the context of milling %, a single MTA was identified on chromosome 1, which was designated as qMRR1.1. The marker (SNP‐1.3537903) situated on chromosome 1 at the position of 3,538,904 cM elucidated a phenotypic variation of 16.9%.

Grain appearance quality traits

Kernel length

Three SNPs were identified for KL, of which one SNP (GS3‐grain size regulator) was observed on chromosome 3 at the 16733441 cM position, contributing to a phenotypic variation of 13.7%. This specific SNP (Lk3/grain size regulator) had been previously documented by Mao et al. (2010) from the T1 transformants obtained from a cross between Chuan 7 and Minghui 63.

In contrast, the remaining two SNPs (SNP‐12.3580806 and SNP‐12.3520389) were found on chromosome 12 at positions 3,581,808 and 3,521,391 cM, respectively, reflecting a phenotypic variation of 16.1% each. To our knowledge, these two associations on chromosome 12 have not been reported earlier for KL and therefore represent novel loci potentially involved in regulating KL in rice.

Kernel breadth

Four novel SNPs were identified for KB, with a pair of them (S_6033802 and SNP‐6.22145120) situated on chromosome 6 at positions 7,191,499 and 22,146,118 cM, displaying phenotypic variations of 15.4% and 14.4%, respectively. These two SNPs are denoted as qKB6.1 and qKB6.2. The remaining two SNPs (SNP‐10.2367517 and S_11550353) were identified on chromosomes 10 and 11, elucidating phenotypic variations of 20.0% and 15.4%, respectively.

Kernel length to breadth ratio

A pair of SNPs was detected for KLBR, with one previously documented SNP (GS3‐grain size regulator) found on chromosome 3 at the 16,733,441 cM position, accounting for a phenotypic variation of 13.7%. The second novel identified SNP was id3000480, situated on chromosome 3 at 853,802 cM, deciphering a phenotypic variation of 15.5% and was designated as qKLBR3.2.

Grain type

A pair of novel SNPs (S_8372006 and SNP‐11.11996646) was recognized on chromosomes 8 and 11, clarifying phenotypic variations of 14.8% and 16%, respectively. These identified SNPs were denoted as qGT8.1 and qGT11.1, respectively.

Chalkiness

Three novel SNPs were found to be associated with grain CH. Among these, three markers (S_634609, SNP‐1.41839953, and id1026236) were situated on chromosome 3 at 18,891,591, 41,840,997, and 41,065,438 cM, and were designated as qCH1.1, qCH1.2, and qCH1.3, respectively. These markers accounted for phenotypic variations of 21.4%, 15.2%, and 16%, respectively.

Cooking quality traits

Amylose content

For grain AC, two SNPs (S_1191519 and S_6873833) were detected on chromosomes 1 and 6, elucidating phenotypic variations of 15.4% and 15%, respectively. Notably, one of these SNPs (S_6873833) has been previously documented by Amarawathi et al. (2008) and designated as amy6‐1, situated on chromosome 6 at a position 4,234,080–28,130,383 cM in an interval between RM3 and RM217. Conversely, the other novel SNP (S_1191519) was identified on chromosome 1 at a position of 36,829,724 cM.

Gel consistency

Nine significant SNPs were found for GC, of which four SNPs (SNP 6.2418603, S_5891296, id6002123, and S_5891902) on chromosome 6 were previously documented by Tian et al. (2005) and the other five novel SNPs were identified each one on chromosomes 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8, respectively. The phenotypic variation of all the identified SNPs ranged between 15.5% and 29.3%.

Alkali spreading value

Four SNPs (SNP‐6.6652023, S_6026032, S_6028312, and ALK‐SNP2) were identified for ASV. These SNPs are previously documented by Amarawathi et al. (2008) in the Pusa 1121 × Pusa 1342 recombinant inbred line population of rice. The QTL, asv6‐1, identified on chromosome 6 in the marker interval RM3‐RM217 (SSR) with a logarithm of odds value of 2.63 shows phenotypic variation of 6.9%.

The number of candidate genes associated with hulling, milling, KL, KB, KLBR, GT, CH, AC, GC, and ASV are 5, 3, 13, 20, 7, 9, 18, 9, 33, and 4, respectively. DISCUSSION

Genetic basis of quality

Rice grain quality traits are crucial determinants of marketability and consumer acceptance. A wide variation in quality traits was observed among the germplasm, and the traits followed a normal distribution pattern. High heritability and genetic advance observed for kernel dimensions, AC, GC, and ASV indicate predominance of additive gene action (Zafar et al., 2022), implying that these traits can be effectively improved through early‐generation selection, whereas, HRR and CH exhibited moderate heritability and low genetic advance indicating the influence of nonadditive effects (epistasis) and genotype‐by‐environment interactions (Jan & Subhash, 2020). Significant positive correlation was observed between HRR and KB, AC and ASV, and ASV and GC. These results were consistent with previous studies (B. Juliano & Gonzales, 1989; Bao et al., 2004), whereas the negative correlation between ASV and CH agrees with Zhou et al. (2022).

Among 36 SSR markers utilized for molecular diversity studies, 30 were polymorphic showing moderate diversity (PIC = 0.32), which efficiently distinguished germplasm into five clusters. STRUCTURE analysis indicated five SPs, confirming substantial genetic diversity. This structured diversity, which reduces false positives due to population stratification (J. Yu et al., 2018), revealed significant genetic variation among the germplasm, validating their suitability for association analysis.

Using 3945 SNPs, the GWAS identified 31 loci associated with 10 quality traits. Out of these, 20 SNPs were novel, while 11 SNPs had been previously reported related to KL, KLBR, AC, ASV, and GC (Amarawathi et al., 2008; Mao et al., 2010; Tian et al., 2005). Two SNPs (GS3 and SNP‐6.6652023) exhibited pleiotrophy, indicating common regulatory pathways influencing multiple quality traits. Novel SNPs enhance understanding of the genetic architecture of rice quality traits and further validation can provide potential markers for marker‐assisted selection.

Candidate gene analysis identified 121 candidate genes, of which 17 are the potential candidate genes related to quality traits. For hulling percentage, OsZIP7 (zinc transporter for nutrient loading) belonging to the zinc‐regulated/iron‐regulated transporter‐like protein family was associated with the marker id5002699 (Majumdar et al., 2020). For milling percentage, OsLEAIa (late embryogenesis abundant protein) and PEG1 (2‐oxoglutarate and iron‐dependent oxygenase enzyme) were found to be associated with the marker SNP‐1.3537903 (Farrow & Facchini, 2014). Notably, GS3 and OsRING4 were associated with KL and KLBR, confirming their role in regulating grain dimensions (Fan et al., 2006; Yang et al., 2021). Genes involved in MAPK signaling and brassinosteroid biosynthesis (e.g., OsMKK3, OsBAK1, and OsMRG702) were linked with grain size variation, aligning with earlier studies showing hormonal and signal transduction control of grain development (Li, Lu, et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2018). For CH, OsbZIP58 and OsCML19 are found to be associated with transcription factors and calcium signaling pathways affect starch synthesis and grain filling (Liu et al., 2017; Yamamoto et al., 2006). Quality‐related starch traits showed strong associations with Wx (GBSSI where GBSS is granule‐bound starch synthase), SBEI (where SBE is starch branching enzyme), OsSSIV‐2, and OsSSIIa genes, known regulators of starch biosynthesis and cooking quality (Chen et al., 2020; Li, Huang, et al., 2018; Z. Y. Wang et al., 1995; Zhang et al., 2021). The co‐localization of markers linked to both GC and ASV suggests that these traits are controlled by related biochemical mechanisms. Collectively, these results strengthen the evidence for the involvement of known starch‐synthesis genes and identify novel candidate genes contributing to quality variation.

Overall, this study highlights significant genetic diversity and identifies several novel and known genomic regions associated with key rice quality parameters. The integration of SNP‐based association mapping with candidate gene analysis provides valuable insights into the genetic basis of grain quality. These findings can facilitate marker‐assisted selection and pyramiding of favorable alleles into high‐yielding parental lines to develop hybrids combining superior yield and quality.

CONCLUSION

The study identified SNP‐trait associations in Oryza sativa L. using GWAS with a 7K SNP chip, detecting 31 significant regions linked to key quality parameters, such as KL, KB, KLBR, AC, and GC. Out of these, 20 SNPs were novel, while 11 SNPs had been previously reported, including several co‐localized with known QTLs. A total of 121 candidate genes were identified, including 17 key genes with crucial roles in determining grain quality. The markers and candidate genes identified in the study will provide useful insights into the genetic basis for grain quality parameters. Future validation of these identified markers will strengthen their utility in the breeding programs. Once validated, these SNPs can be developed into functional markers for use in precise introgression of desirable alleles into high‐yielding rice backgrounds. Such efforts will accelerate the development of improved rice varieties that combine superior yield with enhanced grain quality, thereby contributing to sustainable rice production and global food and nutritional security.
