Introduction

Percutaneous transluminal septal myocardial ablation (PTSMA) is an established treatment for symptomatic hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy.1 Although delayed atrioventricular block is a recognized complication,2 detailed temporal electrophysiological changes after PTSMA remain poorly described. We report a case of a right ventricular septal pacing lead in which serial intracardiac measurements provided insight into post-PTSMA electrophysiological dynamics.

Case report

A 71-year-old woman with a history of sick sinus syndrome underwent a dual-chamber permanent pacemaker implantation with the ventricular lead positioned in the mid-right ventricular septum (Boston Scientific ACCOLADE™ MRI DR pacemaker, Boston Scientifics INGEVITY™ MRI lead with active fixation). Atrioventricular block gradually developed as well, and within 2 years, she had already become dependent on ventricular pacing. Two years later, she began to experience progressive exertional dyspnea. A transthoracic echocardiography revealed isolated hypertrophy confined to the basal portion of the ventricular septum, but cardiac function at rest was nearly normal. However, provocation with the Valsalva maneuver revealed a peak gradient of 85.2 mmHg due to significant left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) accompanied by systolic anterior motion and severe mitral regurgitation. The wall thickness in the LV was confined to the very base of the septum, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed no late gadolinium enhancement. Therefore, this case was diagnosed as LVOTO caused by sigmoid septum, rather than hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Given the presence of sigmoid septum and LVOTO, she started taking an oral beta-blocker, but they were ineffective. Therefore, PTSMA was performed 7 years after pacemaker implantation. Immediately before the procedure, the pacemaker was programmed to DDD mode. The pacing threshold was 1.0 V at 0.4 milliseconds, and the pacing output was set to 3.5 V at 0.4 milliseconds. The ventricular sensing amplitude was ≥25 mV, and the lead impedance was 784 Ω. The ventricular pacing burden was 100%.

Left ventriculography revealed that the area of interventricular septum hypertrophy was close to the pacing lead (Figure 1). We injected ice water and carbon dioxide into the first septal branch as a target vessel, and confirmed that no pacing failure occurred in this situation (Figure 2). During injection of 0.5 mL of ethanol into the first septal branch, sudden pacing failure occurred, resulting in pacing spikes without capturing QRS complexes. The pacing output at that time was 3.5 V at 0.4 milliseconds. Backup pacing was initiated via the electrode catheter previously placed at the right ventricular apex. 140 seconds after ethanol injection, the pacing failure resolved spontaneously, and the rhythm stabilized with QRS complexes being captured. After ablation of the first septal branch, the resting gradient improved to 19 mm Hg but remained elevated under provocation (69 mm Hg). After the procedure, serial pacemaker measurements were obtained from the ventricular pacing lead (Figure 3). Sensing amplitude decreased immediately post-procedure, reaching a nadir on day 2. Although the voltage amplitude of the electrogram (EGM) had decreased, the EGM waveform was normal without a current of injury. Pacing threshold increased and peaked on day 4 and then began to decline. Lead impedance gradually decreased, reaching its lowest point on day 5, and then began to increase. All parameters stabilized thereafter and remained similar at 1 week. The patient was discharged 8 days after the procedure without any other complications. Discussion

This case provides a unique opportunity to directly observe the dynamic electrophysiological effects of ethanol ablation in vivo through a septal pacing lead positioned within the ablation territory. Although atrioventricular block is a common complication of PTSMA, this case is particularly valuable in that it allows for a detailed assessment of the progression of localized myocardial damage caused by ablation. Ethanol ablation induces controlled myocardial necrosis by creating a localized infarction within the septal myocardium.3 Histologically, this process evolves, beginning with acute cellular injury and edema, followed by inflammation and eventual fibrosis.4 In the present case, these dynamic structural changes were reflected in real-time electrophysiological parameters, demonstrating: 1) immediate reduction in sensing amplitude, 2) delayed elevation of pacing threshold (peaking on day 4), and 3) gradual changes in lead impedance (peaking on day 5). These findings suggest that electrophysiological remodeling after ethanol ablation is not instantaneous but evolves over several days, likely reflecting the progression from acute edema to tissue necrosis and subsequent healing.

A particularly notable finding was the transient pacing failure during ethanol injection, with loss of capture even at high output, followed by spontaneous recovery within minutes. This observation highlights that ethanol ablation produces an immediate but partially reversible electrophysiological suppression before irreversible injury develops, providing insight into the temporal hierarchy of myocardial damage. In this case, although the voltage amplitude of the EGM was reduced, the EGM waveform was normal, and current of injury was not observed. If we had adjusted the EGM scale or the bandpass filter, we might have detected a current of injury.

The delayed peak in pacing threshold (day 4) is clinically important. While acute procedural changes are often monitored, this case demonstrates that maximum electrophysiological impact occurs several days after PTSMA. This timing coincides with the expected peak of myocardial edema and inflammation. This aligns with prior pathological studies suggesting that post-ablation edema progresses over days,4 which may explain the well-known risk of delayed atrioventricular block.

To our knowledge, no previous reports have quantitatively described time-dependent changes in pacing threshold, sensing amplitude, and impedance after PTSMA in this manner. These findings have practical implications: The dynamic changes observed over 3–7 days support current recommendations for prolonged monitoring after PTSMA. Patients with septal leads may be at higher risk of pacing instability, particularly in the early post-procedural period. Routine follow-up should include serial device interrogation, not just electrocardiogram monitoring.

This case supports a staged model of ethanol-induced electrophysiological remodeling: 1) immediate phase (minutes–hours): transient, reversible suppression of excitability and conduction; 2) early phase (days 1–3): myocardial edema with reduced sensing amplitude, reflecting impaired depolarization; 3) intermediate phase (days 3–5): peak tissue injury characterized by elevated pacing thresholds and changes in lead impedance; 4) late phase (≥1 week): stabilization of electrical parameters, consistent with resolution of edema and early scar formation. This staged process provides a mechanistic explanation for both transient pacing failure and delayed conduction disturbances.

Conclusion

This case highlights that ethanol ablation produces dynamic, time-dependent electrophysiological changes that can be directly monitored using a septal pacing lead. These observations provide new insights into the temporal evolution of myocardial injury after PTSMA and support the need for extended post-procedural monitoring.
