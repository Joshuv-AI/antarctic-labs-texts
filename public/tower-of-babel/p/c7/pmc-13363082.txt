Clay pots for reducing fluoride concentration in drinking water
Authors: Amrita Bansal, Richa Nagar, Ashwini Kurli, Alfas Razi M.P., Shilpi Gilra Gupta, Puneet Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesFluoride contamination of drinking water is a major public health challenge in India, leading to dental and skeletal fluorosis. Safe, affordable household interventions are needed to reduce exposure. This pilot study evaluated the effectiveness of locally available clay pots in reducing fluoride concentration in stored drinking water.MethodsTen water samples from different sources in Indore, India, were collected and analysed for baseline fluoride concentration using the SPADNS (sodium 2-(para-sulfophenylazo)-1,8-dihydroxy-3,6-naphthalene disulfonate) spectrophotometric method. Each sample was stored in a clay pot at room temperature for 24 h. Post-storage fluoride concentrations were remeasured. Statistical analysis was performed using a paired t-test.ResultsMean fluoride concentration decreased significantly from 0.72±0.10 mg/L to 0.26±0.06 mg/L, corresponding to an average 64% reduction (P=0.01).Interpretation and conclusionsClay pots provide a simple, low-cost, and culturally acceptable method for fluoride reduction in resource-limited communities.

Fluoride (F⁻) is a highly reactive ion occurring naturally in most water supplies due to geological and anthropogenic sources like fluoride-containing pesticides.1 The optimal fluoride concentration in drinking water is generally considered to be around 1.0 ppm (0.7–1.2 ppm), concentrations exceeding 1.5 mg/L, whether from natural or human sources, are classified as fluoride pollution of groundwater.2

Fluoride contamination in groundwater has emerged as a major global concern, with adverse effects reported across all continents. Excessive fluoride intake is strongly linked to dental and skeletal fluorosis, conditions that not only compromise health but also have serious socio-economic implications.3 The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that nearly 260 million people worldwide are exposed to drinking water containing fluoride levels above the safe limit of 1.5 mg/L.4

In India, fluorosis is a recognised public health issue for over 85 years.5 Fluoride concentrations as high as 48 mg/L have been detected in some water sources. Fluorosis in India has been reported even at 1.5 mg/L, prompting the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) to set a stricter upper permissible limit of 1.0 mg/L in drinking water.6

As of April 2014, government data revealed fluoride prevalence in 230 districts across 19 States, with over 14,000 households in high-risk areas still lacking access to safe drinking water.5

Ali et al3, 2019 reported that the average fluoride concentration in groundwater across different regions of India was highest in Madhya Pradesh (5.98 mg/L). When such groundwater is drawn for drinking and cooking purposes, fluoride gains easy access to the body, causing dental and skeletal fluorosis. Fluorosis does not require drug-based treatment; instead, prevention remains the most effective approach, with recovery possible if the disease is detected early. The cornerstone of prevention lies in access to safe drinking water and appropriate nutritional interventions.6

Several techniques are available for the defluoridation of drinking water, including adsorption, precipitation/coagulation, ion exchange, and membrane processes. Among these, adsorption is considered the most effective, simple, economical, and environmentally compatible method.7

A variety of natural adsorbents have been studied, among which clays, both natural and modified, consistently demonstrate high or comparable adsorption capacities relative to other low-cost materials.8 In India, the diversity of soil types leads to regional variations in clay composition.9 Beyond their traditional uses, clays act as natural scavengers of pollutants by adsorbing or exchanging cations and anions.8

The present study aimed to evaluate the potential of clay pots in reducing fluoride levels in drinking water. The study assessed the adsorption of fluoride in water stored in clay pots for 24 hrs, estimated the fluoride concentration in water obtained from different sources, and compared the levels before and after 24-h storage to determine the effectiveness of clay pots in fluoride reduction.

Methods

This study was undertaken by the department of Public Health Dentistry, Government College of Dentistry, Indore, Madhya Pradesh, India. A locally available clay pot from Indore city was used for the study. A clay pot was sampled, and chemical constituents were analysed in the laboratory using standard methods to determine the type of soil and its chemical composition. As this was a pilot study, ten clay pots were used to obtain preliminary evidence on feasibility and effectiveness before planning larger-scale investigations.

Sample collection and fluoride estimation

Water samples were collected from 10 drinking water sources, including both surface water and groundwater, from different regions of Indore in the month of July 2024 (4 from tap water, 2- borewell, 2- handpump, 2-community storage tank). Water was collected in 10 clean polyethylene bottles, each with a capacity of approximately 500 ml. Bottles were rinsed twice with the distilled water prior to the collection of water samples, as recommended by WHO.10 The bottles were filled, closed firmly, and labelled with the location and sources of the water with the permanent marker. Bottles were sent to the laboratory for analysis of fluoride concentration same day the sample was collected. The initial fluoride concentration in the collected water samples was determined using the SPADNS method.11

The SPADNS method is a spectrophotometric technique used for fluoride analysis in water samples. It is based on the reaction between fluoride ions and the SPADNS reagent (Sodium 2-(parasulfophenylazo)-1,8-dihydroxy-3,6-naphthalene disulfonate), which forms a colour complex that can be measured using a spectrophotometer.

SPADNS dye was mixed with sulphuric acid and zirconyl chloride solution to form the Zirconium-SPADNS complex. Collected water samples were mixed separately with the prepared SPADNS reagent and allowed for the reaction to take place. The dye progressively lightens as fluoride concentration increases. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer, and the reading was compared with a standard calibration curve to determine fluoride concentration. This method offers good precision and linearity over a range of approximately 0.1–10 mg/L with a limit of detection around 0.02–0.05 mg/L. Calibration was performed using standard fluoride solutions prepared from sodium fluoride. Quality control was ensured by running calibration standards with each batch of samples.

Evaluation of fluoride reduction using clay pots

To assess the effectiveness of clay pots in reducing fluoride levels, water samples were stored in ten identical clay pots at room temperature. Each pot was securely covered to prevent contamination from external sources such as dust, airborne particles, or evaporation losses. After the 24-h storage period, fluoride concentration was re-measured using the same SPADNS method to maintain consistency in the analytical approach. The comparison of pre- and post-storage fluoride levels helped evaluate the efficiency of clay pots in reducing fluoride content over time. Due to the pilot nature and laboratory constraints, single measurements were performed; however, strict calibration and standardised procedures were followed to minimise analytical variability.

By using multiple identical clay pots, the study aimed to minimise variability and ensure that the observed fluoride reduction was due to the clay’s adsorption properties rather than external factors. The results were statistically analyzed to determine whether the reduction in fluoride levels was significant and consistent across all samples.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into PSPP (Version 1.4.1, GNU Project, Free Software Foundation, Boston, MA, USA) for windows and descriptive analysis was represented as mean and SD. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality of the data. Data were normal (P>0.05), hence paired t test was used to compare the mean change in fluoride concentration before and after storing in the clay pots for a standardised time.

Results

Table shows that the pre-storage fluoride levels ranged from 0.56 mg/L to 0.83 mg/L, whereas post-storage levels decreased to 0.20 mg/L to 0.34 mg/L. The mean pre-storage fluoride concentration was 0.72 mg/L (SD = 0.10), while the mean post-storage concentration was significantly lower at 0.26 mg/L (SD = 0.06), corresponding to an average reduction of approximately 64% in fluoride levels. A paired t-test confirmed that this reduction was statistically significant (P=0.010).

Soil analysis showed that the clay pots were made of black cotton soil and red clay containing nitrogen (62 mg/kg), potassium (46 mg/kg), and phosphorus (8 mg/kg), which facilitate cation–anion exchange.

Discussion

This pilot study assessed the feasibility of fluoride reduction using clay pots across commonly used household water sources. Including multiple routine sources enhances practical relevance by reflecting typical domestic water use rather than controlled conditions. A consistent and statistically significant reduction was observed, with mean fluoride levels decreasing from 0.72 mg/L to 0.26 mg/L after 24 h of storage. This indicates that clay pots, commonly used in households, may serve as an effective, low-cost, and sustainable option for fluoride mitigation.

Similar findings have been reported by Kofa et al12 (2017). who demonstrated significant fluoride adsorption onto fired clay pots under laboratory conditions, supporting the role of clay minerals in passive fluoride removal during water storage.

Our findings on soil analysis align with literature reporting high fluoride adsorption by kaolinite13 and montmorillonite14 clays due to their surface area and ion-exchange capacity. Red clay with these minerals has achieved up to 88.6% fluoride removal in 24 h15 while natural kaolinite has shown 0.44–0.45 mg/g uptake under mildly acidic conditions.16 Modified kaolinite forms, such as hydrotalcite-like compounds, demonstrate even higher capacities (up to 238 mg/g).17 The 64% reduction observed in our study is therefore consistent with the documented adsorption behaviour of kaolinite-rich clays, reinforcing their practical and scientific relevance.

Since clay mineral composition varies geographically, the findings reflect the performance of the locally available clay pot used in this study and may not be directly generalisable to pots from other regions. Previous studies have shown variability in adsorption efficiency depending on the type of clay and its modification. Kim et al 18 demonstrated the high adsorption potential of pyrophyllite clay rich in silica and alumina. While some researchers have achieved nearly 100% fluoride removal using chemically modified clays such as Fe3⁺-treated bentonite19 or acid-activated siliceous20 minerals and alum-bent21 the current study demonstrates that untreated, locally available clay pots can significantly and consistently reduce fluoride levels in drinking water. This highlights their practical advantage in rural settings where access to advanced treatment technologies is limited.

Other low-cost adsorbents such as fly ash, sand, brick powder,22 guava seeds,23 and fruit peels24 have also been explored for defluoridation. However, their applicability at the household level is often restricted by acceptability, preparation requirements, or availability. In contrast, clay pots are culturally accepted, widely available, and require no additional processing, making them a more feasible and user-friendly option for communities at risk of fluorosis.

It is well established that time, temperature, and pH influence fluoride adsorption.25 In this study, these parameters were standardised by maintaining all clay pots at a similar room temperature, fixing the storage time to 24 h. Laboratory analysis showed that pH values remained within a narrow neutral range (6.5–8.0) before and after storage in all samples, and the reduction was primarily attributable to the adsorption properties of the clay pots. Although adsorption is likely the main mechanism for fluoride removal due to the clay’s surface charge and ion-exchange capacity. Other factors, such as precipitation,26 pH effects,27,28 and ion exchange,28 may also contribute. Future studies with physicochemical and mineralogical analysis could clarify the dominant mechanisms.

The baseline fluoride concentrations in the present study (0.56–0.83 mg/L) were within or close to the limits recommended by WHO and BIS. However, fluorosis endemic regions in India frequently report fluoride levels above 1.5 mg/L. Previous studies by Agarwal et al29 (2003) indicate that using clay-based adsorbents have demonstrated effective fluoride removal even at higher initial concentrations, suggesting that adsorption capacity may increase with fluoride loading. Nevertheless, extrapolation of the present findings to endemic settings should be made cautiously and requires validation.

Overall, the findings support the use of locally available clay pots as an effective preventive measure against fluoride exposure, particularly in rural and resource-limited settings. However, because the study included only ten water samples and lacked a non-clay control container, the finding should be interpreted as preliminary, with limited statistical power and external validity, and therefore requires confirmation through larger controlled studies, particularly in fluorosis endemic settings.
