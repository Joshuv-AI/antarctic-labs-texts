Exosomes in bone health and disease: cellular crosstalk, systemic signaling, and AI-driven advances in regenerative therapy
Authors: Xueying Liu, Wenqing Yu, Chengyu Huang, Zhenmin Wang, Ying Qian, Xin Chen, Gaoyang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exosomes have emerged as critical mediators of intercellular and inter-organ communication in bone biology. Secreted by bone-resident cells such as osteoblasts, osteoclasts, osteocytes, and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), these nanosized vesicles carry diverse molecular cargos that regulate bone remodeling, regeneration, and skeletal homeostasis. In addition to mediating local communication within the bone microenvironment, exosomes also participate in systemic crosstalk communication between bone and other tissues, including skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, gut microbiota, the immune system, the nervous system, and vasculature. Disruption of these exosome-mediated pathways contributes to the development and progression of bone diseases, including osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, osteonecrosis of the femoral head, and bone metastases. This review summarizes current advances in exosome-mediated signaling in both physiological and pathological contexts, with particular emphasis on their roles as biomarkers, therapeutic agents, and drug delivery vehicles. We also discuss the emerging contribution of artificial intelligence (AI) to exosome research, especially in biomarker discovery, disease classification, and target identification, as well as the major challenges that currently limit clinical translation. Together, these insights highlight the potential of exosome-based strategies for precision medicine in bone diseases.

Introduction

Bone diseases—including osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, and osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH)—represent major global public health concerns due to their high morbidity and associated disability rates [1, 2]. The onset and progression of these conditions are closely linked to imbalances in bone metabolism and structural damage, with alterations in the bone microenvironment playing a central role. Traditionally recognized for its structural and metabolic functions, the bone has more recently been redefined as an endocrine organ capable of active communication with other tissues. The bone microenvironment is a complex, dynamic system composed of various cellular and stromal components, including osteoblasts, osteoclasts, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), adipocytes, matrix proteins, vasculature [3–6]. While intercellular communication within this microenvironment has long been attributed to direct cell-to-cell contact and soluble signaling molecules, emerging evidence highlights the critical role of exosomes as novel mediators of cellular crosstalk [7]. Exosomes are small membrane-bound extracellular vesicles (EVs), approximately 30–150 nm in diameter, secreted by multiple cell types—including osteoblasts, osteoclasts, MSCs, and immune cells—and are present in various body fluids and tissues [2, 8–10]. Enclosed by a phospholipid bilayer, they carry diverse bioactive molecules such as nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids [11].

In bone biology, exosome-mediated communication has attracted increasing attention because it links intra-skeletal regulation with systemic inter-organ signaling. Exosomes derived from bone-resident cells influence osteogenesis, osteoclastogenesis, angiogenesis, and immune responses, while exosomal signals from other tissues, such as skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, the gut microbiota, and immune systems, can in turn reshape bone homeostasis and disease progression.

In this review, we summarize current knowledge of exosome-mediated crosstalk in the bone microenvironment and in bone communication with other tissues, emphasizing its relevance to major bone diseases and regenerative strategies. We also discuss the emerging role of AI in exosome research, particularly in biomarker discovery, cargo analysis, and the development of exosome-based precision therapies for bone diseases. In addition, we highlight current challenges, including mechanistic heterogeneity, technical limitations in exosome isolation and characterization, and the need for further translational and clinical validation.

It is important to clarify that EVs comprise several subtypes, including exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies. Given the functional overlap between EVs and exosomes, studies on EVs may offer valuable insights into the role of exosomes in bone homeostasis. Therefore, we have included select high-quality studies that reference the term “extracellular vesicles” in this review. Throughout this review, terminology is used in accordance with that reported in the original studies. The term “exosome” is retained when the cited studies explicitly refer to exosomes, whereas the broader term “EVs” is used when this terminology is adopted in the original articles.

Role of exosomes in cellular communication within the bone microenvironment

Exosomes derived from osteoblasts and their impact on the bone microenvironment

Osteoblasts are the primary cell type responsible for bone formation [3]. Exosomes secreted by osteoblasts are enriched with diverse bioactive molecules, including microRNAs (miRNAs), proteins, and signaling factors. These exosomes mediate communication with other bone-resident cells and vascular endothelial cells, thereby promoting stromal cell osteogenic differentiation, enhancing endothelial angiogenic activity, and modulating osteoclast differentiation, which together contribute to the regulation of the bone microenvironment and the maintenance of skeletal homeostasis (Fig. 1). Representative in vitro and in vivo studies, together with their functional effects, are summarized in Table 1 [12–21]. However, much of the current evidence comes from in vitro studies using established cell lines, and its interpretation is therefore limited by the inability of these models to fully recapitulate the in vivo bone microenvironment.

By contrast, the relatively limited in vivo evidence is particularly important for evaluating physiological relevance. Available animal studies support functional roles for osteoblast-derived exosomes in promoting osteogenic differentiation and suppressing tumor growth in osteosarcoma xenograft models [15], enhancing angiogenesis in vivo [17, 18], and inducing osteoclast-mediated bone loss or stress-associated osteopenia [19, 20].

Representative studies of osteoblast-derived exosomes and their functions in the bone microenvironment

Exosomes derived from osteoclasts and their role in bone resorption

Osteoclasts, derived from the mononuclear macrophage lineage, are the sole cell type capable of physiological bone resorption [22, 23]. They maintain skeletal homeostasis primarily by degrading bone matrix and releasing calcium. Recent studies have demonstrated that, beyond their direct resorptive function, osteoclasts influence the bone microenvironment through the secretion of exosomes, which modulate the activity of neighboring cells (Fig. 1). Osteoclast-derived exosomes have been shown to target osteoblasts and suppress bone formation. These exosomes transfer inhibitory cargos, including miR-214, miR-214-3p, miR-23a-5p, miR-5134-5p, and lncRNA AW011738, which reduce proliferation, differentiation, and bone-forming capacity through pathways such as ATF4, Runx2, JAK2/STAT3, and miR-24-2-5p/TREM1 [24–28].

Osteoclast-derived exosomes also influence chondrocyte function. In vitro studies have shown that exosomal let-7a-5p and miR-212-3p promote hypertrophic and catabolic changes in chondrocytes by disrupting Smad2-related signaling [29, 30]. More compelling in vivo genetic evidence showed that osteoclast-derived exosomes transferred multiple miRNAs to chondrocytes, suppressed TIMP-2 and TIMP-3, reduced cartilage resistance to matrix degradation, and promoted vascular and sensory nerve invasion, thereby accelerating osteoarthritis (OA). Importantly, conditional deletion of Rab27a in osteoclasts blocked this pathological process, restored cartilage integrity, and relieved pain, providing causal evidence for the direct involvement of osteoclast-derived exosomes in OA [31]. Exosomes derived from MSCs and their promoting effect on bone repair processes

MSCs, known for their multidirectional differentiation potential, are widely used in bone regeneration and repair. Emerging studies have shown that MSC-derived exosomes are enriched with a broad array of bioactive molecules—including miRNAs, circular RNAs (circRNAs), and proteins—which play crucial roles in skeletal tissue regeneration. These exosomes deliver pro-osteogenic signals and can also rescue impaired bone formation under stress- or aging-related conditions by restoring mitochondrial function and modulating pathways involved in osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption (Fig. 1) [9, 32–38]. Representative in vitro and in vivo studies, together with their functions, are summarized in Table 3. Consistent with these findings, a recent systematic review and network meta-analysis of 207 preclinical studies further demonstrated that scaffolds loaded with MSCs or MSC-derived extracellular vesicles significantly improved BV/TV in bone defect models. Notably, BMSC-loaded scaffolds outperformed ADSC-loaded scaffolds, and BMSC-derived extracellular vesicles conferred additional benefits compared with cell-free scaffolds [39].

In addition to promoting bone regeneration, MSC-derived exosomes also protect other musculoskeletal tissues. They preserve chondrocyte phenotype, reduce senescence and apoptosis, and attenuate cartilage degeneration [40, 41]. They also show therapeutic potential in intervertebral disc degeneration and spinal cord injury by suppressing cellular stress, inhibiting apoptosis, and promoting regeneration [42, 43].

Overall, BMSC-derived exosomes exhibit considerable potential for bone and related tissue repair. However, their translation remains constrained by donor heterogeneity, challenges in exosome standardization and large-scale clinical-grade exosome production, insufficient long-term safety data, and substantial heterogeneity in current preclinical studies. In addition, the above-mentioned systematic review and network meta-analysis also highlighted substantial heterogeneity across current preclinical studies, including differences in animal models, scaffold materials, extracellular vesicle doses, follow-up periods, and methodological rigor. These limitations reduce cross-study comparability and may introduce bias, underscoring the need for more standardized and rigorously designed studies to facilitate future translation [39].

Exosomes derived from osteocytes

Osteocytes, the mechanosensitive cells embedded within the bone matrix [22, 44], are increasingly recognized as active secretors of exosomes that regulate the behavior of surface bone cells. These cells respond to both mechanical and hormonal stimuli by modulating the composition of their exosomal cargo (Fig. 1). For example, catabolic stimulation with myostatin reduced exosomal miR-218 and suppressed osteogenic differentiation via Wnt/β-catenin inhibition [45]. In contrast, mechanical loading increased pro-osteogenic exosomal miRNAs, including miR-3110-5p and miR-3058-3p, which enhanced osteoblast differentiation [46]. These findings underscore the dual regulatory role of osteocyte-derived exosomes in bone remodeling by mediating both catabolic and anabolic signals. Osteocyte-derived exosomes also contribute to pathological bone loss in systemic diseases. In diabetes, osteocyte-derived exosomal miR-124-3p impaired osteoblast function by downregulating Galectin-3, thereby exacerbating bone destruction in diabetic periodontitis [47]. Current evidence in this subsection remains limited and is derived mainly from mechanistic in vitro studies, underscoring the need for further in vivo and clinical validation.

In summary, the bone microenvironment functions as an interconnected exosomal communication hub. Crosstalk among osteoblasts, osteoclasts, osteocytes, and MSCs via exosomes adds an additional regulatory layer to bone remodeling, complementing classical cytokine-mediated pathways. Disruption of this exosomal signaling can shift the balance of bone turnover toward resorption and contribute to disease. Conversely, targeting or engineering these vesicles offers promising strategies to restore bone formation. Having established this intra-skeletal communication network, the next section explores the systemic role of exosomes in mediating bone–organ interactions in health and disease.

Systemic bone exosomal communication network: inter-organ and inter-system exosomal crosstalk

Inter-organ exosomal crosstalk

Muscle–bone exosomal crosstalk

Bone and skeletal muscle are functionally interconnected through both mechanical coupling and biochemical communication, with exosomes now recognized as important mediators of muscle–bone crosstalk alongside myokines and osteokines (Fig. 2). Recent reviews have further emphasized skeletal muscle-derived exosomes and exosomal miRNAs as important regulators of muscle–bone communication, particularly in the contexts of exercise, sarcopenia, osteoporosis, and osteosarcopenia, while also noting that the underlying mechanisms and in vivo relevance remain incompletely defined [48, 49].

Current studies suggest that muscle-derived exosomes generally support bone formation and bone homeostasis under physiological conditions. Early work demonstrated that myoblasts secreted exosome-like vesicles [50], and subsequent studies showed that these vesicles promoted osteogenic differentiation through the delivery of bioactive cargos such as miR-27a-3p and paired-related homeobox 2 (Prrx2), which activated pro-osteogenic pathways including Wnt/β-catenin and YAP signaling in osteoprogenitor cells and BMSCs [51, 52]. In addition, skeletal muscle-derived extracellular vesicles have been reported to promote osteogenesis while suppressing osteoclastogenesis, thereby improving bone mass and microarchitecture in vivo [53].

Emerging evidence further suggests that muscle–bone exosomal signaling may mediate the beneficial effects of exercise and the detrimental effects of aging. For instance, physical activity increased exosomal release of irisin, a myokine cleaved from FNDC5. These irisin-containing exosomes promoted osteoblast proliferation and suppressed ferroptosis. In ovariectomized mice, this mechanism improved bone mineral density and trabecular microarchitecture, highlighting a novel exosome-mediated muscle–bone axis driven by exercise [54]. Conversely, aging and muscle disuse are associated with altered exosomal profiles that may contribute to bone loss. A notable example is miR-34a, which increases in muscle-derived exosomes with age. Upon uptake by MSCs, miR-34a induced senescence and impaired osteogenic differentiation. These “progeroid” exosomes may link muscle aging to impaired bone regeneration [55]. This mechanism helps explain the parallel development of sarcopenia and osteoporosis in older adults and underscores the therapeutic potential of exercise, which may shift the exosomal profile toward regenerative signaling. Nevertheless, current evidence remains limited, and further in vivo and clinical studies are needed to clarify the physiological significance and therapeutic potential of muscle–bone exosomal communication.

Adipose tissue–bone exosomal crosstalk

Adipose tissue, including marrow adipose tissue and peripheral fat depots, exerts significant endocrine effects on bone. In addition to classical adipokines [56–58], recent evidence suggests that exosomes derived from adipocytes and adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) participate in adipose–bone communication by transferring bioactive lipids, cytokines, and regulatory RNAs (Fig. 2).

Current evidence indicates that adipocyte-derived exosomes exert context-dependent effects on bone. Under physiological conditions, AD-Exos typically exert inhibitory effects on bone formation. Huang et al. [59] reported that bone marrow adipocytes secreted exosomes containing lipid droplets that were transferred to osteoblasts, where they accumulated and triggered ferroptosis—a form of iron-dependent cell death driven by lipid peroxidation. This process impaired osteoblast function and suppressed bone formation, contributing to metabolic bone disorders such as osteoporosis. Conversely, under pathological conditions involving excessive bone resorption, adipocyte-derived exosomes may exert protective effects. In a titanium (Ti) particle-induced osteolysis model, simulating peri-prosthetic bone loss, AD-Exos carrying miR-34a were shown to target and downregulate NLRP3 expression in macrophages. This suppressed M1 macrophage polarization, reduced inflammation, and mitigated bone loss [60].

By contrast, ASC-derived exosomes are more consistently associated with regenerative effects [61, 62]. Proteomic profiling of human ASC-derived exosomes reveals enrichment in proteins involved in immune modulation and osteogenesis [63]. Notably, ASC-derived exosomes are readily obtainable and pose fewer ethical concerns, positioning them as attractive candidates for clinical use [64]. In vivo, studies support the therapeutic potential of ASC-derived exosomes in bone repair. Nan et al. [65] demonstrated that exosomes from miR-378-modified ASCs promoted osteogenesis and angiogenesis by downregulating Sufu and activating the Sonic Hedgehog (Shh) signaling pathway, thereby enhancing repair in glucocorticoid-induced ONFH. Similarly, Zhang et al. [66] showed that ASC-derived exosomes activated the Wnt3a/β-catenin pathway, enhancing osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and promoting fracture healing in type 2 diabetic rats. These findings support the development of adipose-derived exosomes as “off-the-shelf” regenerative therapies for bone loss. However, much of the current evidence remains preclinical, and further in vivo and clinical studies are needed to clarify the therapeutic applicability.

Gut microbiota–bone exosomal crosstalk

The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as an important regulator of bone metabolism through the gut–bone axis [67–70]. Recent evidence suggests that bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) serve as a direct route of microbiota–host communication. By transporting bacterial bioactive molecules to distant tissues, including bone, they can influence osteogenesis, osteoclastogenesis, angiogenesis, and inflammatory signaling (Fig. 2).

A landmark study by Liu et al. [71] demonstrated the bone-protective effects of BEVs isolated from a healthy gut microbiome, including those derived from Akkermansia muciniphila. BEVs from young donor microbiota were shown to cross intestinal barriers, enter bone tissue, and directly modulate bone remodeling. In ovariectomized osteoporotic mice, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from young donors improved bone mass and architecture—effects that were replicated with administration of purified BEVs. These findings indicate that beneficial gut bacteria secrete exosome-like vesicles carrying pro-osteogenic signals, and their depletion through dysbiosis may contribute to bone loss. Supporting this, Chen et al. [72] reported that glucocorticoid (GC) treatment in mice reduced levels of BEVs from Lactobacillus animalis, coinciding with trabecular bone loss and ONFH. Oral supplementation with L. animalis or its derived BEVs reversed these skeletal defects by promoting angiogenesis and osteogenesis while reducing apoptosis, suggesting a causal role for BEVs in maintaining bone integrity under GC challenge.

By contrast, pathogenic BEVs may exert deleterious skeletal effects. BEVs from Filifactor alocis and Porphyromonas gingivalis have been shown to promote osteoclastogenesis and bone loss through inflammatory pathways such as TLR2 signaling [73, 74]. Although mechanistic studies further suggest that BEVs may regulate host signaling pathways including Wnt, PI3K/Akt, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) [75], much of the current evidence remains preclinical. Further in vivo and clinical studies are needed to define the source-specific functions, molecular cargos, and translational potential of BEVs in bone disorders.

Brain–bone exosomal crosstalk

Neuronal signals—including neuropeptides and sympathetic nervous system activity are known to influence bone metabolism [76, 77], while bone-derived factors such as osteocalcin regulate brain functions, including memory and cognition [78]. Recent evidence further suggests that extracellular vesicles may participate in this bidirectional communication, although the field remains at an early stage. One line of inquiry arises from studies of neurotrauma, where patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) often exhibit dysregulated bone remodeling [79, 80]. Singleton et al. [81] demonstrated that TBI triggered the release of EVs from bone marrow cells, which promoted osteoclast differentiation and contributed to systemic bone loss. These EVs activated nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling and increased the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor alpha [TNF-α]) in recipient bone marrow cells.

Conversely, bone-derived exosomes have shown neuroprotective effects, suggesting that bone-to-brain communication may be therapeutically beneficial. Liu et al. reported that exosomes from rat BMSCs (BMSC-Exos) administered intravenously in a cerebral ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) rat model crossed the blood–brain barrier and localized to injured brain regions. These exosomes promoted microglial polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 state, suppressed the NLRP3 inflammasome, reduced neuronal pyroptosis, and alleviated neuroinflammation. Functionally, BMSC-Exos improved both neurological deficits and cognitive function, highlighting their therapeutic potential for ischemic stroke [82]. Similarly, Deng et al. [83] demonstrated that exosomes from BMSCs overexpressing miR-138-5p conferred neuroprotection by delivering miR-138-5p to astrocytes, where it downregulated lipocalin-2 (LCN2), a pro-apoptotic and pro-inflammatory mediator. This resulted in reduced astrocytic apoptosis and inflammation. While these findings primarily address neurological outcomes, they establish that bone-resident cell-derived exosomes can reach the brain and modulate neural responses—a form of therapeutic inter-organ crosstalk.

Clinical studies of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) provide further evidence of brain-bone exosomal communication. AD is frequently associated with low bone mass, and recent research implicates brain-derived EVs in this comorbidity. Liu et al. [84] found that EVs isolated from the brains or plasma of patients with AD and model mice promoted a bone-fat imbalance by shifting BMSC differentiation toward adipogenesis and away from osteogenesis (Fig. 2). Mechanistically, miR-483-5p was highly enriched in AD-derived EVs and suppressed the expression of insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2) in BMSCs. In a complementary study, Jiang et al. [85] showed that EVs derived from young osteocytes trafficked to the brain, where they exerted neuroprotective effects in AD models by ameliorating amyloid pathology and improving cognitive function. Together, these findings suggest that the bone–brain communication via exosomes is bidirectional. Brain-derived EVs may contribute to skeletal degeneration in neurodegenerative diseases, while bone-derived EVs may provide neuroprotection and support cognitive resilience. This emerging axis opens new avenues for therapeutic strategies targeting age-related disorders involving both the brain and the skeleton.

Inter-system exosomal crosstalk

Osteo-immune crosstalk

The dynamic interplay between the immune system and bone—known as osteoimmunology—is well established. Inflammatory cytokines stimulate osteoclastogenesis, while various immune cell subsets, including macrophages and dendritic cells, directly regulate bone remodeling. Recent studies have identified exosomes as additional mediators in this immune–bone interface. Immune cell–derived exosomes can carry cytokines, receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL), and regulatory RNAs, thereby modulating skeletal homeostasis (Fig. 2) [86, 87]. Recent reviews have further underscored the translational significance of exosome-mediated osteoimmune regulation, particularly in the contexts of inflammatory bone loss and bone regeneration [88].

Current evidence indicates that immune cell-derived exosomes exert context-dependent effects on bone remodeling. Among these, macrophage-derived exosomes are the most extensively studied. Exosomes from anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages promote osteogenic differentiation and suppress osteoclastogenesis through cargos such as miR-365-2-5p and IL-10-related signaling [89, 90]. In contrast, exosomes from M1 macrophages exert deleterious effects on bone. Ge et al. [91] reported that, following spinal cord injury, M1 macrophage-derived exosomal miR-155 promoted vascular endothelial–mesenchymal transition and mitochondrial dysfunction, which exacerbated barrier disruption and inflammation. Similarly, Yu et al. [92] reported that M1-derived exosomes carrying miR-98 inhibited DUSP1 and activated the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling pathway, thereby accelerating bone loss in postmenopausal osteoporosis. Interestingly, the bone also exerts regulatory feedback on the immune system via exosomes. Zhang et al. [93] found that BMSC-derived exosomes promoted M2 macrophage polarization. This shift enhanced expression of Arg1 and IL-10, and concurrently promoted osteogenic differentiation by increasing Runx2 and osteocalcin levels.

Beyond macrophages, exosomes derived from other immune cells, such as dendritic cells and granulocytes, also contribute to bone repair and regeneration. Exosomes from mature dendritic cells promoted BMSC-mediated bone regeneration in a thymus-deficient rat femur defect model through the delivery of miRNA-335 [94]. Granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cell (G-MDSC)-derived exosomes attenuated collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) by suppressing Th1 and Th17 cell activation, largely through miR-29a-3p and miR-93-5p [95].

Vascular–bone exosomal crosstalk

Bone is a highly vascularized tissue, and its vasculature plays a crucial role in delivering oxygen, nutrients, and signaling molecules essential for bone development and repair. Angiogenesis is tightly coupled with osteogenesis, especially during bone growth and regeneration. Recent studies have revealed that endothelial cells and other vascular components communicate bidirectionally with bone cells, in part through exosome-mediated signaling. Liu et al. [96] investigated the role of endothelial cell–derived exosomes (EC-Exos) in coordinating angiogenesis and osteogenesis. Their study demonstrated that EC-Exos promoted the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by upregulating zinc finger and BTB domain containing 16 (ZBTB16) and activating the hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) pathway in endothelial cells. This led to the generation of hemogenic endothelial cells (HECs), which formed H-type blood vessels. These HECs, in turn, further stimulated BMSC osteogenesis, establishing a positive feedback loop that robustly coupled angiogenesis and osteogenesis during bone regeneration (Fig. 2).

In addition to enhancing osteogenesis, EC-Exos regulate the balance between bone formation and adipogenesis. Wang et al. [97] reported that EC-Exos containing miR-3p-975_4191 suppressed TNF expression, thereby promoting osteogenesis and inhibiting adipogenesis in BMSCs. Wu et al. [98] showed that EC-Exos, generated in response to vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) stimulation, activated the MAPK/ERK pathway, enhancing osteogenesis and suppressing lipogenesis in GC-induced ONFH. Similarly, Li et al. [99] found that EC-Exos enriched with miR-5p-72106_14 promoted osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs while inhibiting adipogenic lineage commitment.

EC-Exos also contribute to bone regeneration through immunomodulation. Chen et al. [100] demonstrated that exosomes from HUVECs delivered nuclear-enriched transcript 1 (NEAT1), which suppressed inflammation by modulating the DDX3X/NLRP3 signaling axis. This action promoted macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, which in turn enhanced BMSC osteogenic differentiation and migration, supporting bone repair.

In summary, vascular–bone exosomal crosstalk plays a critical role in bone physiology and regeneration. EC-Exos mediate communication with BMSCs to couple angiogenesis with osteogenesis, regulate the balance between osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation, and modulate the immune environment to facilitate repair. These multifaceted roles position EC-Exos as promising targets or vectors for regenerative therapies in bone-related diseases.

Role of exosomes in the pathological bone microenvironment

Role of exosomes in osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mineral density, compromised bone microarchitecture, and increased fracture risk. The condition primarily results from excessive osteoclast activity alongside diminished osteoblast function [56, 101, 102]. Recent evidence has highlighted the critical regulatory role of exosomes in this process. For example, serum-derived exosomal hsa_circ_0006859 was found to be upregulated in postmenopausal osteoporosis and to inhibit osteogenic differentiation while promoting adipogenesis through the miR-431-5p/ROCK1 axis [103]. This lineage imbalance contributed to the deterioration of bone quality and accelerates osteoporotic pathology. In another study, Xie et al. [104] employed quantitative proteomics and in vitro assays to demonstrate that serum-derived exosomes from osteoporotic patients impaired osteoblastic matrix mineralization and simultaneously promoted osteoclast differentiation, further disrupting bone homeostasis. A recent review further emphasized that, although exosome-based approaches show considerable promise for osteoporosis treatment by modulating osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis, their translation remains limited by exosome heterogeneity, poor targeting efficiency, rapid clearance, and the lack of standardized manufacturing and clinical validation [105]. These findings underscore the pathogenic and therapeutic role of altered exosomal signaling in osteoporosis and suggest that specific exosomal RNAs and proteins may serve as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets for managing this prevalent bone disorder.

Role of exosomes in bone tumors and bone metastases

Bone tumors, including osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and Ewing’s sarcoma, as well as bone metastases from breast, prostate, and lung cancers, are characterized by disrupted bone remodeling and progressive skeletal destruction [106, 107]. Increasing evidence indicates that tumor cells interact with the bone microenvironment in part through the secretion of exosomes, which facilitate bone resorption, matrix degradation, and metastatic spread [108]. Representative studies are summarized in Table 4. In primary bone malignancies, exosomes contribute to both tumor aggressiveness and microenvironmental reprogramming. For instance, osteosarcoma-derived exosomal cargos have been shown to enhance migration, invasion, and metastatic niche formation, partly through activation of oncogenic signaling pathways and macrophage polarization [10, 109]. In addition, tumor-derived exosomes can directly disturb bone homeostasis by promoting osteoclastogenesis, thereby exacerbating local bone loss and bone destruction [110].

Multiple myeloma (MM), although classified as a hematological malignancy, significantly impacts skeletal integrity, often causing osteoporosis, bone pain, and fractures. Exosomes play crucial roles in MM pathogenesis and progression. MM-derived exosomes promote osteoclast differentiation, inhibit osteoblast function, and contribute to osteolytic bone loss through both direct effects on bone cells and indirect regulation of stromal cells [111, 112]. Exosomal signaling also supports myeloma progression by enhancing stem-like properties and drug resistance, further underscoring its pathogenic importance [113].

Exosomes also act as crucial mediators of bone metastasis. In prostate and breast cancers, tumor-derived exosomes disrupt bone homeostasis by stimulating osteoclast differentiation and dysregulating osteoblast function. These changes promote either osteolytic or aberrant osteogenic lesions and create a permissive niche for metastatic colonization and growth in bone [114–117].

In summary, exosomes play multifaceted roles in the progression of bone tumors, myeloma, and bone metastases. They facilitate tumor growth, bone degradation, immune modulation, and therapeutic resistance by altering the bone microenvironment. Consequently, exosomes serve are emerging as valuable biomarkers for tumor diagnosis and prognosis, as well as promising therapeutic targets. As research in this field advances, targeting exosomal signaling may represent a transformative strategy for managing bone-associated malignancies and metastases.

Role of exosomes in osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic degenerative joint disease characterized by cartilage degradation, osteophyte formation, synovial inflammation, and abnormal subchondral bone remodeling [2]. Increasing evidence suggests that exosomes play critical roles in OA pathogenesis, particularly in cartilage matrix degradation, inflammatory modulation, and intercellular communication within the joint microenvironment. Representative studies on the role of exosomes in osteoarthritis are summarized in Table 5. Recent reviews have further emphasized that extracellular vesicles in OA are not only pathological mediators within the diseased joint, but also potential biomarkers and therapeutic tools for cartilage repair [118].

Current evidence indicates that exosomes derived from different joint-resident cells exert distinct effects in OA. Exosomes derived from healthy primary chondrocytes show protective effects against OA progression by restoring mitochondrial function and modulating immune responses [119]. However, under OA conditions, alterations in the composition of chondrocyte-derived exosomes can promote disease progression by inducing cartilage degradation and chondrocyte death [120–122]. Synovial cell–derived exosomes also contribute to OA pathology. Exosomes released from inflammatory fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs) aggravate synovial inflammation by promoting macrophage glycolysis and M1 polarization [123], while osteoarthritic fibroblast-like synoviocyte-derived exosomes exacerbate cartilage injury by inducing chondrocyte ferroptosis [124]. These findings support the potential of exosomes as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in OA, although further studies are needed to clarify source-specific functions and improve translational applicability.

Role of exosomes in other bone-related diseases

Beyond osteoporosis, bone tumors, and osteoarthritis, exosomes have also been implicated in several other bone-related disorders, including intervertebral disc degeneration, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL), diabetes-related bone disease, gout, and ONFH. Emerging studies highlight the diverse roles of exosome-mediated signaling in the pathogenesis and progression of these conditions. Notably, recent reviews have also summarized the regulatory roles of exosomes in inflammatory spinal diseases such as ankylosing spondylitis, where exosomal cargos participate in immune modulation and dysregulated bone remodeling [125]. In ONFH, exosomes derived from non-traumatic femoral head necrosis impaired the osteogenic differentiation and migration of MSCs by downregulating CD41 expression, thereby exacerbating disease progression [126]. Similarly, in intervertebral disc degeneration, M1 macrophage-derived exosomes delivered LCN2, a lipid carrier protein, which activated the NF-κB signaling pathway and promoted nuclear myeloid cell senescence, contributing to disc degeneration [127]. Exosomal microRNAs have also been implicated in the pathogenesis of ligament ossification. In OPLL, a significant downregulation of miR-140-5p has been observed in cell-derived exosomes. This microRNA targeted insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) and inhibited the IRS1/PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby suppressing MSC osteogenic differentiation. These findings suggest miR-140-5p as a potential molecular target for therapeutic intervention in OPLL [128]. Metabolic and inflammatory bone disorders are likewise influenced by exosome-mediated signaling. In diabetes-related bone disease, exosomes derived from bone marrow macrophages in diabetic rat models were enriched in miR-144-5p. This microRNA inhibited osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs by targeting Smad1, ultimately impairing fracture healing [129]. In gout-induced bone destruction, neutrophil-derived exosomes stimulated with monosodium urate crystals carried miR-1246, which suppressed osteoblast viability and promoted bone erosion [130].

Clinical applications and research advances

Potential of exosomes in the diagnosis of bone diseases

One of the most promising clinical applications of exosome research in orthopedics is the development of minimally invasive diagnostic tools. Exosomes are continuously secreted into various body fluids—including blood, urine, and synovial fluid—and carry a wide array of molecular cargo such as proteins, RNAs, and lipids that reflect the physiological or pathological state of their cells of origin. As such, exosomes serve as a form of “liquid biopsy,” offering real-time insights into disease processes without requiring invasive tissue sampling [131]. This is particularly valuable in bone-related disorders, where direct access to bone tissue is often limited or clinically impractical. Circulating exosomes thus represent an accessible and informative source of disease biomarkers. Recent advances have focused on identifying exosomal miRNAs and protein signatures associated with bone diseases and leveraging computational tools—including machine learning (ML)—to aid in their discovery and validation.

In osteoporosis, changes in exosomal miRNA composition can indicate imbalances between bone formation and resorption. Shi et al. [132] identified several circulating miRNAs—miR-324-3p, miR-766-3p, miR-1247-5p, miR-330-5p, and miR-3124-5p—associated with bone mineral density. These miRNAs were implicated in the pathophysiology of postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP) and might serve as diagnostic biomarkers. To enhance diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, Luo et al. [133] developed a fluorescence biosensor combining 3D DNA walker-induced CRISPR/Cas12a technology for the detection of exosomal miR-214, a biomarker linked to osteoporosis. This platform exploited the signal amplification capacity of the 3D DNA walker and the collateral cleavage activity of CRISPR/Cas12a, enabling the detection of exomiR-214 at concentrations as low as 20.42 fM. The biosensor effectively distinguished between healthy individuals and patients with osteoporosis, highlighting its potential for early diagnosis.

Building on these findings, Gao et al. [134] employed a ML-based approach to optimize an exosomal miRNA signature for the diagnosis of postmenopausal osteoporosis. Using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression on small RNA sequencing data, they identified a three-miRNA panel—let-7d-3p, miR-24-3p, and miR-550a-3-5p—that effectively distinguished patients with osteoporosis from healthy controls. In the training cohort, each miRNA achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of approximately 0.80, while the combined panel reached an AUC of 0.91, outperforming conventional bone turnover markers such as procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (PINP) and β-isomer of C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (β-CTX). Validation in an independent cohort yielded comparable results. Notably, the panel also differentiated osteopenic individuals—those with low bone mass but not yet osteoporotic—from healthy controls, suggesting its utility for early diagnosis and risk stratification. These results underscore the potential of AI-driven feature selection and risk modeling in exosome-based biomarker development. The integration of exosomal miRNA profiling with ML represents a powerful approach for creating clinically actionable tools for osteoporosis diagnosis, fracture risk prediction, and therapeutic monitoring. For instance, tracking the normalization of miRNA signatures could serve as a biomarker of response to osteoanabolic therapies.

Beyond osteoporosis, exosomes are gaining attention as diagnostic tools in bone tumors and metastases. Jiang et al. [135] found that low expression of exosomal miR-144-3p correlated with poor prognosis in patients with osteosarcoma, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker. Wang et al. [136] further demonstrated that osteosarcoma cells promoted lung metastasis by releasing exosomes containing programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and N-cadherin. Monitoring circulating levels of these exosomal proteins may aid in predicting metastatic risk in patients with osteosarcoma. In the context of bone metastases, Yuan et al. [100] reported that exosomal miR-21 was significantly elevated in serum of patients with breast cancers with bone metastases compared to those without. This finding suggests that miR-21 may serve as both a diagnostic marker and a therapeutic target for metastatic bone disease.

In addition to their role in disease diagnosis and monitoring, exosomes can be leveraged to evaluate bone tissue engineering outcomes and osteogenic activity. For example, Sánchez et al. [137] demonstrated that exosomal alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity in culture supernatants can be used to non-invasively monitor osteoblast function without compromising cell viability. This strategy enables real-time assessment of osteogenic responses to biomaterials and provides a valuable tool for evaluating the regenerative potential of bone-forming scaffolds. Their findings suggest that the bioactivity profiling of exosomes may uncover novel biomarkers relevant to bone repair and regeneration, thereby enhancing the efficiency of biomaterial screening in regenerative medicine.

Collectively, these findings underscore the promise of exosomes as reservoirs of diagnostic information in bone-related diseases. By encapsulating molecular signals from bone-resident cells and potentially from distant organs that influence bone metabolism—into stable, circulating vesicles, exosomes offer an accessible source of disease-specific biomarkers detectable in peripheral blood. Numerous candidate exosomal miRNAs and proteins have been proposed as diagnostic indicators, and with further validation in large, well-characterized patient cohorts, exosome-based assays may evolve into reliable, minimally invasive tools for diagnosing and monitoring skeletal disorders. Moreover, integrating exosomal biomarker profiling with artificial intelligence AI–driven analytics could further enhance diagnostic accuracy. By combining exosome molecular signatures with clinical and demographic variables, AI-based models may improve specificity and predictive power in identifying bone disease states. Although several technical and standardization challenges remain, advances in exosome isolation, quantification, and data interpretation continue to accelerate progress in the field. In the future, exosome-derived biomarkers may complement or even replace conventional bone turnover markers, enabling earlier and more precise detection of skeletal disease activity.

Exosomes as delivery vehicles for the treatment of bone diseases

Exosomes have emerged as promising nanocarriers for therapeutic delivery due to their low immunogenicity, high biocompatibility, and intrinsic targeting capabilities. These natural vesicles are increasingly being explored for delivering drugs, genetic material, and bioactive molecules to the bone microenvironment [138]. Through advanced bioengineering techniques, exosomes can be tailored to target bone tissue and deliver osteogenic factors, anti-bone resorptive agents, gene-editing tools, or anti-tumor drugs, thereby promoting bone repair, modulating bone metabolism, and inhibiting disease progression.

Exosomes have demonstrated efficacy as carriers of osteogenic agents that enhance bone regeneration. For example, Mi et al. [139] utilized EC-Exos to deliver miR-26a-5p. While EC-Exos exhibited bone-targeting and anti-osteoclastic properties, miR-26a-5p promoted osteogenic differentiation. The combined delivery resulted in both enhanced osteogenesis and suppressed osteoclast activity, accelerating fracture repair. Similarly, Dong et al. [140] encapsulated icariin (ICA) within fetal bovine serum–derived exosomes (FBS-Exos), forming an FBS-Exo–ICA complex. This complex significantly improved osteoblast proliferation and bone regeneration compared to icariin alone. In another study, Zhu et al. [37] demonstrated that miR-182-5p inhibited osteoblast differentiation; by loading exosomes with miR-182-5p inhibitors, osteogenic activity and bone regeneration were restored, effectively repairing bone defects.

In conditions involving excessive bone resorption, such as osteoporosis, exosomes can serve as delivery vehicles for molecules that inhibit osteoclastogenesis and inflammation. Yang et al. [141] showed that METTL14 suppressed osteoclast-mediated bone resorption via m6A methylation of activated T cell nuclear factor of type C1 (NFATc1). EphA2 (a receptor tyrosine kinase [RTK])-overexpressing exosomes were used to deliver METTL14 specifically to osteoclasts, reducing bone resorption and avoiding jaw osteonecrosis associated with bisphosphonate use—offering a novel targeted approach for osteoporosis therapy. Additionally, Elashiry et al. [142] encapsulated TGFβ1 and IL10 in dendritic cell-derived exosomes. These immunoregulatory exosomes reduced pro-resorptive cytokine levels by inhibiting dendritic cell maturation and promoting regulatory T cell recruitment, thereby mitigating osteoclast-driven bone loss.

Exosomes have also been utilized to deliver genes, hormones, and anticancer drugs, expanding their therapeutic potential in bone disease. Zha et al. [143] developed a cell-free bone tissue engineering system by loading VEGF genes into ATDC5 cell-derived exosomes, which were anchored into 3D-printed bone scaffolds using the CP05 peptide. This approach significantly enhanced both osteogenesis and angiogenesis. In another study, Yuan et al. [144] utilized exosomes to deliver siRNA and growth hormone to counteract the effects of plasma long non-coding RNA ISSRL in patients with idiopathic short stature (ISS), thereby promoting chondrocyte proliferation and bone formation. Gao et al. [145] loaded berberine into M2 macrophage–derived exosomes for spinal cord injury treatment. These exosomes reduced inflammation, inhibited neuronal apoptosis, and improved motor function. Wei et al. [146] employed MSC-derived exosomes to deliver doxorubicin to osteosarcoma cells via SDF1-CXCR4 axis targeting. This strategy enhanced antitumor efficacy while minimizing cardiotoxicity.

Engineering of exosomes and their application potential in bone tissue engineering

Advancements in gene editing, nanobioengineering, and synthetic biology have enabled the development of engineered exosomes as a novel strategy for bone tissue repair and regeneration. Exosome functionality and tissue-targeting specificity can be precisely modulated through gene editing, content regulation, surface modification, and integration with biomaterials, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy [147]. These innovations hold substantial promise in bone tissue engineering. A recent review by Li et al. [148] summarizes these strategies, including surface modification, gene editing, and nanotechnology-based engineering approaches, as well as their integration with biomaterials for enhanced therapeutic outcomes.

Advances in gene-editing technologies have opened a new dimension for functional reprogramming of exosomes. Tools such as CRISPR/Cas9 not only enable modulation of exosomal cargo at the level of parental cells but also allow exosomes themselves to serve as delivery vehicles for gene-editing systems, thereby facilitating precise genetic intervention in bone and cartilage disorders. For instance, exosomes engineered to activate FGF18 expression in articular chondrocytes via CRISPR/Cas9 were encapsulated in methacrylated hyaluronic acid microspheres to form an injectable microgel system. This system simultaneously promoted cartilage regeneration, alleviated inflammation, and provided sustained joint lubrication, ultimately delaying OA [149]. Another strategy focuses on targeted delivery by fusing liposomes with dendritic cell-derived exosomes displaying chondrocyte-targeting peptides. This hybrid exosome platform successfully delivered CRISPR/Cas9 to chondrocytes and inhibited MMP-13 expression, thereby reducing cartilage matrix degradation and mitigating osteoarthritic pathology [150]. In osteoporosis treatment, researchers have further expanded the application of exosomes as gene-based therapeutic carriers by developing the BT-Exo-siShn3 system derived from induced pluripotent stem cells. In an ovariectomized mouse model, intravenous administration of BT-Exo-siShn3 not only improved bone microarchitecture but also concurrently enhanced osteogenic differentiation, suppressed osteoclastogenesis, and promoted SLIT3-mediated type H vessel formation through Shn3 silencing. This integrated strategy offers a promising approach for targeted gene therapy in osteoporosis [151].

Another key application involves the integration of exosomes with biomaterials to develop exosome-functionalized scaffolds. These composite systems combine the structural support of materials like porous scaffolds, hydrogels, and natural polymers with the bioactivity of exosomes to promote osteogenesis and vascularization. Jin et al. [152] anchored M2 macrophage-derived exosomes onto nanofiber scaffolds with hierarchical porosity, which promoted the formation of type H vessels and coordinated angiogenesis, and osteoclast activity for functional bone regeneration in a critical-sized rat cranial bone defect model. Kang et al. [153] developed a PLGA/Exo-Mg-GA2 composite scaffold that integrated magnesium organic frameworks with human adipose stem cell exosomes, which showed excellent osteogenic, angiogenic and anti-inflammatory properties in a rat calvarial defect model. Liu et al. [154] encapsulated the ER stress-relieving molecule Sephin1 in MSC-derived exosomes and embedded them within high-performance hydrogels, significantly enhancing bone defect repair in diabetic rat models.

To improve targeting specificity, surface-modified exosomes have been developed using ligands such as peptides, antibodies, or glycosylation tags. One widely adopted strategy involves decorating exosomes with bone-affinity peptides such as Asp8, enabling preferential accumulation within mineralized tissue. When combined with osteogenic miRNA cargos such as miR-26 A, this approach enhanced bone formation and effectively counteracts osteoporotic bone loss in vivo [155]. Surface modification has progressed from single bone-binding peptides to strategies integrating peptide targeting with glycoengineering. Fut6 overexpression enriched exosomes with sialyl Lewis X, enhancing E-selectin–mediated recruitment to bone. Combined with the mineral-affinitive peptide AspSerSer6, this approach increased skeletal accumulation in a smoking-related osteoporosis model, alleviated oxidative stress–induced ferroptosis in bone marrow MSCs, and improved osteogenesis and trabecular bone mass [156]. Beyond peptide-based targeting, small-molecule conjugation is also employed to exploit inherent bone affinity. The covalent attachment of bisphosphonates such as alendronate to platelet-derived exosomes created constructs with strong hydroxyapatite-binding capacity, thereby promoting osteogenesis and angiogenesis in glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis models [157]. For osteolytic conditions driven by inflammatory dysregulation, cell-mimetic hybridization has been proposed as a targeting strategy. Fusion of M2 macrophage–derived and BMSC-derived exosomes produced hybrid vesicles that integrated immunomodulatory signaling with pro-regenerative cues. These engineered vesicles promoted reparative macrophage polarization, suppressed osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, and alleviated bone loss in osteolytic models in vivo, highlighting their potential for preventing aseptic implant loosening [158].

Nevertheless, several challenges remain before engineered exosomes can be translated into clinical applications. Most current evidence derives from small-animal models under controlled conditions, while long-term biodistribution, immunogenicity, and safety profiles remain insufficiently characterized. Moreover, many in vitro studies rely on simplified cell culture systems that fail to reproduce the biomechanical loading, vascular architecture, and immune microenvironment of native bone tissue. Furthermore, achieving precise targeting within the bone microenvironment and coordinated regulation of osteogenesis, osteoclast activity, and angiogenesis remains challenging. Therefore, rigorous in vivo validation and scalable translational strategies are essential for clinical advancement.

AI-empowered exosome research in bone diseases

The integration of AI with exosome biology has created new opportunities for advancing research in bone-related diseases. Recent reviews have discussed the emerging role of AI in exosome characterization, biomaterial design, and translational applications in bone regeneration [159]. However, the application of AI specifically in exosome-based research on bone diseases—including biomarker discovery, disease classification, and therapeutic optimization—remains less systematically discussed. Exosomal profiling generates complex, high-dimensional datasets—including miRNA expression, proteomic signatures, and lipidomic patterns—that are ideally suited for AI and ML applications. These algorithms can identify subtle, nonlinear relationships and build predictive models that outperform traditional statistical approaches (Fig. 3). Below, we highlight representative applications of AI in exosome-based bone disease research and discuss current challenges.

Biomarker discovery and disease classification

A major application of AI in exosome research is the identification of diagnostic biomarkers and disease classification. For example, Gao et al. [134] employed LASSO regression to identify diagnostic miRNAs in postmenopausal osteoporosis, developing a predictive model with superior performance. Similarly, in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), ML algorithms have been used to integrate multiple exosomal miRNAs into composite diagnostic scores, outperforming individual biomarkers in sensitivity and specificity [160].

A particularly impactful application of AI has emerged in oncology. Li et al. [161] developed a robust ML framework based on exosomal proteomics to distinguish cancer patients from healthy individuals across multiple tumor types. Using samples from 205 patients with breast, lung, and colorectal cancers and 51 healthy controls, they applied mutual information-based feature selection followed by a random forest classifier. The model identified an optimal panel of 18 exosomal proteins and achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.96 in cross-validation, outperforming other algorithms and generalizing well to independent datasets. Notably, several proteins in the panel, including APOC1, were established cancer-related markers. This study illustrates the utility of ML in mining exosomal proteomic data for clinically actionable biomarkers. This approach can be extended to bone diseases. For example, ML could be applied to exosomal proteomic profiles from patients with osteoporosis and healthy controls to identify a multiprotein diagnostic panel. Chen et al. [162] generated such a dataset through proteomic profiling of plasma-derived exosomes from individuals with varying bone mineral densities. This dataset can support AI-driven stratification of patients into biologically distinct subtypes, such as high- and low-bone-turnover osteoporosis, using unsupervised clustering or supervised learning approaches. AI also holds promise for classifying subtypes of bone tumors. Tang et al. [163] used hierarchical clustering of proteomic data from 272 sarcoma samples to identify subgroups with distinct clinical features. A similar strategy could be applied to exosome profiling in osteosarcoma or metastatic bone tumors. For instance, exosomal signatures from patients with different tumor stages or treatment responses could be analyzed using neural networks or clustering algorithms to uncover “exosomal phenotypes” predictive of prognosis or therapeutic efficacy. Early efforts in this area are currently underway.

Exosome subtype identification

Recent technological advances have enabled high-resolution characterization of EV subpopulations at the single-particle level. High-dimensional single-EV flow cytometry, combined with unsupervised clustering algorithms, has revealed substantial heterogeneity among circulating EVs and identified disease-associated subsets, particularly in neurodegenerative conditions such as AD [164, 165]. In oncology, deep learning models trained on surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) signals from plasma-derived exosomes have achieved high diagnostic accuracy for early-stage cancers and enabled tissue-of-origin classification within complex biofluids [165, 166]. Complementary proteomics-based approaches have also proven effective. For instance, Li et al. [161] applied a random forest classifier to exosomal protein fingerprints to differentiate between cancer patients and healthy individuals and to stratify tumor subtypes, achieving AUC values exceeding 0.90. Moreover, exosomal surface markers such as glypican-1 have demonstrated clinical utility in tracking tumor burden and recurrence [167].

These strategies hold significant promise for bone disease diagnostics. Distinct EV molecular signatures have been reported in skeletal disorders, including differential RNA and protein profiles in OA, osteoporosis, and bone metastases [168]. In principle, ML models trained on plasma EV datasets could classify patients into specific bone disease categories by mapping vesicle-derived molecular signatures to disease-specific patterns. Such a platform could serve as a noninvasive multiclass liquid biopsy for the early and precise differentiation of metabolic bone disorders, degenerative joint conditions, and skeletal malignancies.

Drug and target discovery

AI is increasingly leveraged to identify novel therapeutic targets from exosomal datasets. By comparing exosomal RNA or protein profiles between responders and non-responders to bone-targeting agents, ML algorithms can pinpoint biomolecules consistently associated with treatment outcomes, implicating them in disease-relevant pathways. For example, osteoporosis therapies that normalize specific exosomal miRNA levels suggest these miRNAs may act as regulators of bone turnover and represent potential therapeutic targets. Additionally, deep learning approaches—such as autoencoders—have been applied to integrate multi-omics data from exosomes (miRNAs, lncRNAs, proteins) across large patient cohorts. These integrative models construct regulatory networks and highlight hub nodes that may serve as actionable points for therapeutic intervention.

Current challenges for AI in exosome research

Despite promising advances, several challenges hinder the widespread application of AI in exosome research for bone diseases. First, most current studies are limited by small sample sizes—often involving fewer than 100 subjects—making models prone to overfitting, and limiting generalizability [169, 170]. Addressing this will require multicenter collaborations, standardized sample processing, and larger datasets. Second, ground truth Validation remains a bottleneck. For example, if an AI model predicts that an exosomal signature correlates with high fracture risk, prospective longitudinal data are needed to confirm its predictive validity—an inherently time- and resource-intensive process. Third, interpretability is a critical concern. Clinicians may be hesitant to adopt “black-box” models lacking mechanistic transparency. This has prompted efforts to develop interpretable ML frameworks and incorporate biological priors—such as restricting feature selection to genes or pathways already implicated in bone metabolism—to improve trust and clinical adoption. Technical variability in exosome isolation and processing can also introduce batch effects that confound downstream analyses. Thus, rigorous normalization strategies and inclusion of technical covariates during model training are essential for reproducibility.

Despite these limitations, AI has already accelerated biomarker discovery and translational applications. Multifactorial exosome-based indices—analogous to the FRAX tool—can integrate multiple weak biomarkers into a robust composite score for applications, such as fracture risk prediction. Combined with emerging microfluidics technologies, these models could enable point-of-care diagnostics. For example, microfluidic chips capable of isolating exosomes from finger-prick blood samples are now being integrated with onboard AI analytics for rapid clinical decision making [171].

In summary, AI is transforming exosome research by enabling the analysis of complex, high-dimensional molecular profiles. This interdisciplinary field stands to benefit greatly from closer collaboration between computer scientists, biologists, and clinicians. As large-scale exosomal datasets—such as those from bone health registries—become available, AI is expected to become instrumental in redefining disease subtypes and identifying new diagnostic and therapeutic targets. Exosomal miRNA-based models show promise for predicting osteoporosis risk, while proteomic classifiers offer a path toward stratifying musculoskeletal tumors. In the future, AI may guide personalized exosome-based interventions tailored to the molecular and clinical profiles of individual patients.

Challenges and future perspectives

The expanding field of bone exosome research holds significant clinical potential but faces several challenges that must be overcome to facilitate clinical translation. Below, we outline the key obstacles and future directions in this domain.

Isolation and purification of exosomes

Efficient and reproducible isolation of exosomes remains a major technical hurdle. Current methods—including ultracentrifugation, density gradient centrifugation, immunoaffinity capture, and membrane filtration—often yield preparations with suboptimal purity, low recovery rates, and labor-intensive workflows. Moreover, the presence of abundant proteins and lipids in complex biological fluids such as blood and bone marrow complicates the separation process and may contaminate exosome preparations [2, 172]. Therefore, the development of rapid, high yield, and scalable isolation technologies with enhanced specificity is critical for advancing both research and clinical applications.

Standardization of exosome characterization

The lack of standardized protocols for exosome isolation, storage, quantification, and functional assessment limits reproducibility and impedes clinical application. Variability in exosome source, isolation method, cargo content significantly influences their biological activity. To address this, there is an urgent need to establish robust criteria for exosome identity—including standardized surface markers and cargo profiling—and to harmonize quality control parameters across laboratories. Such standardization will be pivotal in ensuring the consistency, safety, and efficacy of exosome-based diagnostics and therapeutics.

Optimization of dosage and delivery strategies

Precise control over exosome dosage and delivery modalities is essential to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. The biodistribution and biological activity of exosomes are influenced by factors such as vesicle size, surface charge, ligand modification, and route of administration (e.g., intravenous versus local injection). Tailoring delivery strategies to specific disease contexts and bone tissue targets remains a key challenge. Further, optimal dosing regimens—defined by concentration, frequency, and duration—must be established to avoid under- or over-treatment and ensure consistent outcomes across patient populations.

Biosafety and clinical translation

Although exosomes possess favorable attributes such as low immunogenicity and biocompatibility, their long-term safety profiles remain incompletely understood. Comprehensive preclinical studies are needed to assess potential toxicities, immunological responses, and biodistributions patterns following systemic or local administration. Furthermore, rigorous clinical trials are required to evaluate efficacy, optimal formulations, and patient safety. Particular attention should be paid to potential off-target effects and interactions within the bone microenvironment, especially in disease settings characterized by inflammation, immunosuppression, or altered bone remodeling.

Conclusion

Exosomes have emerged as critical mediators of bone biology, orchestrating intercellular and interorgan communication. The concept of a bone exosomal communication network provides a unifying framework to understand how external physiological signals influence skeletal homeostasis and, conversely, how bone-derived exosomes modulate distant organ functions. This review has outlined the roles of intraosseous exosomal signaling among osteoblasts, osteoclasts, osteocytes, and MSCs, emphasizing their regulatory influence on bone remodeling and their therapeutic potential in bone-related diseases. We also highlighted emerging evidence of systemic exosomal crosstalk between the bone and other organs—including skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, gut microbiota, brain, immune system, and vasculature.

These insights are not merely theoretical but have tangible clinical relevance. Circulating exosomes enriched in disease-specific microRNAs and proteins are gaining recognition as promising biomarkers for bone disorders. ML-driven analyses have enabled the development of exosome-based diagnostic panels with high accuracy for conditions such as postmenopausal osteoporosis. On the therapeutic front, preclinical studies support the feasibility of leveraging exosomes as “cell-free” delivery systems to treat skeletal diseases. Exosomes offer distinct advantages over conventional therapies, including low immunogenicity, nanoscale targeting, and the capacity to transport complex bioactive cargos—making them ideal candidates for precise and minimally invasive interventions.

Despite ongoing challenges, such as standardization and delivery optimization, the field is advancing rapidly. The surge in publications on “bone exosomes” and “extracellular vesicles in bone diseases” reflects this growing momentum, with clear shift from descriptive studies toward mechanistic and translational research.

Nevertheless, this review also has several inherent limitations. First, although we aimed to provide a comprehensive and balanced overview, the rapidly expanding and heterogeneous nature of exosome research means that relevant studies may have been omitted or underrepresented, potentially introducing selection bias. Second, many cited findings are based on preclinical models or in vitro systems, which may not fully recapitulate the complexity of human bone physiology and pathology. Third, while AI offers powerful tools for exosome analysis, its clinical translation is still limited by small datasets and lack of standardized validation. Future research should prioritize the development of uniform methodological standards, large-scale clinical validation of exosome-based biomarkers and therapies, and multidisciplinary collaboration to bridge the gap between basic discovery and clinical application. Acknowledging these limitations enhances the transparency and interpretability of the current review and provides a roadmap for future investigation.

Systemic regulation of bone via exosomes marks a paradigm shift in skeletal biology. Bone is no longer viewed as an isolated organ regulated solely by local and endocrine signals, but as part of a broader network of nanoscale communication. This evolving perspective encourages researchers and clinicians to transcend traditional boundaries and consider the bone as an active participant in systemic homeostasis. Looking ahead, patients with osteoporosis and other skeletal disorders may benefit from targeted exosome-based therapies—such as “pro-osteogenic” exosome infusions or engineered exosome-mimetic nanoparticles—used in conjunction with conventional anti-resorptive or anabolic therapies to promote bone regeneration. As exosome research continues to mature, it is poised to become a cornerstone of integrated musculoskeletal care, much like how genomics and proteomics have transformed precision medicine.
