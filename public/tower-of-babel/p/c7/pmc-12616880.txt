Clinical Outcomes and Characterisation of the Autologous Adipose Tissue Harvested With Superficial Enhanced Fluid Fat Injection Method for Treatment of Diabetic Foot Ulcer Undergoing Minor Amputation (SEFFIDiFA Trial)
Authors: Nicola Leone, Francesco Andreoli, Mattia Migliari, Federica Guerra, Samantha Sperduti, Livio Casarini, Roberto Silingardi, Stefano Gennai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are severe complications of diabetes that often lead to major amputations. Despite care advancements, approximately 50% of minor amputations do not heal, resulting in major amputations and increased mortality. This study evaluated the effectiveness of adipose‐derived stem cells (ASCs) obtained through superficial enhanced fluid fat injection (SEFFI) in improving healing rates after minor amputations in DFUs. It was a prospective, single‐arm, observational cohort study. The patients were monitored for half a year using monthly assessments. The primary endpoint was the healing rate of the amputation stumps after minor amputations coupled with ASC injections. Secondary endpoints included safety, feasibility, adverse events and analysis of variables associated with healing including the analysis of the injected mesenchymal adipose stem cell populations. Of the 256 screened patients, 40 were enrolled. At 6 months, 55% of the treated stumps had healed completely. The median healing time was 69 days. Technical success was achieved in all cases without device‐related complications. Multivariable Cox models identified haemodialysis, opioid use and the injected number of CD45‐positive cells as risk factors for healing failure. CD73 expression was positively associated with healing. The SEFFIDiFA trial demonstrated a promising 69% healing probability at 6 months post‐amputation. This minimally invasive approach showed a higher healing rate with fewer complications. This study supports the potential of ASCs in enhancing wound healing in DFUs and highlights the importance of CD73 expression for successful outcomes. Further research is warranted to validate these findings and optimise the technique.Keywords: amputation stump, diabetes complications, diabetic foot ulcer diabetic, mesenchymal stem cells, wound healing

Summary

This study demonstrated that adipose‐derived stem cells (ASCs) injection through a minimally invasive technique (SEFFI) enhances wound healing rate in diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) with few complications.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are one of the most serious complications of diabetes mellitus, with a 19%–34% risk of occurrence during a patient's lifetime [1, 2]. Minor amputations are the treatment of choice in cases of irreversible DFU involving the toes and the forefoot. However, approximately 50% of minor amputations fail to heal [3], preceding a major amputation, resulting in a negative impact on function, psychological disability, quality of life of patients and increased mortality of up to 50% and 80% at 1 and 5 years post‐amputation, respectively [3, 4, 5].

Advanced therapies are currently being investigated to improve DFU treatment outcomes [6, 7, 8, 9]. Among these, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)‐based therapies have emerged as promising strategies to improve the healing process because of their ability to home towards the wound and release potent bioactive factors that can enhance the wound healing process [10, 11, 12]. Particularly, adipose‐derived stem cells (ASCs) have shown promising results [1, 6, 13]. The MiFrAADiF trial [14, 15] demonstrated a notable improvement (80% vs. 46%, p = 0.006) in the healing rate of DFU minor amputation due to local ASCs injection through micro‐fragmented autologous adipose tissue. However, studies on stem cell‐based therapies in DFU minor amputation settings are limited and further analyses are needed.

Among the most recently developed techniques for minimally invasive adipose tissue harvesting and grafting, SEFFI (superficial enhanced fluid fat injection; SEFFICARE system, SEFFILINE, Bologna, Italy) requires less invasiveness, minimal tissue fragmentation, ASCs cluster size reduction and more rapid procedures aimed at favouring grafting viability and its effects on the receiver site [16].

The impact of ASCs in situ injection through the SEFFICARE system on the healing of minor DFU amputations has never been investigated. The objective of this study was to assess the impact of adipose tissue extracted using the superficial enhanced fluid fat injection technique on the healing process following minor DFU amputation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

SEFFIDiFA (SEFFICARE for Diabetic Foot Amputation) was a prospective, physician‐initiated, single‐arm, observational cohort trial conducted with approval from the Local Ethics Committee (protocol no. 0028541/22; NCT05072353) in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. Written consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All clinical phases of the trial were performed (enrollment, treatment, and clinical assessments) between 1 January 2023, and 29 February 2024, at the Diabetic Foot Service, which is part of the Vascular Surgery Department. An expert Biologist of the Unit of Endocrinology conducted a blinded analysis of the harvested adipose tissue.

Participants and Setting

Enrolling was performed according to the following inclusion criteria: diabetes mellitus (types 1 and 2) of both sexes, age > 18 years, and presence of irreversible digital/forefoot ulcer/gangrene (negative X‐ray for osteolytic lesions). Adequate circulation (perfusion) was assessed using a transcutaneous oxygen test at ≥ 30 mmHg, ankle‐brachial index ≥ 0.7, and pressure index finger/arm toe/brachial index ≥ 0.6. Patients who had undergone previous oncological treatments (past 5 years), had ongoing neoplastic lesions, received corticosteroid therapy, or had active vascular issues or inadequate lower‐extremity perfusion were excluded.

Enrollment and clinical assessments were performed by at least two investigators. Eligibility, according to the inclusion criteria, was evaluated in either the outpatient or in‐hospital setting, and was always performed before surgery, representing the study baseline. All patients were clinically assessed within 20 days of amputation and monthly for 6 months thereafter. During each visit, the status of the amputation stump and perfusion were assessed. All visits were performed by two investigators who simultaneously gathered data on a case report form. Patients who deviated from the scheduled visits received phone calls and new appointments within 1 week. If further clinical assessment had been discontinued, the patient would have been lost to follow‐up.

Endpoints and Definitions

Minor amputations included digital amputations (DAs), either toe or transphalangeal amputations, and transmetatarsal amputations (TMAs), also known as fore‐ or mid‐foot amputations.

The primary endpoint was the healing rate of minor amputation stumps treated with local injection of autologous enhanced fat tissue. Healing was defined as complete re‐epithelialization of the stump after suture removal, without signs of dehiscence or infection. These status was considered as failure either in case of foot reoperation or non‐healing amputation at the end of the follow‐up period (Figure 1) [14].

Surgical Procedure and Investigational Device

The enrolled patients were treated with DA or TMA amputation and local injection of autologous enhanced fat tissue, as described below. The care of all patients and amputations was performed according to the best clinical practice [5]. All procedures were performed by a single vascular surgeon who performed foot surgery in a surgical theatre under local or locoregional anaesthesia.

Investigational treatment was provided during the amputation surgical session, according to the SEFFI technique. The SEFFICARE System (SEFFILINE) is a commercially available disposable device. The set contained infiltration and harvesting cannula multiple syringe decanting stand, syringe VacLok 10 mL, 10 and 3 mL luer lock syringes, plugs for luer lock syringe, connectors for luer lock syringes cylindrical container, needles with different calibres and lengths, gauze swabs, drape and drape with openings.

SEFFI is a 5‐step technique meaning preparation, anaesthesia, harvesting, washing and fluidification (Figure 2).

The resulting tissue (2.5 mL per syringe) was used as a fatty graft. A relatively small amount of this sample (0.5 mL) was collected in a 1.5 mL tube for laboratory analysis.

According to the dimension of the amputation stump, from two to three syringes containing fatty tissue were injected. 4–5 mL for single toe amputations were used, whereas 7–8 mL were for multiple digits and forefoot amputations.

The medication was administered after cleaning with sodium hypochlorite and saline solution, using paraffin gauze with a povidone‐iodine solution (10% iodine). Compressive medication was applied at the fat‐harvesting site for 48 h.

Characterisation of the Cell Population

The number of ASCs in the lipoaspirate samples from patients was determined by evaluating the expression levels of mesenchymal and adipose stem cell surface markers CD73, CD90, CD105, CD36 and CD45 [17, 18]. The expression levels were normalised to the total number of cells. Gene expression and cell count analyses were performed using the QX200 Droplet Digital PCR (ddPCR) System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA), as previously described [19]. Briefly, 0.5 mL of solution embedding cells were collected and total RNA and genomic DNA were extracted from 100 mg of each sample using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and quantified using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA).

To assess the gene expression levels of the selected ASCs positive and negative markers, 300 ng of total RNA was used for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis using a high‐capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The expression of target genes was evaluated by ddPCR, using the following TaqMan‐based assays, all purchased from Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.: ddPCR Gene Expression Assay dHsaCPE5058548 for NT5E gene (CD73 protein target), dHsaCPE5029975 for THY1 gene (CD90 target), dHsaCPE5049500 for ENG gene (CD105), dHsaCPE5036760 for CD36 gene (CD36 target), dHsaCPE5031811 for PTPRC (CD45 target), and dHsaCPE5050189 for GUSB gene (housekeeping gene) [20]. ddPCR was performed using a QX200 Droplet Digital PCR (ddPCR) System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.). For each TaqMan assay, 20 μL of the mixture was made up with 6 μL of RNase‐free water, 10 μL of ddPCR Supermix for Probes (#1863023, Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.), 1 μL of the TaqMan assay, and 10 ng of cDNA.

To assess the total number of cells, 10 ng of genomic DNA were analysed using AMELY/AMELX TaqMan Assay (#C_990000001, Thermo Fisher Scientific), which was developed to count the number of these two Y‐ and X‐chromosome‐specific genes [21]. Briefly, 20 μL of the PCR mixture was made up with 7 μL of RNase‐free water, 10 μL of ddPCR Supermix for Probes (#1863023, Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.), 1 μL of the TaqMan assay and 10 ng of cDNA.

For both analyses, droplets were generated using a QX200 Droplet Generator (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.). After PCR amplifications, positive droplets were determined using the QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.) and analysed by QuantaSoft (v.1.7.4) software (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.), as previously described [22].

Statistical Methods

The sample size was calculated considering the proportion of healing stumps as the primary endpoint. The sample size was calculated with a Score Z‐test comparing one proportion based on the literature results to a reference value [14]. Using a significance level of 0.05, a power of 0.8, an H0 proportion of 0.80 and an Ha of 0.6, 36 subjects were included. An additional 10% of the participants were considered, taking into account the eventual dropouts during the study. The final sample size was adjusted to include only 40 patients.

Categorical variables were expressed as absolute and relative frequencies and percentages. The mean and standard deviation were used to describe continuous variants with a normal distribution. Skewed variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges. Healing over time and freedom from dehiscence estimation were based on the cumulative incidence function according to the Kaplan–Meier method, where death or healing failure was censored at the event time point. Between‐group comparisons of continuous and categorical data were performed using the t‐test (or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test) and the chi‐square test, respectively. A univariable Cox regression model tested hypothetical associations between variables and healing failure, and those presenting a p < 0.2 or high clinical relevance were included in a multivariable model. Goodness of fit was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and minimum likelihood ratio. All tests were two‐sided with a 95% confidence interval (CI), and p < 0.05 was considered the threshold for significance. All analyses were performed using Stata 18 (StataCorp College Station, Texas, USA).

At 6 months, 55% (n = 22/40) of the feet treated with superficial enhanced fluid fat injection healed, 35% (n = 14/40) failed because of stump dehiscence, and the remaining 10% failed to achieve complete healing at the end of follow‐up. Kaplan–Meier cumulative function analysis showed healing fractions of 8% (95 CI 2.5–21.6), 31% (95 CI 19.2–48.7), 44% (29.3–61.4), 69% (95 CI 50.4–84.2), and 74% (95 CI 55.6–89.8) at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 months, respectively (Figure 4). In contrast, the rates of freedom from amputation‐related reintervention were 98% (95 CI 73.5–99.6), 95% (95 CI 81.4–98.7), 90% (95 CI 75.3–96.1), 82% (95 CI 65.6–90.9), 79% (95 CI 62.5–0.89) and 76% (95 CI 44.2–78.1), respectively (Figure 5). The median time for healing was 69.5 days [IQR 51.5–106.5].

In all cases, no technical issues precluded the completion of the procedure, accounting for 100% of the feasibility of the procedure. The mean time required for the SEFFI procedure was 36 ± 7 min, and the mean volume of injected purified tissue was 6 ± 4 mL. The safety of the procedure was recorded for all patients, without any adverse events.

Discussion

The SEFFIDiFA trial demonstrated a 69% healing probability at 6 months post‐amputation, indicating a favourable outcome considering that the success rate for TMA is less than 50% [23]. Furthermore, a recent meta‐analysis reported TMA re‐amputation rates greater than 55% [3]. The previously mentioned MiFrAADiF randomised trial demonstrated a significantly higher healing rate in a cohort of patients receiving micro‐fragmented autologous adipose tissue grafting of the stump (80% vs. 46%, p = 0.006) [14, 15]. The present results suggest that the beneficial potential of ASCs is guaranteed by the SEFFI technique in the same setting as the MiFrAADiFtrial [14]. The latter reported two procedure‐related complications (hematoma of the abdominal wall). The present study demonstrated 100% technical success without any device‐related adverse events, highlighting the safety and feasibility of the SEFFICARE device. In the MiFrAADiF trial [14, 15], adipose harvesting was performed using the Lipogems system with a 13‐gauge cannula (approximately 1.8 mm). In the present study, utilising the SEFFICARE technique the adipose tissue was collected using a 0.8 or 0.5 mm cannula. It seems reasonable to assume that the use of a smaller cannula may cause less trauma to the donor site, and consequently, fewer complications. Furthermore, the SEFFICARE cannula is inserted perpendicular to the skin, but its advancement is guided by plastic support, which avoids unintended deep insertion and guides sampling towards the horizontal plane after correct positioning in the subcutaneous tissue. This safety system allows for the collection of adipose tissue by working on a superficial plane, avoiding the risk of going too deep and causing damage and complications at the donor site.

The healing time of the stump is of primary relevance because it has been demonstrated that wound healing within 3 months is associated with a lower risk of major amputation [24]. A recent study demonstrated that the median time to heal after minor amputation in diabetic patients was 87 days (IQR, 54–162 days) [24, 25]. In our study, the mean healing time was 69 days, leading to the hypothesis that the injection of autologous adipose tissue may be associated with positive healing times.

In addition, this study investigated possible risk factors for stump failure to heal. Multivariable Cox models identified haemodialysis, opioid use, and the injected number of CD45 cells as risk factors for healing failure. Recent studies have reported a high prevalence of DFU in patients undergoing dialysis for end‐stage renal disease [26]. Malnutrition is a common issue in haemodialysis patients and a powerful predictor of morbidity and mortality, similar to slow and difficult wound healing [27, 28]. Notably, opioid therapy is a risk factor for dehiscence. It has only recently been reported that neurons of the peripheral nervous system play an important role in wound healing, and some studies have shown that opioids and opioid receptors (OPr) are involved in various phases of wound healing [29]. High levels of beta‐endorphin can lead to the downregulation of OPr expression [30, 31]. In chronic wounds, β‐endorphin levels are markedly increased, resulting in the downregulation of OPr [31, 32]. In chronic wounds, endogenous ligands are produced in high quantities by OPr‐downregulated immune cells. The latter are also targets of exogenous opioids, often taken up by patients with chronic wounds to control pain. It seems reasonable that taking opioid drugs can contribute to constantly increasing the total level of opioid agonists, which can interfere with wound healing.

In cell population analysis, we found that CD73 expression was positively associated with healing (Table 3), suggesting it is a protective factor for re‐intervention, as well as CD105. These data support the presence of ASCs in lipoaspirate and are in line with a recent study that showed that CD73+ ASCs have better therapeutic effects on wound healing [33]. CD45 is a marker of leukocytes involved in activation and regulation of the immune system [34]. The association between CD45 expression and healing failure led us to hypothesise that the proportion of ASCs, rather than other cells, provides a therapeutic action. These findings underscore the importance of patient‐specific characteristics that, once addressed through personalised treatment strategies, may improve healing rates and enhance patient care.

The limitations of the present study include its single‐cohort observational design, which limits its ability to establish causal relationships. The small sample size may have constrained the generalizability of the findings, even though it was powered by relying on the literature data. Moreover, exploring the mechanisms underlying the observed associations between risk factors and healing outcomes will deepen our understanding of DFU pathophysiology and inform targeted intervention.

The potential role of ASCs in other fields of vascular surgery, such as the improvement of the healing of wound dehiscence and/or calcaneal ulcers, should be investigated in future studies.

Conclusion

The SEFFIDiFA trial demonstrated the healing benefits of utilising adipose‐derived stem cells harvested through the superficial enhanced fluid fat injection technique to enhance healing outcomes following minor amputations for DFU. The SEFFI technique is safe, feasible, and minimally invasive, without any observed adverse events, indicating its potential as a viable technique for ASC delivery in DFU management. Several risk factors for healing failure have been identified, and the cell composition may play a primary role in treatment efficacy. Further research is warranted to refine dosing regimens and injection techniques, and to expand patient selection to maximise therapeutic efficacy.

Ethics Statement

SEFFIDiFA (SEFFICARE for Diabetic Foot Amputation) was a prospective, physician‐initiated, single‐arm, observational cohort trial conducted with approval from the Local Ethics Committee (protocol no. 0028541/22; NCT05072353) in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. Written consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.
