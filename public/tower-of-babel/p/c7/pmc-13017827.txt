Time-restricted eating improves intermittent hypoxia-induced dysglycemia
Authors: Chloe P Afif, Katherine P Brewster, Michelle P Zhang, Jennifer Reeves, Megan T Hackbarth, Charlotte Cong, Kristine Olvera, Esteban A Moya, Atul Malhotra, Omar A Mesarwi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy objectives:Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is associated with maladaptive changes to glucose homeostasis. Chronic intermittent hypoxia (IH) modeling OSA in rodents causes similar patterns of dysglycemia. Time-restricted eating (TRE), limiting caloric intake to a fixed daily window, improves the metabolic profile in rodents and humans, though it is unknown whether TRE improves IH-induced dysglycemia in an obese OSA model.Methods:C57BL/6J mice were fed a high-fat diet for 4 weeks, and then divided into four groups: Half in intermittent air (IA) or IH, and half from each group fed either ad libitum or by TRE, restricting high-fat diet intake to 9 h/day during the dark phase; exposures continued for four additional weeks. Glucose tolerance tests were performed before and after exposures.Results:Fasting glucose was reduced in IH-TRE (−25.4 ± 5.6 mg/dL, p < .001) but not in other groups. Glucose tolerance test area under the curve was only increased in the IA-ad lib (3155 ± 924 mg*min/dL, p = .002) and IH-ad lib groups (5267 ± 1161 mg*min/dL, p < .001), but TRE prevented these increases. Insulin was elevated in IA-ad lib relative to other groups (p < .010 for all), implying insufficient insulin response to hyperglycemia in IH-ad lib. Serum proinsulin, and the proinsulin-to-insulin ratio, were elevated in IH-ad lib mice; this was normalized with TRE, along with favorable changes to pancreatic glucokinase, prohormone convertase 1/3, and miR-152–3p.Conclusions:In IH, TRE mitigates adverse hypoxic effects on glucose homeostasis, via improvements in pancreatic insulin secretion. Some beneficial glycemic effects of TRE are accentuated in IH. TRE may represent a novel therapeutic strategy in OSA.Keywords: metabolic syndrome, intermittent fasting, hypoxia, sleep disordered breathing, proinsulin

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a highly prevalent respiratory disorder, affecting nearly 1 billion adults worldwide [1, 2]. OSA is a metabolically vulnerable state; associative and longitudinal data link OSA to the development of insulin resistance, fasting hyperglycemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease, and dyslipidemia [3–7]. Nasal continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is the first-line treatment of OSA and reliably reduces OSA severity [8, 9]. However, there are mixed data regarding the impact of CPAP on glucose homeostasis in OSA. Some studies have demonstrated no effect of CPAP to improve glycated hemoglobin [10, 11], and while meta-analyses have shown some improvements in glycemic indices and/or visceral fat with CPAP, the effect is small and inconsistent [12–15]. Perfect use of CPAP does appear to improve insulin sensitivity [16, 17], but is infrequently achieved in clinical practice. Chronic intermittent hypoxia (IH) is a common experimental tool used to model OSA in animals [18]. IH involves the use of cyclic reductions in the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) during the sleep period, causing similar oxyhemoglobin desaturations as observed in patients with OSA. IH reliably reproduces many metabolic effects of OSA, including glucose intolerance and fasting hyperglycemia, hepatic steatosis, and dyslipidemia [18–22].

Time-restricted eating (TRE) is a practice of caloric intake characterized by the reduction of the daily eating window to a specified hourly limit, usually <10 h [23]. TRE has been the subject of considerable recent research, and studies have demonstrated that TRE is feasible, simple, safe, and supported by high patient adherence [24]. TRE typically causes weight reduction, and reduction in overall body fat [25–27]. In metabolically susceptible populations (e.g. those with baseline dysfunction in glucose homeostasis), TRE appears to reduce fasting and postprandial glucose and to improve insulin sensitivity and pancreatic beta cell function [28–32]. TRE in rodents, even when isocaloric relative to control-fed rodents, similarly improves glucose tolerance, and prevents obesity and hepatic steatosis [33–36].

Based on our observations about these dissimilar effects of IH and TRE, and the need to find novel approaches to address metabolic health in patients with OSA, we aimed to determine whether TRE would improve glycemic indices (fasting glucose and glucose tolerance) in obese mice exposed to IH, modeling OSA in obese humans. We hypothesized that TRE would reduce the severity of IH-induced dysglycemia, due to improvements in insulin sensitivity and driven primarily by reductions in weight and body fat.

Materials and Methods

Mice

64 C57BL/6J male mice aged 8 weeks were used and were housed four per cage. Mice were exposed to 12-h light cycles (Zeitgeber time [ZT] 0–12, corresponding to 7:00 am to 7:00 pm). At the time of euthanasia, liver, epididymal fat, pancreas, and serum were collected for further analysis. Animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of California, San Diego.

Exposures

Mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD, #D12451, 45 per cent kcal% fat, Research Diets, Inc., New Brunswick, NJ) for 4 weeks to induce mild obesity. The mice were then divided into four groups, n = 16/group: Group 1 was exposed to intermittent air (IA) and had ad libitum access to food (the same HFD was used for all groups). Group 2 was also exposed to IA but had food intake restricted to 9 h per day during the active (dark) phase, from ZT 14–23 (TRE). Group 3 was exposed to IH (FiO2 cycling between 0.21 and 0.06 once per minute for 12 h during ZT 0–12, and FiO2 0.21 during ZT 12–24) and was fed ad libitum. Group 4 was exposed to IH and was fed with the TRE paradigm as above. IA exposure was done with a similar gas flow as in IH, but with normoxic gas throughout. These exposures continued for an additional 4 weeks. Body weight and food intake were recorded nightly at ZT 14. Cages were changed each morning at ZT 23 for all animals, to ensure that any residual food left in the cage or bedding material was removed. Early during these exposures, one mouse in the IH-TRE group was found dead for unclear reasons, thought to be unrelated to the experimental design.

Glucose tolerance tests

On the day before the above exposures, and again following 4 weeks of exposure, daytime intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests (GTTs) were performed. Mice were fasted for 5 h beginning at ZT 0.5, and then a basal glucose level was obtained via the tail scratch technique. Mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 1 g/kg glucose. Levels of glucose in the blood were then monitored at 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min after injection. For analysis, the fasting glucose levels were subtracted and the incremental area under the curve (iAUC) was calculated.

Biochemical assays and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Fresh liver and pancreas samples were collected at euthanasia from a subset of mice (n = 8/group) and were stored in −80°C after being flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Liver samples were homogenized, and liver malondialdehyde (MDA) was measured (QuantiChrom TBARS Assay, BioAssay Systems, Hayward, CA). Insulin, proinsulin, glucagon, leptin, and ghrelin were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay in serum samples from all mice (for ghrelin: Neta Scientific, Hainesport, NJ; for others: Crystal Chem, Inc., Elk Grove Village, IL). Low- and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol components (LDL-C and HDL-C) were measured in serum by biochemical assay (Crystal Chem).

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) was used to measure body composition in half the mice from each group (n = 8/group, same subset as described above, PIXImus2 Lunar, Madison, WI), 1–2 days before euthanasia. Mice were weighed before scanning, and each mouse was anesthetized with isoflurane for the duration of the procedure. During the scan, mice were positioned prone, and a single scan was conducted for each mouse. The resulting data were analyzed using PIXImus software (2.10; GE/Lunar). To ensure accuracy, the head was manually excluded from these calculations. The scanner was calibrated on each testing day with an aluminum/lucite phantom, with known properties, following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Pancreatic histology

A small piece of the pancreatic head from a subset of mice (n = 8/group, same subset as described above) was formalin-fixed for 24 h and then sectioned in 5 μm slices. Immunohistochemistry was performed for caspase-3 (rabbit monoclonal, #9662, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) and insulin (rabbit monoclonal, #C27C9, Cell Signaling). Pancreatic β-cell mass was calculated based on the positive insulin staining in 4× slides, divided by total area of pancreas tissue. Samples were quantified in each slide in a de-identified manner by three individuals (C.P.A., K.P.B., O.A.M.) and these quantifications were averaged.

MicroRNA isolation and quantification

Total microRNA (miRNA) was isolated from flash-frozen pancreas samples using a miRNeasy Micro kit (#217084, QIAGEN, Redwood City, CA) according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. cDNA was synthesized using a TaqMan Advanced miRNA cDNA Synthesis Kit (#A28007, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). miR-152–3p was quantitated using quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) using a Taqman advanced assay (mmu482952_mir, #A25576, Thermo Fisher).

Immunofluorescence

Pancreas tissue was used to generate immunofluorescence images based on insulin (rabbit monoclonal, 1:400, #C27C9, Cell Signaling), pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDHA1, rabbit monoclonal, 1:400, #C54G1, Cell Signaling), prohormone convertase 1/3 (PC1/3, rabbit monoclonal, 1:100, #EPR21908, Abcam Inc., Waltham, MA), and glucokinase (GCK, rabbit polyclonal, 1:200, #PA5–15072, Thermo Fisher). QuPath v0.5.1 was used for image analysis [37], which was done in a blinded manner. The five largest pancreatic islets were identified as regions of interest in each sample and were carefully circumscribed. Mean staining intensity was calculated for each fluorescent label in each region of interest, with a pixel size of 0.1 μm. Values from the five islets were averaged per sample (i.e. each mouse pancreas sample was an n = 1 in the presented data).

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons between groups of mice for data resulting in single-point measurements were performed by using two-way ANOVA. Independent variables were the diet (TRE vs. ad libitum) and exposure (IH vs. IA), and the effects of each independent variable and the interactive effect were compared for each outcome of interest. For data analyzed by two-way ANOVA, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used for post-hoc analysis of between-group differences. Longitudinal data (e.g. GTT) were analyzed by multiple t-tests using the Holm-Sidak method, to understand the effect of time as a mediator of any differences observed, in each group separately; these t-tests were adjusted appropriately for multiple comparisons, with total alpha 0.050. For all statistical comparisons, a p value of <.050 was the threshold used for statistical significance. Data are reported as the mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted. Prism 10.4 software (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA) was used for all analyses.

Results

Food intake and body composition

Body weight changes over the experiment duration are shown in Figure 1A. TRE (p < .0001) and IH (p < .0001) both caused a reduction in body weight, but no interactive effect was observed between these variables. Post-hoc analysis showed that TRE significantly reduced body weight in IA-exposed animals (IA-ad lib: +5.2 ± 0.5 g, IA-TRE: +1.7 ± 0.6 g, p < .001), but not in IH-exposed animals (IH-ad lib: −0.7 ± 0.5 g, IH-TRE: −2.5 ± 0.6 g, p = .133). Both IH (p = .043) and TRE (p = .021) caused mildly reduced food intake (Figure 1B), but no effect of TRE specifically in IH or IA alone based on post-hoc analysis. IH caused a reduction in liver weight (p < .0001), but there was no TRE effect (Figure 1C). By contrast, IH (p < .0001) and TRE (p < .001) both reduced epididymal fat weight (Figure 1D), mirroring effects on total body fat based on DEXA (p = .028 for IH effect, p < .001 for TRE effect; Figure 1E), with significant effects of TRE to reduce body weight in IA and IH groups separately, based on post-hoc analysis.

Glycemic effects of TRE

GTTs pre- and post-exposure are shown in Figure 2A. From these were derived fasting glucose data pre- and post-exposure, as the time zero glucose value from each GTT (Figure 2B). All groups of mice were hyperglycemic at baseline, as they had been fed a HFD for 4 weeks prior to exposure. Fasting glucose was reduced over time in the IH-TRE group (153.9 ± 5.2 mg/dL pre-exposure to 128.5 ± 2.0 mg/dL post-exposure, p < .001), but was not changed in any other group. As expected, GTT iAUC increased in mice fed ad libitum, in both IH and IA (Figure 2C), as mice were continued on a HFD throughout the exposures, with a larger magnitude of effect in IH (IA-ad lib: 12195 ± 606 mg*min/dL to 15 350 ± 698 mg*min/dL, p = .002; IH-ad lib: 12 013 ± 639 mg*min/dL to 17 281 ± 970 mg*min/dL, p < .0001). However, TRE effectively prevented any increase in GTT iAUC in both IH and IA (IA-TRE: 12 058 ± 646 mg*min/dL to 10 945 ± 901 mg*min/dL, p = .323; IH-TRE: 11 360 ± 460 mg*min/dL to 10 094 ± 599 mg*min/dL, p = .098).

Fasting glucose was reduced over time only in IH-TRE-exposed animals. (C) Glucose tolerance test iAUC increased over time in ad lib-fed animals. (D) Fasting insulin levels, and (E) HOMA-IR indices near the time of euthanasia. IH was associated with a reduction in fasting insulin relative to IA (p < .0001). (F) Serum glucagon levels near the time of euthanasia were not different between groups. IH, intermittent hypoxia; TRE, time-restricted eating; iAUC, incremental area under the curve; HOMA-IR, homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance; IA, intermittent air. **, p < .010; ***, p < .001; ****, p < .0001.

Fasting insulin at the time of euthanasia was reduced in IH (p < .0001) and by TRE (p = .012), with interaction between these variables (p = .040). Based on post-hoc analysis, insulin was reduced by TRE only in IA-exposed mice (p = .008), without any effect of TRE in IH (Figure 2D). However, insulin levels in IH-ad lib exposed mice were unexpectedly low, as were measures of HOMA-IR, a metric of insulin resistance based on fasting glucose and insulin levels (Figure 2E). Fasting glucagon levels were not different between groups, without significant effects of TRE or IH (Figure 2F). These data suggest that IH in our experiment may cause a failure of pancreatic β-cell insulin secretion, or excess insulin clearance, and that the mechanism of TRE improvements in glucose homeostasis in IH may uniquely derive from improved pancreatic endocrine function.

Pancreatic β-cell mass, serum proinsulin and leptin, and hepatic oxidative stress

To investigate these mechanisms further, we first quantified pancreatic β-cell mass to determine whether TRE improvements in fasting glucose and glucose tolerance in IH were associated with increased β-cell mass, and whether IH caused any reduction in β-cell mass to account for low serum insulin levels. TRE reduced pancreatic β-cell mass (p = .039 for diet effect by two-way ANOVA), but no hypoxic effect was observed (Figure 3A). The effect was consistent between IA and IH groups. IH has been demonstrated previously to induce pancreatic apoptosis [38], but we saw no caspase-3 staining in pancreatic islets from any group. Serum proinsulin levels (Figure 3B) were unexpectedly elevated in IH-ad lib exposed mice, and serum proinsulin-to-insulin ratio was higher in this group than all other groups (IA-ad lib: 1.49 ± 0.24, IA-TRE: 2.66 ± 0.40, IH-ad lib: 4.36 ± 0.49, IH-TRE: 1.41 ± 0.25; p = .031 for IH effect and p = .017 for TRE effect; p < .010 for each group compared with IH-ad lib but no other group comparisons were significant to p < .050; Figure 3C). Both IH and TRE reduced serum leptin levels (p < .0001 for each effect by two-way ANOVA; Figure 3D). No effects of IH or TRE were seen on serum ghrelin levels (Figure 3E). Liver malondialdehyde, a highly mutagenic lipid peroxidation product and marker of tissue redox burden, was elevated in IH (p < .0001; Figure 3F), consistent with earlier studies [21, 39, 40]. Based on post-hoc analysis, TRE reduced liver MDA in IA (p = .019) but no TRE effect was observed in IH (p = 1.000).

(B) Proinsulin levels remained high in IH-ad lib-fed mice despite low insulin levels, leading to (C) an elevation in the proinsulin-to-insulin ratio. (D and E) Leptin and ghrelin in serum samples. Leptin was reduced by IH and by TRE (p < .0001 for each), but ghrelin was unchanged by these exposures. (F) Hepatic malondialdehyde (MDA) was highly elevated in IH (p < .0001), and slightly reduced by TRE (p = .029), with a weak interactive effect (p = .040). Mechanisms further explaining glycemic effects of TRE in IH

To understand how TRE might improve the proinsulin:insulin ratio in IH, we noted that alterations to this metric have been associated with various miRNA, including miR-152 and miR-130a and miR-130b [41]. Of these, miR-152–3p has been shown to be elevated in patients with OSA [42] and is highly overexpressed in response to hypoxia in various cell lines [43, 44]. In addition, miR-152–3p is overexpressed in pancreatic islets from rodent diabetes models and in hyperinsulinemic human donors [41, 45, 46]. Thus, we quantified pancreatic miR-152–3p in whole pancreas and found that this miRNA was reduced by TRE by two-way ANOVA (p = .006). There was no significant hypoxic effect (p = .062), but a significant interactive effect (p = .034), and post-hoc comparisons showed miR-152–3p to be increased in IH-ad lib mice relative to IA-ad lib, and was normalized in TRE in IH (Figure 4A). GCK, which serves as a glucose sensor in the pancreatic islet and is a known downregulatory target of miR-152–3p [41], was increased by TRE in IH-exposed mice (Figure 4B), as was prohormone convertase 1/3 (PC1/3), an enzyme which converts proinsulin to insulin. No changes to pyruvate dehydrogenase α1 (PDHA1), another downstream target of miR-152–3p [41], were observed between groups.

Mechanisms of change to pancreatic proinsulin-to-insulin ratio in IH. (A) miR-152–3p was elevated in IH-ad lib-fed mice relative to other groups. (B) TRE increased glucokinase (GCK), a miR-152–3p target, in pancreatic islets, in IH but not in IA. Prohormone convertase 1/3 (PC1/3) was increased by TRE in IH. No change to islet pyruvate dehydrogenase alpha-1 (PDHA1) was observed. One representative islet from the lower of the median samples from each group are shown in each immunofluorescence figure. IH, intermittent hypoxia; TRE, time-restricted eating. *, p < .050; **, p < .010; ****, p < .0001.

Effects of TRE and IH on serum cholesterol subtypes and hepatic lipids

TRE (p = .002) caused a reduction in serum LDL-C, and IH (p = .014) was associated with an increase in LDL-C (Figure 5A). Post-hoc analysis suggested that TRE improved LDL-C only in IH, and not in IA-exposed animals; in IH, TRE reduced LDL-C by 40 per cent. TRE was associated with a 2.6-fold increase in serum HDL-C in IA-exposed mice (p < .001; Figure 5B), but this effect was not observed in IH (p = .714). IH mildly reduced liver lipid content (p = .036, Figure 5C), but interestingly, TRE had the opposite effect, significantly increasing liver lipids (p < .001). These data suggest that TRE may enhance serum lipoprotein clearance, although this possibility was not tested directly.

Discussion

TRE has been shown in animal and human trials to improve metabolic health (e.g. better glycemic control, lower atherogenic lipids). OSA in humans, and IH in rodents, have been shown to induce a wide variety of metabolic dysfunction. In our experiments, we have demonstrated that TRE can effectively improve fasting glucose and glucose tolerance in obese mice exposed to IH, with some suggestion that the effects of TRE in IH are stronger than in IA-exposed mice (e.g. a TRE-induced improvement in fasting glucose unobserved in IA). Further, we noted that the improvement in glucose homeostasis induced by TRE in IH is associated with an improved (i.e. lower) proinsulin-to-insulin ratio as well as an increase in the proinsulin-to-insulin converting enzyme, and an increase in GCK, corresponding lower pancreatic levels of miR-152–3p in TRE. Finally, TRE reduces serum LDL-C in IH, but increases hepatic steatosis. Taken together, our results suggest that (1) TRE may improve the metabolic profile in IH via unique mechanisms (i.e. improving insulin synthesis and release, rather than changes to insulin sensitivity), and (2) TRE beneficial glycemic effects in IH may occur both by improved pancreatic insulin sensing and proinsulin conversion.

A common response to a body of data suggesting beneficial effects of TRE, whether in humans or animals, is that most such effects are carried forth due to improvements in body weight, particularly in overweight or obesity. Our data strongly suggest that in this OSA model, TRE effects are not driven primarily by reductions in body weight. In IH, we observed a smaller effect of TRE to reduce body weight than in IA, an effect that did not reach statistical significance in IH (but did in IA). By contrast, the effects of TRE on glucose tolerance, fasting glucose, and LDL-C were similar in some cases (glucose tolerance), and more profound in others (fasting glucose, LDL-C), in IH than in IA. DEXA analysis revealed that changes to fat composition were generally reflective of overall patterns of body weight. In addition, changes to body weight over the experiment duration appeared to follow patterns of food intake (i.e. food intake was generally lower in TRE), with the limitation that food intake was measured in group-housed animals, and statistical comparisons were not performed on a per-mouse basis. In sum, although TRE reduces body weight, the effect is less profound in IH than in IA, despite a stronger beneficial glycemic effect. These data provide the first hint that IH is a unique condition which may be distinctively impacted by TRE.

Although type 2 diabetes is conventionally described as a failure of peripheral insulin sensitivity leading to serum hyperglycemia, the critical role of pancreatic β-cell dysfunction in the pathophysiology of type 2 diabetes has recently been the subject of considerable focus [47]. β-Cell failure, including the failure of appropriate proinsulin-to-insulin conversion and insulin exocytosis, are strongly implicated in patients with type 2 diabetes [48]. Moreover, the proinsulin:insulin ratio has been shown in some studies to be a marker of incident diabetes and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction [49–51]. In our model of the hypoxemia of obese OSA, IH is a state of failed insulin response to hyperglycemia, characterized by an elevation in the proinsulin:insulin ratio. This paradigm has been demonstrated previously; Wang et al. also showed that IH causes pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, elevated proinsulin, and reduced insulin secretion. We have now shown that hyperglycemia in IH, and the proinsulin:insulin ratio, are both improved by TRE. Moreover, these observations about TRE effects on the proinsulin:insulin ratio are not demonstrated in IA-exposed mice, lending credence to a conceptual framework that IH, and perhaps OSA, are unique states amenable to metabolic improvement with TRE. In addition to these findings, we have shown that these TRE effects are accompanied by favorable changes in miR-152–3p, GCK, and PC1/3. Previous work has shown that GCK is likely a direct translational target of miR-152–3p [41], and our data suggest that multiple mechanisms may be responsible for beneficial TRE effects on glucose homeostasis. Finally, leptin was reduced by TRE in our experiment, consistent with other studies [34, 52], suggesting a complex interaction between hunger/satiety, glycemic control, and weight which merits further attention. IH in our experiment also reduced leptin, albeit to a smaller degree; there are mixed data about IH effects on serum leptin [53–55], and these effects are likely highly sensitive to the experimental design.

There are a few surprising findings from our data: First, among ad lib-fed animals, insulin sensitivity may have been improved in IH, relative to IA. Whether this is due to weight effects of IH compensating for adverse metabolic effects, or other reasons, is unclear. Second, TRE induced reductions in pancreatic β-cell mass. Given that TRE was also broadly associated with improved glucose homeostasis, it is difficult to see this finding as harmful, but it merits further exploration. Finally, TRE was associated with increased hepatic steatosis, despite lower circulating LDL-C. This finding of increased steatosis in TRE is notably discordant with some previous data [33]. The significance of the balance of these effects is unclear and also warrants additional study.

Our work has a few important limitations. First, direct query of pancreatic β-cell function was not performed. In designing these experiments, we anticipated that TRE would exert any improvements to glucose tolerance primarily through changes to peripheral insulin resistance. Thus, some conclusions about the impact of IH, and TRE, on pancreatic endocrine function derive only from a poor insulin response to hyperglycemia in IH-ad lib-fed mice. However, the clearly deficient insulin response paired with an elevated proinsulin:insulin ratio strongly suggest that IH in our model is a state of β-cell failure of insulin secretion. Second, IH in obese animals is clearly not a perfect encapsulation of the many physiological changes that occur in patients with OSA, but we note that data support the IH model as representative of the hypoxemia of severe human OSA [21], and previous studies have shown that this model successfully reproduces many of the adverse metabolic effects of OSA. Third, we do not provide a lean group as a comparator. This is intentional, as earlier work has shown that TRE has little to no effect in lean mice [33]. Moreover, IH in mice with mild obesity, as in our experiment, may more faithfully represent the hypoxemia of human OSA than IH in lean mice. Finally, our data implicating miR-152–3p, GCK, and PC1/3 as mediators of TRE effects are not fully mechanistic, though future experiments are intended to query effects of these targets directly.

In conclusion, TRE abrogates maladaptive effects of IH on glucose homeostasis in mice with diet-induced obesity and improves serum LDL-C. These effects are not driven by changes to body weight, and they are accompanied by beneficial changes to the proinsulin:insulin ratio, and improvements in pancreatic miR-152–3p and pancreatic islet GCK and PC1/3. We surmise that TRE may have utility in the treatment of metabolic dysfunction in patients with OSA, and our concurrent pilot human clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT06047496) is intended to investigate this possibility.

Statement of Significance

Obstructive sleep apnea is associated with metabolic dysfunction, including adverse changes to glucose and cholesterol homeostasis. Chronic intermittent hypoxia (IH) modeling OSA in animals reproduces many of these effects. Time-restricted eating (TRE), in which the window for caloric intake is shortened, improves many aspects of metabolic syndrome in animals and humans. We exposed mice to IH and assessed the effects of TRE, hypothesizing that TRE would improve glucose tolerance. TRE had uniquely advantageous effects in IH, improving both fasting glucose and glucose tolerance, as well as serum LDL cholesterol. We demonstrated that these beneficial TRE effects are associated with improvements in pancreatic islet function and proinsulin-to-insulin conversion, as well as reduction in miR-152–3p.
