The Human Monoacylglycerol Acyltransferase 2 Inhibitor VB‐85387 Is Associated With Weight Loss in Mice With Obesity
Authors: J. Jose Corbalan, Pranavi Jagadeesan, Chia‐Yu Huang, James R. Beasley, Joseph T. Nickels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGLP‐1 therapies for obesity are limited by side effects and weight regain is common after treatment ends. Therefore, alternative treatments with new mechanisms are needed for sustained weight loss. Human MOGAT2 regulates triglyceride metabolism and its inhibition reduces weight in people with obesity, making MOGAT2 a promising target for obesity therapy.ObjectiveThis study evaluated the therapeutic potential of the VB‐85387 MOGAT2 inhibitor for weight loss.MethodsHigh throughput screening was used to identify human MOGAT2 inhibitors. Diet‐induced mice with obesity were treated with VB‐85387 for 7 days. Body weight, food intake, blood and liver lipid and hormone levels were assessed. Glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity were evaluated via oral glucose tolerance and hyperinsulaemic‐euglycaemic clamp tests. Gene expression of PPARα and SREBP target genes was measured using qRT‐PCR.ResultsHigh throughput screening identified VB‐85387 as a highly potent and selective human MOGAT2 inhibitor. Mice with obesity treated with VB‐85387 experienced weight loss, reduced food intake, improved glucose tolerance, and enhanced insulin sensitivity. Treatment decreased liver triglyceride levels and increased GLP‐1 concentrations. β‐hydroxybutyrate levels were elevated, PPARα‐dependent fatty acid β‐oxidation gene expression was increased, and SREBP‐dependent gene expression was reduced.ConclusionVB‐85387 serves as a starting point for medicinal chemistry lead optimization. Weight loss may be attributed to PPARα activation and a sustained elevation in GLP‐1 levels.

Introduction

MOGAT1/2/3 make up the monoacylglycerol acyltransferase family found in humans [1]. They catalyze the conversion of monoacylglycerides and fatty acid acyl‐CoAs to diacylglycerides, which are used by diacylglycerol acyltransferases to produce triacylglycerides. MOGAT2 activity is observed in the intestines and liver [2]. In the intestine, MOGAT2 facilitates the resynthesis of dietary triglycerides for incorporation into chylomicrons and transport through the thoracic duct [1]. Thus, MOGAT2 plays a critical role in triglyceride metabolism in humans.

Mice deficient in Mogat2 demonstrate resistance to diet‐induced obesity and hepatic steatosis [3]. Deleting Mogat in the intestines produces similar phenotypes [3]. Expressing human MOGAT2 in the intestines eliminates these phenotypes, highlighting MOGAT2's essential role in dietary triglyceride metabolism and healthy body weight [4]. Mice treated with MOGAT2 inhibitors show reduced body weight gain [5, 6, 7]. Moreover, the MOGAT2 inhibitor, BMS‐963272, caused weight loss in healthy people with obesity [8]. Thus, human MOGAT2 represents an established target for treating obesity.

Previous studies have shown that the MOGAT2 inhibitor, VB‐87531, worked synergistically with the GLP‐1 receptor agonist semaglutide and the GLP‐1/GIPR dual receptor agonist tirzepatide to enhance weight loss and decrease food intake [9]. Here, the MOGAT2 inhibitor, VB‐85387, was tested for its effects on weight loss and glucose metabolism as a montherapy. Administration of VB‐85387 to diet‐induced mice with obesity resulted in significant weight reduction that correlated with decreased basal glucose levels, increased β‐hydroxybutyrate concentrations, and reduced blood non‐esterified fatty acid levels. Mice treated with VB‐85387 retained insulin sensitivity under HFD conditions. Hepatic triglyceride content was diminished, and hepatotoxicity was not observed following drug treatment. In treated mice, insulin, leptin, and total ghrelin levels decreased while total GLP‐1 levels increased. PPARα signaling may be induced in mice treated with VB‐85387. Therefore, VB‐85387 may induce weight loss by enhancing satiety and energy expenditure, potentially via increased β‐oxidation, and may augment the weight‐reducing effects associated with elevated GLP‐1 levels. Overall, VB‐85387 showed promise as a lead small molecule inhibitor of MOGAT2 and could serve as a candidate for further medicinal chemistry optimization to develop a potent and selective pre‐clinical compound.

Materials and Methods

Miscellaneous Reagents

Silica gel TLC plates were obtained from Millipore Sigma (Burlington, MA). For HTS and IC50 assays, 2‐oleoylglycerol (Cayman Chemical), oleoyl‐CoA, and CPM were sourced from Millipore Sigma. NBD‐pamitoyl‐CoA and NBD‐oleoyl‐CoA (for TLC) were from Avanti Lipids. General reagents also came from Millipore Sigma, organic solvents from ThermoFisher Scientific, and BMS‐963272 was synthesized by Venenum Biodesign LLC.

High Throughput Thiol‐Fluorescence Assay

The activity of the hMOGAT2 enzyme was measured using a thiol‐fluorescence assay similar to that described [10]. Briefly, MOGAT2 transfers a fatty acid from acyl‐CoA to monoacylglycerol, which produces diacylglycerol and releases coenzyme‐A (CoA). The free sulfhydryl group on CoA reacts with 7‐diethylamino‐3‐(4‐maleimidophenyl)‐4‐methylcoumarin (CPM) to form a fluorescent compound, generating a signal directly proportional to the amount of CoA produced in the reaction.

The high‐throughput screen (HTS) for hMOGAT2 inhibitors was carried out in black 1536‐well plates with an assay volume of 6 μL. Substrate solution (3 μL)—containing 200 μM oleoyl‐CoA and 200 μM 2‐oleoylglycerol in buffer (100 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween‐20, 0.05% BGG)—was added to plates previously prepared with dried library compounds (up to 25 compounds/well), followed by 15 μg/mL human MOGAT2 Sf9 microsomes in 3 μL buffer. After 60 min of incubation, reactions were quenched with 400 μM CPM in 2 μL of 50% ethanol containing the MOGAT2 inhibitor Compound A [11], bringing the final well volume to 8 μL. Plates were protected from light and incubated for another 90 min. Fluorescence was then measured using an EnVision plate reader (Perkin Elmer) with excitation/emission settings of 355/490 nm. Percent inhibition in each well was calculated relative to control wells without inhibitors.

To confirm the inhibitory activity, individual compounds were tested per well using the same assay setup. Confirmed hits underwent further counter screening for assay interference using a CoA‐CPM assay as outlined [12] with compound 2b serving as a positive control for interference.

Compound IC50 values were determined in 384‐well plates with a total assay volume of 15 μL. Human MOGAT2 Sf9 microsomes (7.5 μg/mL) were combined with 100 μM oleoyl‐CoA and 100 μM 2‐oleoylglycerol in buffer, along with a duplicate 11‐point, 2‐fold serial dilution of the test compound. After a 45‐min incubation, reactions were stopped as before with 5 μL of CPM/Compound A, resulting in a final well volume of 20 μL, followed by a 90‐min incubation before measuring fluorescence. Mouse Mogat2 assays followed a similar protocol but used 3.5–10 μg/mL microsomes and 50 μM 2‐oleoylglycerol. hMOGAT2 and Mogat2 enzyme activities were performed using a thiol‐fluorescence assay as described previously [9]. Data were plotted as fluorescence versus log molar compound concentration and fitted to a four‐parameter dose‐response curve using GraphPad Prism 10.6.1 to calculate compound IC50 values.

Thin Layer Chromatography Assays

Construction of MOGAT2‐ and Mogat2‐expressing cells and isolation of protein microsomes required for the high throughput and TLC assays were generated as described [9]. TLC assays were performed as previously determined [9].

Oral Lipid Tolerance Test

Eight‐week‐old male C57BL/6T mice (Taconic Biosciences, Germantown, NY) were fasted overnight. Mice were administered 5 mL/kg olive oil via oral gavage, and triglyceride levels were measured at 0, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h post‐administration using a Cardiochek meter (PTS Diagnostics, Whitestown, IN). Data analysis used two‐way Anova with Bonferroni planned post hoc compared to vehicle for graphing. For AUC determination, 2‐way Anova with Dunnett's post hoc was used.

Eight Day VB‐85387 Weight Loss Study

Sixteen‐week‐old male C57BL/6J DIO (Charles River, Wilmington, MA) mice were individually housed (n = 8). The mice had access to water and a 60% high‐fat diet (Research Diets #12492) ad libitum. Mice were administered vehicle (0.5% Methocel), 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272, or 5, 15, or 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 once per day by oral gavage. Body weight and food intake measurements were recorded daily. Mice were euthanized by CO2‐induced asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation. Mice were not fasted prior to euthanasia. Blood samples were obtained from cardiac puncture. Liver samples were extracted immediately after euthanasia. Body weights were analyzed by a two‐way ANOVA with genotype as a between‐group factor and the time course as a within‐subject factor. All animal studies were approved by Invivotek's IACUC (#97258) and followed the “Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee Handbook.”

Glucose Tolerance and Insulin Test

Mice were fasted 16 h prior to the study. Blood glucose was recorded at baseline, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min following oral gavage of 2 g/kg glucose. Plasma insulin levels were also measured.

Hyperinsulinaemic‐Euglycaemic Clamp Study

Dual cannulation surgery (left carotid and right jugular) was conducted on 16‐week‐old DIO mice at day 5 under general anesthesia (4% isoflurane for induction and 2% for maintenance), employing aseptic techniques. The animals were permitted to recover for 2 days. And a hyperinsulinaemic‐euglycaemic clamp study was conducted as described previously [13]. Mice were fasted for 6 h and subsequently administered a [3‐3H]glucose infusion for 90 min to facilitate glucose homeostasis. This approach enabled the determination of basal hepatic glucose production and whole‐body glucose uptake. The mice were then subjected to a primed‐continuous insulin infusion (16 mU/kg bolus followed by 4 mU/kg/min). Glucose infusion rates were adjusted to maintain euglycemia (∼8 mM) throughout the procedure, with glucose levels monitored at specified time points. Dextrose was provided when required. After achieving glycemia, mice received a bolus of 2[14C]deoxyglucose to assess tissue‐specific glucose uptake. All calculations were performed as described previously [14].

Serum Clinical Chemistries

Serum samples were collected at the end of the study by cardiac puncture. Samples were stored at −80°C until use. Serum samples were tested on an ACE Alera (Alfa Wasswerman) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Lipid Extraction From Mouse Tissues

100 mg liver samples were homogenized and extracted with hexane:2‐propanol (3:2). After vortexing and centrifugation at 5000 X g, the supernatant was washed with 0.9% NaCl and centrifuged. The aqueous phase was removed, and the organic layer was dried under nitrogen and resuspended in isopropyl alcohol for later use.

RNA Isolation and qRT‐PCR Analysis in the Liver

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissue using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA was reversed transcribed using the QuantiTectReverse Transcription kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) and subjected to qRT‐PCR using the Power SYBR RNA‐to‐CT 1‐step kit (ThermoFisher Scientific (Waltham, MA.). PCR primers are listed in Table S1.

Hormone Assay

Plasma was obtained from whole blood by centrifugation. Samples were stored at −80°C until use, and plasma hormone concentrations were determined using the Uplex metabolic combo I (mouse) kit from Meso Scale Diagnostics LLC.

Statistics

In vivo data were analyzed using a two‐way Anova analysis (mixed model) with Dunnett's post hoc compared to chow‐fed mice. Data are presented as an mean ± SD. All other statistical analyses used a two‐way Anova (mixed model) with Tukeys post hoc.

Results

VB‐85387 Is a Highly Potent and Selective MOGAT2 Inhibitor

A high‐throughput screening of human MOGAT2 was conducted using the 5.5‐million compound ECLiPS library [15] with a thiol‐fluorescence assay [9]. VB‐85387, a medicinal chemistry lead molecule from a single lead series, demonstrated strong inhibitory activity against human MOGAT2 (Figure 1A,D) and mouse Mogat2 (Figure 1B,D) enzymes, with IC50 values of 11 and 10.4 nM, respectively. TLC assays with fluorescent acyl‐CoA confirmed nanomolar potency against both enzymes (Figure 1C) and showed no inhibition of human DGAT1 or mouse Dgat1. Additional selectivity assays found no activity against other related acyl‐CoA transferases, including MOGAT1 and MOGAT3 (Figure 1D).

Mice lacking Mogat2 show delayed intestinal triglyceride absorption [16]. Previously, it was found that VB‐87531 treatment produced a similar delay [9]. To assess if VB‐85387 has the same effect, an oral lipid tolerance test was performed using olive oil as the triglyceride source, with BMS‐963272 serving as a control.

Mice given olive oil showed a sharp increase in blood triglycerides, which decreased over time. Treatment with 5, 15, or 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 significantly delayed triglyceride absorption into the blood, matching the effectiveness of 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 (Figure 1E). The mean AUC values were as follows: control mice had 2480 ± 122, mice treated with BMS‐963272 had 1518 ± 58, and those given 5 mg/kg, 15 mg/kg, and 30 mg/kg of VB‐85387 had 1600 ± 17, 1537 ± 94, and 1151 ± 44, respectively (Figure 1F).

VB‐85387 Causes Weight Loss and Reduced Food Intake

DIO mice received 5, 15, or 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 or 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 orally once daily for 7 days. Body weight and food intake were recorded each day. All compounds led to varying degrees of weight loss (Figure 2A). Mice treated with 5 (5.8 + 0.4%) experienced weight loss comparable to those given 15 mg/kg BMS‐963272 (6.1 ± 0.5%) (Figure 2A). Those treated with 15 mg/kg VB‐85387 (8.3 + 0.7%) and mice receiving 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 (10.2 + 0.3%) lost more weight than BMS‐963272 treated animals (Figure 2A). Weight loss due to VB‐85387 was dose‐dependent. Reduced food intake was observed in mice administered 15 or 30 mg/kg VB85387 or those given 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 (Figure 2B). A dose‐dependent trend was also observed. Control mice had an average cumulative food intake of 59.3 ± 2.4. Mice treated with BMS‐963272 showed an average cumulative food intake of 41.8 ± 2.7. For mice treated with VB‐85387 at doses of 5, 15, and 30 mg/kg, cumulative weight loss values were 53.4 ± 2.7, 42.7 ± 2.5, and 34.2 ± 2.3, respectively.

VB‐85387 Treatment Increases Glucose Tolerance

Mice lacking Mogat2 displayed enhanced glucose tolerance [16]. MOGAT2 inhibitors produce a similar effects [5, 6, 7]. After fasting for 6 h, mice received a glucose bolus and glucose, and insulin levels were measured.

Mice receiving vehicle or 5 mg/kg VB‐85387 had higher fasting glucose levels than those given 15 or 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 or 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 (Figure 2C,D). After glucose administration, glucose peaked at 537 + 22 mg/dL and 499 + 14 mg/dL at 15 min for the vehicle and the low‐dose group then gradually decreased to 300 + 14 mg/dL, respectively. Mice treated with doses of 15 mg/kg (374 ± 13 mg/dL) and 50 mg/kg (350 + 13 mg/dL) VB‐85387 or BMS‐963272 (325 ± 17 mg/dL) exhibited lower maximum glucose levels at 15 min and faster glucose clearance, indicating improved glucose tolerance. Similar insulin secretion patterns were observed (Figure 2E): vehicle (11 ± 0.5 IU/mL) and 5 mg/kg VB‐85387‐treated (12 + 2 IU/mL) mice showed marked increases at 15 min, while higher doses of VB‐85387 (1.6 + 0.4 IU/mL) or BMS‐963272 (2 + 0.6 IU/mL) resulted in low insulin levels. Figure 2F shows AUC values for control (935 ± 58), 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 (123 ± 12), 5 mg/kg (1007 ± 124), 15 mg/kg (159 ± 19), and 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 (160 ± 18). The finding that less insulin was needed for a greater glucose excursion suggests improved insulin sensitivity due to drug treatment.

VB‐85387 Treatment Leads to Sustained Insulin Sensitivity

Mice that received BMS‐963272 and 50 mg/kg of VB‐85387 secreted much less insulin, which was linked to improved glucose tolerance. To test whether these mice maintained insulin sensitivity or retained insulin resistance, a hyperinsulinaemic‐euglycemic clamp experiment was performed. The glucose infusion rate remained at 8 mM throughout (Figure 3A), and the insulin infusion rate was consistent before and after the clamp (data not shown). Both the BMS‐963272 group (344 + 3.2 mg/min/kg) and those given 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 (346 ± 2.8 mg/min/kg) showed higher glucose infusion rates, reaching a plateau at 30 min (Figure 3B). Hepatic glucose production was also higher in these groups (BMS‐963272, 7.6 ± 2.4 mg/min/kg; 10.5 ± 1.8) compared to the control group (−6 + 2.2 mg/min/kg) and those given 5 (−7.5 + 1.9 mg/min/kg) or 15 mg/kg VB‐85387 (−5.5 + 1.3 mg/min/kg). This suggests that only BMS‐963272 and the highest dose of VB‐85387 preserved insulin sensitivity.

Mice Treated With VB‐85387 Show Reduced Blood and Lipid Triglycerides Levels

Mogat2 −/− mice demonstrate resistance to hepatic steatosis and exhibit decreased hepatic triglyceride concentrations [16]. In clinical studies, healthy patients with obesity administered BMS‐963272 prior to consumption of a high‐fat liquid meal exhibited a trend toward reduced plasma triglyceride and free fatty acid levels [8]. The effect of VB‐85387 on both plasma and hepatic triglycerides and cholesterol levels was determined.

Mice treated with 15 mg/kg VB‐85387 showed a reduction in serum triglyceride and cholesterol levels by 1.7‐ and 1.3‐fold, respectively; those given 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 demonstrated decreases of 1.7‐ and 2.1‐fold, respectively. Similarly, mice administered 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 experienced reductions of 1.7‐ and 1.3‐fold, respectively, when compared with control mice. Mice administered 5 mg/kg VB‐85387 showed no change in either lipid level (Figure 4A,B).

Mice administered with 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 exhibited significantly lower liver triglyceride levels (2.1‐fold reduction) compared to those receiving 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 (1.8‐fold reduction), as illustrated in Figure 4B. Additionally, mice treated with 15 mg/kg VB‐85387 showed a 1.5‐fold reduction in liver triglyceride levels (Figure 4C). None of the drug treatments resulted in alterations in liver cholesterol levels (Figure 4D).

VB‐85387 Treatment Does Not Cause Liver Toxicity

VB‐85387's effect on liver function was assessed by measuring aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and ALT alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels (Figure 4E,F). VB‐85387's impact on liver function was evaluated by measuring AST and ALT levels. In control mice, mean AST was 189.1 ± 22.3 U/L; VB‐85387 at 15 and 50 mg/kg reduced AST to 141.5 ± 12.1 U/L and 124.1 ± 12.5 U/L, respectively. ALT fell from 125.2 U/L in controls to 73.3 U/L with 50 mg/kg VB‐86387. BMS‐963272 showed no effect on enzyme levels.

Weight Reduction With VB‐85387 Is Associated With Reduced Non‐Esterified Fatty Acid Levels

Mogat2 −/− mice show increased energy expenditure on a high‐fat diet, switching from carbohydrate to fat utilization [16]. RNASeq analysis of jejunal tissues from CDAA‐HFD‐fed mice treated with BMS‐963272 revealed significant upregulation of genes related to fatty acid β‐oxidation [8]. Accordingly, non‐esterified free fatty acid (NEFA) levels were measured. Administration of 15 mg/kg (0.42 ± 0.077 nmol/L; 2.2‐fold change) or 30 mg/kg VB‐85387 (0.24 ± 0.075 nmol/L; 2.3‐fold change), as well as 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 (0.34 ± 0.09 nmol/L; 1.8‐fold change), resulted in a reduction of NEFA levels in mice when compared with control animals (0.81 ± 0.09 nmol/L) (Figure 5A).

Increased β‐hydroxybutyrate levels were seen in mice treated with 15 (109.3 + 12.7 ng/μL) or 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 (187.8 + 16.3 ng/μL) or 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 (161.7 + 14.8 ng/μL) compared to control mice (65.2 + 11.7 ng/μL) (Figure 5B). Mice Treated With VB‐85387 Show Enhanced Fatty Acid β‐Oxidation Gene Expression Suggesting Possible Activated PPARα Signaling

Peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor α (PPARα) signaling induces the expression of genes involved in free fatty acid β‐oxidation [17]. The levels of PPARα, CPT1α (carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1a), ACOT3 (acyl‐CoA thioesterase), and VLCAD (very‐long‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase) gene expression in livers were determined.

Gene expression was influenced by both the type of drug administered and its dosage. In mice treated with 15 or 30 mg/kg of VB‐85387, hepatic PPARα levels increased by 3.7‐ and 7.8‐fold, CPT1α by 3.1‐ and 2.8‐fold, and VLCAD by 1.5‐ and 3.9‐fold, respectively, compared to control animals (Figure 5D). Similarly, administration of 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 resulted in the upregulation of PPARα (6.8‐fold), CPT1α (4.1‐fold), and VLCAD (3.9‐fold) in the liver. Additionally, hepatic ACOT3 mRNA expression was elevated after treatment with 15 mg/kg (2.2‐fold) and 50 mg/kg (7.7‐fold) of VB‐85387, as well as 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 (8.2‐fold), respectively (Figure 5D).

Sterol Response Element Binding Protein 1 Transcriptional Signaling Is Reduced in Mice Treated With VB‐85387

SREBP1 primarily controls de novo fatty acid synthesis, whereas SREBP2 is responsible for cholesterol synthesis. Administration of VB‐85387 or BMS‐963272 to mice resulted in decreased hepatic triglyceride content and lower serum cholesterol levels, suggesting that these compounds may inhibit SREBP‐driven transcriptional activity. The mRNA expression levels of SREBF1 (SREBP1) and SREBF2 (SREBP2) were measured in liver samples, along with the SREBP1 target gene ACC1 (acetyl‐CoA carboxylase 1) and the SREBP2 target gene HMGCS1 (HMG‐CoA synthase 1).

Mice receiving either 10 or 50 mg/kg of VB‐85387 showed reductions in liver expression of SREBF1 by 1.7‐ and 4.2‐fold, and ACC1 by 3.3‐ and 3.4‐fold, compared to untreated controls. Similarly, animals treated with 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 exhibited 2.75‐fold lower SREBF1 and 3.7‐fold lower ACC1 expression (see Figure 5E), whereas the expression of SREBF2 and HMGCS1 remained unchanged.

VB‐85387 Increases GLP‐1 Levels in Mice

Mogat2 −/− mice exhibit elevated secretion of GLP‐1 when administered a HFD [16], while individuals with obesity demonstrated increased GLP‐1 levels following treatment with BMS‐963272 [8]. Hormone concentrations were systematically evaluated across all experimental cohorts. Mice treated with 10 mg/kg (28 ± 2.1 IU/mL) or 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 (20.5 ± 2.5 IU/mL), as well as 10 mg/kg BMS‐963272 (14.3 ± 2.1 IU/mL), displayed reduced basal insulin levels compared to control mice (40.8 ± 2.3 IU/mL) (Figure 6A). GLP‐1 concentrations increased by 1.4‐, 1.6‐, and 2.4‐fold in mice receiving 5, 15, and 50 mg/kg VB‐85387, respectively (Figure 6B). Total ghrelin levels decreased approximately twofold (1345.1 vs. 609.7 pg/mL) in the group treated with 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 (Figure 6C). Leptin levels were reduced 2‐, 1.6‐, 1.9‐, and 2.5‐fold in animals treated with BMS‐963272 (20,947 ± 2237 pg/mL), 5 mg/kg VB‐85387 (25,883 ± 1923 pg/mL), 15 mg/kg VB‐85387 (21,638 ± 1902 pg/mL), and 50 mg/kg VB‐85387 (17,127 ± 1902 pg/mL), respectively, compared to controls (40,235 ± 3352 pg/mL) (Figure 6D).

Discussion

Several MOGAT2 inhibitors have been shown to induce weight loss in mice [5, 6, 7]. In a clinical trial, BMS‐963272 resulted in weight loss among healthy individuals with obesity [8]. The MOGAT2 inhibitor VB‐85387 produced weight loss in obese mice comparable to that seen with BMS‐963272. It also elevated GLP‐1 levels and decreased insulin, total ghrelin, and leptin concentrations. It was previously shown that the MOGAT inhibitor VB‐87531 acts synergistically with GLP‐1 drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide to enhance weight loss and reduce food intake [9]. Whether the further increase in GLP‐1 levels caused by VB‐87531 contributes to the synergistic effect on weight loss during combination therapy remains to be investigated using GLP‐1R knockout mice. Nonetheless, since VB‐85387 raises GLP‐1 levels, GLP‐1 synergy may underlie the pronounced effect observed in mono‐ and combination therapies.

Previous studies show that Mogat2‐deficient mice lose weight only on high‐fat diets due to increased fat use and energy expenditure. Notably, RNASeq from our combination study found upregulation of fatty acid β‐oxidation genes in mice treated with VB‐87531 alone or with GLP‐1 compounds [9]. Although higher β‐oxidation may explain the weight loss in VB‐85387‐treated mice, our data are not conclusive.

VB‐85387 reduced blood and liver triglycerides, suggesting its potential for treating MASLD and/or MASH. It was found that treating mice on a steatotic diet decreased fibrosis but did not alter liver triglyceride levels, possibly due to different diets between studies [8], as our study used a Western‐style diet reflective of typical human consumption. Preliminary evidence has shown that both VB‐85387 and VB‐87531 attenuate hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in mice fed a similar steatotic diet (manuscript in preparation).

The observed decrease in both leptin and total ghrelin was noteworthy. Leptin functions as a satiety hormone, whereas ghrelin promotes hunger [18]. It is important to note that only total ghrelin levels were measured; differentiation between inactive and active forms of ghrelin within these totals was not determined [18]. Still, since mice treated with either VB‐85387 or BMS‐963272 exhibited reduced food intake, it is likely that they also had lowered active circulating ghrelin levels. Regarding leptin, previous studies have shown that mice with obesity receiving tirzepatide treatment also experience reduced levels of this hormone [19].

Hyperinsulinemic‐euglycemic clamp studies demonstrated that treatment of DIO mice that received VB‐85387 reversed the insulin resistance observed in these animals. Notably, drug administration also reduced basal glucose levels and improved glucose tolerance, indicating enhanced insulin‐dependent glucose uptake. However, the glycogen content of the mice was not measured, so the specific pathways of glucose utilization following drug treatment remain undetermined.

In summary, findings show that VB‐85387, when used alone, reduces weight gain in mice with obesity on a Western diet. The compound enhances glucose tolerance and boosts insulin sensitivity. Notably, the reduction of liver triglyceride levels with treatment suggests VB‐85387 could be useful for MASLD/MASH therapy. Its mechanism for weight loss could potentially involve activating PPARα signaling to promote fatty acid β‐oxidation, potentially increasing energy expenditure. It also raises GLP‐1 levels, which makes it beneficial for combination therapy with GLP‐1 agents. Lastly, its weight loss effects are comparable to those observed with the phase I clinical trial drug BMS‐963272.
