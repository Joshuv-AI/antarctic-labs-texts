CD24 recruits tumor-associated neutrophils to promote the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Jun Wang, Hanning Li, Yimeng Liu, Xiang Xiao, Jiapeng Li, Shu Jiang, Jincheng Wang, Yu Cheng, Zetao Song, Yuan Wu, Chaojiang Gu, Shaoyong Chen, Jing Xiong, Huimin Zhang, Yuan Xiang, Xinghua Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment is a significant challenge in the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), necessitating the urgent development of strategies to mitigate its effects.MethodsThe application of bioinformatics methods to predict the expression level of CD24 in HCC and its relationship with the occurrence and development of HCC. Gene-engineered mice and flow cytometry were used to study the immune cell populations regulated by CD24. Cell metabolism analysis, western blotting, and lactate content measurement were employed to assess the impact of CD24 on lactate secretion by HCC cells. Additionally, cell counting kit 8 and colony formation assays were conducted to evaluate the effect of CD24 on the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib. The integration of RNA sequencing, flow cytometry, cell chemotaxis experiments, and ELISA established a robust framework for understanding CD24-mediated neutrophils immune infiltration.ResultsIn this study, we found that CD24 can recruit neutrophils to infiltrate HCC tissues to form tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) and polarize TANs to a protumor phenotype by promoting lactate secretion by HCC cells, thus promoting the progression of HCC. In addition, targeting CD24 can enhance the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib by reducing the accumulation of TANs.ConclusionsOur results reveal the molecular mechanism by which CD24 promotes HCC progression through recruitment of neutrophils infiltrates, raising new insights into the role of targeting CD24 in driving HCC immunotherapy.

Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has one of the highest incidence and mortality rates among malignant tumors worldwide.1 Surgical intervention is currently the best treatment for HCC, but the 5-year survival rate is only 18.1%, and tumor recurrence after tumor resection is common.2 Because of the challenge of early detection of HCC, this results in the vast majority of patients with HCC being already in an intermediate or advanced stage at the time of diagnosis and with no opportunity to receive treatment such as radiofrequency ablation or hepatectomy.3 Sorafenib is a first-line treatment for advanced HCC, although it may extend patients’ median survival. But easily develop resistance.4 Therefore, it is urgent to explore the mechanism of HCC development and find new therapeutic targets.

Recently, more and more immunotherapies targeting the tumor microenvironment (TME) have been developed. The TME is thought to present a more homogeneous pattern of gene expression in patients, making it an attractive target for new therapies targeting HCC.5 Neutrophils are a key mediator of innate immune responses and play an important role in human immune responses. In addition, neutrophils also play an important role in chronic inflammatory diseases, including cancer. Neutrophils can infiltrate into tumors to form tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) and are involved in tumor progression with their phenotypic and functional plasticity.6 7 TANs, on the one hand, can secrete various chemical factors that directly kill tumors, and can also inhibit tumor progression by recruiting and activating immune cells.8 However, there is increasing evidence that TANs can also promote tumor progression. In a mice tumor model, subcutaneous neutrophils promoted metastasis. Depletion of neutrophils with antibodies can reduce tumor metastasis.9 TANs in HCC tumors express programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) ligand, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and inhibit the function of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells through the binding of PD-L1 to PD-1.10 The neutrophils may also influence early tumor progression by promoting the stem cell-like phenotype of HCC cells.11 Therefore, manipulation of neutrophils properties may alter the immune microenvironment of HCC, making it more sensitive to systemic immunotherapy.

CD24 is a highly glycosylated membrane protein containing only 31 amino acids that is attached to the cell surface by a glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor.12 CD24 has been confirmed to be expressed in various tissues and cell types, including immune cells such as neutrophils, neural and cancer cells.1315 Our previous studies also found that CD24 expression levels were elevated in breast cancer tissues and cells, promoting the malignant progression of breast cancer.16 17 Therefore, targeting CD24 is important for the treatment of tumors with high expression of CD24 and poor prognosis. Studies have shown that CD24 is a marker for B10 regulatory B cells, which suppress immune responses by regulating T-cell activity.18 CD24 is highly expressed in regulatory T cells (Treg) and may modulate their immunosuppressive activity.19 The CD24/Siglec-10 interaction has been linked with natural killer cell dysfunction in HCC and could provide another pathway for CD24-mediated immune evasion.20 CD24 may also play an important role in tumor progression. The interaction of CD24 with P-selectin mediates the binding of leukocytes to endothelial cells and activated platelets, thereby promoting cell migration.21 Aberrant expression of CD24 is positively associated with recurrence and metastasis of HCC after tumor resection, suggesting that CD24 may promote the initiation and progression of HCC.22 In addition, CD24 has been shown to promote resistance to sorafenib in HCC.23 The multiple roles of CD24 in the regulation of immune response and carcinogenesis make CD24 an attractive target for cancer immunotherapy. However, whether CD24 can promote HCC progression through manipulation of neutrophils has remained mysterious.

In this study, we found that CD24 can promote the progression of HCC by recruiting neutrophils infiltrates. In addition, silencing CD24 in vitro and in vivo enhanced sorafenib’s anticancer activity in HCC cells. Overall, our results provide not only new biomarkers for the diagnosis of HCC, but also new strategies for combination therapy of HCC.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Human immortalized liver cells THLE-3 (ATCC Cat# CRL-3583, RRID:CVCL_3804) and HCC cell lines (Hepa1-6 (ATCC Cat# CRL-1830, RRID:CVCL_0327), Huh7 (RRID:CVCL_0336) and HCCLM3 (RRID:CVCL_6832)) were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). They were cultured at 37°C in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and in an incubator with 5% CO2.

Patients and tissue samples

The samples of patients with HCC used in this study were all from Hubei Cancer Hospital, and the samples were collected from January 2020 to September 2023. All patients with HCC met the diagnostic criteria for HCC. Experiments involving human specimens are conducted in strict compliance with the ethical standards required and advocated in the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association. The Wuhan University of Science and Technology Medical Ethics Committee and the Ethics Committee of Hubei Cancer Hospital approved the study. All the patients signed the informed consent form.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using cell counting kit 8 (CCK-8, Meilune, Wuhan, China). HCC model cells were seeded at a density of 5×103 cells/well and cultured overnight in 96-well plates. Then, the CCK-8 reagent was added, and OD values were measured at 490 nm wavelength using a SpectraMax M2e Microplate Reader (RRID:SCR_020297) after 2 hours of incubation at 37°C. Cell viability was calculated based on OD values.

Colony formation assay

Long-term cell survival was monitored in colony formation assays. Briefly, HCC model cells or drug-treated cells were seeded into 6-well plates. Replenish the media every 3 days. After 2 weeks, the cells were washed by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with paraformaldehyde. Finally, 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to stain and count the number of colonies.

Western blot

Cells were lysed using Protease inhibitor radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The protein concentration was determined by Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Detection Kit (Meilune, Wuhan, China). Protein extracts of the same concentration were separated by electrophoresis on sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, which was blocked with skim milk for 1 hour at room temperature. It was incubated with antibody at 4°C overnight. The second antibody was added the next day and incubated for 1 hour at normal temperature. Development reactions were performed after washing using an intelligent Chemi-TM analysis system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). The following antibodies were used: anti-β-actin (ABclonal Cat# AC004, RRID:AB_2737399), anti-CD24 (Abcam Cat# ab64064, RRID:AB_2291132), anti-CXCL5 (Abcam Cat# ab9802, RRID:AB_308790), anti-NF-κB p65 (Proteintech Cat# 10 745–1-AP, RRID:AB_2178878), anti-MCT4 antibody (Proteintech Cat# 22 787–1-AP, RRID:AB_11182479).

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using an ultra-pure RNA kit (CWBIO, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The total RNA was then reverse transcribed into complementary DNA using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR Kit (ABclonal, Wuhan, China). β-actin was selected as housekeeping gene to normalize the data of each target gene. The reaction conditions were as follows: 95°C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 20 s, 72°C for 20 s, and melt curve. Three independent experiments were carried out. All primers are listed in online supplemental table 1.

Bone marrow neutrophils separation in mice

The bone marrow cavity of femur and tibia was isolated, and the culture medium was sucked out by 1 mL sterile syringe, then the bone marrow cavity was washed to obtain single-cell suspension. The neutrophils were isolated with mice bone marrow neutrophils isolation kit (ST1641, Solarbio, China). Cells were resuspended with 10 mL PBS, 250×g, and centrifuged for 10 min. Cells were collected for later use after centrifugation.

Cell chemotaxis assay

Neutrophils from humans and mice were added to the upper chamber and conditioned medium to the lower chamber after being suspended with low serum medium. When CXCL5 was blocked, 1 μg/mL of anti-CXCL5 antibody was added to the lower chamber. Place in an incubator overnight. The medium was discarded and the cell was fixed in paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The cells were stained for 20 min with Wright-Giemsa and then washed with PBS. After the chamber was air-dried, photographs were taken under an inverted microscope.

Preparation of mice tumor cell suspension

Euthanize the mice and spray them with 75% ethanol. The tumor from the mice was removed and placed in a petri dish. A small amount of DMEM high-sugar medium was added. The tumor tissue was then shredded with scissors, and 2 mL of 1× tissue digestive fluid was added, digested in a 37°C cell culture chamber for 30 min, and shaken and mixed once every 10 min. After digestion, DMEM high sugar medium was added to stop digestion. The tumor suspension was filtered with a 70 μm cell filter, then the resulting tumor suspension was centrifuged for 5 min at 4°C, 850×g.

Construction of mice tumor model

All animal studies were approved by the Wuhan University of Science and Technology Animal Ethics Committee (No.WQ20220128). Orthotopic transplantation tumor model: C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of anesthetic, and the local area of the mice abdomen was depilated and disinfected. Make a midline incision below the xiphoid cartilage to expose the liver. A syringe was used to slowly inject 1×106 cell suspensions into the liver parenchyma. The abdomen was sutured and the mice were placed on a clean surface until they regained consciousness. It is then returned to its cage and kept alone until the wound is fully healed. After 21 days, C57BL/6J mice were killed and liver tissues were isolated.

Subcutaneous transplantation tumor model: 1×107 cells were injected subcutaneously into the back of mice to establish xenograft tumor model. Mice required to receive drug treatment were given either sorafenib (10 mg/kg, Intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) every two days) or ATG-031 (10 mg/kg, i.p. two times a week) for 2 weeks after day 7. The length and width of the tumor were recorded every 5 days, and the volume of the tumor was calculated (1/2×length×width2). After 28 days, mice were killed and subcutaneously transplanted tumors were isolated.

Determination of lactate content

The relative lactate content in cell and tissue lysates was assessed using a Lactate Assay Kit (BC2235, Solarbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell metabolism analysis

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured using the Seahorse XF system. Cell density was adjusted to 1.5×104/well and seeded into XF 96 plate overnight, then transferred into XF Medium. Subsequently, glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1.5 mM), and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (50 mM) were added; or oligomycin (1.5 mM), carbocarbonyl cyanogen-4-trifluoromethoxy phenylhydrazone (0.5 mM) and rotenone-antimycin (0.5 mM) to assess cell bioenergy. The data is normalized to cell numbers and processed by Wave software (Agilent Technologies). The bioenergy capacity was calculated according to the scheme developed by Mookerjee et al.

Conditioned medium induces neutrophils polarization

After culturing tumor cells in complete medium for 24 hours, remove the medium, wash with PBS, and then add serum-free medium for 2 days. Centrifuge the supernatant at 4°C at 3,000 rpm for 10 min, and slowly aspirate the supernatant as the co-culture conditioned medium (CM). Remove the neutrophils culture medium by centrifugation, and resuspend the cells in a ratio of CM to fresh medium of 1:1. After co-culturing for 24 hours, detect the relevant indicators of neutrophils polarization.

Flow cytometry

The cell suspension was filtered with a 40 μm cell filter to remove impurities, and the filtrate was collected into a new flow tube. The cells were washed two times with a 2% FBS/PBS mixture and then stained using the target antibody. Flow cytometry (FCM) was used to analyze the flow cytometry. The FlowJo VX (V.10.6.2) software is used to analyze FCM results.

Statistical analysis

Experimental results were statistically analyzed in this section using GraphPad Prism (RRID:SCR_002798). The results were presented from three independent experiments. The data of the experimental results in this chapter are expressed as mean±SD. Student’s t-test was used to analyze significance for experimental results between two groups of data. In the figure, *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, and NS represents not statistically significant.

Results

CD24 expression is upregulated in HCC and recruits neutrophils to infiltrate HCC

To assess the expression of CD24 in HCC and its relationship with disease progression, we conducted a database analysis and combined with biological technology validation, and found that CD24 is upregulated in HCC and is associated with HCC progression (Supplement Figure 1). Studies have shown that CD24 promotes tumor development by remodeling the TME. To investigate the influence of CD24 on the TME of HCC, we injected Hepa1-6 cells into the liver of C57BL/6J genetically engineered mice with systemic CD24 knockout (C57BL/6JGpt-CD24aem16Cd1226, later referred to as CD24-KO) to construct an in situ mice HCC model (figure 1A). Compared with wild-type (CD24-WT) mice, the liver tumor area of CD24-KO mice was significantly reduced (figure 1B). Analysis of various immune cells within tumors found that mice in the CD24-KO group had significantly reduced intratumoral TANs compared with CD24-WT mice (figure 1C,D). Whereas, B220+ B cells and F4/80+ macrophages were increased in the intratumoral region of mice in the CD24-KO group compared with the CD24-WT group (figure 1E-H). In addition, we found that CD3+ T cells were decreased in the tumor of CD24-KO mice (figure 1I.J). However, compared with the CD24-WT group, CD8+ T cells in the tumor were increased in the CD24-KO group (figure 1,L). This phenomenon may be attributed to an increase in certain immunosuppressive T-cell subsets. Recent studies have also found that CD24 can promote an increase in the ratio of Tregs and exhausted CD8+ T cells in HCC. We examined the Tregs and exhausted T cells in the tumor tissues of CD24-KO and CD24-WT mice, and the results showed that Tregs and exhausted T cells were significantly reduced in the tumor tissues of CD24-KO mice compared to CD24-WT mice (Supplement Figure 2A, 2B). Because in the tumors of CD24-KO mice, neutrophils were the only immune cells that sustained and specifically decreased in the intratumor region, and we found through The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA) database () analysis that neutrophils accounted for the highest proportion of immune cells in liver cancer. Moreover, compared with other tumors, the proportion of neutrophils in liver cancer was the highest (figure 1M,N). Similarly, we constructed a subcutaneous graft tumor model by subcutaneous injection of Hepa1-6 cells. The subcutaneous tumor volume was measured every 7 days, and the mice were killed after 28 days. The results showed that the volume and weight of subcutaneous transplanted tumors were smaller in the CD24-KO group than in the CD24-WT group (online supplemental figure 2C-E). In addition, H&E staining and Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis were performed on mice tumor tissues. H&E staining showed that the pathological degree of the CD24-KO group was alleviated. Compared with mice in the CD24-WT group, MPO expression in tumor tissues of mice in the CD24-KO group was decreased, and the number of intratumoral TANs was significantly reduced (online supplemental figure 2F,G). This further suggests that CD24 may influence the malignant progression of HCC by promoting the accumulation of TANs. To investigate whether increased TANs in mice are applied to human HCC, according to the IHC scoring, HCC tissues are divided into CD24 high expression group and CD24 low expression group, and further comparisons are made of TANs in HCC tissues with high and low expression of CD24 protein. Consistent with our data from mice models, we observed an increase in TANs in tumors with high CD24 expression compared with tumors with low CD24 expression (online supplemental figure 2H,I). This suggests that the accumulation of TANs in human and mice HCC is associated with upregulation of CD24 expression.

To confirm the involvement of CD24 in recruitment of neutrophils, we performed chemotaxis in vitro using differentiated PLB-985 cells and primary neutrophils isolated from mice bone marrow. PLB-985 is a commonly used acute myeloid leukemia cell line, which can differentiate into neutrophil-like cells after DMSO treatment. PLB-985 cells were treated with DMSO for 5 days and differentiated into mature neutrophil-like cells PLB-985N (online supplemental figure 3A). Differentiated PLB-985N cells were then added to the upper chamber of the transwell migration system and CM of the HCC model cells (silencing of CD24 in HCC cells and control HCC cells) was added to the lower chamber of the transwell migration system (online supplemental figure 3B). Compared to the CM of the control group (KO-NC), the CM of the CD24 knockout group (KO-CD24) reduced the ability to induce migration to PLB-985N cells (online supplemental figure 3C,D). Next, we isolated neutrophils from mice bone marrow (online supplemental figure 3E). Similarly, in CD24 knockdown Hepa1-6 cells, the migration of mice neutrophils to CM was reduced (online supplemental figure 3F,G). These results suggest that CD24 promotes neutrophils recruitment in vitro.

CXCL5 is a key mediator of CD24-recruited neutrophils infiltration

To explore the mechanism by which CD24 promotes neutrophils infiltration in tumor cells, RNA sequencing was performed on the in orthotopic transplanted tumor tissues of CD24-KO and CD24-WT mice. The results showed that the expression of chemokines and cytokines, which promote neutrophils attraction, activation and polarization, was downregulated in the CD24-KO group (figure 2A). Both reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and ELISA results confirmed downregulation of the expression levels of these factors after CD24 silencing in Hepa1-6 cells (figure 2B, online supplemental figure 4A). Because CXCL5 is the most prominent and consistently downregulated chemokine and a well-known neutrophils chemokine, we decided to focus on CXCL5. The expression levels and content of CXCL5 were also significantly decreased in CD24-knockout HCC cells (online supplemental figure 4B,C). To investigate whether CXCL5 is a major mediator of neutrophils recruitment, we used a neutralizing CXCL5 antibody to block CXCL5 in neutrophils chemotactic experiments. The results showed that the CD24-induced increase in neutrophils recruitment was abrogated by anti-CXCL5 antibody (figure 2C,D). These results support that CD24 upregulates CXCL5 expression in HCC cells and that CXCL5 is an important chemokine secreted by HCC cells to recruit neutrophils.

To determine whether CXCL5 expression was also increased in CD24-overexpressing HCC tissues, we first performed IHC staining of CXCL5 on HCC samples from CD24-knockout and non-knockout mice. The expression of CXCL5 was significantly reduced in the HCC tissues of CD24-KO mice compared with that of wild-type mice (online supplemental figure 4D). Similarly, observations in human HCC samples with high or low expression of CD24 showed that the expression levels of CXCL5 were significantly higher in CD24-high-expressing HCC tissues than in CD24-low-expressing tissues (online supplemental figure 4E). These results support our in vitro data and indicate that CD24 is highly correlated with the increase of CXCL5 in HCC cells.

Because CXCR2 is a high-affinity receptor for CXCL5, in the TME, CXCL5 binds to CXCR2 and is involved in the recruitment of immune cells and promotes angiogenesis, tumor growth, and metastasis.27 So we then investigated whether TANs in HCC express CXCR2. We performed IF analysis of CXCR2 and MPO using human and mice HCC samples. The results showed that HCC mice and human HCC with higher levels of CD24 expression contained increased TANs, both of which expressed CXCR2, the receptor for CXCL5 (online supplemental figure 4F,G). In addition, we investigated whether the CXCL5-CXCR2 axis plays a role in the neutrophils of CD24. Our in vitro chemotactic experiments on neutrophils from mice showed that CM from CD24-overexpressing cells had enhanced ability to induce migration of mice neutrophil cells compared with control cells. Treatment with the CXCR2 inhibitor reparixin significantly reduced the migration of CM from neutrophils to CD24-overexpressing HCC cells (online supplemental figure 4H,I). These results suggest that CD24 can promote the recruitment of TANs through the CXCL5-CXCR2 axis.

To further confirm that CXCL5 is a major mediator of CD24-induced TANs accumulation and tumor growth, we blocked CXCL5 using a neutralizing antibody in a mice model of HCC. We implanted Hepa1-6 control cells or CD24-overexpressing Hepa1-6 cells subcutaneously into immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice, followed by intratumoral injection of CXCL5 neutralizing antibody or IgG control in tumor-bearing mice (figure 2E). Consistent with our previous findings, the CD24-overexpressing group had larger tumor volumes compared with the control group treated with IgG. Importantly, CXCL5 neutralizing antibody inhibited the growth of tumors in CD24-overexpressing mice at levels similar to those in the control group (figure 2F–2H). This suggests that CXCL5 is a major mediator of CD24-induced tumor growth in immunocompetent animal models. Further studies found, consistent with our in vitro data, that tumors in the CD24-overexpressing group had increased TANs infiltration compared with those in the control group treated with IgG. Whereas anti-CXCL5 antibody treatment rescued these phenotypes (figure 2I,J). Taken together, our results highlight that CXCL5 is an important mediator of CD24-induced TANs recruitment and tumor growth.

CD24 promotes CXCL5 expression through NF-κb-mediated transcriptional activation

We sought to reveal the mechanism by which CD24 enhances CXCL5 expression. It has been reported that CD24 can play its role by activating nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signal pathway.28 Notably, the transcription factor NF-κB has previously been shown to transcriptionally regulate CXCL5 expression.29 These studies prompted us to explore whether CD24 induces CXCL5 expression through NF-κb-mediated transcriptional regulation. We found that NF-κB expression was upregulated after overexpression of CD24 (figure 3A). The localization of NF-κB in protein lysates of CD24-overexpressing cells and control cells was further analyzed. As expected, CD24 overexpression resulted in an increase in nuclear NF-κB (figure 3B). Next, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation assays to determine whether CD24 induces binding of NF-κB to CXCL5 regulatory regions. The results showed that NF-κB enrichment of the consensus sequence within the CXCL5 promoter was significantly increased in CD24-overexpressing HCC cells compared with control cells (figure 3C). Most importantly, we confirmed that the expression of CXCL5 induced by CD24 is significantly inhibited after treating the cells with the NF-κB specific inhibitor caffeic acid phenethyl ester (CAPE) (figure 3D and E). Collectively, our results suggest that CD24 promotes CXCL5 expression through NF-κB-mediated transcriptional activation.

CD24 induces neutrophils polarization by promoting lactate secretion

Next, we explored whether CD24 is involved in inducing TANs to polarize towards the N2 type. Studies have shown that lactate affects different cell types in the TME. In orthotopic transplanted tumor model mice, lactate levels were significantly higher in tumors than in adjacent normal livers (online supplemental figure 5A). The main pathway for lactate production was derived from glycolysis, and RNA sequencing analysis indicated that glycolysis-related genes were downregulated in tumor tissues of the CD24-KO group (online supplemental figure 5B). After silencing CD24 in Hepa1-6 cells, it was found that ECAR and OCR were reduced after inhibiting CD24 expression (online supplemental figure 5C,D). The effect of CD24 on lactate production in Hepa1-6 cells was further evaluated, showing that silencing CD24 reduced extracellular lactate levels and reduced intracellular pH (online supplemental figure 5E,F). Next, we examined the effect of the CD24-lactate pathway on neutrophils polarization in vitro. We co-cultured freshly isolated bone marrow primary neutrophils with CM from Hepa1-6 cells (control), CD24-overexpressing control group (pLVX-CD24), and Hepa1-6 cells overexpressing CD24 (pLVX-CD24). CD206+ cells (which reflect an N2 polarized phenotype) were measured by flow cytometry. The results showed that when the neutrophils were cultured in CM of pLVX-CD24, the N2 neutrophils increased. Furthermore, direct addition of lactate to the control group medium significantly increased the expression of N2 neutrophils markers, whereas induction of pH-like pyruvate or hydrochloric acid did not have such a significant effect (figure 4A), shows that these changes are not just pH-dependent. Similarly, we obtained the same results in the established human PLB-985N neutrophils (figure 4B). To further confirm the role of lactate, we denatured CM in tumor cells from the control, pLVX, and pLVX-CD24 groups in an effort to inactivate bioactive protein factors. In addition, we dialyzed CM to filter low molecular weight (<3 KD) metabolites. Lactate and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) were used as positive controls. Denatured pLVX-CD24 or exogenous lactate increased CD206 expression on mice bone marrow primary neutrophils, but not in filtered media (figure 4C,D). Similarly, denatured CM of pLVX-CD24 retained its ability to increase N2-related messenger RNA (mRNA) gene expression and decrease N1-related gene expression, whereas filtered CM did not have this phenomenon (figure 4E and F). These results suggest that CD24 induces neutrophils polarization by promoting lactate secretion.

CD24 upregulated the expression of MCT4 in HCC cells

MCT4 plays a key role in lactate transport. We detected the expression of MCT4 in patients with HCC’s samples, and the results showed that the expression of MCT4 in patients with HCC’s cancer tissues was significantly higher than that in adjacent tissues (online supplemental figure 6A,B). Similarly, in human HCC cells, western blot and RT-qPCR results indicated that MCT4 protein and mRNA expression levels were significantly elevated in HCC cells compared with THLE3 cell line (online supplemental figure 6C,D). Next, we sought to determine whether CD24 regulates MCT4 expression. We found that knockdown of CD24 in the Hepa1-6 cell line reduced the expression level of MCT4 (figure 5A,B). Similarly, knockout of CD24 in human HCC cell lines Huh7 and HCCLM3 similarly suppressed MCT4 expression (figure 5C,D). Next, we compared the expression levels of MCT4 in human HCC tissues with high and low expression of CD24. As expected, we observed that tumor tissues with high CD24 expression had higher MCT4 protein expression levels than those with low CD24 expression (figure 5E). We further evaluated the expression level of MCT4 in tumor tissue of HCC mice model. The expression level of MCT4 was reduced in the CD24-KO group compared with the CD24-WT group tumor tissue (figure 5F). These results suggest that CD24 can upregulate the expression of MCT4 in HCC cells.

To investigate the functional role of MCT4 in CD24-overexpressing tumor cells, shRNA was used to knockdown MCT4 (sh-MCT4) in HCC control cells and CD24-overexpressing HCC cells. The results showed that silencing MCT4 reduced the cell viability of HCC cells (online supplemental figure 7A,B). Moreover, silenced MCT4 rescued the increase in extracellular lactate level caused by high expression of CD24, which led to a significant increase in intracellular pH (online supplemental figure 7C,D). In addition, we performed quantitative assessments of ECAR and OCR, which showed that silencing MCT4 led to significant reductions in glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation rates (online supplemental figure 7E,F). Taken together, these results suggest that knockdown MCT4 reduces CD24-mediated lactate secretion and cell viability.

Knockdown of MCT4 inhibited CD24-mediated neutrophils polarization

Based on the above observations, we hypothesized that TANs to the N2-type in HCC may be due to the accumulation of extracellular lactate resulting from upregulation of MCT4 expression by CD24. We cocultured freshly isolated bone marrow primary neutrophils cells with CM derived from pLVX+sh-NC, pLVX-CD24+sh-NC, pLVX+sh-MCT4 and pLVX-CD24+sh-MCT4 tumor cells. The flow cytometry of CD206+ cells showed an increase in N2 polarization when neutrophils were cultured in the CM of pLVX-CD24+sh-NC, while pLVX+sh-MCT4 significantly attenuated the overexpression of CD24-mediated increase in N2 polarization. In addition, direct addition of lactate to CM of sh-MCT4 significantly increased the percentage of N2 neutrophils. Next, we tested the effect of antagonizing lactate receptor GPR81 with 3-hydroxybutyric acid (3-Oba). GPR81 inhibition reversed CM-induced N2 polarization of overexpressed CD24. Similarly, we obtained the same results in established human PLB-985N neutrophils cells (figure 6A). Furthermore, chemotactic experiments showed that the number of neutrophils migrated in the CM of pLVX-CD24+sh NC was the highest compared with pLVX+sh NC, pLVX+sh-MCT4, and pLVX-CD24+sh-MCT4 media (figure 6B). This enhanced mobility is eliminated by 3-Oba. In addition, we detected markers representing neutrophils polarization and found that after co-culturing neutrophils with pLVX-CD24+sh NC CM, the RNA levels of N2 neutrophils markers increased. The knockout of MCT4 reversed this effect, while the RNA levels of N1 neutrophils markers showed the opposite trend (figure 6C).

Further, we treated HCC model cells (pLVX+sh-NC, pLVX-CD24+sh-NC, pLVX+sh-MCT4 and pLVX-CD24+sh-MCT4) for CM degeneration and dialysis. Lactate and TGF-β were used as positive controls. Denatured pLVX-CD24+sh-NC medium increased the expression of CD206 on neutrophils, and MCT4 knockdown reversed this effect, whereas this phenomenon was absent in filtered CM (online supplemental figure 8A). Similarly, knockdown of MCT4 inhibited the ability of denatured pLVX-CD24+sh-NC medium to increase N2-related mRNA gene expression and decrease N1-related gene expression, but not in filtered CM (online supplemental figure 8B,C). Taken together, these results suggest that CD24 enhances N2-type neutrophils cell polarization by promoting lactate secretion, which can be reversed by inhibition of the lactate transporter MCT4 or lactate receptor blockade.

Based on the above experimental results, we further verified at the animal level that we used Hepa1-6 cells overexpressing CD24 with or without MCT4 knockdown to construct a C57BL/6J subcutaneous graft tumor model to detect whether MCT4 is required for CD24-mediated neutrophils polarization to promote HCC progression. The volume of C57BL/6J subcutaneous grafts was measured every 7 days, C57BL/6J was killed and the grafts were isolated after 28 days, and the weight of subcutaneous grafts was recorded. The overexpressed CD24 group had increased tumor volumes compared with the control group. Knockdown of MCT4 significantly inhibited tumor growth in CD24-overexpressing mice (online supplemental figure 9A-C). This suggests that knockdown of MCT4 effectively reduces CD24-mediated tumor growth. More importantly, consistent with our in vitro data, tumors in the CD24-overexpressing group had higher lactate levels and N2 TANs, whereas knockdown of MCT4 rescued these phenotypes (online supplemental figure D-G). In conclusion, our results suggest that MCT4 is required for CD24-induced neutrophils polarization and tumor growth.

Targeting CD24 enhances the sensitivity of HCC to sorafenib

Studies have found that sorafenib therapy increases TANs accumulation in patients with HCC, leading to tolerance.30 Next, we explored whether silencing CD24 could enhance the sensitivity of HCC to sorafenib. When the HCC model cells were treated with sorafenib, the results of the CCK-8 experiment showed that the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib increased after silencing CD24 compared with the control group (online supplemental figure 10A). And CD24-silencing cells were more likely to inhibit cell proliferation after sorafenib treatment (online supplemental figure 10B). Further, we found that silencing CD24 inhibited sorafenib’s recruitment of neutrophils. ELISA experimental results showed that the content of CXCL5 in the medium supernatant was increased in the sorafenib treatment group compared with the control group, whereas silencing CD24 reduced the increased content of CXCL5 caused by sorafenib treatment (online supplemental figure 10C). And silencing CD24 inhibited neutrophils migration to CM after sorafenib treatment (online supplemental figure 10C). Furthermore, RNA levels of N2-type TANs markers were elevated after co-culture with CM after sorafenib treatment, and silencing CD24 reversed this effect, whereas N1 markers showed an opposite trend (online supplemental figure 10D). Similarly, treatment of HCC model cells with anti-CD24 mab ATG-031 yielded the same results (online supplemental figure 11A-E).

Based on the above results, we further validated it at the animal level. We constructed a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model by subcutaneous injection of Hepa1-6 cells in C57BL/6J mice. They were treated with ATG-031 and sorafenib. C57BL/6J mice were killed after 2 weeks of treatment. The results showed that sorafenib-treated mice had smaller tumor volumes than control group mice, while the combination of sorafenib and ATG-031 further reduced tumor volumes (figure 7A–C). Furthermore, CXCL5 expression levels were elevated in tumor tissues of mice treated with sorafenib and had more TANs infiltration, whereas after ATG-031 treatment, we found that CXCL5 expression levels were decreased, and reduced TANs infiltration (figure 7D–G). These results indicate that ATG-031 combined with sorafenib can synergistically inhibit tumor growth.

Discussion

TME of HCC is a complex and structured mixture characterized by abnormal angiogenesis, chronic inflammation, and dysregulated extracellular matrix remodeling, which together lead to an immunosuppressive environment that promotes HCC proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.31 A better understanding of the interaction between TME and HCC cells is critical to the development of new and effective therapies for HCC.32 In this study, we dissected the mechanism by which HCC cells with high CD24 expression promote HCC progression through recruitment of neutrophils infiltration. We first found that CD24 upregulates CXCL5 expression by activating NF-κB, thereby recruiting neutrophils to infiltrate HCC. Further analysis showed that CD24 induced TANs to promote tumor phenotype polarization by upregulating MCT4 expression and promoting lactate secretion. Silencing MCT4 rescues CD24-mediated TANs polarization and tumor growth. More importantly, by targeting CD24, the accumulation of TANs caused by sorafenib treatment was reduced, thereby improving the therapeutic effect of sorafenib on HCC (figure 8).

Under normal physiological conditions, the expression of CD24 gradually increases with the maturation of neutrophils.14 As a ligand for P-selectin, CD24 can mediate the adhesion of monocytes or neutrophils to activated endothelial cells or platelets that express P-selectin.33 After CD24 knockout, neutrophils may be unable to effectively adhere to the vascular endothelium, resulting in a reduced ability to migrate to other normal tissues. Under pathological conditions, CD24 is highly expressed on the cell membranes of various tumor cells, and its role as a biomarker for certain cancer stem cells and as an anti-phagocytic checkpoint has made CD24 a potential therapeutic target for cancer immunotherapy.13 While numerous studies have investigated the mechanisms through which CD24 facilitates the progression of HCC, the specific impact of CD24 on the TME of HCC remains largely unexplored. In this study, we focused on the effect of CD24 on the immune microenvironment of HCC. We found that CD24 recruits neutrophils into HCC by promoting the secretion of chemokine CXCL5 by HCC cells, and neutrophils infiltrates into tumor tissues through the vascular wall to form TANs. Previous research has demonstrated that tumor-derived CXCL1 facilitates the infiltration of TANs in lung cancer, thereby contributing to tumor growth.34 Additionally, investigations in breast cancer models have revealed that TANs function as a “tumor battery”, increasing the accumulation of neutral fats induced by pulmonary mesenchymal cells, which in turn enhances the survival and proliferation of tumor cells.35 Furthermore, TANs have been shown to promote cancer metastasis through the regulation of SLIT2 in gastric cancer cells via exosomal miR-4745–5p/3911.36 Similarly, we observed that knockout of CD24 reduced the accumulation of TANs and thereby inhibited tumor growth, both in orthotopic liver cancer model mice and in subcutaneous xenograft tumor model mice.

Excessive lactate contributes to the establishment of an immunosuppressive environment conducive to cancer cell growth and plays an important role in shaping immune cell function.37 An increasing body of research is dedicated to exploring the interactions between lactate and various immune cell types within the TME to enhance the effectiveness of existing antitumor immunotherapies.38 39 In light of the established immunosuppressive properties of lactate, we sought to examine the potential involvement of the CD24/lactate signaling pathway in the promotion of an immunosuppressive phenotype. Prior investigations have indicated that lactate-induced polarization of M2 macrophages contributes to tumor growth within the TME.40 Our findings revealed that elevated expression of CD24 led to a marked increase in lactate secretion and a metabolic shift towards enhanced glycolysis, with aberrant lactate levels facilitating the polarization of N1 TANs to N2 TANs. Importantly, we also observed that high CD24 expression correlated with the upregulation of MCT4, a key transporter associated with lactate secretion, glycolytic activity, and the malignant progression of tumors, which is linked to poor prognostic outcomes.4143 The silencing of MCT4 was found to counteract lactate-driven immunosuppression, thereby enhancing T-cell infiltration and functionality.44 We observed that silencing MCT4 inhibited the increase of extracellular lactate and the decrease of intracellular pH due to CD24 overexpression. Furthermore, silencing MCT4 reduced the N2 TANs accumulation and tumor growth resulting from CD24 overexpression. These findings suggest that CD24 enhances lactate secretion by HCC cells through the upregulation of MCT4 expression, thereby facilitating the polarization of TANs.

Sorafenib is a first-line treatment for patients with advanced HCC and has shown significant efficacy. However, most patients develop resistance in a short period, leading to rapid disease progression.45 Consequently, there is a pressing need to elucidate the mechanisms underlying sorafenib resistance in HCC and to formulate improved therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing the efficacy of sorafenib while overcoming its resistance. Studies have shown that sorafenib resistance is strongly associated with TME.46 47 TAMs have been shown to promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition and augment stemness in HCC specimens subjected to sorafenib treatment.48 Intratumoral stem cell-like CCR4+ Tregs inhibit CD8+ T-cell activity through the secretion of interleukin-10 (IL-10) and IL-35, thereby contributing to the immunosuppressive milieu and facilitating sorafenib resistance.49 Additionally, sorafenib has been confirmed to drive secondary resistance and rapid disease progression by promoting the accumulation of protumor neutrophils within the tumor.50 TANs promote the progression of HCC and resistance to sorafenib by recruiting macrophages and T-regulatory cells.30 In this study, we found that CD24 plays a key role in promoting the accumulation of protumor neutrophils within the tumor. Therefore, we further analyzed whether blocking CD24 could enhance the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib. The results showed that targeting CD24 treatment increased the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib by reducing the accumulation of TANs.

Conclusions

These findings have significant mechanistic and therapeutic implications. Our data reveal an increased accumulation of TANs and a shift towards N2 polarization in HCC tumors characterized by elevated CD24 expression. Notably, this study is the first to demonstrate that CD24 upregulates the expression of MCT4, leading to an increase in extracellular lactate levels. The resultant abnormal lactate environment promotes the polarization of TANs towards the N2 phenotype. Furthermore, targeting CD24 can enhance the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib by reducing the accumulation of TANs. However, a limitation of the current investigation is that the genetically engineered mice models used are systemic knockouts of CD24 rather than liver-specific knockouts. The elevated expression of CD24 is implicated in the accumulation of TANs in HCC, and further research is warranted to elucidate the interactions between TANs and other immune cell types. Additionally, the specific mechanism by which CD24 regulates MCT4 expression remains to be explored. Nonetheless, our findings provide evidence that targeting CD24 may improve the immunosuppressive TME in HCC, thereby improving the therapeutic efficacy of sorafenib. Consequently, this research offers valuable insights into the development of novel combination strategies aimed at augmenting the effectiveness of sorafenib treatment.
