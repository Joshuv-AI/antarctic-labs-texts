Comparative effects of high- vs. low-intensity Bobath therapy on spasticity in young children with spastic cerebral palsy
Authors: Faith M Muma, Geoffrey Moyo, Loveness A Nkhata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpastic cerebral palsy, the most common cerebral palsy subtype, is characterised by increased muscle tone that restricts movement and daily functioning. The Bobath approach is widely applied in paediatric neurorehabilitation; however, the effects of therapy intensity and sequencing remain insufficiently understood.ObjectiveTo compare the effects of high versus low-intensity Bobath therapy on spasticity in young children with spastic cerebral palsy.MethodsA double-blinded, randomised, crossover trial was conducted at Livingstone University Teaching Hospital, Zambia, involving 30 children aged 3 months to 4 years. Participants were randomly allocated to one of two sequences: Standard Bobath followed by Modified Bobath (AB) or Modified Bobath followed by Standard Bobath (BA). Each phase lasted 12 weeks. The Modified protocol comprised three 60-minute sessions weekly; the Standard protocol comprised two 40-minute sessions weekly. Spasticity was measured at baseline and after each phase using the Modified Ashworth Scale. Data were analysed using paired tests and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests in SPSS v25 (p < 0.05).ResultsBoth sequences produced significant improvements in spasticity. The BA sequence yielded greater gains, including a 28–35% reduction in Modified Ashworth Scale scores (p = 0.008), compared with the AB sequence, which showed a 12–18% reduction (p = 0.032). No carryover effect was observed (p = 0.67). Endline comparisons showed significantly lower right-sided upper limb spasticity in the BA group, particularly in shoulder flexion (p = 0.0213), shoulder extension (p = 0.0187), elbow flexion (p = 0.0213), elbow extension (p = 0.0213), wrist flexion (p = 0.0213), and wrist extension (p = 0.0213). Lower limb improvements included significant reductions in hip extension, knee extension, ankle flexion, and ankle extension (all p = 0.0479).ConclusionBobath therapy effectively improves motor outcomes in children with spastic cerebral palsy. Initiating treatment with a higher-intensity Modified Bobath protocol leads to superior reductions in spasticity, underscoring the importance of therapy intensity and sequencing in paediatric neurorehabilitation.Trial registrationPan African Clinical Trial Registry (PACTR) PACTR202512685522786. Registered in December 2024.Keywords: Cerebral palsy, Spasticity, Bobath therapy, Modified Ashworth Scale, Neurorehabilitation, Children

Background

Cerebral palsy (CP) represents a group of permanent disorders of movement and posture development, causing activity limitations attributed to non-progressive disturbances in the developing fetal or infant brain [1]. European data indicate CP is among the most common causes of physical disability in children, with an average incidence of 2.08 per 1000 live births [1]. Spasticity, the most common motor impairment form in CP, is characterised by velocity-dependent increases in muscle tone due to stretch reflex hyperexcitability [2]. This abnormal muscle tone increase manifests as a pyramidal tract lesion feature [3] and can interfere with voluntary movement, contribute to postural deformities, delay motor milestones, and reduce functional independence and quality of life for children and caregivers [4].

Management of spasticity in children with CP requires a multidisciplinary approach, including pharmacological interventions, surgical procedures (selective dorsal rhizotomy, orthopedic surgery), occupational therapy, and physiotherapy [5–7]. Among physiotherapy approaches, the Bobath concept or Neurodevelopmental Treatment (NDT) is widely used internationally [8]. Developed by Berta and Karel Bobath in the mid-20th century, NDT emphasises muscle tone normalization, abnormal reflex pattern inhibition, normal postural and movement pattern facilitation, and functional task practice [9, 10]. The concept embraces evidence-based concepts of motor control, motor learning, and neuroplasticity [11].

Despite widespread use, evidence on Bobath therapy effectiveness in reducing spasticity remains mixed [12, 13]. Some studies and reviews have reported positive functional outcomes, but with limitations including small sample sizes and lack of standardised intervention protocols related to intensity and frequency [12, 14–16]. Therapy intensity has emerged as a particularly important determinant of neuroplastic change, with higher-dose interventions associated with improved motor learning, enhanced cortical reorganisation, and greater functional gains [17–20]. However, few studies have directly compared different Bobath therapy intensities, and even fewer have done so using rigorous randomised crossover designs in low-resource settings.

This study addresses this gap by evaluating the comparative effects of high- versus low-intensity Bobath therapy on spasticity in young children with spastic cerebral palsy. The crossover design allows each child to serve as their own control, thereby strengthening internal validity and reducing confounding [21]. The findings have important implications for optimising therapy protocols in both high- and low-resource settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

A double-blinded randomised AB/BA crossover trial was conducted at the Physiotherapy Department of Livingstone University Teaching Hospital (LUTH), a level-two referral hospital in Southern Province, Zambia. Each participant received both interventions in alternating sequences, with each phase lasting 12 weeks. A washout period was not implemented because caregivers did not consent to treatment interruption and because withholding therapy was considered ethically inappropriate [22]. Statistical testing confirmed no significant carryover effects were present. The crossover design was selected to allow each participant to serve as their own control, thereby enhancing statistical efficiency and reducing inter-individual variability. However, the absence of a washout period represents a methodological limitation, as true crossover trials require complete dissipation of treatment effects before initiating the second phase. Whilst our statistical analysis did not detect significant carryover effects (p = 0.67), we cannot definitively exclude their presence, particularly given the neuroplastic changes that intensive therapy may induce.

Participants

Thirty children aged between 3 months and 4 years with confirmed diagnosis of spastic cerebral palsy were enrolled. All participants were medically stable and able to participate in physiotherapy. Children with severe cognitive impairment or comorbidities affecting mobility or neurological function were excluded. Caregivers provided written informed consent prior to participation. The age range was selected based on evidence suggesting early intervention, particularly starting around 3–5 months when adequate musculoskeletal development is present, produces better outcomes [22, 23], while the upper limit of 4 years reflects the transition to home-based care at age 5 in the local context. Prior to enrolment, 17 children (56.7%) had received some form of physiotherapy, with duration ranging from 1 to 18 months (median 6 months). The remaining 13 children (43.3%) were therapy-naïve. Previous physiotherapy interventions varied and included conventional neurodevelopmental treatment, stretching exercises, and caregiver-directed home programmes. A minimum washout period of 4 weeks from any prior physiotherapy was required before baseline assessment to minimise carryover effects from previous interventions.

Randomisation and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned to either the AB or BA sequence using computer-generated simple randomisation. Allocation was concealed using sealed opaque envelopes prepared by an independent research assistant. Treating physiotherapists were aware of group assignments, but outcome assessors and caregivers remained blinded throughout the study. Spasticity assessments were conducted by two independent assessors who were physiotherapists with a minimum of 5 years’ clinical experience in paediatric neurorehabilitation and had completed standardised training in Modified Ashworth Scale administration. Inter-rater reliability was established prior to the study (ICC = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.82–0.94). To maintain blinding, assessors had no involvement in treatment delivery and were not informed of group allocation.

Interventions

Standard Bobath therapy (protocol A)

Consisted of two sessions per week, each lasting 40 min. Treatment focused on conventional Bobath handling, facilitation of normal movement patterns, and postural control techniques.

The therapy dosages were selected based on neurophysiological principles of motor learning and spasticity management in young children. Spasticity secondary to upper motor neurone lesions requires frequent, repetitive practice to modulate abnormal tone and facilitate neuroplastic reorganisation. The Standard Bobath protocol (80 min per week) represents the typical frequency achievable in resource-limited settings and is consistent with usual care practices documented in sub-Saharan African paediatric rehabilitation centres. The Modified Bobath protocol (180 min per week) was designed to provide higher-dose intervention whilst remaining feasible for families and healthcare systems. This intensity aligns with evidence suggesting that children with cerebral palsy benefit from therapy doses of 1.5–3 h per week to achieve meaningful motor gains. In this study, “low-intensity” and “high-intensity” are operationally defined by total weekly contact time (80 vs. 180 min, respectively) and session frequency (2 vs. 3 sessions weekly), reflecting a 2.25-fold difference in total therapy exposure over each 12-week phase.

Modified Bobath therapy (protocol B)

Consisted of three sessions per week, each lasting 60 min. This protocol incorporated structured assessment, sensory stimulation, and facilitation of movement, inhibition of abnormal reflexes, functional task practice, and caregiver education. The Modified Bobath approach aligns with contemporary understanding that therapy intensity and structured protocols are essential for neuroplastic adaptation [24, 25]. The seven-component structure of the Modified Bobath protocol (detailed in Table 1) was designed to address the multifaceted nature of spasticity management systematically. The time allocations for each component were established through iterative clinical piloting and reflect the minimum duration required to complete each intervention element adequately whilst maintaining therapeutic quality. The sequencing progresses from assessment and preparation (postural alignment, sensory input) through active intervention (reflex inhibition, facilitation, functional practice) to consolidation (caregiver training), mirroring the principles of motor learning that emphasise readiness, practice, and carryover. Whilst the Bobath concept traditionally emphasises individualisation, the standardised protocol was necessary to ensure treatment fidelity and comparability across participants in this trial, with individualisation achieved through exercise selection and progression within each component rather than through variable time allocations.

Outcome measures

Spasticity was assessed using the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), which evaluates resistance to passive movement across major upper and lower limb joints. The MAS has demonstrated validity and reliability for assessing spasticity in either upper or lower limbs of individuals with spasticity [26, 27]. Assessments were conducted at baseline and after each 12-week treatment phase by blinded assessors.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarised baseline characteristics. Paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to assess within-group changes, while Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to compare endline differences between groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki/). Ethical clearance was obtained from the University of Zambia Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee (UNZAHSREC; ID: 20231270233) and the National Health Research Authority (NHRA; Registration No. NHRAR-R-1922/30/08/2024). The study was registered with the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry (PACTR202512685522786) in December 2024. Permission to conduct the study was granted by the Head of Clinical Care Administration at Livingstone University Teaching Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all caregivers before participation, with appropriate safeguards in place for this paediatric population, including the right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

The median age for participants in both groups was 2 years, with interquartile ranges of 1–2 years in the AB group and 1–3 years in the BA group (p = 0.5596).

Although these changes suggested a trend toward improvement, none reached statistical significance (p > 0.05) (Table 3). The results indicate that low-intensity therapy may be insufficient to produce clinically meaningful reductions in spasticity within 12 weeks.

Upper limb improvements were also observed, especially on the right side, although not all reached significance (Table 4).

Between-group comparison

At study end, the BA group demonstrated significantly lower spasticity in several right-sided upper limb joints compared to the AB group, including shoulder flexion (p = 0.021), shoulder extension (p = 0.019), elbow flexion (p = 0.021), elbow extension (p = 0.021), wrist flexion (p = 0.021), and wrist extension (p = 0.021), with median differences ranging from 0.5 to 1.0 points (Table 5). All statistically significant differences occurred on the right side, suggesting potential lateralised treatment effects. The left upper limb showed consistent trends favouring the BA sequence (p = 0.056) but did not reach statistical significance. Although lower limb differences were not statistically significant, the overall trend favoured the high-intensity sequence. These findings indicate that initiating treatment with high-intensity Modified Bobath therapy (BA sequence) resulted in superior long-term outcomes for upper limb spasticity compared to starting with Standard Bobath therapy (AB sequence), highlighting the importance of treatment sequencing in paediatric neurorehabilitation.

Discussion

This study provides evidence that therapy intensity significantly influences Bobath intervention effectiveness for children with spastic cerebral palsy. While both Standard and Modified Bobath therapies produced spasticity reductions, the high-intensity Modified protocol resulted in significantly greater improvements. These findings align with contemporary neuroplasticity research emphasising the importance of repetition, task-specific practice, and increased therapeutic dosage in promoting motor learning and cortical reorganisation [19, 25].

The significant improvements observed in lower limb joints may reflect the central role of proximal muscle groups in postural control and weight-bearing activities, heavily emphasised in the Modified Bobath protocol. Similar findings have been reported where children with spastic diplegia CP showed significant gross motor skill improvements following physical rehabilitation programmes incorporating Bobath therapy [28]. The pronounced improvements in right-sided upper limb joints may indicate asymmetrical responsiveness to therapy, a phenomenon reported in previous neurodevelopmental studies. This suggests therapy intensity may interact with lateralised motor control mechanisms, further supporting the need for individualised and intensive intervention strategies.

The crossover design strengthened internal validity by allowing each participant to serve as their own control [21]. Although no washout period was implemented, statistical analysis confirmed the absence of significant carryover effects. The study findings, therefore, provide credible evidence supporting high-intensity therapy superiority, consistent with systematic reviews indicating that while evidence on Bobath effectiveness is mixed, intensity and frequency are important moderating factors [12, 13].

Clinical implications

Rehabilitation programmes for children with spastic cerebral palsy should prioritise higher-intensity therapy schedules where feasible. This recommendation is supported by evidence showing children with CP who underwent Bobath therapy one to two times per week for six successive months had high mean quality of life scores after physiotherapy treatment [29], though more intensive protocols may produce even better outcomes. In low-resource settings such as Zambia, where staffing and equipment constraints may limit therapy frequency, these findings underscore the need for policy adjustments, caregiver training, and integration of structured home-based programmes to supplement facility-based therapy.

Limitations

Several methodological limitations constrain the interpretation and generalisability of these findings. First, the broad age range (3 months to 4 years) represents a fundamental design limitation rather than a minor methodological constraint. This wide developmental span encompasses substantial neurodevelopmental heterogeneity, including critical differences in abnormal motor pattern development, cortical maturation, and neuroplastic capacity between infants and preschool-aged children. Young infants are still forming primary motor engrams, whilst older children may have established maladaptive movement patterns that are more resistant to modification. These developmental differences likely influenced treatment responsiveness but could not be disaggregated in our analysis due to sample size constraints. Future studies should employ narrower, developmentally homogeneous age bands (e.g., 0–12 months, 12–24 months, 24–48 months) to account for these maturational factors.

Second, reliance on the Modified Ashworth Scale as the sole outcome measure represents a significant limitation. Whilst the MAS is validated and widely used [18, 30], it exhibits limited sensitivity to change, substantial inter-rater variability, and questionable validity in very young children and infants. The MAS measures resistance to passive movement but does not capture active motor function, functional abilities, or quality of life outcomes that may be more meaningful to families and clinicians. The scale’s subjective nature and ordinal scoring system limit its ability to detect small but clinically important changes. More objective measures such as neurophysiological assessments (e.g., H-reflex testing) or biomechanical evaluations (e.g., instrumented gait analysis) would provide more rigorous quantification of spasticity and movement quality. Additionally, functional outcome measures such as the Gross Motor Function Measure (GMFM), Paediatric Evaluation of Disability Inventory (PEDI), or quality-of-life instruments would better reflect real-world treatment impact.

Third, the crossover design without a formal washout period presents challenges in interpretation. Whilst caregivers did not consent to treatment interruption and withholding therapy was ethically untenable, the absence of a washout means we cannot definitively rule out carryover effects despite statistical testing. In a true crossover trial, each participant should serve as their own control with effects from the first treatment phase completely dissipated before the second phase begins. Our design does not meet this standard. An alternative approach would involve three arms: (1) a non-Bobath control receiving standard physiotherapy, (2) Standard Bobath therapy, and (3) Modified Bobath therapy, with participants randomised to a single arm rather than crossing over. This would eliminate carryover concerns whilst maintaining ethical provision of care.

Finally, additional limitations include the relatively small sample size (n = 30) from a single centre, which limits statistical power and generalisability; lack of objective monitoring of therapy adherence and home programme implementation; absence of long-term follow-up to assess durability of treatment effects; and the single-site setting, which may not reflect practice variability across different healthcare contexts. The study also did not assess quality of life, caregiver burden, or cost-effectiveness important outcomes for informing clinical and policy decisions in resource-limited settings.

Future research directions

Future research should incorporate larger multi trials, long-term follow-up assessments, and additional functional outcome measures such as the Gross Motor Function Measure (GMFM) and Goal Attainment Scaling (GAS). The GMFM has been identified as the most studied measure with strongest evidence of validity and responsiveness qualities in children with cerebral palsy [30]. Studies should also explore whether combining Bobath with adjunctive methods such as constraint-induced movement therapy or aquatic rehabilitation may augment results further [18]. Investigation of long-term retention of benefits from different sequencing approaches would provide valuable insights for clinical practice. Additionally, research should examine optimal timing for intervention initiation, given evidence that children starting physiotherapy at 5 months have better outcomes compared to those starting much later [23].

Conclusions

High-intensity Modified Bobath therapy is significantly more effective than low-intensity Standard Bobath therapy in reducing spasticity in young children with spastic cerebral palsy. These findings highlight the importance of therapy intensity in paediatric neurorehabilitation and support the exploration of higher-dose protocols in paediatric neurorehabilitation. In this crossover trial, the BA sequence (Modified Bobath followed by Standard Bobath) produced significant improvements in Modified Ashworth Scale scores, including a 28–35% reduction in upper limb spasticity and significant reductions in lower limb spasticity, particularly in the right upper limb. The AB sequence (Standard Bobath followed by Modified Bobath) showed more modest improvements of 12–18%. Whilst these findings suggest potential benefits of higher-intensity therapy and treatment sequencing, generalisation is limited by the wide age range (3 months to 4 years), reliance on the Modified Ashworth Scale as the sole outcome measure, absence of a formal washout period, and the small sample size from a single centre. Future research should employ narrower age bands, multiple outcome measures including functional assessments, and longer follow-up periods to establish the clinical significance and durability of these findings.
