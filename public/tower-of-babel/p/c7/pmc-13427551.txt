Cyclic stretch increases transglutaminase 2-mediated serotonylation of extracellular fibronectin in cultured optic nerve head astrocytes: a pilot study
Authors: Rajiv S Rangan, Ammar Kapic, Rafael do Valle, Chandra Lam, Pinkal D Patel, Jennifer H Pham, Ronse Raphi, Laszlo Prokai, Tara Tovar-Vidales, Abbot F Clark
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Glaucoma is a leading cause of blindness characterized by progressive degeneration of the optic nerve. Elevated intraocular pressure, typically associated with glaucoma, exerts biomechanical strain on the optic nerve head, where mechanosensitive astrocytes contribute to profibrotic extracellular matrix remodeling. Cross-linking enzymes such as transglutaminase 2 play a role in this process. A unique function of transglutaminase 2 is its ability to catalyze the post-translational modification of proteins through the transamidation of serotonin onto glutamine residues (“serotonylation”). The serotonylation of extracellular matrix proteins, such as fibronectin, could plausibly be involved in extracellular matrix remodeling. Here, we investigated whether optic nerve head astrocytes could serotonylate extracellular proteins in response to a biomechanical insult.Results: Primary human optic nerve head astrocytes subjected to 0–12% cyclic stretch for 24 h exhibited increased extracellular levels of transglutaminase 2 and serotonin. Correspondingly, serotonylation of immunoprecipitated fibronectin samples, detected by immunoblotting, was 2.5-fold higher in stretched cells. Serotonylation of specific glutaminyl residues of fibronectin due to the catalytic effect of transglutaminase 2 was also proven though an in vitro experiment using nanoflow liquid chromatography–electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS).Conclusions: These findings demonstrate that fibronectin can be serotonylated by optic nerve head astrocytes and that this modification is enhanced by biomechanical stress. While transglutaminase 2 is classically associated with extracellular matrix cross-linking, these results suggest that serotonylation may represent an additional and unexplored mechanism by which transglutaminase 2 contributes to extracellular matrix remodeling in the glaucomatous optic nerve head.Keywords: Glaucoma, Optic nerve head astrocytes, Transglutaminase 2, Extracellular matrix, Stretch, Serotonylation, Fibronectin

Background

The creation and processing of visual information begins in the back of the eye at the neural retina when light passing through the eye is transduced into electrochemical signals by photoreceptors [1], which are then transmitted through interneurons to retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) [2]. RGCs are responsible for sending these signals to the brain, projecting long axons primarily to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, which in turn relays the signals to the visual cortex [3]. Along this path, RGC axons are initially unmyelinated as they travel radially across the surface of the retina towards the optic nerve head (ONH), a region located slightly nasal to the center of the retina. At this convergence, the axons make a 90° turn to pass through the fenestrated tissue structures of the ONH. Only as the axons exit the ONH do they become myelinated, forming the robust optic nerve proper [4]. A degree of precarity might be ascribed to these bare RGC axons as they navigate the ONH, but here they are protected by a dense network of astrocytes. ONH astrocytes are white matter fibrous astrocytes typified by a stellate morphology under quiescent conditions. Their processes envelop bundles of RGC axons, providing them with functional and structural support [5]. Crucially, structural support shields RGC axons against strain from the physiologic biomechanical stresses created by normal fluctuations of pressure within the eye – intraocular pressure (IOP) [6], [7].

Meanwhile, in the front of the eye, biological processes are oriented towards maintaining the refractive properties and clear quality of the anterior chamber, so that focused light may reach the back of the eye free of optical distortions [8]. Here, a clear fluid produced by the ciliary body (aqueous humor) carries nutrients to avascular ocular tissues, such as the cornea and lens, before draining out of the eye, predominantly through the trabecular meshwork, a porous 360° strip of tissue seated within the iridocorneal angle [9]. To maintain the optimal shape and refractiveness of the eye, fluid volume and IOP are balanced by aqueous humor outflow that is commensurate to its production. The trabecular meshwork regulates the outflow of aqueous humor by providing tunable resistance, with IOP driving aqueous humor through the trabecular meshwork. Consequently, increased tissue resistance to aqueous humor outflow at the trabecular meshwork can drive increased IOP [8], [10]. IOP is typically maintained at approximately 15 mmHg, but pathological changes to the structure and function of the trabecular meshwork can cause intractable increases in resistance that drive supraphysiologic elevations in IOP [11], [12]. Elevated IOP produces biomechanical stresses that are transmitted throughout the eye; the resulting mechanical strains at vulnerable ocular tissues can instigate pathological tissue remodeling [13], [14].

At the ONH, elevated IOP causes backward bowing of ONH substructures and subjects the tissue to a variety of extension, compression and shearing strains [15], [16]. ONH astrocytes are biomechanically responsive cells that respond to elevated IOP by engaging in profibrotic extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, which ultimately leads to the thickening and stiffening of connective tissue elements [17], [18]. The fibrosis of the ONH contributes to progressive axonal damage and degeneration, which proceeds to RGC cell body death in the retina [19], [20], [21], [22]. The resulting progressive loss of vision characterizes glaucoma, a major group of optic neuropathies and leading cause of irreversible vision loss and blindness [23]. While glaucoma can have variable etiologies and presentations, it typically manifests as a slow, progressive loss of vision that starts in the periphery of the visual field. Glaucoma is highly associated with elevated IOP and can often be clinically managed by medical or surgical interventions that reduce IOP [24]. But not all glaucoma patients adequately respond to IOP-lowering regimens, leaving them at risk of continued vision loss and blindness. Since the ONH is the initial site of damage to RGCs, understanding how pathological ONH remodeling occurs during glaucoma is crucial to efforts to develop new therapeutics aimed at protecting the optic nerve and retina from glaucomatous damage [25], [26], [27], [28], [29].

Protein cross-linking enzymes appear to play a role in ECM stiffening of the glaucomatous ONH. Since the human ONH is composed of collagenous connective tissue structures, it is expected that lysyl oxidases and transglutaminases would be major enzymatic cross-linkers in this region [30], [31], [32]. Evidencing this is the finding that transforming growth factor β2 (TGF-β2) treatment increases the expression of transglutaminase 2 in cultured ONH astrocytes [33]. TGF-β2 is a profibrotic cytokine that is elevated in the aqueous humor, trabecular meshwork, and ONH of patients with glaucoma; TGF-β2 has been highly implicated in the molecular pathology of glaucoma throughout the eye [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]. As such, the response of ONH astrocytes to TGF-β2 suggests that increased transglutaminase 2 expression is likely involved in glaucomatous ONH remodeling. Transglutaminase 2 (also known as tissue transglutaminase, TGase 2, tTG, TG2, TGM2) is a calcium-dependent transamidase that covalently cross-links the lysine ε-amino group of one protein and the glutamine γ-carboxamide group of another protein [41], [42], [43]. While cellular TGM2 is often inactive due to the low availability of intracellular calcium, it can be secreted into the extracellular space, where abundant calcium can enable its activity [44], [45]. Although understudied, it seems likely that extracellular TGM2 could play an active role in cross-linking extracellular proteins during glaucoma, reducing their turnover and stiffening the ONH matrix.

Intriguingly, lysine-glutamine cross-linking is not the only mechanism by which TGM2 can modify proteins as an active transamidase. TGM2 is also known to transamidate the primary amine groups of small, biogenic monoamines such as serotonin to the glutamine residues of proteins (“monoaminylation” or, specifically, “serotonylation”) [46]. Serotonin, most commonly known for its function as a neurotransmitter, is present in the ONH [47], [48], [49], and is a potent profibrotic mediator in its own right due to its roles in peripheral wound healing [50], [51], [52]. We wondered if, through serotonylation, TGM2 and serotonin might contribute to ECM remodeling in the ONH by modifying extracellular proteins. Serotonylation has been reported as a modification for a variety of proteins, including prominent ECM proteins like fibronectin. In C6 glioma cell cultures, TGM2 serotonylates extracellular fibronectin and promotes aggregation of ECM proteins [53]. Similarly, in cultured aortic smooth muscle cells, fibronectin serotonylation appears to be involved in proliferative cell responses, which may be involved in the pathological remodeling of the pulmonary artery during the development of pulmonary hypertension [54], [55]. Given these extant findings, we sought to examine whether ONH astrocytes were capable of serotonylating ECM proteins and if this process could plausibly be involved in glaucomatous profibrotic ECM remodeling. In this study, we expose cultured human ONH astrocytes to a glaucomatous biomechanical insult and probe for evidence of ECM serotonylation (Fig. 1).

Methods

Cell culture

Primary human optic nerve head (ONH) astrocytes were isolated from human donor eyes and cultured using previously described protocols [56], [57]. In brief, donor eyes were obtained from the UNTHSC Center for Anatomical Sciences within 24 h post-mortem (handled with adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki). The whole globes were bisected and ONH tissues were carefully dissected from the posterior cups. ONH explant cultures were maintained on 12-well plates with Ham's F10 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.3 mg/mL l-glutamine and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2). Once cells migrated out of the explanted tissues and reached confluence, cultures were dissociated with trypsin and replated in serum-free astrocyte basal medium (ScienCell, Carlsbad, CA) to allow for the selective attachment and growth of astrocytes. After 72 h, ONH astrocytes were maintained in astrocyte basal medium supplemented with 5% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cell types were confirmed by immunocytological characterization of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and neural cell adhesion molecule 1 (NCAM/CD56) production [56]. Three biologically distinct primary human ONH astrocyte strains isolated from three separate donors (79-year-old White male, 83-year-old White female, 71-year-old White male) who were free of any ocular diseases were used in all proceeding experiments (biological n = 3). ONH astrocytes were used between passages 4–5. The same three sets of primary cells were used in all control and experimental groups and were maintained under identical culture conditions until the start of any experiment. As such, cell strains will be treated as paired in our analysis to account for any variability produced by their unique backgrounds [58].

Stretch experiments

ONH astrocytes were plated onto BioFlex 6-well flexible-bottom culture plates precoated with collagen IV (Flexcell, Burlington, NC) and allowed to grow to confluence under normal cell culture conditions. Once confluent, cells were serum-deprived for 48 h. Culture medium was then exchanged with serum-free medium supplemented with either 1 ng/mL (for most proceeding experiments) or 10 ng/mL (for ELISA) of serotonin hydrochloride (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA). [Astrocytes are expected to be exposed to the low concentrations of serotonin present in the neural tissue within the posterior segment of the eye; the level of serotonin in the mammalian retina is not well established, but estimates range from 1 to 20 ng/g [59], [60]. As such, we believed the selected serotonin concentrations would be reasonably physiologic. A higher concentration was used in experiments upstream of an ELISA to bring final sample concentrations into an appropriate range of detection.] At this point, the BioFlex culture plates were placed onto a baseplate attached to a FlexCell FX-6000 T programmable tension system. The system exerts a regulated vacuum under the flexible culture surface, pulling it over a firm loading post. This results in an equibiaxial stretch exerted on the culture surface and the adherent cells. We programmed the system to exert 0–12% stretch cyclically at a frequency of 1 Hz (sinusoidal waveform). These parameters were designed to mimic a glaucomatous biomechanical insult, as described by other research groups [61]. Twelve percent stretch is sufficient to elicit pathophysiologic responses in ONH astrocyte cultures at a 24-h timepoint and 1 Hz emulates the ocular pulse typically experienced in the ONH, such that maximal stress is exerted every second [62]. ONH astrocytes were maintained under 0–12% cyclic stretch for 24 h. Control cells were maintained at 0% stretch for 24 h in parallel (i.e., grown on the same type of culture plates and kept on the same apparatus in the same incubator during the experimental period but not exposed to any vacuum-induced strain). When appropriate, cells would additionally receive 1 μM of the TGM2 inhibitor ZED1227 (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ) or the corresponding vehicle (0.05% dimethyl sulfoxide, DMSO). ZED1227, “Vilagletistat”, is a highly selective and highly potent peptidomimetic TGM2 irreversible inhibitor with low cytotoxicity and low cell permeability [63]. After 24 h, samples of conditioned medium were collected and cells were fixed. Large cell debris was cleared from conditioned medium by centrifugation at 10,000 RCF for 5 min.

Serotonin Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Serotonin levels from conditioned medium samples were quantified using a commercially available competitive ELISA kit (catalog # ADI-900-175, Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY), according to the manufacturer's instructions. This assay is based on competition between serotonin in samples and an alkaline phosphatase-conjugated serotonin tracer for binding to anti-serotonin antibodies immobilized on the assay plate, with resulting signal intensities inversely proportional to sample serotonin concentration.

Serotonin standards (0.49–500 ng/mL) were prepared and run in parallel with undiluted conditioned medium samples, each in duplicate. Sample concentrations were determined by interpolation from the generated standard curve.

We attempted to quantify intracellular serotonin levels through similar means but found that serotonin levels were below the detection limit of this ELISA method in our cell lysates.

Immunofluorescence

After 24 h of stretch exposure, ONH astrocytes were washed 3× with warm phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were then fixed with a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 15 min, washed with PBS, and permeabilized with a solution of 0.2% Triton-X 100 in PBS at room temperature for 20 min. Once permeabilized, cells were blocked with SuperBlock blocking buffer (ThermoFisher) for 1 h at room temperature before overnight incubation at 4 °C in a 1:200 solution of an anti-serotonin transporter (SERT) primary antibody in SuperBlock (catalog # PA5–89657, ThermoFisher); “no primary” controls for secondary antibody nonspecificity were incubated in SuperBlock with no primary antibody. Cells were washed 1× with PBST (PBS with 0.1% Tween 20) and 3× with PBS for 5 min each with gentle agitation prior to incubation with a 1:500 solution of an Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then washed 1× with PBST and 3× with PBS for 5 min each with gentle agitation before nuclear counterstaining with Hoechst 33342 (1:10,000 in water, ThermoFisher). Cells were kept in PBS for fluorescence imaging directly on BioFlex plates with a Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted microscope attached to a Lumen200 lamp (Prior Scientific, Cambridge, UK). Image acquisition was performed using the Nuance FX multispectral imaging system (CRi). Image processing was carried out using the Nuance FX software and ImageJ.

Immunoblotting

Proteins from conditioned medium samples were concentrated and prepared by adding 25 μL of StrataClean Resin (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) to 500 μL of conditioned medium and vortexing for 1 min [64]. The resin was then centrifuged (800 RCF, 1 min) and supernatant aspirated. 100 μL denaturing (non-reducing) Laemmli SDS sample buffer was mixed with the resin and the slurry was heated at 95 °C for 5 min before being loaded onto precast NuPage Bis-Tris 4–12% polyacrylamide gels (ThermoFisher).

Using the Invitrogen mini gel electrophoresis apparatus, gels were run for 30 min at 80 V followed by 2 h at 120 V in MOPS running buffer at room temperature. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes at 24 V for 2 h in chilled NuPage transfer buffer with 10% methanol at room temperature. Membranes were blocked for 1 h in SuperBlock or 5% milk in PBST as appropriate and then incubated overnight at 4 °C in primary antibody solutions: anti-TGM2 (1:1,000, ab109200, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-serotonin (1:2,000, S5545, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), or anti-fibronectin (1:2,000, ab2413, Abcam). Afterwards, the membranes were washed 3× with PBST for 15 min, incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000, ThermoFisher) for 1 h at room temperature, and washed again 3× with PBST for 15 min. Immunoblots were visualized using a ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (BioRad, Hercules, CA) after 30–60 s of HRP substrate incubation. Band integrated density values were measured with ImageJ. As appropriate, either PageRuler plus or Spectra high-range protein ladders (ThermoFisher) were used as standards.

When immunoblotting for multiple targets on one membrane was required, membranes were stripped with Restore Western Blot Stripping Buffer (ThermoFisher) for 15 min at room temperature, then washed and re-probed. When total protein staining was required for normalization, gels (run in parallel) were Coomassie stained. Coomassie staining was performed by washing polyacrylamide gels 3× in water for 5 min each immediately after electrophoresis. Gels were then fixed in a 10:40:50 solution of acetic acid, methanol and water before being immersed in GelCode Blue protein stain (ThermoFisher) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, gels were destained by washing in water for 1–2 h at room temperature.

In vitro serotonylation assay

To validate the ability of our selected anti-serotonin antibody to detect serotonylated ECM proteins via immunoblotting, we prepared serotonylated fibronectin samples for testing by reacting human plasma-derived fibronectin, recombinant human TGM2 (rhTGM2), and serotonin under controlled conditions.

Ten μg fibronectin (catalog # 1918-FN, Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN) was incubated with 5 μg rhTGM2 (catalog # 4376-TG, Bio-Techne) and 5 mM serotonin hydrochloride (ThermoFisher) in TGM2 reaction buffer (25 mM Tris with 5 mM CaCl2, pH = 8.0) at a final volume of 30 μL for 6 h at 37 °C. Reactions run without serotonin, without TGM2, or with a final concentration of 1.35 μM of the TGM2 inhibitor ZED1227 served as negative controls, and 3% DMSO was added to all non-inhibitor reactions as a vehicle control. These protocols were similar to those described elsewhere [65].

After the 6-h incubation period, reaction mixtures were mixed 1:1 with 2× Laemmli SDS denaturing sample buffer (non-reducing), incubated for 10 min at room temperature, and loaded onto NuPage Bis-Tris 4–12% polyacrylamide gels for Western blotting as described above.

Validation of In Vitro Fibronectin Serotonylation via LC–MS/MS

To ensure that our in vitro reaction described above successfully produced serotonylated fibronectin, completed reaction mixtures were analyzed via nanoflow liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS).

Thirty μL aliquots of unmodified fibronectin and serotonylated fibronectin were prepared as described in the previous section. These samples were denatured with 8 M urea for 1 h at room temperature, then reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for 1 h at 56 °C. After cooling to room temperature, samples were alkylated with 5 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) for 30 min in the dark. Samples were diluted to achieve a urea concentration of <2 M before the addition of MS-grade trypsin (ThermoFisher) to digest samples overnight at 37 °C. Trypsin was quenched with formic acid, and the digested peptides were desalted using Sep-Pak C-18 cartridges (Waters, Milford, MA). Peptide samples were eluted with acetonitrile (ACN), then dried in a vacuum centrifuge concentrator and stored at −80 °C until prepared for analysis.

Dried samples were reconstituted in a 5% (v/v) aqueous solution of ACN containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. Reconstituted samples were analyzed using data-dependent acquisition (DDA) on an Exploris 480 mass spectrometer equipped with an EASY-Spray source coupled to a Vanquish Neo ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system (ThermoFisher). Sample aliquots were injected onto a ThermoFisher Scientific Acclaim PepMap 100 Å octadecylsilica (C18) trap column (2 cm × 75 μm i.d., packing particle size of 3 μm) at 1 μL/min, followed by elution and reversed-phase separation on an Acclaim™ PepMap™ 100 Å C18 packed column (15 cm × 75 μm i.d., packing particle size of 2 μm) at 300 nL/min using a 100 min gradient program (elution: 5 to 40% v/v ACN in water, containing 0.1% v/v formic acid). Nanoelectrospray source was set to a positive mode at 3.0 kV. Full-scan MS was acquired at a resolution of 120,000 with the top 20 MS/MS spectra acquired using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD), with only multiply charged precursor ions (z ≥ 2) selected for fragmentation. After selecting the ion to be fragmented, dynamic exclusion was set for 30 s.

RAW files were initially processed using Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (ThermoFisher). Parameters for both searches were set as follows: 25 ppm parent ion tolerance, 0.80 Da fragment ion mass tolerance, trypsin as the protease, and a maximum of two missed cleavages. Fixed modifications for the search included cysteine carbamidomethylation, while variable modifications included methionine oxidation, N-terminal acetylation, and glutamine serotonylation. The MS/MS spectra were searched against the Homo sapiens UniProt protein sequence database using the Mascot search engine (Matrix Science, Boston, MA) in Proteome Discoverer 2.4, and the outputs were validated by Scaffold 5 (Proteome Software, Portland, OR) relying on its built-in MSFragger algorithm and requiring > 95% probability at the peptide level by Peptide Prophet and > 99% probability at the protein level by Protein Prophet. Representative spectra were annotated using the Interactive Peptide Spectral Annotator (IPSA, Coon Research Group, University of Wisconsin-Madison) [66].

Fibronectin immunoprecipitation

To specifically examine the serotonylation of fibronectin, fibronectin was enriched from conditioned medium via immunoprecipitation. After clearing samples of cell debris by centrifugation (10,000 RCF, 5 min), a rabbit polyclonal anti-fibronectin antibody (1:100, ab2413, Abcam) and Halt protease inhibitor (1%, ThermoFisher) were added to 500 μL conditioned medium, which were incubated on a rotating platform at 4 °C overnight. 0.25 mg of pre-blocked, pre-washed Pierce protein A/G magnetic beads (ThermoFisher) were then added to the samples and incubated on a rotating platform at room temperature for 1 h. Beads were collected with a magnetic stand and then washed 3× with Pierce IP wash buffer for 5 min each and 1× with H2O for 1 min. Proteins were eluted from beads via a 10-min room temperature incubation in 100 μL denaturing SDS buffer (Pierce Lane Marker Sample Buffer; non-reducing) with agitation.

Statistical analysis

Data normality were tested using a Shapiro-Wilk test. While our data generally “passed” this normality test, it should be noted that with an n of 3, our experiments are weakly powered to reject the null hypothesis of population normality [67]. All quantitative data are reported as means ± standard deviation (SD). As appropriate, means were compared using either a two-tailed paired t-test or a repeated measures ANOVA (RM ANOVA, sphericity assumed, with multiple comparisons assessed by Tukey's HSD test). On graphs, all differences associated with p-values below 0.05 are denoted by a single asterisk (*), without further stratification by significance level. Actual p-values are reported in the results text. All analyses were carried out on GraphPad Prism 10 software.

Results

Higher serotonin concentrations are maintained in the culture medium of stretched ONH astrocytes

Prior to stretch experiments, ONH astrocyte culture medium was supplemented with serotonin to serve as a substrate for TGM2-mediated serotonylation of extracellular proteins. To confirm that supplemented serotonin remained present in the extracellular environment and could serve as a substrate for TGM2-mediated serotonylation, serotonin concentrations were measured in conditioned medium collected after 24 h of stretch exposure.

Extracellular serotonin levels were quantified using a competitive ELISA. After 24 h, serotonin remained detectable in medium from both control cells and cells exposed to 0–12% cyclic stretch. The mean extracellular serotonin concentration was 2.97 ± 0.84 ng/mL in control cells and 7.29 ± 0.25 ng/mL in stretched cells. The difference between the groups was statistically significant (mean of differences = 4.32 ± 1.02 ng/mL, p = 0.018), indicating that stretched ONH astrocytes may maintain higher levels of extracellular serotonin (Fig. 2).

We suspected that decreased serotonin uptake driven by changes in the serotonin transporter (SERT) may have been responsible for this difference in final extracellular serotonin concentrations; however, we observed no clear change in SERT levels or localization in stretched ONH astrocytes (Fig. 3). It is plausible that a change in SERT function or an alternate mechanism may be involved, instead. Serotonylation may also itself influence serotonin detection, as the covalent binding of serotonin's primary amine terminus likely leaves some of serotonin's immunogenic sites exposed for antibody binding [68].

Stretch increases extracellular transglutaminase 2 levels

The presence of extracellular TGM2 was probed on immunoblots of concentrated conditioned medium samples. The major specific band observed at ∼80 kDa is characteristic of TGM2 (Fig. 4A). The TGM2 antibody used to produce these blots has previously been independently validated to specifically detect an 80 kDa TGM2 band but is known to produce a nonspecific 50 kDa band [69]; we observed a prominent nonspecific and uncharacterized band at ∼50–60 kDa. Additional high molecular weight bands are thought to be TGM2 bound to higher molecular weight ECM proteins, to which TGM2 can be cross-linked [70], [71].

Numbering denotes independent cell strains. B) Integrated density values of ∼80 kDa TGM2 bands normalized against corresponding total protein bands. Error bars represent SD, * indicates statistical significance (p < 0.05; paired t-test).

TGM2 protein levels were 2.57-fold higher (p = 0.025) in concentrated conditioned medium samples from ONH astrocytes exposed to 0–12% cyclic stretch for 24 h when compared to corresponding controls (Fig. 4B). This suggests that stretch elevates extracellular TGM2 levels. We suspect that this is the result of increased TGM2 secretion but other molecular responses, such as decreased proteolytic degradation, could be involved in or responsible for this effect.

Extracellular protein serotonylation appears to be elevated after stretch

To examine the potential response of extracellular TGM2-mediated serotonylation to stretch, we treated cells under both control and stretched conditions with 1 μM of the TGM2 inhibitor ZED1227 and then probed for evidence of serotonin bound to extracellular proteins. On immunoblots of concentrated total protein from conditioned medium samples, we observed that the signal from an anti-serotonin antibody was most prominently and consistently associated with high molecular weight protein bands (>270 kDa) (Fig. 5A). We suspected that a portion of this signal represented serotonin bound to fibronectin and quantified the serotonin signal detected at these heavy bands against corresponding fibronectin bands (Fig. 5B). The serotonin signal was increased 1.36-fold in ONH astrocytes exposed to 0–12% cyclic stretch for 24 h over controls (p = 0.010). In stretched cells, TGM2 inhibitor treatment decreased serotonin signal by 1.47-fold (p = 0.004). Taken together, these observations indicate that stretch increases fibronectin-associated serotonin signal and that this association is TGM2-dependent. Therefore, stretch plausibly stimulates fibronectin serotonylation. While these observations were statistically significant, the effects are modest. This can be partly attributed to broad serotonin bands and inconsistent fibronectin levels across our samples.

To more robustly demonstrate and clearly quantify fibronectin serotonylation in response to stretch, we decided to enrich fibronectin from control and stretched cells before again probing for serotonylation. But first, we wanted to validate that our serotonin antibody was actually capable of selectively binding serotonylated fibronectin.

In vitro validation of serotonin immunoblotting as a means of detecting serotonylated fibronectin

To validate our approach for detecting serotonylated proteins using an anti-serotonin antibody, fibronectin samples were prepared under conditions permissive for TGM2-mediated serotonylation as well as control conditions in which serotonylation was absent or inhibited. Following Western blotting, the anti-serotonin antibody produced clear bands that overlapped with fibronectin (Fig. 6). These bands were essentially absent in samples lacking serotonin. In samples containing serotonin but no TGM2, a faint serotonin signal was observed, likely reflecting nonspecific interactions. Additionally, inclusion of a TGM2 inhibitor attenuated the serotonin signal associated with fibronectin bands in samples containing both TGM2 and serotonin.

Western blotting was performed using samples of human fibronectin (10 μg) incubated with 1) 5 μg rhTGM2 alone, 2) 5 mM serotonin alone, 3) 5 μg rhTGM2 + 5 mM serotonin, or 4) 5 μg rhTGM2 + 5 mM serotonin +1.35 μM ZED1227 (TGM2 inhibitor). From left to right is shown a representative blot probed for serotonin, a blot probed for fibronectin, and a gel stained for total protein.

Total fibronectin levels were consistent across conditions, as confirmed by fibronectin immunoblotting and total protein staining (Fig. 6). Notably, fibronectin migration patterns were altered in the presence of active TGM2, with bands retained at higher molecular weights, consistent with TGM2-mediated protein cross-linking.

These results suggest that the selected anti-serotonin antibody is capable of specifically binding serotonylated proteins when immunoblotting. To confirm that the samples exhibiting serotonylation signal contained serotonylated peptides, samples of unmodified and modified fibronectin were analyzed via LC–MS/MS. Several peptides from fibronectin samples serotonylated in vitro were found to contain glutamine residues with 159 Da serotonyl marks (176 Da serotonin mass – 17 Da NH3 mass) (Fig. 7). In control samples, there was no detection of serotonylation on any fibronectin peptides. Representative spectra demonstrate clear 79.5 (159/2) mass-to-charge (m/z) shifts between doubly charged parent ions for serotonylated fibronectin peptides and corresponding peptides from control samples (Fig. 8). Taken together, our western blot and LC-MS/MS results indicate that TGM2 can serotonylate fibronectin in vitro and that serotonylated fibronectin can be detected at appropriate bands on immunoblots using an anti-serotonin antibody. These findings are consistent with prior reports using similar approaches [[72], [73], [74], [75], [76], [77], [78], [79], [80], [81]].

Discussion

In this study, we determined whether transglutaminase could mediate serotonylation of extracellular proteins in cultured ONH astrocytes subjected to a glaucoma-like biomechanical stress in the form of 0–12% cyclic stretch. Our primary finding was that fibronectin serotonylation increased following 24 h of stretch. Although we do not directly demonstrate that secreted TGM2 mediates the serotonylation of fibronectin within the extracellular space, our observations of elevated extracellular TGM2 protein levels, increased extracellular serotonin concentrations, and reduced serotonylation in the presence of a low-permeability, selective TGM2 inhibitor are consistent with this mechanism.

Fibronectin is a large ECM glycoprotein that is secreted by cells as a dimer composed of two ∼250 kDa subunits linked at their C-termini by disulfide bonds. Alternative splicing can produce many fibronectin isoforms, allowing multi-adhesive fibronectin glycoproteins to participate in a wide range of biological functions [82]. At the cell surface, fibronectin tethered to integrins can polymerize to form a complex fibrillar matrix that serves as a scaffold for the deposition of collagen and other ECM components, highlighting its role in ECM organization [83]. Fibronectin is a component of various structures within the ONH and is prominently localized around vascular structures, within connective tissue plates in the lamina cribrosa, and within the peripapillary sclera [84], [85], [86], [87], [88], [89], [90]. In genetic models of glaucoma, elevated fibronectin levels in the ONH and optic nerve proper appear to be involved in profibrotic tissue remodeling [91], [92]. In human glaucoma eyes, there is increased expression of fibronectin and the fibronectin FN-EDA isoform in the ONH [93]. It is therefore reasonable to consider that post-translational modifications, including monoaminylation and serotonylation, may influence fibronectin function and ECM structure.

Serotonylation has been shown to influence the function of a wide range of proteins. TGM2 exhibits a broad substrate specificity and can serotonylate potentially thousands of proteins [94]. Notably, serotonylation has been found to influence transcription through the modification of histones, cytoskeletal dynamics through the modification of actin-associated proteins, metabolism through the modification of GAPDH, among other processes [65], [95], [96]. As previously discussed, the serotonylation of fibronectin has been demonstrated to promote ECM aggregation and other processes related to pathologic tissue remodeling in cellulo [53], [54], [55]. Our in vitro results may suggest how serotonylation might influence fibronectin form and function. For instance, several of our detected serotonylated peptides were in or near the N-terminal region of fibronectin. This is consistent with previous reports of TGM2's high efficiency for modifying and crosslinking glutamines of the fibronectin N-terminal [71]. This region is of critical importance for fibronectin assembly, so it is possible that serotonylation in this region could influence the final structure of fibrillar matrices [83], [97], [98]. Other modified peptides were detected more centrally or towards the C-terminal region, which could influence the binding of fibronectin, respectively, to the surfaces of cells or to other molecules including growth factors, heparins and fibrin [82].

ONH astrocytes are mechanically sensitive cells that contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling during the pathogenesis of glaucoma [99]. In this context, our finding that cyclic stretch increases fibronectin serotonylation suggests that biomechanical stress may regulate such TGM2-mediated post-translational modification of ECM components. Given the established roles of fibronectin in matrix assembly and fibrosis, serotonylation may represent a mechanism by which TGM2 activity influences ECM organization in the glaucomatous ONH. While further studies are required to define the functional consequences of this modification, these findings support a broader hypothesis that monoaminylation of extracellular proteins may contribute to tissue remodeling in glaucoma.

This study serves as a pilot investigation into the potential involvement of serotonylation in the response of ONH astrocytes to biomechanical stress. Technical and conceptual limitations of our study should be acknowledged.

First, we did not confirm the TGM2-dependence of fibronectin serotonylation in our immunoprecipitated samples, where this effect was most prominently observed, because we encountered issues with ZED1227 interfering with immunoprecipitation efficiency. In follow-up studies we intend to employ genetic approaches, such as knockdown or knockout of TGM2, to more definitively establish TGM2's role in the serotonylation of extracellular proteins.

Second, our analysis focused on fibronectin as an initial target for extracellular serotonylation due to the prominence of serotonin signal observed at higher molecular weight bands when Western blotting. However, serotonin-associated signals were also detected at lower molecular weights, suggesting that additional ECM proteins are likely to be subject to serotonylation. Additional work will be needed to define the complete extracellular serotonylome and characterize its response to biomechanical stresses.

Finally, our biomechanical model represents an approximation of glaucomatous stress. Glaucoma patients typically exhibit IOPs of >21 mmHg [100]. ∼15% stretch stress is expected to model an IOP of ∼50 mmHg [101], [102], [103], [104], [105], [106]. Utilizing the Flexcell to exert 0–12% cyclic stretch at a 1 Hz frequency for 24 h simulates a severe, acute glaucomatous pressure elevation and has been demonstrated to stimulate glaucoma-like responses in ONH cells [61], [107]. This should only serve as a starting point in the investigation of glaucomatous biomechanical strain, as it cannot completely recapitulate the complex biomechanics of the ONH which are chronically subjected to circumferential stresses from adjacent scleral tissues, translaminar pressure differentials, shear stresses. [15], [108], [109], [110], [111]. Additionally, the substratum stiffness of the BioFlex culture plates (930 kPa) exceeds that of native ONH tissue structures and may independently influence cellular responses [112], [113], [114]. The culture plate coating itself is another variable that must be considered. We utilized collagen IV coated plates to partially mimic the ECM organization of the ONH, as collagen IV produced by astrocytes is a major component of the basement membrane in the ONH [115]. Plates coated with other ECM proteins are likely to influence the ONH astrocyte phenotype and potentially modulate experimental results [116]. To fully characterize how biomechanical and ECM dynamics alter serotonylation by ONH astrocytes, further experiments will need to include additional timepoints, substrate types, and degrees of strain.

A closing point of discussion concerns the biological context of serotonylation within the ONH. While serotonin is present in neural tissues of the mammalian eye, its abundance within the ONH is likely limited. Serotonin has been detected in the retina, where it may be produced by tryptophan hydroxylase positive amacrine cells and taken up by bipolar cells [117], [118]. But within the ONH it appears restricted to a subset of retinopetal axons and vascular structures [47], [48], [49]. Given that both serotonin and fibronectin are associated with ONH vasculature, it is conceivable that serotonin could participate in fibronectin serotonylation near blood vessels. However, whether endogenous serotonin levels in the ONH are sufficient to drive meaningful serotonylation of ECM proteins, especially if in the presence of other competing substrates, remains an open question. In addition, the findings presented here may extend beyond serotonin specifically. TGM2 is capable of monoaminylating proteins utilizing a variety of biogenic amines, including dopamine and histamine, which may be more abundant in the retina and optic nerve [119], [120]. As such, serotonylation can be viewed as a representative example of a broader class of TGM2-mediated modifications that may influence ECM biology.

Importantly, even independent of monoaminylation, our results demonstrate that TGM2 activity is responsive to biomechanical stress in ONH astrocytes. This is consistent with findings in other tissues showing that injury can promote transient TGM2 activation through its release into calcium-rich extracellular environments [44]. Notably, even the canonical cross-linking functions of TGM2 in the ONH remain unstudied. Further investigation of TGM2-mediated cross-linking, monoaminylation, and related activities in this tissue may reveal important mechanisms of ECM remodeling and identify novel therapeutic targets for glaucoma.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings demonstrate that transglutaminase 2 produced by optic nerve head astrocytes can serotonylate fibronectin, and that this activity is enhanced by biomechanical stress. Given the central role of fibronectin in extracellular matrix organization, these results highlight a potentially unexplored mechanism by which glaucomatous biomechanical stresses could promote remodeling of the optic nerve head. While further work will be required to define the functional consequences and in vivo relevance of fibronectin serotonylation, this study provides an initial characterization of a transglutaminase 2-mediated post-translational modification as a plausible contributor to glaucoma pathophysiology.
