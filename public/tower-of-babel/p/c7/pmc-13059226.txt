The impact of parental divorce on the comorbidity of depression and social anxiety symptoms in adolescents and young adults: evidence from symptom and Bayesian network analysis
Authors: Xinyuan Zou, Yichao Lv, Shujian Wang, Liang Zhang, Zijuan Ma, Gang Liu, Yanqiang Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDivorce has become a fact for more families and could bring unfavorable outcomes in offspring’s mental health. Yet, the relation between parental divorce and children’s depression and social anxiety remains unclear. The current study, adopting the symptomatic perspective, aims to identify the symptomatic characteristics and developmental trajectories of depression and social anxiety related to parental divorce.MethodsThis study used a convenience sampling method, collecting data from 5,372 college students, and after the filtering process, 3,617 participants (females = 2303 (63.6%), Mean age = 18.8) were left for further analysis. The patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and social anxiety scale (SAS) were used. Participants were divided into two groups according to their parents’ marital status. Both groups’ symptomatic network and directed acyclic graph network were constructed.ResultsThe divorced group scored significantly higher on all symptoms except “Concentration” (PHQ7), “Motor” (PHQ8), “Suicide” (PHQ9), and “Anxiety in unfamiliar situation” (SAS2). “Guilt”(PHQ-6) appears to be the most prominent symptomatic feature with the highest bridge expected influence value. The robust triggering relation exists between “Anxiety in unfamiliar situation” and “Anxiety in normal situation” (SAS3), and does not differ between the two groups.Conclusion“Guilt” (PHQ-6) was identified as the symptomatic characteristic related to parental divorce, and the generalization of anxiety from specific situations to unfamiliar situations exists across the two groups. The findings suggest that improving self-esteem and reducing feelings of guilt and shame may help alleviate both depression and social anxiety, especially for adolescents who have experienced parental divorce.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26776-9.Keywords: Parental divorce, Depression, Social anxiety, Network analysis, Bayesian model

Introduction

Parental divorce and its influence on children’s mental health

The past twenty years have witnessed a drastic rise in the divorce rate (from 1% in 2002 to more than 2% in 2023 in China) [1]. More specifically, in the third quarter of 2025 in China, over two million couples filed for divorce, a figure which accounted for nearly half of the number of marriage registrations during the same period [2]. Parental divorce, viewed as the most common type of childhood adversity [3], could have a negative effect on children’s mental health [4]. Bronfenbrenner [5] stated in her ecological system theory that children’s health development requires sufficient support from their living circumstances. Of all the levels of the ecological system, the family system - defined as the microsystem - has the greatest direct and immediate impact on children’s development [5]. Parental divorce could bring great instability to the structure of the family system and hinder children’s normal development, increasing their vulnerability to mental problems, including depression and anxiety [6]. Moreover, according to the family system theory, parental divorce could be seen as the outcome of the maladaptation of the family function, and children in a dysfunctional family usually serve as the scapegoat who shows increasing internalizing problems [7].

In line with family systems theory, a longitudinal study suggests that, following parental divorce, individuals experience significantly higher levels of both externalizing and internalizing problems than those whose parents remain married. This effect is persistent and increasing, whereas the difference before divorce is not significant [8]. Similarly, a meta-analysis reviewed 115 published papers and proposed that during the post-divorced period, divorced families are more dysfunctional, showing more interparental conflicts, and children in these families show increasing internalizing and externalizing problems [9].

The relation between parental divorce and social anxiety, depression

Despite robust evidence proving the negative impact of parental divorce on mental health issues, the relation between parental divorce and specific mental problems, such as depression and social anxiety, remains unclear. Depression and social anxiety are prevalent among college students and could bring multiple adverse outcomes among adolescents (including protracted impairments in interpersonal, social, educational, and occupational functioning) [10, 11]. Theoretically speaking, parental divorce could contribute to the occurrence of depression and social anxiety. As the attachment theory states, the foundation of secure attachment requires healthy and supportive interaction between children and their parents [12]. Indeed, parental divorce could hinder the development of secure attachment, increasing the risk of obtaining an insecure attachment style [13]. As Bowlby proposed [14], there are two internal working models in an individual’s behavioral system: the working models of others and self. Individuals with an insecure attachment style may construct dysfunctional working models, showing difficulties in interacting with others and feeling bad about themselves [14]. Indeed, individuals who have experienced parental divorce may face a higher risk for both social anxiety and depression [15, 16]. Consistently, the results of a national study show that adolescents from divorced families showed more social fear and a higher level of depression compared to those from cohabited families [4], and a population-based longitudinal study similarly suggests that parental divorce could have a prolonged influence on an individual’s vulnerability to depression [17].

However, there also exist studies drawing the reverse conclusion. A case-control study indicates that after controlling for health state and childhood trauma, parental divorce cannot significantly predict depression [18]. Meanwhile, another study collected data across three generations, which also implies that parental divorce has a very limited effect on their offspring’s depression [19]. Furthermore, although evidence suggests that parental divorce may contribute to increased levels of relationship anxiety, attachment anxiety, and interpersonal avoidance behaviors [4, 20, 21], no study has directly proved that parental divorce could or could not directly contribute to greater vulnerability to social anxiety.

Insights from the symptomatic perspective

One possible explanation for the inconsistency in previous studies might be that the traditional study takes depression and social anxiety as a unitary disease entity and describes them with the sum scores of scales [4, 15, 18]. Although this perspective, based on the diagnostic system, is efficient in distinguishing individuals with a higher likelihood of experiencing depression or social anxiety, it may overlook the high degree of heterogeneity of mental health issues [22]. The heterogeneity of depression may imply that two individuals, despite having similar scores on the scale, may differ in the characteristics of symptoms and could be divided into different subtypes [23]. In the previous approaches to distinguish the subtypes of mental disorders, the symptom-based subtypes (such as psychotic depression and anxious depression) and etiology-based subtypes (such as adjustment disorder and early trauma depression) convey most subtypes of depression [23, 24]. Similarly, studies also found heterogeneous symptom features in social anxiety [25]. Indeed, it is crucial to identify the potential adverse impact of parental divorce by examining the distinctive symptom manifestations among individuals from divorced and intact families.

Apart from the heterogeneity in symptom features, the developing trajectories of depression and social anxiety may also differ in different groups [26, 27]. As the previous study revealed, people with lower income, lower education, and higher stress may show poor developmental trajectories of depression [26]. Meanwhile, similar results were also found in social anxiety [28]. Considering that robust evidence has demonstrated that parental divorce could have a negative effect on individuals’ mental health state [8, 9], it is reasonable to assume that the developmental trajectories of depression and social anxiety may differ among college students who experienced parental divorce and those who did not.

The current study

Network analysis could be an appropriate method to fill the two gaps in the existing literature. Network analysis, based on the symptomatic perspective, views mental problems as co-existing and interacting symptoms [29]. By revealing the connections between different symptoms, network analysis can identify the most salient symptoms and reveal the interactions between different symptoms [30]. Therefore, by making a comparison between adolescents from divorced families and those from intact families, network analysis enables researchers to illuminate the possible unique symptomatic features that are related to parental divorce. Moreover, by adopting the Bayesian model, network analysis allows researchers to explore the possible triggering relation between symptoms and locate the potential heterogeneity in the developmental trajectories [31]. Indeed, there are three main aims in the current study:

Method

Participants

The participants (N = 3617, females = 2302 (63.6%), Mean age = 18.8, age range from 16 to 26) in the current study were college students in universities in Nanjing, Jiangsu province, China. Among all participants, 381 (10.53%) experienced parental divorce, while 3236 participants’ parents remained married.

Procedures

In the present study, all participants were recruited using convenience sampling, conducted from October to December 2023, through the online platform “Wenjuanxing”. Survey links were distributed via class-specific WeChat groups by student mentors. The mentors addressed all participants’ questions within the groups. Participation was voluntary, and students could complete the survey on their mobile devices. Before all the measurements began, an electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants. A total of 5,372 students participated in the study, and 1,616 students failed to pass the attention test and were identified as careless responders. After filtering out all careless responders, the data of 3756 participants was left. Meanwhile, considering the current study focusing on the possible influence of parental divorce on adolescents, only participants who reported that their parents were in a state of marriage or divorce met the selection criterion. At last, the data of 3617 participants (females = 2302 (63.6%), Mean age = 18.8) were used in the current study. All participants were divided into the divorced group (N = 381, females = 251 (65.9%), Mean age = 18.8) and the non-divorced group (N = 3236, females = 2051(63.4%), Mean age = 18.8) according to the marital status of their parents. The research was examined and approved by the ethics committee of Beijing Normal University.

Measures

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

In the current study, depression was assessed using the PHQ-9 Chinese version, which showed great reliability and validity among the Chinese general population Wang, Bian [32]. The PHQ-9 includes nine items describing symptoms of depression, such as “little interest or pleasure in doing things”. All items are rated on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“nearly every day”), that describes the frequencies of the symptoms. A higher score in the PHQ-9 may indicate more severe depression. Based on the current participants, the PHQ-9 shows good internal consistency and reliability, with Cronbach’s α reaching 0.93 and McDonald’s ωt reaching 0.93.

Social anxiety scale for adolescents (SAS-A)

In the present study, social anxiety was measured using the SAS-A Chinese version, with good reliability and validity among Chinese adolescents [33]. The SAS-A contains three dimensions describing the different aspects of social anxiety, including fear of negative evaluation (SAS1), social avoidance and distress in new situations/ with strangers (SAS2), and social avoidance and distress generally/ with acquaintances (SAS3). There are a total of 18 items in the SAS-A, which were all rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always). A higher score in the SAS-A represents more severe social anxiety. The SAS-A has good internal consistency and reliability among the current participants, with Cronbach’s α reaching 0.97, McDonald’s ωt reaching 0.98, and McDonald’s ωh reaching 0.88.

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis

In this study, we used R (version 4.3.2) for data analysis. To begin with, a descriptive analysis comparing the depression symptoms, social anxiety symptoms, and demographic information between the divorced group and the non-divorced group was conducted. After that, Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω were calculated.

The estimation and comparison of the network and centrality index

To explore the relation between depression and social anxiety among the divorced and the non-divorced groups, the Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM) was used [34]. To further regularize the network structure and provide more convenience in understanding the relation between symptoms, the Extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) and graphical least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) were utilized [35, 36].

Additionally, to demonstrate the symptoms linking depression and anxiety, the bridge expected influence (BEI) values were calculated. The BEI is valued by the sum of all edge weights that connect the node with nodes that belong to other symptom communities, pointing out the symptom connecting multiple communities [37].

Apart from that, to locate the difference between the networks of the two groups, we conducted the network comparison test (NCT) between the global strength, the edges, and the BEIs of the two groups [38]. The global strength indicates the closeness of symptoms in the network, with a higher global strength representing that the symptoms are more closely connected in the symptom network. The edges are the relations between the two symptoms [34].

Directed acyclic graph model

To investigate the possible causal relation between symptoms of depression and anxiety, we constructed the directed acyclic graph (DAG) model of the two groups separately. The DAG model is a type of Bayesian model, constructed by doing 5000 bootstrapped networks with the causal information and measuring their fitness on the current sample [31]. The DAG model may provide information regarding the direction and strength of the connection between symptoms, and may also offer insights into which symptoms are causally important [39].

Verification of the stability and accuracy of the network

To verify the accuracy, the bootstrapped confidence intervals (CI) (95%) of edges in the symptom network were calculated using the nonparametric bootstrap. A narrower CI interval may suggest a more reliable and accurate estimation of the network model. In addition, to examine the stability of the network model, the correlation stability coefficient (CS-C) of the BEI was calculated. The CS-C refers to the maximum proportion of the sample that can be removed while the correlation coefficient of the BEI among the original sample and the after-dropped sample remains at least 0.7 at the proportion of 95% [35]. According to the published study, the CS-C should be at least 0.25 and is preferable to 0.5 [40].

Meanwhile, considering the unignorable diversity of the sample sizes in the two different groups, we conducted 200 random resamplings on the non-divorced group at the sample size equal to that of the divorced group. After that, we built a symptom network based on the resampled data and made a comparison to test whether the networks based on the original data and the resampled data were homogeneous.

Discussion

The current study focuses on the comparison of symptomatic features of depression and anxiety and the relation between depression and anxiety among college students from divorced families and those from intact families. Some findings are worth discussing.

More severe symptoms among the divorced group

The results indicated that college students with divorced parents scored significantly higher on depression symptoms including “Anhedonia” (PHQ1), “Sad mood” (PHQ2), “Sleep” (PHQ3), “Energy” (PHQ4), “Appetite” (PHQ5), and “Guilt” (PHQ6) as well as social anxiety symptoms including “Afraid of negative evaluation” (SAS1) and “Anxiety in normal situation” (SAS3). This finding suggests that, in general, the divorce of the parents may have a negative influence on the mental health state of college students. Aligned with this, previous studies conducted in different areas also found that adolescents from divorced families showed significantly more severe depression compared to their counterparts [4, 41]. This consistency may imply that the negative effect of parental divorce exists across different cultural contexts.

As mentioned earlier, the healthy development of adolescents and young adults requires reliable and supportive systems, and the family usually serves as one of the most important microsystems [5]. Parental divorce may bring unignorable shocks to the ecological system to which individuals belong [42]. Additionally, changes in the family system may lead to increasing feelings of uncertainty and insecurity as well as unfavorable feelings such as sadness, hopelessness, and social withdrawal, adding challenges to constructing their self-identity [43, 44]. Indeed, considering the increasing challenges college students from divorced families may confront, more attention and intervention are needed to improve their mood state.

“Guilt” as the important symptomatic feature among the divorced group

The results from the symptomatic network indicate that there are more bridge symptoms in the symptomatic network of the divorced group than in that of the non-divorced group. Considering that more bridge symptoms may indicate a closer relation between depression and anxiety, this difference may further support our previous finding that college students from divorced families showed worse mental health states.

Moreover, in the symptomatic network, “Guilt” appeared to be the bridge symptom with the top BEI value and was unique in the symptomatic network of the divorced group. This finding implies the important role that “Guilt” may play in connecting depression and social anxiety. Moreover, “Guilt” also showed the strongest edge linking depression and social anxiety (“Guilt” with “Anxiety in unfamiliar situation”). This result further proves that “Guilt” is vital in linking depression and social anxiety. Guilt is normally defined as a kind of negative self-conscious emotion that involves people’s reactions toward their own characteristics and actions [45] and is described as “Feeling bad about yourself - or that you are a failure or have let yourself or your family down” [46]. Since “Guilt” only served as the bridge symptom in the network of the divorced group, we may infer that the vital role of “Guilt” could be related to the separation of their parents. Similarly, Miralles and Godoy [13] studied the long-term effect of divorce on emotional consequences and also identified guilt as a popular and major consequence of parental separation. As previous studies stated, guilt always appears among divorced parents [47]. It is commonly discovered that divorced parents may worry about the negative impact of divorce on their children, questioning their capacity to take care of their children [47, 48]. In the family system, the guilt of parents, if not dealt with properly, may transfer to the guilt of the children [49]. Indeed, the parents’ guilt may serve as one possible resource for the guilt of the college students.

In the symptomatic network, the strongest edge linking depression and social anxiety exists between “Guilt” and “Anxiety in normal situation”. This result differs slightly from previous empirical studies, most of which do not find a significant relation between guilt and social anxiety among both clinical samples and general samples [50, 51]. This diversity could be explained by the different definitions and measurements of guilt in the current study and former studies. In previous studies, guilt is defined as the emotion that arises due to one’s perception of having committed moral transgressions in which their action or inaction contributed to unfavorable consequences [52], which were measured using the Test of Self-conscious Affect [50]. However, “Guilt” in the current study is defined as one symptom of depression and described as “Feeling bad about yourself - or that you are a failure or have let yourself or your family down” [46]. In addition, since PHQ-9 comes from the diagnostic criteria of major depression in DSM-5, the symptom “Guilt” is described as “Feelings of worthlessness or excessive or inappropriate guilt (which may be delusional) nearly every day (not merely self-reproach or guilt about being sick)” in DSM-5 [53]. Indeed, in the current study, the symptom “Guilt” may result in feelings of guilt, shame, and low self-esteem [54]. According to a systematic review based on 60 empirical studies, shame could perpetuate social anxiety and may be rooted in early adverse childhood experiences, such as the separation of parents [55]. Similarly, another meta-analysis also reviewed that low self-esteem and internalizing symptoms (including depression and social anxiety) are mutually connected [56]. Indeed, the strong relation between the symptom “Guilt” and other symptoms of social anxiety is reasonable. Given the important role of “Guilt” in the network, this finding may suggest that enhancing self-esteem and alleviating feelings of shame could be effective in coping with the comorbidity of depression and social anxiety among college students from divorced families.

The possible developing mechanism of social anxiety among college students

In the current study, we use the DAG network to explore the possible directional relation between symptoms of depression and social anxiety. The results reveal that in both DAG networks, the pathway from “Anxiety in unfamiliar situation” to “Anxiety in normal situation” is stable and strong. This finding indicates that the potential mechanism underlying the development of social anxiety may be the generalization of anxiety in specific situations to broader contexts.

Consistent with the current study, Zeytinoglu et al. [57] followed 291 participants from early childhood to adolescence. They proved that the developmental mechanism lies in the generalization from shyness (defined as discomfort in a specific social context) to social anxiety [57]. Similarly, an epidemiological study based on a random population-based sample including adolescents and young adults also identified anxiety and avoidance in special situations, such as speaking or performing in front of others, as the initial and most prevalent symptom of social anxiety [58]. However, another longitudinal study covering participants’ whole adolescent period suggests that anxiety in unfamiliar situations rises through age and peaks in the middle of adolescence, while anxiety in normal situations stays stable during the whole adolescent period [59]. The inconsistency might come from the different focus of the study. Nelemans and Meeus [59] adopted the latent growth model and concentrated on the developmental routine of each symptom, but did not explore the possible directional relation between symptoms. The current study could provide further insight into the development of social anxiety, which involves generalizing anxiety from unfamiliar situations to everyday situations. However, it has to be mentioned that the present study is based on a cross-sectional sample and has limitations in deducing the directional relation. Therefore, more longitudinal or multi-site data studies [60] are needed to provide more robust evidence.

Implications

The current study identified the critical and unique role of the symptom “Guilt” in the symptomatic network of depression and social anxiety experienced by college students whose parents have divorced. The finding suggests that more attention should be given to emotions such as guilt and self-blame within the family system when parents decide to divorce. In combination with previous studies, couples or family therapy that focuses on addressing these emotions both for parents and college students could help college students adapt to changes in family structure and prevent negative mental health outcomes.

Limitation

While the results yielded significant insights, there are still some limitations that warrant attention. First, the sample sizes of the divorced and non-divorced groups in the DAG network based on the resampled participants in the non-divorced group are unequal, which may compromise the stability of our results. Despite our efforts to mitigate its influence by resampling, the results in the current study still need to be interpreted with caution. More specifically, the DAG network based on the resampled participants in the non-divorced group is slightly different from that based on the non-divorced group (for more details, see Figure S2 in the supplementary). This diversity suggested that the size of the sample could affect the results in the DAG network. Indeed, in the discussion part, we only focus on the stable results existing in most DAG networks based on different samples. This diversity reminds future researchers to be cautious when selecting sample sizes for the DAG network method. Furthermore, the present study employed a Bayesian network to investigate the potential causal relations between symptoms. It has to be mentioned that although the Bayesian network could provide clues in deducing the directional connections, it still needs to be further tested by future longitudinal studies.

Lastly, previous studies have indicated that the influence may vary depending on the time when the divorce occurred, and divorces that occur during adolescence may yield more unfavorable outcomes [17]. Yet, in the current study, the divorce timing was not included as the control variable. Moreover, several other family-related factors, such as the quality of post-divorce parental relationships, custody arrangements, and the level of parental conflicts, can also have a significant influence on individuals’ psychological outcomes [61], which, however, were not included in the current study. Indeed, further studies may consider taking more family-related factors into consideration to carry out a more rigorous and comprehensive conclusion.

Conclusion

The current study focused on the symptomatic characteristics and the relation between depression and social anxiety. By making the comparison between college students from divorced families and intact families, the present study testifies to the negative influence of divorce on college students’ mental state. First, the severity of most symptoms of depression and social anxiety is greater among college students from divorced families than their counterparts. Second, “Guilt” served as the bridge symptom linking depression with social anxiety, specifically among college students with divorced parents. Third, the DAG network indicated that the most robust directional relation commences in “Anxiety in unfamiliar situation” and points towards “Anxiety in normal situation”. Based on these findings, the study may suggest that interventions for the symptom “Guilt”, such as reducing the feelings of shame and guilt as well as improving self-esteem, could help dissociate the comorbidity of social anxiety and depression, especially for those from divorced families. Moreover, early intervention in the discomfort and anxiety in unfamiliar situations might be useful in slowing down or preventing the development of social anxiety.
