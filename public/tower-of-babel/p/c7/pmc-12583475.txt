Single epidural bolus hydromorphone for acute postpartum pain after vaginal delivery: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Yixing Lu, Wengui Wei, Wenqing Jiang, Qiumei Liang, Shunzhong Jing, Yanlv Qin, Tao Chi, Siyan Liu, Yunan Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Postpartum pain significantly affects maternal recovery and overall health, underscoring the need for effective analgesic strategies. In this randomized controlled trial, 460 term parturients who underwent vaginal delivery were assigned to receive either a single epidural bolus of hydromorphone (HY group, n = 230) or an equivalent volume of normal saline (NS group, n = 230). Within 48 h postpartum, pain scores were significantly lower in the HY group than in the NS group (p < 0.001), with a greater proportion of women achieving pain scores ≤ 3 (76.47% vs. 27.93%; OR = 8.39, 97.5% CI 5.15–13.67). The HY group required fewer supplemental analgesics (6.79% vs. 24.77%; OR = 0.22, 97.5% CI 0.11–0.44), and the time to first rescue analgesia was prolonged (15.63 ± 5.98 vs. 12.01 ± 5.01 h). Although the incidence of adverse effects, including nausea, vomiting, dizziness, pruritus, and urinary retention, was greater in the HY group (p < 0.05), sleep disturbances were less common (5.43% vs. 30.18%; RR = 0.13). Furthermore, the HY Group exhibited superior maternal satisfaction (p < 0.001) and enhanced post-partum autonomy (p = 0.009), whereas no between-group differences in inflammatory markers were observed. Low-dose epidural hydromorphone provides effective postpartum pain management and represents a viable approach for acute postpartum analgesia, contingent on a favorable risk–benefit profile.Trial registration number: ChiCTR2200064687 (registration date: October 14, 2022; https://www.chictr.org.cn/)Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22232-7.

Introduction

While many women report positive emotional experiences postpartum, they are often associated with significant pain, including perineal, uterine, and lower back pain1. These nociceptive experiences can precipitate psychological distress, such as anxiety and depression, thereby impacting postpartum recovery, lactation, and maternal–infant bonding2. Studies have shown that 92% of women experience perineal pain on the first postpartum day, which, without intervention, can persist for up to two months3. When perineal muscle damage exceeds 50%, perineal pain and dyspareunia may persist for up to 10 days and 6 months, respectively4,5. Effective perinatal pain management, including intrapartum and postpartum analgesia, can significantly reduce postpartum complications6.

Postpartum pain management is a critical component of obstetric care. Current strategies for managing pain following vaginal delivery include physical therapy, pharmacological interventions, and epidural analgesia7–10. While epidural analgesia is the gold standard for intrapartum pain relief, providing significant analgesia during labor11, the management of postpartum pain presents unique challenges. Maternal concerns regarding the use of oral analgesics during breastfeeding, coupled with adverse effects such as prolonged dizziness and sedation associated with intramuscular opioids, complicate pain management. The absence of standardized clinical analgesic protocols for obstetric pain management underscores the need for novel pharmacological agents and therapeutic modalities. Hydromorphone, a semisynthetic μ-opioid receptor agonist derived from morphine, is highly lipophilic, enabling rapid blood–brain barrier penetration and selective μ-opioid receptor binding, thus providing sustained analgesic effects12. Epidural administration of 0.5–1 mg hydromorphone has demonstrated efficacy in postoperative pain relief, with an acceptable adverse effect profile13. However, its application in postpartum pain management requires further investigation.

Considering the aforementioned context, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the analgesic efficacy and associated adverse events of low-dose epidural hydromorphone in the postpartum population. This study aimed to provide evidence-based recommendations for refining postpartum pain management strategies and enhancing maternal outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study adhered to the Good Clinical Practice guidelines and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. It was prospectively registered with Chictr.org.cn (No. ChiCTR2200064687; Principal Investigator: Yixing Lu; first registered on 14/10/2022; first patient enrollment on 3/5/2024). This single-center randomized clinical trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region Maternal and Child Health Hospital on October 9, 2022 (approval No. [2021–3] 23). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, and the study adhered to the CONSORT guidelines for randomized clinical trials.

Patients

This study included 460 parturients who underwent vaginal delivery with labor analgesia between May 2024 and December 2024. The inclusion criteria were normal cognitive function, age ≥ 18 years, gestational age ≥ 37 weeks, and American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classifications I–III. The exclusion criteria were a history of chronic pain, upper respiratory tract infections, inadequate labor analgesia (VAS > 3), postpartum nerve injury, postpartum hemorrhage, and symphysis pubis diastasis. Intervention

Two hours after spontaneous vaginal delivery, the anesthesiologist initiated the study protocol. The designated medication was prepared in a 2.5 mL volume and administered through an epidural catheter. The treatment cohort received a single epidural bolus of hydromorphone (0.5 mg in 2.5 mL), whereas the control group received an equivalent volume of normal saline via a single bolus injection. Both groups received the injection through the existing epidural catheter, followed by a 1 mL saline flush.

Supplemental analgesia protocol

Pethidine (Dolantin)

When maternal pain intensity surpasses a score of 6 on the visual analog scale, the attending obstetrician administers a single intramuscular dose of 100 mg. Repeated dosing within 24 h is not indicated.

Diclofenac sodium suppository

In cases where the patient reports a pain score exceeding 6 and declines opioid analgesics, a 50 mg rectal dose is administered. For severe pain, subsequent doses may be repeated at 12-h intervals.

Acetaminophen

A 0.5 g oral dose is administered to patients presenting with concurrent pyrexia and pain. Supplemental doses may be repeated at 6-h intervals as needed.

Randomization and blinding

The randomization sequence for this study was generated using the SAS (version 9.4) PROC PLAN procedure. The participants were assigned to either the hydromorphone or control group at a 1:1 ratio through a block randomization scheme with a block size of four to ensure group balance. An independent statistician concealed the allocation sequence within sequentially numbered opaque envelopes. Investigators responsible for administering the intervention were tasked solely with medication assignment based on envelope numbers and were blinded to the subsequent data collection and analysis processes. To preserve blinding, patients were not informed of their group assignments, and allocation details were concealed from the investigators, data collectors, and statisticians involved in the efficacy evaluation.

Labor analgesia protocol

All parturients received standardized intrathecal labor analgesia via L3/4 puncture, with a catheter placed 3–4 cm in the epidural space and administered by an external pulsatile electronic pump. The infusion comprised 0.08% ropivacaine and sufentanil (0.33 µg/mL) in 150 mL, starting with a 7 mL bolus, followed by continuous infusion at 8 mL/h, with patient-controlled boluses of 8 mL every 60 min and a lockout of 20 min. The dosing and concentrations were adjusted as clinically indicated.

Secondary outcomes included time to first rescue analgesia (hours from study drug administration to first request for rescue), time to first urination (hours from study drug administration to first spontaneous void), incidence of adverse events (nausea/vomiting, dizziness, pruritus, urinary retention, insomnia), functional independence (postpartum activities), maternal satisfaction, inflammatory markers (WBC, hs‑CRP), and neonatal adverse events.

The time to first analgesia request was defined as the time elapsed (in hours) from the administration of the study drug (hydromorphone or saline) to the first patient-initiated request for supplemental pain medication.

Time to first urination is the interval in hours from epidural drug administration to the initial spontaneous urination, characterized by a free-flowing stream rather than dribbling.

Insomnia was characterized as a patient-reported symptom involving substantial difficulty in initiating or maintaining sleep during the initial postpartum night, as documented in the medical record or corroborated through a 24-h clinical interview.

Postpartum activities were assessed using the Functional Independence Measure (FIM), which ranges from 18 (total assistance required) to 126 (complete independence). In this study, we considered women to have achieved independent activity if they obtained a score of 7 (complete independence) in all items relevant to self-care, mobility, and sphincter control domains.

Pilot study

A pilot feasibility study at our institution (March–April 2024) assessed procedural viability and preliminary data for power analysis. Twenty parturients with epidural labor analgesia undergoing spontaneous vaginal delivery were randomized 1:1 to receive a single epidural bolus of hydromorphone (0.5 mg, n = 10) or isotonic saline (n = 10) two hours postpartum. Pain was measured using the VAS at 2, 12, 24, and 48 h postpartum. The hydromorphone group showed a higher rate of effective analgesia (VAS ≤ 3) at 48 h (40% vs. 20%) and lower rescue analgesic use (10% vs. 30%). No severe adverse events occurred; mild pruritus and nausea were noted in the hydromorphone group. These results confirmed the protocol feasibility and informed effect size estimates for future sample size calculations.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined using PASS 2015 software (NCSS, Kaysville, Utah, USA). Among the predefined primary outcomes, pain score reduction at 48 h was the most pronounced effect, which was assessed by comparing the proportion of patients receiving effective analgesia (VAS ≤ 3) across different interventions. To control the family wise Type I error due to the three pre-specified co-primary outcomes, a conservative two-sided significance level of α = 0.025 (97.5% CIs) was employed. Based on pilot data and prior literature, the following parameters were established: power = 90%, alpha = 0.025, equal group size (N1 = N2), analgesia efficacy rate in the control group (P2) = 25%, and in the intervention group (P1) = 41%. A two-sided test indicated a required sample size of 209 participants per group. Accounting for an anticipated attrition rate of 10% in both arms, the final sample size was adjusted to 230 participants per group, resulting in 460 participants.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %), and between-group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test (χ2). Continuous variables were evaluated for normality using the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Normally distributed data are expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs), and between-group comparisons were analyzed using independent sample t-tests. Nonnormally distributed data are presented as medians (quartiles) [M (P25, P75)], and between-group comparisons were performed using the rank-sum test. Odds ratios (ORs) or risk ratios (RRs) with 97.5% confidence intervals (97.5% CIs) were used for categorical variables, whereas effect sizes for continuous variables are presented as mean differences (MDs) with 97.5% confidence intervals (97.5% CIs). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. To align with our predetermined significance threshold (α = 0.025) employed in the sample size determination, all confidence intervals have now been uniformly updated to 97.5% CIs. This modification ensures methodological consistency and mitigates the risk of inflated Type I error rates across multiple primary endpoints.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 460 women who underwent vaginal delivery were enrolled in this study. Seventeen participants were excluded because of postpartum nerve injury (4), pubic symphysis separation (4), acute postpartum hemorrhage (3), migraine (1), or loss to follow-up (5). None of the complications was directly attributable to the study interventions. The final analysis included 443 participants: 221 in the hydromorphone (HY) group and 222 in the normal saline (NS) control group. The baseline characteristics, including age, body mass index, gestational age, duration of second-stage labor, duration of labor analgesia, mode of delivery, and neonatal birth weight, did not differ significantly between the groups (p > 0.05). Epidural consumption

All parturients received a standardized analgesic regimen (0.08% ropivacaine + 0.33 μg/mL sufentanil), with an hourly consumption of 2.64 μg sufentanil and 6.4 mg ropivacaine. Statistical analysis showed no significant difference in labor analgesia duration or epidural drug consumption between the groups (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Analgesic efficacy and satisfaction

The proportion of women who experienced effective maternal analgesia, defined as a pain score ≤ 3, was significantly greater in the HY group than in the NS group (76.47% vs. 27.93%; OR 8.39; 97.5% CI 5.15–13.67; p < 0.001). Postnatal pain following normal labor was predominantly observed in the perineal, lumbosacral, and mixed pain categories, with a notable reduction in the incidence of acute pain in the HY group, particularly in the mixed pain category (9.95% vs. 38.29%; OR 0.18, 97.5% CI 0.10–0.32; p < 0.001). Furthermore, the HY group demonstrated a lower likelihood of requiring supplemental analgesic medication postpartum (6.79% vs. 24.77%; OR 0.22, 97.5% CI 0.11–0.44; p < 0.001) and exhibited a longer duration before the first supplemental analgesic medication was needed (15.63 ± 5.98 vs. 12.01 ± 5.01 h, p = 0.02). Although the frequency of medication refills was lower in the HY group (1.13 ± 0.35 vs. 1.33 ± 0.70; p = 0.302), this difference was not significant. Adverse effects and inflammatory markers

The HY group presented a markedly greater incidence of emesis (26.24% vs. 8.11%, RR 4.03, 97.5% CI 2.11–7.72; p < 0.001), dizziness (25.34% vs. 14.41%, RR 2.02, 97.5% CI 1.16–3.50; p = 0.004), and pruritus (32.58% vs. 7.21%, RR 6.22, 97.5% CI 3.20–12.09; p < 0.001). Additionally, the time to first urination was significantly longer in the HY group than in the NS group (2.50 [IQR 1.83–4.82] h vs. 2.04 [IQR 1.5–3.0] h, p = 0.002), and the incidence of urinary retention was notably greater in the HY group (20.81% vs. 7.66%, RR 3.17, 97.5% CI 1.61–6.24; p < 0.001). In contrast, the incidence of insomnia was significantly lower in the HY group than in the NS group (5.43% vs. 30.18%; RR 0.13, 97.5% CI 0.06–0.28; p < 0.001). At 24 h postpartum, inflammatory marker levels, as indicated by leukocyte counts (13.53 ± 3.26 vs. 13.91 ± 3.20, p = 0.217) and ultrasensitive C-reactive protein levels (62.69 ± 38.00 vs. 65.93 ± 36.20, p = 0.359), were reduced in the HY group, although these differences were not statistically significant. Neonatal-related characteristics

Some neonates are admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit for medical indications. No untoward events, including somnolence or diminished oxygen saturation, were documented in either cohort within the ward, and all infants demonstrated the capacity to receive sufficient amounts of breast milk or formula.

Discussion

Main findings

Perineal lacerations (67.87%, 68.92%) and episiotomies (23.98%, 23.42%) were the predominant labor-associated morbidities within our cohort, consistent with the existing literature14. Recognized maternal injury risk factors include labor progression anomalies, fetal macrosomia, ineffective expulsive efforts, and fetal compromise15,16. Such injuries contribute to acute postpartum pain syndromes, including perineal, lumbar, uterine contraction, or mixed pain17; however, pain assessment and management are frequently underprioritized in Chinese obstetric practice, where clinical emphasis remains on uterine contractility, wound healing, and hemorrhage control. This, compounded by maternal health literacy deficits, results in delayed or inadequate analgesic interventions. Puerperal pain adversely affects sleep quality, early mobilization, urinary function, and maternal-infant bonding, highlighting the necessity for optimized analgesic protocols.

In this context, our randomized controlled trial demonstrated that a single epidural bolus of hydromorphone (0.5 mg) administered postpartum significantly attenuated acute postpartum pain, extended the duration before rescue analgesia was required, and reduced the need for supplemental analgesics. Maternal satisfaction and functional independence metrics were also favorably affected. Nonetheless, hydromorphone administration was correlated with a higher incidence of opioid-related adverse events, although no neonatal adverse effects were observed.

Analgesic effect

Our results corroborate the existing literature, indicating that epidural opioid delivery offers consistent and prolonged analgesic efficacy in obstetric and surgical patient cohorts18. Epidural opioids exert their analgesic effects by binding to G protein-coupled µ-opioid receptors in the spinal cord, inhibiting the release of substance P and excitatory neurotransmitters, thereby impeding nociceptive transmission. Hydrophilic opioids, such as hydromorphone, offer prolonged analgesia due to their extended duration of action12. In this study, a single epidural bolus of hydromorphone (0.5 mg) was administered immediately after vaginal delivery via an in-situ labor epidural catheter to achieve sustained postpartum analgesia. Prior studies have shown that epidural hydromorphone (0.6 mg) provides analgesia for a median duration exceeding 13 hours13. Our study demonstrated that epidural hydromorphone administration significantly reduced postpartum pain. Specifically, there was a significant reduction in the incidence of acute pain in the HY group (49.77% vs. 88.29%), and a marked decrease in the likelihood of experiencing mixed pain (9.95% vs. 38.29%). Effective analgesic management (VAS ≤ 3) was achieved within 48 h in 76.47% of the participants compared with 27.93% in the control group. The prolonged analgesic effect of hydromorphone decreases the need for additional postpartum analgesic interventions. Conversely, women in the NS group were more likely to require supplementary analgesics (pethidine and diclofenac sodium) for intolerable pain (24.77% vs. 6.79%) and initiate medication earlier (MD 3.63 h, 97.5% CI 0.14–7.12; p = 0.02). While previous studies have primarily focused on postoperative analgesia, our study highlights the efficacy of epidural hydromorphone for maternal analgesia after vaginal delivery. Our findings suggest that the analgesic effectiveness of this regimen is comparable to that of conventional postoperative regimens. Overall, these results support the reliable use of hydromorphone for pain management after vaginal delivery.

Adverse effects

Consistent with previous research on neuraxial opioid administration, our findings indicate an increased incidence of nausea, emesis, pruritus, and urinary retention in patients receiving hydromorphone19,20. Nausea and emesis were the most frequently observed ADRs, affecting 26.24% of the patients. Regardless of whether epidural hydromorphone or saline was administered, most participants who experienced nausea and vomiting presented symptoms within 8 h of delivery, with emesis coinciding with oral intake and the absence of nausea during periods of fasting. The epidural analgesic pump used for intrathecal labor analgesia included sufentanil. Both sufentanil and hydromorphone exert analgesic effects by binding to opioid receptors (μ, κ, and δ) in the nervous system, potentially causing adverse effects. Opioid receptor activation in the gastrointestinal tract inhibits smooth muscle contractility and peristalsis, resulting in gastric retention and elevated intragastric pressure. Furthermore, these agents act on the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) in the brainstem, stimulating the vomiting center and subsequently inducing nausea and emesis21. In the NS group, parturients who received sufentanil for labor analgesia demonstrated a comparatively lower incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) (8.11%). The use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as intra-anal diclofenac sodium, in 46 patients in the NS group may have indirectly influenced this outcome through the inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis22. Although the HY group presented a relatively high incidence of PONV, these events were generally mild and transient and were effectively managed through aggressive rehydration, antiemetic administration, and careful monitoring. Postpartum dizziness was more prevalent in both groups (25.34% in the HY group and 14.41% in the NS group), with most cases occurring within the first 6 h postpartum. In addition to opioid-induced central nervous system depression, factors such as inadequate rest, physical exertion during labor, and insufficient energy replenishment likely contribute to early postpartum weakness, fatigue, and drowsiness.

Intrathecal opioid administration often elicits diffuse cutaneous pruritus, particularly with hydrophilic morphine derivatives23. The pathophysiology of opioid-induced pruritus likely involves pruritoceptive neural pathway activation via μ-opioid receptors, specifically the μ-3 subtype, in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord and the central nervous system. Opioids may also induce histamine release from mast cells, causing localized or generalized pruritus. Intrathecal delivery-related pruritus primarily stems from central mechanisms and not from typical allergic responses. Pruritus severity is correlated with cerebrospinal fluid opioid concentrations24. Research has shown that epidural hydromorphone (1 mg) induces pruritus in 58% of parturients, with 31% requiring antipruritic treatment13. In this study, hydromorphone (0.5 mg) resulted in a 32.58% incidence of pruritus. Winter xerosis may also increase the incidence of pruritus in the winter. Clinical observations have revealed variable pruritus intensity and presentation among parturients, which are typically transient and mild; however, severe, generalized pruritus lasting up to 24 h has been reported. Therapeutic strategies for opioid-induced pruritus include dopamine antagonists, μ-opioid receptor agonists/antagonists, and intrathecal or oral μ-opioid receptor antagonists, which are all effective in prophylaxis and treatment24. The majority of pruritus experienced by the parturients in this study resolved spontaneously. During the winter study period, symptomatic management with antihistamines and topical emollients was used for the treatment of severe pruritus.

Prior investigations have established primiparity, intrathecal labor analgesia, operative vaginal delivery, and episiotomy as notable risk factors for postpartum urinary retention (PUR)25,26. Intrathecal anesthesia compromises the sympathetic and parasympathetic neural pathways, resulting in external urethral sphincter relaxation and disruption of the micturition reflex, thereby increasing the likelihood of urinary retention. Opioids suppress the micturition reflex via μ-opioid receptor activation within the spinal cord, thereby diminishing afferent signaling essential for bladder contraction27. Hydromorphone, a hydrophilic opioid with prolonged cerebrospinal fluid retention, may diffuse to supraspinal micturition centers, inhibiting voluntary urination. Furthermore, episiotomy and instrumental delivery induce direct trauma to the perineal and pelvic floor musculature, impairing the pudendal and sacral nerve innervation. This leads to aberrant micturition reflexes and compromised detrusor function, ultimately resulting in urinary retention. The median time to the first postpartum urination was longer in the hydromorphone cohort (2.5 h) than in the normal saline group (2.04 h), with a significantly elevated risk of urinary retention (RR 3.17, 97.5% CI 1.61–6.24). PUR is a multifactorial condition encompassing neurological, muscular, and psychological factors28. Consequently, meticulous monitoring of postpartum urinary function is imperative, along with early mobilization, bladder training, and pharmacological or catheterization interventions as indicated. Prophylactic indwelling catheterization remains the standard clinical practice for patients at a heightened risk of PUR, such as those who have undergone episiotomy or sustained third-degree perineal lacerations.

Clinical implications

Postpartum pain, which is frequently intensified by physical activity, can significantly hinder early mobilization. Although extended bed rest may temporarily mitigate discomfort, it impairs gastrointestinal motility recovery and elevates the risk of venous thromboembolism29. In our randomized controlled trial, epidural administration of hydromorphone (0.5 mg) did not impair the lower limb motor function. As a semisynthetic opioid derivative with high analgesic potency, hydromorphone provides effective pain relief at low doses without exacerbating motor blockade30,31. Adequate analgesia in the hydromorphone group facilitated autonomous ambulation and self-care, whereas participants in the normal saline group required increased assistance owing to perineal pain. Consequently, a greater proportion of women in the hydromorphone cohort achieved independence activity (76.02% vs. 64.41%; OR 1.75, 97.5% CI 1.09–2.81).

Hydromorphone administration was correlated with a significantly reduced incidence of insomnia (5.43% vs. 30.18%), indicating that effective analgesia may facilitate enhanced sleep. These results underscore the necessity for additional investigations into the impact of postpartum pain management strategies on sleep quality and maternal recovery processes.

Consistent with prior research on cesarean section analgesia, epidural opioid administration did not negatively impact maternal lactation outcomes or neonatal feeding behaviors32,33. No opioid-related neonatal adverse events were documented in either of the cohorts. Inflammatory biomarker analysis indicated a non-significant trend toward reduced leukocyte counts and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels in the HY group, which may be attributable to the limited sampling scope. Preclinical investigations suggest that opioids may modulate proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 (IL-1), via μ-opioid receptor activation34,35, highlighting the need for further studies involving extended monitoring and serial sampling to elucidate their immunomodulatory effects.

The administration of hydromorphone through a retained labor epidural catheter provides a straightforward and efficacious postpartum analgesic modality. A single bolus administration mitigates the risks associated with extended catheterization, decreases the likelihood of infection, facilitates early ambulation, and maintains perineal hygiene, thereby enhancing maternal recovery.

Study limitations

The study’s limitations include its single-center design, which limits generalizability, assessment restricted to 48 h post-intervention, reliance on subjective VAS pain scores, potential confounding from standardized intrapartum sufentanil, and lack of evaluation of epidural hydromorphone’s impact on breastfeeding outcomes. No neonatal adverse events were observed; however, opioid transfer and its effects on infant neurobehavior warrant further investigation. Future studies should incorporate systematic monitoring of feeding, milk transfer, and neonatal sedation to ensure patient safety.

Conclusion

A single epidural bolus of hydromorphone (0.5 mg) administered after vaginal delivery provided effective postpartum analgesia, prolonged analgesic duration, and improved maternal satisfaction and independence in our study. Although opioid-associated adverse effects were more prevalent, they remained predominantly mild and manageable in nature. These results indicate that low-dose epidural hydromorphone may offer a balanced analgesic strategy for postpartum pain management, meriting validation through larger multicenter randomized controlled trials with prolonged follow-up periods.
