Active Surveillance for Localized Prostate Cancer: A Retrospective Single‐Center Study
Authors: Akihiko Sugino, Yoshiyuki Miyazawa, Shun Nakazawa, Yusuke Tsuji, Yuji Fujizuka, Seiji Arai, Yoshitaka Sekine, Masashi Nomura, Hidekazu Koike, Hiroshi Matsui, Kazuhiro Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundActive surveillance (AS) offers a strategy to limit unnecessary treatment of prostate cancer while maintaining oncologic safety, yet its adoption in Japan remains limited. To provide Japan‐specific evidence, we evaluated the clinical outcomes of patients undergoing AS at our institution.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 86 patients who selected AS between 2012 and 2024. Eligibility criteria included ≤ cT2a disease, prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) level of ≤ 10 ng/mL, Gleason score (GS) of 3 + 3, and 2 or fewer positive cores, with selected inclusion of GS 3 + 4 or 4 + 3 based on patient preference. AS discontinuation was based on clinical or pathological progression or patient preference. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Gunma University (approval no. IRB2025‐033, 2310).ResultsThe mean observation period was 48 months. No prostate cancer–specific deaths occurred. AS persistence rates were 89% at 1 year, 55% at 5 years, and 27% at 10 years. Older age (≥ 75 years) was associated with longer AS persistence (p = 0.021). Overall, 69% of patients underwent repeat biopsy, most commonly at 1 year. Pathological upgrading occurred in 35% at the first and second repeat biopsies and in 20% at the third. Acceptance of the 1‐year repeat biopsy increased from 46% in the early period (2012–2016) to 64% in the later period (2017–2024).ConclusionsAS demonstrated favorable long‐term safety and durability. Improving adherence to repeat biopsy protocols may further enhance AS management in Japan.Registry and Registration No. of the Study/TrialN/A

Introduction

Prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) testing, widely used for prostate cancer (PC) screening, has been associated with a substantial risk of overdiagnosis. It has been reported that up to 50% of PC detected through screening may represent overdiagnosed cases in a report from the early 2000s [1]. According to a recently published meta‐analysis by the US Preventive Services Task Force, overdiagnosis has been reported to occur in approximately 20.7% to 50.4% of prostate cancers detected through screening [2]. Overdiagnosis and overtreatment pose significant clinical challenges because they are linked to adverse outcomes including erectile dysfunction, urinary incontinence, and radiation‐induced cystitis and proctitis following radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy. Furthermore, when androgen deprivation therapy is administered to patients with biologically insignificant prostate cancer resulting from overdiagnosis, patients may be exposed to unnecessary adverse effects, including sexual dysfunction, bone fragility, hypertension, dyslipidemia, impaired glucose metabolism, and cognitive decline. Active surveillance (AS) has emerged as a promising management strategy to address these concerns. By deferring definitive treatment, AS is aimed at mitigating the consequences of overdiagnosis while delaying the onset of treatment‐related morbidities. This approach enables the management of low‐risk PC without compromising either quality of life or the potential for cure. Notably, previous studies have shown no significant difference in overall survival (OS) between patients managed with AS and those undergoing immediate radical treatment, underscoring the clinical utility of AS in appropriately selected patients [3, 4, 5].

AS is an effective management strategy for low‐risk localized PC and is widely adopted in Western countries. In a Swedish national registry study published in 2017, AS was selected in 91% of very low‐risk cases and 74% of low‐risk cases [6]. Similarly, in the United States, the adoption rate for AS in low‐risk PC increased from 26.5% in 2014 to 59.6% in 2021 [7]. By contrast, the uptake of AS in Japan remains limited. A survey conducted in 2023 reported that the selection rate for AS among patients with low‐ or intermediate‐risk PC was only 12.5% [8]. Compared with these international data, AS appears to be underutilized in Japan. At our institution, 53.1% of patients with low‐risk disease are managed with AS [9], which is relatively high within the Japanese context. To contribute to the development of evidence specific to the Japanese population, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 84 patients with PC who selected AS at our institution.

Patients and Methods

Patients and Study Design

We retrospectively collected and analyzed clinical data from the medical records of 86 patients who elected for AS at our institution between January 2012 and August 2024. Referring to the inclusion criteria used in prior clinical studies of active surveillance, we defined the following criteria [5, 10, 11]. The inclusion criteria for AS at our institution were as follows: clinical stage ≤ T2a, PSA concentration of ≤ 10 ng/mL, Gleason score (GS) of 3 + 3 = 6, and 2 or fewer positive biopsy cores. In selected cases where patients strongly preferred AS, ≥ T2b, GS of 3 + 4 = 7 or 4 + 3 = 7 was also permitted. After enrollment, follow‐up consisted of PSA testing every 3 months, transrectal ultrasound and digital rectal examination every 6 months, and annual imaging studies including computed tomography, bone scintigraphy, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The first repeat biopsy was scheduled 1 year after initiating AS, with subsequent biopsies determined based on PSA kinetics and/or worsening imaging findings. If the PSA doubling time is less than 3 years or if there is a sudden increase, we consider progression and consider imaging tests or biopsy. In the absence of such changes, routine repeat biopsies were performed every 3 years following the initial 1‐year biopsy. Triggers for transitioning from AS to curative treatment included patient preference, rising PSA levels, radiologic progression, or reclassification based on pathologic findings at repeat biopsy. In such cases, treatment decisions were made through shared decision‐making with the patient. All management decisions adhered to standard clinical practice.

Statistical Analysis

We evaluated AS persistence rates, PC‐specific survival, and OS using the Kaplan–Meier method and log‐rank test. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R Version 4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism Version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Institutional Ethics Approval

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gunma University Hospital (Approval No. IRB2025‐033, 2310).

Results

In total, 86 patients were retrospectively enrolled between January 2012 and December 2024. The patients' characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean observation period from initiation of AS to the last follow‐up was 48 months (median, 39 months). The mean PSA level was 5.7 ng/mL (median, 5.2 ng/mL; range, 2.2–13.4 ng/mL). Seventy‐seven patients (90%) had a GS of 3 + 3, 8 patients (9%) had a GS of 3 + 4, and 1 patient (1%) had GS of 4 + 3. Among the 86 patients included in this study, two were classified as high‐risk. In these cases, we clearly explained that active surveillance is not recommended according to current clinical guidelines; however, at the patients' request, active surveillance was undertaken with particularly strict and careful follow‐up. Two patients died of other causes (bacterial pneumonia and liver cancer), and no PC‐specific deaths were observed.

Figure 2 shows the AS persistence rate. The persistence rates were 89% at 1 year, 55% at 5 years, and 27% at 10 years (Figure 1A). There were no significant differences in AS persistence among the three risk categories (p = 0.59) (Figure 1B). Similarly, persistence did not differ significantly according to GS (p = 0.54) (Figure 1C). Patients aged ≥ 75 years showed a significantly higher AS persistence rate (p = 0.021). Patients who declined repeat biopsy tended to have higher AS persistence (p = 0.083). Continuation of AS was observed in 29/67 (43%) patients in the < 75 group and 17/19 (90%) in the ≥ 75 group, whereas switching to curative treatment occurred in 36/67 (54%) and 1/19 (5%), respectively. Overall, the distribution of outcomes differed significantly between age groups (Fisher's exact test, p < 0.001) (Table S1).

In our clinical protocol, the initial repeat biopsy was set at, and recommended for, 1 year after enrollment. We evaluated the timing of this repeat biopsy for each patient (Figure 3). The interval from AS initiation to the first repeat biopsy was categorized as within 450 days (1 year), 450–800 days (2 years), and > 801 days (> 2 years). Overall, 59 patients (69%) underwent repeat biopsy, while 27 (31%) did not. Among those who underwent repeat biopsy, the first repeat biopsy was performed at 1 year in 47 patients (55%), at 2 years in 7 patients (8%), and at 3 years in 5 patients (5%). There was no significant difference in re‐biopsy acceptance between the < 75 and ≥ 75 groups (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.78) (Table S2).

We then compared the incidence of pathological upgrading at the time of repeat biopsy across risk classifications (Table 2). Up to 3 repeat biopsies were performed. The pathological upgrading rates were 35% at the first repeat biopsy, 35% at the second, and 20% at the third. Among low‐risk patients, the upgrading rates were 40% at the first repeat biopsy, 33% at the second, and 25% at the third. Among intermediate‐risk patients, the upgrading rate was 50% at the second. Among high‐risk patients, the upgrading rate was 50% at the first repeat biopsy. Figure 4 shows the acceptance rate of the first repeat biopsy at 1 year. The acceptance rate increased from 46% in the early period (2012–2016) to 64% in the late period (2017–2024), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.09). We investigated the reasons for initial repeat biopsy. 48 of 60 cases (80%) were protocol‐based biopsies, while the remaining cases were radiological progression in 4 cases (7%), PSA progression in 1 case (2%), patient preference in 3 cases (5%), and clinician‐led decision‐making in 4 cases (7%). There was no significant difference between the groups aged ≥ 75 years and < 75 years (p = 0.26) (Table S3).

Discussion

In the present study, we reported real‐world, relatively long‐term (12‐year) outcomes of AS at a single academic institution in Japan. The AS persistence rate is one of the key indicators for evaluating AS management. In the Prostate Cancer Research International: Active Surveillance (PRIAS) study, which is the largest known multicenter prospective AS cohort worldwide, the persistence rates at 2, 5, and 10 years were 77.3%, 48.0%, and 27.0%, respectively [5, 12]. Similarly, an Italian report demonstrated persistence rates of 87.8% at 1 year, 59.0% at 2 years, and 54.5% at 3 years [13]. A report from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) including 2907 patients showed persistence rates of 76% at 5 years and 64% at 10 years [14]. Data from the Japanese PRIAS‐JAPAN cohort revealed continuation rates of 77% at 1 year, 45% at 5 years, and 23% at 10 years [15].

In our study, the AS persistence rates were 89% at 1 year, 55% at 5 years, and 27% at 10 years, which were comparable to those reported in previous studies. However, the acceptance rate for the first repeat biopsy was 46% between 2012 and 2016 and increased to 64% between 2017 and 2024. Although this trend is encouraging, it remains lower than the 80%–83% reported in the PRIAS trial [12, 15]. In real‐world practice, analyses of the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database have shown that AS is often not conducted in strict adherence to the protocols used in randomized controlled trials [16]. To ensure the safe implementation of AS, it is essential for both healthcare providers and patients to share a clear understanding of its goals and to maintain consistent communication throughout management. We aimed, therefore, to refine our clinical practice further, to improve adherence to repeat biopsy protocols.

In our cohort, comparative analyses of AS persistence under various conditions revealed no significant differences based on risk classification or tumor grade at diagnosis. The PRIAS study has also recently included intermediate‐risk cases with a GS of 3 + 4 = 7, and it has been shown that this expansion does not adversely affect outcomes [15, 17]. Our findings were consistent, though the number of such cases in our cohort was limited and warrant further investigation. Notably, no PC‐specific deaths occurred in our series, supporting the safety of AS at our institution and aligning with evidence from randomized controlled trials [4].

As noted above, the acceptance rate for the initial repeat biopsy in our cohort was lower than that reported in previous studies such as PRIAS. Among patients who underwent the initial repeat biopsy, 13.9% subsequently underwent additional repeat biopsies after 2 years or more. In the analysis of reclassification rates at repeat biopsy, no significant differences were observed across risk categories. The overall reclassification rate in our study was also comparable to that reported in PRIAS‐JAPAN. Previous studies have shown that approximately 20%–30% of patients experience reclassification during AS [5, 12, 15, 17]. In a cohort study that followed 1043 patients who selected active surveillance (of whom 425 subsequently underwent definitive treatment), patients who did not adhere to follow‐up biopsy were reported to have higher rates of post‐treatment recurrence (19% vs. 12%; p = 0.003) and metastasis (7% vs. 2%; p = 0.0003), even after adjustment for age, prostate‐specific antigen level, and grade group. These findings indicate that adherence to follow‐up biopsy is an important factor associated with treatment outcomes [18]. Low‐adherence to scheduled AS protocols, including follow‐up biopsies, represents an important clinical issue, and studies have examined the key reasons contributing to such discontinuation. Kinsella et al. conducted a systematic review examining factors that influence adherence to and continuation of AS. The authors concluded that successful adherence to AS protocols requires not only consideration of patient and disease characteristics but also adequate understanding among healthcare professionals, families, and society. They emphasized that systematic communication from healthcare providers and the development of clear guidelines are key factors in facilitating AS and ensuring protocol adherence. Furthermore, they proposed defining active surveillance as a model of chronic disease management [19]. Recently, the use of MRI prior to biopsy has become increasingly common. A strategy involving annual evaluation of MRI findings to guide the need for follow‐up biopsy has been proposed, with studies demonstrating its potential usefulness [20]. MRI‐based assessment may be integrated into future AS protocols, and continued research in this field is anticipated.

At our institution, cognitive‐targeted biopsy based on pre‐biopsy MRI was introduced in 2017, followed by MRI–ultrasound fusion biopsy in 2024. MRI fusion biopsy has been reported to improve the detection of clinically significant PC compared with conventional biopsy methods [21, 22, 23]. We plan to continue monitoring how the introduction of MRI‐based biopsy techniques will influence AS selection and subsequent biopsy outcomes. Over the 12‐year observation period, the acceptance rate of repeat biopsy showed an upward trend, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure 4). We intend to continue providing thorough explanations to support patient decision‐making.

This study has several limitations. First, it represents a single‐institution experience with a relatively small number of patients. Future collaborative studies involving affiliated institutions are warranted to establish a larger cohort and further validate our findings. Second, the acceptance rate for repeat biopsy was relatively low, which may have affected the AS persistence rate. Although we routinely explain the importance of repeat biopsy and obtain consent at AS initiation, patients' preferences and circumstances can change over time, potentially influencing their willingness to undergo subsequent biopsies.

In conclusion, our 12‐year analysis of AS outcomes at our institution revealed no PC‐specific deaths, suggesting a favorable long‐term prognosis among patients managed with AS. Although AS persistence was comparable to that reported in previous studies, improving patient adherence to the first repeat biopsy emerged as an important area for future clinical practice.
