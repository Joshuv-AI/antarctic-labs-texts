An Enigmatic Case of Vanishing Pounds
Authors: Benjamin Davies, Martha Yearsley, Subhankar Chakraborty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCollagenous gastritis is a rare disorder of abnormal subepithelial collagen deposition that remains poorly understood. It is characteristically associated with > 10 μm of subepithelial collagen with evidence of an inflammatory response in the lamina propria. We report a rare dual diagnosis of collagenous gastritis and collagenous duodenitis in an elderly man who previously underwent gastric antrum biopsy without identification of subepithelial collagen deposition.

INTRODUCTION

Collagenous gastritis (CG) is a rare disorder of abnormal subepithelial collagen deposition that remains poorly understood since it was first described in a pediatric patient in 1989.1 It is histologically described as the deposition of more than 10 μm of subepithelial collagen with evidence of an associated inflammatory response in the lamina propria.2 It is reported to have a bimodal age distribution with a first peak at ages 10-19 and a second peak of women aged 60 and older.3 Abdominal pain and anemia are clinically present in the pediatric population whereas diarrhea is observed to be common in adults.3–5 We report a rare dual diagnosis of CG with collagenous duodenitis in a man who previously underwent gastric antrum biopsy without identification of subepithelial collagen deposition.

CASE REPORT

A 79-year-old man with a history of gastroesophageal reflux disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, iron deficiency anemia, and hyperlipidemia was referred to our gastroenterology clinic for significant weight loss and vomiting. The patient reported losing more than 31 kg over a 15-year span, with a 6.8-kg loss occurring in the preceding 6 months of this initial visit. He had been experiencing bloating, early satiety, nausea, and postprandial vomiting, leading to decreased oral intake. His vomiting had been ongoing for about 9 months at the time of presentation. Taking famotidine 20 mg oral at bedtime and 40 mg oral every morning provided temporary relief. He had no history of chronic steroid, alcohol, or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use.

An upper endoscopy performed 8 months earlier at outside hospital for the same symptoms had noted a diffusely atrophic gastric mucosa. Biopsies taken from the gastric antrum had revealed acute and chronic inflammation with increased eosinophils. Helicobacter pylori stain was performed and was negative. Computerized tomography scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis with contrast showed no significant abnormality to explain his weight loss. Prior evaluation for celiac disease was negative. An esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) was performed which revealed nodular gastric mucosa and flattened duodenal mucosa. Endoluminal functional lumen imaging probe (EndoFLIP) was simultaneously performed during the patient's EGD. EndoFLIP imaging results were suggestive of normal esophago-gastric junction opening (EGJ distensibility index between 3.6 and 4.2 mm2/mm Hg at intraballoon volumes of 40-60 mL), normal contractility of the esophagus (repetitive antegrade contractility), and reduced distensibility of the pylorus (6.2-7.6 mm2/mm Hg at intraballoon volume of 50-60 mL). The pylorus was dilated up to 20 mm. After dilation, the distensibility of the pylorus had normalized (11.8-14.8 mm2/mm Hg at intraballoon volume of 50-60 mL). Pyloric and duodenal biopsies showed evidence of chronic inflammation with areas of subepithelial collagen deposition (Figure 1) consistent with CG and collagenous duodenitis, respectively. Notably, the patient reported immediate improvement of his nausea and vomiting after pyloric dilation. As H. pylori was ruled out, and due to the endoscopic and histological findings of nodular gastric mucosa and subepithelial collagenous plate, the diagnosis of CG was made.

He was prescribed budesonide, pantoprazole, metoclopramide, and simethicone for medical management. At 3-month follow-up, small bowel enteroscopy showed nodular gastric, duodenal, and jejunal mucosa. There was stenosis in the gastric antrum, which was dilated to 20 mm. Gastric biopsies showed chronic atrophic gastritis and focal intestinal metaplasia. Compared with previous biopsy findings, there was reduced thickening of subepithelial collagen in the gastric mucosa (Figure 2) and resolution of subepithelial collagen in the duodenum. Diagnostic colonoscopy with terminal ileum intubation did not reveal collagenous colitis. At his 5-month follow-up, he continued to experience symptomatic improvement with better appetite and weight gain.

DISCUSSION

Diagnosing CG can be challenging due to its nonspecific symptoms of nausea and weight loss. Even when EGD and gastric biopsy are performed, the diagnosis, such as in this case, can still be elusive. It is sometimes associated with H. pylori,6,7 but our patient consistently tested negative. He had previous biopsies obtained from the antrum. During our evaluation, we found the prominent presence of nodular mucosa in the pylorus, so we obtained biopsies from that region. The classic endoscopic features of CG include nodular gastric mucosa, diffuse mucosal changes, mosaic-like pattern, absence of ulceration, and thickened subepithelial collagen layer.8 The differential diagnosis includes lymphocytic, eosinophilic, or autoimmune gastritis, H. pylori-associated gastritis, reactive gastropathy, Crohn's disease, and medication induced gastritis. In this case, autoimmune gastritis was excluded by negative anti-parietal cell antibody whereas other differentials were ruled out based on histology.

This case highlights the importance of obtaining biopsies from areas with nodular or cobblestone appearance in the gastric mucosa as it may increase diagnostic yield. In addition, the inflammatory presence at the lower esophageal or pyloric sphincter caused by CG may contribute to decreased sphincter distensibility. The pyloric sphincter did not appear stenotic on endoscopy nor offered any resistance to passage of the endoscope. Given the resolution of vomiting and normalization of pyloric distensibility after dilation, we hypothesize that vomiting was caused by functional pyloric stenosis. The improvement in appetite and weight likely resulted from the use of budesonide and subsequent resolution of subepithelial collagen band in the duodenum. EndoFLIP assessment and treatment may serve as a helpful adjunct for symptom management in patients with CG.

Moreover, there is a recognized relationship between CG and collagenous colitis, as both share the histological feature of subepithelial collagen deposition. Although these are distinct entities affecting different areas of the gastrointestinal tract, they sometimes occur together suggesting a common underlying mechanism.9 Recognizing this, we performed colonoscopy in our patient 9 months after he started budesonide which revealed focally thickened subepithelial collagen in the cecum, but not in any other region of the colon. Although no definitive treatment has been established for CG, our patient benefited from budesonide, pantoprazole, metoclopramide, and simethicone.

Due to risk of recurrence or persistent disease, long-term follow-up is important for optimal management. Follow-up involves regular monitoring by a gastroenterologist through endoscopies with biopsies to assess disease progression. There are no current specific guidelines because of the rarity of the disease. In our patient, we continue to monitor him with an upper endoscopy annually given atrophic gastritis. Biochemical investigations to monitor nutritional status such as hemoglobin, vitamins (B12, folate), iron studies, and complete metabolic panel are recommended to identify and correct abnormalities early. Referral to a registered dietician can be beneficial in those with unintentional weight loss.
