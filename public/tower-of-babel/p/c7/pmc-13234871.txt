Novel Chitosan Bioink Derived from Eurycantha calcarata (Lucas, 1869) for 3D Bioprinting of Cell-Compatible Scaffolds
Authors: Zhenying Mei, Julie Lagouarde, Olga Volkova, Virginie Baylot, Christophe Candet, Philippe Barthélémy, Pavel Kuzhir, Guilhem Godeau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Three-dimensional (3D) printing of eco-friendly, naturally derived biopolymers such as chitosan offers great potential for biomedical applications. In this work, Eurycantha calcarata insects were used as the source of chitosan with a yield of 22.2% and a deacetylation degree of 77–78%, comparable to that of commercial chitosan. After extraction, characterizations were performed on the obtained compound (including FTIR, NMR, SEM, TGA, and XRD). Formulated into chitosan:gelatin hydrogels (EC:G = 1:3, 1:4, 1:5), the blends exhibited solid–liquid transitions at 29–33 °C depending on gelatin content. 3D-printed scaffolds achieved >90% shape accuracy and remained stable for 21 days in a culture medium. HeLa cells cultured on the scaffolds showed up to 99.6% viability after 3 days, demonstrating excellent cytocompatibility. By valorizing insect-derived waste, this work presents a sustainable and high-performance chitosan bioink, addressing the growing demand for eco-friendly materials in 3D bioprinting.

Introduction

Today, mankind is increasingly interested in natural polymers such as cellulose and chitosan for sustainable development. − The use of such natural and fully biocompatible materials in sustainable engineering is becoming increasingly important in view of the growing number of pollutants. Chitin and highly deacetylated chitin (chitosan) have a wide range of applications as natural biopolymers. , Chemically, chitin can be described as a linear polymer of β-(1,4)-linked N-acetylglucosamine (Figure a). Due to its poor solubility in most solvents (water and organics), chitin is difficult to use. This low solubility comes from its strong molecular interactions and compact macromolecular structure. To overcome this lack of solubility, chitin is usually deacetylated before industrial use. After deacetylation (deacetylation degree higher than 50%), chitin is often described as chitosan. Chitosan has a completely different solubility from that of chitin due to the free amine group released during deacetylation. Due to their properties (biocompatibility and biodegradability), chitin and chitosan have found wide applications in food, biomedical, agricultural, and textile industries. ,− Chitosan has a variety of applications, with three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting being of particular relevance. This area of research is currently growing, and in biology and preclinical evaluation, in vitro cell culture is critical. Traditional 2D cell culture involves cells growing on a flat surface, which limits cell interactions with other cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM), resulting in flattened cell morphology and a lack of biomechanical cues. Conversely, 3D cell culture involves the embedding of cells within a matrix, such as a hydrogel, which closely mimics the natural cellular microenvironment. − This facilitates the realization of physiologically relevant cell morphologies and functions by enabling realistic cell signaling, migration, and differentiation in three dimensions. ,, Three-dimensional (3D) culture is an ideal model for simulating complex diseases and drug responses, as it closely replicates in vivo conditions. Despite the fact that 3D printing technology has been the subject of extensive investigation for the purpose of tissue engineering, there has been a paucity of research on the use of chitosan-based materials. Further modifications and formulations are required in order to increase the printability of chitosan-based scaffolds. In addition, it is essential to identify natural sources of materials that will enhance the biocompatibility of the resulting materials.

Chitin, a prevalent biopolymer in nature, is frequently isolated from seafood waste, including crustaceans such as shrimp, lobster, and crab, as well as from coral and fungi. Chitin and chitosan from crustacean sources may trigger allergic reactions because of the presence of allergenic proteins. − It is anticipated that the demand for chitin and chitosan will increase in the near future owing to the expanding range of their applications and utilization. Consequently, it is imperative to investigate alternative sources that can satisfy the anticipated increase in demand. Insects are considered to be a potential source for the future industrial production of chitin and chitosan. The chitin and chitosan extraction from insects belonging to various orders including Coleoptera, Dictyoptera, Diptera, Ephemeroptera, Hemiptera, Hymenoptera, Lepidoptera, Odonata, and Orthoptera were reported and summarized. ,− The utilization of insects as a source of protein for human and domestic animal consumption has garnered significant attention due to their high nutritional value, efficient use of space and water, and reduced greenhouse gas emissions when compared with conventional animal protein production methods. However, as with seafood such as shrimp, lobster, and crab, the large-scale consumption of insects will result in the creation of waste that will require disposal. Therefore, it is possible to extract chitin and chitosan from these industrial wastes for the purpose of value creation.

While chitosan:gelatin hydrogels have been widely studied for 3D bioprinting, most existing formulations rely on chitosan derived from crustacean sources, which may pose allergenicity risks and raise sustainability concerns. Although insect-derived chitosans have been characterized in recent years, their potential as bioink components remains largely unexplored. In particular, systematic studies evaluating the printability, stability, and cytocompatibility of insect-derived chitosan–gelatin hydrogels are still lacking. This study addresses this gap by extracting chitosan from Eurycantha calcarata (Lucas 1869) (Figure b) and formulating it into chitosan:gelatin hydrogels for 3D bioprinting. The prepared chitosan was fully characterized using various methods, including deacetylation degree determination, evaluation of the ash content, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The printability, stability, and cytocompatibility of the resulting hydrogels were systematically evaluated to assess their suitability as a sustainable bioink for biomedical applications.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37 wt %), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), and sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The chemicals and solvents used for the present work are synthetic grade and were used without purification. The E. calcarata used was obtained from Parc Phoenix insectarium (Nice, France). Eggs were from sexual reproduction. Specimens were breed in a glass terrarium with fresh water and food plants (temperature of 21–25 °C and humidity of 70–85%) until the specimens died naturally. After death, the specimens were dried and kept in the laboratory stock. Observations were carried out on dried specimens. No specimens were sacrificed in the present work. Fetal bovine serum (FBS), minimum essential medium (MEM), nonessential amino acid solution (100X) (NEAA), Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium 1640 - GlutaMAX, penicillin-streptomycin, a live/dead cell staining kit, and sodium pyruvate (100 mM) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. All water mentioned was Milli-Q water (resistivity ≥ 18.2 MΩ·cm).

Chitosan Preparation

Chitosan was prepared following a four-step process comprising demineralization, deproteinization, deacetylation, and bleaching, similar to that reported in ref .

Characterization of Prepared Chitosan

1H NMR Characterization

All 1H NMR analyses were performed on a Bruker 400 MHz NMR spectrometer. The deuterated solvant used was CF3COOD. All chemical shifts are reported in ppm. The deacetylation degree (DDA) was calculated using a method previously reported that compares signals from acetylated and deacetylated glycosamine units: 1 DDA(%)=[H1‐D/(H1‐D+H‐Ac/3)]×100 In this equation, H1-D corresponds to the integration of the proton 1H peak of deacetylated saccharide and H–Ac corresponds to the integration of the three protons of the acetyl group peak.

Deacetylation Degree Titration

E. calcarata dried chitosan was dissolved in an aqueous HCl solution (0.1 M). After complete dissolution of the chitosan, the solution was titrated with a NaOH solution (0.1 M). The pH was continuously monitored using a pH meter. All titrations were performed using a Seven Compact pH meter (Mettler Toledo). Each condition was tested in triplicate. The degree of deacetylation (DDA) of the chitosan was determined using the following formula: 2 DDA(%)=2.03V2−V1m+0.0042(V2−V1) In the present equation, m corresponds to the sample mass (g) and V 1 and V 2 (mL) correspond to the volumes of NaOH solution at the deflection points. These two deflections can be respectively linked to the HCl and chitosan hydrochloride, and 2.03 and 0.0042 are coefficients resulting from the molecular weight of the chitin monomer unit and the difference between the molecular weights of chitin and chitosan monomer units, respectively.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) Analysis

X-ray diffraction patterns of the chitosan samples isolated from E. calcarata were recorded using a Panalytical X’Pert Pro diffractometer equipped with an Xcelerator fast detector, operating at 45 kV and 30 mA. The X-ray radiation was generated by a Cu Kα source (λ = 0.15418 Å). Diffraction data were collected over a 2θ range of 5 to 75°.

The crystallinity index (CrI) of the isolated chitosan samples was calculated from the XRD data using the following equation: 3 CrI=[(Icr−Iam)/Icr]×100 In this equation, I cr represents the measured intensity at 2θ = 19.6° and I am represents the measured intensity at 2θ = 12.6°. I cr and I am correspond respectively to the values for crystalline lattices and the amorphous region of the E. calcarata chitosan.

Elemental Analysis

Elemental analysis of E. calcarata chitosan was performed using a Flash EA 1112 series elemental analyzer (Thermo Finnigan) equipped with Eager Xperience software.

Preparation of E. calcarata Chitosan:Gelatin (ECG) Hydrogels

Gelatin, a denatured protein derived from collagen, is used in hydrogel formulations due to its excellent properties (biocompatibility, biodegradability, and promotion of cell adhesion ability). When blended with chitosan, gelatin can improve the mechanical properties and printability of the resulting hydrogels while maintaining a cell-friendly microenvironment. In this study, gelatin was incorporated to enhance the rheological behavior and structural integrity of the chitosan-based bioink for 3D bioprinting applications.

E. calcarata Chitosan (EC) Solution Preparation

Chitosan (600 mg) was dissolved in 2% acetic acid solution in water (20 mL). The dissolution was carried out at 40 °C using an ultrasonic bath (chitosan final concentration: 3% w/v). After dissolution, the solution was filtered while still warm. The pH of the solution was then increased to 4.7. The pH adjustments were carried out using a 0.5 M aqueous solution of sodium hydroxide.

Gelatin Solution Preparation

The gelatin (3.6 g) was dissolved in water (20 mL) at 40 °C using an ultrasonic bath. The solution was then filtered after complete dissolution of the gelatin. The filtration was performed while the solution was still warm.

ECG Hydrogel Preparation

EC solution was mixed with gelatin solution at different ratios (EC:G = 1:3, 1:4, and 1:5). The pH was adjusted to 6 using a solution of NaOH (0.5 M in water).

Cross-Linking Strategies

TPP was dissolved in deionized water to get a TPP aqueous solution with a concentration of 10% (w/v). The cross-linking was performed by immersing the printed structures into the cross-linking solutions for 1 h. After immersion, the cross-linked materials were subsequently rinsed with water.

General Characterization of Both Chitosan and ECG Hydrogels

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

All scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations were carried out using a Phenom Pro X desktop SEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples were sputter-coated with gold using a Quorum Q150R S sputter coater and imaged at accelerating voltages of 5 kV and 10 kV.

TGA and DSC Measurements

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) were performed simultaneously using a STA 449 F5 Jupiter ECO thermal analyzer (Netzsch). Samples were heated from 35 to 850 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen flow of 50 mL·min–1. The temperature was then maintained at 850 °C for 4 h.

FTIR Characterization

All Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using a Spectrum Two FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer) equipped with a universal Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR) accessory. Spectra were collected over the wavenumber range of 4000–500 cm–1.

Characterization of Prepared ECG Hydrogels

Rheological Measurements of ECG Hydrogels

Rheological measurements were carried out using an 502 (Anton Paar) rheometer in parallel plate geometry (PP40/TI, plate radius of 2 cm, gap of 0.5 mm). To prevent water evaporation, a solvent trap was used during measurement. Storage (G′) and loss (G″) moduli were measured in a linear viscoelastic regime (strain amplitude γ = 0.5%, oscillation frequency f = 1 Hz) as a function of temperature imposed on the lower rheometer plate using an integrated Peltier element. For each tested condition, three replicates were considered.

3D Printing Assays of ECG Scaffolds

3D printing was carried out using a Bio-X bioprinter from Cellink (Boston, MA, USA). The printing was run using an extrusion process. The cartridge was maintained at room temperature. The temperatures of the substrate and bed were respectively kept constant at 22 and 10 °C. A bioprinting nozzle (22G) was used to print hydrogels (Figure SI 1). The printing parameters, consisting of the translational speed of the printing head (the jet flow speed) of 5 mm·s–1 and the extrusion pressure within 10 kPa, were selected and kept constant throughout the study to guarantee proper and continuous filament formation.

Printing Accuracy Characterization

The shape fidelity of the 3D-printed samples was evaluated for each formulation. Dimensions (length and width) were measured immediately before and after cross-linking in the TPP solution. For each formulation, five replicate samples were analyzed. Regarding fiber dimensions, the diameter of each fiber was measured at six different points under each condition and then averaged. All measurements were performed using an optical comparator (Keyence IM-Series). The quantitative data obtained for the different conditions were subsequently compared to the theoretical values using the following equation: accuracy(%)=100n∑1n(1−|dr−ds|ds) 4 In this equation, d r is the measured sample dimension and d s is the theoretical dimension (i.e., d s = 10 mm for length and n is the number of samples considered (n = 5 × 6 different points).

Stability Test

A stability test was performed on samples from different formulations to evaluate their degradation after immersion in RPMI 1640 - GlutaMAX medium at 37 °C. At predefined time points (t = 0 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 1 day, 2 days, 3 days, 7 days, 14 days, and 21 days), the samples were removed from the medium, gently wiped with tissue paper, and weighed. For each tested condition, three replicates were analyzed. The degradation ratio was calculated using the following equation: 5 degradation ratio(%)=(W0−Wt)/W0×100% In the present equation, W t represents the weight of samples at a given time and W 0 represents the initial weight of samples before immersion in the medium.

In Vitro Cytocompatibility of ECG Scaffolds

As preliminary test, an in vitro cytocompatibility assay was performed using the HeLa cell line (American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)-number: CCL-2) to assess the cytocompatibility of the printed scaffolds. To avoid contamination, the gelatin and chitosan were sterilized using UV irradiation for 1 h. The printed structures (10 mm × 10 mm × 5 mm) were placed in contact with HeLa cells. Six replicates were considered for all tested conditions. HeLa cells cultured in complete medium were used as the positive control (i.e., live cells), while HeLa cells cultured in complete medium and 0.1% Triton X-100 were used as the negative control (i.e., dead cells). These controls represent the optimal and the worst cell viability conditions, respectively. To prepare the complete medium, 500 mL of RPMI 1640 - GlutaMAX was supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), 5 mL of MEM nonessential amino acid solution (100X) (NEAA), 5 mL of sodium pyruvate (100 mM), and 5 mL of penicillin-streptomycin solution. 3D-printed ECG scaffolds were seeded with 5 × 103 cells per well in a 24-well plate and cultured in 500 μL of complete medium at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Subsequently, 3 days after seeding, the viability of HeLa cells on the 3D-printed ECG scaffolds was evaluated with a Tecan Infinite M1000 PRO microplate reader.

After 3 days, the cell-seeded printed scaffolds were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). A LIVE/DEAD cell staining kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was then used according to the manufacturer’s instructions to assess the cell viability. Briefly, 2 μM calcein-AM (live dye) and 4 μM ethidium homodimer-1 (dead dye) were mixed to get the staining solution. This solution was evenly added to the scaffold surface and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Excess dye was removed by washing with PBS, after which the scaffolds were mounted on microscope slides and imaged under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axiovert 200M). Results are presented as average values with the standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the statistical differences between experimental and control groups, with a p value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results and Discussion

E. calcarata Chitosan Characterization

Chitosan Preparation

In the literature, diverse strategies have been investigated for chitin extraction and chitosan preparation. − For the purposes of this investigation, a straightforward chemical approach already used for Heliocopris chitosan extraction was employed and is explicated comprehensively in section . The chitosan preparation process from E. calcarata specimens comprised four consecutive steps: demineralization, deproteination, deacetylation, and bleaching. The overall yield of the ultimate product chitosan was determined to be 22.2%.

1H NMR Characterization

The degree of deacetylation (DDA) was determined using both 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figure SI 2) and titration (Figure SI 3). Due to the polymeric structure of the chitosan extracted from E. calcarata, all signals were relatively broad. The characteristic peaks were observed at δH = 2.65 ppm (H–Ac), 3.97 ppm (H2 of deacetylated units), 4.10–4.90 ppm (H2 to H6), 5.25–5.50 ppm (H1 of acetylated units), and 5.55–5.85 ppm (H1 of deacetylated units). The integrals of the H1–D and H–Ac signals were 1.2 and 1.0, respectively. Using eq , the DDA of the chitosan from E. calcarata was calculated to be 78.2%.

Deacetylation Degree Titration

The titration curve displayed two distinct inflections, each corresponding to a different stage of the reaction (Figure SI 3). The first inflection point reflected the neutralization of the excess HCl in the solution, while the second indicated the chitosan hydrochloride deprotonation. Based on the data and eq , the degree of deacetylation (DDA) of E. calcarata chitosan was found to be 77.3 ± 2.0%, which aligns well with the DDA value obtained by NMR. The DDA of E. calcarata chitosan (77–78%) falls within the range reported for commercial chitosan (75–95%) and is comparable to values reported for other insect-derived chitosans such as Tenebrio molitor (70–80%) and Heliocopris beetles (80–85%).

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) Analysis

For the comprehensive characterization of chitosan, XRD investigations were undertaken on E. calcarata chitosan, as shown in Figure SI 4. Peaks were observed at 10.8 and 20.0°, which was consistent with insect chitosan in the literature. ,, The XRD measurements align with established chitosan patterns, facilitating the determination of the crystallinity index (Crl). The crystallinity index (CrI) of E. calcarata chitosan (12.2%) is lower than that of typical commercial chitosan (10–30%), indicating a more amorphous structure that may enhance the flexibility and hydration capacity, which are beneficial for hydrogel formation and 3D bioprinting.

Elemental Analysis

Elemental analysis results of E. calcarata chitosan are presented in Table . The obtained experimental data are all consistent with the expected theoretical elemental proportions for chitosan. , The high nitrogen content in E. calcarata chitosan reflects the number of amino groups (NH2) present. The high amino group quantity in chitosan significantly affects its solubility, viscosity, and compatibility. These factors contribute to its effectiveness in 3D printing applications by enhancing the flowability, improving material interactions, and ensuring structural stability. Amino groups can increase the viscosity of chitosan solutions by forming more hydrogen bonds, ensuring that it has the right flow characteristics for 3D printing. Amino content affects the gelation and hardening rates, which in turn impact the accuracy and stability of printed structures. In addition, a high amino content may enhance the compatibility with other materials, such as biopolymers and synthetic materials, by reacting with acidic groups in those materials, potentially improving the overall performance of the 3D-printed structures.

Scanning Electron Microscopy Analysis

To observe the surface morphology of the original E. calcarata sample and the extracted chitosan, SEM observations were performed. This analysis aimed to assess whether any structural changes or surface modifications occurred during the extraction process as these changes might affect the suitability of the material for subsequent applications (such as gel formation).

Before and after a series of treatments, the surfaces presented similar morphologies without any specific structure.

SEM images of a raw sample (a, b) and chitosan (c, d) from E. calcarata.

Thermal Analysis

The thermal characteristics of E. calcarata chitosan were subjected to analysis, along with spectroscopic characterization. To get the degradation profile of chitosan, the sample was heated from 35 to 850 °C at a controlled rate, followed by an isothermal hold at 850 °C for 4 h. The resulting profile revealed two distinct degradation stages. The first stage, occurring up to approximately 100 °C, corresponds to the evaporation of adsorbed moisture, accounting for a 10% weight loss. The second stage is linked to the main thermal decomposition of the chitosan backbone and is centered at around 271 °C (Figure SI 5). After the 4 h isothermal period at 850 °C, the remaining residue can be attributed to mineral matter or ash content. The ash content is an important parameter, as it may drastically impact the properties of the material. In numerous applications involving chitosan, especially for hydrogel preparation, a low ash level is preferable. In the case of the E. calcarata chitosan analyzed in this work, the residual ash percentage was identified as 10% of the initial sample mass.

The insights into the thermal behavior of chitosan were further evaluated through the differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curve. A degradation peak was observed at 315 °C, and this peak can be associated with chitosan (Figure SI 6). Under the same measurement condition, commercial shrimp chitosan exhibits similar behavior. This degradation temperature aligns with values reported in the literature for chitosan. Thermal behavior analysis and ash content determination provide essential insights into the stability and purity of the chitosan sample, which are critical factors for its applications. The E. calcarata chitosan, with thermal stability and purity comparable to those of commercial chitosan, presents a valuable alternative source. This offers significant potential for sustainable and high-quality chitosan production, reducing the reliance on traditional marine sources and expanding the scope of its industrial applications.

FTIR Characterization

The chitosan from E. calcarata exhibits strong absorption bands at 3430–3330 and 3250–3320 cm–1 (Table and Figure SI 7), corresponding to the stretching vibrations of O–H and N–H bonds, respectively. The sp3 C–H stretching vibration of the CH2 groups appears at 2820–2960 cm–1, while the CO stretching vibration of the amide group is observed at 1630–1660 cm–1. Additionally, the N–H bending vibrations are visible in the region of 1540–1575 cm–1. As expected, the IR spectrum of E. calcarata chitosan is consistent with data reported in the literature for shrimp-derived chitosan.

ECG Hydrogel Characterization

SEM Analysis

The prepared ECG hydrogels were examined by SEM. The imaging was performed on dried hydrogel samples (Figure ). The micrographs reveal a membranelike structure, which is consistent with the morphology of dry chitosan:gelatin hydrogels reported in the literature.

Thermal Analysis

The thermal stability and thermal decomposition of the dried ECG hydrogels were investigated using TGA, and the results are shown in Figure . The curve of non-cross-linked ECG hydrogels showed a main zone of weight loss of about 45.0%, from 295 to 310 °C, coming from the intermolecular disintegration and the decomposition of polymeric chains between E. calcarata chitosan and gelatin. These observations are consistent with the results described in the literature for a similar hydrogel. The curve of ECG hydrogels cross-linked with TPP showed a weight loss of about 12.2–13.3%, at 195–202 °C, ascribed to the disintegration between TPP and chitosan:gelatin gels; the weight loss of about 29.8 to 36.2%, at 329–338 °C, represented the decomposition of polymeric chains between E. calcarata chitosan and gelatin. These results indicate that the thermal stability of the ECG hydrogels was enhanced through cross-linking with TPP.

FTIR Analysis

The interactions between E. calcarata chitosan and gelatin in the blend and the effect of TPP cross-linking under different conditions were investigated by FTIR spectroscopy (Figure ). The characteristic vibrations of amide III (C–N and N–H), amide II (N–H and C–N), amide I (CO and N–H, primarily the carbonyl group), and amide A (N–H) were observed. Comparison of the spectra of the pristine materials with those of the blends under each cross-linking condition revealed peak shifts and the disappearance of certain bands. In all FTIR spectra of the ECG hydrogels, regardless of the chitosan:gelatin ratio or cross-linking condition, the carbonyl peak shifted from 1624 to 1632–1637 cm–1 and the amino peak shifted from 1560 to 1535–1543 cm–1. Additionally, the gelatin peaks at 3063 cm–1 (amide A) and 2933 cm–1 (amide B) disappeared in the blends, indicating electrostatic interactions between chitosan and gelatin (Table ). Overall, the IR bands observed for E. calcarata chitosan, gelatin, and the ECG hydrogel blends are consistent with data reported in the literature.

Rheological Measurements

The measurements shown in Figure indicate that, for all three formulations, the storage modulus (G′) exceeds the loss modulus (G′′) at low temperatures. For the 1EC:3G gel, G′ gradually decreases between 10 and 24 °C, signaling the onset of gel melting. Above 24 °C, both moduli drop sharply, reflecting rapid melting of the gel. At 29 °C, G′ becomes nearly equal to G′′, marking the solid-to-liquid transition (gel melting point). These results suggest that the 1EC:3G gel remains fully solid at 24 °C but is completely molten at 29 °C. Similar behaviors are observed for two other tested formulations (1EC:4G and 1EC:5G), except for the melting temperature (assigned to the crossover of G′ and G′′ curves), which is 31 °C for 1EC:4G and 33 °C for 1EC:5G. Therefore, we reveal a slight increase in the melting temperature with an increasing proportion of gelatin in the ECG blend. These properties make the material suitable for 3D printing applications. The gel should be extruded at a temperature lower than the melting temperature. In extrusion-based printing, initially, the material has good shape preservation. During the extrusion phase, as shear forces increase, viscosity decreases. After extrusion, the temperature of the bed is much lower than the melting temperature, and the viscosity increases so that it can preserve the printed shape.

Measurement of storage (G′) and loss (G′′) moduli as a function of the temperature (γ = 0.5%, f = 1H) for ECG hydrogels with different formulations.

3D Printing Assays

In the context of 3D printing, preservation of the printed shape is of great importance. Indeed, after printing, there is a risk that the hydrogel may flow under the combined effects of gravity and its inherent fluidity, thereby losing its intended printed shape. The investigation of shape fidelity for each formulation of 3D-printed scaffolds was performed by measuring the dimensions, such as the length and width (Figure ).

Example of 3D printing scaffolds: (A) 3D printing process, (B) 3D-printed scaffolds, and (C) measurements of the scaffolds.

Compared to the designed structures, all printed structures showed a high degree of fidelity with dimensional variations of less than 10%. The formed hydrogels did not flow after printing, and the printed shapes remained well preserved for several hours at room temperature (images in Figure were captured after 2 h). This observation indicates that the formulated hydrogel was suitable for bioprinting.

Stability Test

A stability test was conducted in vitro on the printed samples for each gel formulation, maintaining the samples in RPMI medium 1640 - GlutaMAX at 37 °C for up to 21 days (Figure ). A comparison of the 1EC:3G, 1EC:4G, and 1EC:5G formulations showed that the weight variation was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) between the 1EC:3G and 1EC:4G conditions at any time point during the stability test. In contrast, the 1EC:5G scaffolds exhibited significantly higher weight loss compared to the other formulations after day 7 (p < 0.05), indicating gelatin-dependent degradation behavior. The study by Tiziana et al. also investigated the stability of TPP cross-linked chitosan:gelatin scaffolds. The weight loss of 1 chitosan:3 gelatin scaffolds was approximately 25% after 3 days when the cross-linking time was 60 min. In comparison, the 1EC:3G and 1EC:4G scaffolds in this study lost about 20% after 21 days, demonstrating superior long-term stability.

Stability of ECG hydrogels at different test times.

The comparison of stability results for scaffolds with or without cross-linking indicates that non-cross-linked scaffolds exhibited higher stability than TPP-cross-linked ones under the same conditions. This counterintuitive observation may be explained by the fact that TPP cross-linking can alter the hydrogel’s porosity and network structure, potentially facilitating water penetration and accelerating the hydrolysis of labile bonds. Additionally, TPP may interact with components in the culture medium, leading to localized ion exchange or structural modifications that promote the degradation. Further studies are required to elucidate the precise mechanisms that are responsible for this behavior.

In Vitro Cytocompatibility Test

Cytocompatibility is a critical factor in assessing the clinical potential of the tissue-engineering scaffolds. To evaluate this parameter for the ECG scaffolds, HeLa cells were seeded onto the surfaces of the different scaffolds, cultured for 3 days, and then subjected to a live/dead cell assay. Quantitative cell viability data and fluorescent images of the live/dead-stained cells are presented in Figures and , respectively. Among all of the scaffolds tested, the 1EC:3G formulation showed the highest cell viability, reaching 99.6% on day 3. A similar result was found for 1EC:4G scaffolds, with a cell viability of 98.7% on day 3. Meanwhile, 1EC:5G, 1EC:3G/TPP, 1EC:4G/TPP, and 1EC:5G/TPP scaffolds also presented high viability, with values of 91.9, 90.7, 84.7, and 84.6% for HeLa cells on day 3, respectively. Statistical analysis revealed that the 1EC:4G/TPP and 1EC:5G/TPP scaffolds were significantly different from the control group (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed for the other formulations (p > 0.05). A comparison of cell viability between scaffolds with and without TPP cross-linking showed that non-cross-linked scaffolds consistently exhibited higher cell viability, although these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons). The higher cell viability observed in non-cross-linked scaffolds may be attributed to their greater hydrophilicity and less dense network structure, which could facilitate nutrient diffusion and cell infiltration. In contrast, TPP cross-linking may introduce residual ions or alter the surface chemistry, potentially affecting the initial cell attachment. These findings are consistent with the stability results (Figure ), where cross-linked scaffolds also showed faster degradation.

It is important to recognize the limitations of this cytocompatibility assessment. The study was conducted using only one cell line (HeLa) and a single time point (3 days), which may not fully capture long-term cell behavior, including proliferation, migration, and differentiation. Future studies should include extended culture periods (e.g., 7–14 days) and additional cell types to better evaluate the biological performance of these scaffolds.

Despite these limitations, the observed cell viabilities are consistent with findings reported for chitosan-based scaffolds in the literature. For example, previous studies have shown that chitosan:gelatin scaffolds are typically highly cytocompatible, supporting cell viability above 90% within 72 h with no significant difference compared to negative controls.

The fluorescent images of the live/dead cell assay on the printed ECG scaffolds showed high cell viability with only a few dead cells observed. As indicated by the arrows, cells were embedded within the 3D matrix of the gels, closely mimicking the natural cell microenvironment. Unlike traditional 2D cell culture, which results in a monolayer with limited cell–cell and cell–ECM interactions, the 3D architecture of these scaffolds supports more physiologically relevant cell morphologies and functions. , These results confirm that the printed ECG scaffolds feature favorable cytocompatibility and nontoxicity, positioning them as promising candidates for future 3D bioprinting applications. Our findings align with other studies showing that chitosan-based scaffolds support high cell viability and promote natural cell behavior in 3D cultures.

Conclusions

We report in this work the successful extraction of chitosan from E. calcarata insects with a yield of 22.2% and a deacetylation degree of 77–78%, comparable to those of commercial and other insect-derived chitosans (Table SI 1). The extracted chitosan was formulated into gelatin-based hydrogels (EC:G = 1:3, 1:4, 1:5), which exhibited solid–liquid transitions between 29 and 33 °C and good printability, achieving shape accuracy exceeding 90%. Printed scaffolds remained stable for up to 21 days in culture medium and supported HeLa cell viability above 99% after 3 days, indicating excellent cytocompatibility.

Compared with existing chitosan:gelatin bioinks, the material derived from E. calcarata offers a sustainable alternative by valorizing insect-derived waste. While its performance is competitive with that of conventional sources, further optimization such as extended cytocompatibility studies and mechanical testing would strengthen its applicability in tissue engineering. Overall, this work establishes insect-derived chitosan as a promising and eco-friendly bioink for 3D bioprinting applications.
