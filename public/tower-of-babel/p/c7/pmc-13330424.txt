Twin live birth after resuscitative cesarean delivery in a woman with eclampsia and cardiac arrest in a low-resource setting

Introduction

Resuscitative cesarean delivery (RCD), previously referred to as perimortem cesarean section (PMCS) or perimortem cesarean delivery (PMCD), refers to cesarean delivery performed during maternal cardiac arrest primarily to improve maternal resuscitation by relieving aortocaval compression [1]. It is often accompanied by severe complications such as cerebral hemorrhage, pulmonary edema, and multi-organ failure, which can rapidly progress to cardiac arrest if not managed promptly [2]. Cardiac arrest in pregnancy, though rare, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 30,000 pregnancies, carries a high mortality rate due to the physiological changes of pregnancy that complicate resuscitation efforts [3]. Resuscitative cesarean delivery, defined as cesarean delivery performed during maternal cardiac arrest, is primarily a maternal resuscitative intervention aimed at relieving aortocaval compression and improving the effectiveness of cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Neonatal survival, although an important secondary benefit, is not the primary indication for the procedure. However, it is associated with significant risks, including hemorrhage, infection, and neonatal morbidity [4].

Globally, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, including eclampsia, account for approximately 14% of maternal deaths, with the majority occurring in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia [10]. In Africa, eclampsia remains a major contributor to maternal mortality, with studies reporting a case fatality rate of up to 15% in some regions [11]. In Kenya, hypertensive disorders are the second leading cause of maternal death, accounting for 16% of all maternal mortality, with eclampsia being a significant contributor [8]. Cardiac arrest in pregnancy, though less common, is often fatal, with survival rates as low as 6.9% in high-income countries and even lower in low-resource settings due to delays in recognition and intervention [12]. The interplay of these conditions—eclampsia, cardiac arrest, and the need for PMCS—creates a complex clinical scenario that requires rapid, coordinated, and skilled management to prevent catastrophic outcomes.

The rarity and significance of this case lie in the convergence of multiple high-risk factors: a twin pregnancy, severe preeclampsia progressing to eclampsia, and subsequent cardiac arrest necessitating PMCS. Twin pregnancies are associated with a higher risk of hypertensive disorders, including preeclampsia, due to increased placental mass and hormonal changes [13]. The rapid progression of eclampsia to pulmonary edema and cardiac arrest in this case underscores the aggressive nature of the disease and the challenges in managing such emergencies, particularly in resource-limited settings. Furthermore, the successful delivery of live twins via PMCS in the context of maternal cardiac arrest highlights the importance of timely intervention and the potential for favorable neonatal outcomes even in dire circumstances [14].

This case highlights several clinically important issues beyond the performance of resuscitative cesarean delivery itself. First, twin gestation may exacerbate aortocaval compression and further compromise cardiopulmonary resuscitation effectiveness during maternal cardiac arrest. Second, the case illustrates the challenges of implementing guideline-based maternal resuscitation in a low-resource setting where advanced airway support, intensive care resources, blood products, and rapid multidisciplinary response may be limited. Finally, despite timely resuscitative cesarean delivery, the absence of maternal return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) emphasizes the severity of cardiovascular collapse associated with eclampsia complicated by pulmonary edema.

Case presentation

A 29-year-old gravida 2, para 1 woman at 34 + 5 weeks’ gestation with twins presented to a tertiary referral hospital in Kenya with a one-day history of epigastric pain, which was sharp, persistent, and severe, associated with headache, dizziness, visual disturbance, and dry cough. She also reported lower abdominal pain radiating to the back, increasing in intensity and frequency, associated with drainage of non-foul-smelling liquor that started four hours prior to admission. There was no history of per vaginal bleeding or prior convulsions.

The patient had been attending regular follow-ups twice monthly at the high-risk clinic (HRC) until the nationwide doctor’s strike caused her to miss four appointments. Antenatal blood pressures were within normal limits, and she was not on antihypertensive medication. Her past medical history included a prior cesarean section in 2017 for severe preeclampsia during a previous twin pregnancy.

On examination, the patient appeared acutely ill, with the following findings: she was confused, dizzy, and in obvious respiratory distress. Her blood pressure was severely elevated at 210/149 mmHg, pulse rate was 140 bpm, and oxygen saturation was 85% on room air. Chest auscultation revealed bilateral crepitations, suggestive of pulmonary edema. Abdominal examination showed a tender uterus with fundal height consistent with 34 + 5 weeks’ gestation. Fetal heart rates were 120 bpm for the first twin and 118 bpm for the second twin. Neurological examination revealed hyperreflexia and clonus, consistent with eclampsia. Initial laboratory investigations were limited at presentation due to the emergent nature of the case. Available results included hemoglobin 13 g/dL, white blood cell count 10.7 × 10⁹/L, and platelet count 179 × 10⁹/L. Liver function tests, renal function tests, and coagulation profile were not available prior to deterioration.

Based on the patient’s severe hypertension, pulmonary findings, hyperreflexia, clonus, and altered mental status, a clinical diagnosis of severe preeclampsia with pulmonary edema was made immediately on admission. Initial emergency management, including intravenous magnesium sulfate, oxygen supplementation, antihypertensive therapy with intravenous labetalol, and intravenous furosemide, was initiated promptly. A portable AP chest X-ray was obtained concurrently to further evaluate the extent of pulmonary edema and exclude other cardiopulmonary pathology.

Shortly after admission, her condition rapidly deteriorated. She became unresponsive, experienced generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and frothy secretions emanated from her mouth and nose. Cyanosis ensued, and vital signs (blood pressure, oxygen saturation) became unrecordable, indicating cardiac arrest.

Immediate resuscitation efforts were initiated, including high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and continuous suctioning of airway secretions to clear frothy secretions. Despite these efforts, return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was not achieved after approximately 6 min. Resuscitation included bag-mask ventilation with supplemental oxygen, continuous chest compressions, suctioning of frothy airway secretions, intravenous epinephrine administered according to ACLS protocol, and ongoing left uterine displacement. Advanced airway intubation was not immediately available. CPR was continued for approximately 5 min following delivery without ROSC before death was declared.

Given the absence of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) despite ongoing cardiopulmonary resuscitation and the advanced gestational age, emergency resuscitative cesarean delivery was performed to relieve aortocaval compression and improve maternal resuscitative efforts. Intraoperative findings revealed a retroplacental hematoma and dark, thin/watery blood, consistent with abruptio placentae. The cervix was 8 cm dilated.

The procedure resulted in the extraction of live twins:

Second Twin (Male): Birth weight: 2.4 kg, Apgar scores: 2/4/7.

Second Twin (Male): Birth weight: 2.4 kg, Apgar scores: 2/4/7.

Umbilical cord blood gas analysis was not available due to resource limitations at the facility.

Both neonates received immediate resuscitation and were transferred to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU). No blood transfusion was administered during the resuscitative efforts or perioperative period due to the rapid maternal deterioration and absence of immediate indication of massive hemorrhage. Despite continued resuscitation efforts following delivery, the patient did not regain spontaneous circulation.

Laboratory findings included a baseline full hemogram (FHG) with WBC: 10.7, HB: 13, and PLT: 179. The patient’s death was attributed to acute cardiovascular collapse, flash pulmonary edema, and eclampsia. A postmortem examination was ordered; however, the relatives declined.

At follow-up several months after delivery, both twins were reported by the family to be thriving and meeting age-appropriate developmental milestones. They have shown no signs of long-term neurological or developmental complications and are meeting all age-appropriate milestones. The family reports that the twins are healthy and active, with no ongoing medical concerns. The first twin (female) now weighs 6.5 kg, has achieved normal developmental milestones, and is breastfeeding well. The second twin (male) now weighs 7.0 kg, is also breastfeeding well, and has shown no signs of developmental delay. Figure 1 shows the twins at their current age, healthy and thriving.

Discussion

This case represents a critical obstetric emergency involving severe eclampsia, pulmonary edema, and cardiac arrest in a twin pregnancy, culminating in a perimortem cesarean section (PMCS). The management and outcomes of this case provide valuable insights into the pathophysiology of these conditions, the rationale for PMCS, and the challenges encountered in resource-limited settings.

Eclampsia, a severe complication of preeclampsia, is characterized by the onset of seizures and is a leading cause of maternal mortality worldwide, particularly in low-resource settings [1]. The pathophysiology of eclampsia involves endothelial dysfunction, systemic vasoconstriction, and end-organ damage, which can lead to complications such as cerebral hemorrhage, pulmonary edema, and multi-organ failure [2]. Several factors may have contributed to failure to achieve ROSC in this patient. Severe pulmonary edema likely caused profound hypoxemia and impaired oxygen delivery during resuscitation. The patient may also have experienced significant metabolic derangement associated with severe eclampsia and placental abruption. In addition, twin gestation and advanced pregnancy likely worsened aortocaval compression and reduced CPR effectiveness prior to uterine decompression.

In this case, the patient’s rapid progression from severe preeclampsia to eclampsia and subsequent cardiac arrest highlights the aggressive nature of the disease. Cardiac arrest in pregnancy, though rare, is associated with high mortality due to the physiological changes of pregnancy, such as increased cardiac output and aortocaval compression, which complicate resuscitation efforts [3]. The survival rate for maternal cardiac arrest is approximately 6.9%, with timely PMCS being a critical intervention to improve outcomes [4].

Eclampsia arises from the failure of placental vascular remodeling, leading to placental ischemia and the release of anti-angiogenic factors such as soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1) and soluble endoglin (sEng) [5]. These factors cause widespread endothelial dysfunction, resulting in hypertension, proteinuria, and end-organ damage. In severe cases, this can progress to pulmonary edema, as seen in this patient, due to increased vascular permeability and left ventricular dysfunction [6]. Cardiac arrest in pregnancy is often precipitated by hypoxemia, hemorrhage, or thromboembolic events, with eclampsia being a significant contributor [7]. The physiological changes of pregnancy, such as reduced functional residual capacity and increased oxygen demand, further exacerbate the risk of hypoxemia and cardiac arrest [8].

After approximately 20 weeks of gestation, the gravid uterus can significantly compress the inferior vena cava and aorta in the supine position, reducing venous return, cardiac output, and the effectiveness of chest compressions during cardiac arrest. Resuscitative cesarean delivery relieves this compression, improving preload and potentially enhancing maternal resuscitation efforts. Resuscitative cesarean delivery is primarily performed to improve maternal resuscitation during cardiac arrest by relieving aortocaval compression, improving venous return, and enhancing cardiac output during CPR. Although fetal survival may improve following rapid delivery, neonatal benefit is considered secondary to maternal resuscitation. The rationale for PMCS is based on the principle that relieving aortocaval compression can improve venous return and cardiac output, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) [9]. Recent American Heart Association (AHA) and European Resuscitation Council (ERC) guidelines continue to emphasize early high-quality CPR, left uterine displacement, airway optimization, and resuscitative cesarean delivery within 4–5 min of maternal cardiac arrest when ROSC is not achieved [15, 16].

Twin gestation may have contributed significantly to the resuscitative challenges observed in this case. Compared with singleton pregnancies, twin pregnancies are associated with greater uterine size and increased aortocaval compression, which can further impair venous return and reduce the effectiveness of chest compressions during maternal cardiac arrest. The enlarged gravid uterus in multifetal gestation may therefore worsen maternal hemodynamic compromise and reduce the likelihood of ROSC if uterine decompression is delayed. Furthermore, resuscitative cesarean delivery in twin pregnancy presents additional technical and logistical challenges, including prolonged extraction time, greater risk of hemorrhage, and the simultaneous need for neonatal resuscitation of two infants.”

This case is unique due to several factors. Twin pregnancies are associated with a higher risk of preeclampsia and eclampsia due to increased placental mass and hormonal changes [11]. The presence of twins likely contributed to the severity of the patient’s condition and the rapid progression to cardiac arrest. Despite the maternal cardiac arrest and delayed PMCS, both twins were delivered alive and survived without long-term complications. This highlights the potential for favorable neonatal outcomes even in dire circumstances. The case occurred in a resource-limited setting, where delays in recognition and intervention are common. The successful outcome, despite these challenges, underscores the importance of preparedness and multidisciplinary coordination in managing obstetric emergencies.

The management of this case was fraught with challenges, including the rapid progression of eclampsia to cardiac arrest, limited resources, and the need for immediate decision-making. Key lessons learned include the importance of early recognition of preeclampsia with severe features and prompt initiation of antihypertensive and anticonvulsant therapy. Delays in treatment can lead to catastrophic outcomes, as seen in this case. Effective management of obstetric emergencies requires close collaboration between obstetricians, anesthesiologists, neonatologists, and nursing staff. In this case, the decision to perform PMCS was made in consultation with the obstetric team, highlighting the importance of teamwork. The lack of advanced monitoring and resuscitation equipment in resource-limited settings poses significant challenges. Hospitals in such settings should prioritize the availability of essential obstetric emergency supplies and training for healthcare providers.Detailed documentation and institutional review of maternal cardiac arrest cases remain essential for quality improvement, training, and optimization of obstetric emergency response systems, particularly in low-resource settings.

While PMCS was performed slightly beyond the recommended 4-minute window, the successful delivery of live twins demonstrates that favorable outcomes are possible even with delayed intervention. However, efforts should be made to adhere to guidelines to maximize the chances of survival for both mother and neonate.

This case highlights the critical importance of early recognition, aggressive management, and timely intervention in the management of severe eclampsia and cardiac arrest in pregnancy. The successful delivery of live twins via PMCS, despite the challenges encountered, underscores the potential for favorable outcomes even in resource-limited settings. This case also serves as a reminder of the need for ongoing training, preparedness, and multidisciplinary collaboration to improve maternal and neonatal outcomes in obstetric emergencies.

The low-resource context likely contributed significantly to the unfavorable maternal outcome. Advanced airway management, invasive hemodynamic monitoring, immediate intensive care support, bedside echocardiography, rapid blood gas analysis, and massive transfusion capability were limited or unavailable during resuscitation. These limitations may have impaired optimization of oxygenation, identification of reversible causes of arrest, and post-arrest critical care. In many low-resource settings, delays in referral, shortages of trained personnel, and limited emergency preparedness further complicate the management of maternal cardiac arrest.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of timely intervention in managing severe eclampsia and cardiac arrest in pregnancy. Despite challenges, the successful delivery of live twins via PMCS demonstrates the potential for favorable neonatal outcomes. The case underscores the need for preparedness, multidisciplinary coordination, and resource allocation in obstetric emergencies.
