Nitrate maintains mitochondrial membrane integrity by increasing Sialin-FKBP8 granules via liquid-liquid-phase separation
Authors: Xue Wang, Huan Liu, Minwei Yue, Zhaochen Shan, Jinsong Wang, Xiaogang Wang, Dong Zhang, Songlin Wang, Lei Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondria, the metabolic centers of cells, play a pivotal role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and regulating physiological processes. Mitochondrial membrane disruption and consequent dysfunction are key pathogenic mechanisms underlying various diseases, including irradiation (IR)-induced salivary gland damage. Our previous studies have demonstrated that nitrate exerts preventive effects against mitochondrial dysfunction and IR-induced salivary gland injury. However, the mechanism remains unclear. In the present study, supplementation with nitrate significantly reduced mitochondrial damage to salivary epithelial cells in the context of IR-induced salivary gland damage in vivo and cellular injury to human salivary gland cells (HSGs) in vitro. Nitrate alleviated mitochondrial membrane damage in salivary epithelial cells mainly by maintaining the integrity of the mitochondrial membrane. Mechanistically, nitrate upregulates the expression of the nitrate transporter Sialin in salivary epithelial cells, along with H+ importation and subsequent cytoplasmic acidification. The high expression of Sialin, coupled with a low cytoplasmic pH, synergistically promotes the interaction between Sialin and FK506-binding protein 8 (FKBP8), leading to the formation of Sialin-FKBP8 granules through liquid-liquid phase separation. These granules anchor to the outer mitochondrial membrane, thereby maintaining mitochondrial membrane integrity. Notably, the Sialin R494A mutation inhibited the Sialin-FKBP8 interaction and abolished the protection of nitrate. Collectively, this study is the first to elucidate the mechanism by which nitrate protects against mitochondrial membrane damage, providing a potential strategy for preventing IR-induced injury and other mitochondrial damage-related diseases.Keywords: Nitrate, Sialin, FKBP8, mitochondrial membrane injury, Liquid ‒ liquid phase separation

Introduction

Salivary glands play crucial roles in digestion, lubrication, maintaining oral microenvironment stability, and antibacterial activity and are essential for physical health [1]. However, irradiation (IR) therapy, a primary treatment for head and neck tumors, frequently induces irreversible salivary gland dysfunction, resulting in symptoms such as xerostomia (dry mouth), dental caries, mucosal inflammation, fungal infections, and taste alterations [2,3]. Current treatments alleviate symptoms transiently but fail to restore glandular structure or function. Consequently, developing strategies to prevent or reverse IR-induced salivary gland damage remains a critical urgently need.

Loss of acinar cells, mitochondrial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress in tissues are the key mechanisms of IR-induced salivary gland dysfunction [4,5]. Mitochondrial impairment represents a central pathogenic mechanism, as these organelles drive cellular energetics. Mitochondrial dysfunction includes dysregulated mitochondrial dynamics, compromised ATP synthesis, disrupted calcium homeostasis, and metabolic reprogramming [6]. Impaired mitochondrial function is a key mechanism in salivary gland dysfunction caused by IR damage as well as other diseases, including neurological disorders, cardiovascular disorders, and liver diseases [[7], [8], [9]]. Specifically, radiation disrupts mitochondrial membrane integrity, downregulating anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., BCL-2, BCL-XL), increasing membrane permeability, and triggering cytochrome c-mediated apoptosis [10]. Subsequent Bak/Bax oligomerization induces inner membrane herniation and cytoplasmic leakage [11]. Persistently elevated permeability promotes calcium influx and reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction [12], activating the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) [13,14]. This cascade culminates in osmotic imbalance, inner membrane fragmentation, and pyroptosis [14,15]. Damaged mitochondria undergo either localized repair (e.g., via membrane stabilization complexes) [16,17] or mitophagy [18]. Thus, preserving mitochondrial membrane integrity may offer a fundamental therapeutic strategy against radiation-induced glandular dysfunction.

Nitrate, which is an inorganic anion abundantly found in green leafy vegetables, exerts protective effects in stroke, radiation injury, and cardiovascular disease [[19], [20], [21]]. Our previous study revealed that nitrate mitigates radiation-induced salivary gland damage by restoring mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), suppressing ROS, activating PI3K-Akt signaling [21,22], and inhibiting the ROS/NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD pyroptosis axis [22]. While nitrate typically proceeds via the nitrate-nitrite-NO pathway, we observed no significant changes in key NO-synthesizing enzymes (iNOS, XOR) post-IR [22].Notably, dietary nitrate preserves mitochondrial function by enhancing ADP sensitivity, limiting ROS, and modulating acetylation via SIRT1 [23]. All these evidences suggest nitrate may confer direct mitochondrial protection through mechanisms independent of canonical NO signaling.

Sialin, a nitrate transport channel in mammalian membrane, which is crucial for the physiological function of nitrate in the body [24]. Clinical and experimental studies have consistently shown that elevated nitrate levels correlate with increased Sialin expression [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Our prior work further indicated that Sialin responds to nitrate stimulation, and the nitrate‒Sialin ring protects damaged mitochondria by reducing mitochondrial ROS in salivary gland and restoring the mitochondrial membrane potential [21,22,24,27]. Beyond nitrate transport, Sialin deficiency impairs epithelial barriers, stem cell function, and neurological integrity [29,30], underscoring its multifunctional role in cellular homeostasis. Given its high expression in salivary acinar cells and its implication in mitochondrial protection, Sialin emerges as a potential key mediator of nitrate's mitochondrial protective effects in IR-induced salivary gland damage.

Mitochondrial membrane repair is a dynamic interplay of mitochondrial membrane proteins. FK506-binding protein 8 (FKBP8) is a multifunctional protein localized to the outer mitochondrial membrane that plays a pivotal role in mitochondrial dynamics, autophagy, and metabolic regulation [31,32]. It recruits BCL-2/BCL-XL to inhibit apoptosis [33], and serves as a mitophagy receptor, can recruit LC3A to form autophagosomes and clear damaged mitochondria [18]. Recent evidence reveals its direct role in membrane repair: FKBP8 stabilizes membrane architecture through interactions with repair proteins like dysferlin in muscle systems [17], suggesting broader functions in membrane integrity maintenance.

In this study, the effects of nitrate on mitochondrial damage were assessed in IR-induced salivary gland damage in vivo and in the context of human salivary gland (HSG) cells injury in vitro. We further explored the underlying mechanism, focusing on whether Sialin interacts with FKBP8-via liquid-liquid phase separation to form granules that anchor to the outer mitochondrial membrane-thereby maintaining membrane integrity. Elucidating this pathway may not only clarify how nitrate protects mitochondrial membranes but also provide a novel therapeutic target for preventing IR-induced salivary gland damage and other mitochondrial membrane-related diseases.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 J mice (aged 6 weeks and weighing 18–20 g) were purchased from Beijing SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The animals were randomly assigned to three experimental groups, with 6 mice in each group: (1) control group, mice received no irradiation and were provided with standard drinking water without nitrate supplementation; (2) IR group, mice were subjected to irradiation alone and supplied with drinking water without nitrate; (3) IR + nitrate group, mice underwent irradiation and were given drinking water supplemented with 4 mM nitrate. Sodium nitrate (NaNO3; catalog number S8170; Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved with drinking water as previously reported [28,29]. Nitrate supplementation at a concentration of 4 mM was initiated one week prior to irradiation and maintained throughout the entire observation period. For the irradiation procedure, mice were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation. They were positioned in a supine posture on the experimental platform of a Radsource 2000 X-ray instrument, and the submandibular gland region was exposed to a single irradiation dose of 15 Gy (dose rate: 1 Gy per minute; total irradiation time: 15 min) [21,22].

Slc17a5-floxed allele (Slc17a5fl/fl) mice and K14-Cre-ERT mice were purchased from the Cyagen Bioscience Inc. The K14-Cre mice were crossed with the Slc17a5fl/fl mice to generate K14-Cre; Slc17a5fl/fl mice, and epithelial cell-specific knockout of K14-Cre; Slc17a5fl/fl was induced by intraperitoneal injection of tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich; T5648) with a single injection at 200 mg/kg body weight [29]. The K14-Cre; Slc17a5fl/+ C57BL/6 (6-week-old) mice were used as controls for K14-Cre; Slc17a5fl/fl. Mouse tails tips were obtained at 10 days after birth, and DNA were extracted for PCR genotype identification. Animals were maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions and had free access to water and food. Animals were assigned randomly to receive 15 Gy one-dose irradiation or non-irradiation. The experimental part was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Capital Medical University (Ethical code: No. AEEI-2020-197).

Cell culture and transfection

The human salivary gland cells line (HSGs) was cultured as described previously [24,27]. HSGs cell lines were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 humidified incubator, and routine detection of mycoplasma was performed regularly. Nitrate was dissolved in PBS and added to the cells after being filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. Nitrate (1 mM) was added in the medium after cell adhesion growth (4 h after cell seeded). Cells were irradiated with Radsource 2000 X-ray instrument at a dose of 5 Gy (dose rate was 1Gy/min and irradiation time was 5 min) [22,27]. CCCP (10 μM) was added before mitochondrial function was measured and the incubation time was 20 min.

The Sialin and FKBP8 overexpression and knockdown lentiviruses, as well as overexpression lentiviruses with HA-tag and Flag-tag, and FKBP8 plasmids with mcherry fluorescence were purchased from Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The wild type Sialin plasmids and Sialin R494A mutation plasmids with Flag-tag, as well as and FKBP8 plasmids with HA-tag were constructed from Mabnus Biotech Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The plasmids were transfected into HSGs cells with Lipo3000 transfection reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific; L3000075). The lentiviruses were transfected into HSGs cells, and the cells were selected using medium containing 1 μg/ml puromycin (Shanghai Genechem) for 7 days. Western blot detection was used to verify knockdown and overexpression efficiency.

Immunofluorescence staining

The submandibular glands were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, dehydrated with gradient ethanol and then embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated with a series of ethanol solutions in double distilled water. After that, sections were processed with antigen retrieval by boiling the slides in sodium citrate buffer (10 mmol/L, pH 6.0) for 20 min. Then, the sections were immersed in PBS with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.2 % Triton X-100 at room temperature for 1 h, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS for 10 min three times, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, the sections were sealed with DAPI containing tablets (Sigma-Aldrich; F6057). IF images were taken using a NIS confocal microscope.

HSG cells were fixed 4 % paraformaldehyde and incubated with PBS with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.2 % Triton X-100 at room temperature for 1 h. Then the antibodies incubation, DAPI staining and image acquisition were the same as for tissue sections.

The primary antibodies used were as follows: rabbit polyclonal anti-AQP5 (1:100; Abcam; ab315855), rabbit polyclonal anti-TOM20 (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific; PA5-110506), rabbit polyclonal anti-OPA1 (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific; PA5-79771), rabbit polyclonal anti-LC3A/B (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology; 12741), rabbit polyclonal anti-SLC17A5 (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific; PA5-30517), rabbit monoclonal anti-FKBP8 (1:200; Abcam; ab129113).

The secondary antibodies used were as follows: donkey polyclonal anti-mouse IgG (H + L) and Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific; A-21202), donkey polyclonal antirabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 594 (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific; A-21207).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The submandibular glands were cut into 1 mm3 pieces, and were fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich; G6257) at 4 °C for 3 h. The tissue was washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB) and the fixed in 1 % osmium tetroxide. The tissue was dehydrated in gradient acetone and then infiltrated, embedded, and polymerized. The processed specimens were cut into ultrathin sections with a thickness of ≈70 nm, double stained with uranium lead, and subsequently observed under a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7700 Exalens).

For cell electron microscopy sample preparation: first, cells were digested with 0.25 % trypsin-non EDTA and then centrifuged for cell precipitation. Next, the cells were immobilized in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 2 h, and the rest of the procedure was followed by the preparation of the tissue sample.

JC-1 and TMRE staining

Mitochondrial membrane potential in HSGs cells was measured using JC-1 fluorescence probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific; T3168) and TMRE assay kit (Beyotime; C2001S). Cells were seeded in 4-cell confocal dishes after digestion and resuspension. After being incubated with nitrate for 24 h, they were cultured with JC-1(10 μg/ml) and TMRE probe (10 μM) at 37 °C for 20 min, respectively. Then the cells were washed for two times with detection buffer, and then observed under confocal microscope.

Mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) assay

Cells mitochondrial membrane permeability was detected using an mPTP fluorescence assay kit (Beyotime; C2009S) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After two washes with PBS, the cells were treated with calcein AM and calcein AM + CoCl2, respectively. Then the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 45 min in the dark, and further replaced with prewarmed medium containing 10 % FBS and incubated for another 30 min in the dark. Finally, wash the cells with PBS and observed under confocal microscope.

Mitochondrial isolation

Mitochondria were isolated with the mitochondria isolation kit for cultured cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 89874). Briefly, cells were seeded in a 15 cm dish and cultured for 48 h and were lysed with lysis buffer A (including protease inhibitors) for 2 min on ice. Then reagent B was added to the lysate and incubated for 5 min, then reagent C (including protease inhibitors) was added and centrifuged 700×g for 10 min. The supernatant 12000 ⅹ g was centrifuged for 15 min at 4 °C. Then transfer the supernatant (cytosolic proteins) into a new tube. Add reagent C (including protease inhibitors) to the pellet and centrifuged it again (12000 ⅹ g for 5 min at 4 °C). The pellet was the isolated mitochondria. Subsequently, Western blot analysis was employed to assess the purity of mitochondria.

Western blot assay

Cell total proteins and mitochondrial proteins were collected by using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime; P0013B) with protease inhibitors cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 87786). The proteins were separated by 10 % SDS-PAGE (Bio-rad; 1610183), and then transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5 % milk with PBS, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies: rabbit monoclonal anti-SQSTM1 (1:1000; Abcam; ab109012), rabbit polyclonal anti-LC3A/B (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology; 12741), rabbit polyclonal anti-SLC17A5 (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific; PA5-30517), rabbit monoclonal anti-FKBP8 (1:1000; Abcam; ab129113), mouse monoclonal anti-BCL-2 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology; 15071), rabbit monoclonal anti-BCL-XL (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology; 2764), rabbit monoclonal anti- Cytochrome C (1:1000; Abcam; ab133504), mouse monoclonal anti-SDHA (1:1000; Abcam; ab14715), mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH (1:1000; Abcam; ab8245), rabbit monoclonal anti-Drp1 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology; 8570), rabbit monoclonal anti-DDDDK (1:1000; Abcam; ab205606), rabbit polyclonal anti-HA (1:1000; Abcam; ab9110) at 4 °C overnight. After incubation with secondary antibodies, goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:2000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology; SC-2004) at room temperature, membranes were used western ECL substrate (Beyotime; P0018S), followed by exposure of the membranes to film and digital imaging. Image J software was used for gray scale analysis, and each independent group was technically repeated 3 times to obtain the mean value as a sample.

Electrophysiological measurements

The standard whole-cell patch clamp method was used to assess changes in Sialin ion channel membrane currents [24]. First, we detect the current changes in the Sialin ion channels within the cell membrane by decreasing the concentration of NO3− (50/30/10/1 mM, pH = 4) in the extracellular solution. Subsequently, in an extracellular electrode solution containing 1 mM NO3− and with a pH of 4, the response changes of the Sialin ion channel to different nitrate concentrations were detected. The cells were randomly divided into 6 groups (control and different concentrations of nitrate: 10 μM, 100 μM, 1 mM, 4 mM, 8 mM). Single cells were attached to the coverslip and, after the cells were adhere, 1 mM nitrate was added to the culture medium (4 h after cell seeded). Furthermore, under the conditions that the extracellular electrode solution is 1 mM NO3− and the pH is 4, we divided the cells into the following six groups: (1) control group without nitrate; (2) 1 mM nitrate group; (3) Sialin overexpression group (OE Sialin) with 1 mM nitrate; (4) OE vector group with 1 mM nitrate; (5) Sialin knockdown group (sh Sialin) with 1 mM nitrate; (6) sh vector group with 1 mM nitrate. Digital data were analyzed using pClamp 10.6 and OriginPro 2024, and further statistical analysis was performed using Graphpad prism 9.0.

Total nitric oxide detection

We examined the intracellular concentration of total nitric oxide in the following six groups of cells (control\1 mM nitrate\OE Sialin+1 mM nitrate\OE vector+1 mM nitrate\sh Sialin+1 mM nitrate\sh vector+1 mM nitrate) respectively. Briefly, the level of NO in cell lysates was directly detected according to the Griess methods using Total Nitric Oxide Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0024). The optical density at 540 nm was recorded using a SpectraMax i3x and the concentration of NO was calculated from the standard curve. Six biological replicates for each group and three more technical replicates for each sample.

Intracellular NO release

The intracellular NO release was detected using a NO fluorescence probe (DAF-FM DA, S0019, Beyotime). Briefly, HSGs cells were inoculated into 4-cell confocal dishes after digestion and resuspension. After being incubated with nitrate for 24 h, they were incubated with DAF-FM DA probe (1:1000) at 37 °C for 20 min. Then the cells were washed for three times with detection buffer, and then observed under confocal microscope.

Saliva collection

Saliva and blood samples were collected as described previously [22,29]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized and injected intraperitoneally with pilocarpine (2 mg/kg), and placed in a prone position on a 30° inclined plate with the head tilted slightly downward. Ten minutes after the pilocarpine injection, one end of the capillary pipette was placed at the bottom of the mouth, and after the remaining saliva in the mouse's mouth flowed out, the other end was placed in a pre-weighed, ice-cold plastic tube. Saliva was collected for 30 min, and the whole saliva flow rate under pilocarpine stimulation was calculated.

Nitrate levels detection

The determination of nitrate concentrations in saliva and blood was carried out using the Total Nitric Oxide and Nitrate/Nitrite Parameter Assay Kit (KGE001, R&D systems). According to the manufacturer's protocol, saliva samples were filtered using ultrafiltration tubes, and serum was collected supernatant at 3000×g for 20 min at room temperature. The optical density data at 540 nm was recorded using a SpectraMax i3x and the concentration of nitrate was calculated from the standard curve.

Mitochondrial metabolite detection

The SMG tissue metabolites analysis was performed and analyzed by Novogene (Beijing). Briefly, an equal volume of supernatant (20 μl) from each treated sample was used as a QC sample, and 200 μl is used for detection. LC separation was performed on an UPLC Amide column using a Agilent UHPLC 1290 system (Agilent). The mobile phase was composed of 0.1 % formic acid and 10 mM ammonium acetate in (A) 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonia water and (B) 95 % acetonitrile. The gradient program was as follows: from 0 to 0.5 min, 5 % A + 95 % B; from 7 to 8 min, a linear gradient to 35 % A + 65 % B; from 8 to 9 min, 60 % A + 40 % B; from 9 to 12 min, 5 % A + 95 % B. The flow rate was 500 μl/min. The column oven temperature was maintained at 40 °C. The LC system was connected to a triple-quadruple mass spectrometer AB5600 Triple TOF(Agilent).

Liquid Chromatography–Tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS) analysis

The proteins were obtained from control, IR and IR + nitrate groups. Samples were prepared for LC-MS/MS analysis using EasyPep Mini MS Sampe Prep kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 40006). After sample processing, they are analyzed using a mass spectrometer (Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Agilent 1260 LC (Agilent). ProteinPilot software was used to analyze the raw data. A 1 % false discovery rate (FDR) threshold was applied to both peptide and protein levels in the mass spectrometry data. Proteins were annotated using the UniProt and MitoMiner 4.0 databases.

Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

Lentivirus with HA-tag overexpressing Sialin was transfected into cells and expanded. The HA binding protein complex was obtained using magnetic HA-tag IP/Co-IP kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 88838) and SDS-PAGE was performed. The gel was then digested by trypsin and detected by mass spectrometry (Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in series with an Agilent 1260 LC (Agilent). ProteinPilot software was used to analyze the raw data. A 1 % false discovery rate (FDR) threshold was applied to both peptide and protein levels in the mass spectrometry data. Proteins were annotated using the UniProt and MitoCarta 2.0 databases.

Immunoprecipitation and Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were lysed using IP lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 87787) with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 78440) and centrifuged at 13000 ⅹ g at 4 °C for 15 min to obtain total protein lysates. Then lysates were incubated 2 h at 4 °C with protein A/G agarose beads (Beyotime; P2055). Then, the corresponding primary antibody and IgG antibody (as control) were added to the lysate overnight at 4 °C, respectively. The samples were washed two times with PBS and boiled with 5 ⅹ loading buffer at 95 °C for 10 min. Finally, the proteins were detected by immunoblot assay.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assay

For FKBP8 droplets in vitro, the FRAP module of the NIS confocal microscopy was used to acquire FRAP data. Briefly, droplets with mCherry labeled proteins were bleached using the laser beam. After bleaching, images were captured continuously along time (1 image per 1.53s).

Purification of Sialin and FKBP8 proteins

Summarily, the overexpressing Sialin lentivirus containing HA-tag and the overexpressing FKBP8 lentivirus containing Flag-tag were transfected into HSG cells respectively. The transfected cells were screened with puromycin for 7 days and expansion. Sialin proteins were purified by using anti-HA magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 88836) and HA peptide (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 26184). FKBP8 proteins were purified by using anti-DYKDDDDK magnetic agarose (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 36797) and DYKDDDDK peptide (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 36805). According to the manufacturer's instructions, both HA-tagged and Flag-tagged proteins were used gentle elution methods. The concentration of purified protein was determined by BCA (Beyotime; P0010).

Measurement of intracellular pH

The 2′,7′-bis- (2- carboxyethyl)–5- (and- 6)- carboxyfluorescein acetoxymethyl ester (BCECF AM, Thermo Fisher Scientific; B1170) was employed to detect the intracellular pH value. After seeded a fixed number of cells in a clear-bottom black 96-well plate and incubated them overnight in a CO2 incubator to allow the cells to attach. On the following day, we incubated the cells with 1 μM of BCECF-AM for 60 min. Subsequently, the plate was washed with Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS). Then, the BCECF fluorescence was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader, and the pH was calculated. The fluorescence ratio was obtained using the SpectraMax i3X plate reader (excitation wavelengths: 490 and 440 nm; emission wavelength: 535 nm). The ratio of BCECF-AM fluorescence at 490/440 nm is a function of pH.

Atomic modeling analysis

The structures of SLC17A5 in complex with FKBP8 were predicted using the AlphaFold3 multimer programs, The conformation with the highest score was used for dynamic simulation or analysis [34]. GROMACS package (version 2023.03) was applied to run conventional MD simulations to investigate the changes in conformation of SLC17A5-FKBP8 complexes [35]. The force field amber14sb were employed to parameterize proteins, respectively [36]. The TIP3P was used for the waters. This SLC17A5-FKBP8 complexes was solvated in an octahedral water box, and then the charge of the system was neutralized by adding 0.150 M chloride and sodium ions. First, the steepest descent minimization method was used to minimize the energy of the system by 50,000 steps. In the next step, we restricted the position of heavy atoms to run both NVT equilibration and NPT equilibration by 50,000 steps. The system temperature was maintained at 300 K, and the system pressure was maintained at 1 bar. Upon completion of the two equilibration phases, the system was now well-equilibrated at the desired temperature and pressure. A 100-ns unrestrained simulation was carried out. Every 20 ps, the energy and coordinate system of the trajectory was saved. In the simulation trajectory, ChimeraX were used to map interaction patterns and animate kinetic trajectories. MM-GBSA method has been widely adopted in the estimation of binding free energy in drug research. In our work, the MM-GBSA calculation was performed using the gmx_MMPBSA.

Statistical analyses

All experiments were randomized into groups by block randomization. Data collection and analysis were performed blindly. No samples and animals were excluded from analysis. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t tests; one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons between more than two groups. Data were expressed as the means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses and graphical generation of data were done with GraphPad Prism 9.0. Differences were considered significant at the value of P < 0.05. In multiple group comparisons, the letter-based method was utilized to denote significant differences. Specifically, the presence of the same letter implies no significant difference between groups (P > 0.05), whereas different letters signify significant differences between groups (P < 0.05). We used Image-J software to analyze the sum Integral optical density (IOD) of TOM20, AQP5, LC3, OPA1, Sialin and FKBP8 protein. We did six biological replicates for each group and three more technical replicates for each sample.

Results

Nitrate alleviates IR-induced mitochondrial damage in a mouse model of IR-induced salivary gland injury

Studies have reported the preventive effect of inorganic nitrate on IR-induced salivary gland injury in mice [21,22]. First, we verified this effect of nitrate by using a mouse model of salivary gland irradiation injury (Fig. S1A). Consistent with previous findings, nitrate supplementation significantly increased salivary flow rate in irradiated mice (Fig. S1B). Irradiation (IR)-induced mitochondrial dysfunction is a critical mechanism underlying salivary gland epithelial cell death. To explore the role of nitrate in mitigating IR-induced mitochondrial damage, we initially examined the expression of TOM20 (a mitochondrial marker protein) and aquaporin 5 (AQP5, a salivary gland functional protein) in the IR-induced salivary gland injury model. Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis showed that protein levels of both TOM20 and AQP5 in salivary epithelial cells were significantly lower in the IR group compared with the non-irradiated control group, while were significantly higher in the IR + nitrate group than in the IR group (Fig. 1A–C).

Mitochondria serve as the primary energy hubs of cellular metabolism and play a central role in regulating metabolic networks. They can sense fluctuations in specific metabolites within their metabolic pathways, thereby modulating mitochondrial genome-dependent biosynthesis [37]. To determine whether nitrate ameliorates mitochondrial dysfunction by regulating tissue metabolism during salivary gland radiation damage repair, we performed untargeted gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) metabolomics analysis. The data revealed significant metabolite perturbations between the control and IR groups, as well as between the IR and IR + nitrate groups (Fig. S1C). Among these differentially expressed metabolites, N6-succinyl adenosine was found to be closely associated with mitochondrial nucleotide synthesis (Fig. S1D). We subsequently utilized transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to assess mitochondrial morphological changes. In the control group, salivary epithelial cells contained mitochondria with normal round or rod-shaped morphologies, displaying clearly visible cristae arranged perpendicular to the mitochondrial long axis. In contrast, the IR group exhibited disorganized salivary epithelial cell structures, accompanied by mitochondrial swelling, reduced cristae numbers, and indistinct cristae morphology. Additionally, disruption of the outer mitochondrial membrane was observed, with exposure of the inner membrane to the cytoplasm. Notably, mitochondrial morphology and integrity in the IR + nitrate group were largely restored to normal levels (Fig. 1D and E).

During apoptosis, increased mitochondrial membrane permeability is followed by mitochondrial network remodeling, which triggers mitophagy. We therefore examined the expression of LC3 (an autophagy marker protein) in salivary glands. IF results demonstrated that nitrate supplementation reversed the IR-induced upregulation of LC3 protein levels in salivary epithelial cells (Fig. 1F and G). Collectively, these findings indicate that nitrate effectively protects against IR-induced mitochondrial membrane damage.

Nitrate protects mitochondria from damage to HSGs caused by IR

To further verify whether nitrate in vitro attenuates the IR-induced cellular damage, we used the HSGs cellular radiation injury model. Mitochondrial membrane potential changes in IR-exposed HSG cells were evaluated using JC-1 and TMRE fluorescent probes [38]. JC-1, a widely accepted indicator of mitochondrial membrane potential, revealed that IR exposure led to a significant reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential relative to the control group, while nitrate supplementation markedly restored mitochondrial membrane potential in the IR + nitrate group compared with the IR group (Fig. 2A and B). Consistent with these findings, TMRE staining-which specifically accumulates in metabolically active mitochondria-showed that the number of active mitochondria was significantly higher in the IR + nitrate group than in the IR group (Fig. 2C and D). Subsequently, mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) probes were used to assess mitochondrial membrane permeability [39]. IR exposure resulted in a significant increase in mitochondrial membrane permeability compared with the control group, whereas this IR-induced permeability increase was notably attenuated in the IR + nitrate group (Fig. 2E and F). We next examined the expression levels of TOM20 and OPA1 (a mitochondrial inner membrane marker). IF analysis showed that IR exposure downregulated both TOM20 and OPA1 expression relative to the control group , while nitrate supplementation significantly reversed these reductions in the IR + nitrate group (Fig. 2G–J). TEM was performed to assess mitochondrial ultrastructural changes. IR-exposed cells exhibited characteristic mitochondrial damage, including swelling, membrane rupture, and cristae fragmentation. In contrast, both control cells and IR + nitrate-treated cells maintained normal mitochondrial morphology, with intact membranes, well-organized tubular structures, and distinct cristae (Fig. 2K and L). To further explore the mechanisms underlying nitrate-mediated mitochondrial protection, we examined mitochondrial damage-related proteins: IR exposure significantly decreased the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins BCL2 and BCL-XL compared with the non-irradiated control, while nitrate supplementation in the IR + nitrate group reversed this downregulation. Conversely, IR-induced cytochrome c release was significantly reduced in the IR + nitrate group relative to the IR group (Fig. 2M and N). Collectively, these results indicate that nitrate plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial structural integrity and functional homeostasis following radiation exposure.

Nitrate protects against IR-induced mitochondrial damage via Sialin

To further investigate the specific molecular mechanisms underlying nitrate-mediated protection of mitochondrial function, we performed proteomic analysis of whole-cell proteins from the control, IR, and IR + nitrate groups using mass spectrometry (MS) (Fig. S2A). Quantitative analysis and differential expression screening identified 244 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in the IR group compared with the control group (146 upregulated, 98 downregulated). Similarly, comparison of the IR + nitrate group versus the IR group revealed 278 DEPs (187 upregulated, 91 downregulated) (Fig. S2B). Among these, 39 proteins were common to both comparisons. Subsequent screening of these 39 shared DEPs by cellular localization identified 5 mitochondrial proteins and 5 membrane-localized proteins (Fig. S2C). Within this subset of 10 DEPs, the nitrate-related protein Sialin was found to be significantly downregulated in the IR group versus the control group, with its expression restored in the IR + nitrate group compared with the IR group (Fig. S2D). Sialin is a nitrate transporter that senses and transports nitrate into cells [24,27], and has been implicated as a key regulatory protein in nitrate-mediated repair of radiation-induced salivary gland damage. We first investigated whether varying concentrations of nitrate could regulate Sialin protein expression. HSG cells were pre-incubated with nitrate at concentrations of 10 μM, 100 μM, 1 mM, 4 mM, and 8 mM. Specifically, WB analysis revealed that nitrate at concentrations of 10 μM and 100 μM exerted no significant influence on Sialin expression. When the nitrate concentration exceeded 1 mM, it could promote the expression of Sialin protein, and this promoting effect reached a plateau at 4 mM (Fig. S3A and B). Given that Sialin is well-characterized as a nitrate transporter, we further sought to verify whether the observed changes in Sialin expression would correspond to alterations in nitrate transport activity. To this end, whole-cell patch-clamp electrophysiological recordings were employed to quantify the influx of nitrate. To more accurately simulate physiological conditions, we set the extracellular solution to a pH of 4 and set the concentrations of NO3− at 50 mM, 30 mM, 10 mM, and 1 mM, respectively. The results indicate that when the concentration of NO3− in the extracellular solution is 1 mM, the Sialin ion channel on the cell membrane can be activated (Fig. S3C and D). Based on this, we set the extracellular solution at a pH of 4 and a NO3− concentration of 1 mM. Subsequently, we pre-incubated cells with different concentrations of nitrate (10 μM, 100 μM, 1 mM, 4 mM, 8 mM) and measured the changes in the Sialin current across the cell membrane. The findings indicate that-the change in current exhibited a positive correlation with the expression level of Sialin (Fig. 3E and F).

In order to confirm that nitrate regulate mitochondrial function and maintain mitochondrial membrane stability through Sialin. We first assessed Sialin expression across groups in vitro. qPCR and WB analyses demonstrated that nitrate upregulated Sialin expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. S4A and B), consistent with previous reports [27,28]. Further, WB analysis was used to verify the purity of mitochondrial proteins and analyze the expression level of Sialin protein in the mitochondrial proteins isolated from the three groups (control, IR and IR + Nitrate). The data revealed an increase in Sialin expression in the IR + nitrate group compared with the IR group (Fig. S4C- E). IF staining showed Sialin in vivo expression was significantly reduced in the IR group relative to the control group, but markedly elevated in the IR + nitrate group compared with the IR group (Fig. 3A and B). Notably, immunofluorescence-based colocalization analysis showed that Sialin and TOM20 colocalized in normal acinar cells of the control group, with most Sialin localized to mitochondria. In the IR group, both Sialin and TOM20 expression were significantly decreased, accompanied by reduced colocalization. Conversely, colocalization of these two proteins was significantly enhanced in the IR + nitrate group compared with the IR group (Fig. 3C).

K14 is expressed in epithelial cells throughout the body, and K14-positive cells have been identified as stem/progenitor cells involved in salivary gland regeneration. To explore the role of Sialin in maintaining mitochondrial function, we generated a K14-specific Sialin knockout (CKO) mouse model utilizing the Cre/LoxP system (Fig. S4F and G). IF analysis further revealed that TOM20 expression was decreased in Sialin CKO mice, with reduced colocalization between TOM20 and Sialin (Fig. 3D-F). In addition, Sialin CKO downregulated AQP5 expression (Fig. 3G and H). TEM revealed morphological abnormalities in submandibular gland mitochondria of Sialin CKO mice, including swelling, rounding, membrane rupture, and cristae fragmentation (Fig. 3I), with a concurrent increase in damaged mitochondria (Fig. 3J). Using a submandibular gland IR injury model in Sialin CKO mice, IF analysis showed that IR significantly reduced AQP5 and TOM20 expression in Sialin CKO mice, and nitrate supplementation failed to alleviate salivary gland injury (Fig. S4H and I).

Sialin deficiency results in mitochondrial membrane damage in vitro

To further explore the important role of Sialin on the maintenance of mitochondrial function. We next used knockdown Sialin cells to detect its effect on mitochondrial function (Fig. S5A). JC-1 and TMRE staining results showed that knockdown of Sialin caused a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential under normal conditions (Fig. 4A and B). Moreover, following treatment with carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP)-an agent that induces mitochondrial membrane impairment-mitochondrial membrane potential was significantly lower in the Sialin-knockdown group compared with the vector control group (Fig. 4A and B). Subsequently, we evaluated mitochondrial membrane permeability across groups. mPTP assay results revealed that, under normal conditions, Sialin knockdown increased mitochondrial membrane permeability. After CCCP treatment, while mitochondrial membrane permeability was elevated in the vector group, it was significantly greater in the Sialin-knockdown group compared with the vector group (Fig. 4C). These results indicated that knockdown of Sialin aggravated CCCP-induced mitochondrial membrane damage.

TEM observations showed that, under normal conditions, mitochondria in the vector group exhibited a normal rod-shaped morphology with intact inner and outer membranes and distinct cristae. In contrast, mitochondria in the Sialin-knockdown group displayed swelling, rounding, disrupted inner and outer membranes, and fragmented or absent cristae. Following CCCP treatment, the vector group showed partial mitochondrial swelling, membrane rupture, cristae fragmentation or loss, and an increased number of damaged mitochondria. Notably, mitochondrial damage was more severe in the Sialin-knockdown group, characterized by a higher number of damaged mitochondria, increased membrane rupture, and extensive cristae fragmentation or disappearance (Fig. 4D).

As CCCP dissipates mitochondrial membrane potential and activates Drp1, it also acts as a fission inducer. To determine whether Sialin knockdown induces mitochondrial fission, we measured the expression of Drp1, a marker of mitochondrial fission. WB and IF analyses revealed no significant difference in Drp1 expression between the Sialin-knockdown and vector groups (Fig. S5A and B). Comparison of mitochondrial function between CCCP-treated and CCCP + nitrate-treated groups showed that nitrate retained its protective effect even after fission induction (Fig. S5C and D). We then applied Mdivi-1, a specific mitochondrial fission inhibitor, to Sialin-knockdown cells. Results demonstrated that inhibition of fission did not significantly alter mitochondrial function in the Sialin-knockdown group (Fig. S5E and F). Collectively, these results indicate that Sialin is a key protein in maintaining mitochondrial functional stability and exerts a direct protective role in preserving mitochondrial function.

Sialin overexpression decreases disruption of mitochondrial function in vitro

To investigate the role of Sialin in maintaining mitochondrial function, we established stable cell lines with Sialin overexpression. Under normal conditions, Sialin overexpression did not alter mitochondrial membrane potential. JC-1 staining results showed that following treatment with CCCP, mitochondrial membrane potential was reduced in the vector control group, whereas Sialin overexpression significantly alleviated this reduction (Fig. 5A). mPTP assay results revealed no significant difference in mitochondrial membrane permeability between the Sialin-overexpression and vector control groups under untreated conditions. After CCCP treatment, mitochondrial membrane permeability was increased in the vector control group but significantly decreased in the Sialin-overexpression group (Fig. 5B). Consistently, TMRE staining results showed the number of metabolically active mitochondria in the Sialin-overexpression group was significantly higher than that in the vector control group after CCCP exposure (Fig. 5C). We subsequently examined the protein expression of TOM20 and OPA1, and no difference in the expression levels of these two proteins was found between the Sialin-overexpression and vector groups under normal conditions. After CCCP treatment, the expression of OPA1 in the vector group decreased by 49.96 %, that of TOM20 decreased by 47.07 %, and the expression of both proteins was greater in the Sialin overexpression group than in the vector group (Fig. 5D and E). TEM observations showed that, under normal conditions, mitochondria in both the vector control and Sialin-overexpression groups exhibited intact membranes and well-defined cristae. After CCCP treatment, the vector control group displayed obvious mitochondrial swelling, cristae fragmentation or loss, and an increased number of damaged mitochondria. In contrast, the Sialin-overexpression group showed a reduced number of damaged mitochondria, with relatively normal cristae morphology (Fig. 5F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that nitrate-mediated protection of mitochondrial function is dependent on Sialin expression.

Sialin preserves the integrity of the mitochondrial membrane by directly interacting with FKBP8

Under basal conditions, Sialin mediates pH-driven nitrate transport. Notably, pre-incubation with nitrate for 24 h significantly increased Sialin ion channel currents compared with the control group (Fig. S6A and B). Previous studies have shown that Sialin transports nitrate into cells, where nitrate exerts protective effects via the nitrate-nitrite-nitric oxide (NO) pathway. We therefore measured intracellular total NO levels: under basal conditions, intracellular total NO content was approximately 0.65 μmol/L, whereas incubation with 1 mM nitrate increased this to approximately 14.12 μmol/L (Fig. S6C). To further confirm whether NO acts on mitochondria, we co-stained cells with DAF-FM DA (a NO fluorescent probe) and MitoTracker (a mitochondrial targeting dye). Results showed that nitrate supplementation increased mitochondrial NO content compared with the control group (Fig. S6D and E). However, no significant difference in intracellular cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) levels was detected (Fig. S6F). Subsequently, we evaluated these parameters in HSG cells with Sialin knockdown or overexpression. Sialin knockdown significantly reduced Sialin ion channel currents compared with the vector control group, even with nitrate supplementation (Fig. S7A and B). Meanwhile, intracellular total NO and mitochondrial NO contents were significantly lower in the Sialin-knockdown group than in the vector control group (Fig S7C- E). Conversely, Sialin overexpression significantly increased Sialin ion channel currents (Fig. S7F and G), along with elevated intracellular total NO and mitochondrial NO contents compared with the vector control group (Fig. S7H- J). Collectively, these findings indicate that Sialin-mediated nitrate transport is critical for nitrate's protective effect against mitochondrial injury. Furthermore, Sialin itself may exert additional protective effects on mitochondria beyond its role in nitrate transport.

Given that Sialin is a membrane protein, we hypothesized that it contributes to mitochondrial protection by interacting with other proteins. To test this, we generated HSG cells overexpression HA-tagged Sialin and used immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (IP-MS) to identify potential Sialin-interacting proteins (Fig. S8A). IP-MS analysis identified 409 and 260 potential interactors in the Sialin-overexpression (OE) and vector control groups, respectively, with 205 proteins showing significant differential expression in the OE group. Among these differentially expressed proteins, 35 were associated with mitochondria, including 10 mitochondrial membrane-related proteins: VDAC2, AGK, OXA1L, SFXN1, TIMM50, TIMM23, SLC25A18, TOMM70A, FKBP8, and COQ5 (Fig. 6A). Immunoprecipitation experiments confirmed that Sialin and FKBP8 co-precipitated (Fig. 6B). Using Sialin-overexpression cell lines, we then analyzed the subcellular localization of Sialin and FKBP8. Colocalization analysis revealed that Sialin overexpression promoted the formation of FKBP8 puncta and enhanced the colocalization of FKBP8 with Sialin (Fig. 6C). FKBP8 localizes to the outer mitochondrial membrane and is involved in mitophagy regulation: it functions as a mitophagy receptor that recruits LC3A to damaged mitochondria to initiate autophagosome formation. Additionally, FKBP8 is recognized as an anti-apoptotic protein that inhibits mitochondrial apoptotic pathways. To investigate the role of FKBP8 in maintaining mitochondrial function, we constructed FKBP8-overexpression cell lines and assessed mitochondrial function (Fig. S8B). JC-1 staining showed that under basal conditions, FKBP8 overexpression alone slightly reduced mitochondrial membrane potential; however, after CCCP treatment, mitochondrial membrane potential was significantly higher in the FKBP8-overexpression group than in the vector control group (Fig. 6D). The mPTP assay results revealed that under basal conditions, mitochondrial membrane permeability was higher in the FKBP8-overexpression group than in the vector control group, but after CCCP treatment, permeability was significantly lower in the FKBP8-overexpression group (Fig. 6E). The TMRE staining showed no significant difference in the number of active mitochondria between the FKBP8-overexpression and vector control groups under basal conditions; after CCCP treatment, the number of active mitochondria was reduced to 47.58 % of baseline in the vector control group but remained at 75.29 % in the FKBP8-overexpression group (Fig. 6F). Consistent with these findings, FKBP8 overexpression reduced the basal expression of OPA1 and TOM20, but after CCCP treatment, the expression of both proteins was significantly higher in the FKBP8-overexpression group than in the vector control group (Fig. 6G and H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Sialin binds to and promotes the aggregation of FKBP8, and FKBP8 overexpression mitigates CCCP-induced mitochondrial dysfunction.

Sialin binds to FKBP8 and forms granules through liquid‒liquid phase separation (LLPS)

To investigate whether nitrate maintains mitochondrial functional stability by promoting FKBP8 expression or Sialin-FKBP8 binding, we constructed FKBP8-knockdown HSG cells (Fig. S8B). JC-1 staining showed that mitochondrial membrane potential decreased in both the FKBP8-knockdown and vector control groups after CCCP exposure; notably, the membrane potential in the FKBP8-knockdown group was significantly lower than that in the vector control group following nitrate supplementation (Fig. 7A). The mPTP assay results revealed no significant difference in mitochondrial membrane permeability between the FKBP8-knockdown and vector control groups after CCCP treatment. However, nitrate supplementation reduced mitochondrial membrane permeability in the vector control group but failed to exert a significant effect in the FKBP8-knockdown group (Fig. 7B). The TMRE staining results showed a consistent trend: after CCCP treatment, the number of active mitochondria did not differ significantly between the FKBP8-knockdown and vector control groups, whereas nitrate supplementation increased active mitochondria in the vector control group but had no significant effect in the FKBP8-knockdown group (Fig. 7C). These findings indicate that nitrate-mediated protection against mitochondrial damage is dependent on FKBP8 expression.

To explore whether FKBP8 exerts its effects in nitrate-mediated mitigation of mitochondrial dysfunction through Sialin-FKBP8 interaction and direct targeting to mitochondria, we examined the mitochondrial localization of FKBP8 and TOM20 in Sialin-overexpression cells. IF analysis revealed distinct differences in FKBP8 localization between groups: in Sialin-overexpression cells, FKBP8 colocalized with TOM20, indicating enhanced targeting of FKBP8 to mitochondria (Fig. 7D and E). It has been reported that cells respond to injury stress by forming membraneless organelles via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), which facilitates repair of damaged lysosomal membranes. We therefore hypothesized that Sialin overexpression promotes FKBP8 aggregation on mitochondria and that Sialin-FKBP8 binding induces condensate formation to repair mitochondrial membrane damage.

To verify LLPS of FKBP8, we generated an mCherry-FKBP8 fusion protein (exhibiting red fluorescence), with the vector control expressing only mCherry (Fig. S8C). The IF results showed that FKBP8 formed red punctate red foci, which aggregated and fused into larger assemblies. We further tested this hypothesis using live-cell time-lapse imaging combined with fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP). mCherry-FKBP8-overexpression plasmids were transfected into Sialin-overexpression, Sialin-knockdown, and control cells. Notably, in Sialin-overexpression cells, mCherry-FKBP8 foci showed fluorescence recovery at the bleached site within ∼30 s, displaying characteristics of phase-separated structures (Fig. 7F). In contrast, no fluorescence recovery was observed in the other two groups. Additionally, increased Sialin expression and acidification (pH ∼6.5, induced by hydrochloric acid [HCl] addition) promoted the formation of FKBP8 phase-separated droplets (Fig. 7G). We then used immunofluorescence to examine Sialin-FKBP8 interaction during nitrate-mediated alleviation of salivary gland irradiation injury. IF results showed enhanced Sialin-FKBP8 binding in salivary glands of the IR + nitrate group compared with the IR group (Fig. 7H). Intracellular pH was measured using the BCECF-AM probe: under basal conditions, intracellular pH was 7.04; after nitrate supplementation, it decreased to 6.85; in the IR + nitrate group, the pH was ∼6.67 (Fig. 7I). What's more, we also observed that at 1 mM nitrate, there was an enhancement in the co-localization of Sialin and FKBP8 (Fig. 8D). Similar nitrate-induced enhancement of Sialin-FKBP8 binding was observed in mucosal healing (MH) and traumatic brain injury (TBI) models (Fig. S8E and F), suggesting that this protective mechanism is not restricted to radiation injury but may be conserved across other physiological and pathological contexts. Collectively, these data demonstrate that nitrate promotes Sialin-FKBP8 liquid-liquid phase separation on mitochondria, forming aggregated condensates that mitigate mitochondrial functional damage.

Sialin-FKBP8 interaction is critical for nitrate protection against mitochondrial damage

To determine whether the Sialin-FKBP8 interaction is essential for nitrate's protective effects, we performed atomic-level modeling to simulate their binding interface. Briefly, we utilized AlphaFold3 to predict the complex structure of SLC17A5 (encoding Sialin) and FKBP8 based on their amino acid sequences (Fig. 8A). The predicted complex structure was further refined, and stable trajectories from molecular dynamics simulations were extracted for energy and binding mode analyses. Potential Sialin-FKBP8 binding sites were identified based on the binding energy contributions of individual amino acids at the interaction interface (Fig. 8B). Among these, Sialin residues Thr360 and Arg494, and FKBP8 residue Arg225 exhibited the most significant binding energy contributions (Fig. 8C). Thus, we selected Arg494 of the Sialin protein, which had the greatest binding contribution, for site-directed mutagenesis. After constructing the Sialin R494A mutation (MUT) plasmid for cell transfection, we initially employed a co-immunoprecipitation assay to verify the alterations in the binding between Sialin and FKBP8. The results indicated that, in comparison with the wild type (WT), the binding between Sialin and FKBP8 was significantly diminished in the MUT (Fig. 8D). Nitrate supplementation significantly enhanced Sialin-FKBP8 interaction in the WT group; however, even with nitrate supplementation, this interaction failed to be enhanced in the MUT group (Fig. 8E). We further explored the role of Sialin-FKBP8 binding in radiation injury responses. Under normal conditions, compared with the WT group, the MUT group showed no significant changes in the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins BCL2 and BCL-XL, but exhibited increased cytochrome c levels (Fig. 8F). After IR exposure, the MUT group displayed significantly downregulated BCL2 and BCL-XL expression, and significantly upregulated cytochrome c expression compared with the WT group (Fig. 8G). Additionally, we transfected Sialin-knockdown cells with either WT or MUT Sialin plasmids and evaluated mitochondrial damage-related proteins under basal and IR conditions. Under non-IR conditions, no notable differences in these proteins were observed between the WT and MUT groups. However, in both IR and IR + nitrate groups, the MUT group showed significantly reduced BCL2 and BCL-XL expression, and significantly increased cytochrome c expression compared with the WT group (Fig. 8I). These results indicate that mutation of the Sialin-FKBP8 binding site significantly abolished nitrate's protective effect against radiation-induced mitochondrial damage. Thus, the Sialin-FKBP8 interaction represents a critical pathway through which nitrate alleviates mitochondrial radiation injury.

Discussion

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a key modulator of IR-induced salivary gland dysfunction as well as other diseases. In the present study, we identify a novel mechanism by which inorganic nitrate protects against IR-induced mitochondrial damage in salivary glands (SGs). Nitrate upregulates the nitrate transporter Sialin, which, via H+ cotransport, acidifies the cytoplasm and promotes Sialin's interaction with the outer mitochondrial membrane protein FKBP8. This interaction triggers liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) to form Sialin-FKBP8 condensates, which anchor to the outer mitochondrial membrane and stabilize its integrity. These findings not only clarify how nitrate mitigates IR-induced SG injury but also reveal a previously unrecognized role for Sialin-FKBP8-mediated LLPS in mitochondrial membrane protection.

Mitochondria, vital for cellular energy and apoptosis regulation, are highly susceptible to ionizing radiation (IR), leading to mtDNA damage, elevated mtROS, and increased membrane permeability [40,41]. Compromised mitochondrial membrane integrity triggers cytochrome C and Smac release, activating caspase-9-dependent apoptosis [42,43], and promotes mtDNA leakage, stimulating the cGAS-STING inflammatory pathway, exacerbated by Ca2+ influx and ROS surges [[12], [13], [14],44]. Therefore, preserving mitochondrial membrane integrity is paramount in countering IR-induced SGs dysfunction and related disorders. While our prior work established nitrate's protective role against IR-induced SGs injury and mitochondrial dysfunction. In our previous studies, we demonstrated that nitrate protects against radiation-induced salivary gland injury in miniature pigs, Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, and C57 mice. This protective effect was observed across a range of nitrate concentrations (0.25–5 mM) in both preventive and therapeutic settings, with efficacy generally increasing in a dose-dependent manner [21,22,27]. Our recent findings confirmed that a nitrate concentration of 4 mM elicits significant therapeutic effects in multi-disease models [[45], [46], [47]]. Besides, other research groups have reported that in C57 mice, 4 mM nitrate can maintain mitochondrial function by enhancing sensitivity to ADP and regulating acetylation via SIRT1 [23]. Therefore, 4 mM nitrate was selected as the drinking water concentration for the animal experiments in this study. Given that the in vivo nitrate concentration differs from that in drinking water, we first conducted an in vitro nitrate concentration screening using HSG cells to determine the appropriate concentration for cell experiments. The results showed that when the nitrate concentration exceeded 1 mM, it could promote the expression of Sialin protein, and this promoting effect reached a plateau at 4 mM. Therefore, we selected 4 mM nitrate in vivo and 1 mM in vitro here for subsequent experiments and extend these observations by showing that nitrate's protective effect is dependent on the direct preservation of mitochondrial membrane integrity.

Although exogenous nitrate is known to sustain NO levels via the nitrate-nitrite-NO pathway [48,49], and NO exhibits complex, often contradictory effects on mitochondria (modulating respiration, ROS/RNS production, and permeability transition [50,51]. However, although many studies have reported beneficial effects of NO on mitochondrial function, it has also been found that NO and its derivatives have direct effects on mitochondria associated cell death. In the present study, while nitrate increased intracellular and mitochondrial NO levels, consistent with some studies, we observed no concomitant change in cGMP, a key NO effector. This aligns with our previous study, no differential changes in iNOS or XOR expression in IR-induced submandibular glands [22], and the reports suggesting nitrate benefits (e.g., in skeletal muscle) may involve SIRT1 rather than canonical NO signaling [23]. Given the dual effects of NO on membrane permeability, the Sialin-FKBP8 granules dependent mitochondrial membrane-targeted pathway offering a more reliable protective mechanism of nitrate.

Sialin, a conserved nitrate transporter critical for mammalian nitrate homeostasis, forms a robust positive feedback loop with nitrate [[27], [28], [29]]. Through colocalization analysis, we subsequently revealed Sialin localizes to the outer mitochondrial membrane of acinar cells, with nitrate specifically enhancing its expression at this site. The Sialin CKO mice demonstrated that loss of Sialin reduced mitochondrial membrane-localized Sialin, decreased salivary functional marker AQP5, and exacerbated mitochondrial membrane damage. Sialin CKO also decreased nitrate levels in saliva and serum, and exogenous nitrate failed to rescue IR-induced salivary gland injury in these mice. In vitro, Sialin knockdown did not disrupt mitochondrial fission. Yet, Sialin overexpression mitigated mitochondrial membrane damage and dysfunction, confirming that Sialin is a direct and necessary mediator of nitrate's mitochondrial protection.

We next identified that Sialin interacts with outer mitochondrial membrane protein FKBP8. Functional assays revealed that FKBP8 overexpression reversed CCCP-induced reductions in mitochondrial membrane potential, attenuated the increased membrane permeability, and restored the inner/outer membrane protein levels. Conversely, FKBP8 knockdown exacerbated CCCP-induced damage, and nitrate failed to rescue mitochondrial injury in FKBP8-deficient cells, establishing FKBP8 as indispensable for nitrate's protection. Notably, Sialin overexpression promoted FKBP8 aggregation, which colocalized with the outer mitochondrial membrane marker TOM20, linking this interaction directly to mitochondrial membranes.

Given that the damage of organelle membranes triggers repair via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS)-derived membraneless granules [52,53], we explored whether Sialin-FKBP8 interactions involve LLPS. LLPS is a cellular phenomenon characterized by the formation of macromolecular condensates through dynamic, reversible molecular interactions between specific proteins and/or nucleic acid polymers [54]. The biomolecular condensates formed by LLPS have been widely shown to be directly involved in a variety of mitochondria-related processes and diseases, such as metabolism, aging, and cardiovascular disease [[55], [56], [57]]. Therefore, we used PSPHunter and iUPred to predict the phase separation capability of FKBP8 and found that there is an IDR region at the N-terminus of FKBP8, suggesting that FKBP8 has phase separation capability [58]. The FRAP experiments confirmed Sialin overexpression accelerates fluorescence recovery in FKBP8-mCherry condensates, validating dynamic phase separation. In addition, increasing Sialin levels or acidifying the conditions (pH ∼6.5 with HCl) promoted FKBP8 droplet formation. It was aligning with our BCECF-AM measurements: intracellular pH (global cytoplasmic/organellar) dropped from 7.04 (normal) to 6.85 (nitrate), and 6.67 (IR + nitrate), which was similar to the reported intracellular pH under acidosis or hypoxia [59,60].

As a pH-dependent H+/NO3− cotransporter, Sialin imports both ions, acidifying the cytoplasm and the mitochondrial matrix. IR-induced extracellular acidification, combined with extracellular nitrate, enhances Sialin opening, driving H+ accumulation that promotes Sialin-FKBP8 LLPS. Our data highlight Sialin-FKBP8 LLPS as the primary mechanism, suggesting that this protection extends beyond IR to any context with extracellular acidification. We propose a model: nitrate upregulates Sialin, boosting nitrate/H+ cotransport and cytoplasmic acidification; this triggers conformational changes in mitochondrial Sialin, driving LLPS with FKBP8 to form condensates that anchor to and stabilize the outer mitochondrial membrane.

The atomic modeling analysis and molecular dynamics trajectory calculations of SLC17A5 and FKBP8 supported the formation of a stable Sialin-FKBP8 complex. Targeting the predicted interface, we generated a Sialin R494A mutation plasmid. After cell transfection, co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that Sialin R494A mutation significantly impaired its binding to FKBP8, regardless of nitrate exposure. Furthermore, in vitro radiation injury models demonstrated that Sialin R494A mutation markedly abolished the protective effect of nitrate against radiation-induced damage. These findings identify Sialin-mediated FKBP8 LLPS as a key pathway for nitrate's mitochondrial membrane protection, advancing mechanistic understanding of nitrate's role in mitochondrial health. Targeting Sialin-FKBP8 interactions thus holds promise for treating mitochondrial membrane-related diseases.

In the present study, although we observed that nitrate induces the upregulation of Sialin protein expression-with electrophysiological results further confirming an increase in intracellular nitrate transport-there were no significant changes in intracellular NO or cGMP levels. These findings suggest that the protective effect of nitrate on mitochondrial function is independent of the nitrate-nitrite-NO pathway. Further investigations revealed that Sialin interacts with FKBP8 via its R494 residue, thereby maintaining mitochondrial membrane stability. Notably, previous studies have identified E171 and E175 as the key residues responsible for Sialin-mediated nitrate transport [61]. Collectively, our current results indicate that the protective effect of Sialin on mitochondria is decoupled from its nitrate transport function. A critical technical consideration is the subcellular localization of Sialin: it is distributed not only in the plasma membrane but also in mitochondria and lysosomes, with distinct functions depending on its organellar residence. For example, lysosomal Sialin primarily mediates sialic acid transport [62], whereas plasma membrane-localized Sialin is responsible for nitrate transport. Our experimental data demonstrated that the differential changes in Sialin predominantly occur in mitochondria. This is presumably one of the underlying mechanisms explaining the observed discrepancy between Sialin expression/localization and the alterations in ion current detected at the plasma membrane. However, the specific role of mitochondrial Sialin remains to be fully elucidated. Therefore, clarifying the multifaceted functions of Sialin-particularly its mitochondrial role-will be essential for delineating the contribution of its nitrate transport activity to disease protection.

Although our data strongly corroborate that Sialin-mediated FKBP8 liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) is a crucial pathway underlying nitrate's protection against mitochondrial membrane damage, several limitations need to be taken into consideration. First, studies have shown that the physiological nitrate concentration ranges from 0.05 to 1 mM, and can reach 1 mM under stress [63,64]. In addition, after beetroot juice consumption, the nitrate concentration in the blood can reach 0.3 mM, while that in saliva can reach as high as 15 mM [65,66]. It is therefore important to note that nitrate concentrations in saliva are substantially higher than those in the blood. This implies HSG cells are more likely to be exposed to a supraphysiological nitrate environment. In the future, further in-depth studies on nitrate metabolism, sensing, and intracellular response mechanisms are required to achieve the effect of preventing and treating diseases using nitrate at physiological concentrations. Second, as previously mentioned, a key consideration is that Sialin localizes to the cell membrane, mitochondria and lysosomes, shows distinct functions according to subcellular localization (lysosomal Sialin mediates sialic acid transport, cell membrane-localized Sialin is responsible for nitrate transport), and the specific role of mitochondrial Sialin remains to be fully clarified. It is necessary to further clarify the multifaceted functions of Sialin in the future, which is crucial for delineating the role of its nitrate transport activity in disease prevention. Third, there are significant interspecies differences in salivary gland anatomy, physiology, and nitrate handling among humans, miniature pigs, and rodent models, and the observed variations in nitrate sensitivity in these models further highlight the complexities of translating findings from animal and cell culture studies to human physiology and clinical applications. Fourth, while we have demonstrated Sialin-FKBP8 LLPS in both in vitro and in vivo models, the precise biophysical properties of these condensates, such as their viscosity, the stoichiometric ratio of Sialin to FKBP8, and their dynamic assembly and disassembly kinetics, still need to be comprehensively characterized. Fifth, the potential influence of Sialin-FKBP8 LLPS on other organelles involved in mitochondrial quality control, such as lysosomes (through mitophagy) or the endoplasmic reticulum (through mitochondrial-endoplasmic reticulum contact sites), remains unexamined and warrants systematic analysis. Sixth, validation in clinical settings, including primary human salivary gland cells or patient-derived models, will be critical for translating these preclinical findings into potential therapeutic strategies. Despite these knowledge gaps, our research identifies Sialin-FKBP8 as a pivotal mediator of nitrate's protective effects on mitochondrial membrane integrity, providing a foundation for future mechanistic and translational investigations.

In conclusion, our findings reveal that nitrate protects against IR-induced SGs damage by activating a Sialin-FKBP8 LLPS that preserves mitochondrial membrane integrity. This mechanism expands our understanding of nitrate's biological actions (beyond NO signaling) and identifies new roles for Sialin and FKBP8 in mitochondrial homeostasis. Targeting the Sialin-FKBP8 interaction could offer a novel strategy to prevent IR-induced SGs dysfunction and other mitochondrial membrane-related diseases.
