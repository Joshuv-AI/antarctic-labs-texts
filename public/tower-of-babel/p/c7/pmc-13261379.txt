Risk Factors for Pneumocystis Jirovecii Pneumonia Among Kidney Transplant Recipients in Southeast France: A 10 Years Case‐Control Study
Authors: Solenne Hulot, Raphaël Godefroy, Barbara Doudier, Léa Delorme, Sébastien Cortaredona, Catherine Sartor, Coralie L'Ollivier, Stéphane Burtey, Valérie Moal, Nadim Cassir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground Pneumocystis jirovecii is an opportunistic fungus responsible for Pneumocystis pneumonia (PJP) in hosts with impaired cell‐mediated immunity, particularly solid organ transplant recipients. The aim of our study was to identify risk factors associated with PJP in kidney transplant recipients (KTRs).MethodsWe conducted a single‐center, retrospective, case‐control study (1 case per 2 controls) of KTRs from May 2014 to May 2024. Cases were defined as KTRs with a confirmed diagnosis of PJP, while controls were matched on transplant and hospitalization dates (±3 months) and had not developed PJP during the follow‐up period. Statistical analysis was performed using conditional logistic regression models to examine potential risk factors for PJP, with a forward stepwise selection procedure to identify significant variables.ResultsA total of 37 KTRs with PJP and 74 matched controls were included. The median time from transplantation to diagnosis was 60 months. Two cases occurred before prophylaxis was discontinued, followed by 11 cases within the next six months. Baseline characteristics were similar between cases and controls. The number of day hospital or outpatient visits in the year prior to inclusion was significantly higher in cases than in controls (mean 11.11 vs. 8.89 visits, p = 0.04). Severe lymphopenia (<0.5 G/L) was the most significant risk factor for the development of PJP.ConclusionsThe most significant risk factor observed was severe lymphopenia. These findings underscore the importance of strengthening infection prevention measures, including strict adherence to standard precautions, appropriate isolation protocols, and targeted prophylaxis for high‐risk individuals during periods of increased transmission. Further studies are needed to optimize PJP prophylaxis for KTRs with severe lymphopenia.Keywords: epidemic, kidney transplant recipients, kidney transplantation, pneumocystis jirovecii

Introduction

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is an important cause of morbidity and mortality in hosts with compromised cell‐mediated immunity, particularly in solid organ transplant recipients [1]. In kidney transplant recipients (KTRs), risk factors for PJP include CMV infection [2], lymphopenia (<1,000/mm3), elevated creatinine levels, mTOR inhibitors [3], [4], ABO incompatible transplantation, and the use of belatacept [5].

Before the advent of PJP prophylaxis, approximately 5%–15% of patients receiving SOT developed PJP [6]. Now that PJP prophylaxis is widely available, the prevalence of this condition has decreased to 0.3%–1.7% [6]. International recommendations for prophylaxis in KTRs vary. While the European Renal Association recommends 4 months posttransplant [7], the American Society of Transplantation recommends anti‐Pneumocystis prophylaxis for all SOT recipients for at least 6–12 months posttransplant, lifelong prophylaxis may be indicated in patients with a history of previous PJP infection or chronic cytomegalovirus (CMV) disease [8]. Despite the widespread use of PJP prophylaxis, the infection still occurs, with reports of PJP emerging even after the prophylactic regimen is completed. There remain several gaps in our understanding, particularly regarding the impact of routine prophylaxis and its discontinuation [6]. It is therefore important to study the risk factors for the development of PJP in patients receiving routine prophylaxis and after discontinuation of prophylaxis, as the mortality associated with this infection remains as high as 18% in recent studies [2].

Nosocomial outbreaks of PJP in KTRs have been widely reported [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Epidemiological studies strongly suggest person‐to‐person airborne transmission of P. jirovecii. Molecular analyses have confirmed the clonal spread of strains within outbreak clusters, supporting this hypothesis [10]. However, the underlying host factors and healthcare setting conditions that may influence transmission in this population are not well defined [10]. With the aim of assessing the risk factors for PJP independent of potential outbreak periods, we performed a 10‐year retrospective case‐control study (1:2) in a single kidney transplant center. Matching criteria included time of transplantation and hospital stay.

Materials and Methods

We conducted a retrospective, single‐center, case‐control study to investigate the incidence rate, and risk factors associated with PJP in KTRs at the University Hospital, AP‐HM, Marseille, between May 2014 and May 2024.

All KTRs who developed PJP with a functional graft during the study period were included as cases. The diagnosis of PJP was confirmed by positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) on respiratory specimens (bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or induced sputum) together with compatible clinical (e.g., progressive dyspnea, nonproductive cough, fever, and hypoxemia) and radiological findings (bilateral ground‐glass opacities or diffuse interstitial infiltrates on chest CT). Serum β‐D‐glucan testing was not routinely performed at our center. Patients with positive PCR were excluded if an alternative diagnosis was determined by the attending physician or if they experienced clinical improvement without specific anti‐Pneumocystis therapy suggesting Pneumocystis jirovecii was more likely a colonizer than the primary pathogen. We did not systematically exclude patients with co‐detection of another pathogen.

Selection of matched controls: For each case, two control patients were matched on the basis of time of transplantation (within ±3 months) and were admitted to the kidney transplant unit within ±3 months of the index case. Age was not included as a matching criterion. The index date for cases was defined as the date when microbiological results confirming PJP became available. For control patients, the index date was assigned as the date closest to that of the corresponding case. Control patients did not develop PJP during the study period.

Data collection: Data were collected from medical records (paper and electronic), including demographics (patients’ ages were recorded at the time of transplantation and at the time of study inclusion), transplant characteristics (donor type, donor age, recipient age, primary kidney disease, and dialysis duration), immunosuppressive regimens (induction and maintenance), acute rejection episodes (identified and treated as such by the attending physician according to clinical and histopathological criteria; the interval between the transplant date and the rejection event was systematically recorded), prophylaxis against PJP, lymphocyte counts, number of ambulatory visits in the year prior to inclusion, specifically outpatient consultations and day hospital admissions, and opportunistic infections (cytomegalovirus and BK virus). Additional biological parameters collected at enrolment were lymphocyte count and MDRD‐estimated glomerular filtration rate.

Definition: CMV infection was defined as a positive blood PCR for CMV DNA with a threshold of ≥500 copies/mL. CMV disease was diagnosed when organ involvement was associated with CMV DNAemia [17]. Although prior studies have identified CMV infection within 3–6 months prior to PJP as a risk factor, this study did not specifically analyze the timing of CMV infection. Anti‐calcineurin drug levels were measured on the day of inclusion or the following day, as part of routine clinical care for hospitalized patients, and categorized as follows: for tacrolimus, high (>8 ng/mL), medium (6–8 ng/mL), and low (<6 ng/mL); and for cyclosporine, high (>160 ng/mL), medium (140–160 ng/mL), and low (<140 ng/mL). Ambulatory visits were defined as nephrology unit interactions in the year preceding inclusion, specifically categorized as either outpatient consultations or day hospital admissions.

Immunosuppressive regimen and prophylaxis: Induction therapy consisted of either anti‐thymocyte globulin (THYMOGLOBULIN) or anti‐IL2 receptor antibodies (SIMULECT) together with corticosteroids. The choice of induction agent reflected the standards of care in place at the time of transplantation. Maintenance immunosuppression consisted of an anti‐calcineurin agent (cyclosporine or tacrolimus), an antimetabolite (azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil) and corticosteroids. Dosing was individualized based on therapeutic drug monitoring and post‐transplant course and complications. A few patients were treated with mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors (mTORi). Belatacept was not used during the study period.

Our center used a universal prophylaxis strategy with trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole (TMP‐SMX). The prophylaxis regimen has evolved over time, since 2006 patients have received 400/80 mg/day of TMP‐SMX for 6 months after kidney transplant. As of July 2018, prophylaxis has been extended until two consecutive lymphocyte counts exceed 1 G/L. Patients intolerant to TMP‐SMX were offered alternative prophylaxis with atovaquone, or aerosolized pentamidine.

Ethics: This study was approved by the local ethics committee (Assistance Publique Hôpitaux de Marseille) and registered under number PADS24‐156_dgr. Written informed consent was not required according to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis: Given the matched design of this case‐control study, we used conditional logistic regression models to examine potential risk factors associated with post‐transplant Pneumocystis pneumonia (PJP). The exact method was chosen due to the small sample size and sparse data in some covariate categories [16]. Variables with a p‐value ≤ 0.20 in univariable analyses were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. A forward stepwise selection procedure was then applied, with an entry threshold of 10% (p < 0.10), to identify the most parsimonious set of variables independently associated with the outcome. Variables with a high proportion of missing data were excluded from multivariable analysis to avoid biased estimates and unstable models. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

The study included 111 patients, including 37 cases of post‐transplant pneumocystis between May 2014 and May 2024 at the university hospital of Marseille and 74 controls. The mean difference in hospitalization dates between cases and controls was two days. Cases and controls were comparable with respect to age, sex ratio, type, and rank of transplant, and initial kidney disease. The mean time to PJP presentation after transplantation was 60 months (5 years), with an interquartile range (IQR) of 10 to 85 months (Table 1). Most of patients (74.8 %) were on triple immunosuppressive therapy, 18.9 % on double therapy and 5.4 % on monotherapy. Among the 37 cases and 74 controls, 93.9% of cases, and 78.5% of controls received prophylaxis (p = 1.000). TMP‐SMX was the most commonly used regimen, accounting for 67.7% of cases and 75.8% of controls (p = 0.854). The completion rates of prophylaxis were similar between groups (93.6% in cases vs. 90.3% in controls, p = 0.444), and the median duration of prophylaxis was 6 months for both groups (p = 0.313).

In univariate analysis, a significant association was found between a low total lymphocyte count on the day of diagnosis and the development of PJP. Specifically, patients with PJP were more likely to have a lymphocyte count below 0.5 G/L (55.6% vs. 29.7% in controls, p = 0.014) (Table 1). Almost half (40.5 %) of cases and 25.7 % of controls developed CMV infection between transplantation and enrolment, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.143) between the two groups. The number of post‐transplant rejections was similar between the two groups (10.8%). Among these patients, 63.6% were treated with corticosteroid boluses (Table 1). Blood levels of calcineurin inhibitors were comparable between the two groups. Forty‐eight percent were classified as low (35.5% vs. 53.6%, p = 0.404), 49.0% as intermediate (61.3% vs. 43.5%, p = 0.386), and the remainder as high (3.2% vs. 2.9%, p = 1.000). 97.3% of patients received long‐term oral corticosteroids, with a dose of 5 mg/day in 56.4 % of cases. Two cases received mTOR inhibitors compared to none in the control group.

The lymphocyte count at discontinuation of PJP prophylaxis was <0.5 G/L in 48.2 % of cases and >1.5 G/L in 6.2 % of cases. Two cases of PJP occurred prior to discontinuation of prophylaxis. Eleven cases occurred within 6 months after discontinuation, followed by 4 cases in the second year and 20 cases from the third to the 33rd year (Figure 1).

We analyzed the number of outpatient visits for both cases and controls and found a higher number of visits among cases compared to controls (mean 11.1 vs. 8.9, p = 0.004).

In the multivariable exact conditional logistic regression analysis, the risk of PJP was significantly higher with an increasing number of consultations (OR = 1.23; p = 0.002) and significantly lower among patients with a lymphocyte count ≥ 0.5 at inclusion (OR = 0.32; p = 0.032). A negative trend was also observed with the duration of dialysis before transplantation, which was inversely associated with the risk of PJP (OR = 0.99; p = 0.073) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, conducted at a large French transplant center and involving 37 KTRs with PJP, we analyzed the risk factors associated with the development of PJP. We observed a peak incidence of PJP in 2018, with an attack rate of 1.08%. Over a 10‐year surveillance period, annual attack rates ranged from 0% to 1.08%, with a mean of 0.2%, which is lower than the incidence reported in most published studies (0.3%–2.6%) [17, 18, 19]. However, broader epidemiological data suggest that attack rates can reach 4% during outbreaks, highlighting the role of local epidemiological contexts and the potential for cluster transmission. A temporal peak in incidence has been identified between 6‐ and 12‐months post‐transplant, corresponding to the cessation of prophylaxis. Importantly, the risk of PJP extended well beyond this early post‐transplant period, with almost half of cases occurring more than three years after transplantation. There is growing evidence that prolonged exposure to alloantigens and long‐term immunosuppressive therapy can induce a state of functional T‐cell exhaustion. This state is characterized by increased expression of inhibitory receptors (e.g., PD‐1 and TIM‐3) and reduced effector function [20, 21, 22]. Although no study has demonstrated a direct link between T‐cell exhaustion and susceptibility to PJP in transplant recipients, the underlying immunological environment suggests that this link exists. Therefore, monitoring exhaustion markers in peripheral blood may be a promising strategy for identifying patients at higher risk for late‐onset infections. Due to its infrequent use among our patients, we could not evaluate prophylaxis with atovaquone. Furthermore, two cases of PJP occurred despite inhaled pentamidine prophylaxis, raising concerns about the efficacy of this regimen. By contrast, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no reported cases in the literature among patients receiving trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis. Although generally well tolerated [23, 24], pentamidine may be less effective [25]. These support the potential benefit of reintroducing TMP‐SMX prophylaxis, particularly if the initial discontinuation was due to mild and reversible adverse effects such as liver enzyme elevation or cytopenia, which are often multifactorial in the posttransplant setting.

Consistent with previous studies [3, 4, 6], we observed a higher prevalence of increased immunosuppression in patients who developed PJP. A lymphocyte counts below 500 cells/µL was the strongest predictor of an increased risk of PJP. Due to the retrospective nature of our study and the lack of systematic immunophenotyping, CD4+ lymphocyte counts could not be analyzed. Although several studies have investigated whether CD4+ lymphocyte counts could serve as a predictive marker for PJP development in non‐HIV individuals, as they do in HIV‐infected patients, none have validated this as a reliable predictor in this population [26, 27, 28]. In contrast to some studies [29, 30], we did not find a clear association between rejection episodes and PJP. However, the small number of recorded cases (n = 12) and the variability in their timing prevented a robust statistical analysis. Specifically, the sample size was insufficient to assess the 3–6 month pre‐index period identified in previous reports as high risk [31]. This discrepancy may be due to the routine administration of anti‐Pneumocystis prophylaxis following rejection episodes in our study, as well as our limited sample size. In addition, we did not observe a significant association between specific immunosuppressants, such as mTOR inhibitors, and PJP, nor did we find a strong association between CMV infection and PJP. Although prior studies suggest that CMV infection within three to six months before PJP may increase risk, we did not examine this timing aspect in our analysis. The present data are consistent with recent studies [32] suggesting that CMV infection may be a marker of increased immunosuppression rather than an independent risk factor for PJP.

In addition, our study raises the hypothesis of person‐to‐person transmission in PJP. Although historically considered a reactivation of latent infection, more recent evidence suggests that PJP may often result from de novo infection [33]. Pneumocystis jirovecii colonization, which can occur in any population [34], is an important aspect of its life cycle with important clinical implications. Furthermore, the organism has been detected in air samples collected in the environment of colonized patients [35, 36] and can be transmitted by air [36, 37, 38] with healthcare workers acting as asymptomatic carriers [39, 40]. Our finding of increased patient traffic within the nephrology outpatient department as a risk factor for PJP is consistent with previous observations [10, 41]. However, it is important to acknowledge that patients with more comorbidities, complications, or higher levels of immunosuppression are also more likely to be hospitalized or to have more frequent outpatient visits. This could confound the relationship between patient traffic and PJP risk. When interpreting our findings, this potential confounding factor should be considered, as it may influence the frequency of visits and the likelihood of exposure to Pneumocystis jirovecii.

As a retrospective, single‐center study, our results are subject to inherent bias and potential limitations in data completeness, which may affect the generalizability of our findings to other populations. The relatively small sample size may also limit the statistical power to detect significant associations, particularly for less common risk factors, and may not fully represent the broader population of KTRs. Additionally, the limited number of events and variables included in the multivariate analysis increases the risk of residual confounding, despite balancing through matching. Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. The matched case‐control design increases the robustness of our findings by minimizing potential confounding factors such as outbreak periods. Additionally, lymphocyte counts were only taken at diagnosis, which prevented an assessment of prior fluctuations or alternative causes of lymphopenia. Nevertheless, our matched case‐control design strengthens the analysis by reducing confounding factors and accounting for transient events related to hospitalization in both cases and controls. This approach also helps minimize biases associated with the protocol change implemented in 2018.

The long‐term use of TMP‐SMX prophylaxis in patients with lymphopenia remains debatable. Since PJP has been shown to occur during episodes of acute lymphopenia [17], future studies should focus on identifying the contributing factors of these episodes. These factors may include concurrent viral infections or overdosing on immunosuppressants (e.g., in the context of diarrhea). This could help inform more targeted and individualized prophylactic strategies.

Conclusions

This study highlights the vulnerability of KTRs with Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia due to both impaired immunity and potential exposure in healthcare settings. However, further prospective multicenter studies are needed to confirm this association. Although nosocomial transmission of PJP has been suggested in some settings, our findings raise the possibility of increased patient exposure within the healthcare environment, warranting further investigation. The most significant risk factor observed was severe lymphopenia. These findings underscore the importance of strengthening infection prevention measures, including strict adherence to standard precautions, appropriate isolation protocols, and targeted prophylaxis for high‐risk individuals during periods of increased transmission.
