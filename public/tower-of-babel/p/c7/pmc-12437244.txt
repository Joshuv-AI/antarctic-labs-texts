Analysis of Self-Sufficiency and Factors Affecting Migration Between Medical Service Regions in Korea Among CKD Patients
Authors: Eun Mi You, So Young Jang, Julee You, Eun-Jeong Kwon, Seokwoo Park, Ho Jun Chin, Ki Young Na, Kwang-Pil Ko, Hyejin Lee, Yu Jin Choe, Taehwan Noh, Sejoong Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a significant public health issue, characterized by high morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. The increasing global prevalence of CKD, attributed to aging populations and rising incidences of diabetes and hypertension, poses challenges affecting 8.2% of adults in Korea. Despite the presence of adequate local healthcare facilities, many patients seek medical care outside their region, leading to escalated costs and regional disparities. This study aims to understand why CKD patients, across various disease stages, opt to receive care outside their home regions and provides insights into improved healthcare resource management.MethodsThis study examined the inter-regional mobility patterns of 37,748 CKD patients in South Korea, utilizing National Health Insurance Service data from 2018 to 2022. Patients were categorized into three groups based on disease stage and treatment. The study assessed factors influencing healthcare service utilization, including individual factors, medical institution factors, and community factors. χ2 tests and t-tests were applied, and multilevel logistic regression was employed for further analysis.ResultsThe self-sufficiency rate, which is the percentage of cases where the treatment hospital is located in the patient’s residential region, was notably high in metropolitan areas such as Seoul, Gyeonggi, and Incheon. Patients undergoing kidney transplantation exhibited the lowest self-sufficiency rates. Older patients and those with lower income tended to use local medical services regardless of the stage of CKD. Patients who visited tertiary hospitals at least once presented lower self-sufficiency rates, while facilities with fewer than 500 beds and community factors such as a lower proportion of elderly individuals and higher population densities were associated with higher self-sufficiency rates.ConclusionThe movement of CKD patients across various healthcare regions is influenced by CKD characteristics, medical institution factors, and community factors. To enhance CKD patients' self-sufficiency, clear criteria for hospital selection, particularly for kidney transplants, are necessary, and healthcare policies should also take into account the diverse factors impacting self-sufficiency.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a significant public health concern defined as a progressive condition characterized by the gradual loss of kidney function, potentially leading to end stage kidney disease (ESKD).1 The global prevalence of CKD stages 1–5 among adults aged 20 years and older is 13.4%.2 The high global prevalence of CKD can be attributed to an aging population and the rising incidence of diabetes mellitus and hypertension.3 In Korea, the prevalence of CKD among adults aged 20 years and older is 8.2%.4 According to the 2023 Korean Renal Data System (KORDS) from KSN, the number of patients with ESKD undergoing renal replacement therapy in Korea reached 137,705.5

As CKD progresses, it is associated with numerous complications, including cardiovascular events, hypertension, anemia, mineral and bone disorders, fluid overload, imbalances in electrolytes and acid-base and kidney failure, often necessitating renal replacement therapy.67 These complications increase in prevalence and severity, leading to elevated morbidity, mortality, and reduced quality of life for patients.8 Progression of CKD to ESKD results in a significant social, economic, and medical crisis for affected individuals.9 Medical costs for dialysis patients exceed ten times those of individuals without kidney disease, and healthcare expenditures for ESKD patients represent 3.2–4.1% of total national healthcare spending, placing a substantial economic strain on the nation.1011

In light of these rising healthcare demands, despite having sufficient medical facilities locally, people often travel to other regions, particularly metropolitan areas, instead of utilizing hospitals in their own area. In rural areas with limited medical resources, patients frequently need to seek care outside their home region.12 However, they often bypass the nearest tertiary hospital in their region, opting instead for hospitals in other areas, particularly metropolitan regions. This phenomenon could be attributed to over-accessibility to healthcare, as modern transportation and infrastructure advancements facilitate easy travel to cities and preferred hospitals. Furthermore, the lack of mechanisms to encourage the use of local medical facilities contributes to this issue. When patients opt for treatment in large metropolitan hospitals rather than local options, it increases their burden, disrupts continuity of care through unnecessary inter-regional movement, and heightens social costs and national healthcare expenditures.13

Previous studies have examined inter-regional healthcare utilization by dialysis patients, yet research on healthcare utilization across regions by CKD stage is scant. This study aimed to analyze the patterns and reasons behind patients with various stages of CKD seeking medical care outside their local region. Additionally, the findings could provide a basis for developing effective policies that promote the use of regional hospitals and address the disparities in healthcare resource distribution, taking into account patient preferences.

METHODS

Research data and analysis subjects

This study analyzed the inter-regional mobility patterns of CKD patients and the factors influencing them. We utilized claims data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), which included diagnosis information based on International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) (Korean Standard Classification of Diseases) codes, eligibility data, and medical institution data. Additionally, we used regional statistical data from the National Statistics Portal operated by Statistics Korea, which included variables such as population density, the proportion of the elderly population, the number of businesses, the unemployment rate, and financial independence.14 By integrating the NHIS care unit data with the Statistics Korea data, we examined the mobility patterns and influencing factors of CKD patients across various regions.

The study subjects were inpatient cases, and the claim data from the NHIS spanning five years, from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022, were utilized. The total number of claims for all diseases was 38,289,478, with a sample of 5,867,016 stratified by region. Approximately 80,000 individuals were selected per metropolitan city or province, among which 37,748 were CKD patients (Fig. 1).

CKD patients were defined based on ICD codes identified during hospitalization. The analysis categorized patients into three groups. The CKD with non-dialysis group included CKD stage 1–4 patients, defined as GFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 with kidney damage such as hematuria, proteinuria or 15 ≤ GFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. The ESKD with conservative treatment and dialysis group included GFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 patients, regardless of dialysis status, as well as those receiving hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis. The ESKD with kidney transplant (KT) group consisted of KT recipients, including both deceased donor and living donor transplants (Supplementary Table 1).

Variable selection

Dependent variable

The dependent variable was defined as the alignment between a patient’s residential area and the medical institution used. Healthcare service area consistency was met when ≥ 50% of visits within a year occurred in the residential area, while inconsistency was defined as < 50%. Regional self-sufficiency rate represents the proportion of patients in a region meeting this criterion, indicating how well local healthcare services meet residents’ needs.

Independent variables

1) Individual factors

Individual factors included gender, age, insurance premium quintile, and comorbidity index (Charlson Comorbidity Index, CCI). Gender and age were determined based on the date of medical service initiation in each respective year, with age groups categorized as below 29, 30s, 40s, 50s, 60s, 70s, and 80 years or older. Income quintiles were divided into five groups: 1st (highest), 2nd, 3rd, 4th, and 5th (lowest). The CCI was calculated using diagnosis codes from the NHIS claims data, which includes comprehensive information on diagnoses, medical procedures, hospitalization records, and patient age. Based on the calculated CCI, patients were categorized into three groups: 0, 1–2, and 3 or more.1215

2) Medical institution factors

Medical institution factors included whether the patient visited a tertiary hospital and the number of hospital beds. Visits to tertiary hospitals were categorized into two groups: none or at least one, and refer only to inpatient visits. Furthermore, for tertiary hospital visits, the location of the hospital was either within or outside the patient’s residential area. The number of hospital beds was categorized into three groups: fewer than 500, 500 to 999, and 1,000 or more. Hospital beds encompassed general wards, psychiatric closed wards, intensive care units, isolation rooms, and sterile rooms.

3) Community factors

Community factors included population density, proportion of elderly population, number of businesses, unemployment rate, and financial independence. Population density was measured as the number of individuals per unit area. The proportion of the elderly population represented the percentage of residents aged 65 and older. The number of businesses was derived from the comprehensive business statistics, and given that data from Statistics Korea was available only until 2020, results from previous years were presumed constant for 2021 and 2022. The unemployment rate was calculated as the percentage of unemployed individuals among the economically active population (employed + unemployed). Financial independence was defined as the ratio of funds local governments could allocate freely, including their own revenues and local grants, within their total budget.

Statistical analysis

To investigate the general characteristics, self-sufficiency, and healthcare service area mobility of CKD patients by stage, χ2 tests and t-tests were conducted. Multilevel logistic regression analysis was employed to determine the factors influencing healthcare service area mobility for each patient.

The analysis model was structured in four stages. Model 1 addressed inter-regional factors exclusively. Model 2 added individual factors alongside regional factors to evaluate their influence on the dependent variable. Model 3 incorporated medical institution factors into Model 2 to further explore their impact on the dependent variable. Finally, Model 4 included community factors in Model 3 to identify all factors affecting the dependent variable.

Statistical analysis was conducted using Python (version 3.8.16).

Ethics statement

The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. X-2310-859-901). Informed consent was waived because the study used anonymized public data provided by the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS).

RESULTS

Alignment between residence and treatment locations by region

An analysis of regional self-sufficiency rates for all diseases from 2018 to 2022 revealed that the Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon area consistently maintained a high self-sufficiency rate of approximately 96% throughout the years. Meanwhile, regions closer to the capital, such as Gangwon, Daejeon/Sejong/Chungnam, and Chungbuk, reported lower self-sufficiency rates, with percentages around 76%, 76–78%, and 67–68% respectively. No significant variation was detected in self-sufficiency rates across regions during the comparison of years before and after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 (Fig. 2).

Regional self-sufficiency rates for all diseases (2018–2022). Self-sufficiency rates across regions from 2018 to 2022, with Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon maintaining high rates (~96%) and lower rates observed in Gangwon (~76%), Daejeon/Sejong/Chungnam (76–78%), and Chungbuk (67–68%). No significant changes were noted before and after the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic.

Self-sufficiency rate of CKD patients based on disease stage

The ESKD with KT group exhibited a lower self–sufficiency rate, ranging from 57.5–60.2%. In contrast, the other two groups demonstrated higher rates of 82.8–87.3%, without significant yearly fluctuations. Additionally, a comparison between KT group and the non-CKD group revealed a significantly lower self-sufficiency rate in the KT group. Over five years, the self-sufficiency rate of the KT group displayed a gradual upward trend (Table 1).

Self-sufficiency rate of CKD patients according to general characteristics

Further analysis of self-sufficiency rates based on general characteristics showed similar rates between genders in the CKD with non-dialysis group. Rates were higher among patients aged 80 and above and those in the lowest income bracket. Patients who had at least one visit to tertiary hospitals recorded lower self-sufficiency rates. This pattern persisted across the other two groups (Table 2).

In Model 1, focusing solely on regional factors, the CKD with non-dialysis group in the Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon region demonstrated higher local healthcare utilization compared to other areas. Conversely, Chungcheongbuk-do, Gangwon-do, and Jeollabuk-do experienced significantly lower utilization. Similar trends were observed in the ESKD with conservative treatment and dialysis groups, with the Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon area also showing high usage, while Chungcheongbuk-do, Gangwon-do, and Daejeon/Sejong/Chungnam had the lowest rates. The ESKD with KT group also reported higher utilization in the Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon area, whereas Chungcheongbuk-do, Jeju, and Gangwon exhibited the lowest.

Model 2, which incorporated both regional and individual factors, showed consistent regional influences as Model 1. Gender did not significantly impact any group. In the CKD with non-dialysis group, individuals aged 60 and above and those with the lowest income had higher local healthcare utilization; however, CCI displayed no significant effect on self-sufficiency. The trend was similar in the ESKD with conservative treatment and Dialysis groups, but age did not significantly increase self-sufficiency in individuals over 80 in the ESKD with KT group.

In Model 3, which considered regional, individual, and medical institution factors, the regional factor remained unchanged. There were no significant changes in individual factors within the CKD with non-dialysis group and the ESKD with conservative treatment and dialysis group. However, in the ESKD with KT group, age did not significantly impact self-sufficiency. A CCI score of 3 or higher was positively associated with self-sufficiency. Not visiting tertiary hospitals and having hospitals with fewer than 500 beds were associated with increased utilization of local healthcare.

In Model 4, which incorporated regional, individual, medical institution, and community factors, the influences of regional and individual factors were altered. In the CKD with non-dialysis group, no significant differences were observed in self-sufficiency rates across regions, genders, or age groups. Nonetheless, the lowest income bracket exhibited significantly higher self-sufficiency rates. Self-sufficiency rates were also markedly higher in visits to tertiary hospitals and in facilities with fewer than 500 beds. Regions characterized by a smaller elderly population and higher population density displayed elevated self-sufficiency rates (Fig. 3). In both the ESKD with conservative treatment and dialysis groups, the Gangwon region registered significantly lower self-sufficiency rates compared to the Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon region, with other areas showing no notable differences. Patients aged 60 and above demonstrated higher self-sufficiency than those below 29 years of age. Similarly, lower income levels were associated with increased self-sufficiency. The CCI revealed no significant effects. Not visiting tertiary hospitals and having fewer than 500 beds were associated with greater self-sufficiency, as were regions with lower elderly population ratios and higher population densities (Fig. 4). In the ESKT with KT group, self-sufficiency was lower in the Gangwon region compared to the Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon area. Age showed no significant impact, yet lower income levels were associated with elevated self-sufficiency rates. Visits to tertiary hospitals had no noticeable effect, but facilities housing fewer than 500 beds and areas with higher population densities correlated with increased self-sufficiency rates. Across all groups, variables such as the unemployment rate, number of enterprises, and fiscal independence did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

This study analyzed the alignment between residential areas and treatment locations for CKD patients, examining regional, individual, medical institution, and community factors. The Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon area had a high self-sufficiency rate of ~96%, whereas Gangwon, Daejeon/Sejong/Chungnam, and Chungbuk had lower rates. The KT group had a lower self-sufficiency rate than the other two groups. Older patients and those in the lowest income bracket showed higher self-sufficiency, while visits to tertiary hospitals correlated with lower rates. A bed capacity under 500 beds was linked to higher self-sufficiency. Regions with fewer elderly and higher population densities had high self-sufficiency.

Self-sufficiency refers to the capacity to evaluate the extent to which patients utilize medical institutions within their own residential area, as well as to assess the status of patients who seek medical services in other regions. A high self-sufficiency rate suggests that a significant portion of the region’s medical demands is met locally, thereby reducing the need for patients to travel to other areas.16 When analyzing self-sufficiency rates by region, the Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon area exhibited a high rate, which can likely be attributed to the high concentration of medical facilities in the capital region, thus enhancing healthcare accessibility. Previous research corroborates the observation that the presence of larger medical institutions within a region is associated with increased self-sufficiency rates. On the other hand, regions such as Gangwon, Daejeon/Sejong/Chungnam, and Chungbuk exhibited lower self-sufficiency rates. This is likely because these areas are geographically close to the capital region, and with advances in transportation, such as the KTX, patients are increasingly traveling to large medical institutions in metropolitan areas, reducing local treatment rates and increasing patient migration to the capital.17

The KT group exhibited particularly low self-sufficiency rates, likely due to kidney transplant patients typically undergoing surgery at tertiary hospitals and continuing follow-up care at the same facilities. According to the Ministry of Health and Welfare’s Korea Organ Donation Agency, kidney transplants, both deceased and living, were predominantly performed in the Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon region from 2018 to 2022, where tertiary hospitals are concentrated. The KT group exhibited the greatest regional mismatch compared to other groups, potentially stemming from the absence of clear criteria for selecting hospitals. Although HIRA provides evaluations for chronic diseases and cancers, no certification system exists for kidney transplants, leaving patients to rely on online reviews. A network-based accreditation system could address this by considering factors beyond transplant volume, such as post-transplant care, surgeon training, and regional collaboration. Implementing a regionalized organ procurement system, similar to UNOS, could balance transplant accessibility and reduce centralization in Seoul. Accreditation could also incentivize partnerships between high-volume centers and regional hospitals, fostering skill-sharing and improving nationwide transplant care. This approach would enhance transparency, quality, and equity in kidney transplant services.

The lowest income group, including medical aid recipients, displayed the highest self-sufficiency rate despite incurring lower out-of-pocket costs at higher-level institutions compared to those with general health insurance. This trend might be attributed to the economic burdens like transportation costs. Conversely, individuals with a history of tertiary hospital visits exhibited lower self-sufficiency rates, possibly due to their preference for continuing treatment at familiar hospitals where they receive ongoing care.

Fewer hospital beds were associated with higher self-sufficiency rates, consistent with prior studies that found regions with more beds had higher rates of out-of-region inpatient services.18 Community factors such as low unemployment, high fiscal independence, low elderly population, and high population density were associated with higher self-sufficiency rates, suggesting that economically stable regions with larger institutions promote local healthcare use. Self-sufficiency was highest in Seoul, Gyeonggi, and Incheon, followed by Busan, Ulsan, and Gyeongsangnam-do, then Gwangju and Jeollanam-do. From 2018 to 2022, population density was highest in Seoul, followed by Busan and Gwangju (Statistics Korea). Gyeonggi had the most general hospitals, followed by Seoul, Busan, Gyeongsangnam-do, and Gwangju. These findings suggest that higher population density correlates with greater self-sufficiency, likely due to the higher number of general hospitals and advanced medical institutions. Previous research also indicated that demographic structure, socioeconomic status, geographic characteristics, and regional distinctiveness directly influence healthcare utilization self-sufficiency rates, as confirmed through model verification using covariance structure analysis.19 Logistic regression demonstrated that regional factors influenced self-sufficiency across all models except for Model 4, where community factors mitigated the regional effects, underscoring the role of community in reducing regional disparities in self-sufficiency rates.

Our study has several limitations. First, CKD was defined using ICD-10 codes, which may not precisely capture early-stage CKD due to non-mandatory coding in South Korea. This limitation could lead to underdiagnosis, particularly with incomplete documentation. Second, this study focused solely on inpatients, excluding outpatient follow-up locations and medical quality as factors influencing self-sufficiency. Patients tend to trust institutions they visit frequently, increasing the likelihood of choosing the same hospital for admission. This may explain why those receiving outpatient care outside their region contribute less to local self-sufficiency. Despite sufficient hospital bed availability in some areas, low self-sufficiency suggests hospital selection is influenced by medical quality—an aspect not included in our analysis. Finally, while this study examined healthcare utilization patterns, future research should explore how regional self-sufficiency affects CKD progression, mortality, and major adverse cardiovascular events. Investigating whether inter-regional healthcare movement impacts patient outcomes could provide valuable insights for policy and management.

This study examined the self-sufficiency and factors influencing migration between medical service regions of CKD patients in South Korea. Results indicated a high self-sufficiency rate in the capital region, notably lower among kidney transplant patients compared to other groups. To enhance the self-sufficiency rate among CKD patients, especially kidney transplant recipients, establishing clear criteria for selecting hospitals for kidney transplants is crucial. Additionally, it is important to consider the impact of community factors on the self-sufficiency rate when developing healthcare policies.
