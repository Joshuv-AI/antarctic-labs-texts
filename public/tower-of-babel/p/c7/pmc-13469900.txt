Effectiveness of an Innovative Leg Support Ring for Lower Limb Muscle Rehabilitation Among Older Adults in Eastern Thailand: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Kaittisak Wataksorn, Phannathat Tanthanapanyakorn, Pitchapa Sankasem, Nattaya Rorakklang, Atitaya Samaikun, Chanthima Khumthong, Nonlapan Khantikulanon, Chaninan Praserttai, Sootthikarn Mungkhunthod
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThailand is rapidly transitioning to an aged society. Lower limb muscle weakness among older adults is associated with increased fall risk, loss of independence, and reduced quality of life (QoL). Although exercise can improve strength and mobility, many community-dwelling older adults face barriers to participation. We evaluated the effectiveness of an innovative leg support ring for lower limb muscle rehabilitation among older adults in Eastern Thailand.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial, repeated-measures study included 64 adults aged 60 years or older with lower limb muscle weakness and activities of daily living (ADL) scores of 9–11. The intervention group (n=32) used the leg support ring for 12 weeks (3–5 day/wk), while the control group (n=32) received standard care and continued usual physical exercise. Leg motor power, range of motion (ROM), ADL, and QoL were assessed at baseline, immediately post-intervention, and at the 3-month follow-up. Data were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance.ResultsCompared with control participants, the intervention group showed significantly greater improvements in motor power of the quadriceps, hamstrings, hip flexors, ankle dorsiflexors, and ankle plantar flexors (p<0.001) and ROM for hip flexion, hip extension, knee flexion, and ankle plantar flexion (p<0.05). ADL and QoL also demonstrated significantly greater improvements (p<0.001). These improvements were sustained or further increased at the 3-month follow-up.ConclusionsThe leg support ring program was associated with improvements in lower limb strength, mobility, functional independence, and QoL among older adults. Its simplicity and accessibility may support integration into community-based health promotion programs for active aging.Keywords: Aged, Muscle weakness, Resistance training, Lower extremity, Activities of daily living

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Thailand is rapidly becoming an aged society, a major demographic shift with substantial implications for sustainable national development [1]. According to the 2003 Elderly Act, people aged 60 years and older are classified as older adults [2]. The proportion of older adults increased from 19.93% in 2023 to 20.96% in 2024, exceeding 20% of the total population [3]. In response, the 13th National Economic and Social Development Plan (2023–2027) emphasizes active aging, innovation, and the strengthening of health systems to improve quality of life (QoL) among older adults [4].

Aging is accompanied by complex biological, psychological, and social changes that affect daily functioning. A key physiological concern is sarcopenia, the age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass and strength, which becomes more pronounced after age 60 years [5,6]. Lower limb weakness is particularly important because it affects mobility, balance, and the ability to perform activities of daily living (ADL), thereby increasing fall risk and reducing independence [7–9]. Regular physical activity and rehabilitation are essential for preserving muscle strength; however, many older adults become less active due to fear of falling, pain, or limited access to suitable exercise options [10,11].

Exercise and rehabilitation programs can improve muscle strength and delay physical decline in older adults, and resistance training and home-based rehabilitation have been shown to enhance muscle performance, balance, and mobility [12,13]. However, these interventions often require facilities, equipment, or professional supervision, which can limit accessibility in resource-limited rural settings [14]. Participation can be further hindered by fear of falling, exercise-related pain, and poor adherence [15]. Simple, low-cost assistive devices may therefore provide a practical alternative. The leg support ring facilitates lower limb movement, reduces muscle fatigue, and promotes safe, regular exercise [16], potentially improving adherence and functional outcomes through mechanical support and repetitive motion [17].

Despite evidence supporting exercise interventions for older adults, research on assistive devices for community-based lower limb muscle rehabilitation in Thailand remains limited. Existing studies have primarily focused on structured resistance or balance programs delivered in clinical or institutional settings, which may be impractical for community-dwelling older adults [18–20]. This gap underscores the need to evaluate simple, community-adapted rehabilitation innovations. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the effectiveness of an innovative leg support ring for improving lower limb muscle strength among older adults in Eastern Thailand. The findings may inform evidence-based strategies to promote physical activity, reduce fall risk, enhance functional independence, and support public health policies aligned with Thailand’s active aging goals.

METHODS

Study Design

This randomized controlled trial (RCT), repeated-measures study with 2 groups was reported according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) checklist [21]. The trial was registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20260417004). Mean scores for leg motor power, range of motion (ROM), ADL, and QoL were compared between the intervention and control groups. The intervention group participated in a 12-week leg support ring program (3–5 day/wk), whereas the control group received standard care and continued usual exercise. We hypothesized that, compared with the control group, the intervention group would demonstrate greater improvements in leg motor power, ROM, ADL, and QoL immediately after the intervention and at the 3-month follow-up.

Sample Size

The sample size was calculated for an F-test using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for repeated measures with within–between interaction in G*Power (University of Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). The calculation assumed a 95% confidence level, a type I error rate of 0.05, 95% power, an allowable error of 5% (0.05), and an effect size of 0.186 [22]. The required total sample size was 64 participants, with 32 participants in the intervention group and 32 in the control group.

Participants

The study population comprised 496 community-dwelling older adults aged ≥60 years who resided in the catchment area of Yanri Subdistrict Health Promoting Hospital. Eligible participants had physician-diagnosed lower limb muscle weakness and moderate functional dependence, defined as ADL scores of 9–11. Inclusion criteria were the ability to read and write Thai, the absence of hearing or communication impairments, and no previous use of the innovative leg support ring or similar devices. Older adults with severe comorbidities, cognitive impairment, or conditions that limited safe participation were excluded, as were individuals who were unable to complete the study period, declined participation, or lacked caregiver consent.

Sampling

A multistage sampling procedure was used. In the first stage, a sampling frame of 496 community-dwelling older adults in the catchment area of Yanri Subdistrict Health Promoting Hospital was obtained from local health records. Using simple random sampling, 200 individuals were initially selected. In the second stage, eligibility screening was conducted according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, yielding 147 eligible participants; another 83 individuals were excluded because they did not meet the eligibility criteria, declined participation, were unable to commit to the study, or lacked caregiver consent. In the third stage, simple random allocation was performed at the individual level using a lottery method to assign participants to either the intervention group (n=32) or the control group (n=32). Individual-level randomization avoided allocation by village and minimized potential clustering related to community characteristics. This approach improved comparability between groups and allowed outcomes to be analyzed at the participant level using repeated-measures ANOVA. Because participants were allocated to study groups after eligibility screening, the unit of allocation and analysis was the individual participant.

Intervention Program

The intervention was developed based on Pender’s Health Promotion Model, which emphasizes regular exercise to prevent complications and enhance functional ability among older adults [23].

The innovative leg support ring was designed to assist individuals with lower limb muscle weakness or mobility limitations in performing safe lower limb exercises. The device consists of a supportive ring attached to a stable frame with elastic ankle straps. Participants performed seated leg lifts against controlled elastic resistance, allowing safe, low-impact strength training intended to reduce injury risk while promoting progressive lower limb muscle strengthening.

The intervention was implemented over 12 weeks. Participants performed the exercises 3–5 day/wk, with 2–3 session/day, and each session lasted approximately 20 minutes. The program comprised 5 structured exercises using the innovative leg support ring and was designed to strengthen lower limb muscles, improve flexibility, and promote safe, repetitive movement. Participants completed 10–15 repetitions per exercise per round, with guidance on proper posture and breathing. Exercise intensity was maintained at a moderate level appropriate for older adults with lower limb muscle weakness, with an emphasis on controlled movement against the elastic resistance provided by the device. As participants became more familiar with the exercises, progression was encouraged by gradually increasing repetitions within the prescribed range and improving movement control and endurance. Guidance on proper posture, breathing, and safe movement patterns was provided to ensure correct exercise performance (Figure 1).

Participant positioning using the innovative leg support ring. Written informed consent for publication of this photograph was obtained from the participant.

The program comprised 5 key exercises using the leg support ring: (1) alternate leg lifting to strengthen the quadriceps and hip flexors and improve coordination; (2) leg abduction–adduction to enhance thigh strength and hip stability; (3) side tilting at 45° to promote lateral stability, balance, and hip mobility; (4) cycling-like movements to engage multiple lower limb muscles and improve joint mobility; and (5) simultaneous leg extension and flexion holds to improve flexibility, endurance, and controlled muscle activation. Initial sessions were supervised by trained health volunteers to ensure correct technique and appropriate exercise intensity; thereafter, participants continued the exercises independently at home with periodic monitoring (Table 1).

The control group received standard care and continued their usual physical activities during the same period without using the innovative leg support ring. These activities primarily consisted of light daily movements and routine home-based exercises commonly practiced by older adults in the community, such as walking and simple limb movements, performed at each participant’s own pace without structured resistance training. In contrast, the intervention group performed a structured exercise program using the innovative leg support ring for 12 weeks, with exercises conducted 3–5 day/wk, 2–3 session/day, and approximately 20 minutes per session. During the 3-month follow-up period, the intervention group discontinued use of the leg support ring, and the control group continued their usual daily activities without further structured monitoring or support from the research team. Village health volunteers conducted regular home visits and telephone follow-ups during the 12-week intervention period to monitor adherence, encourage continued participation, reinforce safe exercise practices, and provide motivation for both groups. Outcomes were assessed at baseline, immediately after the 12-week intervention, and at the 3-month follow-up using standardized instruments.

Measurement Tools

The research team developed or adapted the study instruments using standard tools. The instruments were designed and modified based on a review of the relevant literature [23–25].

(1) Part 1: Personal information questionnaire. This instrument comprised 9 items: sex, age, body mass index, education level, occupation, income, marital status, chronic illnesses, and presence of a primary caregiver. (2) Part 2: Motor power assessment. Lower limb muscle strength was assessed using a standard motor power grading scale ranging from 0 to 5 [26]. A score of 0 indicated no muscle contraction, whereas a score of 5 indicated normal strength, with full ROM against gravity and full resistance. Scores of 1–4 represented progressively greater ability to generate movement against gravity and resistance. (3) Part 3: ROM assessment. Joint mobility was assessed using a goniometer to measure the angles and directions of leg movement [27]. ROM reflected the maximal voluntary movement achievable without obstruction, providing an objective measure of functional capacity. (4) Part 4: ADL. These activities were assessed using the Barthel Index, which evaluates an individual’s ability to perform 10 basic self-care and mobility activities essential for independent living [28]. The total score ranges from 0 points to 20 points, and results are interpreted as follows: 0–4 points, complete dependence; 5–8 points, severe dependence; 9–11 points, moderate dependence; and 12–20 points, mild or no dependence. (5) Part 5: Thai version of the World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQoL-BREF). QoL was assessed using the Thai version of the WHOQoL-BREF, which was developed and validated by the World Health Organization [29]. The instrument assesses 4 domains: physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environment. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating better QoL. Scores are interpreted as follows: 26–60, poor QoL; 61–95, moderate QoL; and 96–130, good QoL.

The validity of the tools was reviewed by 3 experts: a medical specialist, a public health professional, and a rehabilitation expert. The index of item-objective congruence yielded values between 0.93 and 1.00. Face validity was also confirmed to ensure that the items were clear and relevant. Feedback from experts and members of the target population was used to revise the items, improve clarity, and align the tools with the study objectives. Reliability was examined in a pilot test with 30 older adults from the same region who did not participate in the main study. The pilot test showed Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.92 for ADL and 0.95 for QoL.

Bias

Selection bias was minimized through individual-level randomization using a lottery method. Although performance bias could not be completely avoided because participants and providers were not blinded to group allocation, efforts were made to reduce this bias by providing comparable attention and standard care to both the intervention and control groups. Detection bias was minimized by using outcome assessors who were blinded to group allocation; assessors were not involved in delivering the intervention and were instructed not to ask participants about group assignment during outcome measurements, including motor power, ROM, ADL, and QoL. Attrition bias was minimized through regular follow-up and ongoing participant support during the 12-week intervention period.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29.0.1 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with the significance level set at 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. The primary outcome was leg motor power, and secondary outcomes included ROM, ADL, and QoL. Intervention effects on these outcomes were examined using repeated-measures ANOVA, with the group×time interaction considered the main effect of interest. Assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variance, and sphericity were assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, Levene test, and Mauchly test, respectively. When the sphericity assumption was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were performed to control for type I error due to multiple pairwise comparisons.

Ethics Statement

This study was approved by the Valaya Alongkorn Rajabhat University Ethics Committee (REC No. 0001/2025; COA No. 0042/2025; June 6, 2025) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participation was voluntary, and participants had the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Written informed consent for publication of the identifying photograph in Figure 1 was obtained from the participant. Caregiver consent was additionally obtained for safety, given the home-based program and participants’ functional dependence.

RESULTS

Participant Flow

The principal investigator screened 147 community-dwelling older adults (aged ≥60 years) with ADL scores of 9–11, indicating moderate dependence. Based on predefined criteria, 83 individuals were excluded: 45 did not meet additional inclusion criteria, 23 declined participation, and 15 were unable to commit to the study or lacked caregiver consent. The remaining 64 participants were enrolled and allocated to the intervention group, which used the innovative leg support ring (n=32), or to the comparison group, which received standard care (n=32). All participants completed assessments at baseline, immediately after the intervention, and at the 3-month follow-up, with no loss to follow-up (Figure 2).

Flow of participants through the study according to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Baseline Data

At baseline, most participants were women (70.3%) and were aged 60–69 years (68.8%). The largest proportions had completed secondary education (43.8%), were married (78.1%), earned ≤5000 Thai baht/mo (76.6%), and were unemployed (62.5%). Most participants reported at least 1 chronic disease (71.9%), and body mass index classifications included normal weight (35.9%), overweight (20.3%), and obesity (43.8%). All participants had a caregiver, typically a child or spouse. No significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups in demographic or health-related variables (p>0.05), indicating baseline comparability. Baseline ADL and QoL were also comparable between groups. All participants had moderate ADL dependence, with mean ADL scores of 10.66±0.54 in the intervention group and 10.53±0.50 in the control group. Participants also had moderate QoL, with mean scores of 88.72±3.69 and 88.59±2.96, respectively. These between-group differences were not significant (all p>0.05), confirming baseline equivalence (Supplemental Material 1).

Effects of the Leg Support Ring Program on Leg Motor Power

Repeated-measures ANOVA was used to examine changes in outcomes over time between groups, with the group×time interaction serving as the primary indicator of intervention effectiveness. Significant interaction effects indicated differential changes between groups across time points. As shown in Table 2, the 12-week leg support ring program significantly improved lower limb motor power. Significant between-group effects were observed for the quadriceps (F=30.01, p<0.001, ηp2=0.259), hamstrings (F=17.46, p<0.001, ηp2=0.101), hip flexors (F=54.44, p<0.001, ηp2=0.181), ankle dorsiflexors (F=29.51, p<0.001, ηp2=0.171), and ankle plantar flexors (F=20.43, p<0.001, ηp2=0.058). The Mauchly’s t-test did not indicate a violation of the sphericity assumption (p>0.05), and within-subject analyses showed significant improvements over time (p<0.05), suggesting a strong group×time interaction. Large effect sizes were particularly evident for particularly evident for the quadriceps and hip flexors. Post hoc analyses confirmed baseline comparability (p>0.05; Table 3). Motor power increased significantly in the intervention group immediately after the intervention (quadriceps, p=0.001; hamstrings, p=0.023; all other muscle groups, p<0.001), and these gains were sustained or further increased at the 3-month follow-up (all p<0.001), indicating greater improvements than standard care.

Effects of the Leg Support Ring Program on Range of Motion

As shown in Table 2, the 12-week leg support ring program significantly improved lower limb ROM. Repeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated significant between-group effects for hip flexion (F=12.34, p<0.001, ηp2=0.175), hip extension (F=8.11, p=0.006, ηp2=0.057), knee flexion (F=6.00, p=0.017, ηp2=0.080), and ankle plantar flexion (F=12.11, p<0.001, ηp2=0.146). The Mauchly test did not indicate a violation of the sphericity assumption (p>0.05), and within-subject analyses showed significant improvements over time (p<0.05), suggesting a strong group×time interaction. Post hoc comparisons confirmed baseline equivalence (p>0.05; Table 3). The intervention group showed greater ROM gains immediately after the intervention than the control group for hip flexion (p=0.004), hip extension (p=0.005), knee flexion (p=0.011), and ankle plantar flexion (p=0.002). These improvements were sustained or further increased at the 3-month follow-up (all p<0.05), demonstrating superior mobility outcomes compared with standard care.

Effects of the Leg Support Ring Program on Activities of Daily Living and Quality of Life

As shown in Table 2, the 12-week leg support ring program significantly improved ADL and QoL. Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant between-group effects for ADL (F=15.25, p<0.001, ηp2=0.146) and QoL (F=13.18, p<0.001, ηp2=0.105). The Mauchly test did not indicate a violation of the sphericity assumption (p>0.05), and within-subject analyses showed significant improvements over time (p<0.05), suggesting a strong group×time interaction. Post hoc comparisons confirmed no baseline differences for ADL (p=0.346) or QoL (p=0.882; Table 3). Immediately after the intervention, the intervention group demonstrated greater gains in ADL and QoL than the control group (both p<0.001), and these improvements were sustained at the 3-month follow-up (ADL, p<0.001; QoL, p=0.007). These significant group×time interactions suggest that the leg support ring intervention was associated with improvements in functional independence and QoL beyond time-related changes alone.

Exercise Adherence and Safety Outcomes

Adherence to the exercise program was high among participants in the intervention group. Based on monitoring records from village health volunteers and telephone follow-up calls during the 12-week intervention period, all participants completed the prescribed exercise program throughout the intervention period. No participants withdrew from the study, resulting in a completion rate of 100%. During the study period, no adverse events, injuries, or device-related complications were reported. Participants were able to perform the exercises safely during initial supervision by trained health volunteers and subsequently at home. Regular monitoring through home visits and telephone follow-up during the 12-week intervention period helped ensure correct device use and reinforced safe exercise practices. These findings suggest that the innovative leg support ring appeared to be a safe and feasible rehabilitation tool for community-dwelling older adults.

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated that a 12-week innovative leg support ring program significantly improved motor power across major lower limb muscle groups. These effects may have resulted from controlled resistance that promoted neuromuscular adaptation, motor unit recruitment, and muscle hypertrophy through repeated submaximal contractions [22,24,30]. Beyond statistical significance, the magnitude of the observed effects was substantial, with large effect sizes (ηp2=0.058–0.259) for several muscle groups, particularly the quadriceps and hip flexors. These muscles play critical roles in standing, walking, and transfer movements; therefore, improvements of this magnitude may indicate clinically meaningful gains in functional mobility and stability among older adults. The supportive design of the leg support ring facilitated safe, repetitive movement and may have enhanced proprioception, coordination, and exercise adherence while reducing fear of falling [31]. Targeting multiple muscle groups simultaneously produced functional strength gains, consistent with previous evidence on community-based resistance training in older adults [19,22,32].

The intervention was also associated with significant improvements in hip, knee, and ankle ROM. Although the statistical effects ranged from small to large (ηp2=0.057–0.175), these changes remain clinically relevant because even modest improvements in joint mobility may enhance gait mechanics, facilitate daily movements such as sitting and standing, and reduce compensatory movement patterns in older adults. These gains may be partly explained by mechanotransduction, whereby repeated, controlled stretching enhances muscle length, tendon elasticity, and joint flexibility [33]. Multiplanar movements promoted dynamic stretching, joint lubrication, and both passive and active ROM, whereas gradual strengthening at extended joint positions supported functional mobility [34]. Improved ROM facilitates daily activities and may contribute to reduced fall risk, consistent with evidence that assisted, resistance-based mobility exercises enhance joint function in community-dwelling older adults [14,35].

The leg support ring was further associated with significant improvements in ADL and QoL, with moderate effect sizes (ηp2≈0.105–0.146), indicating meaningful benefits beyond physiological outcomes. Improvements in muscle strength and joint mobility may have enabled participants to perform daily activities such as walking, dressing, and transferring more safely and independently, thereby promoting functional autonomy [20]. These functional gains may also have enhanced self-efficacy and perceived capability, consistent with Pender’s Health Promotion Model [23], and may have contributed to improved psychological well-being and social participation [36]. In this study, QoL was evaluated using the overall WHOQoL-BREF score rather than domain-level scores, because QoL was treated as a secondary outcome reflecting the broader functional impact of improvements in lower limb motor power, ROM, and ADL. Assessing overall QoL allowed the study to capture the global effect of improved physical functioning on participants’ well-being. These findings are consistent with previous evidence that resistance-based and functional exercise programs can enhance both physical performance and overall QoL in older adults [37,38]. Unlike many prior studies conducted in clinical or urban settings [39,40], this intervention was implemented in a rural Thai community, highlighting the leg support ring as a practical and accessible rehabilitation strategy for resource-limited settings.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. Individual-level randomization minimized selection bias, though the open-label design may have led to performance bias, as participants and volunteers were aware of group allocation. In addition, the study was conducted in a single rural community in Eastern Thailand, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare systems with different demographic, socioeconomic, or healthcare-delivery contexts. Furthermore, the relatively short 3-month follow-up period limits conclusions about the long-term sustainability and effectiveness of the intervention.

Future studies should use balanced control conditions and more robust analytical methods. Nevertheless, the innovative leg support ring represents a feasible, low-cost, community-based rehabilitation option for rural Thailand. Its simple, home-based design requires minimal supervision and can be supported by village health volunteers and caregivers, which may improve adherence and reduce access barriers. Compared with clinic-based approaches, this model offers greater scalability for underserved populations, although adaptation to local contexts will be necessary for sustainability.

This study used a structured, theory-based, community-supported intervention with safe, practical exercises for older adults with lower limb muscle weakness. Validated measures of motor power, ROM, ADL, and QoL, together with high adherence, no loss to follow-up, and support from village health volunteers, helped strengthen the internal validity of the findings. These findings suggest that health-promoting hospitals and community networks should consider integrating leg support rings into rehabilitation programs, with trained volunteers supporting safe use and adherence. Accessible, home-based assistive devices may help maintain independence, reduce fall risk, and improve QoL in line with active aging policies. Future studies should use larger, multi-center RCTs with longer follow-up periods and comparisons with conventional interventions. Further research should also examine underlying neuromuscular and psychosocial mechanisms to optimize the implementation of this community-based approach.
