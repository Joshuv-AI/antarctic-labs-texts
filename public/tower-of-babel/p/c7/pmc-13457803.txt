A deep learning pipeline for liver macromolecular proton fraction quantification without subject-specific B1 acquisition using spin-lock MRI
Authors: Hongjian Kang, Vincent W. S. Wong, Jiabo Xu, Jian Hou, Baiyan Jiang, Queenie Chan, Ziqiang Yu, Qiuyi Shen, Winnie C. W. Chu, Weitian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMacromolecular proton fraction (MPF) is a promising noninvasive biomarker for staging liver fibrosis. However, current post-processing requires B1-inhomogeneity acquisition and manual region of interest (ROI) selection, introducing subjectivity and variability. In this study, we propose a deep learning pipeline that enables liver MPF quantification without subject-specific B1 acquisition by leveraging an atlas-derived B1 map constructed from measured B1 data.MethodsThis retrospective study used data collected at one institution from April 2019 to October 2019. The pipeline contains three models: a segmentation network for obtaining liver masks, a registration network for aligning the atlas B1 map with liver masks, and a quantification network for MPF quantification. An uncertainty-guided strategy is proposed to automatically select ROIs for assessing liver MPF. The accuracy of MPF quantification was evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE), structural similarity index measure (SSIM), and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR). Reproducibility of liver MPF measurement was assessed by comparing manually selected ROIs from two experts with automated ROIs, employing intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Bland-Altman analysis.ResultsThe study included 44 patients (mean age, 59.4±9.7 years; 20 male patients, 24 female patients). MAE for MPF quantification within the whole liver and ROI are 0.49%±0.31% and 0.45%±0.26%, respectively. ICC of liver MPF assessments are 0.931 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.887, 0.963] between expert 1 analyst and automated analysis, 0.949 (95% CI: 0.924, 0.971) between the expert 2 analyst and automated analysis, and 0.938 (95% CI: 0.903, 0.959) between expert 1 and expert 2 analyst. Mean bias [95% limits of agreement (LOA)] were 0.042% (−0.514%, 0.598%), −0.029% (−0.445%, 0.388%), and 0.033% (−0.497%, 0.563%) for expert 1 vs. automated analysis, expert 2 vs. automated analysis, and expert 1 vs. expert 2, respectively.ConclusionsThe proposed deep learning pipeline enables liver MPF quantification without subject-specific B1 acquisition by employing an atlas-based B1 substitution strategy, while maintaining high reproducibility in a fully automated manner.

Introduction

Chronic liver disease poses a significant global healthcare challenge (1,2). Liver fibrosis, the process of excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix proteins including collagen resulting from persistent liver injury, is a key diagnostic and prognostic feature of chronic liver disease (3,4).

Currently, histological assessment of liver biopsy specimens remains the widely accepted method for determining the presence and stage of liver fibrosis (5,6). However, liver biopsy is invasive and subject to errors caused by sampling and intra-observer variability, making it unsuitable for screening or repeated monitoring (7). Consequently, developing noninvasive and objective methods to assess and monitor liver fibrosis is of great importance in clinical hepatology.

Several promising imaging methods have been developed for detecting liver fibrosis (8-11). However, these methods still have limitations, such as insufficient sensitivity in detecting early-stage fibrosis or the requirement for additional hardware.

Previous studies have explored the use of semi-quantitative magnetization transfer ratio (MTR) measurements to detect collagen deposition in the liver, but the findings have been controversial (12-14). MTR measurement is not specific to macromolecules (collagens) and is confounded by pulse sequence parameters. The measurement of macromolecular proton fraction (MPF), a parameter based on magnetization transfer (MT) that reflects the content of macromolecule pools, offers greater tissue specificity and is independent of acquisition parameters (15). It has been reported that MPF has the potential to detect liver fibrosis, although evidence to date comes from studies with limited sample sizes (16). However, the conventional quantitative MT approach, which relies on saturation radiofrequency (RF) pulses, requires the acquisition of T1, B1, and B0 maps in addition to MT-weighted images for quantification. This complexity makes it difficult to apply in the liver due to image misregistration caused by respiratory motions and dynamic blood flow.

Recently, a method termed MPF quantification based on spin-lock (MPF-SL) has been proposed for MPF mapping (17,18). Preliminary work indicates that this method shows promise for imaging liver fibrosis (19). The workflow of MPF-SL for MPF assessment is shown in Figure 1. When performing MPF-SL, four spin-lock-prepared images (dynamic scans) are acquired within a brief breath-hold. The signal associated with the water pool is decoupled from that of the MT pool based on spin-lock relaxometry and is removed from the acquired signal. This feature of MPF-SL allows for MPF measurement without direct water saturation effects and eliminates the need for knowledge of T1 or the acquisition of a T1 map for MPF quantification. However, a B1 map is still required for MPF quantification with MPF-SL. Specifically, the MPF-SL framework employs a rotary-echo spin-lock RF pulse for spin-lock preparation, and the quantification relies on two groups of spin-lock parameters. Methods

Subjects

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Joint CUHK-NTEC CREC, Hong Kong, China (No. 2016.662) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were collected from April 2019 to October 2019. Inclusion criteria included: (I) subjects aged between 18 and 70 years; and (II) clinical, radiological, and/or histological evidence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Exclusion criteria included: positive hepatitis B surface antigen or anti-hepatitis C virus antibody, current or past history of hepatocellular carcinoma or liver decompensation, and history of liver resection or transplantation. MRI acquisition and annotation

All MRI scans were performed at the Department of Imaging and Interventional Radiology, Prince of Wales Hospital, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, China on a 3-T MRI scanner (Achieva TX, Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands). The body coil was used as the transmitter with the dual-transmit enabled, and a 32-channel cardiac coil (Invivo Corp., Orlando, FL, USA) was used as the receiver. The MPF-SL pulse sequence was implemented on this scanner using the vendor-native PARADISE programming platform.

Axial images, including the MPF-SL acquisitions, were acquired with phase encoding along the anterior-posterior direction. Six slices of MPF-SL were acquired through the largest cross-section of the liver, with each slice obtained during a 12-second breath-hold. A two-dimensional (2D) multi-slice B1 map based on the dual refocusing echo acquisition mode (DREAM) was also acquired in the transverse plane, with a scan time of 13 seconds during a breath-hold (20). Additionally, standard clinical volumetric T2-weighted (T2W) images covering the whole liver were acquired using a multi-slice 2D fast/turbo spin echo sequence. MRI sequence parameters are summarized in Table 2. The spin-lock parameters were selected to satisfy the condition Δω(1)/ω1(1)=Δω(2)/ω1(2) as derived in ref. (17), balancing MPF signal contrast and specific absorption rate (SAR) constraints. No SAR-related scan interruptions were observed across all participants in this study. The TSL of 50 ms was chosen to balance MPF signal contrast, SAR constraints, and breath-hold duration feasibility for abdominal imaging at 3T, following the parameter optimization described in ref. (17). Different inter-slice gaps were used for the two acquisitions (Table 2). The DREAM B1 stack (5 mm × 15 mm) was prescribed so that its through-plane coverage fully encompassed that of the MPF-SL stack (6 mm × 7 mm), with additional margin beyond the cranial and caudal MPF-SL slices. The thicker B1 slices were necessary to keep the acquisition within a single 13-second breath-hold given the longer per-slice time of the DREAM sequence, and are acceptable because the B1 field varies smoothly in the through-plane direction in the liver at 3-T (21). The B1 images were manually matched with the MPF-SL images at the same anatomical level in vivo by visual inspection of intra-hepatic landmarks on the localizer images, without any rigid or deformable registration.

During MPF-SL acquisition, a shimming box was applied to the right lobe of the liver to reduce B0 field inhomogeneity, and RF shimming was utilized to minimize B1 RF inhomogeneity. Blood signal suppression was achieved using a double inversion recovery combined with fast/turbo spin echo readout (22), and fat suppression was accomplished with spectral pre-saturation with inversion recovery (SPIR) in MPF-SL imaging.

For MPF calculation, a ROI was manually drawn to cover a large portion of liver parenchyma while excluding major intrahepatic vessels, the gallbladder and the areas with visible artifacts. Mean MPF within the ROI was calculated to represent the MPF of the corresponding slice. This single-ROI design follows the established clinical evaluation protocol for liver MPF-SL fibrosis staging in ref. (19), in which a single representative ROI per slice yields a summary mean MPF value consistent with the clinical endpoint of fibrosis staging. To assess reproducibility and compare with the proposed method, two experts independently selected ROIs. The manually selected ROIs and corresponding mean MPF values served as reference measurements for evaluating the agreement of automated liver MPF assessment. Direct geometric agreement between automated and expert-defined ROIs was not assessed; instead, the validation focused on the reproducibility of mean MPF values extracted from the ROIs, which represents the clinically relevant endpoint in this study.

Deep learning pipeline

This study aimed to develop a pipeline to automatically quantify MPF maps using the previously proposed MPF-SL protocol (17), which includes acquisition of four spin-lock-prepared images and a B1 map, with automated ROI selections for MPF assessment. The feasibility of quantifying liver MPF without acquiring a B1 map was also evaluated. All data splits were performed on a patient-wise basis. Atlas construction, registration network training, and quantification network training were performed exclusively using the training set, with the test set held out entirely for final evaluation to ensure strict separation and avoid data leakage.

Figure 2 provides an overview of the proposed pipeline, which consists of three networks: a segmentation network, a registration network, and a quantification network. For segmentation, clinical T2W images were input into the segmentation network FS to obtain liver masks. Multi-scale superpixels (small, perceptually homogeneous regions where each region groups neighboring pixels with similar intensity and texture characteristics) with anatomical similarity were obtained using the Simple Linear Iterative Clustering (SLIC) algorithm from the T2W images (23). For registration, an atlas B1 map paired with the binary liver mask was input into the registration network FR as the moving and fixed images, respectively, to generate the deformation field φ. A spatial transformation module was then used to obtain the warped B1 map for MPF quantification. For quantification, the binary liver mask was applied to the spin-lock-prepared images to remove background signals. The quantification network FQ then received these images along with the warped B1 map as input and output the predicted MPF map. An uncertainty map was generated using the Monte Carlo Dropout method (24). Finally, multi-scale superpixels from the T2W image fused with the uncertainty map were used to automatically obtain the ROI following a rule-based strategy described in the “Uncertainty-guided ROI selection” section. All the networks were initialized with randomization. Thirty-three subjects were used for training (including atlas construction and network training) and 11 subjects were held out for testing.

The general framework of our proposed deep learning pipeline, consisting of three networks: a segmentation network FS, a registration network FR, and a quantification network FQ. FR is used in two stages: during training, each subject’s liver B1 map (moving image) is registered to the reference subject’s liver mask (fixed image) to construct the atlas B1 map; during inference, the atlas B1 map (moving image) is warped to the subject’s liver mask (fixed image) to generate a subject-specific B1 map. Network architecture

In this study, nnUNet (25) pre-trained on T2W images from the CHAOS dataset (26), was used as the segmentation network FS, and VoxelMorph (27), trained from scratch using our liver MRI dataset, was used as the registration network FR.

Figure 3 illustrates the architecture of the quantification network FQ, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) model. FQ takes a vector of values of four spin-lock-prepared images and the corresponding B1 value as input and outputs the MPF value. To adapt the MLP as a Bayesian network to estimate uncertainty, two dropout layers with a dropout rate of 0.2 were added to the last two layers. Ground truth MPF maps were generated using the dictionary-based fitting procedure described in ref. (17), as also adopted in ref. (19); the fixed tissue parameters and the simulated MPF range used to construct the dictionary are detailed in Appendix 1. It should be noted that the quantitative error metrics reported in this study reflect agreement between the proposed pipeline and this specific reference implementation using measured B1 maps, rather than deviation from an absolute biophysical standard. The mean square error (MSE) loss function was used for training. A UNet (28) was also employed for image-wise prediction comparison to the MLP.

The architecture of the quantification network FQ. The network takes as input a vector of five values: four spin-lock-prepared MR signals (MTog1, MTog2, MnoTog1, MnoTog2, where MTog and MnoTog denote signals acquired with and without the adiabatic inversion toggling RF pulse at two spin-lock parameter settings, respectively) and the corresponding B1 value, from which Rmpfsl is derived given the TSL. Training was performed using MSE loss, with ground truth MPF maps generated by the physics-based reference MPF-SL pipeline of ref. (17). Atlas B1 generation

To achieve MPF quantification using MPF-SL without additional acquisition of B1 map, we investigated an approach to generate a representative atlas B1 map that reflects normal liver anatomy while preserving inhomogeneity distribution in sub-segments. The atlas B1 generation process is included in Figure 2. The atlas liver B1 was generated in two steps. First, the binary liver masks of all training subjects were non-rigidly registered to a reference subject with typical liver size and anatomy, and pixel-wise majority voting was applied to the registered binary masks to produce a consensus liver mask. Second, the corresponding quantitative B1 maps were warped using the same deformation fields, and a weighted average was computed using weights proportional to the inverse of the local variance across subjects, thereby giving higher influence to regions with more consistent B1 values. The liver B1 map of each subject (moving image IM) in the training set was registered to a standardized liver mask (fixed image IF) via the registration network FR, generating a warped B1 map I′M.

It should be noted that although the proposed framework eliminates the need for subject-specific B1 acquisition during inference, it does not remove dependence on B1 information entirely. Instead, an atlas-derived B1 map constructed from measured B1 data acquired under the same scanner and imaging protocol is used as a substitute. Therefore, the applicability of this approach is conditional on matched acquisition settings.

Uncertainty-guided ROI selection

To develop automated ROI selection, we investigated the approach to use neural network to calculate uncertainty of MPF quantification and use this uncertainty map to guide automated ROI selection. Specifically, the ROI was obtained by fusing superpixels generated from the T2W image with the uncertainty map using the following rule-based strategy:

Statistical analysis

MPF quantification accuracy of the proposed pipeline was evaluated against the ground truth MPF maps generated by the reference MPF-SL pipeline (19) using root MSE (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), structural similarity index measure (SSIM), and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

The reproducibility of liver MPF measurement was evaluated pairwise (expert 1, expert 2, automated) using Bland-Altman analysis, where each data point represents the mean MPF value within the ROI of a single slice from a single subject, yielding a total of 66 data points (11 subjects × 6 slices per subject). Metrics obtained and presented include intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), bias, SD, limits of agreement (LOA), within-subject coefficient of variation (wCV), and coefficient of reproducibility (RDC). Bland-Altman plots and scatterplots were generated. Corrected P values less than 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

The accuracy of atlas B1 registration was evaluated at two levels. At the geometric level, the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) between the subject liver mask and the atlas liver mask was computed before and after registration to quantify spatial alignment improvement. At the intensity level, the MAE between the warped atlas B1 map and the subject-specific measured B1 map was computed within each of the eight liver sub-segments across test subjects.

Evaluation of hyperparameters selection

We also conducted a comparative analysis for hyperparameter selections, including the dropout rate of quantification network. The results are shown in Figure S1. By comprehensive analysis of RMSE, MAE, SSIM, and PSNR with different dropout rates, we determined that the optimal value for the dropout rate is 0.2. We also evaluated the effectiveness of multi-scale superpixels with different region sizes (scale 1 =40×40, scale 2 =20×20, scale 3 =10×10). The results of ablative analysis are shown in Table S2. The combination of all three scales (scale 1 + scale 2 + scale 3) achieved the best overall performance and was therefore adopted for ROI prediction in all main experiments reported in this study.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed a deep learning pipeline automating the post-processing for MPF-SL. Our findings demonstrated that the proposed method can enable MPF quantification without subject-specific B1 acquisition during inference by substituting an atlas-derived B1 map generated from measured data, and provide reproducible automated MPF assessment in the liver using an uncertainty-guided ROI selection strategy in a fully automated manner. This standardized post-processing framework has the potential to improve the feasibility of using MPF-SL in clinical workflows.

B1 inhomogeneity in the local transmitted magnetic field degrades image quality and introduces spatially varying errors in the transmitted flip angle, affecting the existing quantitative MT mapping methods (15,17). Consequently, a B1 map is necessary for reliable MPF quantification, which increases scan time and complicates the workflow. B1 inhomogeneity mainly stems from two physical effects: the conductivity and permittivity of the imaged tissues (29,30). In abdominal imaging at 3-T, these effects combine to result in signal voids, which can be particularly severe depending on body geometry and composition. A recent study reported significant B1 inhomogeneities in the liver, indicating large variations between eight different liver sub-segments (21). Leveraging this statistical anatomical prior, we designed an atlas-based method to generate corresponding B1 maps from an existing atlas B1 map, mitigating the need for acquiring a B1 map for each patient. We demonstrate that our method has potential to provide reliable MPF quantification of the liver without the need for subject-specific B1 map acquisition. It should be noted that the primary motivation for this atlas-based approach is not simply to reduce acquisition time, but rather to eliminate the dependency on a vendor-specific B1 mapping sequence that is not universally available across scanner platforms, thereby making MPF-SL quantification viable in a broader range of clinical settings. It is also important to note the atlas B1 map itself was constructed under specific acquisition conditions. During MPF-SL data collection, dual-transmit RF shimming was applied with a shimming box targeted to the right lobe of the liver, which reduces spatial B1 variation within the ROI. Consequently, the atlas B1 map reflects the residual B1 distribution after shimming, rather than the native unshimmed field. Both the atlas B1 spatial pattern and the training targets of the quantification network were therefore derived under shimmed conditions, meaning the pipeline implicitly depends on this specific shimming strategy. Application of the pipeline to data acquired without equivalent RF shimming capabilities, or with a different shimming approach, would alter the underlying B1 distribution and would likely require reconstruction of the atlas and retraining of the quantification network under the corresponding matched conditions. More broadly, translation to a different scanner vendor or transmit architecture would in general require both re-calibration of the atlas B1 map and re-training of the quantification network under matched conditions, as the B1 spatial distribution is inherently specific to the transmit coil geometry, shimming strategy, and field strength of the source scanner. The modular design of our pipeline with atlas construction and network training as separable components, is intended to facilitate such platform-specific adaptation, although the associated data collection requirements should be recognized as a practical consideration for future multi-site deployment.

MT effect can be used to probe macromolecular environment of tissues. Quantitative MT provides measurements specific to tissue properties and thus more accurate tissue characterization compared to conventional semi-quantitative approaches such as MTR, which is confounded by acquisition parameters (13). However, quantitative MT typically requires knowledge of the water pools to quantify MT parameters, which often prolongs the scan time and creates challenges to applications in moving organs such as the liver. An advantage of the spin-lock-based quantitative MT approach MPF-SL is that it can be used to remove the signal from the water pool to simplify the quantification model. However, from a clinical perspective, the use of MPF-SL technology still requires optimization in post-processing. In previous MPF-SL studies, manual ROI selection was performed to avoid artifacts, major vessels, liver edges, and lesions. This manual process introduces unavoidable subjectivity and potential measurement variability. To reduce inter- and intra-rater variability, we proposed an uncertainty-guided strategy combined with multi-scale superpixels to provide anatomical semantics for ROI selection. High uncertainty areas generally correspond to areas with uncommon values, including blood vessels and regions with artifacts, which should be avoided in ROI selection. By leveraging quantification uncertainty estimates, we can filter out unreliable predictions and preserve only reliable ones.

Note the choice of the scale of superpixels during image segmentation is crucial for ROI selections: a smaller scale results in larger superpixels that poorly maintain anatomical boundaries, while a larger scale results in smaller, irregularly shaped superpixels with challenging neighborhood relationships (23). For liver segmentation in this study, a superpixel size of 10×10 (scale 3) to 40×40 (scale 1) is generally sufficient. Scale1 works well for liver segmentation but may be less effective in filtering out superpixels based on uncertainty thresholds in certain cases. Scale3 ensures the algorithm handles small liver regions near the edges without failing. Note the multi-scale superpixels achieve a better performance.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the method may fail in the presence of large motion artifacts. Secondly, all data were acquired at a single center using one 3T Philips system with consistent RF transmission and acquisition settings, which may limit generalizability to other MRI platforms or field strengths. The atlas-derived B1 map was constructed under these matched conditions, and the proposed strategy assumes consistent scanner hardware, RF transmission characteristics, and acquisition parameters between atlas construction and deployment. However, B1+ distribution is influenced by scanner hardware, RF transmission design, coil configuration, field strength, and subject body habitus. Substantial deviations in vendor platform, parallel transmission architecture, field strength, or coil configuration may alter the spatial B1 profile relative to that represented in the atlas, potentially introducing systematic bias in MPF estimation if a scanner-matched atlas is not available. Although no systematic failure cases attributable to atlas substitution were observed within our testing cohort under matched conditions, the robustness of the method across different vendors, field strengths, or substantially different acquisition settings remains to be systematically evaluated. Multi-vendor and multi-center studies will be required to assess cross-platform reproducibility and robustness. Finally, extreme variations in body habitus, particularly in patients with obesity where altered tissue conductivity and geometry can substantially change B1 patterns, may not be fully captured by a single atlas-derived B1 map. Further validation in more diverse populations will be necessary.

Looking ahead, extension of the proposed framework to 1.5-T is also of interest. At lower field strength, liver B1 inhomogeneity is substantially less severe due to reduced dielectric effects (21), and SAR constraints on spin-lock implementation are correspondingly relaxed, potentially making a simpler B1 approximation sufficient in place of full atlas-based substitution. Whether atlas-based B1 correction remains necessary at 1.5-T warrants empirical investigation in future work.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed and validated a novel deep learning pipeline for liver MPF quantification and assessment using spin-lock MRI. The method eliminates the need for subject-specific B1 map acquisition by employing an atlas-based approach, thereby simplifying the workflow. Additionally, the pipeline incorporates an uncertainty-guided strategy for automated ROI selection, which reduces inter- and intra-rater variability and enhances reproducibility. By streamlining post-processing, this approach represents a promising step toward facilitating the broader adoption of MPF imaging for noninvasive liver fibrosis assessment, pending further validation in multi-center and multi-vendor settings.
