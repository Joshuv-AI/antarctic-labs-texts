Direct pulp capping versus partial pulpotomy using RetroMTA® in deeply carious primary molars with normal pulp or reversible pulpitis: a superiority randomized controlled trial
Authors: Chanakarn Intawong, Chanika Manmontri, Papimon Chompu-inwai, Areerat Nirunsittirat, Nattakan Chaipattanawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronal pulpotomy (CP) has commonly been used after vital pulp exposure in deeply carious primary molars, although some concerns remain regarding unnecessary pulp removal and procedural morbidity. With the increasing use of calcium silicate cements (CSCs), more conservative vital pulp therapies, such as direct pulp capping (DPC) and partial pulpotomy (PP), have received growing attention. However, direct comparative clinical evidence between these approaches in primary molars is limited. Accordingly, this study sought to compare the cumulative survival probabilities of DPC and PP using RetroMTA® in deeply carious primary molars with normal pulp or reversible pulpitis.MethodsThis university-based, assessor-blinded, parallel-group superiority randomized controlled trial enrolled children aged 3–9 years with at least one eligible primary molar. Eighty-eight molars were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either DPC or PP. All treatments were performed by postgraduate dental students following a strict protocol under the supervision of a single instructor, and all molars were restored immediately with stainless steel crowns (SSC). Follow-up examinations were scheduled approximately every 6 months for up to 24 months. The primary outcome was the cumulative survival probability of pulp treatment, defined by the fulfilment of both clinical and radiographic success criteria. Kaplan–Meier analysis and log-rank tests were used to compare survival probabilities between groups.ResultsSeventy-eight molars were available for final analysis (40 DPC; 38 PP). Only two radiographic failures occurred in the DPC group, comprising pathological external root resorption at 7 months and furcation radiolucency associated with SSC dislodgement at 11 months. The cumulative survival probabilities were 93.8% for DPC and 100% for PP over the follow-up period (mean: 13.3 months; range: 6–23 months), with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.153).ConclusionsPP was not demonstrated to be superior to DPC using RetroMTA®. Both treatments showed favorable short-term survival under controlled conditions; however, the limited follow-up duration (mean: 13.3 months) and the low number of failure events warrant cautious interpretation. Further adequately powered studies with longer follow-up are required before routine clinical use can be recommended.Trial registrationThe study was retrospectively registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry on 8 May 2024 under the registration number TCTR20240508001.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08674-z.Keywords: Deep carious lesions, Direct pulp capping, Partial pulpotomy, Primary molars

Background

In the management of deep carious lesions in primary teeth with normal pulp or reversible pulpitis, indirect pulp treatment (IPT) using a selective caries removal approach is strongly recommended, with moderate certainty of evidence at 24 months [1]. Nevertheless, challenges in accurately diagnosing pulpal status in children, along with concerns regarding residual bacterial infection following selective removal, have led some clinicians to continue favoring non-selective caries removal [2]. Consequently, when vital pulp exposure occurs following non-selective caries removal, coronal pulpotomy (CP) has traditionally been the preferred treatment among clinicians [3, 4]. Furthermore, this approach is supported by a recent systematic review indicating that CP with calcium silicate cements (CSCs), such as Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (MTA), achieves long-term clinical success comparable to IPT in primary teeth [5]. This high success rate is attributed to CSCs’ excellent biocompatibility, bioactivity, antimicrobial properties, and superior sealing ability [6].

Despite the favorable outcomes of CP, concerns remain regarding its biological and procedural implications. CP may involve the unnecessary removal of healthy coronal pulp tissue [7], increase the risk of dentinal wall weakening [8], potentially lead to iatrogenic furcal perforation [9], cause greater postoperative pain [10], and require a longer operative time [11], which may result in greater discomfort and reduced cooperation in children. Collectively, these limitations have increased interest in conservative, biologically driven vital pulp therapies, such as direct pulp capping (DPC) and partial pulpotomy (PP), which preserve healthy coronal pulp tissue and are well supported by clinical guidelines for permanent teeth [12, 13].

DPC is a more conservative and time-saving treatment, causing less discomfort in children than CP; however, its indication is currently limited to primary teeth diagnosed with normal pulp status and a pinpoint (≤ 1 mm) mechanical or traumatic pulp exposure [14–16]. This restriction may reflect the limited quantity and heterogeneity of high-quality clinical evidence available at the time of guideline development. Nevertheless, emerging evidence suggests that the use of MTA may improve the success of DPC in primary teeth with deep cavitated carious lesions that result in vital pulp exposure ≤ 1 mm in diameter during caries excavation [17–19]. Furthermore, in this clinical context, Chatzidimitriou et al. [20] reported favorable outcomes with DPC using CSCs as pulp-capping agents in primary molars diagnosed with normal pulp or reversible pulpitis, even with a pulp exposure size up to 2 mm. In addition, Dimitraki et al. [21] reported comparable success rates between DPC and CP in primary teeth when MTA was used as the medicament, regardless of the size of pulp exposure, thereby supporting reconsideration of the current restrictive indications for DPC.

However, a key limitation of DPC is the lack of removal of potentially infected and inflamed coronal pulp tissue, which may compromise healing in carious pulp exposures where the extent of inflammation cannot be reliably assessed clinically [22]. PP may therefore be considered a biologically sound and conservative alternative to CP, involving the superficial removal of approximately 2 mm of coronal pulp tissue at the exposure site, followed by placement of a biocompatible material [23]. In mature permanent teeth with carious pulp exposure and reversible pulpitis, DPC and PP using CSC demonstrated comparable success rates at the 12-month follow-up [24]. In primary molars with deep carious lesions, comparable overall 24-month success rates have been reported between PP using MTA and CP using formocresol [7]. Nevertheless, the recent American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) guideline does not recommend PP for primary teeth, restricting its indication to immature permanent teeth [14].

To the best of our knowledge, direct comparative evidence between DPC and PP remains scarce, particularly in primary molars treated with contemporary CSCs. RetroMTA® (BioMTA, Seoul, South Korea), a recently developed fast-setting CSC, offers excellent biocompatibility, strong sealing ability, and minimal tooth discoloration [25], making it suitable for pediatric use. A few clinical studies have evaluated the application of RetroMTA® in CP of primary molars [26, 27], and it may also improve healing responses and favorable clinical outcomes when used with DPC and PP. Therefore, this university-based superiority randomized controlled trial aimed to compare the survival probabilities of DPC and PP using RetroMTA® in deeply carious primary molars diagnosed with normal pulp or reversible pulpitis over a follow-up period of up to 24 months. The null hypothesis was that PP would not be superior to DPC in terms of survival probability.

Methods

Study design, study participants, and ethics approval

This study was designed as a university-based, randomized, assessor-blinded, parallel-group, superiority clinical trial to compare the survival probabilities of DPC and PP using RetroMTA® as a medicament for deeply carious primary molars in pediatric patients attending the Pediatric Dental Clinic, Faculty of Dentistry, Chiang Mai University, between August 2023 and April 2025.

This study was approved by the Human Experimentation Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Chiang Mai University, Chiang Mai, Thailand (registration number: 50/2023). It has been registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20240508001). The study procedures were fully explained to the participants and their legal guardians, and written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians prior to participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Its design and reporting followed the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [28] and the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement [29].

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was estimated using an automatic calculation program for superiority trials [30]. The estimation was based on the primary outcome measure, the percentage difference in cumulative survival probabilities between DPC and PP. As no clinical study has directly compared these interventions in primary molars with deep carious lesions, survival estimates were derived from separate published clinical trials, reporting 18-month survival probabilities of 73.6% for DPC [21] and 100% for PP [31]. Assuming an effect size of 26.4%, with 90% power and a two-sided significance level of 5%, 30 molars per group were required. Allowing for a potential 20% loss to follow-up, the final minimum sample size was increased to 38 molars per group.

Eligibility criteria

Pediatric patients classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) I or II, aged 3 to 9 years, attending the Pediatric Dentistry Clinic, Faculty of Dentistry, Chiang Mai University, were recruited. Patients presenting with at least one carious primary molar were screened by the main investigator (N.C.) according to predefined clinical and radiographic eligibility criteria.

The clinical inclusion criteria were primary molars that: (1) had extensive active carious lesions at high risk of pulp exposure during non-selective caries removal; (2) were deemed restorable with stainless steel crowns (SSCs); (3) had no history of spontaneous or nocturnal pain; (4) had no clinical signs of swelling/abscess, fistula/sinus tract opening, tenderness to percussion/palpation, or abnormal mobility; (5) had at least one pulp exposure ≤ 2 mm in diameter [20] measured by using a double-ended dental amalgam plugger; and (6) had vital pulp, as judged by its appearance (bright red color) and the presence of profuse bleeding from the pulp exposure site that could be controlled within 4 min [32]. However, primary molars were excluded if signs of pulp necrosis were present, including absence of bleeding or the presence of pale, necrotic pulp tissue.

The radiographic inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the presence of caries radiolucency extending into inner one-third of dentin but not reaching the pulp, corresponding to International Caries Classification and Management System (ICCMS) radiographic code RC5 [33]; (2) the absence of periapical or furcation radiolucency; (3) the absence of pathologic external or internal root resorption; and (4) the presence of physiologic root resorption less than one-third of the root length with an intact lamina dura. However, primary molars were excluded if additional radiographic findings not meeting the above criteria were present, including calcification or pulp canal obliteration, as well as developmental anomalies affecting root or pulp morphology.

Randomization, allocation concealment and blinding

The participants were unaware of the types of treatment performed for each molar as they were blindly and randomly assigned to receive either DPC or PP, with a balanced treatment allocation (1:1). The random allocation sequence was generated using a web-based randomization generator [34], employing a randomized block design with a block size of four, prepared by an independent statistician. Allocation concealment was performed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE). These envelopes were prepared by a research assistant who was not involved in the clinical trial recruitment or intervention procedures. The envelopes were opened sequentially by a dental assistant only after vital pulp exposure was confirmed following non-selective caries removal, ensuring allocation concealment. In cases where patients had two eligible primary molars included, the second molar was assigned to different intervention.

Study interventions

All treatments were completed in a single dental visit using basic behavior guidance techniques and were performed by 12 postgraduate pediatric dentistry students who were thoroughly trained in the details of the techniques and procedures involved. Operator blinding was not feasible because of inherent differences between the intervention techniques. However, all procedural steps were conducted according to strict, standardized protocols and supervised by a single experienced instructor (N.C.).

Preoperative bitewing and periapical radiographs were obtained for each molar before treatment, in accordance with AAPD guidelines, to evaluate caries extent and periapical or furcation pathology [35]. Whenever feasible, radiographs were acquired using bite tabs for bitewing radiographs and Snap-A-Ray holders for periapical radiographs to ensure consistent positioning and minimize variations in angulation.

The carious molar was anesthetized using topical anesthesia (One Touch; Hager Worldwide, Ontario, Canada), followed by the administration of 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Septanest SP; Septodont, Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, France). After administration of local anesthesia, the molar was isolated with a rubber dam and cleaned using a rubber cup with pumice and water. The cavity was prepared using a high-speed round diamond bur. Non-selective carious tissue removal was then performed using a slow-speed round steel bur and a sharp spoon excavator, proceeding from the peripheral to the central part of the cavity. When there was a pulp exposure, the cavity was rinsed with normal saline solution and pulp vitality was assessed based on its appearance, including a resilient texture, bright red color, and bleeding overflow from the exposure site. If pulpal bleeding was controlled by exerting light pressure for 4 min with sterile cotton pellets moistened with normal saline solution, a dental assistant opened the envelope containing the assigned intervention.

For the DPC group (Fig. 1), RetroMTA® was mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and gently applied over the exposure site and surrounding dentin to a thickness of approximately 1.5 mm. In contrast, for the PP group (Fig. 2), the pulp at the exposed area was amputated to a depth of 2 mm using a sterile high-speed carbide bur (no. 330) with water cooling. The wound surface was irrigated with normal saline solution and hemostasis was achieved by using moist cotton pellets moistened with the same irrigant. If pulpal bleeding could not be controlled within 4 min, the molar was excluded. Then, RetroMTA® was mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and applied to the wound and surrounding dentin to a thickness of approximately 1.5 mm.

Following both interventions, all treated molars were based with a resin-modified glass ionomer cement (Vitrebond™; 3 M ESPE, MN, USA) or reinforced zinc oxide-eugenol (IRM®; Dentsply Sirona, DE, USA), and followed by the placement of SSCs (Stainless Steel Primary Molar Crowns; 3 M ESPE, MN, USA). All crowns were cemented with self-cured resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RelyX™ Luting 2; 3 M ESPE, MN, USA).

Patients were recalled for follow-up approximately every 6 months, and all treated teeth were clinically and radiographically re-examined in the same pediatric dental clinic.

Outcome evaluations

The primary outcome of this study was the cumulative survival probability of DPC or PP, defined as achieving both clinical and radiographic success at approximately 6-month follow-up intervals up to 24 months.

Clinical outcomes were evaluated by one of two American Board-certified experienced pediatric dentists (P.C. or C.M.), who were blinded to the treatment allocation. Clinical failure was defined as the presence of any of the following signs or symptoms: (1) history of spontaneous pain; (2) tenderness to percussion or palpation; (3) abnormal tooth mobility; (4) swelling or abscess; and (5) fistula or sinus tract.

Radiographic outcomes were independently evaluated by two postgraduate students in pediatric dentistry and were blinded to the treatment allocation, with all radiographs masked to conceal the pulp chamber. Criteria for radiographic failure were defined as the presence of: (1) widening of the periodontal ligament space; (2) loss of the lamina dura; (3) periapical and/or furcation radiolucency; or (4) pathologic external or internal root resorption. Teeth that exfoliated naturally, exhibited significant mobility related to physiologic root resorption, or presented with pulp canal obliteration (PCO) during follow-up were not considered failures. These cases were retained in the final analysis and categorized as successful outcomes. Prior to the radiographic evaluation, the assessments of both examiners were calibrated against that of C.M., whose assessment served as the gold standard. Calibration was performed using 20 sets of radiographs obtained at baseline and during follow-up visits from cases treated with CP in primary molars. The level of agreement was assessed using the weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient, interpreted according to Landis and Koch [36]. Inter-examiner reliability demonstrated substantial agreement, with a kappa value of 0.77. The re-evaluation was performed two weeks later, yielding intra-examiner kappa values of 0.78 for examiner 1 and 0.77 for examiner 2.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the study participants in the two treatment groups were compared using the t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables. Clinical, radiographic, and overall success outcomes were calculated as percentages within each treatment group.

For the survival analysis, the event of interest was treatment failure, defined as the occurrence of any clinical or radiographic failure according to the predefined assessment criteria. The survival time was calculated as the interval from the date of treatment (time origin) to the date of failure (event) or to the date of the last follow-up visit for teeth that remained successful, which were treated as right-censored observations. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate cumulative survival probabilities, and the log-rank test was applied to compare the cumulative survival probabilities between the DPC and PP groups. Moreover, the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was conducted to address missing outcomes from teeth that never returned for follow-up using four imputation strategies, including treating all lost cases as surviving, treating all lost cases as failed, and applying best-case and worst-case scenarios. In the best-case scenario, missing outcomes were imputed as survival in the PP group and failure in the DPC group, with the opposite assignments applied in the worst-case scenario. To permit inclusion in survival analyses, failure events imputed at the time origin were assigned a minimal positive survival time (0.01 months), as survival-time variables must be strictly positive for valid estimation.

In addition, Cox proportional hazards regression with robust standard errors clustered at the patient level was planned to account for the clustering of multiple teeth within individuals. However, owing to complete separation, with no failure events observed in either the DPC or PP group, hazard ratios (HR) could not be reliably estimated; thus, Cox regression analysis was not feasible. Consequently, between-group comparisons were supplemented by estimating the absolute difference in successful outcomes using a linear probability model with patient-level cluster bootstrap resampling.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 16.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study participants

The flowchart of study participants in this trial is illustrated in the CONSORT diagram (Fig. 3). A total of 99 primary molars from 82 children were initially assessed for eligibility. Eleven molars did not meet the inclusion criteria; the reasons for exclusion are shown in Fig. 3. The remaining 88 primary molars from 71 children (mean age: 5.99 ± 1.55 years; 40 males and 31 females) were randomized equally into two treatment arms (44 molars per group).

During the follow-up periods of this trial (6 − 24 months), four molars in the DPC group and six molars in the PP group never returned for follow-up, resulting in an overall attrition rate of 11.4%. Consequently, 78 primary molars from 64 children, comprising 40 in the DPC group and 38 in the PP group, were finally available for analysis. Treatment outcomes

The mean follow-up period was 13.28 ± 4.34 months (range: 6–23 months), with a median (interquartile range) of 13 (10, 16) months. Clinical success was 100% in both the DPC (40/40) and PP (38/38) groups. Radiographic success was 95.0% in the DPC group (38/40) and 100% in the PP group (38/38). Examples of radiographic success for both treatments are shown in Figs. 4 and 5.

Only two radiographic failures occurred in the DPC group, comprising pathological external root resorption at 7 months and furcation radiolucency associated with SSC dislodgement at 11 months. Internal root resorption was not observed in any case. Characteristics and reasons for failure are summarized in Table 2. Consequently, the overall success rates were 95.0% for the DPC group and 100% for the PP group. In addition, 6 of 78 treated teeth (7.7%) exhibited PCO, comprising 2 of 40 teeth in the DPC group (5%) and 4 of 38 in the PP group (10.5%).

The Kaplan-Meier cumulative survival curves for primary molars treated with DPC and PP are presented in Table 3; Fig. 6. At the 6-month follow-up, both treatment groups demonstrated a cumulative survival probability of 100%. In the DPC group, survival decreased to 93.8% at 12 months and remained stable thereafter, whereas the PP group maintained a 100% survival probability throughout the follow-up period. Comparison of the survival curves showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.153). Consistent with these results, ITT analyses (Table 4) yielded largely similar findings across multiple imputation strategies, with the exception of the best-case scenario, in which PP demonstrated a higher survival probability (p = 0.013). Overall, these findings did not provide robust evidence supporting the superiority of PP over DPC. Some of the participants had more than one tooth included; thus, the analyses accounted for clustering at the patient level. Due to complete separation, with no failure events observed in the PP group, Cox proportional hazards regression models for estimating HRs were not estimable. Therefore, the between-group absolute difference in success rates was estimated using a linear probability model with patient-level cluster bootstrap resampling. The success rate was 100% (38/38) in the PP group and 95.0% (38/40) in the DPC group, corresponding to a difference of 5.0% (95% CI: −1.0%; 11.0%, p = 0.102).

Discussion

Although DPC and PP are increasingly advocated as conservative alternatives to CP in cases of vital pulp exposure following non-selective caries removal [5, 21], the AAPD guideline recommends DPC only in carefully selected cases and recognizes the limited evidence supporting the use of PP for carious pulp exposures in primary teeth [14]. In addition, comparative studies between DPC and PP in primary molars remain scarce, particularly when contemporary CSCs are used. Thus, this randomized controlled trial was designed to address this guideline-relevant evidence gap by directly comparing the survival probabilities of DPC and PP using RetroMTA®. This study is the first to apply a superiority trial design to directly evaluate whether PP is superior to DPC, reflecting the fundamental differences between the two techniques. PP involves the removal of a small portion of inflamed coronal pulp, whereas DPC preserves the entire pulp tissue. These fundamental differences may influence healing responses in primary teeth, which are characterized by a more reactive pulp and a greater propensity for inflammatory spread.

Although PP demonstrated a higher cumulative survival probability of 100% over the 24-month follow-up period compared with 93.8% for DPC, the between-group difference was not statistically significant, and the superiority of PP over DPC could not be established. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as the absence of failure events in the PP group resulted in complete separation, precluding reliable estimation of hazard ratios using Cox regression. In addition, the very low number of failure events likely limited the statistical power and precision of the effect estimates. Moreover, ITT analyses yielded largely comparable results, with a higher survival probability for PP observed only in the best-case scenario. These findings are consistent with those reported by Taha et al. [24], who observed comparable 12-month success rates for PP (91.5%) and DPC (81.3%) in mature permanent teeth with carious pulp exposure. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, it indicated a trend favoring PP. Thus, longer-term follow-up may be required to determine whether clinically meaningful differences in outcomes emerge over time.

The success of DPC and CP in primary teeth is strongly influenced by the choice of pulp-dressing material, particularly ProRoot® MTA [37, 38]. These approaches provide superior outcomes due to their excellent biocompatibility, sealing ability, and bioactivity. However, their prolonged setting time (165 min), difficult handling, risk of tooth discoloration from bismuth oxide, and higher cost limit their practicality in clinical settings [6]. These drawbacks have driven the development of newer, faster-setting CSC formulations such as RetroMTA®, which provides a much shorter setting time (3 min), easier handling, and reduced grey discoloration, making it more suitable for pediatric use [25]. In addition, previous studies have supported the use of RetroMTA® in primary teeth, including its effectiveness as a pulpotomy material in primary molars [26, 27, 39], consistent with the favorable outcomes observed in the present study. Taken together, the evidence supports the conclusion that conservative vital pulp therapies, whether DPC or PP, can achieve high survival rates for deep carious primary teeth with vital pulp exposure when RetroMTA® is used.

However, in the present study, two failures were observed in the DPC group, identified solely through radiographic findings. One tooth showed furcation involvement, and the other exhibited pathological root resorption. No cases of internal root resorption were detected. These failures may be attributed to the higher inflammatory burden associated with proximal caries [40] and to suboptimal placement of the capping material, as application along axial walls can limit the diffusion of bioactive components toward the occlusal pulp tissue [41]. In addition, only one of the two failures involved SSC dislodgement, which may have resulted in microleakage and subsequent pulpal irritation. These observations highlight the importance of strict clinical protocols in DPC. As this procedure does not involve removing inflamed pulp tissue, distinguishing it from CP, its success depends largely on the pulp’s healing capacity and the host immune response. Therefore, careful debridement of the pulp exposure site, adequate irrigation, and ensuring that the CSC is placed directly over the exposed pulp are essential to reduce inflammation and enhance pulpal healing [42]. Moreover, providing a well-sealed SSC restoration immediately after DPC is crucial, as it protects against microleakage and bacterial contamination that could compromise the procedure’s success [43].

Given the discrepancy between guideline recommendations and emerging clinical evidence, the pulpal diagnosis and appropriate size of pulp exposure for DPC in primary teeth remains a matter of debate. The AAPD currently recommends performing DPC only in a primary tooth with a normal pulp following a small pulp exposure (≤ 1 mm in diameter) [14]. However, in previous clinical studies, DPC has been performed on teeth diagnosed with normal pulp or reversible pulpitis, regardless of exposure size [20, 21, 44]. Notably, only one study by Chatzidimitriou et al. [20] proposed a maximum cut-off exposure size of 2.0 mm for DPC in primary teeth, with 12-month survival rates of 84% regardless of the pulp-capping material used. Following the protocol of the aforementioned studies, primary molars diagnosed with normal pulp or reversible pulpitis and presenting with pulp exposures ≤ 2 mm following non-selective caries removal were included in the present study. Unexpectedly, all failures were found in teeth with normal pulps and small pulp exposures of less than 1.0 mm in diameter (Table 2). This finding contradicts past recommendations suggesting that larger carious pulp exposures may negatively influence the prognosis of DPC in primary teeth [45].

To the best of our knowledge, no clinical investigation in primary teeth has evaluated whether the number of pulp exposure sites affects the clinical and radiographic outcomes of DPC. We hypothesized that multiple pulp exposures may impose a greater inflammatory burden, thereby reducing the likelihood of successful pulpal healing. Consequently, the present study included cases of primary molars with multiple pulp exposures to provide preliminary insight into this potential relationship. Interestingly, all such cases demonstrated successful clinical and radiographic outcomes during follow-up. This finding is consistent with those of Bogen et al. [46], who reported favorable outcomes of DPC using MTA in carious permanent teeth with multiple pulpal exposures. These observations suggest that multiple pulp exposures may not necessarily contraindicate DPC in primary teeth, provided the exposed pulp appears bright red and that hemostasis is achieved within a few minutes. Nevertheless, prospective observational studies in primary teeth are required to further elucidate the impact of this factor on treatment outcomes.

According to the current AAPD guideline on pulp treatment, PP is recommended for traumatic and carious pulp exposures in young permanent teeth [14]; however, clinical evidence supporting its use in primary teeth remains limited. Given this gap, the potential role of PP in primary molars warrants further consideration. In the present study, PP performed with RetroMTA® achieved a 100% overall success rate over a mean follow-up period of 13.28 ± 4.34 months. These favorable outcomes are consistent with the findings of Ebrahimi et al. [31], who reported a 100% success rate at 18 months using MTA as the medicament for PP in deeply carious primary molars. However, studies with longer follow-up periods have demonstrated lower success rates, with Tungjitphianpong et al. [47] and Nematollahi et al. [7] reporting success rates of 83.3% and 81.8%, respectively, at 24 months. Collectively, these findings suggest that extended observation periods may be required to detect late-occurring failures following PP in primary teeth.

The long-term success of pulp treatment depends strongly on the ability of the final restoration to prevent microleakage along the restoration margins [48]. Although all teeth in this study received immediate SSC placement following pulp therapy, the prefabricated nature and limited marginal adaptation of SSCs may compromise long-term sealing [49]. In addition, their short height may result in inadequate coverage of deep proximal cavities, and occlusal perforations in heavily stressed areas can occur, potentially leading to treatment failure [50]. Nevertheless, from a clinical perspective, because DPC and PP are less invasive than CP and have demonstrated effectiveness in managing deeply carious primary teeth, direct restorations (e.g., resin composite) may be considered when sufficient sound tooth structure remains. This approach may reduce the need for SSC placement compared with that observed with CP.

PCO is commonly observed after vital pulp therapy and is attributed to uncontrolled odontoblast-like cell activity. PCO indicates retained pulpal vitality and is not considered a failure [11, 47]. In the present study, PCO was detected in 6 of 78 treated teeth (7.7%), including 2 of 40 teeth in the DPC group (5%) and 4 of 38 in the PP group (10.5%). This incidence was lower than that reported in previous studies in primary molars, with Tungjitphianpong et al. [47] reporting 60% and Nematollahi et al. [7] reporting 28% incidence of PCO after PP at 24 months, whereas Dimitraki et al. [21] observed 20% after DPC at 12 months. It should be noted that the greater amount of coronal pulp removed in PP may provide a stronger stimulus for odontoblast-like cell activation and tertiary dentin deposition, which could explain its higher PCO incidence compared with DPC. However, variations in the reported incidence of PCO across studies should be interpreted with caution, as PCO assessment is inherently subjective and depends on differing radiographic criteria, operational definitions of PCO (partial versus complete canal obliteration), pulp dressing material used, and follow-up durations. In addition, radiographic assessment of primary molars, particularly those in the upper arch, is often limited by root superimposition and overlapping permanent tooth buds. Moreover, dentin bridge formation could not be assessed in our study because SSCs obscured visualization of the pulp-dentin complex.

The present findings have important clinical implications for the management of deep carious lesions in primary teeth diagnosed with normal pulp or reversible pulpitis and complicated by vital pulp exposure following non-selective caries removal. Although superiority of PP over DPC could not be demonstrated, PP offers a stronger biological rationale by selectively removing superficially inflamed tissue while preserving healthy coronal pulp and tooth structure. In clinical situations where pulp exposure can be avoided, IPT remains the preferred minimally invasive approach; however, when vital pulp exposure occurs, PP combined with RetroMTA® may represent an efficient and biologically favorable treatment option suitable for routine pediatric dental practice.

This study has several limitations. First, operator blinding was not feasible due to the nature of the procedures, which may have introduced performance bias; however, adherence to strict protocols and close supervision helped minimize variability. Second, treatments were performed by multiple postgraduate students, which may have contributed to inter-operator differences; however, all operators were thoroughly trained and supervised by a single experienced instructor to standardize clinical execution. Third, this clinical trial was conducted in a single university-based center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings or practitioners with varying levels of experience. Fourth, variations in radiographic angulation, which are difficult to standardize in pediatric patients, may have influenced the interpretation of radiographic outcomes and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Fifth, although randomization resulted in comparable baseline characteristics, stratified randomization was not performed; this may have allowed residual heterogeneity related to preoperative variables (e.g., pulp status or cavity type), which could potentially influence treatment outcomes. Sixth, due to the very low number of failure events, regression-based analyses to evaluate the effect of preoperative variables were not performed, as such models would be statistically unstable and prone to overfitting. Seventh, the sample size was calculated based on an anticipated 26.4% difference between groups. However, the observed difference was only 5.0%, with both treatments exceeding the success rates reported in the reference studies. The study was therefore underpowered to detect this smaller-than-expected difference, and the non-significant result should not be interpreted as equivalence.

Finally, while the follow-up period ranged from 6 to 24 months, the mean follow-up duration was 13.28 ± 4.34 months, and only a limited number of teeth reached the 24-month time point. As shown in Table 3, only two teeth per group were available for assessment at 24 months; therefore, the cumulative survival estimates at this time point should be interpreted with caution. Longer-term evaluations are required to fully assess the durability of pulp healing, the longevity of SSCs, and the potential progression of pulp canal obliteration over time. Nevertheless, this follow-up duration is clinically meaningful, as early failures related to misdiagnosis of pulpitis severity and inadequate removal of inflamed pulp tissue typically manifest within days of treatment [51]. Notably, no additional failures occurred in either group after 11 months, providing preliminary evidence of treatment stability.

Future studies should incorporate larger sample sizes to ensure adequate statistical power for detecting modest but clinically meaningful differences between treatment groups, particularly in settings where overall success rates are high and failure events are relatively rare. In addition, extended follow-up periods and multi-center designs are recommended to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Comparative trials evaluating different types of vital pulp therapies, including IPT, DPC, PP, and CP, are also needed to better define optimal management strategies for deeply carious primary molars. Furthermore, future research should explore the use of pre-mixed CSCs in vital pulp therapies for primary teeth, as their ready-to-use formulation may reduce operator-dependent variability and improve consistency of clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, both DPC and PP using RetroMTA® demonstrated favorable short-term survival outcomes under controlled clinical conditions. However, given the limited follow-up duration (mean: 13.3 months) and the low number of failure events, these findings should be interpreted with caution. While the results suggest the potential feasibility of conservative vital pulp therapies in selected primary molars, further adequately powered multicenter trials with longer follow-up and inclusion of guideline-endorsed comparator treatments are required before clinical equivalence or routine clinical use can be recommended.
