Physical activity among active-duty US military servicemembers with overweight and obesity
Authors: Natalie M. Yarish, Jessica L. Morse, Jennalee S. Wooldridge, Erin E. Dooley, Niloofar Afari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo examine physical activity (PA) among US military servicemembers (SM) with overweight and obesity (OW/OB) and if they meet Physical Activity Guidelines (PAG). To assess differences in PA by sex and associations between PA and body composition.MethodsPA duration and intensity were objectively collected via accelerometers and self-reported daily average via self-report International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) among US active-duty SM (n=113, 58% female, Mean BMI = 33.3 ± 3.9 kg/m2). Sex, body mass index (BMI), and body fat % (BF% via circumference and an equation) were measured. Accelerometer PA and sedentary time were computed for IPAQ categories who met PAG. PA sex differences were assessed with t-tests and chi-square. Pearson correlations examined associations between PA, BMI, and BF%.ResultsAccelerometry showed SM engaged in a daily average of 93 minutes of moderate to vigorous PA (MVPA), 145.2 minutes of light, and 307 minutes of sedentary activity. The IPAQ showed SM engaged in 91 minutes of MVPA, 121.1 minutes of walking, and 423 minutes sitting. Males engaged in more accelerometer-derived MVPA (p = 0.04) than females. Accelerometry showed 100% of SM met the PAG while the IPAQ showed 90% met the PAG. The IPAQ and accelerometer MVPA metrics were correlated (r = 0.26; p < 0.01). Sedentary time was correlated with BMI (r = 0.25; p < 0.01). Sitting was correlated with BF % (r = -0.26; p = 0.02).ConclusionsOverall, SM with OW/OB meet PAG. PA levels among SM are related to sex, BMI, and BF%.

Introduction

Physical activity (PA) is associated with many health benefits, including better physical functioning, higher quality of life, and reduced risk of dementia [1]. Conversely, inadequate PA is associated with increased risk for premature mortality as well as numerous non-communicable diseases, including coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and some cancers [2]. According to the Department of Health and Human Services’ 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans (PAG), adults should engage in at least 150 min of moderate intensity aerobic PA per week or 75 min of vigorous intensity aerobic PA or an equivalent combination of the two for health benefits [3].

Although estimates vary, research suggests 70–80% of US adults do not meet the PAG, and thus are considered insufficiently active [1]. However, PA levels may differ between civilians and active-duty servicemembers (SM). Among civilian men, adherence to PAG decreases across the lifespan—41.3% of those ages 18–34 years meet guidelines, falling to 29.4% for ages 35–49 years, 21.6% for ages 50–64 years, and 15.3% for those 65 years or older [4]. Only 28.7% of young adult females (18–34 years old) meet PAG, decreasing to 22.7% of those aged 35–49, to 17.6% of those aged 50–64, and 10.8% of those aged 65 and over [4]. Research supports that PA tends to be lower among civilians with overweight (OW; body mass index (BMI) of 25–29.9 kg/m2) or obesity (OB; BMI \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\ge\:$$\end{document} 30 kg/m2) compared to healthy weight adults [5], where civilians with OW/OB had 0.70–0.82 lower odds of meeting PAG compared to normal weight adults [6]. Additionally, PA levels may differ between male and female civilians, with higher percentages of females deemed insufficiently active (69% of male and 78% of female adults in the US are insufficiently active) [7]. Little is known about differences in PA between male and female SM. To our knowledge, there have been no studies examining whether SM with OW/OB meet PAG, and if there are sex differences in meeting PAG among active-duty SM.

Physical activity and body composition (e.g., BMI) are two critical considerations in military service enlistment and retention. Normal weight active-duty SM have been shown to meet PAG, particularly showing SMs engage in more PA than civilians, with one study indicating 82% of Army soldiers meet PAG [8]. Another study of Army soldiers noted 76% reported sufficient engagement in moderate or vigorous PA to meet PAG, and 88% passed the Army Physical Fitness Test [9]. However, it is unclear whether active-duty SMs with OW/OB meet PAG. As of 2020, only 2 in 5 young adults are weight-eligible (BMI ranging from 19.0 to 27.5 kg/m2) while only 3 in 4 young adults report enough physical activity levels to prepare them for military service [10]. Among enlisted SMs, approximately 19% experience OB [11], with estimates of OW/OB as high as 67.3% [12]. One study found differences in behavioral predictors of meeting PAG for a large sample of US Air Force personnel, where OW/OB was the strongest negative predictor for males and low aerobic exercise frequency was the strongest negative predictor for females [13]. According to the studies mentioned above, rates of engagement in PA appear to be significantly higher and the prevalence of OW/OB slightly lower among active-duty military personnel compared to civilian US adults.

Physical inactivity can have severe consequences as SM who do not meet the physical fitness and body composition standards of the US military may be discharged [14]. Furthermore, physical fitness is crucial for SM, as it promotes the maintenance of a strong military force, ensures the ability of SMs to perform assigned duties at optimal proficiency, and fosters good physical and mental health [15]. Physical fitness and good health promote coping with the physical and mental challenges associated with military service [16]. On the other hand, poor physical fitness and OW/OB negatively impact physical performance and military readiness, are associated with long-term health problems, and convey heightened risk for all-cause mortality [11].

Physical activity is a multidimensional construct, and single measures fail to capture all aspects of PA [17]. Self-report measures of PA can be used to assess activity type and time spent in various activities but are subject to recall and social desirability bias [18]. For this reason, there is growing use of wearable activity sensors, such as accelerometers, that continuously record objective activity counts to supplement information about PA collected via self-report [19]. An understanding of PA levels in SM with OW/OB using standardized measures is needed to characterize this population and develop tailored interventions for this underserved group. Thus, the current study aimed to (1) determine if male and female US active-duty military SM met recommended PAG of ≥ 150 min of moderate to vigorous (MVPA) minutes per week through both self-report and accelerometer-derived PA, (2) determine whether there are sex differences in PA levels of military SM, and (3) explore associations between self-reported PA, accelerometer-derived PA, BMI, and body fat percentage (BF%) among SM with OW/OB.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Participant data for the current study came from (i.e., pre-intervention) self-report and objective (accelerometer-derived) assessments of PA from a parent study assessing a behavioral weight loss program called ShipShape [20]. The original study enrolled 178 active-duty US military SM with OW/OB and/or who were at risk or who had failed a Navy conducted physical assessment between December 2017 and January 2020 [20]. This study was in-person and transitioned to online during COVID [21]. Eligibility criteria included being a US military active-duty SM, between the ages of 18–69 years old, free of physical limitations that would prevent participation in physical fitness activities, and not pregnant or planning to become pregnant in the next 6 months [20].

The protocol for this study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of VA San Diego Healthcare System, Navy Medicine Readiness and Training Command, San Diego, the Research and Development Committee of VA San Diego Healthcare System. All participants provided HIPAA authorization and informed consent either in writing or by telephone using an institutional review board-approved script.

Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics

Participants self-reported sociodemographic and service history information including age, sex (male/female), race and ethnicity, relationship status, income, living situation (e.g., on-base, off-base), number of children, pay grade, occupation/duty assignment (e.g., technician, corpsman), work status (e.g., full duty, limited duty, medical board), and current military rank (e.g., enlisted (E) or officer (O); lower numbers reflect lower rank).

Body mass index

Weight was measured (to the nearest 0.1 kg) using a stadiometer (Health o meter, Professional Model #500KL) for in-person participants and virtual participants used a study-provided calibrated digital scale (Withings Body Smart Weight & BMI Digital Scale, Issy-les-Moulineaux, France). Height was measured (to the nearest 0.1 cm) using the stadiometer at measured in person or self-reported for virtual participants. Weight and height were used to calculate BMI as weight in kg divided by height in m2.

Body fat percentage (BF%)

Body fat was estimated through measurements of neck and waist circumference (to the nearest hundredth) for males, and neck, waist, and hip circumference for females. BF% was calculated with a health and fitness Navy Physical Fitness Assessment (PFA) app (Vandersoft, Navy PFA). During COVID, participants were mailed measuring tapes and instructed to take self-measurements of neck and waist circumference for males, and neck, waist, and hip circumference for females [21].

Physical activity

The self-report International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) short-form was used to assess PA minutes for the past 7 days [22]. IPAQ outcomes include minutes per week of moderate to vigorous intensity physical activity (MVPA), walking, and sitting. The number of minutes per day was truncated at 180 before computing weekly minutes based on the IPAQ truncation protocol [23, 24]. Based on IPAQ scores, participants were grouped into 3 separate categories, health enhancing physical activity (HEPA) (i.e., 3 or more days of vigorous-intensity activity and accumulating at least 1500 Metabolic Equivalent Time (MET)-minutes; or 7 or more days of any combination of walking, moderate-intensity or vigorous intensity activities achieving a minimum of at least 3000 MET-minutes per week), minimally active (i.e., 3 or more days of vigorous activity of at least 20 min per day; or 5 or more days of moderate intensity activity or walking of at least 30 min per day; or 5 or more days of any combination of walking, moderate-intensity or vigorous intensity), and inactive (do not meet the criteria of minimally or HEPA active), which is based on the IPAQ scoring protocol [25].

For objective measures of PA, participants were instructed to wear the ActiGraph GT9X Link device (ActiGraph, Pensacola, FL, USA) for 24-hours/day for 7 days on their non-dominant wrist, except during water-based activities (e.g., showering or swimming) for a baseline assessment. The ActiGraph is a tri-axial accelerometer that has consistently been shown to be both a valid and reliable measure of PA duration, frequency, and intensity [26, 27]. Accelerometer-derived PA was scored into two PA metrics: (1) counts per minute (CPM) as cumulative activity “counts” (ActiGraph’s proprietary metric) per day to derive the average amount of total “movement” throughout the day (i.e., higher counts indicating more activity throughout the day), and (2) via the R-package GGIR to derive inactive time, light activity, MVPA, and time spent in bed [28, 29] using the Hildebrand cut points [30, 31]. Sedentary time during the day was calculated from inactive time minus time spent in bed. Valid wear time was based on previous research such that participants who wore the accelerometer at least 16 h per day for at least 3 weekdays and 1 weekend day were included in the final sample [29, 32]. Accelerometer-derived PA data was processed from a standard waking to waking time where each day is defined as the time the participant wakes up one day to the time they wake up the next day [33]. All categories of PA and sedentary time are reported in average daily duration of minutes spent in each activity.

Statistical analysis

Means and standard deviations of sociodemographic characteristics, IPAQ, and accelerometer-derived PA were examined for the full sample and for males and females separately. T-tests determined whether there were statistically significant differences between male and female participants on self-report IPAQ and accelerometer-derived PA. Effect sizes for the t-tests were calculate using Cohen’s d (0.2 = small effect; 0.5 = moderate effect; 0.8 = large effect). Average IPAQ and accelerometer-derived PA were described by the 3 IPAQ categories: inactive, minimally active/meets PAG, and HEPA/exceeds PAG. Pearson’s correlations were calculated between the IPAQ, accelerometer- derived PA, BMI, and BF%. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were considered strong (r between 0.7 and 1 or -0.7 and − 1), moderate (r between 0.3 and 0.69 or -0.3 and − 0.69), or weak (r between 0.29 and 0 or -0.29 and 0) [34]. Analyses were conducted in R Studio [35] using R version RStudio 27 (https://www.r-project.org/) and IBM SPSS (Version 31.0) [36].

Results

Of the 178 participants in the parent study, 113 (US Navy = 111; US Marines = 2; Mean age = 30.4 ± 6.7; 58% female; 64% White; 28% Hispanic) participants had valid accelerometer data during the baseline assessment (shown in Table 1). Participants’ mean BMI was in the obesity range (Mean BMI = 33.3 ± 4 kg/m2) and both males and females had a BF% above standard recommendation (Mean BF% for males = 28 ± 5.6; females = 40.3 ± 5.8). Most participants were married (73%) and resided in off-base housing (76%), and less than half had one or more children (43%). The majority of the participants’ paygrade was E3-E6, with an average of 8 ± 6 years in the military, and 73% were full duty.

Discussion

Almost all active-duty US military SM with OW/OB in this study met or exceeded the recommended PAG of 150 min of weekly MVPA. According to accelerometer-derived MVPA, 100% of service members met the PAG with a daily average of 93 min of MVPA. According to IPAQ categorization derived MVPA, 90% of service members met the PAG with a daily average of 91 min of MVPA. A 2005–2006 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) study that examined accelerometer-derived PA across BMI categories showed that approximately 3.2% of their sample of US civilians met the PAG, and less than 3% with OW/OB met the PAG guidelines [37]. Another accelerometer-derived PA surveillance study showed that adults with OW/OB engaged in significantly less daily PA (i.e., OW = 327.5 CPM; OB = 283 CPM) compared to normal weight participants (360 CPM) [38]. Our results are contrary to previous studies among non-active duty SM [37, 38] likely due to the differences in populations studied. Additionally, we used wrist-worn accelerometers while the other studies [37, 38] used hip-worn accelerometers and processed with different cut-points which could also explain differences. Our study shows that active-duty SM with OW/OB are much more physically active than adult civilians with OW/OB. This could be due in part to the PA associated with daily jobs and activities of SM, and/or the PA-supportive military culture.

Except for accelerometer-derived MVPA, we did not find any sex differences on any of the IPAQ or accelerometer-derived PA metrics. On average, active-duty US Navy male SM engaged in approximately 13 more daily minutes of accelerometer-derived MVPA compared to their female counterparts. Although the difference in daily MVPA is small, the cumulative amount totals at least 50 additional minutes of MVPA per week. This could partially be explained by job duties, as female SM may hold less PA intense jobs compared to male SM. A 2016 report showed that 25% of enlisted military women worked in administration, 15% worked in health care, and less than 5% worked in an occupational specialty like infantry, gun crews, or seamanship specialists [39]. In contrast, more than 20% of enlisted military men worked in electrical and mechanical equipment repair and more than 15% worked in an occupational specialty like infantry, gun crews, or seamanship specialists, which tend to involve more PA.

Overall, self-reported PA and accelerometer-derived PA were correlated; however, the effect sizes were small, suggesting some discrepancies in individuals’ report of PA engagement and accelerometry. The discrepancy may also reflect that accelerometry is most accurate for walking and running and may not capture some forms of strength training or body weight exercises self-reported on the IPAQ. The significant associations between self-report IPAQ and accelerometer-derived MVPA and walking/light PA are consistent with studies of civilians [40, 41]. We did not find an association between IPAQ sitting and accelerometer-derived sedentary time, which has also been found among civilians [40, 42, 43], perhaps indicating SM are more aware of their engagement in PA as opposed to their lack of PA. Given the mixed findings, more research is needed among the military population to make any recommendations for replacing accelerometry derived PA with the IPAQ.

Lastly, accelerometry revealed participants with higher BMI spent more time sedentary than those with lower BMI. This finding aligns with previous studies among civilians linking sedentary behavior with OW/OB [38]. Unexpectedly, BF% was inversely correlated with IPAQ sitting time, although the effect size was small. This contradicts previous studies that have found individuals with lower BF% tend to sit less than those with higher BF% [44]; however, Liao and colleagues (2024) found this association was strongest among those who did not meet PAG. Perhaps in our sample of active-duty SM with OW/OB, almost all of whom met PAG, sitting time did not have the same effect. It also may be that participants’ self-reported IPAQ sitting time grossly underestimated their actual sitting time as seen in the difference between accelerometer-derived sedentary time and IPAQ sitting minutes, and the association we observed between IPAQ sitting time and BF% is a result of self-reporting bias [45].

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to examine levels of PA from both self-report and accelerometry among male and female active-duty SM with OW/OB to understand the unique strengths and needs of this population in terms of weight management. Representation of female SM is another strength of the study as male SM tend to be overrepresented in studies of military personnel. This study also has limitations, including the short length of accelerometry assessment and suboptimal adherence with the accelerometry wear protocol that resulted in a reduction of sample size from 178 to 113. Some studies also indicate reactivity bias with accelerometry and suggest omitting the first week of data [46], which was not possible in the current study as only one week of accelerometry data was collected at baseline. In addition, wrist-worn accelerometers may overestimate PA compared to traditional hip-worn accelerometers [47] and may account for some of the discrepancy between accelerometer-derived PA among people with OW/OB in our study and prior studies [37, 38]. Other studies suggest bias in self-reporting PA [45]. Thus, it is possible some of our results are affected by these biases, and future studies should include more days of accelerometer wear to test for reactivity bias and incentivize wear to improve adherence.

Conclusion

Nearly all or all SM in the current study met PAG via self-report and accelerometry, respectively. Active-duty US Navy male SM engaged in approximately 13 more daily minutes of accelerometer-derived MVPA compared to their female counterparts. There were no other sex differences on any of the IPAQ or accelerometer-derived PA. Physical activity engagement is a strength of this population that can be leveraged in future interventions. However, intervening on PA alone may not contribute to changes in body composition (e.g., BF% or BMI) required to meet US military weight requirements. Future research should examine whether increased focus on nutrition, stress management, sleep, and mental health concerns may be more helpful for promoting changes in body composition.
