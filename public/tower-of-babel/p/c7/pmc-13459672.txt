Sociodemographic differences in glaucoma awareness and knowledge among the general population of Nepal: A population-based study
Authors: Ranjan Shah, Anil Paudel, Sailesh Kumar Mishra, Prakriti Sharma, Pathiyil Ravi Shankar, Nisha Jha, Sabina Shrestha, Reena Yadav, Chet Raj Pant, Bharat Bahadur Chand, Anil Gorkhaly, Daya Shankar Chaudhary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlaucoma contributes significantly to vision impairment worldwide and ranks as Nepal’s third most preventable cause. Early detection of glaucoma remains crucial to halting its progression and preventing vision loss. This study assessed awareness of glaucoma among Nepalese adults.Design and MethodsThe population-based cross-sectional survey was conducted between May 2017 and March 2018 using multistage stratified random sampling. A total of 5,000 household heads from 100 clusters across 15 randomly selected districts in Nepal were interviewed. The operational definition of ‘awareness’ included having heard of glaucoma, while having ‘knowledge’ comprised understanding its causes, symptoms, and treatment. Descriptive and bivariable analysis using chi-square test were used to assess associations.ResultsAmong the 4,993 respondents, 3904 (78.2%) had heard of at least one eye disease, but only 738 (18.9%) were aware of glaucoma, highlighting a substantial knowledge gap. Among those aware, 67.6% did not know its cause, and 48.4% were unaware of treatment options. Difference in glaucoma awareness was observed across sociodemographic characteristics such as ethnicity, religion, education, and occupation (p < 0.05). Disadvantaged Janajatis (27.0%) and individuals with secondary or higher education (27.9%) had higher awareness, whereas Dalits (6.1%), housewives (9.6%), and wage laborers (9.9%) had the lowest.ConclusionsTo minimize blindness caused by glaucoma in Nepal, targeted, community-based education should focus on high-risk groups such as Dalits, housewives, and daily wage laborers. Integrating glaucoma awareness and screening within primary healthcare systems, using local media, and engaging community health workers can enhance outreach and reduce cultural barriers.

Introduction

Glaucoma is the second most significant contributor to blindness, accounting for 15% of all cases of blindness globally. 1 An estimated 64.3 million people worldwide have glaucoma, with a disproportionate impact on individuals living in Asia.2,3 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), 3.4% of individuals aged 50 years and older in Southeast Asia are blind. 4 Across South Central Asia, the reported incidence of glaucoma is 3.5%. 5 Overall, the incidence of glaucoma in Asia ranges between 2.1% and 5.0%.5,6 By 2040, the worldwide total number of glaucoma patients is expected to reach 111.8 million, with Asia and Africa experiencing the greatest increases in prevalence. 3

Despite substantial progress in Nepal’s eye health system over the past three decades leading to self-reliance in eye care services,7–10 the overall decline in the prevalence of blindness has been modest, from 0.84% in 1981 11 to 0.35% in 2012 7 and further to 0.28% in 2021. 10 Glaucoma has consistently remained the third most common cause of preventable vision loss in Nepal, accounting for approximately 3.2% of cases in 1981, 5.9% in 2012, and 5.8% in 2021.7–11 A recent population-based study conducted in Nepal indicated that the overall prevalence of glaucoma among people over 40 years old was 1.80%. 12

Research from India revealed that awareness and knowledge of common eye conditions, including glaucoma, are very poor. 13 A prompt diagnosis of glaucoma is vital for preventing disease progression and avoiding vision impairment.14,15 Limited awareness of glaucoma is a key factor contributing to its late diagnosis,16,17 which significantly increases the risk of irreversible blindness. 18 A study from Eastern Nepal reported that only 5.5% of eye care patients exhibited some knowledge and understanding of glaucoma. 19

Most of the existing research in Nepal has been carried out in hospital-based settings.20,21 Nepal’s eye health system focuses primarily on treatment-oriented services rather than promotive and preventive. Despite the increasing burden of glaucoma, awareness and understanding of the disease remain limited, with no data available on knowledge among the general population. Therefore, the present study aimed to describe awareness and knowledge of glaucoma and examine their distribution by sociodemographic characteristics among Nepalese adults.

Design and Methods

Study design and setting

This was a population-based cross-sectional survey conducted between May 2017 and March 2018. The study was part of a broader national knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) survey related to eye health services, 22 from which glaucoma-specific data were extracted for analysis.

Sample size and sampling techniques

This study employed multistage stratified sampling. During data collection, Nepal’s former administrative division into five development regions (prior to the shift to a provincial structure) was used as the first level of stratification. At the second level, districts were stratified by ecological zones—Mountain, Hill, and Terai—and one district from each ecological zone within each development region was randomly selected, resulting in three districts per region and a total of 15 districts. At the third level, wards within village development committees (VDCs), the smallest administrative units at the time, were used as strata. A total of 100 wards were selected proportionally, with greater representation allocated to districts with a larger number of wards. Wards were randomly selected based on this proportional allocation.

In the third stage, clusters were defined within each selected ward, with each tole (a neighborhood or small settlement unit) considered as a cluster. In instances where a selected cluster was too large, sketch mapping and segmentation were conducted to divide it into smaller, manageable units. Conversely, if a selected cluster was too small to meet the required sample size, adjacent clusters were included.

Within each selected cluster, 50 households were chosen using systematic random sampling, resulting in a total sample size of 5,000 households. The starting point for household selection in each cluster was determined by identifying a prominent local landmark and selecting the first household in the northward direction from that point. The multistage sampling ensured representation across ecological and administrative regions of Nepal.

Study variables

The study included variables related to glaucoma awareness (having heard of glaucoma), knowledge (understanding of causes, symptoms, and treatment options), and sociodemographic characteristics such as age, ethnicity, religion, education level, and occupation. Ethnicity categories followed the national sociocultural classification commonly used in Nepal’s health information management system and public health research to reflect social stratification relevant to health inequities. These variables were selected based on their relevance to health literacy and access to health information and were predefined in the parent KAP survey protocol.

Validity and reliability

The face and content validity of the semi-structured questionnaire were ensured through review by subject matter experts. The tool was pretested in non-selected study sites among 10% of the total sample to refine its clarity and applicability. Internal consistency was assessed via Cronbach’s alpha, with a threshold of ≥0.80 indicating good reliability.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data was collected through face‒to-face interviews with household heads or household members aged 18 years and above (if head of household were not available during visit) via a structured approach. The questionnaire captured sociodemographic characteristics, as well as awareness and knowledge related to the causes and treatment of glaucoma. Daily supervision was conducted to ensure data completeness and quality. Any inconsistencies or missing responses were addressed by revisiting respondents the following day.

The data were entered via Epi Info software and subsequently extracted to excel for cleaning and later imported into STATA 13 for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies (n) and proportions (%)) were used to summarize the awareness and knowledge level regarding glaucoma, its causes, and treatment. Cross-tabulation was performed to examine the distribution of glaucoma awareness across sociodemographic characteristics. Furthermore, chi-square test was applied to examine difference in awareness of glaucoma across sociodemographic characteristics. Missing data were minimal (<1%) and handled using complete case analysis. Sampling weights were not applied in this secondary analysis because proportional allocation across ecological zones and districts was maintained during sampling.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethical Review Board of the Nepal Health Research Council (Ref No. 112/2017). Permission to conduct the survey was also granted by the Department of Health Services, Ministry of Health and Population, Government of Nepal. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection after explaining the study objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality of respondents’ information was strictly maintained through anonymized data handling and secure data storage. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents

Out of 5,000 respondents, complete data from 4,993 were analyzed. The median age of the respondents was 43 years with interquartile range 17 years (ranging from 18–87 years). Over half (51.6%) of the respondents were aged between 18 and 44 years. The majority belonged to the upper caste (40.1%), followed by disadvantaged Janajatis (23.9%). Hinduism was the predominant religion (82.7%), followed by Buddhism (9.5%). In terms of education, 61.0% had no formal education, whereas only 13.1% had completed ten years of schooling or higher. Awareness and knowledge of glaucoma

Among the 4,993 respondents, 3,904 (78.2%) reported having heard of at least one eye disease. Among these 3904 respondents, 738 (18.9%) had specifically heard of glaucoma and were subsequently asked follow-up questions regarding their knowledge of its causes and treatment (Figure 1), (Figure 2).Figure 1.Sample flowchart showing participant eligibility across study phases.Figure 2.Awareness of glaucoma (n=3904).

Among the 738 respondents who had heard about glaucoma, the majority (67.6%) did not know anycause of glaucoma. The remaining 32.4% (n=239) identified at least one cause. Only a small proportion identified causes such as old age (19.1%), genetics (12.3%), delayed cataract treatment (7.3%), unhygienic behaviors (1.8%), and supernatural beliefs. Other causes included eye/face injuries and prolonged use of steroids (Figure 3). Among the same 738 respondents who had heard of glaucoma, 48.4% (n=357) were unaware of any treatment options. Among the 51.6% (n=381) who identified at least one treatment option, 38.2% mentioned eye drops or ointments, whereas the others cited cleanliness (19.2%), hygienic practices (7.9%), and traditional healers (0.4%). Additionally, 4.3% believed that there is no available treatment for glaucoma. Glaucoma awareness according to sociodemographic characteristics

There was no statistically significant difference in the distribution of glaucoma awareness among the different age groups (p=0.526), with similar proportions observed among young (19.3%), middle-aged (17.9%), and elderly individuals (20.0%). However, significant differences were observed across other sociodemographic characteristics. In terms of ethnicity, disadvantaged Janajatis had the highest level of awareness (27.0%), whereas Dalits reported the lowest level (6.1%) (p < 0.001). Similarly, religion categories showed statistically significant difference glaucoma awareness (p<0.001); Buddhists (39.9%) and Christians (34.0%) demonstrated greater awareness than Hindus (16.1%) and Muslims (10.3%). Educational status also showed statistical significance with awareness (p < 0.001); individuals with secondary education or higher had greater awareness (27.9%) than those with no formal education (16.8%). Discussion

In the present study, only 18.9% of participants reported being aware of glaucoma, indicating a substantial gap in population-level awareness. Population-based studies conducted in Nepal, including the Bhaktapur Glaucoma Study and the Jiri Eye Study, have reported glaucoma prevalence estimates of 1.6% and 4.2%, respectively, with more than 90% of affected individuals being unaware of their condition at the time of diagnosis. 23 In this context, the low level of awareness observed in our study appears consistent with the high proportion of undiagnosed cases reported in these studies, suggesting a persistent gap between disease burden and public knowledge.

There are different caste groups in Nepal, with Brahmins and Chettris being at the top of the hierarchy and Dalits being at the bottom. There are also different ethnic groups in Nepal outside the formal caste system, termed ‘Janajatis’. A significant proportion of respondents who were aware of glaucoma had a limited understanding of its causes (67.6%) and available treatment options (48.4%). Comparably low levels of awareness have also been documented in previous studies conducted in Nepal.12,19,24 A low level of knowledge restricts proper treatment of the condition. This may indicate inadequacies in both the comprehensiveness and effectiveness of public health communication. The extremely low levels of knowledge among those with no or lower levels of education and among members of marginalized ethnic communities (e.g., Dalits and disadvantaged non-dalit terai caste) are indicative of inequities associated with access to education, health services, and culturally appropriate health information.

The major findings of this study are aligned with several previous studies conducted both within Nepal and internationally. For example, KAP with respect to eye health services in Nepal reported that a considerable proportion of the population demonstrated familiarity with eye health issues (approximately 78%), whereas knowledge and understanding of conditions such as glaucoma were mostly inadequate and varied by education level and region. 22 Basic awareness does not always mean full knowledge about the disease. Similarly, a hospital-based study carried out in central Nepal revealed that only 21% of people visiting tertiary-level care centers had heard of glaucoma, and awareness was significantly associated with education and occupation. 24 These patterns are consistent with our findings but may not be directly comparable due to methodological differences and setting based differences. Internationally, a study from Kunming, China, on primary glaucoma patients revealed that even among diagnosed people, misunderstandings and limited knowledge about the causes and treatment of glaucoma were evident. 25 Additionally, research focusing on the hilly regions of Nepal has reported limited awareness and knowledge of eye diseases, particularly in rural areas where many individuals rely on traditional healers or other sources for medical care. 26 Previous research from the hilly region of Nepal yielded similar results, where 17.4% of respondents were aware of glaucoma. 26 Even fewer people were aware of glaucoma in two other studies from eastern Nepal and Bhaktapur, which reported awareness levels of only 5.5% 19 and 2.4%, respectively. 12

The findings of the study further demonstrated that education level, occupation and ethnicity were significantly associated with knowledge of glaucoma. Individuals with higher education attainment and people engaged in service-related occupations were likely to have better knowledge about glaucoma. This could reflect better access to health information, routine eye check-ups and exposure to health education. Similarly, a study conducted in tertiary hospitals in Nepal revealed that literate patients had significantly greater awareness levels of glaucoma than illiterate patients did. 24 These findings reinforce the importance of addressing sociodemographic characteristics such as education, occupation and ethnic disparities when designing glaucoma awareness programs.

Limitations

This study presents important insights into glaucoma knowledge among the general population in Nepal; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. The survey assessed general awareness and basic knowledge of glaucoma but did not incorporate a detailed, validated knowledge score or explore understanding of different types of glaucoma, risk factors, or progression patterns. As a result, the depth and accuracy of respondents’ knowledge could not be fully evaluated. The reliance on self-reported data may have introduced recall and social desirability biases. Additionally, the questionnaire was administered only to household heads, potentially overlooking intra-household variations in awareness, especially among youth and women not designated as household heads. The use of bivariable analysis, including cross-tabulation and chi-square tests, does not account for potential confounding factors, and therefore the observed differences across sociodemographic characteristics should be interpreted as descriptive rather than independent associations. However, these patterns provide a useful basis for generating hypotheses for future research. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable baseline evidence to inform targeted public health interventions and supports the need for more comprehensive and longitudinal studies on glaucoma awareness in Nepal.

Conclusion

This population-based study found that approximately 1 in 5 respondents (18.9%) had heard of glaucoma among those aware of at least one eye disease in Nepal. Among those aware of glaucoma, more than two-thirds (67.6%) could not identify any cause, and nearly half (48.4%) were unaware of any treatment options.. Awareness of glaucoma was significantly differedby sociodemographic factors, with the lowest levels observed among marginalized groups such as Dalits, housewives, and wage laborers.

To reduce the burden of glaucoma-related vision loss, targeted health education initiatives must be prioritized within national eye care programs. Community-based interventions should focus on high-risk populations and utilize locally appropriate communication tools, including radio, television, and interpersonal channels. Integrating glaucoma education and screening into rural primary health care services and engaging community health workers can further strengthen outreach and improve early detection.

Overall, these findings highlight the urgent need for sustained, inclusive, and culturally sensitive public health strategies to improve glaucoma awareness in Nepal. Future research should explore the barriers to knowledge dissemination and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions to ensure equitable access to eye health information and services across all population groups.
