Development and validation of the clinical educator self-regulated learning scale (CTSRS): insights from faculty development
Authors: Szu-Han Wang, Yin-Yu Chen, Ming-Ju Hsieh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective faculty development is crucial for improving clinical teaching methods, especially in the context of educational changes and public health crises. Many current programs do not have personalized assessment instruments, which diminishes their potential impact. This research employed Zimmerman’s self-regulated learning (SRL) model to develop a tool that evaluates clinical educators’ capacity to adjust and regulate their teaching approaches. The aim of this research was to develop and validate a Clinical Educator Self-Regulation Scale (CTSRS) designed for clinical faculty, with the goal of identifying and addressing deficiencies in clinical teaching approaches through customized faculty training initiatives.MethodsThe development of the CTSRS involved multiple stages, starting with the review of an established Chinese teachers’ SRL scale, alongside narrative interviews with 52 clinical faculty members. This process resulted in 78 preliminary items, which were then refined by a panel of experts to produce 35 items. Pilot testing involving 25 experienced clinical educators led to additional adjustments and culminated in a final set of 35 items. The validation study included 378 clinical educators from five medical institutions and utilized confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to validate the structure of the scale. Item retention was based on standardized loadings ≥ 0.50 and theoretical relevance.ResultsConfirmatory factor analysis indicated that the scale had an optimal eight-factor structure, including past teaching experiences, intrinsic interest and engagement, self-adjustment during the teaching process, teaching opportunities, resistance to change in teaching methods, peer comparison and self-evaluation, help-seeking and peer collaboration, and application of learning from faculty development. The model demonstrated good fit (χ² = 1116; p < .05; CFI = 0.90; TLI = 0.89; NFI = 0.86; RMSEA = 0.07) and strong convergent validity (all items had standardized factor loadings above 0.60 and average variance extracted (AVE) above 0.50). The final scale model consists of 30 items.ConclusionsThe CTSRS offers a reliable instrument to identify and improve self-regulatory teaching skills in faculty development programs. Its psychometric characteristics suggest potential for broader implementation, with the prospect of meaningful enhancements in clinical education practices. Subsequent research will focus on cross-cultural validation, linguistic adaptation, and the design of interventions informed by the scale to support improvements in clinical teaching.Clinical trial numberN/A. Background

The importance of faculty development in medical education has significantly grown due to rapid advancements in healthcare, changing educational approaches, and the evolving roles of clinical educators [1, 2]. Today’s medical education is constantly evolving with new technologies, medical knowledge, and teaching methods emerging [2–4]. Educators must stay updated and adaptable to effectively prepare the future generation of healthcare professionals. The COVID-19 pandemic has further highlighted the necessity for resilient and flexible teaching approaches that can address unforeseen challenges [5, 6]. Faculty development programs empower educators to adapt to these changes by advocating for creative teaching approaches and strengthening their capacity to assist students in challenging clinical scenarios [7, 8]. To address these requirements, faculty development programs have transformed into comprehensive endeavours that provide a range of educational options, including workshops, classes, peer mentoring, and conferences [2, 9]. These training efforts extends beyond enhancing teaching techniques; they encompass comprehensive endeavors throughout institutions aimed at elevating clinical instruction professionalism while also enhancing infrastructure and advancing educational capabilities [7, 8, 10]. This holistic strategy for faculty development is crucial as it enhances not only the teaching standards but also cultivates a supportive learning atmosphere for both educators and students [11]. By aligning clinical educators with contemporary educational goals, these initiatives strive to elevate the level of medical education.

While many studies highlight the potential of these programs to improve teaching practices [12–15], research suggests that actual behavioural changes following faculty development interventions are often limited or absent [9, 16, 17]. Clinical educators often struggle to apply their learning from faculty development programs in practical settings, indicating a mismatch between theoretical knowledge and the implementation of enhanced teaching practices [18–20]. Various factors contribute to this disparity, such as program design, relevance of training to daily activities, and institutional culture. Faculty development research often focuses on the self-reported reactions, attitudes, knowledge, and skills of participants. However, this emphasis may overlook tangible changes in behaviours or within faculty systems [21, 22]. Self-reported outcomes are commonly emphasized in studies evaluating faculty development programs, particularly in terms of participant satisfaction and perceived knowledge gains [9, 23]. However, evidence of behavioural change based on external observation or peer reports is less frequently reported. Although 65% included self-reported behavioural change, only 35% had other-reported behavioural change measurements [9]. One of the important missions of faculty development programmes is to drive change; however, despite participants’ satisfaction, peer buy-in is rarely recognised [24]. Faculty development often fails to establish “a direct link to educators’ on-going educational activities” [25]. Unless participants of a faculty development programme can see the relevance and can put their learning into practice, no behavioural change will occur.

This implementation gap may be further exacerbated by the unpredictable and variable nature of clinical teaching environments. In contrast to students in school setting where schedule is organised around fixed classes timetable, clinical teaching is far more unpredictable and varied from setting to setting. Adaptive clinical educators can apply self-regulation skills to boost students’ learning enhancing their teaching outcomes [26]. Contemporary learning theories consider that learners architect knowledge actively in the learning processes, and the way learners to construct knowledge and the used strategies is context related [27]. Self-regulation learning emphasises not only the agent of individual in the process, but also how the social context influences the process [27].

Since faculty development is part of continuous education, social-cognitive model of self-regulation is a useful framework in our consideration of examining how individuals translate their learning into practice. Self-regulated learning (SRL) has been classically defined as:“… self-generated thoughts, feelings and actions that are planned and cyclically adapted to the attainment of personal goals [28]”.

According to Zimmerman [28], self-regulation can be divided into three phases: forethought phase, performance phase and self-reflection phase. Forethought phase is setting a scene for action; performance phase arises during monitoring efforts and control attention and action. The last phase, self-reflection, refers to how a person responds to the experiences during performance phase [28]. Self-regulation involves actively constructing knowledge, setting goals, and adapting behaviour based on feedback [29].

Despite the promise of self-regulation frameworks, translating educational inputs into meaningful behavioural change remains a persistent challenge. This may stem from misaligned training content, variable institutional support, or the inherent unpredictability of clinical education settings [9, 26]. While this study is grounded in Zimmerman’s individual-cantered model of SRL, the complex nature of clinical education also invites consideration of socially shared regulation and evolving professional identity. Socially shared regulation of learning highlights the co-construction of goals, monitoring, and strategies within group contexts, which is particularly salient in team-based clinical teaching environments [27, 29]. Moreover, professional identity formation—a central developmental process in clinical educators—can influence and be influenced by self-regulatory practices [30]. Faculty development may serve as a crucible for identity shifts, and tools like the CTSRS may help elucidate how reflective and collaborative practices shape these transformations. Integrating such frameworks in future research may offer a richer understanding of regulation dynamics beyond the individual level. To bridge these challenges, educators must be capable of setting instructional goals, applying strategies flexibly, and reflecting on their impact—core competencies of SRL [28]. However, there is currently a lack of tools specifically designed to assess clinical educators’ self-regulation abilities. While self-regulation has been widely studied among students and pre-service teachers, few instruments have been tailored to clinical educators or validated in clinical teaching contexts [31, 32]. Developing and validating such an instrument can significantly improve the design and impact of faculty development programs by identifying strengths and areas for improvement in self-regulation among educators. This study aims to develop a scale that accurately measures self-regulation skills among clinical educators, with the objective of informing faculty development programs to enhance the effectiveness of clinical teaching.

Methods

Study design and setting

A mixed-method, cross-sectional design was employed to develop and validate a scale aimed at evaluating clinical educators’ SRL in the context of teaching. The CTSRS was designed to assess how clinical educators regulate their teaching behaviors, particularly after participating in faculty development programs. The scale development followed a sequential exploratory approach, integrating qualitative interviews to generate items and quantitative validation using factor analysis to confirm the structure. This methodology aligns with best-practice recommendations for educational measurement development, particularly in healthcare setting. The study took place at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital in Taiwan, an 11,225-bed medical centre with seven branches across the country. Ethical approval was obtained from the Chang Gung Medical Foundation Institutional Review Board (IRB: 104-9774B), and all procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

We recruited participants from various medical disciplines, including medical doctors, nurses, and other healthcare professionals. Eligible participants were clinical educators who had completed a structured three-month faculty development program offered at one of four Chang Gung Memorial Hospital branches (Linkou, Keelung, Chiayi, or Kaohsiung). Informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to data collection, and participation was entirely voluntary.

Procedure

The development of the CTSRS incorporated both qualitative and quantitative methods. Following the guidelines for rigorous instrument development in educational research outlined by Artino Jr., La Rochelle, and colleagues [33], the CTSRS was developed and validated in two phases. Phase One involved item generation through in-depth interviews with clinical educators, followed by iterative expert review to refine the item pool. These interviews were guided by Zimmerman’s three-phase model of SRL and aimed to explore educators’ real-world challenges and strategies in regulating their teaching. Phase Two focused on testing the scale’s psychometric properties, including construct validity and reliability, using confirmatory factor analysis with a larger sample.

Phase one: development of the scale

The scale development began with a series of narrative interviews involving 52 clinical educators. Participants had a mean age of 42.6 years and an average of 11.3 years of teaching experience. The sample included 28.8% male (n = 15) and 71.2% female (n = 37). Professional roles were distributed across physicians (n = 18, 34.6%), nurses (n = 20, 38.5%), and other health professionals—including dietitians, radiologic technologists, and pharmacists (n = 14, 26.9%). The interviews centered around their experiences during faculty development and the application of new knowledge and skills in clinical teaching. Additionally, the interviews explored factors that either facilitated or hindered the implementation of training outcomes within their professional practice as trainers, trainees, or training managers. These interviews aimed to gain detailed insights into the self-regulatory practices of clinical educators, their teaching experiences, challenges, and strategies. The interviews were semi-structured and theory-informed, using Zimmerman’s three-phase model of SRL to guide data collection. We created a unique interview specifically for this study, which has not been published previously. An English version of the interview question is available in Supplementary 1. Open-ended questions were used to facilitate in-depth discussions and allow participants to share personal stories reflecting aspects of SRL in clinical settings.

Following these interviews, preliminary findings were reviewed by an expert panel composed of six experienced academics and practitioners in medical education. The panel carefully assessed the content, provided crucial feedback, and suggested modifications to ensure that the scale items accurately reflected the core dimensions of SRL as identified during the interviews. This panel also helped ensure theoretical coherence with Zimmerman’s framework, linking emerging codes to self-monitoring, goal-setting, self-evaluation, and adaptive functioning. This iterative process between narrative collection and expert evaluation played a vital role in refining the scale into a robust tool tailored for assessing self-regulation among clinical educators.

Drawing on Zimmerman’s model of self-regulation and insights from semi-structured interviews, the initial version of the CTSRS was crafted, comprising 78 items distributed across eleven factors. This first iteration of the CTSRS was administered to a purposive sample of 25 clinical educators to identify and refine questions that lacked clarity or relevance. Participants had a mean age of 43.1 years and an average of 12.7 years of teaching experience. Of the sample, 44.0% were male (n = 11) and 56.0% were female (n = 14). Professional roles included physicians (n = 9, 36.0%), nurses (n = 10, 40.0%), and other health professionals such as family physicians, radiologists, and respiratory therapists (n = 6, 24.0%). The criteria for item refinement were meticulously established to ensure the scale’s relevance and precision. Initially, the focus was on clarity and specificity; any item prone to misinterpretation or ambiguity was either modified or omitted. Each item was evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from one (strongly disagree) to five (strongly agree).

Redundancy was rigorously addressed; overlapping content led to the consolidation of items, retaining only those that were most comprehensive and representative. Detailed discussions among experts determined which items most clearly and directly measured the intended constructs.

In addition, practical examples drawn from the narrative interviews were used to confirm the contextual appropriateness of items, ensuring ecological validity and relevance to real-world clinical teaching challenges. This iterative process of evaluation and revision effectively reduced the number of items from 78 to 35, enhancing both the scientific rigor and applied utility of the scale.

Phase two: validation of the CTSRS scale

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was employed to validate the hypothesized structure of the CTSRS, which was developed through a theory-informed, mixed-method approach. CFA was selected over exploratory factor analysis (EFA) because the item structure was guided by Zimmerman’s cyclical model of SRL and supported by empirical data from narrative interviews with clinical educators. This a priori conceptualization justified the use of CFA to test the model’s construct validity rather than explore latent structures [34, 35]. Each factor was designed to reflect a specific domain of self-regulation in clinical teaching. For instance, “Self-adjustment during the teaching process” and “Peer comparison and self-evaluation” map onto Zimmerman’s performance and self-reflection phases. The integration of theoretical constructs and qualitative insights ensured that the model was both educationally meaningful and contextually grounded. AMOS version 28.0 was used to conduct CFA [36]. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: Chi-square (χ²), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), following accepted thresholds (e.g., CFI > 0.90, RMSEA < 0.08) [37]. Standardized factor loadings were examined for statistical significance, and items with loadings below 0.50 were removed [38]. Prior to CFA, content validity was established through expert panel review. Six medical educators reviewed the draft items for clarity, relevance, and alignment with SRL theory. Although formal computation of content validity indices (CVI) was not performed, items were retained only after consensus approval through iterative refinement. This decision was based on the iterative nature of item refinement, where expert consensus was prioritized through multiple structured feedback rounds rather than a single quantitative CVI rating. The use of consensus panels in medical education instrument development is a recognized alternative for content validation when guided by strong theoretical alignment and context-specific expertise [33].

A total of 378 clinical educators from five academic medical institutions in Taiwan were recruited using purposive sampling. Inclusion criteria required active involvement in clinical teaching and recent completion of a faculty development program. The mean age was 41.3 years, with an average of 12.1 years of teaching experience. The sample included 21.2% male (n = 80) and 78.8% female (n = 298). Professional roles included physicians (n = 107, 28.3%), nurses (n = 139, 36.8%), and other specialists such as those in pediatrics and surgery (n = 132, 34.9%). We acknowledge the non-random nature of the sample and the potential for selection bias, particularly the possibility that participants with higher motivation for faculty development may have been more likely to participate. Additionally, institutional culture may have influenced participant responses. These limitations suggest caution in generalizing results to other populations or systems without further validation.

After the first CFA, six items (Items 3, 8, 17, 18, 19, and 26) were removed due to standardized loadings below the 0.50 threshold. These items were also reviewed for conceptual redundancy and weak interpretability. A second CFA was performed on the revised 30-item scale, which retained the original eight-factor structure. Each factor consisted of 2 to 9 items, with final factor loadings ranging from 0.60 to 0.95, indicating strong internal consistency and construct alignment.

The final model yielded acceptable fit indices: χ2[377] = 1116.17, p <.05), and the value for the CFI (0.90), TLI (0.89), NFI (0.86), and RMSEA (0.07). Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the eight factors ranged from 0.73 to 0.92, indicating good internal reliability. Figure 1 presents the standardized path diagram. Discussion

The main aim of this research was to create and validate the CTSRS using CFA to confirm its reliability and structural validity among clinical educators. The results strongly support that the CTSRS consists of an eight-factor structure, aligning well with established theoretical frameworks of SRL, particularly Zimmerman’s model. This study prioritized theory-driven confirmatory analysis rather than data-driven exploration, as the hypothesized model was based on a priori constructs. The statistical assessments carried out through CFA demonstrated acceptable model fit, as indicated by various key indices including significant Chi-square statistics test. The CFI, TLI, and NFI all exceeded the acceptable threshold of 0.80, suggesting a good fit of the model to the data. The RMSEA fell below 0.08, further supporting the adequacy of the model. These indices collectively suggest that the hypothesized factor structure is a valid representation of the underlying constructs of self-regulation among clinical educators. The included items demonstrated strong factor loadings, with values ranging from 0.60 to 0.95, indicating their significant impact on the defined factors. Moreover, the moderate to high inter-factor correlations provided evidence of both independent and interconnected dimensions within the scale, thereby supporting its theoretical foundation. These results confirm that the CTSRS is a valid and reliable tool for evaluating self-regulation skills among clinical educators. Its validation contributes not only to educational assessment development but also offers practical relevance for faculty development planning.

To contextualize the CTSRS findings within the broader landscape of existing literature on SRL in clinical education settings, it is vital to explore how these findings align with and expand upon previous research. The validation of the CTSRS through CFA not only underscores the multidimensional nature of self-regulation in clinical teaching but also integrates and builds upon conceptual frameworks established by earlier studies. Zimmerman conceptualizes SRL as a cyclical, tri-phasic process involving goal setting (forethought), monitoring and strategic implementation (performance), and self-reflection. The various dimensions captured by the CTSRS reflect this cyclical process while providing a practical tool that encompasses theoretical constructs such as goal orientation, strategic planning, and reflective adaptation in teaching practices. This linkage not only validates the scale’s theoretical underpinnings but also exemplifies how abstract SRL concepts can be operationalized in clinical teaching environments.

Extant research in clinical education often highlights the importance of educators having strong self-regulation skills to effectively manage the dynamic and frequently unpredictable nature of clinical environment [31, 39–41]. Studies have demonstrated that educators with higher levels of self-regulation can better adapt their teaching strategies to meet diverse student needs, leading to improved learning outcomes [26, 32, 41]. The CTSRS offers empirical evidence supporting these findings by providing a validated measure to assess these crucial skills among clinical educators. Additionally, this scale addresses a significant gap identified in prior research—the absence of specific, validated instruments for measuring SRL in clinical educators, which has historically hindered the systematic study and enhancement of SRL skills in this group. Previous scales, such as the one developed by Capa-Aydin, Sungur, and Uzuntiryaki [31], focused on pre-service teachers, rather than on experienced clinicians in the workplace setting. Their model, while also grounded in Zimmerman’s theory, assessed nine SRL-related factors including Goal Setting, Intrinsic Interest, Performance Goal Orientation, Mastery Goal Orientation, Self-Instruction, Emotional Control, Self-Evaluation, Self-Reaction, and Help-Seeking. They underscore that external influences can be more potent than individual ones in certain contexts during the process of self-regulation.

While both the CTSRS and the Capa-Aydin model aim to assess aspects of SRL, differences in structure and population underscore the contextual tailoring each tool. Our scale’s factors, such as “Self-Adjustment during the Teaching Process” and “Intrinsic Interest and Engagement,” closely coincide with Capa-Aydin et al.‘s Self-Instruction and Intrinsic Interest factors. However, our scale does not explicitly differentiate Goal Orientation into Mastery and Performance as in their model, suggesting a potential area for refinement. Furthermore, our factor “Help-Seeking and Peer Collaboration” corresponds with their Help-Seeking factor, highlighting shared emphasis on collaboration. We have incorporated “Impact of Previous Teaching Experiences” as a separate element, emphasizing the influence of past teaching contexts and mentors on present practices—an area not specifically addressed in their model. Conversely, the Capa-Aydin model includes “Emotional Control,” which our scale omits, reflecting a possible gap in addressing affective self-regulation in our instrument. These distinctions suggest that each scale offers unique strengths suited for different educational contexts: our scale may be more applicable to adaptive teaching strategies in post-training clinical practice, while theirs may suit emotionally responsive pre-service environments.

In practical terms, the results from the CTSRS CFA carry significant implications for clinical practice and educational policy. By offering a reliable tool for assessing self-regulation, educational leaders can more accurately identify areas where educators may benefit from targeted professional development. Personalized learning plans and mentorship models could be structured around the eight SRL domains assessed by the CTSRS. Furthermore, literature suggests that improving educators’ self-regulation skills can result in more effective and adaptable pedagogical approaches, ultimately enhancing patient care outcomes through better-trained practitioners. Thus, the scale not only contributes to theoretical and empirical discussions but also serves as a catalyst for practical improvements in medical education.

While the development of the CTSRS specifically for clinical educators marks a significant advancement in targeted clinical educational assessments, several limitations must be addressed. Primarily, the cultural specificity of the scale may restrict its applicability and relevance outside of the Taiwan and Asia context. Cultural differences in educational practices and perceptions of self-regulation could limit the generalizability of the findings to educators in other regions. This necessitates further studies to explore the scale’s cross-cultural reliability and validity. Additionally, the scale, being developed in Chinese, raises challenges related to linguistic validation. If there are intentions to use the scale internationally, it will require rigorous translation processes to ensure that the translated versions retain the original psychometric properties, a process that is complex and may introduce inconsistencies. Moreover, adapting the scale for use in different cultural settings would require not only translation but also careful consideration of cultural nuances that may influence how self-regulation is understood and practiced in different educational environments. Furthermore, this focus on a specific national context may restrict the use of the scale in global or multicultural educational studies without extensive adaptation and validation. Such limitations highlight the need for additional research to modify and validate the scale for broader applications, ensuring its utility in international research collaborations and educational programs that involve diverse educator populations.

Recognizing these limitations is crucial for understanding the scope and application of the CTSRS within and beyond the Taiwan context. Future research should prioritize criterion-related validation, longitudinal stability, and predictive value of CTSRS scores in diverse settings. The lack of significant behavioural changes following traditional faculty development programs, as highlighted in previous studies, indicates the necessity for more personalized and impactful training interventions. It is important to recognize that educators may require a variety of support systems to address their specific needs. The CTSRS offers a reliable tool to identify specific areas where educators excel or need improvement in self-regulation, thus enabling more targeted and effective faculty development programs. By addressing these identified areas, educators can better translate theoretical knowledge into practical teaching improvements.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated the CTSRS, a theory-driven instrument designed to assess self-regulatory processes among clinical educators following faculty development. The results of the confirmatory factor analysis support a robust eight-factor structure grounded in Zimmerman’s cyclical model of SRL. The final scale demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties, including strong internal consistency, good model fit, and conceptual coherence. These findings indicate that the CTSRS is a valid and contextually grounded instrument for assessing clinical educators’ self-regulation within healthcare education settings. The development of the CTSRS addresses a significant gap in the literature by offering a context-specific measure tailored to practicing clinical educators rather than pre-service teachers or generic educational populations. By capturing multiple dimensions of self-regulated teaching behavior—including forethought, performance monitoring, and self-reflection—the CTSRS offers educators, administrators, and researchers a valuable tool for identifying strengths and areas for growth in instructional practices. The scale’s alignment with both theory and lived teaching experiences enhances its relevance to faculty development evaluation, program design, and individualized educator support. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The sample was drawn exclusively from Taiwanese medical institutions, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Cultural and contextual factors, as well as the original language of the instrument, may influence the interpretation and applicability of the scale in other settings. Future research should focus on cross-cultural adaptation, criterion validity, and longitudinal assessment to confirm the CTSRS’s utility in diverse educational systems. A formal cross-cultural validation process would include: [1] forward and backward translation of the instrument using standardized linguistic validation procedures [2], cognitive interviews with target users in the new setting to evaluate semantic equivalence, and [3] pilot testing followed by multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (MG-CFA) to assess measurement invariance across cultural or language groups. In sum, the CTSRS offers a novel and psychometrically sound tool for understanding and enhancing self-regulated teaching practices among clinical educators. Its application can inform faculty development programs, promote reflective teaching, and ultimately contribute to improved learner outcomes in medical education. By providing a structured way to assess and support educators’ self-regulation, the CTSRS contributes meaningfully to both educational theory and the advancement of clinical teaching practice.
