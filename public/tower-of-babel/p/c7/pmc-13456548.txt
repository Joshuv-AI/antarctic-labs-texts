App Versus Logbook for Weight Monitoring and Help-Seeking Behavior in Heart Failure: A Single-Group Quasi-Experimental Study: A Single-Group Quasi-Experimental Study
Authors: Norma Caples, Frances O’Brien, Mary Mooney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Weight monitoring as part of heart failure (HF) self-care enables timely intervention to prevent hospitalizations. Apps with prompts may improve adherence to monitoring and earlier help-seeking behavior compared with paper logbooks.Objective:This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the Fluid Heart Tracker App compared with a paper logbook for weight monitoring in patients with HF, focusing on monitoring adherence, help-seeking behavior, and hospitalization.Methods:A quasi-experimental, multicenter study was conducted in 2 consecutive 12-month phases, guided by the Situation-Specific Theory of HF Self-Care. A logbook was used initially (phase 1), followed by the Fluid Heart Tracker App (phase 2). Of 184 participants, 136 completed both phases. Analyses compared adherence, help-seeking behavior, and hospitalization outcomes between both phases.Results:App adherence was higher (median: 346.5 days, interquartile range [IQR] = 299–360) than logbook use (median: 315 days, IQR = 36–365; P < .001). Help-seeking behavior increased from 43.4% in phase 1 to 73.5% in phase 2 (P = .031), while the longest delay reduced from 14 to 2 days (P < .001). Males were more likely to initiate app use (P = .046); age was not a barrier (P = .668). Hospitalization rates according to app use frequency did not differ (P = .384). Declines were observed in the number of participants hospitalized (P = .0066) and the number of episodes (P = .0035).Conclusions:These results support integrating simple, alert-enabled apps such as the Fluid Heart Tracker into HF programs to strengthen self-care and reduce preventable admissions.

Heart failure (HF) is a chronic syndrome associated with high morbidity and mortality, particularly in adults over 60 years of age.1,2 Sudden weight gain due to fluid retention is a key indicator of worsening HF. Current guidelines recommend daily weight monitoring as a critical self-care strategy to support early detection and timely medical consultation.3,4 Recognizing significant weight gain and seeking timely medical advice can slow HF progression and improve quality of life.3,5,6 Conversely, delays in seeking medical consultation are associated with poorer outcomes, increased hospital admissions, and higher mortality risk.7,8 An increase of 2 kg over 3 to 7 days is typically considered clinically significant.3,9

Traditional weight monitoring in HF relies on paper logbooks, which have limitations, particularly for patients with numeracy and cognitive impairments that are prevalent in this population.10–14 In this context, numeracy difficulties relate to calculating and interpreting weight changes over several days, as the onus is on the patient to recognize when a clinically significant weight increase has occurred. Effective self-care requires patients to recognize, interpret, and respond to symptom changes, as outlined in the Situation-Specific Theory of Heart Failure Self-Care, which highlights that effective self-care requires 3 interrelated processes: self-care maintenance, symptom perception, and self-care management.15 The theory also emphasizes that even when weight monitoring is part of routine HF self-care, the decision to take action is shaped by naturalistic decision-making, whereby patients draw on prior experience, knowledge, skills, and personal values, including whether they believe self-care is manageable and likely to prevent deterioration. Many patients find symptom perception challenging, which can lead to delays of up to 7 days before seeking medical consultation and is associated with poorer outcomes,16,17 while delays of over 5 days are associated with a 1.93-fold higher risk of cardiac events.18

Digital health solutions, including mobile apps, offer new opportunities to improve self-monitoring and enhance patient engagement.19 First developed in 2012 for HF,20 these apps are widely accepted for use, including among older adults.21–24 However, clinicians and patients have highlighted the need for a simple, user-friendly HF app.21–27 A recent narrative review noted that while app-based interventions show promise, most include multiple features beyond weight monitoring, and evidence specific to alert-enabled weight tracking remains limited.28

The Fluid Heart Tracker App was designed to simplify daily weight monitoring and provide an immediate alert when clinically significant weight gain occurs. No previous study has directly compared app-based weight monitoring with paper logbooks using objective patient actions. The results of this study provide real-world evidence on whether a simple alert-enabled app can improve adherence to weight monitoring, support timely help-seeking, and reduce preventable hospitalizations in people with HF.

In this study, our aim was to evaluate the effectiveness of the Fluid Heart Tracker App compared with a paper logbook for weight monitoring in patients with HF, focusing on adherence, help-seeking behavior, and hospitalization outcomes. A secondary aim was to examine the influence of sex, age, and educational level on app engagement.

Methods

The Fluid Heart Tracker App

The Fluid Heart Tracker App was developed by a small open-source volunteer team (Open Source Volunteers Extended, OSV-X) consisting of a software engineer, a graphic designer, and a cardiac nurse who supported project coordination. The first author (HF nurse specialist) conceptualized the app based on clinical needs identified in HF practice, supported by guideline-recommended daily weight monitoring and evidence from prior research.28 Development was completed within a few weeks using a simple closed-loop design to minimize technical complexity and reduce the risk of software bugs. Manual weight entry was retained to mirror the paper logbook process and to prioritize a simple, low-cost design suitable for widespread use. No participants reported difficulty using the app during either the pilot testing or the 12-month study period.

Its primary function is to support and simplify daily weight monitoring. Users manually enter their weight in kilograms, pounds, or stone, and entries can be made at any point within a 24-hour period; however, weights cannot be entered retrospectively to ensure real-time accuracy and reduce the risk of backfilled values. Users can review previous entries using the on-screen navigation buttons (Figure 1).

Fluid Heart Tracker App Interface Illustrating the Alert System. The left panel shows a stable weight prompt, and the right panel shows a red alert prompt triggered by significant weight gain, prompting medical consultation.

The app automatically calculates rolling 7-day weight trends. When a weight gain of ≥2 kg within 7 days is detected, an alert appears on the user’s screen prompting them to contact a healthcare provider, consistent with European and American guidelines for HF self-care.3,4 The red alert reinforces the importance of timely help-seeking for worsening HF symptoms.29–32 If no significant weight change occurs, no alert is generated.

The app was intentionally designed to support patient autonomy in self-care rather than to function as a telemonitoring system. Data are stored locally on the user’s device, reflecting patient and clinician feedback, the need to minimize complexity, and the intention to avoid additional clinical infrastructure or data-governance requirements. During phase 2 of the study, patients were asked to engage with the app for daily weight monitoring and to continue using it for the 12-month period.

Study Design and Participants

This multicenter study was conducted across 12 tertiary Irish hospitals with established HF services led by specialist nurses. Although geographically diverse to ensure national representation, all sites adhered to the same national HF care guidelines, thereby supporting consistency in routine care and data collection. This quasi-experimental study used a single-group, pre–post design, comparing the same cohort of patients over 2 consecutive 12-month periods (phase 1: logbook phase; phase 2: App phase) of weight monitoring.

Eligible patients with HF were identified by the HF nurses at each participating site during routine clinic visits. Patients were provided with verbal and written study information, and written informed consent was obtained before enrollment. A designated HF nurse at each site supported recruitment, confirmed eligibility, and assisted participants with app installation and orientation. The HF nurses were part of the existing clinical HF teams and received standardized written instructions and a verbal briefing from the first author to ensure consistency across sites; monthly follow-up checks after study commencement identified no procedural concerns.

Pilot Study

Before the implementation of the app, usability testing was undertaken with 31 patients who confirmed that the app was simple, intuitive, and user-friendly. Based on feedback, minor refinements were made before implementation. These included simplifying the introductory screen and setting the daily weight-entry screen as the default landing page to streamline use. All participants indicated they would recommend it to others.33

Current Study

The app could be downloaded to Android and Apple smartphones. Patients used it independently for daily weight monitoring at home. Eligible patients with HF who agreed to participate provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Patients were recruited to the study at the start of phase 2, and consent was obtained to collect their data from the logbook they used in the previous 12 months and to use the app for weight monitoring for the next 12 months. A designated HF nurse–assisted patients at each site in downloading the Fluid Heart Tracker App to their smartphones at the start of phase 2. They received verbal instructions about the app, together with the app information leaflet (Supplemental Digital Content 1, Appendix 1, https://links.lww.com/JCN/A398).

As weight recording in a logbook was part of standard care, retrospective collection reflected real-world practice, avoided unnecessary participant burden, and complied with ethical approval conditions.

The inclusion criteria of the study were as follows: attending a participating HF clinic, over 18 years of age, meeting New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class II or III, and having ownership or access to a smartphone, which provided a practical safeguard against possible significant visual, dexterity, or cognitive limitations that could affect app use. Exclusion criteria included NYHA class I or IV, inability or unwillingness to use the app, or receiving palliative care. The HF nurse managed recruitment during routine clinic visits. Participants served as their own controls to reduce bias from nonrandomization. National ethical approval to conduct the study was granted in 2020 (20-NREC-COV-105). Approval covered both phases of the study.

Data Collection

Participant recruitment began on March 21, 2021, and concluded on November 2, 2022. Before the study began, the Director of Nursing at each research site was approached to obtain permission for the local HF nurses to undertake the study responsibilities, including identifying eligible patients, obtaining consent, and supporting data collection. Each HF nurse received a pack containing essential documents, including patient information leaflets, consent forms, data collection forms with accompanying detailed guidance, and the app user guide leaflet. One of the researchers (N.C.) conducted training sessions with the HF nurses to maximize reliability and cross-site consistency, regarding their roles, responsibilities, and the study documentation.

Data were collected over 2 consecutive 12-month periods, corresponding to the study’s phase 1 and phase 2. At each site, the HF nurse was responsible for retrospectively collecting phase 1 data following patient recruitment. During the logbook phase, daily weight monitoring and recording were part of routine, nurse-led HF care. At each clinic visit, the HF nurse reviewed the logbook, reinforced the importance of daily weighing, and encouraged accurate self-recording. This ongoing review and reinforcement formed part of standard care and was intended to support genuine, contemporaneous weight logging rather than retrospective reconstruction. For phase 2, the HF nurse collected prospective app data, with data extracted at 3-month intervals across the 12-month study period to align with routine HF clinic review cycles.

The key variables that were collected included the following: (1) adherence to weight monitoring, measured as the number of days weight was recorded in the logbook during phase 1 and in the App during phase 2; (2) help-seeking behavior, defined as the number of days taken to seek medical consultation following significant weight gain, derived from the logbook during phase 1 and from the App alert during phase 2. Emergency department short-stay (≤23 hours) observation episodes were classified as help-seeking behavior contacts rather than hospital admission; and (3) hospitalization, which included both the number of admissions and the length of stay.

The HF nurses recorded help-seeking behavior based on patient self-reports and health record reviews, documenting the time between weight gain and medical consultation in both phases. Each HF nurse electronically submitted pseudonymized site data to the researcher, who merged, cleaned, and checked all files for completeness before analysis.

Sample Size Calculation

Statistical advice was sought to estimate power calculations and to ensure the study was adequately powered. Given the absence of prior research comparing the use of a logbook to an app, pilot data from the first 20 participants in this study were used. Based on these pilot data, with an assumed small-to-moderate effect size of 0.30 and a desired power level of 80%, a minimum sample size of 90 to 110 participants was determined to be necessary to detect statistically significant effects.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized participant demographics and baseline characteristics. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare adherence between the logbook and App phases. Pearson’s chi-square test was applied to assess associations between categorical variables (e.g., sex and education) and uptake of the app. Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare continuous or ordinal variables (e.g., age, left ventricular ejection fraction, and NYHA class) between the groups. Kruskal–Wallis tests examined associations between app engagement and clinical status (NYHA class and the number of hospitalizations). Logistic regression was used to further investigate the effect of sex on initial app uptake.

To assess whether demographic or clinical characteristics influenced the depth of engagement (total days used), multiple linear regression analyses were conducted separately for phase 1 (logbook use) and phase 2 (app use), adjusting for sex, age, and NYHA class. Adjusted analyses, therefore, accounted for these key demographic and clinical covariates, addressing potential confounding effects.

Each participant was the unit of analysis and served as their own comparison. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York),34 with statistical significance set a priori at P < .05. Only participants with complete data for both phases 1 and 2 were included in the final analyses (n = 136). No prespecified causal pathways were tested; analyses were exploratory, focusing on differences between the outcomes across phases.

Results

Sample Demographics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were recorded at the start of phase 2. Of the 392 participants screened, 208 were excluded due to a lack of smartphone access, leaving 184 eligible participants. All 184 received the logbook intervention in phase 1 and initiated the app intervention in phase 2.

During phase 2, 48 participants did not complete follow-up due to death (n = 11), illness or withdrawal before starting (n = 28), loss to follow-up (n = 4), or HF nurse withdrawal, meaning that 1 site discontinued participation when the designated HF nurse left her post and no replacement was available to continue study procedures (n = 5). The remaining 136 participants completed both study phases and were included in the final analyses (Figure 2).

Participants engaged with the app significantly more often than with the logbook (P < .001). Median days of use and IQRs for both phases are presented in Table 2. Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to examine whether changes in adherence between intervals were associated with changes in NYHA class or the number of hospitalizations in each phase. No significant associations were found (NYHA: P = .658; hospitalizations: P = .279), indicating that variation in adherence was not explained by clinical status alone.

Impact of Sex, Educational Level, and Age on Logbook or App Use

A Pearson’s chi-square test examining initial app uptake showed a significant association between sex and app use (P = .046). Simple logistic regression showed that females had 54.4% lower odds of using the app than the logbook, indicating that males were more likely to use the app than females. Additionally, educational level did not significantly influence engagement with the logbook (P = .540) or the app (P = .650).

To examine whether demographic or clinical characteristics influenced the depth of engagement with each monitoring method, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted, including sex, age, and NYHA class for both the logbook and the app. During the App phase, sex (P = .49), age (P = .80), and NYHA class (P = .59) were not significantly associated with the total number of days the app was used. In contrast, during the logbook phase, older age was significantly associated with greater engagement, with older participants recording more logbook days than younger participants (β = 2.35 days/year; P = .044), while sex (P = .48) and NYHA class (P = .72) were not significant predictors.

Impact of Logbook and App Use on Help-Seeking Behavior

Help-seeking behavior improved markedly during the App phase. Although the number of significant weight-gain episodes was similar across phases (phase 1: 213; phase 2: 215), a substantially higher proportion of these episodes resulted in medical consultation during phase 2 (73.5% vs. 43.4%; P = .031). The same-day help-seeking also increased sharply (86.1% vs. 28.6%; P < .001). The longest delay in seeking care decreased from 14 days in the logbook phase to 2 days in the App phase, and the median delay was reduced from 2 days (IQR = 2.0–4.25) to 1 day (IQR = 1.0–1.0), indicating more timely help-seeking during app use (P < .001, Mann–Whitney U test). All values are summarized in Table 2.

Hospitalization Rates

Hospitalization variables were nonnormally distributed (Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk, both P < .001); therefore, nonparametric tests were used. The frequency of app use was not associated with hospitalization outcomes (Kruskal–Wallis, P = .384).

Across phases, hospitalization rates decreased substantially. The number of participants hospitalized declined from 24 in phase 1 to 10 in phase 2 (McNemar’s test, P = .0066), and total HF-related admissions fell from 39 to 16 (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = .0035). Median length of stay increased slightly from 5 days (IQR = 4.0–8.25) to 7 days (IQR = 4.0–14.0), although this difference was not statistically significant (P = .683). Discussion

In this quasi-experimental study evaluating app-based versus logbook-based weight monitoring, adherence to daily weighing was higher among the app. However, engagement with the app declined notably in the final 3 months of the 12-month period. Help-seeking behavior improved substantially during the app phase, with more patients seeking medical review, and doing so more rapidly following significant weight gain. While app-use frequency itself was not associated with hospitalization outcomes, the overall number of patients hospitalized and the total number of admissions were lower during the App phase. However, the length of stay did not differ significantly between the phases. Sex differences were observed in initial app uptake, with men more likely than women to begin using the app; however, once adopted, sustained engagement was comparable between sexes. Age and educational level did not influence engagement with either the logbook or the App. These results indicate that, among patients who own a smartphone, the app supported more consistent monitoring and timelier symptom–response behavior than the traditional logbook. This summary of results is elaborated upon below in relation to adherence, help-seeking behavior, demographic influences, and hospitalization outcomes.

Adherence and Engagement

Previous studies evaluating app engagement included additional app features such as education, alongside weight monitoring.35,36 Compared with other studies, use of the Fluid Heart Tracker App was high, with engagement rates of 98.5% at 90 days (interval 1) and 97.8% at 180 days (interval 2). In contrast, studies assessing apps with multiple functions rather than a single weight-monitoring feature have shown lower engagement rates: for example, a mean engagement rate of 78% over 30 days35 and a median rate of 85% (IQR = 71%–93%) over 6 months.36 The simplicity of the Fluid Heart Tracker App may have contributed to the high adherence rate to its use, aligning with prior research showing that perceived ease of use enhances self-monitoring.37

Engagement with our app declined over time, with only 30.1% of participants recording weight daily by the final interval. This pattern is consistent with the broader challenge of sustaining engagement in chronic disease self-care.38 Features such as reminders21 or gamification35 have been previously used to promote app engagement, but further studies of longer duration are needed to assess their impact. Although patients generally engage more effectively with digital tools that provide clear feedback, such as alerts, daily monitoring may feel less necessary or more burdensome when they perceive themselves as clinically stable.39,40 This study did not include qualitative data to explore reasons for disengagement, so it remains unclear whether reduced use reflected digital fatigue, a perceived reduction in need, or other contextual factors. Importantly, no technical issues were reported during the study period. Patterns of strong early uptake followed by later attrition are consistent with what has been described in the literature as a “novelty effect,” where initial enthusiasm for a new digital tool diminishes over time.41,42 A decline in motivation or usability barriers is also a key factor in reduced use over time.43 These data underscore the importance of user-centered design, frequent feedback, and reinforcement. Despite our efforts with simplicity, pilot testing, alerts, and regular clinic review, the marked adherence drop in interval 4 suggests that even well-designed interventions may be vulnerable over time. Future interventions should integrate user feedback and include periodic clinical engagement to sustain motivation.39,44

Help-Seeking Behavior

The app appeared to support improved help-seeking behavior, with a substantial increase in the same-day medical consultations during the App phase, likely prompted by its alert feature. The longest delay in seeking care was 2 days. While we did not directly assess reasons for this delay, it may plausibly reflect weekend access barriers, when Irish hospital HF services are closed and only on-call general practitioners are available. This interpretation is supported by prior research highlighting that delays before hospital admission, including clinic or primary care contacts, are often overlooked in studies of HF care.45 Future research should explore the underlying reasons why some patients delay seeking care despite recognizing significant weight gain, as understanding these behavioral and contextual factors may inform targeted interventions to further strengthen HF self-care.

Sex and Age

Sex differences in engagement were notable: males were significantly more likely to initiate app use than females. However, once women began using the app, their level of engagement, measured by total days of use, was similar to that of men, indicating that the sex difference relates to adoption rather than sustained use. The overrepresentation of male participants in the sample may limit the generalizability of these results, particularly regarding female engagement, and should be addressed in future studies through more balanced recruitment.

By contrast, age did not influence engagement, suggesting that older adults were receptive to using the app. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that older patients with HF can successfully adopt digital health tools when usability is prioritized.46,47

Hospitalization Patterns and Contributing Factors

Improvements in help-seeking behavior were accompanied by encouraging trends in hospital outcomes. While hospitalization rates based on app use frequency were not significantly different, fewer participants were hospitalized, and there were fewer total admissions during the App phase. The median length of stay increased compared to the logbook phase, which may reflect a shift in case mix, whereby patients with milder decompensation were managed in the community, leaving only those with more severe presentations to be admitted, a trend that has been associated with longer hospital stays in HF populations.48,49 In this context, the improved help-seeking seen during the App phase may have enabled earlier intervention for less unwell patients, reducing their need for admission and disproportionately leaving sicker patients in the hospitalized group. This interpretation is speculative and should be explored in future studies.

In addition to this case-mix shift, several nonintervention factors may also have contributed to differences in hospitalization outcomes. Continuity of care with the same HF nurse, greater confidence in HF self-management over time, and routine service access patterns (such as reduced service availability at weekends) may all have influenced when and how patients sought medical attention. These influences are consistent with the natural progression of HF self-care behaviors in long-term, nurse-led programs.

Given the single-group, pre–post design, other factors unrelated to the intervention, such as changes in patients’ health status over time or increasing familiarity with managing their HF, may also have contributed to differences between the logbook and App periods. The logbook period represents a single 12-month retrospective summary derived from contemporaneously completed logbooks as part of routine HF care, whereas the app period reflects a subsequent 12-month prospective period. Because repeated within-phase measurements were only available for the app period, segmented regression or time-series analyses across both phases were not feasible. The results should, therefore, be interpreted within the strengths and constraints of a pragmatic, real-world service evaluation rather than as definitive causal effects.

Although app-use frequency was not associated with hospitalization, help-seeking behavior improved markedly during the App phase. A greater proportion of patients sought medical consultation (73.5% vs. 43.4%), and the same-day help-seeking increased from 28.6% during the logbook phase to 86.1% with the app. Delays were almost eliminated, with a median delay of 1 day and a maximum of 2 days. This limited variability prevented formal mediation analysis, as a mediator with minimal variance cannot be modeled. These results suggest that the reduction in hospitalizations is more likely to reflect the earlier help-seeking prompted by the app, supported by timely clinical review within the HF service, rather than a dose–response relationship between App-use frequency and outcomes.

Appraising Design and Applicability

The pragmatic design, embedded in routine HF nurse-led care, further supports real-world applicability. Importantly, participants had already been attending the same HF nurse for 12 months before switching from the logbook to the app, which reduces the likelihood that behavioral changes were driven solely by the Hawthorne effect of research participation. Embedding interventions within nurse-led HF programs is known to strengthen adherence and improve outcomes.50,51 These factors support the interpretation that the improvements observed during the App phase reflect genuine enhancements in self-care practice.

In this study, more than half of screened patients (53%) did not own a smartphone, which is higher than national estimates.52 We did not collect age data for the 208 excluded patients; however, HF clinic populations typically include a high proportion of older adults, who have lower smartphone ownership than the general community. Among the 136 participants who completed both phases, 84 (61.8%) were aged ≥65 years, underscoring the older age profile of the cohort. These factors suggest that the findings are most applicable to the subgroup of patients with HF who have access to, and can use, a smartphone. As smartphone adoption continues to increase among older adults, the proportion of patients with HF able to engage with digital self-care tools is likely to expand.

Strengths and Limitations

Several methodological features strengthen this study. Phase 1 logbook data were collected as part of routine nurse-led HF care, where logbooks were routinely reviewed at clinic visits and the importance of daily weighing was reinforced. This ongoing clinical oversight supports the authenticity and contemporaneous nature of weight entries, providing an objective record of preintervention weight monitoring. phase 2 data were prospectively captured, allowing for a more complete ascertainment of outcomes, such as help-seeking behavior. Hospitalization outcomes were consistently obtained from hospital records in both phases, ensuring reliable measurement. Participants also served as their own controls, reducing variability related to individual characteristics such as digital literacy. Importantly, the nurse-led, real-world design reflects how digital tools can be integrated into existing HF management, enhancing the external validity of the results.

Nonetheless, limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of phase 1 data collection may have affected the completeness of data compared with the prospective design of phase 2, particularly for help-seeking behavior. Internal validity was also limited by the absence of randomization and potential selection bias, as only participants with complete logbook data who remained alive and engaged in care progressed to phase 2. Additionally, although a sex difference in App engagement was observed, the study did not collect data on potentially relevant explanatory factors such as comorbidities, employment status, or caregiving responsibilities. These unmeasured variables may have contributed to differences in app engagement. These factors may have influenced the results but also reflect the pragmatic design, which aimed to capture routine practice alongside structured, monitored app use.

Overall, the strengths of this study outweigh its limitations, and the results provide important insights into how simple, alert-enabled digital tools can be integrated into routine HF care to support patient self-management.

Conclusion

This quasi-experimental study compared daily weight monitoring using a paper logbook with the Fluid Heart Tracker App in patients with HF across 12 centers in Ireland. The results support the use of simple, alert-enabled apps to enhance HF self-care, consistent with the situation-specific theory of HF self-care. The app achieved significantly higher adherence to weight monitoring, timely medical consultation following weight gain, and fewer hospitalizations compared with the logbook. Although the median length of stay increased, this was not statistically significant, and overall hospital utilization declined.

Importantly, this study demonstrates that patients, including older adults, can successfully adopt digital apps when usability is prioritized, although sex differences highlight the need for strategies to ensure equitable engagement. The real-world, nurse-led design enhances the external validity of these results, showing that streamlined digital apps can be integrated into routine care.

In practice, incorporating straightforward apps such as the Fluid Heart Tracker into HF programs could strengthen patient engagement, support earlier intervention, and potentially reduce preventable admissions, ultimately lessening the healthcare burden. Future research should focus on sustaining long-term engagement, addressing sex-related digital barriers, and evaluating the cost-effectiveness of this approach to inform wider implementation.
