Brain Abscess Treatment Followed by Sinonasal Mucormycosis—Report of Three Cases
Authors: Mohammadreza Firouzifar, Seyed Hadi Samimi Ardestani, Shirin Irani, Maliheh Akbarpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Brain abscess following sinonasal mucormycosis is a rare and life-threatening complication. Since our knowledge is mostly based on case reports, a well-documented way of treatment for such cases is yet to be found. Case Description A retrospective case series study was conducted at a tertiary hospital. Patients whose brain abscesses were treated without opening the dura during surgery were selected for the study. The patients received radical debridement of necrotic tissues as well as the infected bones adjacent to the brain abscess. Then, they were treated medically. Three patients were included in the study. All patients had a history of diabetes and COVID-19 before developing symptoms of sinonasal infection. The average size of the abscesses was less than 2 cm. Conclusion Removing infected tissues and bones surrounding the brain abscess without opening the dura can be a viable treatment option for mucormycosis-induced brain abscesses of less than 2 cm.

Introduction

Brain abscess is a severe infection that can lead to life-threatening complications and even death, despite advancements in treatment. The paranasal sinuses can be the most common source of infection for brain abscess. 1 Meta-analysis has shown that a pathogen can be identified in 68% of brain abscess cases, and multiple bacteria are identified in 23% of positive cultures. 2 Various pathogens play a role in brain abscesses, the most common of which are Streptococci, Staphylococci, Anaerobes, and Enterobacteriaceae. Fungal pathogens are more common in people with an immune system deficiency. 3 The most commonly affected areas are the frontal, temporal, parietal, cerebellar, and occipital lobes. 4 Symptoms of brain abscess vary widely among patients, with the classic triad of headache, fever, and focal neurological deficit observed only in 20% of patients. Men are more likely to be affected by brain abscesses. 2 5 MRI with contrast is the preferred imaging method for accurate diagnosis and follow-up of brain abscesses. 5 Various treatment options exist for brain abscesses, including external surgical methods such as craniotomy to stereotactic aspiration. The endoscopic endonasal method is a newer and less complicated method used for skull base abscesses. 6 7 Finally, the patient’s condition, underlying diseases, the size and location of the brain abscess, and the surgeon’s expertise are all factors to consider when determining the best treatment approach. Although variable treatment options exist, there is no consensus on the treatment methods for mucormycosis-induced brain abscesses. In this paper, we want to propose a treatment paradigm for this problem.

Cases Presentation

Surgery Technique

All the patients were diagnosed with sinonasal mucormycosis and received surgical and medical treatment through functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS), endoscopic debridement, and antifungal treatment. On the postoperative MRI, a brain abscess was detected, and osteomyelitis or infected bone close to the abscess cavity was suspected. Another surgery was planned under general anesthesia. The patients were positioned in reverse Trendelenburg, and the mucosa was prepared using a cotton sponge soaked in 1% lidocaine and 1/1000 epinephrine. An intranasal evaluation was performed using either a 0- or 30-degree endoscope. The skull base was exposed, and any suspicious infected or sequestered bone was removed using a diamond burr and Kerrison rongeurs. The location of the brain abscess was determined through imaging before and during the surgery. The adjacent bone was removed without incising the dura. Finally, the nasal cavity and sinuses were rinsed with serum containing antibiotics, and Gelfoam containing clindamycin were inserted into the sinonasal cavity to promote hemostasis and mucosal healing.

Follow-up after Surgery

After surgery, patients received antibiotics and antifungals according to the consultation of the infectious disease specialist. Clinical symptoms were monitored, and postoperative imaging was requested after 2–4 weeks for further investigation.

Results

In the present case series, the decision to avoid intradural drainage was based on a combination of clinical, radiological, and metabolic factors rather than on a single criterion. A total of 12 patients with brain abscesses were initially examined in this study. However, nine patients were excluded due to drainage through endoscopic (eight patients) and craniotomy (one patient) approaches. The average age of the included patients was 41 years, and all patients had a history of COVID-19 before the onset of sinonasal symptoms. Discussion

Treating brain abscesses following sinonasal mucormycosis can be challenging, especially in patients with underlying conditions such as immunodeficiency. In this study, all patients had diabetes and were also treated with remdesivir and systemic corticosteroids for COVID-19, which can further weaken the immune system and increase susceptibility to sinonasal mucormycosis, as previous studies have shown. 8 9 Proper surgical debridement, antifungal treatment, and improving the immune status are crucial in the treatment of sinonasal mucormycosis. 9 10 In the present case series, the decision to avoid intradural drainage was based on a combination of clinical, radiological, and metabolic factors rather than a single criterion. All patients had small intracranial abscesses (<2 cm in maximal diameter), without evidence of mass effect, raised intracranial pressure, or progressive neurological deterioration. In addition, rapid correction of underlying immunosuppression, particularly optimization of glycemic control in patients with diabetes mellitus—was achieved early during treatment. Among the causes that lead to immunodeficiency, diabetes mellitus usually has a better prognosis as an underlying cause. In three patients in this study, brain abscesses were detected after initial surgical and medical treatment. Blood sugar control, according to the endocrinologist consultation, and intravenous amphotericin B treatment were continued for the patients after surgery. Another important consideration was the suspected presence of infected bone adjacent to the abscess cavity. We hypothesized that persistent infection within necrotic skull base bone might act as a continuous source of infection. Therefore, aggressive endoscopic removal of infected tissues and adjacent bone, without opening the dura, was performed to reduce the infections load while avoiding the risks associated with intradural surgery. This approach resulted in both clinical improvement and radiological regression of the abscesses on follow-up imaging. Similar successful results have been reported in a study by Kao et al., 10 which recommended medical treatment for brain abscesses smaller than 2 cm. It is important to note that in the previous studies, the focus was not on the mucormycosis-induced brain abscesses, as these abscesses are considered poor prognostic factors for the patients’ survival. Our institutional experience also includes patients with mucormycosis-related brain abscesses who required intradural drainage, which has been reported previously. 7 These patients typically presented with larger abscesses, neurological deterioration, or more extensive intracranial involvement. This contrast highlights that intradural drainage remains an essential component of management in select cases, while a conservative extradural approach may be sufficient in carefully chosen patients with small abscesses and controlled underlying immunosuppression. Although intradural drainage remains the standard of care in select patients with large abscesses, mass effect, or neurological deterioration, our findings suggest that a subset of patients with mucormycosis-related brain abscesses may be managed successfully without intradural intervention. However, due to the limited number of patients, a formal comparative statistical analysis was not feasible. The findings suggest that treating brain abscesses by removing infected tissue and bone adjacent to the abscess without opening the dura may be a viable treatment option. However, further research is needed to validate these findings and explore additional treatment options. A preprint of this work is available online. 11

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, small sample size, and lack of standardized cognitive and cost-related outcome measures. Consequently, parameters such as length of hospital stay and long-term neurocognitive outcomes could not be reliably compared between patients treated with and without intradural drainage.
