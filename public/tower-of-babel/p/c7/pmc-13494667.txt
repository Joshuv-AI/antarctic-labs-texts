Factors affecting representativeness in home‐based dementia activity interventions: a literature review
Authors: Jiska Cohen‐Mansfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost persons living with dementia (PLwD) reside in community settings, which has driven the development of a wide range of home‐based activity interventions. However, the external validity of these studies remains uncertain because the representativeness of study samples is rarely reported. Assessing representativeness is essential for estimating intervention reach and informing health equity and resource allocation decisions. This review evaluates the extent to which participants in home‐based activity trials reflect the population of community‐dwelling PLwD and identifies sources of selection bias.A targeted review was conducted using three complementary strategies: analyses of two prior systematic reviews, a PubMed search (2020 to 2025), and a review of relevant follow‐up publications. Inclusion was limited to peer‐reviewed studies that reported detailed recruitment flowcharts and screening data and had initial recruitment pools exceeding 70 participants.The review revealed that most studies provided limited detail on participant selection and frequently initiated recruitment reporting only after caregiver–care recipient dyads had already been assembled. Among the nine studies included, the mean refusal rate among eligible individuals was 57%. In addition, only 24% of initial referrals, on average, completed the first phase of an intervention. Primary barriers to participation included caregiver refusal, lack of an available caregiver/facilitator, and health‐related issues. Based on these observed recruitment flows and pre‐selection processes, home‐based intervention models are estimated to reach fewer than 20% of the target population.These findings suggest that home‐based activity interventions for PLwD are applicable to only a limited segment of the intended population. This “representativeness gap” appears largely attributable to reliance on facilitator–care recipient dyads, which inherently excludes the growing number of PLwD living alone and fails to account for the substantial burdens experienced by primary caregivers. Future research should prioritize the development and evaluation of alternative models, such as technology‐assisted and community‐supported approaches, to enhance scalability, inclusiveness, and equity.

INTRODUCTION

Over 57 million people worldwide were living with dementia in 2019, a number projected to increase to approximately 153 million by 2050. 1 The annual global cost of dementia was estimated at $1.3 trillion, comprising 16% direct medical care costs, 34% social sector costs (including long‐term care), and 50% informal care costs. 2 Most persons living with dementia (PLwD) reside in community settings, 3 which prompted a surge in home‐based activity interventions. 4 , 5 , 6 These interventions encompass a wide range of approaches, including exercise. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 music, 11 reading, 11 cognitive stimulation, 12 and occupational therapy. 13 Although many studies report favorable outcomes, the external validity of these interventions remains unclear. The extent to which these findings can be generalized to the broader population of community‐dwelling PLwD is difficult to assess because the representativeness of study samples is rarely evaluated or reported.

Examining representativeness is essential to (1) determine how well interventions address the activity needs of community‐dwelling PLwD, (2) ensure that solutions are developed for the most vulnerable PLwD, and (3) inform decisions regarding resource allocation.

This paper examines how well participants in home‐based activity intervention studies represent the broader population of community‐dwelling PLwD and identifies systemic sources for selection bias that limit the reach and equity of these interventions.

METHODS

A targeted narrative review was conducted to assess representativeness, using three complementary strategies: (1) an analysis of two recent systematic reviews 4 , 5 on activity interventions for community‐dwelling PLwD, including papers published between 1998 and 2019; (2) a PubMed search on October 8, 2025, using the terms intervention, dementia, and home for the years 2020 to 2025; and (3) two additional studies identified through the literature review. Inclusion was limited to peer‐reviewed activity intervention studies that (1) targeted community‐dwelling PLwD, (2) provided detailed recruitment flowcharts and screening data, and (3) had relatively large initial recruitment samples (>70 individuals or dyads). Data extraction focused on attrition from the initial recruitment to enrollment at the end of the first phase of intervention, noting reasons for exclusion or refusal. The methods sections of each paper were reviewed for details on participant selection. and all reported reasons for exclusion were documented to assess how representative participants in these intervention studies were of the broader population of community‐dwelling PLwD.

RESULTS

Study selection and characteristics

Study selection

The two systematic review papers yielded 32 references (after removal of two duplicates), of which four met the inclusion criteria. 8 , 9 , 10 , 12 The PubMed search produced 77 potential entries, of which three were eligible. 7 , 13 , 14 Two additional studies (one study covered by two relevant papers) were identified during the literature review, 11 , 15 , 16 resulting in nine studies included for analysis. Of the studies, three were based in the United Kingdom (UK), 8 , 12 , 14 two were US studies, 12 , 13 and the rest were diverse (international, 11 , 15 Hong Kong, 7 Finland, 10 , 17 Israel 16 ). The content of the activity interventions varied and included music, 11 reading, 11 exercise, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 17 cognitive interventions, 12 and others. The requirement of using dyads

It was not surprising that all included studies recruited caregiver–care recipient dyads. The caregiver often served as an interventionist/facilitator, guiding and prompting the PLwD during the intervention and reporting on the impact of the intervention.

New Interventions for Independence in Dementia Study.

To place these findings in context, we also examined the inclusion criteria used across studies (Table 2). The studies varied in the way inclusion and exclusion criteria were presented. Some clearly distinguished between inclusion and exclusion criteria (e.g., Baker et al., 15 Chan et al., 7 Cohen‐Mansfield and Cohen, 16 Fortinsky et al., 13 Orgeta et al. 12 ), yet some did not clearly differentiate between them (e.g., McCurry et al., 9 Pitkälä et al. 10 ). However, given that many studies relied on referrals, participants could be excluded for not fitting either inclusion or exclusion criteria (e.g., Baker et al., 15 Chan et al., 7 Lowery et al., 8 Orgeta et al., 12 Rapaport et al. 14 ). Across studies, inclusion criteria primarily focused on the PLwD's functional status, needs, and suitability to the intervention and required the participation of a facilitator or caregiver who was willing and able to comply with the study procedures. The required relationship between the facilitator and the PLwD ranged from cohabiting spouse providing substantial care to a friend with more limited contact. Thus eligibility depended not only on the characteristics of the PLwD but also on the availability of a suitable facilitator or caregiver, creating a source of selection that might limit representativeness.

In contrast, several studies used existing databases to identify potential participants and apply preliminary eligibility screens (e.g., Pitkälä et al., 10 , Fortinsky et al., 13 , Pitkälä et al. 17 ). For example, Pitkälä et al. 10 started the recruitment with a random sample drawn from the Alzheimer's Disease Drug Reimbursement Register of the Kela National Pension Fund of Finland. Overall, most studies relied on referral‐based recruitment and only a minority using population‐based registers or databases. Yet, in both cases, the initial recruitment sample already consisted of a select population, such as dyads.

Proportion of original sample completing the first phase of intervention

The proportion of the original sample who completed the first phase of the intervention is shown in line 5 of Table 1. These proportions ranged from 42% in Chan et al.’s 7 Hong Kong study to 11% in Rapaport et al.’s 14 study, with a mean of 24% and a median of 22%. These proportions relate to preselected original samples described above, rather than to all community‐dwelling PLwD.

Reasons for exclusion

Defining caregivers/facilitators

Lack of available caregiver or facilitator and caregiver refusals to participate were major reasons for exclusion from studies (Table 1). As can be seen in Table 2, the definition of “caregiver or facilitator” varied widely, ranging from co‐residing spouses 17 to family caregivers who had at least weekly contact with the PLwD 14 to individuals, family, friends, or formal caregivers willing to assist with intervention activities. 16 Table 4 provides a tabulation of the identity of the facilitator/caregiver across the studies and demonstrates the great variability in the identities of the caregivers who facilitate the intervention across studies. Even when the PLwD were married, the facilitators of the intervention were often not the spouse. Across studies, between 26% and 100% of PLwD were married; the rate at which the spouse was the facilitator ranged from 100% to 0%, and the rate at which an adult child was the facilitator ranged between 72% and 0%. These figures underscore the heterogeneity of facilitators’ roles and relationship across studies, which complicates comparisons.

Variability in reporting reasons for exclusion

Studies also differed in the specificity with which they described exclusion. For example, Orgeta et al. 12 noted exclusions for “not having an available caregiver” but did not provide counts for potential participants excluded for each exclusion category. Another re‐occurring challenge was distinguishing between refusals and the reasons for refusals. Several studies cited caregiver time constraints as a reason for refusal. Cohen‐Mansfield and Cohen, 16 for instance, contemplated whether caregivers who did not have the time to participate in the activities should be considered “refusing” or “unavailable.” Orgeta et al. 12 provided more granular refusal categories, such as “Not available owing to holiday/family/work commitments” or “Prefers group activities/does activities at home/considers intervention not suitable.” However, most refusals across studies were not accompanied by explanations. Both reasoned and unreasoned refusals are categorized as “refusals” in Table 1.

The burden of refusal

After excluding individuals who were ineligible due to health‐ or study‐related criteria (e.g., unreachable, geographic constraints, prior participation in a similar study), the percentage of eligible individuals who refused participation is shown in the final line of Table 1. Refusal rates ranged from 39% to 68% with an overall mean of 57% and a median of 59%. No refusal percentage is reported for Pitkälä et al., 10 as most exclusions resulted from non‐response to the initial solicitation, a category that may indicate refusals but may also reflect other causes. Several other studies also included non‐response to solicitation 12 or exclusions of parts of the sample without providing the reasons, 9 , 14 though to a lesser extent.

Quantifying rates for reasons for refusal/multiple reasons for refusal

The refusal percentages reported in Table 1 likely underestimate barriers to participation because only one reason for non‐participation was recorded. In Cohen‐Mansfield and Cohen, 16 for example, nine individuals provided multiple reasons for declining participation. Common secondary reasons included having no one to facilitate the intervention (n = 4, in addition to the seven listed in Table 1) and insufficient time to facilitate the intervention (n = 2, in addition to the 14 listed), suggesting that such barriers may have been even more influential than reflected in the tabulated data. Single‐reason coding, which was the methodology provided by most studies, thus biases the distribution of barriers.

DISCUSSION

The interpretation of these findings focused on (1) estimating the proportion of community‐dwelling PLwD who may realistically benefit from the growing number of home‐based activity interventions, (2) identifying the most prevalent reasons for exclusion from these studies, and (3) interpreting the underlying mechanisms driving the major exclusion categories.

Representativeness

Ideally, a representative study would include all community‐residing PLwD within a defined geographic area, including those living alone, those without a primary caregiver, and those with functional impairments. No such comprehensive dataset was used by any of the studies identified. Consequently, selection processes preceded the formation of the initial sample in each study.

Representativeness of PLwD living alone

Caregiver‐mediated activity interventions implicitly assume that community‐dwelling PLwD live with, or have access to, an available caregiver who can support intervention delivery. Demographic trends challenge this assumption. White et al. 3 reported that between 2015 and 2021, the proportion of PLwD who resided with others decreased from 59% to 49%, while the proportion living alone increased from 16% to 22%.

To what extent are PLwD who live alone represented in the analyzed studies? Whereas in some studies 10 they are clearly underrepresented, because of the focus on co‐residing spouses, many studies do not specify this, but in studies such as Fortinsky et al., 13 in which 26% of the PLwD were married and 72% of the activity facilitators were children of the PLwD, it is possible that persons living alone with dementia were included, but that was not specified. The reviewed studies varied considerably in regard to the extent to which they accommodated the needs of community‐dwelling PLwD who may lack a spousal or child interventionist. In some studies, PLwD living alone were clearly excluded, as in spouse‐focused designs, whereas in others, they may have been included but were not identified as such.

Estimated reach of interventions

Line 5 of Table 1 presents the percentage of initial referrals that actually completed the first phase of each study. These percentages ranged from 11% 14 to 42%, 7 with a mean of 24%. However, these figures were derived from samples that were not representative of the broader population of community‐dwelling PLwD. In some of the studies (e.g., Baker et al. 11 , 15 ) recruitment was limited to pre‐identified dyads who had already expressed interest in participation. Consequently, the proportion of community‐dwelling PLwD who would be both eligible for and participate in the types of interventions described in the literature is likely substantially lower than 24%, and probably less than 20%. Nevertheless, given the large and growing number of community‐dwelling PLwD and their substantial unmet needs, reaching even this segment of the population could make a meaningful contribution to improving well‐being.

Representativeness and effectiveness

The importance of representativeness ultimately depends on the effectiveness of the intervention being evaluated. Although a comprehensive review of effectiveness of home‐based interventions for PLwD is beyond the scope of this paper, two reviews have examined the outcomes of such interventions. De Almeida et al. 4 conducted a systematic review of home‐based physical activity programs and concluded that these interventions were generally safe and effective in slowing cognitive decline and improving a range of functional outcomes, including behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia and activities of daily living. In contrast, Tan et al. 5 reviewed a broader range of home‐based activity programs, including occupational therapy, cognitive interventions, and music therapy, and reported more mixed findings. They identified factors associated with greater effectiveness, including aligning activities to participants’ interests and abilities, and, assuring that caregivers were involved, yet did not perceive the intervention as too demanding or challenging.

These findings suggest a potential tension between effectiveness and representativeness. Caregiver involvement appears to enhance intervention effectiveness, yet the requirement for an available and willing caregiver may substantially reduce the proportion of PLwD who are eligible to participate. Thus a key feature associated with intervention success may simultaneously contribute to the representativeness gap identified in this review. Given that these reviews were published in 2020 and 2022, an updated synthesis of the effectiveness literature is warranted.

Barriers to participation

A notable limitation across the reviewed studies is the incomplete reporting of reasons for exclusion. Pitkälä et al. 10 stated that 61% of those approached to participate did not respond to recruitment efforts but did not specify why. Similarly, Orgeta et al. 12 reported that 16% of the original sample did not respond to solicitations. Two other studies 9 , 14 reported exclusions without providing the reasons for them. Moreover, whereas the line labeled “other exclusions” in Table 1 generally refers to participants not meeting the study's inclusion criteria, the specific exclusions are not always specified. For example, data on how many potential participants had no caregiver available are not available in some studies because they are embedded in the other exclusions (e.g., Baker et al., 15 Orgeta et al. 12 ).

Consequently, the available data do not allow for a reliable estimate of how often lack of a facilitator prevents participation. This issue is obscured by (1) the frequent use of dyads as the starting point in the reported recruitment, (2) multiple exclusion reasons were generally not reported, and (3) the tendency to subsume “no available caregiver” within broader categories such as general study exclusion criteria. This limitation is particularly salient given the evidence that a growing number of PLwD reside in the community without an available caregiver. 3

Other exclusion reasons, such as health issues, functional limitations, or death, reflect characteristics inherent in the population for whom the interventions were developed. Both PLwD and their caregivers, particularly spouses, but often also adult children, are older adults who may face their own health‐related barriers. For many, adding the responsibility of facilitating an intervention may not be feasible.

Caregiver burden as a barrier

The substantial burden experienced by caregivers of PLwD, e.g., Mattos et al., 18 likely contributes to refusals and dropouts. Caregivers reported that they were “too busy,” “stressed,” or “depressed” to assume the additional responsibility of facilitating an intervention. 12 Some studies explicitly linked intervention‐ or study‐related participation to increased caregiver burden. For instance, Suttanon et al. 19 and Wesson et al. 20 identified intervention‐related burden as a reason for attrition, with the latter reporting a trend toward increased burden in the intervention group.

Although some interventions reported reductions in caregiver burden, 8 others have not. 12 , 21 The activities required of caregivers in home‐based interventions involving tailored activity programs resulted in an increased workload on caregivers as discussed in Tan et al. 5 and Prick et al., 22 who, evaluating an intervention based on Teri et al., 23 found that both caregivers and their care recipients experienced the program as overly burdensome.

The caregiver paradox

The aforementioned findings underscore an inherent paradox in home‐based interventions for PLwD. While such interventions aim to enhance functioning and well‐being among PLwD, they often place additional demands on caregivers who are already burdened by caregiving responsibilities. Although caregivers generally have the best interests of care recipients at heart, they often experience substantial caregiving burden. Home‐based activity interventions rely on caregivers to facilitate intervention delivery and provide outcome data, thereby increasing the demands placed on them. This paradox contributes to high refusal rates and mixed outcomes and highlights the need to mitigate or eliminate caregiver burden associated with intervention delivery. The burden associated with intervention delivery should therefore be viewed not merely as a limitation of existing interventions but as a central design consideration in the development of future programs.

Strengths and limitations

This review is the first to systematically examine the representativeness gap in home‐based activity interventions for community‐dwelling PLwD and to identify caregiver dependence as a major structural contributor to that gap. The findings suggest that fewer than 20% of community‐dwelling PLwD may be able to benefit from currently available intervention models. Given the large and growing population of community‐dwelling PLwD worldwide, these findings have important implications for setting research priorities, service development, and policy planning.

In addition, analysis of participant exclusion and non‐participation identified a major underlying challenge – the tension between caregiver involvement as a facilitator of intervention and caregiver burden as a barrier to participation. Other important barriers include the absence of an available caregiver or facilitator and health‐related limitations among potential participants. A further strength of this review is the diversity of the studies examined, which varied by country, intervention type, and methodology. Despite this heterogeneity, the studies showed considerable consistency in reporting high rates of refusal and low proportions of initially screened individuals who completed at least the first phase of the intervention.

Several limitations are acknowledged. First, despite an extensive search, only nine studies met the inclusion criteria for full review. Second, none of the studies began with a representative sample of community‐dwelling PLwD, and many provided incomplete details about those who were initially contacted but ultimately excluded before enrollment. Third, the review was restricted to published studies. It is possible that unpublished studies, particularly those experiencing recruitment difficulties, would have reported even lower participation and retention rates, resulting in lower estimates of intervention reach. The review also did not include searches of research registries or direct inquiries to investigators. Although these approaches might have identified additional studies, they would have introduced other methodological challenges regarding data quality and comparability.

Finally, representativeness encompasses dimensions beyond intervention access and participation. This review did not examine the extent to which participants were representative of the broader population of community‐dwelling PLwD with respect to characteristics such as health status, functional impairment, or socioeconomic circumstances. Although health conditions were cited as reasons for exclusion, the available data were insufficient to evaluate how closely study participants reflected the distributions of these characteristics in the broader community‐dwelling PLwD populations from which they were drawn.

Conclusion

Home‐based activity interventions for PLwD show promise but currently apply to only an estimated less than 20% of the intended population. Thus, the findings reveal a significant “representativeness gap.” This limitation stems from the following factors: (1) compounding burden. Rather than providing relief, many programs place additional demands on caregivers who are already overextended by the daily demands of dementia care. This is exemplified in reasons provided for refusal to participate expressed by some caregivers, in the very high refusal rates, and in caregivers who did participate perceiving the programs as burdensome; (2) demographic mismatch and external validity. The increasing proportion of PLwD who reside alone or without an available caregiver renders growing proportions ineligible for these interventions. Further, by excluding those who do not have a caregiver, the studies implicitly exclude the most vulnerable PLwD; and (3) methodological limitations. The recruitment procedures in most studies do not allow estimation of how many potential participants were excluded due to the lack of an available facilitator.

To advance this field, future research and program development should (1) examine the nature of refusals and identify strategies to address them and (2) design alternative models that do not rely on caregiver facilitation, such as technology‐assisted interventions or community‐based supports. These may include telehealth interventions, trained volunteers, paid facilitators, robotic facilitators, and providing transportation to group‐based activities, for example. Expanding access to meaningful engagement opportunities for community‐dwelling PLwD is essential for improving their well‐being and for meeting the ethical obligation of addressing the needs of the full spectrum of community‐dwelling PLwD. This review contributes by quantifying the limited reach of home‐based activity interventions for PLwD and highlighting structural sources of selection bias that future trials should explicitly address.
