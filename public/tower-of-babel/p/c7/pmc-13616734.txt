Randomized trial of a single-session digital intervention targeting emotion-related beliefs and emotion regulation
Authors: Corey R. Roos, R. Kathryn McHugh, Nilofar Vafaie, Jessica Schleider, Riley McDanal, Andrew DeBenedictis, Robert Palmer, Maya John, Victoria R. Votaw, Hedy Kober
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is a lack of accessible and efficacious interventions for emotionally distressed adults. We developed a single-session digital intervention, called Visitor Mindset, targeting beliefs about negative emotions and awareness, clarity, and acceptance of negative emotions. We evaluated the acceptability, feasibility, and short-term efficacy of Visitor Mindset among emotionally distressed adults (n = 530). Participants were recruited via CloudResearch and randomized to Visitor Mindset or control (nature videos with relaxing music). The primary outcome was emotional distress. Secondary outcomes were functional impairment and alcoholic drinks per week. Outcomes were assessed before randomization and at 2-week follow-up. All participants randomized to Visitor Mindset completed the intervention. Acceptability ratings (e.g., perceived helpfulness) for Visitor Mindset were favorable. There was not a significant difference in changes in emotional distress between treatment conditions (d=0.13, p>.05). However, Visitor Mindset, versus control, resulted in greater reductions in functional impairment (d=0.21, p< .05) and drinks per week (d=0.21, p< .05). Improvement in acceptance and reduction in the belief negative emotions last a long time significantly mediated the effects of Visitor Mindset on emotional distress and functional impairment (p’s< 0.05), but not drinks per week. Among participants with at least moderate-to-severe emotional distress, the effect of Visitor Mindset on emotional distress in this case was significant (d=0.34, p< .05), and the effect of Visitor Mindset on functional impairment was stronger (d=0.31, p< .05) in this subsample relative to the effect in the full sample. Functional impairment, but not other outcomes, demonstrated clinical significance. That is, Visitor Mindset participants were 61% more likely than control participants to change from a clinical score to a subclinical score for functional impairment. Visitor Mindset is feasible and acceptable, and may be promising for improving functioning and reducing alcohol use among emotionally distressed adults. Additionally, Visitor Mindset appears to mobilize theorized mechanisms. Future research is warranted with longer-term follow-ups.

Introduction

Emotional distress is highly prevalent1–3. and linked with negative costs to society4–6. Although effective psychological interventions have been developed for emotional distress7, most individuals do not receive any form of treatment8,9. There is a need for accessible and effective interventions to reduce emotional distress, especially those poised to reach people unable to access existing forms of care. Emotional distress often co-occurs with maladaptive health behaviors, such as hazardous alcohol use (more than 14 drinks per week for men and more than 7 drinks per week for women10). It is estimated that about 25–40% of adults engage in hazardous alcohol use. Importantly, drinking to cope with emotional distress is common and linked with greater alcohol-related problems11. Hence, there is also a need to develop more accessible treatment options for reducing both emotional distress and hazardous alcohol use.

Digital interventions can expand the public health reach of interventions for emotional distress and hazardous alcohol use12,13. Brief digital interventions may be especially critical to develop because of the high rates of disengagement from digital interventions (e.g., mental health apps) after a short period of initial use (e.g., completing only one or a few sessions14,15). Single-session interventions may be particularly accessible because they only require participation on one occasion16. Many effective single-session digital interventions have been developed for adolescents17. There is less research on single-session digital interventions for adults. One study evaluated a single-session web-based intervention that draws from cognitive-behavioral therapy and positive psychology18. They found no effects of the intervention on emotional distress and functional impairment18. Another randomized trial tested a single-session psychoeducation intervention among cancer patients19, and found significant effects on emotional distress. One study found that a single-session digital intervention targeting perceived control over anxiety was not effective among adults20. Overall, research on single-session digital interventions among adults is mixed, and relative to research on multi-session digital interventions, is in its early stages. Ultimately, there remains a lack of available and effective single-session digital interventions targeting regulation of negative emotions for adults.

Given their brevity, single-session digital interventions may need to focus on salient mechanisms16,21 that facilitate improvements in mental health. Emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance, three types of emotion regulation skills, are theorized to be particularly important mechanisms underlying mental health22. Emotional awareness is the ability to pay attention to emotions, whereas emotional clarity is the ability to recognize and understand what types of emotions are arising23,24. Emotional acceptance involves allowing emotions to be present, without undue efforts to avoid, suppress, or get rid of them23,25. Emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance are theorized to be foundational emotion regulation skills because they may facilitate other adaptive responses to negative emotions, such as cognitive reappraisal, problem-solving, and goal-directed behaviors26. For example, being aware of one’s emotions and identifying them may help individuals select appropriate skills for responding to particular emotions. Being fully aware of emotion-related negative thoughts may help individuals challenge and reframe these thoughts. Accepting emotions may be essential for preventing initial automatic maladaptive responses to emotions, such as rumination, self-criticism, and avoidance, which may impede the implementation of adaptive responses. Additionally, acceptance may allow individuals to tolerate negative emotions for longer periods of time, thereby providing more time for an individual to choose and implement other adaptive emotion regulation skills. A large body of research shows that emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance are associated with other adaptive emotion regulation skills26,27. And importantly, research consistently demonstrates that greater emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance are associated with less emotional distress28–30, functional impairment31,32, and hazardous alcohol use33,34 across a wide range of clinical and non-clinical populations.

Recent theoretical and empirical work suggests that core beliefs about negative emotions may shape the way individuals try to regulate emotions35–38. For example, beliefs about the usefulness of negative emotions and their typical duration may be important mechanisms influencing the process of regulating negative emotions39–42. Believing that negative emotions last a long time may increase efforts to avoid emotions, and may reduce efforts to regulate emotions because one doubts the emotion will change. Believing negative emotions are useless may prevent one from processing emotions and expressing them. Notably, evidence suggests that beliefs that negative emotions are useless and that they last a long time are associated with greater emotional distress35,36. Recent research also indicates that the belief that negative emotions last a long time is associated with greater alcohol use43,44.

Importantly, beliefs about the usefulness and duration of negative emotions may be particularly relevant because they may influence the above noted emotion regulation skills: emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance. For example, believing that negative emotions are temporary may facilitate acceptance because individuals may be more likely to accept or “sit with” negative emotions if they know they will eventually pass45. Conversely, acceptance may influence beliefs that negative emotions are temporary. That is, accepting negative emotions, instead of suppressing or avoiding them, may provide the opportunity to observe that negative emotions do indeed eventually pass. Indeed, a cornerstone of Buddhism is practicing meditation to experientially observe the rising and passing of emotions46. Moreover, beliefs that negative emotions are useful may facilitate emotional awareness and clarity because individuals may be more motivated to pay attention to and understand emotions if they think they may be useful47. Conversely, emotional awareness and clarity may increase beliefs that negative emotions are useful because noticing and identifying emotions may provide the opportunity for individuals to link specific emotions (e.g., sadness) to potential benefits (e.g., expressing sadness increases social support)46. Several empirical studies have shown that the belief that negative emotions are useless is inversely associated with emotional awareness and clarity48,49. There is a lack of studies examining the link between emotional acceptance and beliefs about the duration of emotions. However, studies show that beliefs about the controllability of negative emotions—which is related to beliefs about duration—is associated with emotional acceptance49. Overall, it is plausible that emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance may work synergistically with beliefs about negative emotions to improve mental health.

A single-session digital intervention targeting emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance, and beliefs about the duration and usefulness of negative emotions may also be valuable given several evidence-based therapies target these mechanisms. This includes.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy50, Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction51, Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy52, Dialectical Behavior Therapy53, and Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention54— which are each efficacious in reducing emotional distress and/or alcohol use55–58. For example, in Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction individuals practice emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance via mindfulness meditation, and they develop new beliefs about emotions via group discussion about the nature of emotions. Some of the interventions noted above have been successfully implemented digitally59–61. Nevertheless, these digital interventions are either paired with clinician-based support61, and/or include multiple modules that need to be completed over time (e.g., 4–8 weeks; 59, 61). These features may limit scalability. For example, individuals may not be able to access a clinician to support them or may be unable or unwilling to complete many modules over time.

Altogether, there remains a need for highly accessible interventions, such as single-session self-guided digital interventions, to reduce the public health burden related to emotional distress and hazardous alcohol use. Further, such interventions may have potent effects if they directly target the core mechanisms of emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance, and beliefs about the duration and usefulness of negative emotions. Accordingly, our team developed a single-session digital intervention for adults, called Visitor Mindset, that targets such mechanisms to reduce emotional distress, functional impairment, and hazardous alcohol use. We recruited emotionally distressed adults (n = 530) and conducted a randomized controlled trial comparing the effects of Visitor Mindset versus a control intervention (i.e., watching nature videos with relaxing music). Given that single-session interventions and mindfulness- and acceptance-based interventions (in-person) have shown efficacy for both mild/moderate and severe emotional distress55,62, we reasoned that Visitor Mindset may be effective across the spectrum of severity for emotional distress, and thus we opted to recruit participants with emotional distress ranging from mild to severe.

Our primary hypotheses were that Visitor Mindset, as compared to the control intervention, would result in significantly greater reductions in emotional distress, functional impairment, and drinks per week. We also hypothesized that Visitor Mindset would result in significantly greater improvements in emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance, and greater reductions in the beliefs that negative emotions are useless and last a long time.

Understanding how and why interventions work is important for informing precision-based care63. Hence, as exploratory analyses, we also conducted mediation analyses to test whether putative mechanisms mediated the effects of treatment condition on outcomes, as well as moderation analyses to test whether baseline emotional distress moderated treatment effects. The moderation analyses were conducted to explore whether Visitor Mindset would have the strongest therapeutic effects among individuals with more severe symptoms of emotional distress. We suspected this may be the case because emotion-related beliefs and emotion regulation skills may be most relevant and important for those experiencing more frequent and intense distressing emotions.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedures

We used Amazon Mechanical Turk (M-Turk) via CloudResearch64,65 to recruit emotionally distressed adults. Numerous studies show that online platforms such as CloudResearch are viable approaches for randomized trials18,20,65,66, as well as single-session interventions18,20,67. Participants were able to participate in the study anonymously. Figure 1 shows the CONSORT diagram.

Fig. 1Consort diagram.

Consort diagram.

The final sample included 530 participants. Inclusion criteria were: 18 years of age, residing in the United States, fluent in English, and reporting at least mild emotional distress, as indicated by a score of 3 or greater on the Patient Health Question-4 (PHQ-4; 68). We chose this cut point because we were interested in the effects of Visitor Mindset among individuals with varying levels of emotional distress, including those with mild symptoms. Exclusion criteria included failure to pass quality control questions (described below), exiting the study prior to randomization, obvious lack of English fluency, entering random responses in open response questions, responding with copy and pasted responses from text earlier in the intervention, and completing the baseline survey in less than 180 s (indicating the participant rushed through the survey without fully reading each question).

We opted not to have alcohol use behavior as an eligibility criterion. Instead, we were interested in focusing on a broader sample of emotionally distressed adults, which would enable us to examine rates of any alcohol use and hazardous alcohol use in this sample.

All surveys in this study were administered with Qualtrics. Individuals completed a brief screening survey with a 4-item measure of emotional distress. The brief screening also included quality control questions to ensure high-quality data by excluding potential “bots” or inattentive participants. An example quality control question was listening to a recording and then typing in the word spoken (Zebra). If a participant was preliminarily eligible after the screening, participants were directed to the baseline survey, which included questions on demographics, emotional distress, functioning, beliefs about emotions, mindfulness-based emotion regulation skills, and alcohol use. Participants were then immediately randomized to an intervention. Randomization was automated via code in Qualtrics. After completing the intervention, individuals were immediately directed to a post-intervention survey, which includes questions on the acceptability of the interventions (e.g., helpfulness). The post-intervention survey only included items on acceptability, not any of the outcomes or mechanisms. Two weeks later, participants were sent a link via CloudResearch with the follow-up survey, which included the same questions as the baseline survey, with the exception of demographics. Participants were paid $6 for completing assessments at baseline and $6 for completing assessments at 2-week follow-up. We chose this compensation rate based on the length of our assessment and on compensation rates in similar studies69. This study was approved by the Yale University Institutional Review Board in the United States, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. All methods were conducted in accordance with the regulations of the Yale University Institutional Review Board, as well as the Helsinki Declaration.

Measures

Emotional distress (brief screening measure)

In the initial brief screening survey, participants completed the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4; 68), a four-item self-report measure of emotional distress in the past 2 weeks, with responses: 0 = not at all, 1 = several days, 2 = more than half the days, and 3 = nearly every day. This measure includes 2 items assessing depressive symptoms and 2 items assessing anxiety symptoms. Total scores range from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating greater emotional distress. Participants were eligible if their total score was 3 or more68. A total score of 3 indicates mild emotional distress68. Cronbach’s alpha reliability for the PHQ-4 was 0.921.

Demographic information

In the baseline survey, participants were asked to report their age, gender, ethnicity, race, educational attainment, and employment status.

Acceptability ratings

In the post-intervention survey, participants in both conditions answered questions about perceived helpfulness (“I found the online course helpful”), ease-of-use (“I found the program easy to use”), visual appeal (I thought the visual content of the online program was appealing”), and how well the program kept them engaged (The online program did a good job at keeping me engaged”). Responses for each item ranged from 0 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree.

Intervention feasibility

Qualtrics recorded whether participants completed their intervention. Intervention feasibility was measured as rates of intervention completion.

Emotional distress (outcome measure)

In the baseline and 2-week follow-up survey, participants completed the Patient Health Questionnaire Anxiety and Depression Scale (PHQ-ADS; 70), a sixteen-item self-report measure of emotional distress in the past 2 weeks, with responses ranging from 0 = not at all to 3 = nearly every day. The PHQ-ADS is comprised of the Patient Health Questionnaire depression scale (PHQ-9) and the seven-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7). Total scores range from 0 to 48, with higher scores indicating greater emotional distress. Mild emotional distress is indicated by scores ≥ 10, moderate by scores ≥ 20, and severe by scores ≥ 3070. Cronbach’s alpha reliability for the PHD-ADS was 0.921 at baseline and 0.933 at the 2-week follow-up.

Functional impairment

In the baseline and 2-week follow-up survey, participants completed the Work and Social Adjustment Scale (WSAS; 71), a five-item self-report measure of functional impairment, with responses ranging from 0 = not at all to 8 = very severely. The WSAS assesses the degree to which individuals perceive their mental health is impairing their functioning in life domains, including work, home management, social leisure activities, private leisure activities, and close relationships. Total scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores on this measure indicating greater functional impairment. Minimal functional impairment is indicated by scores < 10, mild by scores ≥ 10, moderate by scores ≥ 20, and severe by scores ≥ 3071. Cronbach’s alpha reliability for the WSAS was 0.894 at baseline and 0.913 at the 2-week follow-up.

Alcoholic drinks per week

In the baseline and 2-week follow-up surveys, participants completed the Daily Drinking Questionnaire (DDQ72), which asked participants to estimate the typical number of standard drinks they have on each day of the week, in the past 2 weeks. Participants were shown a graphic to assist with estimation of standard drinks (e.g., a 12 fluid ounce 5% alcohol/volume beer is 1 standard drink). Total drinks per week was computed from these responses.

Emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance

In the baseline and 2-week follow-up survey, participants completed the Emotion Regulation Skills Questionnaire (ERSQ73), a 27-item self-report measure of past week emotion regulation skills. The ERSQ is comprised of nine subscales of emotion regulation skills. For this study, we focused on the emotion regulation skills of awareness, clarity, and acceptance. Each of these scales included 3 items with responses ranging from 0 = not at all to 4 = almost always. Example items include: awarenes subscale (“I paid attention to my feeling”), clarity subscale (“I was clear about what emotions I was feeling in the moment”), and acceptance scale (“I accepted my emotions”). We examined mean scores per item for each subscale. Higher scores indicate better emotion regulation abilities. For the awareness subscale of the ERSQ, Cronbach’s alpha reliability was 0.817 at baseline and 0.825 at the 2-week follow-up. For the clarity subscale of the ERSQ, Cronbach’s alpha reliability was 0.928 for baseline and 0.932 for the 2-week follow-up. For the acceptance subscale of the ERSQ, Cronbach’s alpha reliability was 0.842 at baseline and 0.918 at the 2-week follow-up.

Beliefs about the usefulness negative emotions

In the baseline and 2-week follow-up surveys, participants completed items from the Negative-Usefulness subscale of the Emotion Beliefs Questionnaire (EBQ; 35), which includes 4 items each with responses ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. The EBQ does not include a time window and instead asks about beliefs in general. An example item of the Negative-Usefulness subscale includes: “People don’t need their negative emotions.” We examined mean scores per item. Higher scores indicate greater beliefs that negative emotions are useless. For the Negative-Usefulness subscale of the EBQ, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability was 0.883 at baseline and 0.888 at the 2-week follow-up.

Beliefs about the duration negative emotions

In the baseline and 2-week follow-up survey, participants completed two items from the Leahy Emotion Scheme Scale (LESS; 41) and two items from the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS; 23). The two items from the LESS included: “I fear that if I allowed myself to have a strong feeling, it would never go away” and “Strong negative emotions only last for a short period of time” (reverse-scored). The two items from the DERS included: “When I am upset, I believe that I’ll end up feeling very depressed” and “When I am upset, I believe I will remain that way for a long time.” These items from the DERS and the LESS were selected because they had the most relevance to duration of negative emotions. The decision to use both the LESS and the DERS was made a-priori, and we opted for this approach to provide greater coverage of the construct and to have 4 items, instead of only 2, to increase internal reliability. For both the LESS and the DERS, responses ranged from 0 = not at all to 4 = almost always. We examined mean scores per item across the four items. Higher scores indicate greater beliefs that negative emotions last a long time. For the four items used to assess beliefs about the duration of negative emotions, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability was 0.760 at baseline and 0.790 at the 2-week follow-up.

Visitor mindset intervention

Our team developed Visitor Mindset, a single-session web-based intervention for emotionally distressed adults. The intervention was designed to take approximately 30 min to complete, and includes brief animated videos, audio-guided mindfulness exercises, and text-based information and question prompts. As noted above, Visitor Mindset has a targeted focus on improving emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance, as well reducing beliefs that negative emotions last a long time and are useless. We designed the intervention to mobilize all of these mechanisms, given the potential beneficial synergistic effects among emotion beliefs and emotion regulation skills (e.g., acceptance reducing beliefs that negative emotions last a long time, which in turn enhances acceptance). Given the intervention is only a single-session, we aimed to develop content that was focused, not too complex, and easy-to-remember. Hence, Visitor Mindset focuses on one core concept, a Visitor Mindset, that encompasses all of the targeted mechanisms, and this concept is taught via one central metaphor: emotions as visitors at one’s house. For this metaphor, negative emotions are visitors that knock at the door of one’s house and visit for a temporary period and then leave. Throughout the interventions, via didactic text and videos, and experiential exercises, participants are guided in developing a Visitor Mindset, which means: (a) understanding that negative emotions are “visitors” that are temporary and sometimes helpful, and (b) noticing, identifying, and accepting these visitors (e.g., allowing them to visit without undue efforts avoid, push away, or fight visitors). Visitor Mindset is presented as the opposite of Intruder Mindset, which involves viewing negative emotions as intruders that are long-lasting, dangerous, and rarely helpful. With an Intruder Mindset, negative emotions are intruders breaking into a house, staying for long period of time, and stealing or damaging a person’s belongings. With an Intruder Mindset, one tries to defend themselves against the “intruder.” And having an Intruder Mindset means not trying to understand negative emotions and not accepting and allowing negative emotions to visit.

Our team drew upon existing mindfulness- and acceptance-based interventions50,52,53,55,74. For example, similar to existing interventions, we included: (a) the poem “The Guest House” which likens emotions to guests or visitors at one’s house, (b) a metaphor to convey the concept of acceptance, (c) information on the function of negative emotions, and (d) brief mindfulness exercises to offer participants the opportunity to actively practice emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance—both with and without imaginal exposure to a situation eliciting negative emotions. Visitor Mindset is distinct from these interventions in its targeted focus on beliefs about negative emotions via the metaphor of emotions as visitors. While existing interventions have some content on beliefs about negative emotions, this content is not the core focus and is just one component. Visitor Mindset is also unique in its focus on what we have termed a “Visitor Mindset,” (the opposite of Intruder Mindset) which includes both adaptive emotion beliefs (e.g., emotions are temporary) and adaptive responses to emotions (accepting and allowing this emotion to pass, knowing it is temporary) that stem from these beliefs. And in the intervention, we frame a successful outcome from the intervention as increasing one’s Visitor Mindset. This term is used repeatedly, and we clearly delineate how and why this specific mindset is critical for emotional well-being. Finally, in developing the intervention, we also drew from single-session interventions designed by the authors67,75,76.

The video-based mindfulness exercise (see Table 1, number 9) included a variety of photos designed to elicit different negative emotions (e.g., photo of a fire). A total of 6 unpleasant photos were shown, with each photo displayed on screen for about 10 s. The number of photos and duration on the screen was chosen to keep the exercise brief while also providing adequate opportunity to practice for each photo. Participants were instructed to practice Visitor Mindset by noticing and accepting emotions as they viewed the photos.

Visitor Mindset also included brief content (see Table 1, number 14) related to drinking to cope (e.g., reflecting on whether drinking to cope is effective in the long-run) and changing one’s drinking patterns (e.g., identifying reasons for change). This content was only shown (via branching functions in Qualtrics) if participants reported at least some drinking in the past 2 weeks.

Relaxing with nature (control intervention)

Our team developed the control intervention, called Relaxing with Nature, a single-session web-based intervention. Relaxing with Nature involved viewing videos with nature scenes paired with relaxing music. The instructions at the beginning of the intervention were: “Focus your full attention on the nature scenes shown to you. Try to notice the details of each image.”

We designed Relaxing with Nature to match the features of Visitor Mindset, but without including any content on awareness, clarity, and acceptance of emotions or the concepts of Visitor and Intruder Mindset. Accordingly, Relaxing with Nature:1 includes brief audiovisual videos, text-based information, and question prompts;2 is hosted in Qualtrics;3 takes about 30-minutes to complete; and4 includes images of the same animated narrator.

Each video was about 3 min long and focused on a particular types of nature scene (e.g., ocean, mountains, etc.). The question prompt after each video was an open-ended question that asked: “What did you like about the video you just watched?” We chose this question because it was a reasonable question to ask participants to facilitate self-reflection, similar to the open-ended questions in Visitor Mindset. We chose just this one question, rather than multiple questions, because all the videos of the nature scenes were very similar.

Both Relaxing with Nature and Visitor Mindset included features to prevent inattentive skimming. For example, participants could not advance to the next section of the intervention until the length of time for the video or audio-guided exercise expired. Participants had to answer question prompts before advancing. Multiple choice questions with a correct answer were included to encourage attentiveness.

Statistical analyses

The primary outcome was emotional distress. Secondary outcomes were functional impairment and alcoholic drinks per week. The primary mechanisms included beliefs about the usefulness and duration of negative emotions, as well as awareness, clarity, acceptance of negative emotions. Models with drinks per week as the outcome only included the subset of individuals (n = 326; 61.9%) who reported at least some drinking on the baseline survey. Mediation and moderation analyses (described below) were exploratory and conducted to provide preliminary findings about potential treatment matching variables and mechanisms of change.

All analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software (Version 28). To estimate within-treatment condition changes in outcomes and mechanisms from baseline to 2-week follow-up, we conducted paired samples t-tests and computed Cohen’s d effect sizes. To estimate the effects of treatment condition on outcomes and mechanisms, we conducted multiple regression analyses that controlled for the baseline value of the given dependent variable. We computed Cohen’s d effect sizes for differences between the conditions. To estimate the interrelationships of each emotional belief variable with each emotion regulation variable, we conducted correlation analyses. To estimate the indirect (i.e., mediated) effect of treatment condition on outcomes via the mechanisms, we conducted parallel mediation analyses with the PROCESS add-on in SPSS (Model 4), which uses the distribution of products of coefficients approach with bias-corrected bootstrapped confidence intervals77. Parallel mediation models include multiple mediators in a single model. Hence, we included all putative mechanism variables as mediators in the models. The parallel mediation models test the indirect effects of one mediator, while controlling for the other mediators. To evaluate baseline emotional distress as a moderator of treatment effects, we conducted moderation analyses with the PROCESS add-on in SPSS (Model 1), and the Johnson-Neyman procedure was applied to estimate the specific range of values of the moderator variable for which the effects of treatment conditions are significant77,78. To further understand the nature of significant moderation effects, we computed the effect size of treatment for individuals with scores falling within the region of significance77.

Power analyses were conducted with G*Power Version 3.1. These analyses suggested that to have 80% power to detect a small-to-moderate treatment effects (i.e., d=0.36) in the context of multiple regression models with one covariate, we would need to enroll at least 511 participants. For all statistical models examining the effect of the intervention on outcomes or mechanisms at 2-week follow-up, we used complete case analysis (i.e., listwise deletion) for all statistical models79.

As an exploratory aim, we evaluated the clinical significance of intervention effects. That is, for emotional distress and functional impairment, we analyzed the percentage of participants who shifted from a clinical score at baseline to a subclinical score at 2-week follow-up, by treatment condition. Additionally, we analyzed the percentage of participants who shifted from hazardous drinking at baseline to no hazardous drinking at 2-week follow-up, by treatment condition. Chi-square tests were conducted to evaluate the statistical significance of the difference in percentages by treatment condition.

Assessment completion rates

All 530 enrolled participants completed the baseline survey. A total of 520 (98%) completed the post-intervention survey immediately after the intervention, whereas 483 (91%) completed the 2-week follow-up survey. Baseline emotional distress was not significantly associated with missing data (p < .05). There was no significant difference by treatment condition in missing data for the 2-week follow-up (p < .05).

Intervention completion rates and duration

All participants in both conditions completed the intervention they were assigned to. The mean number of minutes taken to complete the intervention was 49.31 [standard deviation (SD) = 98.67] across conditions, 27.21 (SD = 23.23) for Relaxing with Nature, and 70.26 (SD = 132.59) for Visitor Mindset. Further inspection of the data on duration revealed outliers for Visitor Mindset, including 1811, 1223, and 562 min. In these cases, it is likely that the participant left their device and completed the intervention later in the day. Given these outliers, to provide further clarity we examined the percentage of participants completing the intervention in either 30, 45, and 60 min or less. Across conditions, the percentage completing in 30 min or less was 42.7%, in 45 min or less was 64.4%, and in 60 min or less was 82.9%. For Relaxing with Nature, the percentage of participants completing the intervention in 30 min or less was 80.2%, in 45 min or less was 92.7%, and in 60 min or less was 97.4%. For Visitor Mindset, the percentage of participants completing the intervention in 30 min or less was 7.3%, in 45 min or less was 37.7%, and in 60 min or less was 69.2%.

Acceptability ratings

Acceptability ratings ranged from 0 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree. For Visitor Mindset, acceptability ratings were: overall helpfulness 3.31 (SD=0.73), ease-of-use 3.5 (SD=0.57), appeal of visual content 3.07 (SD=0.80), and how well the program kept them engaged 3.33 (SD=0.76) For Relaxing with Nature, acceptability ratings were: overall helpfulness 2.84 (SD=0.92), ease-of-use 3.76 (SD=0.46), appeal of visual content 3.48 (SD=0.68), and how well the program kept them engaged 2.96 (SD=0.96). Compared to Relaxing with Nature, Visitor Mindset had significantly higher scores for overall helpfulness (p<.001) and how well the program kept them engaged (p<.001). However, compared to Relaxing with Nature, Visitor Mindset had significantly lower scores on ease-of-use (p<.001) and appeal of visual content (p<.001).

Moderation analyses

There was a significant interaction effect between treatment condition and baseline emotional distress in the prediction of changes in emotional distress [B (SE)=-0.15(0.07), p=.03, 95% CI = − 0.28, − 0.01], such that Visitor Mindset, versus control, resulted in greater reductions in emotional distress among individuals with higher baseline emotional distress. Specifically, the region of statistical significance (p’s < 0.05) for the effect of treatment condition on changes in emotional distress was when the moderator variable (baseline emotional distress) was a total score of 23 or more. Among individuals at or above a score of 23 (i.e., moderate to severe), the effect size of treatment condition on improvement in emotional distress was d= 0.34, 95% CI= 0.03, 0.66. In contrast, among individuals with scores below 23 (i.e., mild), the effect size of treatment condition on improvement in emotional distress was d= 0.02, 95% CI = − 0.23, 0.20.

There was also a significant interaction effect between treatment condition and baseline emotional distress in the prediction of changes in functional impairment [B)SE)=-0.21(0.07),, p=.003, 95% CI = − 0.34, − 0.06], such that Visitor Mindset resulted in significantly greater reductions in functional impairment among individuals with higher baseline emotional distress. Specifically, the region of statistical significance (p’s < 0.05) for the effect of treatment condition on changes in functional impairment was when the moderator variable (baseline emotional distress) was a total score of 21 or more. Among individuals at or above a score of 21 (i.e., moderate to severe), the effect size of treatment condition on improvement in functional impairment was d= 0.31, 95% CI= 0.03, 0.60. Among individuals with a score below 21 (i.e., mild), the effect size of treatment condition on reductions in functional impairment was d= 0.14, 95% CI = − 0.09, 0.37.

We did not find a significant interaction effect between treatment condition and baseline emotional distress in the prediction of changes in drinks per week [B(SE)=0.04(0.06),, p=.50, 95% CI = − 0.07, 0.16).

Testing for clinical significance

For shifting from a clinical score (≥ 10) on the PHQ-ADS (emotional distress) at baseline to a subclinical score (< 10) at follow-up, in Visitor Mindset the frequency was 72 (31% within this condition) and for control was 66 (28.4%). The odds ratio was 1.13 and was not significant (p=.54). For shifting from a clinical score (≥ 10) on the WSAS (functional impairment) at baseline to a subclinical score (< 10) at follow-up, in Visitor Mindset the frequency was 71 (39%) and for control was 51 (28.3%). The odds ratio was 1.61 and was significant (p=.03). Hence, participants who received Visitor Mindset were 61% more likely than control to shift from a clinical to a subclinical score for functional impairment. For shifting from hazardous drinking at baseline to no hazardous drinking at follow-up, in Visitor Mindset the frequency was 19 (37.3%) and for control was 13 (23.2%). The odds ratio was 1.96 but was not significant (p=.11).

Discussion

We developed a single-session digital intervention for emotionally distressed adults, called Visitor Mindset. Then we conducted a randomized controlled trial comparing Visitor Mindset to a control intervention (i.e., videos of nature scenes paired with relaxing music) among emotionally distressed participants (n = 530) recruited from CloudResearch/M-Turk.

Participants rated Visitor Mindset favorably across several acceptability dimensions, and had significantly higher ratings than control for helpfulness and how engaging the intervention was. However, the control condition had significantly higher ratings for ease-of-use and visual appeal, suggesting these aspects of Visitor Mindset could be improved. All participants who began their intervention completed it. Although we estimated that Visitor Mindset would take 30 min to complete, very few participants (7.3%) completed Visitor Mindset in 30 min or less. Most participants (69.2%) completed Visitor Mindset in 60 min or less. Hence, Visitor Mindset took longer to complete than we predicted. A 60-minute single-session Visitor Mindset intervention may be viable as it may provide an effective balance between intervention dose and brevity. However, a 30-minute version, if equally as effective as the 60-minute version, would be more feasible. In our future work, we can review the components of the current Visitor Mindset intervention, and then decide which components to shorten or remove in order to create a 30-minute version. Because emotional acceptance and beliefs about the duration of negative emotions were significant mediators (see below), Visitor Mindset could be shortened to focus primarily on these mechanisms.

There was no significant difference between treatment conditions in changes in emotional distress; both conditions demonstrated significant, medium-sized reductions in emotional distress. These results suggest that the control condition, which involved observing many pleasant nature scenes with relaxing music over 30 min, may actually be helpful for relieving emotional distress. Indeed, other studies have found beneficial effects of nature images and/or relaxing music for reducing emotional distress80,81. It is possible that Visitor Mindset was too brief and perhaps a longer intervention (e.g., 90 min) would have a greater impact on emotional distress. Additionally, non-specific factors like expectation of benefit may have played a role in reductions in emotional distress in both groups.

Visitor Mindset did have a significant effect on secondary outcomes. As compared to the control condition, Visitor Mindset led to significantly greater reductions in functional impairment and drinks per week. These findings suggest that Visitor Mindset may be particularly useful for enhancing adaptive functioning and reducing maladaptive behaviors such as alcohol use. This is consistent with the focus of mindfulness- and acceptance-based interventions in facilitating conscious, value-guided responses to difficult emotions82.

Although the effect sizes are small in this study, they are consistent with other digital single-session interventions for depression and anxiety (~ 30–60 min), which also find small effects on average83–85. Importantly, a digital single-session intervention with small effects can still be valuable and have a substantial public health impact because it may be particularly accessible and can be widely implemented to a substantial percentage of the population. Essentially, interventions with small effects and strong public reach may have a similar public health benefit as interventions with moderate-to-large effects but low-to-moderate public reach. Self-guided interventions for depression and anxiety that offer a larger dose than single-session interventions, such as digital multi-session interventions12,86,87 and bibliotherapy88(e.g., a self-guided workbook with several chapters) tend to show medium effect sizes, rather than small. However, these interventions may be less accessible than a single-session intervention.

Exploratory analyses revealed that Visitor Mindset may have the most clinically significant effects on functional impairment. Participants who received Visitor Mindset were 61% more likely than control to shift from a clinical score at baseline to a subclinical score at 2-week follow-up for functional impairment. This finding suggests that Visitor Mindset may be particularly effective for attenuating the degree to which one’s mental health impairs daily functioning (e.g., ability to work, taking care of kids, engaging in leisure activities, maintaining close relationships). Given its focus on emotional acceptance, Visitor Mindset may help individuals tolerate mental health symptoms, such as anxiety and depression, which may facilitate their ability to actively engage in daily activities even if these symptoms are present.

Tests of clinical significance were not statistically significant for emotional distress and drinks per week. Visitor Mindset may not have enough alcohol-specific content. Future versions of Visitor Mindset could be tailored for hazardous drinking, with additional features such as learning and practicing acceptance of other important affective states like alcohol craving.

Notably, moderation analyses indicated that Visitor Mindset led to greater reductions in emotional distress than the control condition among a subset of participants reporting moderate or severe baseline emotional distress. It is possible that individuals with higher baseline distress may have had more “room” to reduce their symptoms, or they may have more frequent negative emotions and therefore more opportunities to apply skills learned from Visitor Mindset89. Importantly, Visitor Mindset al.so led to the most potent reductions in functional impairment among the subset of participants reporting moderate or severe baseline emotional distress. Taken together, these findings suggest that Visitor Mindset may be particularly suitable for individuals with at least moderate to severe emotional distress. This is consistent with studies that have found that mindfulness-based interventions are most efficacious among individuals with more severe symptoms89–92.

Changes in each of the emotion beliefs variables were significantly correlated with changes in each of the emotion regulation variables. This provides preliminary evidence that beliefs about emotions and emotional awareness, clarity, and acceptance interrelate and may function together to promote mental health. As compared to the control condition, Visitor Mindset led to significantly greater improvements in all mechanisms, including the belief that negative emotions are useless and last a long time, and awareness, clarity, and acceptance of negative emotions. This provides preliminary evidence that Visitor Mindset may be effective in mobilizing mechanisms of change—which is important for informing precision-based delivery of treatment21.

When conducting parallel mediation models with all mechanisms in the model, only acceptance and beliefs about the duration of negative emotions significantly mediated treatment effects on emotional distress and functioning, suggesting these may be the most critical mechanisms to target. The finding regarding acceptance as a mediator is consistent with research work suggesting that acceptance, rather than only awareness, is crucial for reducing aversive reactivity to emotions45,93,94. Beliefs about duration of negative emotions may have emerged as a mediator because it may have conceptual overlap with acceptance. That is, acceptance in part involves allowing negative emotions to rise and fall as they are, which may involve an implicit understanding that emotions are temporary. Of note, changes in the belief that negative emotions last a long time had a medium-sized inverse association (r=.35) with changes in acceptance, and this was the largest association for correlation tests between emotion belief variables and emotion regulation variables. It is possible that acceptance and the belief that negative emotions are temporary are key interrelated process that together promote positive change. The mediation findings suggest that it may be worthwhile to refine Visitor Mindset by prioritizing content on acceptance and beliefs about the duration of negative emotions.

In mediation models with drinks per week as the outcome, none of the mechanism variables were significant mediators. It may be the case that the mechanism variables in this study do not have a direct effect on drinking, but could possibly indirectly affect drinking via other variables, such as drinking to cope motives95 or relief craving96. Alternatively, given that the alcohol-related content in Visitor Mindset focused on motivation, it is possible that motivation to change—which was not measured—explained the reductions in drinking.

Awareness, clarity, and beliefs about the usefulness of negative emotions were not significant mediators, yet it is important to note that Visitor Mindset, compared to control, significantly improved these mechanisms. Hence, we found at least some evidence that Visitor Mindset impacts the targeted mechanisms. It is possible that paying attention to and understanding negative emotions may not be as essential as accepting and tolerating negative emotions45,93,94. For example, one can still notice and label negative emotions while also applying maladaptive coping skills, such as avoidance. Beliefs about the usefulness of negative emotions may have failed to mediate effects because the core theme and most of the content of Visitor Mindset focuses on beliefs about duration, while a relatively smaller amount of content related to beliefs about usefulness.

This study has several limitations. Notably, we recruited paid workers from M-Turk/ CloudResearch. Although studies show that this approach is viable, it is possible that participants were not attentive when completing assessments or the interventions. While we had some quality-control attention checks, participants still could have answered questions haphazardly and completed the intervention while carrying out other activities on their device. We did not assess the quality of responses to open-ended questions in the interventions, which is a limitation. Future studies should more closely examine the quality of responses to open-ended questions, such as the length of the written response or time spent writing, which would provide another indicator of engagement in the intervention. There were no live interactions, in-person or telehealth, between participants and study staff. This could have influenced results because it may have been easier for participants to complete the study with careless responding and lack of adequate engagement. It is possible that demand characteristics affected the study results. For example, based on the name of the intervention, i.e., Visitor Mindset, participants may have guessed that the purpose of the study was to change a person’s mindset towards negative emotions, and therefore, to be a “good participant,” may have exaggerated their responses to questionnaires that assessed beliefs about emotions. Alcohol-related intervention content was only in Visitor Mindset, not the control intervention. Thus, assessment reactivity could explain the reductions in drinking. Further, the current study only included a 2-week follow-up. This is a relatively short period, and it is not known whether treatment effects are durable over longer periods. Additionally, this study cannot elucidate the temporal ordering of mechanistic processes becauseoutcomes and mechanisms were measured concurrently. Another limitation is that we conducted many statistical tests, which increases the likelihood of finding false positives. And the use of listwise deletion for missing data is a limitation. However, it is important to note that missing data at the 2-week follow-up was minimal. Although Visitor Mindset included some brief content related to alcohol use and drinking to cope, we did not target recruitment to those with problematic alcohol use, and less than a quarter of participants reported hazardous alcohol use. Future research might examine the efficacy of Visitor Mindset in populations with hazardous drinking and alcohol use disorder and might examine coping motives as a mechanism of the intervention.

This study also has strengths. We created a novel single-session intervention that centers around a metaphor of negative emotions as visitors (not intruders), which supports the development of a new “visitor mindset” towards negative emotions. The focus on a single metaphor and a single mindset is unique. The simplicity and targeted focus on a Visitor Mindset may have been valuable to promote therapeutic change with a relatively small intervention dose. Our focus on beliefs about emotions is also unique. Generally, there is limited work on interventions—single-session or multiple-session— that have a strong and targeted focus on beliefs about negative emotions. Our study thus provides novel findings that emotion-related beliefs may be important processes to modify via behavioral interventions. Another strength of this study is that we conducted parallel mediation models with all putative mechanism variables. This allowed us to assess which mechanisms may be the most important in driving intervention effects. We also conducted moderation analyses which allowed us to assess whom Visitor Mindset may be most effective for. Finally, this study included a relatively large sample.

In conclusion, this study did not find a significant effect of Visitor Mindset on changes in emotional distress. However our study provides preliminary evidence that Visitor Mindset may be feasible and acceptable, may improve functioning and reduce alcohol use, may exert its therapeutic effects via changes in acceptance of and beliefs about the duration of negative emotions, and may be most effective among individuals with moderate-to-severe (versus mild) emotional distress. Future work might test this intervention among a sample that is recruited through different means than M-Turk/CloudResearch, such as emotionally distressed adults in the community recruited via social media. Visitor Mindset could also be evaluated as an adjunct among individuals enrolled in treatment for anxiety disorders or depression. Future studies can test the effects of Visitor Mindset on outcomes at follow-ups longer than 2 weeks (e.g., 3 months). Finally, future research might involve the development and evaluation of a brief (not just a single-session) version of Visitor Mindset (e.g., four weekly 30-minute modules), which may also be a valuable and accessible format of the intervention. Multiple sessions would provide a greater dose of the intervention and could have a stronger effect on mechanisms and outcomes. Visitor Mindset has potential to be implemented in a range of setting as an adjunctive mental health resource, such as mental health clinics, medical environments, and schools. Providers or teachers could refer individuals to a website to complete Visitor Mindset for free. Future research can study these possibilities for implementation. Overall, Visitor Mindset is a promising novel digital intervention warranting further investigation.
