The role of endoscope-assisted septectomy and membranectomy for complex chronic subdural hematomas: safety, efficacy, technical feasibility. Patient series
Authors: Mehdi Khaleghi, Adnan Hussain Shahid, Osama Elkadi, Sudhir Suggala, Garrett Dyes, Asa Record Barnett, Danner Butler, Ursula Noelle Hummel, Jai Deep Thakur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDBurr hole craniostomy and conventional craniotomy are respectively associated with high recurrence rates and morbidities in the evacuation of septated chronic subdural hematomas (CSDHs). The safety and efficacy of endoscope-assisted septectomy and membranectomy (ESM) via a mini-craniotomy are investigated in this subset of patients.OBSERVATIONSThirteen patients with a mean age of 75 ± 11.5 years and a median modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 4 were included. Surgeries had a mean craniotomy size of 3.6 ± 0.5 cm and a median duration of 142 minutes. Initial postoperative CT imaging confirmed complete hematoma evacuation. Six patients (46.2%) underwent perioperative middle meningeal artery embolization (MMAE) without complications. The mean length of hospital stay (LOS) was 8 ± 3.9 days. Lower preoperative Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores (p = 0.049) and larger midline shifts (p = 0.024) were significantly associated with LOS > 5 days. Median mRS scores significantly improved at discharge (p < 0.015) and at the last follow-up (p < 0.009), yielding a low-tier mRS score (≤ 2) in 53.8% of patients at discharge and 92.3% favorable last follow-up outcomes. A higher admission GCS score was associated with a low-tier at-discharge mRS score (p = 0.048). Complete brain reexpansion occurred within a mean time of 6.2 ± 4.1 months, with no significant correlation with radiological and operative features or MMAE history. During a mean follow-up of 17.4 ± 13.4 months, no recurrences were observed.LESSONSESM is an effective, minimally invasive alternative for treating septated, multiloculated CSDH, offering minimal recurrences. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, controversies persist regarding the approach of choice for symptomatic chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH). This is particularly important in older patients, given the 11%–21% recurrence rate associated with standard techniques, as well as subsequent hospital readmissions, repeat surgeries, and complications.1,2 Surgical management of liquefied, homogeneous hematomas is straightforward using conventional twist-drill craniostomy, with or without surgical irrigation port systems, or burr hole craniostomy (BHC).3 However, using such techniques is less effective in septated and multiloculated hematomas, where poor access to neomembranes and limited visualization of organized clots come with increased risk of hematoma recollection.4,5 Middle meningeal artery embolization (MMAE) has recently gained attention as an emerging adjunct, with a probable role in decreasing recurrence rates by targeting neovascularization.6–8 Nevertheless, its effectiveness in complex hematoma morphologies has remained under investigation.

In this context, craniotomy, with or without membranectomy, has been proposed for better addressing neomembranes and trabeculations, identifying microbleeding sources, and evacuating compartmentalized clots, thereby practically reducing reaccumulation risks. Rigid9–12 or flexible13 endoscopic visualization is increasingly being used as an adjunct, although the surgical nuances and feasibility of endoscopic evacuation in multicompartment CSDH through mini-craniotomy are not well detailed, likely due to the limited adoption of these methods. We thus conducted a single-center study to specifically evaluate the safety, efficacy, and complication profile of endoscope-assisted septectomy and membranectomy (ESM) in a cohort of patients with septated CSDH during the early years of academic neurosurgical practice.

Study Description

Study Design and Participants

Demographic, clinical, and radiological data of consecutive patients with CSDH who underwent ESM at our academic medical center between July 2020 and April 2025 were retrospectively analyzed. The decision to perform ESM was based on preoperative CT findings indicating a septated, nonhomogeneous, nonhypodense hematoma in symptomatic patients. Preoperative classification relied on CT imaging due to its accessibility and adequacy for identifying such hematomas, with MRI reserved for cases requiring further characterization of septa or clot complexity, although not utilized in this cohort.

Included were patients who met all three of the following criteria: 1) radiological evidence of multiloculated or septated CSDH with heterogeneous or iso-/hyperdense components with > 5-mm midline shift or ipsilateral sulcus effacement on noncontrast CT, 2) neurological symptoms attributable to mass effect (e.g., contralateral pronator drift, hemiparesis, or seizures), and 3) absence of coagulopathy or uncontrolled systemic illness precluding surgery.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) simple, homogeneously hypodense CSDH suitable for conventional BHC; 2) subdural hematomas with major acute components; 3) concurrent intracranial pathology requiring craniotomy for other indications; and 4) poor medical condition contraindicating general anesthesia.

Intraoperative endoscopic observations were used for determining hematoma characteristics, including fibrous septations, outer and inner membranes, unstable bridging veins, and active bleeding points. Perioperative prophylactic MMAE was adopted in patients with a high risk of recurrence, such as those with a midline shift > 5 mm, unstable membranes, and highly trabeculated cases, as an adjunct, according to a joint decision between attending neurosurgeons and the neurointerventionist. Recurrence was defined as either symptomatic hematoma reaccumulation or radiological enlargement of a persistent hematoma within 6 months of the index surgery. In cases of bilateral CSDH, the side with the larger measure was used for analysis. Postoperative outcomes were assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at hospital discharge and at the last follow-up visit. Patients were categorized into low-tier (mRS score ≤ 2) and high-tier (mRS score > 2) scores. An unfavorable outcome was defined as any increase in the last follow-up mRS score directly related to surgery. A brief literature review of endoscope-assisted mini-craniotomy case series involving > 10 patients was added to contextualize our results.

Technical Nuances

After inducing general anesthesia, patients are positioned supine with the head placed on a horseshoe headrest and rotated 45° away from the hematoma side. The ipsilateral shoulder is supported with a bolster to optimize the exposure. A linear skin incision is made slightly medial to the superior temporal line, centered over the hematoma’s greatest thickness. After subgaleal dissection and elevation of the pericranium, a roughly 3- to 4-cm frontoparietal mini-craniotomy is fashioned. The dura is coagulated and opened in a cruciate manner to open the outer membrane (OM) and initially decompress the chronic or subacute hematoma component. After irrigating the cavity with copious warm saline, a 4-mm 0° rigid endoscope is first introduced anteriorly (frontal) for initial inspection and removal of solid clot. Fibrous septations are released using a combination of up-angled endoscopic bipolar forceps and low-profile microscissors to facilitate communication between loculated hematoma pockets and the subdural space. Bleeding bridging veins are coagulated and severed under direct visualization. While protecting cortical vessels, the inner membrane (IM) is gently grasped upward, followed by a “partial” membranectomy. Multiple slash incisions are applied on IM, aiming to encourage earlier brain reexpansion.

Minor oozing is controlled with continuous irrigation. Subsequently, the OM is examined for friability and bleeding points. Areas with unstable features are cauterized using straight endo-bipolar forceps and removed with microforceps (Fig. 1, Videos 1 and 2) Similar steps are performed for posterior (parieto-occipital) membranectomy. Overaggressive thin IM or stable OM peeling, particularly in the posterior corner, is avoided to prevent iatrogenic fresh bleeding. Deep membranes and hemorrhagic points are addressed using a 30° angle to ensure better access to bleeding vessels, membranous adhesion, and organized clots in dependent corners without applying cortical traction. Both IM and OM are sent for histological examinations. Hemostasis is achieved using warm saline irrigation, hydrogen peroxide, and topical hemostatic agents. Areas over the membrane deemed at risk for postoperative bleeding are reinforced with Gelfoam, thrombin strips, and fibrin glue. A subdural drain is placed under direct endoscopic visualization, typically toward the posterior dependent (parieto-occipital) region. In frontotemporal cases, the tip is directed anteriorly to maximize fluid capture. The subdural space is filled with saline to reduce pneumocephalus, followed by approximating the dura using a dural graft as necessary. The bone flap is secured with a cranial plate system, and the incision is closed in layers. A postoperative CT scan is obtained on the same day to assess the adequacy of evacuation and identify any complications. Drains are typically kept in place for 3–5 days, with an extension if the fluid appears serosanguineous. Patients are instructed to remain flat for 48 hours postoperatively to promote brain reexpansion, followed by gradual mobilization.

Statistical Analysis

Mean with standard deviation and median with interquartile range are used to report continuous variables. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage. A chi-square test was conducted for categorical comparisons. The nonparametric Mann-Whitney U-test was applied to compare nonnormally distributed continuous data between two independent groups. Pre- and postoperative mRS scores were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27, where a p value < 0.05 was assumed significant.

Demographic and Preoperative Clinical-Radiological Results

The study included 13 patients, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.6:1. The mean age was 75 ± 11.5 years (median 77 years, IQR 68–85.5 years, range 49–88 years). Ten patients (76.9%) had a prior minor head trauma within the preceding 3 months, mostly due to a ground-level fall (9 patients, 69.2%), and 1 patient secondary to repeated assault. Two cases (15.4%) were recurrent CSDH following surgery performed by other surgeons—one after BHC and MMAE with subsequent trauma, and one due to reexpansion of a persistent post-BHC hematoma. Use of an anticoagulant (AC) or antiplatelet (AP) agent was noted in 76.9% of patients: 6 (46.2%) were on AP therapy (aspirin/dual therapy), and 4 (30.8%) were on direct oral AC (apixaban or rivaroxaban) therapy. The most frequent presenting symptoms other than headaches were contralateral limb weakness (5/13, 38.5%), altered sensorium (3/13, 23.1%), or both (4/13, 30.8%). Four patients (30.8%) had dysphasia or aphasia, and 1 presented solely with headache, but examination revealed a subtle focal neurological deficit (unilateral pronator drift). The median baseline Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and mRS scores were 14 (range 11–15) and 4 (range 3–5), respectively. Initial CT scans showed holohemispheric CSDH in 11 patients (84.6%) and frontotemporal CSDH in 2. Bilateral hematomas were seen in 2 cases (15.4%). Ten patients (76.9%) had acute hematoma components (Hounsfield units > 60). The mean maximum hematoma thickness and midline shift were 23.2 ± 6.7 mm (range 14.8–37 mm) and 9.4 ± 3.2 mm (range 5.4–16.6 mm), respectively (Table 1).

Operative Characteristics

The mean craniotomy size was 3.6 ± 0.5 cm (range 3.1–4.5 cm). Surgeries had a median duration of 142 minutes (IQR 86–199 minutes, range 57–470 minutes), and the mean estimated blood loss was 56.5 ± 63.8 mL. Two patients had bilateral CSDH, of whom one underwent bilateral ESM with a surgery time of 470 minutes and blood loss of 250 mL. Subdural drain tips were placed posteriorly (parieto-occipital) in 84.6% of patients, while 2 patients with localized frontotemporal hematomas received anterior (frontal) drain placement. Rapid intraoperative brain expansion that could have impeded endoscopic insertion and membranectomy did not develop in any patients.

On postoperative day 1, GCS scores ranged from 9 to 15 (median 14, IQR 14–15), with 6 patients (46.2%) scoring 15 and 5 patients (38.5%) scoring 14. Most patients (84.6%) showed early consciousness and motor functional recovery within 24 hours after surgery. Initial postoperative CT scans confirmed complete evacuation of the hematoma in all cases. The mean maximum postoperative pneumocephalus thickness was 17.8 ± 8.6 mm (range 1.6–29.1 mm), and the mean midline shift was 5.2 ± 2.51 mm (range 1–9.8 mm), with progressive resolution in subsequent imaging. Six patients (46.2%) underwent perioperative prophylactic MMAE; 1 additional patient had a failed MMAE attempt due to a torturous carotid artery from fibromuscular dysplasia. The mean subdural drain duration was 3.6 ± 1.3 days (range 2–6 days), and the mean length of hospital stay (LOS) was 8 ± 3.9 days (range 3–16 days, median 7 days, IQR 5–11 days). Lower preoperative GCS scores (p = 0.049) and larger preoperative midline shifts (9.7 mm [IQR 9–11.1 mm] vs 6 mm, p = 0.024) were significantly associated with LOS > 5 days (Fig. 2). In-hospital mortality occurred in 1 patient (7.7%) following intractable seizures and respiratory failure. One patient with multiple comorbidities (hematological and breast malignancies, uremia) died from sepsis and multiorgan failure unrelated to surgery during early postoperative rehabilitation. No other surgical morbidity, such as infection, acute rebleeding, intracranial hypotension, or wound complications, was observed (Table 1).

Postoperative Outcomes

Median mRS scores improved significantly from 4 preoperatively to 2 (IQR 1–3, p < 0.015) at discharge and 1 (IQR 0–3, p < 0.009) at the last follow-up. An mRS score ≤ 2 was observed in 7 patients (53.8%) at discharge and 75% at the last follow-up. Twelve of 13 patients (92.3%) achieved favorable outcomes at the last follow-up. A higher admission GCS score was significantly associated with a low-tier mRS score at discharge (p = 0.048). Discharge mRS scores improved in 10 patients (76.9%), worsened in 2 (related to surgery in 1 patient), and remained unchanged in 1 at the last follow-up, yielding an mRS score of 0 (asymptomatic) in 6 of 12 patients (50%) and an mRS score of 2 in 3 patients (25%). Low-tier last follow-up mRS scores were observed in 100% of patients without a history of MMAE versus 40% in those with (p = 0.018), although the difference was not significant in at-discharge scores (71.4% vs 33.3%, p = 0.17). All patients with available 2-week postoperative CT imaging showed nonprogressive thin subdural hygroma, which resolved on subsequent surveillance. One neurologically stable patient had transient hygroma enlargement with increased midline shift at 2 weeks, which returned to baseline in 1 month and resolved fully by 12 months. The mean time for complete brain reexpansion (i.e., subdural hygroma resolution) was 6.2 ± 4.1 months (median 5 months, range 2–12 months). No significant correlation was found between reexpansion time and any of the following variables: age, preoperative GCS or mRS score, hematoma thickness, midline shift, MMAE history, craniotomy size, operative time, blood loss volume, pneumocephalus size, or drain duration (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). During a mean follow-up of 17.4 ± 13.4 months (range 0–35 months), no recurrences were observed.

Informed Consent

The necessary informed consent was obtained in this study.

Discussion

Observations

Despite advancements in operative techniques and neuroanesthesia care, symptomatic CSDH recurrences requiring reoperation remain a significant challenge.1,3,14 This is particularly pronounced in the older population with associated risk factors such as frequent falls, AC or AP use, cerebral atrophy, and other comorbidities, which contribute to a chronic disease course. While BHC is the standard of care in most centers, it is less effective for septated or trabecular hematomas, where unnoticed solid clots distant from the burr hole view and unstable membranes may lead to ongoing fibrinolysis, osmotic expansion, and relapse.15 In our institutional experience, these lesions typically demonstrate incomplete resolution following BHC due to complex internal septations or organized membranes. Advancing the irrigation tube blindly is unsafe and may endanger fragile bridging veins and cortical vessels. Craniotomy has historically been used to address complex clots, offering lower recurrence rates than BHC and twist-drill craniostomy.16,17 However, its associated morbidity has prompted the development of less invasive techniques, such as mini-craniotomy with or without endoscopic assistance. Although several endoscopic BHC series have yielded satisfactory results, this study focused on the technical nuances and early outcomes associated with ESM through mini-craniotomy in managing septated, multiloculated CSDHs.

Role of Septectomy and Membranectomy

The formation of neomembranes in CSDH begins with an initial subdural hemorrhage, often from bridging vein rupture, triggering an inflammatory response in the dural border cells.18 This leads to proliferation of dural fibroblasts and macrophages, forming the OM within days, characterized by neovascularization with fragile capillaries that promotes recurrent microbleeds and exudation.15,19 The IM develops later from arachnoid adhesion and fibroblast ingrowth, acting as a semipermeable barrier that sustains osmotic gradients and fibrinolysis, facilitating hematoma expansion via kallikrein-kinin system activation.20,21 In septated CSDH, fibrous septations arise from organized clots and persistent inflammation, compartmentalizing the hematoma and enhancing recurrence. From a technical standpoint, two critical factors must be addressed to reduce hematoma reaccumulation risk: 1) meticulous removal of multiloculated organized clots and fibrous septations, along with secure control of bleeding bridging veins or friable OMs under direct visualization, and 2) expediting brain reexpansion to obliterate dead space and minimize traction on friable bridging veins without iatrogenic cortical retraction. This may occur in homogeneous, mildly septated CSDHs using BHC, whereas multiloculated hematomas warrant precise septectomy and IM release, all of which can be achieved under endoscopic illumination. Although MRI provides a more accurate assessment of hematoma and septa thickness, a thorough analysis of CT scans to identify strips and columns can guide surgical planning in favor of endoscopic techniques.22 The optimal management of IMs and OMs is still debated, given the controversial effect on functional outcomes and concerns about risks associated with longer surgery.23 Our median operative time was 142 minutes, similar to the 40- to 157.5-minute reported range in endoscopic series with or without membranectomy (Table 2). Evidence from previous craniotomy cohorts supports the role of inner membranectomy in recurrence prevention.9,23 The IM, although avascular, typically adheres to the arachnoid surface and acts as a key limiting barrier for brain reexpansion18,24 (Fig. 3).

Despite prolonging the anesthesia time, careful septectomy and inner membranectomy have decreased the recurrence rates to below 5% in the most recent studies, regardless of hematoma type. No recent endoscopic cohort has determined complete brain reexpansion times; however, this occurred with a median of 5 months in our study, with the most rapid recovery occurring within the 1st postoperative month. Notably, a reexpansion rate of ≤ 40% and a complex hematoma type were key predictors of recurrence in the study of Han et al.25 However, the latter was not reflected in our pure-septated/multiloculated hematoma series, which had 0% recurrence. Although earlier studies correlated a midline shift > 10 mm with lower brain reexpansion,5 we observed no association between demographic variables, MMAE history, or radiological features and reexpansion rates, which may be attributed to our small cohort. Conversely, the OM, which contains prominent neovasculature with permeable endothelial gap junctions,19 plays a central role in ongoing inflammation, fibrinolysis, osmotic fluid transits, and thus silent reaccumulation.20,21 Although a meta-analysis on membranectomy cases showed lower recurrence rates with comparable morbidity and mortality rates to the nonmembranectomy group,23 complete OM removal has not consistently eliminated recurrence. Singh et al. reported a 1.47% recurrence rate using both inner and outer membranectomy, although this was associated with the longest operative time (157.5 minutes) in our review.14 In our approach, we adopted a selective strategy, involving gentle peeling of thin IM versus partial removal/multiple slash relaxing incisions for thick IM rather than complete inner membranectomy, considering its lesser effectiveness on the natural history and potential risk to cortical tissue. We typically preserve healthy OM to minimize early postoperative rebleeding, while removing or coagulating only friable and hemorrhagic segments to address the exudative process.

Endoscopic Approach Advantages and Challenges

Despite advancements in endoscopic techniques for intraventricular and skull base exposures,26–30 the impact on functional outcomes of patients with CSDH is still under evaluation, given potential learning curves. Two meta-analyses demonstrated lower recurrence rates in endoscope-assisted surgery versus conventional BHC (3.1% vs 13.8%).31,32 We found endoscopy helpful in opening noncommunicating pockets, destroying neomembrane, irrigating deep isolated hematomas in corners or the “membrane horizon,” and accurately placing drain tips, all of which collectively contribute to low recurrence rates.3,9 The most recent series used rigid endoscopes for improved illumination and safe manipulation, a notable advantage over flexible scopes.33,34 Yadav et al. reported safe clot removal in cases with rapid intraoperative brain expansion using a 1-cm silicon brain retractor centered over the hematoma’s frontal tip.10 Instead, we tend to create a similar-sized mini-craniotomy over the thickest hematoma, where a retractorless freedom of work is less affected by rapid brain reexpansion. Endoscopic assistance was particularly beneficial for acute clots, which accounted for 76.9% of our cases. Yokosuka et al. applied endoscopic evacuation of acute and subacute hematomas, achieving 87% hematoma evacuation and a 9% rebleeding rate.11 They concluded that preoperative GCS scores are predictive of early outcomes, a factor also shown to be the only determinant of low-tier at-discharge mRS scores in our study. Similarly, in their study, Egemen et al. reported a 90.5% favorable outcome rate in all types of CSDH, despite omitting membranectomy, marking the preoperative Markwalder neurological grade as the sole predictor.35 Our favorable outcome rate was 92.3% at the final follow-up, reflecting the minimal long-term morbidity associated with ESM. Nevertheless, 2D visualization, brain laceration risk, inaccessibility due to rapid brain reexpansion, and a learning curve that may extend operative time are downsides of the endoscopic approach. Our median operative time (142 minutes) was longer than those reported in the series by Májovský et al.34 and Mobbs and Khong,4 where < 50% of patients underwent ESM. Still, we believe the benefits of avoiding conversion to craniotomy and reoperation in older patients outweigh these concerns. We place the subdural drain posteriorly (dependent) in holohemispheric CSDHs (84.6% in this study), a strategy that may reduce recurrence and minimize the risk of anterior misplacement.36,37

Analysis of Outcomes

The midline shift decreased in all patients, and 84.6% recovered consciousness and motor function within 24 hours. The mean LOS was 8 ± 3.9 days (median 7 days), consistent with the 4–10 days reported in the literature. While extended hospital LOS could increase overall hospital expenses, similar to the 25% higher costs observed with a bedside port system (compared to BHC) due to comorbidities in the study of Mohan et al.,38 ESM’s 0% recurrence rates over a mean of 17.4 months and our very low postoperative seizure rate (7.7%) may offset these by averting reoperations and improving quality of life, which contribute substantially to CSDH-related morbidity and annual US healthcare costs.1,6 Lower preoperative GCS score and larger midline shift were associated with longer LOS. While these variables are known to affect recurrence and functional outcomes in larger series,39 their impact on LOS in endoscopic cases, which could be a topic in family consultation, has not been investigated before. Our perioperative mortality and complication rates were 7.7%, which is close to those of the endoscopic series, ranging from 0% to 1.47% and 0% to 5.3%, respectively. At discharge, 76.9% showed mRS improvement, and favorable outcomes reached 92.3% at the final follow-up. However, the small cohort size limits comparisons with larger series, which underscores the need for cautious interpretation and larger studies to confirm ESM’s efficacy in septated CSDH. Yan et al.5 reported a 96.1% favorable outcome, a 5.3% complication rate, and a shorter mean operative time (76.9 ± 16.3 minutes), concluding lower medical resource consumption. Although only OM coagulation was performed in this cohort, the authors found lower recurrence rates than BHC.

Recurrence rates varied between 0% and 10.5% in the reviewed series, 3 of which investigated hematomas of all types. Priyadharshan et al. reported the highest recurrence rate (10.5% during 6 months) with pure inner membranectomy, possibly due to a lack of OM management.9 These rates are lower than the 13.7%–24.5% rates reported in conventional BHC.5,40 Poor brain expansion, thick membranes, persistent pneumocephalus or midline shift, and AC or AP use are known recurrence risk factors.39,41 Noteworthy, AC or AP use in our cohort was 77%, higher than the 2.9%–51% range in comparable studies (Table 2).

No major complications, such as disabling stroke, occurred in our perioperative prophylactic MMAE group. With 46.2% of patients receiving MMAE, its role as a confounder is notable. Subgroup analysis revealed a significant association between the low-tier last follow-up mRS scores with an MMAE history (40% vs 100% of those without), although at-discharge rates were not significantly different (33.3% vs 71.4%). This may reflect selection bias and confounding by indication rather than a detrimental effect of embolization itself, as MMAE was used in more complex cases with larger midline shifts (> 5 mm), more complex or organized hematomas, or antithrombotic use (77% of the cohort), who inherently carried a higher baseline risk of poor recovery. Although MMAE did not correlate with reexpansion or functional improvement in our series, its possible effect on decreasing recurrence rates by targeting OM neovascularization and inflammation7 should be further investigated in larger studies. Schwarz et al. reported a 4.5% recurrence rate with postoperative prophylactic MMAE, although they did not clarify the proportion of septated hematomas.42 Similarly, the randomized controlled trial by Fiorella et al. showed a 16% recurrence in the embolization group versus 36% in standard treatment alone.43 Whether this novel modality has any benefits on functional recovery in ESM cases needs further exploration.

Limitations

Our small sample size limits statistical power and precludes definitive conclusions, particularly for detecting rare complications or performing robust subgroup analyses (e.g., MMAE impact). The retrospective single-center nature and lack of a control group reduce the generalizability of findings and may introduce selection bias and overestimate favorable outcomes or underestimate complications. While our 0% recurrence rate aligns with other reported rates of 0%–10.5%, these findings are exploratory rather than confirmatory and thus warrant validation in larger multicenter randomized trials. Although our results should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size and potential confounders such as MMAE, this study may improve the current insights into the technical feasibility and outcomes of ESM through mini-craniotomy, particularly relevant to early-career practice and for educational purposes.

Lessons

ESM offers an effective, minimally invasive alternative for treating septated, multiloculated CSDH. The approach achieved desirable brain reexpansion, minimal recurrence, and favorable functional recovery in complex hematoma morphologies. Although operative times are longer, the potential benefits—reduced reoperation rates and improved safety—justify this strategy. Randomized prospective studies are warranted to validate the long-term efficacy of ESM in the MMAE era.
