FBLN2 promoter hypermethylation: a negative prognostic biomarker and anti-PD-1 response predictor in clear cell renal cell carcinoma via high-throughput CpG Island screening
Authors: Wenfeng Liao, Lianzi Yu, Lufang Zhang, Jie Shao, Guanhua Li, Jialei Hua, Shuya Zhao, Li Jia, Yawei Han, Aimin Zhang, Xin Yao, Yueguo Li, Dong Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), with rising incidence globally, shows heterogeneous responses to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). DNA methylation dysregulation impacts tumor immunity, necessitating predictive CpG methylation biomarkers for ICB efficacy.MethodsGenome-wide methylation profiling (TCGA/GEO) identified prognostic promoter CpG islands linked to CD8⁺ T cell infiltration. Single-CpG resolution analysis guided TaqMan probe design, validated in two independent cohorts: non-ICB (n = 335) for prognosis and ICB-treated (n = 45) for immunotherapy prediction.ResultsHigh-throughput methylation analysis identified hypermethylation at the FBLN2 promoter (chr3:13590414–13591008), which correlated with advanced tumor stage, higher grade, and poorer overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). This locus exhibited significant associations with CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and IFNγ pathway activation. In the non-ICB cohort, validation using a 5-CpG TaqMan assay confirmed FBLN2 hypermethylation as an independent prognostic marker for both OS (HR = 3.45, P < 0.001) and PFS (HR = 2.43, P = 0.023). Subsequent IHC analysis of 74 tumor specimens demonstrated strong concordance between FBLN2 percent methylation ratio (PMR) and CD8⁺ T cell density (r = 0.62, P < 0.0001). In the anti-PD-1-treated cohort, high FBLN2 methylation was associated with a favorable immunotherapy response (Cohen’s weighted Kappa = 0.607, P < 0.001) and predicted superior PFS (median 11.2 vs 3.4 months, HR = 0.35, P = 0.006), suggesting its potential as a novel predictive biomarker for ICB efficacy.ConclusionsFBLN2 promoter hypermethylation is a promising prognostic biomarker in ccRCC and a potential predictor of immunotherapy response. These findings warrant further investigation to validate its potential for enhancing personalized patient management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-01981-2.Keywords: Methylation, CpG Island, FBLN2, Clear cell renal cell carcinoma, Prognosis, Immunotherapy response predictor

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC), the most common malignant tumor of the kidney, shows a rising global incidence [1]. Clear cell RCC (ccRCC), accounting for 75–80% of RCC cases, is often diagnosed at an advanced stage [2]. Approximately 20–30% of patients present with initial distant metastases, and another 30% relapse post-nephrectomy, particularly those with locally advanced disease [2]. Metastatic ccRCC has a poor prognosis, with a five-year survival rate of roughly 10% [1]. Significant progress has been made in treating advanced ccRCC, with the introduction of targeted therapies like tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 [3]. However, the lack of reliable biomarkers to predict treatment efficacy is a significant challenge. Traditional risk stratification methods based on the TNM staging system and morphologic grading are inadequate, as patients with similar features can have vastly different responses to therapy [4]. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify novel molecular markers to predict treatment benefits, especially for immunotherapies, to enable more personalized and effective therapeutic strategies.

One approach to addressing this challenge is by classifying tumors based on their immune cell infiltration and immune response within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Hot tumors, characterized by high immune cell infiltration and a high mutational burden, are often referred to as “immune-inflamed” and are more likely to respond to immunotherapy, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors [5]. These tumors exhibit increased interferon-γ (IFN-γ) signaling, abundant immune cell infiltration, and the presence of immune-activating chemokines and antigen-presenting cells [6, 7]. In contrast, cold tumors, also known as “immune-desert” or “immune-excluded” tumors, have minimal immune cell infiltration and low immunogenicity, making them resistant to immunotherapy [8]. These tumors often lack pre-existing anti-tumor immunity and fail to attract immune cells, resulting in poor response to immunotherapies [8]. Assessing the hot or cold status of tumors can therefore serve as a predictive marker for the efficacy of immunotherapy, guiding treatment decisions and improving patient outcomes. For instance, in ccRCC, the immune landscape can vary significantly, with some tumors exhibiting a hot phenotype and others a cold phenotype [9]. Understanding this classification is crucial for selecting appropriate therapies, as hot tumors are more likely to benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors, while cold tumors may require additional strategies to enhance immune cell infiltration.

Epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation, play a crucial role in regulating gene expression without altering the DNA sequence. In ccRCC, DNA methylation patterns are often disrupted, with global hypomethylation and CpG island hypermethylation [10]. Hypermethylation of CpG islands in the promoter regions of key immune-related genes is a primary mechanism of immune evasion [11]. For instance, the silencing of genes encoding chemokines (e.g., CXCL family molecules) and adhesion molecules effectively removes the signaling pathways required to recruit cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells into the tumor bed [12]. This process contributes directly to the formation of an immunologically ‘cold’ or ‘immune-excluded’ microenvironment, rendering the tumor resistant to immune attack. Conversely, focal hypomethylation can paradoxically bolster anti-tumor immunity. The demethylation and subsequent re-expression of endogenous retroviral elements (ERVs) can create novel tumor antigens and trigger a ‘viral mimicry’ response [13]. This activates innate immune sensing pathways, leading to the production of type I interferons and fostering a pro-inflammatory, or ‘hot,’ tumor microenvironment that is more susceptible to immunotherapy. Moreover, DNA methylation plays a crucial role in regulating the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, which in turn affects T cell activity [14]. Thus, CpG island methylation profiles indicate tumor aggressiveness and have potential as predictive markers for immune infiltration and immunotherapy efficacy.

Given the pivotal role of DNA methylation in ccRCC progression and its association with immune cell infiltration, this study aims to identify novel CpG island methylation biomarkers that can predict responses to immunotherapy. We employed high-throughput screening of genome-wide methylation data from publicly available databases to systematically analyze CpG islands significantly correlated with immune cell infiltration, particularly CD8⁺ T cells, and patient prognosis. Our approach integrates large-scale methylation data with clinical outcomes and immune infiltration metrics to identify candidate CpG islands associated with immune cell dynamics in the tumor microenvironment, hypothesizing that the methylation status of such an island could reliably predict treatment efficacy and survival. The identification of such a marker would help address the biomarker gap, offer new insights into ccRCC molecular mechanisms, and contribute to the goal of enhancing immunotherapy precision.

Materials and methods

Patients and specimens

This study included a retrospective cohort of 335 ccRCC patients who underwent surgery at the Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (TMUCIH) between January 2012 and April 2022. This cohort, hereafter referred to as the TMUCIH Non-ICB Cohort, was used for prognostic validation. All specimens were confirmed by pathologic examination, and tumors were staged according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification. Patient follow-up was conducted via clinic visits or telephone interviews every 4–6 months to monitor disease status and record survival data. The follow-up period ranged from 6 to 80 months, with a data cutoff of April 10, 2022.

To assess the predictive value of target CpG island methylation for immunotherapy efficacy, we established the TMUCIH ICB Cohort, comprising 45 patients with metastatic ccRCC treated at TMUCIH between July 2018 and October 2023. These patients received either anti-PD-1 monotherapy (as second-line treatment post-TKI) or first-line anti-PD-1-based combination therapy. Clinical endpoints included response to ICB, evaluated using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) v.1.1 [15], and progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from ICB initiation to objective tumor progression or death.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Public data preprocessing

Methylation data (Illumina HumanMethylation450 BeadChip), mRNA expression data (TPM, transcript per million), and associated clinical data for ccRCC were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA-KIRC cohort) via the GDC Data Portal for the initial training set. Additionally, public methylation and expression profile datasets GSE105260, GSE113501, GSE105288, and GSE22563 were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database for validation. Methylation data were processed in the R statistical programming environment as below: i) samples with > 20% missing probes were excluded; ii) probes with > 10% missing data across samples were excluded; iii) remaining missing values (NAs) were imputed using the impute package; iv) all methylation data were then normalized using the limma package.

DNA extraction, quantification, and bisulfite conversion

Genomic DNA was extracted from tissue samples by using a commercial DNA extraction kit (Promega, Madison, WI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quantity and purity were assessed by Nanodrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Genomic DNA (200 ng) was bisulfite-converted using the EZ DNA-Methylation Gold kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Control DNA treated with methyltransferase

To create a fully methylated control, genomic DNA from peripheral blood leukocytes (Promega, Madison, WI) was treated with CpG methyltransferase (M.SssI, Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) at a concentration of 1 U/μg overnight at 37 °C. To ensure complete methylation, M.SssI was added a second time after an initial 2-h incubation. The sample was then bisulfite-converted as described above.

MethyLight assays and bisulfite sequencing PCR

Sequence information for the primers and probes is listed in Additional file 1. Probes were labeled either with a black hole quencher (FBLN2) or a minor groove binder non-fluorescent quencher (Alu repeat). PCR for MethyLight assays was performed as described previously [16]. The percent of methylated reference (PMR) was calculated by dividing the FBLN2/ALU ratio of a sample by the FBLN2/ALU ratio of the M.SssI-treated control DNA and multiplying by 100. All samples were tested in duplicate. Bisulfite sequencing PCR analysis was conducted as described previously [17].

Demethylation assay

Human RCC cell lines 786-O and Caki-1 (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Beijing, China) were treated with 10 µM 5-aza-2-deoxycytidine (5-Aza-dC, Sigma-Aldrich) for 3 days. The medium, containing 5-Aza-dC or 0.1% DMSO (vehicle control), was replaced every 24 h. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed as previously described [18]. Primer sequences are provided in Additional file 1.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

CD8⁺ T cell infiltration was measured using IHC on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked, and sections were incubated with a primary anti-CD8 antibody (1:100, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) overnight at 4 °C. CD8⁺ T cell infiltrate scores were determined by a pathologist blinded to the methylation data. Staining was scored semi-quantitatively based on the density of positive cells as: Negative (0), Weak (1), Moderate (2), or Strong (3), as described previously [19].

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance for comparisons between two groups was determined using the Mann–Whitney U-test. For comparisons among three or more groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. Associations were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median PMR value or an optimal cutoff determined by the survminer R package. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to identify independent prognostic factors. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with the log-rank test was used to compare survival differences. The agreement between radiographic response and methylation levels was assessed using Cohen’s weighted (Fleiss–Cohen weights) kappa coefficient. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Integrative bioinformatic screening identifies FBLN2 promoter methylation as a prognostic and predictive candidate in ccRCC

To identify CpG islands with prognostic and potential predictive value, our multi-step bioinformatic screening process utilized the TCGA-KIRC cohort as the training set, integrating methylation, RNA-seq, and clinical data (Fig. 1). The screening began with 27,166 CpG islands, which were first filtered to include only those in gene promoter regions. We then selected for islands that were significantly hypermethylated in ccRCC tissue versus normal tissue (FDR < 0.001; Δβ > 0.05) and were also associated with advanced clinical stage and higher Fuhrman grade. To identify prognostically relevant candidates, we required a strong correlation with poor OS and PFS (FDR < 0.001 for both). Furthermore, we filtered for islands whose methylation was significantly associated with CD8⁺ T cell infiltration scores and showed a robust inverse correlation with corresponding gene expression (FDR < 0.001). Finally, evidence from public GEO datasets confirming gene re-expression after demethylation was used as a final selection criterion.

Workflow illustrating the multi-step process for identifying CpG islands with potential prognostic significance and predictive value for immunotherapy efficacy. Our integrative screening identified two CpG islands with strong prognostic and predictive potential: chr3:13,590,414–13591008 (FBLN2) and chr2:233,251,361–233,253,414 (ECEL1P2). Given that FBLN2 has critical biological functions in maintaining basement membrane integrity, is linked to cancer progression via the extracellular matrix (ECM), and its promoter methylation is frequently reported in other malignancies [20–24], we prioritized this candidate for further validation.

FBLN2 promoter hypermethylation is associated with aggressive clinicopathological features and poor prognosis in ccRCC

Analysis of the TCGA-KIRC dataset revealed that the CpG Island in the FBLN2 promoter was significantly hypermethylated in ccRCC tumors compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 2A). This hypermethylation was positively correlated with higher tumor stage and grade (Fig. 2B, C). These findings were validated in independent GEO datasets, where GSE105260 and GSE113501 also showed that advanced-stage tumors had markedly higher FBLN2 methylation levels (Additional file 2A, B).

In survival analyses, high methylation of FBLN2 was associated with significantly poorer OS and PFS (Fig. 2D, F). Multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed FBLN2 methylation an independent prognostic factor for both OS and PFS (Fig. 2E, G). Furthermore, the methylation level of FBLN2 was significantly and positively correlated with CD8 T cell infiltration, as estimated by ssGSEA and CIBERSORT (Fig. 2H, I). Conversely, there was a significant negative correlation between FBLN2 methylation and its gene expression levels (Fig. 2J), a result supported by analysis of the GSE105288 dataset (Additional file 2C). Collectively, these analyses of public datasets indicate that FBLN2 promoter hypermethylation is significantly associated with aggressive tumor features, poor prognosis, and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in ccRCC, and it inversely regulates FBLN2 gene expression.

Probe-level analysis reveals a core subset of CpG sites driving FBLN2’s prognostic and immunomodulatory role

Probe heterogeneity, caused by differences in probe design, specificity, and location, arises from factors such as uneven methylation patterns within a single-CpG island, technical variability in probe manufacturing and hybridization, and biological differences across cell types, tissues, or disease states [25, 26]. This variability presents significant challenges for accurate methylation assessment, particularly in large-scale studies using platforms like Infinium HumanMethylation arrays. To investigate this probe-level heterogeneity, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the nine probes located within the FBLN2 promoter-associated CpG Island and its N-shore (Fig. 3A, B). Analysis of the TCGA-KIRC, GSE105260, and GSE113501 datasets revealed distinct patterns among the probes. A core group of seven probes (all island probes excluding cg00201234) showed a consistent, stepwise increase in methylation that correlated with advancing tumor stage (I-IV) and grade (I-IV) (Fig. 3C, D, Additional file 3A, B). Methylation at these same seven sites was also significantly and inversely correlated with FBLN2 mRNA expression (Additional file 3C, D, E, F, G, H, I, J and K). Furthermore, in univariate Cox analysis, higher methylation at eight of the nine probes (excluding only cg00201234) was significantly associated with poorer OS and PFS (Table 1). This suggests that hypermethylation within the CpG Island and its N-shore region contributes to transcriptional silencing of FBLN2, potentially driving ccRCC progression.

Building upon these findings, we next examined the correlation between methylation at each of the nine probe sites and the tumor immune landscape, particularly focusing on the IFNγ response, which plays a crucial role in cancer immune surveillance [27]. A specific subset of six probes (cg19392656, cg16604516, cg17561417, cg18603228, cg01896761, and cg18406197) showed a significant positive correlation with infiltration scores of activated CD8⁺ T cells, as estimated by both ssGSEA and CIBERSORT (Fig. 4A, Additional file 4). Methylation at these six sites also correlated positively with the infiltration of multiple other immune cell types, including T helper cells, natural killer cells, and macrophages (Fig. 4A). Using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), methylation at these same six probes was also significantly and positively correlated with IFNγ response pathway scores derived from multiple databases (BIOCARTA, HALLMARK, REACTOME, and WikiPathway) (Fig. 4B). This was further supported by a direct positive correlation between methylation at these six probes and the expression of IFNG itself (Additional file 5). The remaining three probes (cg12700904, cg00201234, and cg02027717) did not show these consistent associations with immune infiltration or the IFNγ response. Taken together, these results demonstrate significant probe-level heterogeneity within the FBLN2 CpG Island, identifying a core subset of six probes whose methylation levels are robustly associated with clinical markers of disease progression and key features of an inflamed tumor microenvironment.

A validated MethyLight assay confirms FBLN2 methylation as an independent prognostic marker in a clinical cohort

To validate our findings in a clinical cohort, we developed a MethyLight assay targeting the most informative CpG sites within the FBLN2 promoter (Fig. 5A). In a cohort of 335 ccRCC patients, the FBLN2 PMR was significantly associated with patient age, T classification, lymph node and distant metastasis status, TNM stage, and Fuhrman grade (Table 2). Consistent with this, the median FBLN2 PMR was significantly higher in advanced (10.26%) versus localized (2.91%) ccRCC and in high-grade (6.97%) versus low-grade (1.71%) ccRCC (Fig. 5B, C). To confirm a direct link between promoter methylation and gene silencing, we treated 786-O and Caki-1 ccRCC cell lines with the DNA demethylating agent 5-aza-dC. MethyLight analysis confirmed a reduction in FBLN2 PMR following treatment, which was accompanied by a restoration of FBLN2 mRNA expression (Fig. 5D), a finding that is further supported by our re-analysis of public data (GSE22563) showing consistent FBLN2 upregulation across 11 different RCC cell lines following similar demethylation treatment (Additional file 6). Bisulfite sequencing further verified dense methylation of the FBLN2 promoter in untreated cells and significant demethylation after 5-aza-dC treatment (Additional file 7).

We next assessed the prognostic value of FBLN2 methylation in our patient cohort. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that patients with high FBLN2 PMR had significantly shorter OS (P < 0.0001) and PFS (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5E, F). This prognostic significance for both OS and PFS was maintained when patients were stratified by Fuhrman grade and localized disease stage (Additional files 8A, B and C, 9A, B and C). In patients with advanced disease, specific PMR cutoffs also successfully stratified patients for both OS and PFS (Additional files 8E, 9E). In univariate Cox regression analysis, FBLN2 methylation was significantly associated with both OS and PFS (Table 3). To determine whether it was an independent predictor, we performed a multivariate Cox regression analysis that included other significant clinical variables. The analysis confirmed that FBLN2 methylation level was an independent prognostic factor for both OS (HR: 3.45, 95% CI: 1.76–6.79, P < 0.001) and PFS (HR: 2.43, 95% CI: 1.13–5.25, P = 0.023), alongside other variables such as age, Fuhrman grade, and lymph node metastasis (Table 3). Collectively, these results establish that quantitative FBLN2 promoter methylation, validated in a large clinical cohort, is not only functionally linked to gene silencing but also serves as a robust and independent predictor of aggressive disease and poor patient survival in ccRCC.

High FBLN2 methylation predicts favorable immunotherapy response

Finally, we investigated whether FBLN2 methylation could predict patient response to immunotherapy. We first sought to validate the association between FBLN2 methylation and immune infiltration in a cohort of 74 ccRCC tissue samples. Analysis of these samples confirmed a significant positive correlation between FBLN2 PMR and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration scores, as determined by IHC (r = 0.46, P < 0.0001, Fig. 6A, B).

Patients with high PMR exhibited significantly better PFS

Building on this, we assessed the predictive value of FBLN2 methylation in a cohort of 45 advanced ccRCC patients who received anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy. In this cohort, patients who achieved a partial response (PR) had significantly higher FBLN2 PMR levels compared to non-responders (non-PR) (Fig. 6C). Concordance analysis between PMR categories (low, medium, high) and clinical response categories (progressive disease, stable disease, PR) revealed substantial agreement (Cohen’s weighted Kappa = 0.607, Fig. 6D). Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients with high FBLN2 PMR (above median) experienced significantly longer PFS following immunotherapy compared to patients with low PMR (Fig. 6E). In summary, these findings demonstrate that FBLN2 methylation is not only correlated with CD8⁺ T cell infiltration but also serves as a predictive biomarker for improved clinical response and survival in ccRCC patients treated with immunotherapy.

Discussion

Clear cell RCC often presents with distant metastases or recurs post-nephrectomy, necessitating more effective treatment strategies. Despite advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapies, predicting treatment efficacy remains challenging due to the lack of reliable biomarkers. Our study highlights the potential of CpG island methylation as a valuable biomarker. Specifically, we discovered through high-throughput screening that hypermethylation of the CpG island in the FBLN2 promoter region is significantly associated with advanced tumor stages, poor survival outcomes, and immune cell infiltration, particularly CD8⁺ T cells. These findings were validated in clinical specimens, and we further evaluated the predictive value of this methylation for treatment efficacy in patients undergoing immunotherapy. Collectively, these results suggest that the FBLN2 methylation profile serves as a powerful dual-purpose biomarker, identifying patients with aggressive disease who may, paradoxically, be primed for a favorable response to immunotherapy.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the promoter of FBLN2 exhibited hypermethylation associated with decreased expression in various malignant diseases. Our analysis of the TCGA KIRC dataset revealed similar findings, with increased methylation on the FBLN2 promoter and a negative correlation between mRNA expression and methylation. Additionally, patients with advanced or high Fuhrman grade ccRCC exhibited significantly higher methylation levels of FBLN2. However, this study differs from the previous research in several key aspects. First, we comprehensively investigated the methylation status of FBLN2 in ccRCC for the first time, using an unbiased high-throughput screening approach that integrated genomic data with immune contexture and patient prognosis. Secondly, we focused on the clinical significance of FBLN2 promoter methylation, particularly its independent prognostic value for ccRCC. Finally, we employed the MethyLight assay to quantitatively assess FBLN2 methylation in both an 8-year ccRCC follow-up cohort and a cohort of patients receiving immunotherapy, providing robust validation of our findings. Crucially, our study highlights the predictive potential of methylation patterns in target CpG islands for immunotherapy efficacy, emphasizing the correlation between FBLN2 methylation and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, which could guide personalized treatment strategies.

While high-throughput array-based platforms provide valuable insights into the DNA methylation landscape, validation of specific genes remains crucial for clinical translation. Cost-effective, PCR-based methods like MethyLight offer the high sensitivity and feasibility required for biomarker application [28]. In our study, after high-throughput screening identified the prognostic and immune-correlative potential of the CpG Island in the FBLN2 promoter, we carefully analyzed the heterogeneity of the individual array probes within this region. This high-resolution analysis revealed that different CpG sites within the island have distinct functional consequences. We identified a core cluster of six probes whose methylation status was strongly and consistently correlated not only with FBLN2 gene silencing and patient prognosis but also with CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and IFNγ pathway activation. In contrast, other probes, such as the distal site cg12700904, presented a different functional profile. Although its hypermethylation was also associated with reduced FBLN2 expression and poor prognosis, it showed no correlation with the immune markers. This critical finding suggests that while multiple sites can contribute to gene silencing, the regulation of the tumor immune phenotype is specifically tied to the epigenetic status of a core regulatory element within the FBLN2 promoter. This insight guided our subsequent methodology: to develop the most robust and clinically relevant biomarker, we designed our TaqMan MethyLight assay to specifically quantify methylation within this functionally validated core cluster. This targeted approach was essential for confirming the prognostic value of FBLN2 methylation and its strong correlation with the immune landscape, underscoring its potential as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy.

Current biomarkers for assessing immunotherapy efficacy, such as PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and microsatellite instability (MSI), have several limitations, including tumor heterogeneity, dynamic changes in expression, and modest predictive power in ccRCC [29–31]. These issues reduce their reliability and accessibility in clinical settings. In contrast, methylation biomarkers, particularly CpG island methylation, offer several advantages over traditional biomarkers. DNA methylation patterns are relatively stable and less prone to the dynamic changes seen with protein expression or mutational burden, providing a more consistent biomarker [32]. Methylation changes can also be highly specific to certain cancer types, including ccRCC, allowing for more precise stratification of patients [33]. As demonstrated in our study, hypermethylation of the FBLN2 promoter is associated with advanced tumor stages, poor survival outcomes, and immune cell infiltration. This apparent paradox—where a marker of poor prognosis predicts favorable immunotherapy response—may be explained by the biological consequence of FBLN2 silencing. Loss of FBLN2, an extracellular matrix protein, could induce remodeling and sterile inflammation, creating an immunologically “hot” tumor microenvironment that, while driving intrinsic tumor aggression, also renders it susceptible to immune checkpoint blockade. However, it is important to consider that methylation changes might also be a consequence of other underlying biological processes rather than direct drivers of disease. To address this, further studies are needed to elucidate the causal relationship between methylation changes and tumor behavior. Despite this, the robust association of FBLN2 methylation with clinical outcomes provides a valuable tool for predicting immunotherapy efficacy. By integrating methylation biomarkers with other molecular and clinical data, we can develop more comprehensive models to guide personalized treatment strategies, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Some limitations of this study should also be noted. Firstly, a key limitation is that our analysis of bulk tumor tissue cannot definitively identify the specific cellular origin of the FBLN2 promoter hypermethylation. However, our findings point strongly toward the ccRCC tumor cells themselves as a primary source, as our in vitro demethylation assays on pure cancer cell lines directly confirmed their capacity to regulate FBLN2 expression via methylation. Nevertheless, a significant contribution from the tumor stroma, particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), is also highly plausible. As the main producers of the extracellular matrix, of which FBLN2 is a key component, CAFs undergo extensive epigenetic reprogramming and likely contribute to the methylation signature detected in bulk samples [34]. Future studies using techniques like single-cell multi-omics or laser capture microdissection will be required to deconvolve the precise cell-type-specific contributions. Secondly, our study lacks direct FBLN2 protein expression data. This was not due to an oversight, but rather to significant technical challenges with commercially available antibodies that prevented us from obtaining reliable and specific staining. While this highlights the robustness and clinical applicability of DNA methylation as a biomarker, we acknowledge that correlating methylation with protein expression is a critical step. The strong inverse correlation we established between methylation and FBLN2 mRNA expression (from TCGA data and our demethylation experiments) provides compelling evidence for gene silencing. Nevertheless, the development of a validated FBLN2 antibody to investigate its protein expression and functional role in the tumor microenvironment remains an important direction for future research. Thirdly, the influence of aging on DNA methylation is a well-established biological phenomenon [35–39]. Given that age-related hypermethylation often occurs at CpG islands and that age itself was an independent prognostic factor in our cohort, it is crucial to consider its potential confounding effect. Future research should aim to disentangle the specific contributions of age-related versus cancer-specific methylation to the FBLN2 signature. Furthermore, our functional validation was primarily conducted in vitro. While providing strong mechanistic support, future studies employing in vivo models are warranted to dissect the precise mechanisms by which FBLN2 modulates the tumor microenvironment in a more physiologically relevant setting. Finally, this study was designed to establish FBLN2 methylation as a biomarker, not to explore therapeutic strategies. Investigating whether epigenetic modulation of FBLN2 could sensitize ccRCC tumors to immune checkpoint blockade represents a promising future direction for developing novel combination therapies, although the upstream mechanisms driving this methylation state also remain to be clarified.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we establish promoter hypermethylation of FBLN2 as a powerful dual-role biomarker in ccRCC, which simultaneously predicts poor prognosis and a favorable response to immunotherapy. This apparent paradox is explained by the biomarker’s strong association with an inflamed, immune-infiltrated tumor microenvironment, thereby priming the tumor for immunotherapy. By progressing from unbiased bioinformatic discovery to a clinically applicable quantitative assay, our work establishes FBLN2 methylation as a promising biomarker poised to enhance the precision of patient stratification and management in ccRCC.
