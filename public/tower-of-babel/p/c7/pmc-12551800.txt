Smoothened Agonist Treatment Mitigates Vasogenic Edema, Neuroinflammation, and Neurological Dysfunction Following Traumatic Brain Injury in Mice
Authors: Shotaro Michinaga, Kenta Takahashi, Yasuhiro Ogawa, Hiroyuki Mizuguchi, Shigeru Hishinuma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTraumatic brain injury (TBI)—a serious brain damage caused by accidents, falls, and sports—causes vasogenic edema, neuroinflammation, and neurological dysfunction resulting from blood–brain barrier disruption. Sonic hedgehog (Shh), a secretory protein belonging to the hedgehog family, protects cerebrovascular and neuronal function via the patched‐1 (Ptch‐1)–smoothened (Smo)–Gli pathway. In this study, we investigated the effects of Smo agonists (Shh and SAG) and antagonist (Jervine) on vasogenic edema, neuroinflammation, and neurological dysfunction in mice following TBI. Male ddy mice (8 weeks old) were used to minimize the influence of gonadal hormones on the results. A TBI model was established by inflicting a fluid percussion injury (FPI) on mouse cerebrum or cultured cells. Shh expression increased in mouse cerebrums and cultured astrocytes after FPI. Vasogenic edema was assessed by Evans blue extravasation into brain tissue and increased brain water content. Evans blue extravasation and brain water content increased after FPI. Repeated intracerebroventricular administration of recombinant Shh reduced Evans blue extravasation and brain water content in the cerebrum after FPI, whereas treatment with Jervine, an Smo antagonist, aggravated these conditions. Recombinant Shh administration also decreased the expression of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in the cerebrum after FPI. Notably, repeated intravenous administration of SAG, a small‐molecule Smo agonist, reduced FPI‐induced vasogenic edema and neuroinflammation, and improved neurological dysfunction in mice. Furthermore, SAG treatment increased the expression of vascular protective factors and tight junction proteins. These results suggest that Smo agonists are promising therapeutic agents for TBI.Keywords: blood–brain barrier, brain edema, neuroinflammation, neurological dysfunction, smoothened, sonic hedgehog, traumatic brain injury

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a serious brain damage caused by accidents, falls, and sports, resulting in unexpected death or serious sequelae, such as motor paralysis and cognitive dysfunction. Although emergent treatments are essential to prevent these pathological conditions, the efficacy of current treatment options is limited. TBI‐induced pathogenesis includes blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption, brain edema, and neuroinflammation. BBB disruption induces vasogenic edema and neuroinflammation by excessive extravascular fluid accumulation and extravascular inflammatory cell infiltration into the cerebral parenchyma, thereby triggering severe and persistent neuronal dysfunction [1, 2, 3]. Therefore, vasogenic edema and neuroinflammation treatments may be promising strategies for TBI alleviation.

Sonic hedgehog (Shh), a secretory protein belonging to the hedgehog family, is essential for brain development [4, 5]. Shh exerts its actions by interacting with patched‐1 (Ptch‐1) and smoothened (Smo) proteins. In the absence of Shh, Ptch‐1 inhibits Smo‐mediated downstream signaling. When Shh binds to Ptch‐1, Smo is activated and Gli is translocated from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, thereby activating the transcription of its target genes [6, 7, 8]. In the adult brain, Shh reduces BBB permeability by increasing tight junction protein (TJP) expression in endothelial cells [9, 10]. In various experimental animal models of brain injury, Shh treatment has been shown to reduce BBB disruption [11, 12, 13]. Shh signaling activation also exerts neuroprotective actions against ischemic damage [14], whereas Shh signaling inhibition aggravates brain damage [15]. The use of an Smo agonist SAG was found to prevent ischemic damage and improve long‐term behavioral outcomes and cognitive function in model animals [16, 17, 18]. The use of another Smo agonist, purmorphamine, was also neuroprotective in ischemic stroke and hemorrhage models [19, 20]. In contrast, BBB impairment was aggravated following Smo antagonist treatment in experimental rats with subarachnoid hemorrhage [21]. These findings suggest that Smo agonists protect against BBB disruption and neurological dysfunction in the damaged brain.

Relationships between Shh and TBI have been demonstrated in both patients and experimental animals. Shh protein level is decreased in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with TBI [22]. Previously, we reported increased expressions of Shh, Ptch‐1, and Smo in TBI mice [23]. Wu et al. [24] demonstrated that exogenous Shh treatment improved the neurological outcome in TBI rats. Furthermore, our previous study suggested that exogenous Shh treatment alleviates TBI‐induced BBB disruption in mouse models [23]. Pringle et al. [22] found that Smo agonist SAG treatment promoted recovery of motor function in TBI mice, indicating that Shh–Smo signaling may be protective against TBI. However, these studies did not verify the effects of SAG and Smo antagonist on TBI‐induced vasogenic edema and neuroinflammation. Moreover, the effects of SAG on brain endothelial cells and astrocytes following TBI were not examined.

In this study, we performed fluid percussion injury (FPI) for creating TBI models. FPI is a commonly used model of TBI typically applied either midline or lateral. Midline FPI induces a diffuse TBI with bilateral structural injury, while lateral FPI induces a local cortical injury in the ipsilateral hemisphere, and both subcortical and contralateral structures are diffusely injured [25]. To assess the effects of Smo agonist and antagonist on the local contusion, lateral FPI was performed in this study. We initially examined the effects of Shh and Jervine, a Smo antagonist, treatments on vasogenic edema and neuroinflammation in TBI mouse models. Subsequently, the effects of SAG treatment on TBI‐induced vasogenic edema, neuroinflammation, and neurological dysfunction were assessed. We further configured in vitro TBI models and investigated the effects of Shh and SAG treatments on the expression of vascular protective factors and TJPs in brain endothelial cells following TBI.

Materials and Methods

Animal Experimental Design

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH publication number: 8023). All protocols were approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of the Meiji Pharmaceutical University (Approval numbers: 202206 and 202207). Male mice were used to minimize the influence of gonadal hormones on the results. Male ddY mice (8 weeks old, 32–37 g) were purchased from Japan SLC Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan) and housed in groups of four per cage (202 × 313 × 130 mm) with a 12‐h light/dark cycle in a pathogen‐free room. The temperature and humidity were controlled at 22°C ± 2°C and 50% ± 10%, respectively. The mice were divided into different experimental groups based on pseudo‐randomization using shuffled cards according to a previous study [26]. The sample size of six mice per group was determined based on the sample size in our previous study [27, 28].

Lateral FPI in the Mouse Cerebrum

To create a TBI model, an FPI was inflicted on the dura of the mouse cerebrum using a fluid percussion device (FP302, AmScien Instruments, Richmond, VA, USA) as previously described [29]. To anesthetize, medetomidine (0.3 mg/kg) (135‐17473, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan), midazolam (4.0 mg/kg) (135‐13791, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), and butorphanol (5.0 mg/kg) (021‐19001, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) in saline (H1ZAC6, HIKARI PHARMACEUTICAL Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) were intraperitoneally administered. A 3‐mm‐diameter hole was opened in the left hemisphere via the skull at a position 2.5 mm posterior and 2.5 mm left lateral to the bregma. A lure fitting (VRS306, ISIS Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was placed in the hole and fixed using glue gel (43594, Henkel Japan Ltd., Yokohama, Japan). The fixed lure fitting was connected to the tube of the FPI device after it was filled with a saline solution. Hydraulic impact was delivered at 1.2–1.4 atm. After FPI, the incision was sutured, and mice were kept in raised cages. In sham‐operated mice, the lure fitting was fixed to the left hemisphere in the same manner without a hydraulic impact. To minimize pain after the FPI, the mice were anesthetized for several hours. The local injury was observed in the mouse cerebrum after FPI (Figure S1A,B).

Cultured Cells

The isolation of astrocytes was performed according to established methods [27, 30, 31]. Cultured astrocytes were obtained from ddY mice (2 days old). The meninges were carefully stripped from the collected cerebrums to prevent the contamination of fibroblasts. The collected tissues were suspended with trypsin (15090‐046, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and centrifuged at 181 × g for 5 min. The collected pellet was suspended in Eagle's minimum essential medium (MEM; 05900, Nissui Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Prepared cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells/cm2 in 75‐cm2 flasks (353135, Corning, NY, USA) with MEM‐supplemented 10% fetal bovine serum (S1780‐500, Biowest, Riverside, MO, USA) and maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2 conditions. After 7–10 days, culture flasks were shaken at 250 rpm for several hours to remove protoplasmic cell layers with small process‐bearing cells. The monolayer cells were treated with trypsin, reseeded in 35‐mm culture dishes (150318, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and then incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 conditions for 5–7 days. At this stage, more than 90% of cells showed immunoreactivity for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; astrocyte marker) but not platelet‐derived growth factor receptor β (PDGFRβ; pericyte marker) (Figure S2).

The bEnd.3 cell, a cell line‐derived from mouse brain vascular endothelial cell, was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/cm2 in 75‐cm2 culture flasks and grown in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (05919, Nissui Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum. After 5–7 days, the cells were seeded in 35‐mm culture dishes or six‐well plates (353046, Corning, NY, USA) and then incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 conditions for 3–5 days. Two days after non‐serum incubation, bEnd.3 cells were treated with recombinant Shh (rShh; 0.2, 2, and 20 μg/mL) or SAG (10, 100, and 1000 nM) and then incubated for 3 h.

In Vitro FPI

Jayakumar et al. [32] found that a direct impact by the in vitro FPI induced NF‐κB activation and swelling in cultured astrocytes similar to TBI model animals. Additionally, we found that the in vitro FPI increased the factors promoting BBB disruption in cultured astrocytes [28]. Therefore, the in vitro FPI was conducted to assess the effects of the direct impact on cultured cells. The in vitro FPI was established as previously described [28, 32, 33]. Briefly, cultured astrocytes or bEnd.3 cells were grown in individual 35‐mm culture dishes; thereafter, a dish of cultured astrocytes or bEnd.3 cells was set in a chamber of the cell trauma device (FP302, AmScien Instruments). The upper half of the chamber was filled with sterilized saline, and a tube of the chamber was connected to the FPI device. A hydraulic impact was delivered to cultured astrocytes or bEnd.3 cells. In the preliminary experiments, we microscopically observed no sign of remarkable cell death after in vitro FPI. Immediately after in vitro FPI, the bEnd.3 cells or cultured astrocytes were treated with rShh (2 μg/mL), SAG (100 nM), and Jervine (10 μM) and then incubated for 3 h.

Intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) Administration

Mouse rShh (ab75431, Abcam, Tokyo, Japan), Jervine, an Smo antagonist (S4747, Selleck, Tokyo, Japan), or vehicle such as 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (472301, Sigma‐Aldrich, Tokyo, Japan) in saline was administered into the lateral ventricle according to our previous protocol [23, 26, 29]. At 3 days before FPI, a guide cannula (CXG‐X; Eicom, Kyoto, Japan) was set in the right hemisphere. The scalp was incised at the midline and the skull was exposed. A burr hole was made by a dental drill at a position of 0.2 mm posterior and 1.0 mm right lateral to the bregma. A guide cannula was inserted through the burr hole and fixed on the skull by dental cement, where a tip of the cannula was positioned at 2.0 mm depth from the surface of the skull. The i.c.v. administration was performed through the fixed guide cannula. The i.c.v. administration of rShh was performed from the guide cannula twice a day between 3 h and 3 days after FPI. The combined i.c.v. administration of rShh and Jervine was performed using the same schedule. Additionally, i.c.v. administration of rShh and Jervine was performed between 3 and 7 days after FPI in some experiments.

Intravenous (i.v.) Administration

Mice were placed in a fixator (551‐BSRR, Natsume, Tokyo, Japan), and SAG (N‐Methyl‐N′‐(3‐pyridinylbenzyl)‐N′‐(3‐chlorobenzo[b]thiophene‐2‐carbonyl)‐1,4‐diaminocyclohexane), an Smo agonist (AG‐CR1‐3506‐M005, AdipoGen, San Diego, CA, USA), or saline was injected into the tail vein once a day between 3 h and 3 days after FPI, according to our previous protocol [33]. Jervine, an Smo antagonist, was administered into the lateral ventricle from the guide cannula 30 min before SAG treatment.

Determination of BBB Disruption

BBB disruption was determined by assessing Evans blue extravasation into the brain tissue, as previously described [26]. Saline containing 4% Evans blue (E2129, Sigma‐Aldrich) was intravenously administered at a dose of 3 mL/kg. Sixty minutes later, 100 μL of circulating blood was collected to determine serum Evans blue concentration. Mice were perfused from the left ventricle with phosphate‐buffered saline (sc‐24946, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Dallas, TX, USA) under anesthesia. Subsequently, the coronal brain tissues from 0 to 5 mm posterior to the bregma were obtained using a brain slicer (MK‐MC‐01, Muromachi, Tokyo, Japan). The collected tissues were weighed and incubated with formamide (065‐00436, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) at 55°C overnight. Evans blue concentration was evaluated by measuring the absorbance at 655 nm. Evans blue extravasation was determined using the following formula: Evans blue extravasation (%) = Brain Evans blue concentration (μg/g) × 100/Serum Evans blue concentration (μg/mL).

Evaluation of Brain Edema via Brain Water Content

Brain water content was determined as previously described [27]. The coronal brain tissues from 0 to 5 mm posterior to the bregma were obtained using a brain slicer. The prepared tissues were wet‐weighed and dried at 80°C overnight, after which the dry weight was measured. Brain water content was evaluated using the following formula: Brain water content (%) = (wet weight − dry weight) × 100/wet weight.

Each reverse transcription product was added to the SYBR Green Master Mix (QPK‐212, Toyobo Co. Ltd., Osaka, Japan), and the amplification reaction was performed in a thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems StepOne Real‐time PCR systems version 2.3, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Enzyme Immunoassay

The coronal brain tissues (0–5 mm posterior to the bregma) from the injured cerebral hemisphere were homogenized in 500 μL of the radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (16488‐34, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (04080‐24, Nacalai Tesque) and a phosphatase cocktail (07574‐61, Nacalai Tesque). The collected lysates were centrifuged at 20 000 × g for 10 min (KUBOTA 3700). Subsequently, the supernatants were collected and their protein contents were determined using the Pierce bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (23228, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The Shh peptide level in the supernatant was determined using an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay kit (RK08780, ABclonal Technology Co. Ltd., Woburn, MA, USA). The enzyme immunoassay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Immunoblotting

The prepared protein samples were subjected to 7.5% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (IPVH07850, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies against Ptch‐1 (1:2000, ab53715, Abcam), Smo (1:2000, ab235183, Abcam), Gli‐1 (1:2000, ab273018, Abcam), claudin‐5 (1:2000, 352500, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and occludin (1:2000, 71–1500, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Next, the membranes were reacted with peroxidase‐conjugated rabbit (1:2000, 12‐348, Millipore), mouse (1:2000, 12‐349, Millipore Billerica, MA, USA), or goat (1:2000, sc‐2354; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) secondary antibody. The membranes were also reacted with β‐actin antibodies (1:2000, ab8229, Abcam). A chemiluminescence kit (Chemi‐Lumi One L, 07880‐70, Nacalai Tesque) was used for protein detection. The intensities of protein bands were determined using ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). Typical untruncated immunoblot images are shown in Figure S3.

Nuclear Extraction

The coronal brain tissues (0–5 mm posterior to the bregma) from the injured cerebral hemisphere were prepared, and nuclear extraction was performed using a Nuclear Extract Kit (40010, Active Motif, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The prepared nuclear extract samples were subjected to 7.5% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against Gli‐1 (1:2000, ab273018, Abcam) and then reacted with peroxidase‐conjugated rabbit secondary antibodies (1:2000, 12‐348, Millipore). The membranes were also reacted with histone H1.4 antibodies (1:2000, H7665, Sigma‐Aldrich). A chemiluminescence kit (Chemi‐Lumi One L, 07880‐70, Nacalai Tesque) was used for protein detection. The intensities of protein bands were determined using ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

Immunohistochemistry

The mice were intracardially perfused with 50 mL phosphate‐buffered saline (sc‐24946, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (26126‐25, Nacalai Tesque). The perfused brains were incubated in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, and then in 15% sucrose (30404‐45, Nacalai Tesque) overnight at 4°C. The fixed brain was placed on a brain slicer, and coronal slices (0–6 mm posterior to the bregma) containing the core region and its surrounding areas were collected. Six frozen sections (30 μm thick) were prepared using a cryostat (CM1510, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany); thereafter, the sections were mounted onto slides (MAS‐03, Matsunami Glass Ind. Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against ionized calcium‐binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1; microglia marker, 1:400, 019‐19741, Wako) or myeloperoxidase antibodies (MPO; neutrophil marker, 1:400, ab208670, Abcam). Primary antibodies were detected using fluorescein isothiocyanate‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:400, 210‐1202, Rockland, PA, USA) or rhodamine‐conjugated IgG antibodies (1:400, AP307R, Millipore). The labeled cells were observed under an epifluorescence microscope (Axio Imager M2, Zeiss, MA, USA).

Behavioral Tests

All behavior tests were assessed by an identical researcher and performed under the blind condition. In all groups, pronounced weight loss was not observed within the behavioral testing period (Figure S4A).

Rotarod test: The rotarod test was performed using a Rotarod Treadmill (MK‐610A, MUROMACHI KIKAI CO. LTD.). Before the test, the mice were trained over three consecutive days. The training and testing consisted of four trials, and the mice were made to run for 300 s or until falling from the treadmill. The maximum rotation speed of the treadmill was 40 rpm (acceleration duration: 60 s, from 4 to 40 rpm). The average running time was assessed. To ensure the criteria of normal neurological function, mice that could run over 250 s as an average running time after training were selected, and mice that did not match the criteria were not used in subsequent experiments. The extreme decrease in running time implies neurological dysfunction.

Beam walking test: The beam walking test was performed using a parallel bar (diameter: 1 cm, length: 50 cm). As the goal point, a box was set at the opposite side of the start point at the end of the bar. Before the test, the mice were trained over three consecutive days. The training and testing consisted of four trials, and the mice were placed on the parallel bar for 1 min. To assess neurological dysfunction, the average walking distance as well as the number of falls and missteps of the hindlimb from the bar were recorded. The number of missteps was converted to the number per 50 cm. All mice could walk across from the start point to the goal point on the bar within 1 min without extreme falling and missteps after training. The extreme decrease in walking distance as well as the increase in the number of falls and missteps imply neurological dysfunction.

Novel object recognition test: Novel object recognition test was performed for assessing the recognition memory of mice based on the natural tendency of animals to preferentially explore novel rather than familiar objects, which does not need external motivation, reward, or punishment [34]. The mice were placed in a box (202 × 313 × 130 mm) with two identical triangular objects as a familiar object and allowed to freely explore the box for 10 min. One hour later, one of the identical objects was replaced with a novel square or cylindrical object. Thereafter, the mice were allowed to further explore the box for 10 min. Object recognition was considered when the mice touched an object with the forepaws or sniffed an object while running around the objects; sitting or climbing the objects were not recorded. The number of times the mice touched or sniffed an object was counted, and the results were expressed as a percentage of touching counts for the novel object per touching counts for familiar and novel objects. Before testing, all mice did not show extreme preference for identical objects (Figure S4B). To except confounding variables such as motivation and exploration tendencies, tests were performed at 3, 5, and 7 days after FPI because the exploration behavior was inadequate and the object‐touching ability was considerably low in the injured mice at 1 and 2 days after FPI (Figure S4C).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Ekuseru‐Toukei software (ver. 3.20; Social Survey Research Information Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Normality tests were performed with normal probability plots by using this software before statistical analysis. Data are represented as mean ± standard error of six independent samples (n = 6) and were determined using a one‐way analysis of variance followed by a post hoc test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The effect size was assessed by a Cohen's d value.

Results

Increased Shh, Ptch‐1, Smo, and Nuclear Gli‐1 Expressions in the Mouse Cerebrum After FPI

We previously found that Shh, Ptch‐1, and Smo expressions were increased in mice with cerebral injury after FPI [23]. Similar to our previous results, Shh expression was significantly increased in mice with cerebral injury than in the sham group 3–10 days after FPI (Figure 1A). The protein expressions of Ptch‐1 and Smo were also significantly increased in mice with cerebral injury at 3–10 days after FPI (Figure 1B). Although the protein expression of Gli‐1 did not change at 1–10 days after FPI, nuclear Gli‐1 protein expression increased at 3–10 days after FPI (Figure 1C,D).

Our previous study indicated that Shh expression increased in astrocytes following FPI [23]. Moreover, some previous studies have shown that astrocytes are the predominant cell type for Ptch‐1 and Smo in the adult mouse brain [35, 36, 37] while Alvarez et al. [9] and our previous study [23] found that these expressions are also observed in brain endothelial cells. In the in vitro experiments, the mRNA expression of Shh significantly increased in cultured astrocytes after the in vitro FPI, whereas the mRNA expressions of Ptch‐1 and Smo significantly increased in bEnd.3 cells after the in vitro FPI (Figure S5A–C).

Shh Alleviates FPI‐Induced BBB Disruption via Smo

To assess BBB disruption, Evans blue extravasation into the brain was examined. Our previous study indicated that BBB disruption was clearly observed within 3 h and peaked at 3 days after FPI [29]. To investigate the effect of Shh against progression to the peaked phase of BBB disruption after FPI, repeated i.c.v. administration of rShh was performed from 3 h to 3 days after FPI (Figure 2A). The rShh treatment reduced Evans blue extravasation compared to that in vehicle‐treated mice with cerebral injury (Figure 2B). rShh administration did not affect Evans blue extravasation in the non‐injured cerebrum (Figure 2B). The effect of rShh on Evans blue extravasation was inhibited by the combined administration with Jervine (Figure 2C). Jervine administration mildly increased Evans blue extravasation at 3 days after FPI (Figure 2C).

Shh Reduces FPI‐Induced Brain Edema via Smo

Our previous study indicated that the brain water content was increased within 3 h and peaked at 3 days after FPI [29]. To investigate the effect of Shh against progression to the peaked phase of brain edema after FPI, repeated i.c.v. administration of rShh was performed from 3 h to 3 days after FPI. The rShh treatment reduced the brain water content in the cerebrum after FPI compared to that in vehicle‐treated mice with cerebral injury (Figure 5A). The effect of rShh administration on the brain water content was inhibited by the combined administration with Jervine (Figure 5B). Jervine administration did not change the brain water content 3 days after FPI (Figure 5B). The repeated i.c.v. administration of rShh from 3 to 7 days after FPI also reduced the brain water content, although the effect of Shh was inhibited by Jervine administration (Figure 5C). Similar to Evans blue extravasation, repeated i.c.v. administration of Jervine from 3 to 7 days after FPI significantly increased the brain water content compared with that in the vehicle‐treated group (Figure 5C).

SAG Administration Alleviates Vasogenic Edema and Neuroinflammation in the Mouse Cerebrum After FPI

Although i.c.v. administration of rShh alleviates FPI‐induced vasogenic edema and neuroinflammation, exogenous Shh may not enter the central nervous system if administered through peripheral tissues. Accordingly, the effects of intravenously administered SAG, a small‐molecule Smo agonist, on FPI‐induced vasogenic edema and neuroinflammation were examined. To investigate the effect of SAG against progression to the peaked phase of vasogenic edema after FPI, repeated i.v. administration of SAG was performed from 3 h to 3 days after FPI (Figure 7B). SAG treatment promoted the nuclear translocation of Gli‐1, which was comparable with the Shh treatment effect (Figure 7A). Mice that underwent SAG treatment demonstrated lower Evans blue extravasation than mice in the vehicle‐treated group (Figure 7C). The effect of SAG was inhibited by i.c.v. administration of Jervine (Figure 7C). Claudin‐5 and occludin mRNA expressions were significantly increased by SAG administration in both non‐injured and injured cerebrums (Figure 7D). Furthermore, Ang‐1 and TIMP‐1 mRNA expressions were significantly increased by SAG administration (Figure 7E,F). These effects of SAG were inhibited by Jervine treatment (Figure 7D–F).

SAG Administration Improves Neurological Dysfunction in Mice After FPI

The rotarod and beam walking tests were performed to assess motor coordination and balance. In the rotarod test, there was a tendency for short running times of injured mice on the treadmill compared to their running times before FPI. The average running times for FPI days 0, 1, 2, 3, and 5 were 285, 226, 211, 213, and 211 s, respectively (Figure 9B). In the beam walking test, the injured mouse walking distance on a parallel bar was significantly shorter than their walking distance before FPI, whereas the numbers of falls and missteps from the parallel bar were significantly higher than the numbers before FPI (Figure 9C). SAG administration extended the running times on the treadmill of injured mice compared with those of saline‐treated mice at 2 (211 vs. 250 s), 3 (213 vs. 257 s), and 5 days (211 vs. 247 s) after FPI (Figure 9A,B). Furthermore, SAG administration significantly extended the injured mouse walking distance on the parallel bar and significantly decreased the injured mouse numbers of falls and missteps from the parallel bar compared with those of saline‐treated mice (Figure 9A,C).

Discussion

Shh Alleviates TBI‐Induced BBB Disruption by Increasing the Expression of TJPs and Vascular Protective Factors via Smo

TBI causes serious brain damage and fatal pathological conditions, including vasogenic edema and neuroinflammation, resulting in unexpected death or neurological dysfunction in survivors. However, to date, no promising therapeutic agents have been developed. In the present study, exogenous Shh administration reduced vasogenic edema and neuroinflammation in an experimental TBI mouse model, and the protective effects of Shh were inhibited by an Smo antagonist. Similar to our previous study results [23], the protein expression levels of endogenous Shh, Ptch‐1, and Smo were significantly increased 3 days after FPI in the mouse cerebrum and peaked 7 days after FPI. Moreover, we previously showed that FPI‐induced BBB disruption peaked and naturally recovered at 3 and 7 days after FPI in the mouse cerebrum, respectively [29]. Therefore, the time‐course of increased Shh, Ptch‐1, and Smo expressions corresponded with that of BBB recovery. Furthermore, we found that the administration of an Smo antagonist Jervine from 3 to 7 days post‐FPI exacerbated BBB disruption. These results suggest that endogenous Smo plays a key role in recovery from TBI‐induced BBB disruption.

Contrary to our results in TBI mouse models, Pringle et al. [22] found that the Shh expression level decreased in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with TBI at 1.5 days after injury (range, 0–3 days). In the present study, we collected the mouse cerebral cortex, and the Shh expression level prominently increased 5–10 days after FPI. Therefore, the differences in the collected brain region and TBI periods may explain the discrepancy between our results and those of Pringle et al.

TBI‐induced BBB disruption results not only from direct physical impact but also from decreased expression of TJPs, such as claudin‐5 and occludin [40]. Some studies have suggested that Shh increases TJP expression [9, 11]. In the present study, TJP expression was decreased in TBI mouse models, whereas exogenous Shh administration increased TJP expression; moreover, the effect of Shh administration was inhibited by treatment with an Smo antagonist. In the in vitro experiments, Shh treatment also increased the expression level of TJPs in brain endothelial cells. These results demonstrate that Shh administration alleviates BBB disruption by increasing TJP expression.

Our in vitro studies suggested that the in vitro FPI increased the Shh expression in cultured astrocytes but not in bEnd.3 cells. Therefore, astrocytes may be the main product cells for Shh. Additionally, exogenous Shh treatment increased the expressions of Ang‐1 and TIMP‐1 in cultured astrocytes and bEnd.3 cells as well as in the mouse cerebrums. Ang‐1 increases TJP expression, and TIMP‐1 inhibits MMP‐9‐induced decrease in TJP expression; thus, increased expressions of Ang‐1 and TIMP‐1 may be a protective mechanism against BBB disruption. Hence, astrocyte‐derived Shh may reduce BBB disruption via vascular protective factors. However, there are some limitations regarding the definition of target cells for Shh. Although Alvarez et al. [9] and our previous study [23] suggest the expressions of Smo and Ptch‐1 proteins are observed in brain endothelial cells, some studies suggest that astrocytes are the predominant cell type for Smo and Ptch‐1 in the adult mouse brain [35, 36, 37]. These findings imply that Shh activates hedgehog signaling in astrocytes and that astrocytes may exert downstream effects to protect the BBB via yet unidentified signals. Additionally, we used the bEnd.3 cell line but not mouse purified brain endothelial cells. Although the in vitro FPI increased the expressions of Smo and Ptch‐1 in bEnd.3 cells, RNA‐seq of purified brain endothelial cells demonstrates that there is no upregulation of these molecules after TBI [41]. Therefore, the bEnd.3 cell line may have different properties from purified brain endothelial cells. To better clarify target cells for Shh, more careful considerations should be conducted in the future.

Shh Reduces TBI‐Induced Vasogenic Edema and Neuroinflammation via Smo

Vasogenic edema is a severe condition characterized by increased brain water content resulting from BBB disruption. We previously found that the FPI‐induced increase in brain water content peaked 3 days and decreased 7 days after FPI, similar to that observed in BBB disruption [27, 29]. Therefore, the timing of brain water content reduction was also consistent with that of increased expressions of endogenous Shh and Smo. Similar to the results of BBB disruption, the FPI‐induced increase in the brain water content was decreased and increased by the administration of rShh and Smo antagonist, respectively. These results indicate that Shh may reduce TBI‐induced vasogenic edema via Smo by alleviating BBB disruption. Furthermore, we found that the administration of an Smo antagonist, Jervine, from 3 to 7 days post‐FPI increased the brain water content and Evans blue extravasation. Accordingly, endogenous Smo plays a key role in recovery from TBI‐induced vasogenic edema. Although the present study focused on vasogenic edema, TBI not only induces vasogenic edema but also cytotoxic edema wherein water excessively accumulates in cells [42]. Therefore, the effect of Shh treatment against cytotoxic edema should be investigated in the future.

Neuroinflammation results from the increased expression of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and inflammation‐related enzymes, resulting in permanent neurological dysfunction. In TBI mouse models, the expressions of inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α and IL‐1β), chemokines (MCP‐1 and MIP‐2), and inflammation‐related enzymes (COX‐2 and NOS‐2) were increased in the injured cerebrum, whereas exogenous Shh administration reduced these expressions; however, the effects of Shh administration were inhibited by treatment with an Smo antagonist. These results suggest that Shh may alleviate TBI‐induced neuroinflammation via Smo. Because BBB disruption causes neuroinflammation resulting from the promotion of intravascular inflammatory cell infiltration into the cerebral parenchyma [1, 2], the reduction in BBB disruption may be a suppressive mechanism of Shh for neuroinflammation. However, we did not investigate the detailed suppressive mechanisms of Shh on the expression of inflammation‐related factors. Future studies should provide clarifications along these lines.

SAG Treatment Alleviates TBI‐Induced Vasogenic Edema, Neuroinflammation, and Neurological Dysfunction

To assume clinical application, we investigated the effects of i.v. administration of SAG, a small‐molecule Smo agonist, by its ability to pass through the BBB in TBI mouse models, since exogenous Shh may be unable to enter the central nervous system if administered through peripheral tissues. As expected, repeated i.v. administration of SAG reduced Evans blue extravasation in the cerebrum after FPI and increased the expression levels of TJPs and vascular protective factors such as Ang‐1 and TIMP‐1 in both non‐injured and injured cerebrum. Furthermore, i.v. administration of SAG reduced the brain water content, the expression level of inflammation‐related factors, microglia accumulation, and neutrophil infiltration in TBI mouse models. In the in vitro experiments, SAG treatment also increased the expression levels of TJPs in bEnd.3 cells and increased Ang‐1 and TIMP‐1 in bEnd.3 cells and cultured astrocytes. Therefore, SAG treatment can reduce TBI‐induced vasogenic edema and neuroinflammation by alleviating BBB disruption and increasing the expression levels of TJPs via vascular protective factors.

TBI causes critical neurological dysfunction as serious sequelae. We performed rotarod and beam walking tests to assess motor coordination and balance. As the injured mice showed a short running time on the rotarod treadmill and a short walking distance on a parallel bar with increased numbers of falls and missteps from the bar, the motor function was impaired by the FPI. Repeated i.v. administrations of SAG restored these compromised conditions. Thus, SAG administration can improve TBI‐induced motor dysfunction. Our findings corroborated with those of Pringle et al. [22] who reported that SAG treatment restored motor function in TBI mouse models. Furthermore, we performed the novel object recognition test to assess cognitive function. Seven days after FPI, the injured mice did not show the preference for novel objects, indicating compromised cognitive function in the mice. Notably, the SAG‐treated injured mice showed preference for novel objects, akin to the sham mice. Therefore, SAG administration improved TBI‐induced cognitive dysfunction.

Consideration for SAG Application to Treat TBI in Clinical

To consider clinical application, establishment of the treatment regimen is essential. After TBI, BBB damage, brain edema, and neuroinflammation progress from several hours to days [43]. In this study, SAG treatment was performed from 3 h to 3 days after FPI, and BBB disruption, brain edema, and neuroinflammation were reduced. Accordingly, this treatment schedule might be adequate for reducing the acute damage after TBI. As a route of SAG administration, we selected i.v. administration to demonstrate immediate effects of SAG. The i.v. administration of SAG exerted protective effects while Pringle et al. [22] found that oral administration of SAG also improved recovery of motor function in TBI model mice. Consequently, both i.v. and oral might be adequate routes of SAG administration for alleviating TBI. Heine et al. [44] suggest that SAG crosses the BBB to activate Shh–Smo gene targets in the cerebellum. However, detailed pharmacokinetics and bioavailability of SAG have not been verified. It is necessary to pay close attention to future information. Furthermore, we examined the appropriate dose of SAG for i.v. administration because the effects of i.v. administration of SAG on TBI were not investigated by previous studies. We found that i.v. administration of SAG 1, 5, and 15 mg/kg per day mitigated FPI‐induced vasogenic edema. Subsequently, i.v. administration of SAG 5 mg/kg per day also reduced neuroinflammation and improved neurological dysfunction in TBI model mice. Although further examinations for the appropriate dose are essential, i.v. administration of SAG from 1 to 15 mg/kg once a day for several days may be an available treatment regimen for alleviating acute pathological conditions after TBI.

There are some potential limitations in this study. First, SAG was administered i.v. for up to 7 days in TBI model mice; however, the effects of chronic SAG administration on chronic‐phase TBI should also be examined. Second, we did not investigate the effects of SAG on white matter pathology after TBI. Alexandris et al. [45] found that TBI causes white matter pathology as well as terminal field degeneration across the rostrocaudal axis of the spinal cord and traumatic axonopathy is associated with diverse types of axonal and myelin pathology, ranging from focal axoskeletal perturbations. Third, we did not investigate the adverse side effects and potential off‐target effects of SAG. Accordingly, we should carefully investigate the relevance of the SAG dose used and its adverse side effects and potential off‐target effects in the future.

Conclusions

Our study revealed the following salient findings: First, endogenous Smo plays a key role in the recovery from TBI‐induced vasogenic edema. Second, repeated i.v. administration of SAG alleviates TBI‐induced vasogenic edema, neuroinflammation, motor dysfunction, and cognitive dysfunction. Third, SAG treatment increases the expression level of vascular protective factors and TJPs following TBI. These results suggest that Smo agonists are potentially promising therapeutic agents for TBI.
