Hemostatic Tough Adhesives seal tissue and control hemorrhage
Authors: Daniel O Kent, Phoebe S Kwon, Kyle C Wu, Matthew Torre, Hamza Ijaz, Favour Omafuvwe Obuseh, Brittany Pattison, Arthur Nedder, Francis McGovern, Jeremy Cannon, David J Mooney, Benjamin R Freedman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHemorrhage from internal organs remains a critical challenge in both trauma care and surgical procedures, as existing hemostatic adjuncts frequently fail to provide consistent and effective bleeding control, particularly under conditions of active bleeding or impaired coagulation. Here, we develop and evaluate a Hemostatic Tough Adhesive (HTA) in controlled preclinical models of traumatic solid organ injury and compare its performance against leading commercially available hemostatic agents. The HTA consistently outperformed its counterparts, achieving 100% hemostasis in both liver and spleen injuries within an in vivo preclinical porcine model. In contrast, existing adjuncts exhibited variable and often incomplete efficacy. Beyond immediate hemostasis, the HTA demonstrated prolonged stability and biocompatibility during the postoperative wound healing phase. Notably, the HTA exhibited tissue surface adhesion energy several orders of magnitude greater than that of current hemostatic products, indicating its potential utility for surgical and trauma-related bleeding management.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Bioinspired materials, Biomedical materials

Introduction

Intraoperative blood loss remains a critical challenge, with bleeding-related complications and/or blood transfusions occurring in approximately 30% of inpatient surgical procedures across various specialties1. Surgical complications account for one quarter of all transfusions in the U.S. each year2. Major bleeding is also associated with higher hospitalization costs, in some surgical specialties incurring additional costs exceeding $17,000 per case1. Additionally, bleeding-related complications extend hospital stays, with an average increase of 6.0 days and up to 9.6 days in certain specialties1. These factors collectively increase the risk of major perioperative complications, contribute to greater resource utilization, and place a significant burden on healthcare systems. Furthermore, approximately 40% of trauma-related deaths are caused by uncontrolled bleeding3. Severe or massive intraoperative bleeding has a combined mortality rate of over 20%4 that is further exacerbated by an aging population with comorbidities and anticoagulant use3–5. This problem is compounded in lower resource environments, such as rural areas6, on the battlefield7, in disaster zones8, safety-net healthcare settings9, and developing countries10. Achieving and maintaining hemostasis and preserving organ integrity is paramount to performing safe surgery, particularly when internal organs are repaired, manipulated, divided, or transplanted. Organs, such as the liver, spleen, kidneys, pancreas, and lungs, are particularly vulnerable to disruption, increasing the risk of coagulopathy and excessive hemorrhage11.

Current standard-of-care strategies to control surgical bleeding involve a combination of manual pressure, vascular ligation or embolization, electrosurgical coagulation, or application of surface hemostatic adjunct materials. Despite several products developed to address this pressing need8,12–14, the success of existing commercial products used to achieve hemostasis remains limited, and often require surgical techniques – such as vascular ligation, or thermal coagulation – to achieve reliable hemostasis. Common hemostatic adjuncts fall into three categories based on their effects on clotting: passive, active, and structural. Passive hemostatic adjuncts, which serve as a matrix to facilitate clot formation, are readily available for use, but these materials lose their hemostatic functions in the setting of coagulopathies associated with trauma15, when patient are on anticoagulant medications16, or in patients with inherited bleeding dyscrasias. Active hemostatic adjuncts, which chemically augment clot formation, often have short working periods prior to setting, provide only temporary hemostasis without long-term structural support, may increase the risk of re-bleeding and the need for additional interventions, and often do not display favorable physical characteristics in critical surgical scenarios17. Structural adjuncts promote rapid hemostasis through a combined sealant and adhesive effect via crosslinking, but existing agents may compromise normal dynamic organ function due to their rigidity and have been linked to significant cellular toxicities17,18. While hydrogel-based structural adjuncts have shown promise, they have yet to demonstrate robust tissue adhesion, often failing cohesively, which has limited their capacity to withstand high pressures and flow rates associated with hemorrhage.

The ideal material for treating solid organ hemorrhage while maximally preserving organ tissue would effectively reapproximate wound edges without excessive tension, adequately compress the affected organ to generate tamponade, promote localized thrombosis without adverse systemic effects, be rapidly applicable during emergency surgery, and reliably adhere in the presence of blood or other bodily fluids. Here, we explore a biocompatible hydrogel material synthesized from alginate and polyacrylamide, which, when combined with a chitosan-based tissue-facing adhesive, exhibits favorable characteristics and high adhesion to multiple tissue surfaces. Here we demonstrate that this system, named a Hemostatic Tough Adhesive (HTA), is a promising candidate for treating hemorrhage from internal organs (Fig. 1). We designed a translational research study to evaluate the intrinsic performance of the HTA, in both permanent and degradable forms whose different properties may have different therapeutic benefits, and compare its effectiveness against commercially available hemostatic adjuncts, both on fresh cadaveric tissue and during live porcine surgery for the repair of iatrogenic lacerations.

Results

Hemostasis Tough Adhesives demonstrate high tissue adhesion and failure resistance

Compared to commercially available surgical hemostatic adjuncts, the HTA demonstrated significantly higher resistance to failure during burst pressure testing while adhered to ex vivo porcine pericardial tissue (Fig. 2a) and tensile mechanical properties (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, adhesion strength testing on ex vivo porcine skin showed that HTA achieved an average interfacial toughness of 2500 J/m2, significantly surpassing existing adjuncts (Fig. 2b). HTAs demonstrated strong and reliable adhesion across various tissue types (Fig. 2c, d), with interfacial toughness up to 3376 J/m2, as measured ex vivo by peel testing. Notably, HTA adhered effectively in both wet and dry environments, as well as in the presence of blood, in part due to the hemorepellent properties of the chitosan (Fig. 2e). Hydrogel swelling post implantation has been examined in several studies suggesting minimal additional swelling after post synthesis wash steps19–21.

HTAs can withstand pressures in the supraphysiologic range

HTAs successfully bridged 1.5 cm linear defects in ex vivo porcine aortic tissue, maintaining intravascular hemostasis throughout prolonged cycles that mimicked physiological blood pressure using a peristaltic pump (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Additionally, HTAs withstood supraphysiologic intravascular pressures until failure, demonstrating resilience comparable to BioGlue ex vivo (Supplementary Fig. 2b) and in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

Hemostatic Tough Adhesives control hemorrhage from solid organs during live surgery

To test whether the HTA possessed favorable properties for use as a hemostatic adjunct, HTAs were applied to intraperitoneal and retroperitoneal tissues in pigs during laparotomy (Fig. 3a). Similar to bench performance testing, in vivo blinded and randomized controlled testing revealed robust adhesion to wet and actively bleeding solid organs compared to standard-of-care (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Movie 1). Following biopsy injury, HTAs achieved 100% control of bleeding to liver and spleen, while commercially available hemostatic adjuncts demonstrated variable performance (Fig. 3c). Acute evaluation of biocompatibility found that the HTA did not impact CBC blood panels (Supplementary Fig. 3). The HTA exhibited excellent adhesion and hemostatic roles on actively bleeding kidney (Supplementary Fig. 4) and adhesion to intraperitoneal surfaces, including the bladder (Supplementary Fig. 5). Following euthanasia, the HTA was found to remain in place for at least two weeks on solid organs (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Unlike commercial technologies which are easily displaced within 10 minutes following application, with none remaining in place after closure, the HTA revealed robust adhesion to liver, spleen, and kidney tissues (Supplementary Fig. 6a) while the opposite slide was frictionless (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Both the permanent and degradable formulations of the HTA demonstrated decreased bleeding scores during the index operation to liver and spleen tissues, unlike several commercial products (Supplementary Fig. 7, 8). Hemostatic control and adhesion of the HTA was independent of coagulation-competence, as manipulated via intravenous delivery of heparin (Supplementary Fig. 9). Based on clinical feedback, certain faster setup adhesives are desirable while still maintaining working time. For these reasons, we tested a recently published analog22,23 that could adhere within 60 seconds. A dried HTA was found to enable hemostasis within 1 minute of compression, thus enabling rapid hemostasis compared to standard-of-care (Supplementary Fig. 10).

HTAs provide durable hemostasis and facilitate safe wound healing during the critical postoperative phase

Five animals were included in the survival portion of the study, and all of them survived for the full two-week defined postoperative period. Animals treated with both non-degradable and degradable HTA were included in this study. For these survival implant studies, injury-only control groups were not included due to concerns from our IACUC regarding the risks of prolonged bleeding and associated animal welfare implications. During laparotomy at the end of the study, the injury sites were identified in all organs, with permanent HTAs present in 100% of application sites. The degradable HTAs were no longer present, as expected, since they were designed to degrade within 42 days. None of the animals experienced bleeding-related complications following surgery. After euthanasia, the organs were recovered for laboratory analysis.

Histological evaluation revealed no polymorphonuclear cells in the spleen or liver, suggesting an absence of acute inflammation or infection at the time of tissue sampling (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. 11). However, mild lymphocyte presence was noted in both organs, indicating a low level of chronic or subacute inflammation, with an average lymphocyte score of 2/4.

Discussion

Current strategies for managing hemorrhage from solid organ trauma involve correcting coagulopathy and controlling bleeding through vascular embolization, surgical ligation, or organ removal. However, repairing a fractured, bleeding solid organ is often not feasible due to the limitations of existing materials and techniques. Unlike other tissues, these organs lack dense connective tissue, making them less suitable for suture repair or mechanical ligation. The ability of the HTA to adhere without sutures represents a major advancement in the management of internal organ bleeding.

The HTA differs significantly from many emerging technologies24–26 in several key aspects. The fracture toughness and adhesion energy of the HTA is at least 10-1000x higher, making it more resistant to mechanical failure under stress, which is crucial for medical applications requiring robust adhesion. Unlike alternatives, the HTA is also transparent, making it more suitable for clinical settings where visibility of underlying tissues is important. In addition, the HTA offers significant advantages in terms of storage and handling, as it remains stable at room temperature and does not require refrigeration or complex preparation. Moreover, the HTA can be used effectively as a patch to cover large defects, providing versatility in a range of clinical applications. Finally, the HTA allows for tunable working time before adhesion begins, offering greater flexibility during application, giving the user more time to position and adjust the adhesive. These distinctions make the HTA a more adaptable and reliable option for a wide range of medical procedures. Unlike traditional hemostatic devices that depend on coagulation cascade proteins such as thrombin and fibrin for adhesion and hemostasis, the HTA adhesion functions through bridging polymer deprotonation upon interaction with tissue substrates23. This process promotes molecular entanglement with the tissue, creating strong adhesion even in the absence of a clot. Conversely, conventional devices may require a clot to form for adherence to tissue, whereas the HTA adheres before clot formation occurs. As a result, the HTA offers greater versatility and effectiveness, especially in conditions where clot formation is delayed or impaired, or where fibrinolysis may degrade adhesion of fibrin-dependent adjuncts.

Although several topical hemostatic agents are FDA approved, regulatory clearance is generally based on demonstration of safety and achievement of hemostasis under defined experimental conditions, rather than consistent improvement in downstream clinical outcomes or superiority over standard-of-care. As highlighted by multiple randomized trials, systematic reviews, and consensus statements,17,27–34 many approved hemostats provide modest, procedure-dependent benefits, and often fail to reduce transfusion requirements or postoperative bleeding complications despite being used routinely. These findings underscore the point that regulatory approval and residual clinical unmet need are not mutually exclusive. Persistent bleeding-related complications in surgical practice, therefore, continue to motivate the development of materials with improved robustness and reproducibility across injury types and physiologic conditions. Importantly, the HTA outperforms standard-of-care treatment techniques in both non-heparinized and heparinized conditions, indicating efficacy beyond native clot formation. Because porcine coagulation is more efficient than human coagulation35,36, animal models may overstate the benefit of coagulation-dependent adjuncts, while HTA’s tamponade-based mechanism may better translate to human settings of trauma, infection, or medical coagulopathy. A previous study reported that EVARREST had superior performance to TachoSil, as TachoSil achieved 0% hemostatic success at 3 minutes and only 4% at 10 minutes 33. Matonick et al. employed an incisional injury (15 mm × 3 mm depth) with a substantially larger patch (3 cm × 4.5 cm), extending approximately 1.5 cm beyond the wound margins, whereas our study used a deep punch injury (6 mm diameter, ~1 cm depth), with a 15 x 40 mm patch ( < half the wound margin). In addition, excess blood and fluid were removed prior to application in Matonick et al., whereas the HTA was applied directly to the bleeding surface in the present study, enabling effective placement in a wet environment. Review of publicly available FDA documentation for several commercially-available products highlight safety considerations, including inflammation, adhesion formation, re-bleeding, and thrombotic events, particularly in the context of repeat or chronic exposure. These observations do not diminish the clinical utility of existing products, but rather, they contextualize their limitations and inform design priorities for next-generation hemostatic materials. Together, enhanced adhesive robustness and attention to biocompatibility represent key differentiators of the present approach.

This work adds to our previous studies20,37–39, as the HTA maintains high interfacial toughness (greater than 1000 J/m²) in vivo, allowing it to resist detachment despite tissue motion, swelling, and degradation. While the present work builds on the foundational tough hydrogel framework40, it integrates multiple advances that were not previously combined or validated in large-animal acute and survival studies. Specifically, the hemostatic tissue adhesive incorporates enhanced and accelerated adhesion mechanisms and a degradable adhesive chemistry21–23, enabling faster and more reliable hemostasis under clinically relevant conditions. The convergence of these features, together with in vivo validation beyond prior studies, establishes the innovation of the current approach. The present work represents the an integrated system validated in a clinically relevant large-animal active-bleeding model, establishing hemostatic efficacy under conditions that directly mimic real surgical and traumatic hemorrhage. We believe this translational demonstration is a substantial advance beyond prior reports, which lacked evidence of robust performance in dynamic, high-flow injury environments.

Unlike conventional hemostatic agents that rely on blood coagulation to generate adhesion, the HTA forms a stable and direct interface with tissues. The chitosan bridging polymer penetrates both the hydrogel matrix and tissue, forming a topologically entangled interface that resists mechanical disruption. In addition to this physical interlocking, covalent amide bonds form via carbodiimide chemistry between tissue carboxyl groups and chitosan amine groups, further reinforce the interface. Interchain hydrogen bonding between deprotonated chitosan and ECM components also contributes to bond strength. The hydrogel’s double-network structure, composed of reversible ionic crosslinks with alginate and a covalently crosslinked polyacrylamide matrix, dissipates mechanical stress and prevents crack propagation or delamination. Electrostatic interactions between the positively charged chitosan and negatively charged tissue proteins further reinforce the interface and can reform after disruption, maintaining adhesion even during localized degradation or swelling. These adhesive mechanisms are sustained by a minimal inflammatory response, which helps maintain the contact between the hydrogel and tissue surface. Together, these interfacial bonding mechanisms, combined with the hydrogel’s inherent mechanical toughness and biocompatibility, contribute to the long-term stability of HTA adhesion in vivo. The mechanism of action for hemostasis is mechanical containment of the bleeding site. Containment results in coagulation due to platelet aggregation from a local tamponade effect on the bleeding surface, which appears to be independent of the presence of hematologic coagulation factors, and remains effective even under active bleeding or coagulopathic conditions.

There are many strategies for hemorrhage control that are leveraged by existing hemostatic adjuncts. Passive hemostatic adjuncts (e.g. Arista, Gelatin, Gelfoam, Oxidized Regenerated Cellulose (ORC), SurgiCel, Surgifoam, Omnistat) function by their ability to facilitate the body’s natural clotting mechanisms to generate a clot. Many of these time-tested products are employed broadly in surgery due to their simplicity and well-characterized functions, with recent iterations improving upon deployment strategies. The main drawbacks in this category of materials include reduced hemostasis once saturated, minimal adhesion to tissues, and poor function in the setting of coagulopathies (such as those associated with trauma15, in patients who are pharmacologically anticoagulated16, or in patients with inherited bleeding dyscrasias23). By contrast, active hemostatic adjuncts (e.g. EVARREST, FloSeal, Gelfoam-JMI, Surgiflo, Tachosil, Thrombigel, Thrombin, Tisseel, Vistaseal, Quikclot) promote thrombogenesis by exerting a biochemical effect on the clotting cascade, and this can have certain benefits in scenarios where a coagulopathy may exist or where there can be no tolerance for even miniscule bleeding (e.g. inside the neck). Due to their active nature, many have short working periods prior to setting, and they typically provide only temporary hemostasis without long-term structural support, which may increase the risk of re-bleeding and the need for additional interventions. Although high burst pressures of certain existing hemostatic adjuncts have been reported ex vivo, these agents suffer from excessive stiffness, rapid degradation of the adhesion to tissue, and/or excessive volumetric changes in vivo that may limit their clinical utility in many applications where tamponade of bleeding at physiologic or supraphysiologic blood pressure would be necessary. A third class of adjuncts promotes hemostasis through a combined sealant and adhesive effect, primarily for vascular anastomoses (e.g. BioGlue). Through crosslinking, these agents work quickly as tissue sealants, but they may compromise normal dynamic organ function due to their rigidity and have been linked to cytotoxicity18. While hydrogel-based sealants in this category have shown promise, they have yet to demonstrate robust tissue adhesion, often failing cohesively, which limit their capacity to withstand high pressures associated with hemorrhage41.

In the first phase of the HTA study, bench performance tests demonstrated that the system displayed tissue surface adhesion energy 400–2400x higher than other adjuncts, suggesting its potential to withstand high physiologic stress without any additional reinforcements. To further validate its performance under physiomimetic conditions, ex vivo testing using a peristaltic pump on porcine aorta was performed to show burst pressures of aorta excess of 600 mmHg (Fig. S2). The HTA was not designed to precisely match the mechanical parameters of specific target tissues, but rather to achieve compliant, viscoelastic behavior suitable for soft tissue adhesion. The measured storage modulus (10–20 kPa) and tan δ (0.2–0.4) correspond to a loss modulus of ~2–8 kPa, which lies within the range reported for soft organs such as liver and spleen42.

In the second phase, we validated bench tests in acute and chronic implant studies and investigated the effectiveness of the HTA. The porcine model was chosen due to its anatomical similarity to humans and its established validity in preclinical translational surgical studies32. As demonstrated in our prior studies20,22,37, the materials maintain multi-week adhesion in vivo despite swelling. While swelling can modestly reduce adhesion energy, the adhesion remains sufficient for robust tissue integration over clinically relevant timescales, as shown in our longitudinal evaluations. The HTA’s ability to exert a tamponade effect ultimately achieving hemostasis represented a significant advancement in the field, as no current surgical adjuncts can achieve adhesion to form an impermeable barrier without additional compression. Furthermore, the HTA successfully stopped bleeding in both non-heparinized and heparinized conditions, a clinically relevant finding since life-threatening hemorrhage frequently occurs concurrently with coagulopathy, whether induced for medical purposes or resulting from underlying illness. Finally, the HTA remained in place following irrigation with saline and remained in place on the organ surface for at least 2 weeks post implantation, which is unlike any of the approved adjuncts tested against the HTA, all of which tended to fall off within minutes. This underscores its compatibility with normal physiology, as most tissue surfaces and many organs in their entirety undergo dynamic motion, such as sliding, bending, dynamism, and volumetric changes. Such a system supports longer term protection against breakthrough bleeding during the acute phase following injury and the potential for reducing unwanted adhesion formation between bleeding sites and adjacent organs.

The HTA is transparent applied as a pre-formed patch, allowing the user to visually inspect the tissue surface through the adjunct and directly determine success of the treatment. Our study suggests minor tissue responses to the HTA samples placed in the spleen and liver, in contrast to other previous work in bioadhesives and sealants12. For example, FDA-approved gelatin-based hemostatic adjuncts (e.g. Surgifoam) have been suggested to form thicker fibrous capsules with high foreign body and inflammatory responses.

Future investigations will assess HTA with more complex injuries, for longer time intervals to evaluate long-term performance and potential adverse effects, while exploring the application of the hydrogel in other areas of surgery. To minimize bias in comparing HTA with other adjuncts, we implemented blinding strategies where possible, including assigning specific roles during procedures and ensuring that the researcher responsible for randomizing investigative materials were positioned away from the bedside to prevent external influences. It is noted that 6 mm circular punch biopsy injuries were for the purpose of standardizing the injuries in a controlled research setting, correlating roughly to AAST Organ Injury Scale grade I-II injuries. However, real-world traumatic injuries often present as irregular, jagged fractures, and disrupted hilar structures, making future studies critical to assess HTA’s performance in more complex injury patterns. Additionally, the study was not designed to evaluate long-term outcomes such as delayed local or systemic toxicity, highlighting the need for further research over extended periods. The faster setup evaluated may be further accelerated in future studies as other works have recently proposed hemostatic properties in <15–20 seconds and will be tested against these proposed systems, potentially through integration with other pharmacologically active agents that may further promote coagulation.

Methods

Ethical statement

The in vivo portion of the experiment was approved by the Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee at CBSET, Inc. (Protocol Number: 100309) and the Animal Research Committee at Boston Children’s Hospital/Harvard Medical School (Protocol #00001490).

Hydrogel synthesis

The HTA was synthesized by mixing one syringe containing 2% sodium alginate [MVG or LF20/40], (Pronova, Novamatrix Norway)] and 12% acrylamide (Sigma, A8887) in HBSS (Gibco), 2% N,N’-methylenebis(acrylamide) (Sigma, M7279), and TEMED (Sigma, T7024), with a second syringe containing 6.6% ammonium persulfate (Sigma, A9164), and 0.75 M calcium sulfate dihydrate (Sigma, 31221). The gel was cast into molds (80 x 15 x 1.5 mm3) sealed on both sides with glass and left to crosslink for 24 h. After 24 h, tough gel strips were removed from molds and stored in sealed plastic bags at 4 °C. For degradable tough gels, the degradable PEGDA (Mn: 250 Da, Sigma, 475629) crosslinker stock solution was mixed with the alginate monomer mixture prior to syringe mixing with calcium sulfate dihydrate, TEMED, and ammonium persulfate. For in vivo experiments, gel strips were prepared as 15 × 40 mm patches; these may be further trimmed to the desired dimensions as required.

Hydrogel mechanical properties testing

Pure shear tests were carried out to measure the matrix toughness. The free ends of HTA and the substrate were attached to acrylic pieces with cyanoacrylate (Krazy Glue), to which the machine grips were attached. A mechanical testing system (Instron 3342, 50 N load cell) was used to apply unidirectional tension, while recording the force and the extension. The loading rate was kept constant at 100 mm/min. From the stress-stretch curves, the matrix maximum stretch, maximum stress, and fracture toughness were calculated using custom MATLAB code.

HTA application to tissue

Ultrapure Chitosan HCl (54046) was obtained from Heppe Medical Chitosan (HMC), Halle, Germany. Chitosan (2%wt/vol) with or without coupling reagents 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC, Sigma E6383) and sulfated N-hydroxy-succinimide (sNHS, Thermofisher, PG82071) (12 mg/mL) were quickly mixed in water by vortexing. A final concentration of 12 mg/ml EDC/sNHS was used in adhesion experiments with coupling reagents. This mixture was applied to the surface of the tough gel (50 μL/cm2) (0.5 mL total/sample) and compressed to tissue surfaces.

Ex vivo tissue testing

Fresh porcine tissue was prepared for benchtop testing at room temperature by removing adjacent connective tissue using standard surgical instruments. A 1x HBSS solution was used to maintain hydration of the tissue. Samples of skin, liver, spleen, and aorta were utilized. Adhesion to tissues with HTA was performed utilizing the previously described technique. Products used for comparison, including Evarrest™, Tachosil™, Surgiflo™, Floseal™, and Surgicel SNoW®, were applied to tissues based on the manufacturer’s guidelines. Aortic burst pressure testing was performed by applying the investigative materials over 1.5 cm longitudinal incisions on samples of aorta, which were then attached to a peristaltic pump (MasterFlex #1230016) perfused with starched and dyed water. A pressure gauge attached to the circuit measured the maximum pressure before failure of the investigative material.

In vivo hemostasis study design

Our study was an in vivo porcine survival study comparing hemostasis between the investigative agent (HTA) and existing commercial agents advertised and clinically used for controlling bleeding during operative surgery, using a validated protocol for defining successful hemostasis.

In vivo surgical procedure and hemorrhage model overview

A total of 7 live female Yorkshire pigs were used for the solid organ hemostasis trial; 4 were non-survival studies and 3 were survival studies. Only female animals were used, and sex was not evaluated as a biological variable due to the limited sample size and primary focus on acute and chronic hemostatic performance. Personnel present at each index operation included, at minimum, a veterinary specialist, surgeon, research scientist, and data collector. Pigs were anesthetized and intubated, prepped and draped in a sterile fashion. Systemic anticoagulation in the form of intravenous heparin at a dose of 50–200 IU/kg was given to the first non-survival animal with target activated clotting time (ACT) of 200 to 350 s. A midline ventral laparotomy was made to expose the abdominal organs, and the abdominal wall was retracted. A 6 mm circular biopsy punch was brought into the field for use in each injury. Following the defined surgical study for each animal the laparotomy was closed or the pig was euthanized.

Anesthesia

The swine were premedicated with Telazol 4.4–6.6 mg/kg, xylazine 1.2–2.2 mg/kg IM, and atropine 0.04 mg/kg IM, after which anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane (2% to 3%) and oxygen. Bupivacaine was available for local analgesia at a dose of 3 mg/kg. For perioperative analgesia, buprenorphine 0.01 mg/kg via intravenous or intramuscular administration was utilized. Postoperatively, banamine 1-2 mg/kg via intravenous or intramuscular administration as needed was given, and a transdermal fentanyl patch with dosage of 1–4 µg/kg for 72 h was available as needed.

Definitions

Successful hemostasis was defined as no visual active blood loss out of the injury site observed during the 2-minute post-application time interval. Failure to achieve hemostasis was defined as ongoing bleeding despite additional pressure application, and/or material application, etc. to control bleeding from interfering with other subsequent injury treatments. Breakthrough bleeding was defined by blood actively running out of the treatment site.

Liver injuries

Any lobe of the liver could be used for injuries. A biopsy punch was utilized to create a parenchymal injury, after which the devitalized tissue plug was removed using Metzenbaum scissors. The tissue was then allowed to bleed freely for 30 seconds. In non-survival pigs, blood collection was attempted using gauze during this time, which were weight pre- and post- bleeding to determine blood volume lost. A visual assessment of the rate of bleeding was made by the data collector, and an attempt at manual control of bleeding with pressure and gauze was made by the surgeon and monitored by the data collector. The data collector then generated a score on the VIBe bleeding severity scale, which was later cross-tabulated with the SPOT bleeding score15. After confirmation and scoring of bleeding, the research scientist randomly assigned a hemostatic agent to be applied to the injury by the surgeon. The agent was held with manual pressure for 3 minutes, after which the site was observed for hemostasis. The time to hemostasis and volume of applied agent were recorded. Following the declaration of hemostasis, the site was observed for 2 additional minutes to evaluate breakthrough bleeding. If initial hemostasis was not achieved within the first 60 seconds, additional agent could be applied. If hemostasis did not occur after 3 minutes of observation, the treatment was considered a failure. If breakthrough bleeding (re-bleeding) was observed during the 2-minute observation period following the declaration of hemostasis, this was also noted as a failure.

Spleen injuries

A series of bleeding sites was prepared for testing by creating multiple 6 mm parenchymal injuries with a biopsy punch, similar to the injuries made on the liver. The tissue plug within the defect was then amputated from the deep tissue attachment with scissors and removed with forceps. The severity of bleeding from biopsy style injuries was graded on the VIBe bleeding severity scale, which was later cross tabulated with the SPOT bleeding score15. After confirmation of consistent bleeding from each injury site, the injury was blotted with a surgical sponge or gauze to remove pooled blood. The adhesive was applied and the time to hemostasis and volume of adhesive applied were recorded. Following the declaration of hemostasis, an additional 2-minute observation period could be utilized to ensure no breakthrough bleeding. If initial hemostasis was not achieved within 60 seconds, additional adhesive could be applied. If hemostasis did not occur after 3 minutes of observation, the treatment was considered a failure. If breakthrough bleeding (re-bleed) was observed during the 2-minute observation period following the declaration of hemostasis, this was also noted as a failure.

Kidney injuries

A series of bleeding sites were prepared for testing by creating incision injuries using a 6 mm biopsy punch. After confirmation of consistent bleeding from each injury site, the injury was blotted with a surgical sponge or gauze to remove pooled blood. The adhesive was then applied, and a stopwatch (or other appropriate timing device) was immediately started. The time to hemostasis and volume of adhesive was recorded. Following the declaration of hemostasis, an additional 2-minute observation period could be utilized to ensure that no breakthrough bleeding occurred. If initial hemostasis was not achieved within 60 seconds, additional adhesive could be applied. If hemostasis had not occurred after 3 minutes, this was noted as a failure, but the total observation time was still documented. Hemostasis was defined as no visual active blood loss observed during the 2-minute post-application observation time interval. Failure to achieve hemostasis was treated if needed with manual pressure application, and/or material application, etc. to control bleeding from interfering with other subsequent injury treatments.

Aortic injuries

The abdominal aorta was exposed by retracting other abdominal organs. The exposed aortic sections could be superior or inferior to the renal arteries. The diameter of the untouched aorta was measured just prior to the defect creation. A vascular clamp was used to isolate a section of the aorta but not completely obstructing flow. After the clamp was placed, the aorta was incised 2 cm in the longitudinal axis to create the defect. Once the defect was created, a repair was performed using conventional surgical techniques and sutures, and the adhesive was applied and allowed to adhere for 5 minutes prior to release of the clamp. The clamp was then removed and the aorta was observed for 15 minutes to ensure no bleeding. If severe active bleeding as noticed after the clamp release or during the 15-minute observation, additional adhesive, manual pressure, vascular clamping, or other surgical attempts could be applied to control the bleeding and would be documented.

At the beginning of the 15-minute observation, the repair sites and the distal and proximal regions were evaluated, and the success to achieve hemostasis was determined as absence of bleeding or only presence oozing, at 0 minutes. The result was documented. At 1, 3, 5, 10, and 15-minute time points, the sites were re-evaluated and the results were documented, if no active bleeding or any additional intervention occurred.

Survival study

The pigs that had been selected for the survival portion of the study were evaluated immediately for recovery from the operation and thereafter for the pre-determined period of two weeks. Behavior assessments of the animals as well as wound assessments were conducted daily to monitor for possible postoperative complications.

Post-mortem analysis

Data was collected during the study period and analyzed.

Histology

Representative uninjured and injured sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin following standard procedures. To assess biocompatibility, the authors involved in the animal surgery (D.O.K., P.S.K., F.O.O., and B.R.F.) selected tissue regions of interest (ROIs) containing the surgical site and bioadhesive implantation for each animal. A board-certified histopathologist, blinded to the treatment group, assessed at least 5 high powered fields in each of the designated ROIs for biocompatibility with a semiquantitative evaluation of inflammatory cells, necrosis, and tissue response (i.e. neovascularization, fibrosis, and fatty infiltrate).

Statistics & reproducibility

Based on past study40, a power analysis was conducted to determine the minimum sample for all assays. Sample sizes for individual experiments are reported in the corresponding figure legends. Animals and injury sites were randomized to treatment groups at the time of experimentation using a random assignment procedure. Blinding was implemented where feasible; specifically, investigators responsible for outcome assessment were blinded to treatment allocation during data collection. All attempts at replication were successful, and representative results are shown where applicable. Statistical analyses were performed as described in the figure legends with exact P values reported where appropriate.
