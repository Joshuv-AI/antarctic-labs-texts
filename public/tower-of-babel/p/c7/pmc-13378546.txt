The origin of Cretaceous-Palaeogene impactor revealed by nickel isotopes
Authors: Georgy V. Makhatadze, Frédéric Moynier, Leslie-Anne Brun, Steven Goderis, Philippe Claeys, Christian Koeberl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sixty-six million years ago, non-avian dinosaurs and other taxa went extinct during one of the largest mass extinctions in the history of life on Earth. The extinction was likely caused by an asteroid of unknown origin, responsible for the Chicxulub impact structure. Existing evidence identifies the impactor as an asteroid, similar to carbonaceous chondrites. However, carbonaceous chondrites are a diverse class of meteorites, leaving the exact nature of the impactor unclear. By measuring Ni isotopes in marine clays containing the impact ejecta, we constrain the impactor to one group of carbonaceous chondrites—CO chondrites—and some ungrouped chondrites. This study highlights the use of Ni isotopes in fingerprinting extraterrestrial materials on Earth. Given that CO chondrites are “dry” compared to the other carbonaceous chondrites and would have delivered much less volatiles, which are thought to be essential in causing the extinction, this work allows further studies of the mechanism behind the extinction.

INTRODUCTION

The Cretaceous-Palaeogene (K-Pg) mass extinction (also referred to as end-Cretaceous), being one of the “big five” mass extinctions, famously led to the disappearance of non-avian dinosaurs, but also pterosaurs and ammonites. Overall, it is estimated that of all the families, genera and species present at that time, 17%, 50% and 85%, respectively, went extinct (1). Coinciding with the extinction, an impact event happened resulting in the Chicxulub impact structure on Yucatán Peninsula, Mexico (2), which is interpreted to have been the main cause of the extinction (3–5), as originally proposed by Alvarez et al. (6). As indicated by a worldwide K-Pg boundary layer, containing abundant ejecta material from the impact site (7) as well as soot (8), the extinction mechanism was likely linked to an “impact winter” (i.e., a consequence of sunlight being partially blocked from reaching surface by the abundant dust in the atmosphere) and a release of volatiles that affected the composition of the atmosphere and the ocean, that followed the event (9, 10). While the exact mechanism and the extent of the impact-extinction of link are debated, the consensus is that the impact undoubtedly played a major role. The impactor itself is estimated to be a significant contributor to the ejected dust and volatiles (9, 11). Most importantly the impactor is expected to have contributed approximately 40% of the released S (∼4 × 1016 g of S in the form of SO2 and SO3), as well as smaller fractions of C (∼3 × 1016 g in the form of CO2), H2O (∼2 × 1017 g) and the halogens to the emissions from the impact site, assuming a CM-like impactor with a diameter of 10 km (11). As such, to further our understanding of the K-Pg mass extinction, it is necessary to understand the origin of the Chicxulub impactor itself, especially since different carbonaceous chondrites (CCs) which are the potential impactors (see below) can have very different volatile element abundances (12). Finally, documenting the provenance of the impactor is of importance in the assessment of the risks associated with potential future impact events and their consequences (13).

The existing evidence (7, 14–18), including Cr (19, 20) and Ru (21) isotope studies, requires a CC-like impactor. Other options, such as non-carbonaceous chondrites, achondrites and iron meteorites, are robustly excluded by these studies, most importantly based on the abundances and abundance ratios of the platinum group elements (PGEs) (7, 22). However, CCs are a diverse class of meteorites that represent vastly different parent bodies. While different studies favour different CC groups, most agree with CM or CR chondrites being at least a possibility (7, 14, 15, 18, 19). However, no study offers certainty and generally they allow for a variety of interpretations. Moreover, recent isotope evidence suggests that CI chondrites sample a fundamentally distinct Solar System reservoir, relative to the rest of the CCs (23–26). Additionally, ungrouped CCs represent many unique parent bodies unsampled by the groups and are often understudied. This includes the recently proposed groups, such as CY (27), CT and CZ (28) chondrites, and requires their inclusion into the consideration of potential origin of the K-Pg impactor.

Nickel isotope signatures can further elucidate the nature of the impactor. Specifically, we utilise Ni isotope compositions that are internally-normalised to correct for mass-dependent fractionation, associated with both the chemical history of the sample and instrumental mass-bias. Instead, these signatures primarily reflect uneven mixtures of different nucleosynthetic components of Ni and possibly a radiogenic contribution of 60Fe to 60Ni. Further in the text, we use “isotope compositions” to mean such internally-normalised isotope signatures (also often referred to as “nucleosynthetic signatures” and “isotope anomalies”), unless explicitly stated otherwise where relevant. Nickel isotopes carry several advantages. Nickel has five stable isotopes that allow for up to three separate internally-normalised signatures. Nickel is abundant enough for a high-precision isotope analysis in virtually all types of available early Solar System solids, from iron meteorites (29) to refractory inclusions (30), allowing comparison with a wide variety of potential impactors. Finally, CI chondrites have a distinct Ni isotope signature from the rest of the CCs (31), hence making it possible to distinguish a CI-like impactor clearly. To take advantage of that, in this study, we report high-precision internally-normalised Ni isotope data for samples from five K-Pg boundary sections at Stevns Klint (Denmark), Caravaca (Spain), as well as Furlo, Frontale and Fornaci (Italy). Additionally, to document the compositional diversity of the potential impactors, we report data for 11 CCs, sampling some of the key groups (CI, CM, CO and CV), as well as the ungrouped meteorites, including those from the proposed CT, CY and CZ groups.

Most CCs studied here with the exception of Orgueil (CI1) and Y-82162 (C1/2-ung or the proposed CY1) display typical CC μ60Ni61/58- and μ62Ni61/58-values in the range from −13.3 ± 4.2 to −4.7 ± 6.5 and from 5.0 ± 8.0 to 22.9 ± 11.0, respectively. Orgueil and Y-82162 have μ60Ni61/58-values of 0.4 ± 3.5 and 1.2 ± 3.7, respectively, typical for CI chondrites (31). As such, the CCs form two distinct groups and only one proposed CT3 chondrite, notably with a larger than usual uncertainty (NWA 14139), has a composition intermediate between the two groups. The μ60Ni61/58- and μ62Ni61/58-values between the two groups are weakly correlated. The K-Pg boundary samples exhibit anti-correlated μ60Ni61/58- and μ62Ni61/58-values ranging from −7.9 ± 4.7 to 0.3 ± 2.3 and from −3.1 ± 8.6 to 8.8 ± 6.1, respectively. The overall population can be characterised with by a mean and a variance (2SD) of −3.9 ± 5.1 and 4.6 ± 8.9 for μ60Ni61/58- and μ62Ni61/58, respectively. The trend stretches from the Bulk Silicate Earth (BSE) composition towards the typical CC values, approximately in the following order: Fornaci, Frontale, Furlo, Stevns Klint and Caravaca, where the Stevns Klint and Caravaca samples have CC-like compositions, while the other samples display BSE-like compositions. This is in a broad agreement with the PGE evidence suggesting that Stevns Klint and Caravaca sections contain approximately 5% ejecta by mass, while Fornaci and Frontale contain 1% and Furlo contains 0.5% ejecta, approximately (7).

DISCUSSION

Carbonaceous chondrites as potential impactors

To provide the framework to interpret the K-Pg boundary data, we must first characterise the Ni isotope composition of the CCs, the potential impactors. Nickel isotope data exist for most CC groups, barring the CK and CL chondrites, as well as the ungrouped CCs Tagish Lake and Tarda (24, 31, 33). However, among the ungrouped CCs, studies have identified several potential groups that are not formally recognised yet. These include the proposed CY (27), CT and CZ (28) chondrites studied here. Unsurprisingly, they lack high-precision isotope data for not only Ni, but also for most elements other than the O used for classification, with the exception of Ti, Cr and Fe data for the proposed CY chondrites (34, 35). An essential feature of CCs in some of the isotope systems, such as Mg (26), Fe (36) and Ni (24), is that CI chondrites are isotopically distinct from the rest of the CCs, which is often interpreted as CIs sampling a different reservoir. Figure 1 shows the Ni isotope systematics of the CCs. Most ungrouped meteorites plot in a relatively tight cluster together with the majority of CCs, indicating likely shared or similar reservoirs of origin. However, the proposed CY chondrite sample (Y-82162) shares the same Ni isotope signature as CI chondrites, which is distinct from the rest of the CCs. The CY chondrites are a proposed group of meteorites that share similarities with both CI and CM chondrites. One of their most prominent features, unusual for the other CCs, is the presence of a post-hydration thermal metamorphism, likely due to an impact (27, 37–39). The proposed CY chondrites are usually split into CY1 (including the analysed here Y-82162) and CY2 subgroups, CI-like with no chondrules and CM-like with chondrules, respectively (37). Questions arise, whether they sample distinct parent bodies and whether the proposed CY1 subgroup accreted chondrules initially, which were later made unrecognisable by aqueous alteration (37), as opposed to accretion without chondrules in the first place. Specific Ti isotope signatures suggest distinct parent bodies (34, 35). However, it is possible for those to be significantly heterogeneous between samples of the same parent body given a heterogeneous distribution of calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions, considering they are very enriched in highly isotopically anomalous Ti (40). To test whether any sample mass related bias exists, we compare μ50Ti49/47-values with sample mass from the earlier studies (34, 35) (fig. S1). Except for one data point, the two subgroups have distinct Ti isotope compositions regardless of the sample size. This supports the idea that the proposed CY1 and CY2 subgroups sample two distinct parent bodies. While the CY2 subgroup may sample a unique parent body, previously unrepresented in meteorite collections, the CY1 subgroup was proposed to originate from the same parent body as CI chondrites based on the similar O, Ti, Cr and Fe isotope signatures (34, 35). The identical Ni isotope signature shared by the proposed CY1 and CI chondrites further supports this idea. Overall, with the addition of the new data, CCs sample two major Ni isotope reservoirs to serve as potential origin of the K-Pg impactor.

Nature of the Cretaceous-Palaeogene impactor

K-Pg boundary samples, with Ni isotope compositions intermediate between BSE and CCs, can be used to constrain the nature of the K-Pg impactor. These samples represent variable mixtures of the local sediments, target rock and the impactor material, the latter two of which were spread-out globally in the aftermath of the impact. No nucleosynthetic Ni isotope variability has been recorded for Ni isotopes among the terrestrial mantle and crustal samples (41) and none is expected, barring impact-contaminated rocks, such as the ones studied here, and pre-late veneer rocks (42). Moreover, no significant mass-independent isotope effects that could mimic the nucleosynthetic isotope variability are expected for Ni isotopes in terrestrial rocks (41, 43, 44). As such, the BSE Ni isotope composition can be reasonably used as a proxy for both the local sediments and the target rocks. Figure 2 compares the K-Pg boundary samples to chondrites and BSE in a μ60Ni61/58-μ62Ni61/58-space. Some of the samples, from Furlo and Fornaci, have compositions overlapping with the enstatite chondrites (EC), however these samples are also very close to the BSE composition. This can be easily explained by these samples being the least impactor-enriched (7). To infer the mixing line and its extraterrestrial end-member, a York regression through the K-Pg boundary samples and BSE as a proxy for the isotope composition of the terrestrial rocks can be used. Since the regression passes through the majority of the CCs but not CI and the proposed CY chondrites, the latter two can be unambiguously excluded as potential impactors in agreement with Cr (19) and Ru (21) isotope data, as well as other constraints (7, 14). To further constrain the impactor origin, we investigate the mixing relationship in μ60Ni61/58–1/[Ni]-space (Fig. 3), where we show a weighted regression through the K-Pg boundary samples and the upper continental crust (UCC). We use the Ni isotope composition of the BSE as a proxy of the UCC, given the lack of nucleosynthetic Ni variability among terrestrial reservoirs. This analysis readily excludes most other potential impactor compositions, including CM chondrites, currently most favoured in the literature (14). Instead, the Ni data strongly necessitate an impactor of the CO or the proposed CT composition. We also note that using a proxy for the target rocks and local sediments different from the UCC is most likely to result in similar or even lower Ni concentrations (45), which would move the inferred mixing line further from the majority of the CCs towards the CI chondrites, that are excluded as an option based on the Fig. 2.

So far, a CM-like impactor has been favoured based on multiple lines of evidence. Chromium isotopes were interpreted to reflect a CM impactor, however CV and CR provide a near-match as well (19). Ruthenium isotopes suggest any CC with only a CI-like impactor robustly excluded (21). Moreover, PGE ratios favour a CO or a CM impactor, while also allowing for CR chondrites (7). A fossil meteorite, found in the K-Pg boundary in the Northern Pacific and which may be the remnant of the Chicxulub impactor, matches CV, CO, CR and, potentially, CM chondrites (15). Finally, the K-Pg boundary samples in Stevns Klint (Denmark) contain high amounts of α-amino-isobutyric acid (α-AIBA) and racemic (i.e., with sub-equal amounts of enantiomers of both chiralities) isovaline (ISOVAL), both of which are amino acids that are rare in terrestrial materials, yet abundant in carbonaceous chondrites. As such, their abundance has be interpreted to stem from their delivery during the impact (18). A CM or a CR chondrite impactor matches the α-AIBA/ISOVAL ratio (14, 46, 47). However, the α-AIBA/Ir ratio in the K-Pg samples is too high to confidently allow for a CM chondrite (18), leaving only CR chondrites (46–48). None of the other chondrite groups, including CO chondrites, have appropriately high abundances of amino acids and their particular compositions (49).

As such, the CO or the proposed CT chondrite impactor implied by Ni isotope data presented here is contradicted by two lines of evidence in the literature, namely Cr isotopes and amino acids abundances. When a larger Cr isotope dataset is considered (50–55), CO chondrites overall have a higher μ54Cr50/52-signature, than what was known based on a single meteorite measured originally (19), which reconciles the Cr and Ni isotope evidence. Figure 4 shows a mixing line between upper continental crust (BSE isotope composition) and CO chondrites in a μ60Ni61/58-μ54Cr50/52-space. As predicted, with the additional Cr isotope data, the K-Pg boundary samples agree with a CO-like impactor for both Cr and Ni isotope data. Some uncertainty is associated with assuming upper continental crust-like elemental abundances for the terrestrial end-member of the mixing line, which may affect its curvature. However, both the Stevns Klint and the Caravaca sections are some of the most enriched in the ejecta, comprised of approximately 5% impactor material (7) by mass. This corresponds to at least approximately 60% of Cr and 90% of Ni in these clays deriving from the impactor, assuming mixing with UCC (56). These fractions would be even higher if the pre-impact sediments in these localities were depleted in Cr and Ni relative to the UCC. As a result, the Cr and Ni isotope compositions of these samples are nearly indistinguishable from the CO isotope composition. As such, the conclusion would not change even under a different assumption about the elemental abundances in the pre-impact sediments. A similar exercise can be performed for comparing Ni and Ru isotope evidence, which supports the same conclusion (see Fig. 5). Alternatively, C and N stable isotopes challenge the constraint based on the amino acid studies. Specifically, bulk K-Pg boundary samples and their individual amino acids are enriched in light C and N isotopes, implying a terrestrial biogenic origin, likely associated with thermal alteration of coal, as opposed to being the result of extraterrestrial delivery, since, at least in CM chondrites, amino acids are enriched in heavy isotopes of C and N (57). This demonstrates that CO chondrites are in fact compatible with the existing evidence. Distinguishing between a CO- or the proposed CT-like impactor is not possible at present based on the existing evidence and the presented Ni isotope data, thus requiring further studies. Identifying Cr isotope signature recorded by the proposed CT chondrites is likely the most promising avenue in this perspective. Given the comparative rarity of the proposed CT chondrites (28), CO chondrites are considerably more likely.

Note that for Fornaci data, samples from different sections within the same region were used for Ni and Ru isotopes (Fonte D’Olio section for Ru), which might cause different impactor contributions being recorded by different isotope systems, which can explain the deviation of the Fornaci data point from the mixing line.

Many isotope systems (55) including Ni (31) show that for most meteorites their isotope signatures are characterised by belonging to one of the two reservoirs, carbonaceous (CC) and non-carbonaceous (NC). This includes differentiated meteorites as well, which in many cases have isotope compositions indistinguishable from the chondrites. This raises a question about whether the impactor was a differentiated body. While an achondrite or an iron meteorite impactor is easily discarded based on the Cr and PGE budget and ratios (22), it could still be possible that the projectile was an intact differentiated body preserving both its core and the mantle, and having a chondritic bulk composition. A number of reasons make this possibility highly unlikely. Some studies indicate that the Chicxulub impactor likely originated from the break-up event from a larger asteroid (58), already implying that it was not an intact differentiated body. Additionally, as was mentioned earlier, the fossil meteorite was found that is potentially a remnant of the Chicxulub impactor (15). While this does not present direct evidence considering the possibility of it being an unrelated meteorite, this meteorite is indeed identified as a chondrite. Moreover, the impactor is estimated to be approximately 10–15 km in diameter (6), which is too small a planetesimal to retain sufficient heat to differentiate (59). Finally, the asteroid population seems to be dominated by bodies that are products of collisional fragmentation of larger bodies, at least in the required diameter range (60), while meteorites delivered to the Earth are predominantly primitive (61). As such, the impactor was most likely a primitive body.

The implications of a CO-like impactor on our understanding of the extinction mainly lies with its relatively low volatile element abundances. As highly volatile elements, such as C, N (62) and S (12), in CO chondrites are less abundant than in the typical CCs, questions arise whether the impactor was an essential contributor to the volatiles released during the impact. Most importantly, S released from the impactor is expected to be a major contributor to the extinction (10), which, however, is based on estimates using CM chondrites. CO chondrites, however, would deliver approximately half as much S (12). In conclusion, by combining our new Ni isotope data with the existing evidence, we demonstrate that the composition of the Chicxulub impactor was most likely CO chondrite-like. This identification is possible given nickel’s high abundance in primitive meteorites compared to the Earth’s crust and the distinct isotope compositions they record. Our study highlights the extraterrestrial origin of the K-Pg mass extinction and the isotopes of Ni as a well-suited tool to characterise the nature of extraterrestrial material on Earth. Finally, it opens the door for further research into the connection between the impact and the mass extinction.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Nickel purification

In this study we processed two sets of samples, namely K-Pg-boundary samples and CCs sampling both grouped and ungrouped meteorites. Both sets are supplemented with appropriate geostandards that were processed using the same protocol. The following chemical procedures were all conducted in a clean laboratory environment. Acids used were purified via sub-boiling PFA stills, while NH4OH was purchased as ultra-pure (TraceSELECT, Honeywell Fluka). Water was purified using a Milli-Q system to a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25°C. We took aliquots of the powdered samples, typically ranging from ∼10 mg for CCs (corresponding to ∼100 μg of Ni) up to ∼300 mg for K-Pg samples (∼50 μg of Ni), subject to sample availability and Ni concentration. NIST SRM 2711 and some of NRCC MESS-3 aliquots were up to ∼900 mg due to lower Ni concentrations. The samples were then digested in PFA beakers in a 3:1 mixture of 28 M HF and 16 M HNO3 on a hot plate at 120°C, during at least three days. To finalise the digestion and remove any fluorides that may have formed, we repeatedly dried the samples down on the hotplate, once in 16 M HNO3 and then three times in 10 M HCl. Finally, the samples were centrifuged to separate any potentially remaining solids. Subsequently, the samples were processed through the column chemistry. Nickel is notoriously difficult to isolate from a typical rock sample matrix and even more so from relatively Ni-poor K-Pg boundary clays, with Mg posing the main challenge. Different protocols are required subject to the sample matrix, and as such the K-Pg-boundary samples and the CCs were processed separately. The chondrite protocol for the CCs was essentially a shortened version of the clay protocol for the K-Pg boundary samples (see Table 1). The protocols of the individual chemistry steps are detailed in the table S5.

Separation of Ni and Mg using liquid chromatography is challenging. Dimethylglyoxime (DMG) is highly Ni-selective reagent (63) that is often used for this purpose and it can be used with two main approaches of relevance. One is based on an HCl-acetone-DMG mixture to dissolve the sample and pass it through a cation-exchange column, however Ni in this medium is easily volatilised and can be lost when the sample is dried (31, 64, 65). Instead, we utilised another approach, which is to use a Ni resin, which consists of DMG in the pores of a polymer base (66). This approach, however, requires dissolving a sample in a basic NH4OH-HCl solution with a pH of approximately 9 (67), which can prove difficult depending on the sample matrix. One of the main “troublemaker” elements in this regard is Al. To remove it, we implemented a cation-exchange column with a dilute HNO3-HF eluent (68, 69). However, as most matrix elements interact with the cation-exchange resin in these conditions, to avoid overloading the column, prior Fe-removal (36) and Ca-removal steps (70) were introduced. The Fe-removal and Ca-removal steps were applied to the K-Pg boundary samples only. Finally, a Zn-Co-Fe-removal step was used (68, 71), which is especially important to remove any trace amount of Fe remaining to minimise the effects of the isobaric interference from 58Fe. Due to the low Ni concentrations, some chromatography steps were repeated for the K-Pg boundary samples. To ensure that the prolonged chemistry does not affect the measured isotope compositions, one sample (Stevns Klint-1b, an aliquot taken from Stevns Klint-1a after it was processed through the chemistry) was processed one time extra through the chemistry steps 3 to 5 (see table S5).

Nickel isotope measurements

We conducted high-precision nickel isotope measurements using a Thermo Scientific Neptune Plus MC-ICP-MS located at the Institut de Physique du Globe de Paris. Purified samples were dissolved in 0.5 M HNO3 typically resulting in a Ni concentration of approximately 350 ppb. The solutions were introduced into the mass spectrometer using a nebuliser with an uptake rate of ∼100 μl/min and an ESI Apex HF desolvator with N2 as an additional gas. We utilised Ni Jet sample and X skimmer cones at the interface. The measurements were conducted in high or medium resolution mode, maintaining M/ΔM > 5000. The cup configuration used was 57Fe, 58Ni, 60Ni, 61Ni, 62Ni and 64Ni on L4, L2, C, H1, H2 and H4 Faraday cups, respectively, each of which was fitted with a 1011 Ω amplifier. We decided not to accommodate 66Zn, typically used for interference correction on 64Ni into the cup configuration, as it would require a zoom optics tuning unfavourable for the peak shape and overall data quality, in return for little benefit in this instance. This setup resulted in a typical 58Ni signal of ∼35 V. Measurements were performed on the left shoulder of the peak to avoid ArO-based interferences on masses 57 and 58. Peak centers were conducted in the beginning of each measurement session, which lasted up to 24 hours. NIST SRM 986 Ni isotope standard was used for sample-standard bracketing and a blank solution was measured before each sample or standard. An individual measurement consisted of 100 cycles (15 for the blanks) of 16.777 s integrations. Most samples were measured 10 times (specified as n for each sample in Table 1) and the reported data are means of the n measurements with 2SD outlier rejection. All Ni isotope data were internally normalised according to exponential law to correct for mass-dependent isotope fractionation in nature and in the instrument (72). We chose 61Ni/58Ni and 62Ni/61Ni for the internal normalisation, using the values of 0.016744214 and 3.188479, respectively (32). An isobaric interference from 58Fe on 58Ni was corrected using the measured 57Fe, assuming the initial 58Fe/57Fe-ratio of 0.1338378 (73) was subjected to the same instrumental mass bias as Ni. However, as we preferred not to accommodate 66Zn into the cup configuration and the 64Ni data require an isobaric interference correction for 64Zn, these data are not reported. Additionally, two samples with uncertainties on μ60Ni61/58 or μ62Ni61/58 in excess of 10 and 15 ppm (each measured ∼10 times), respectively, were excluded from the final results as those reflect unfavourable measurement conditions. Specifically, we failed to obtain meaningful data for one sample of the geostandard NRCC MESS-3 and one K-Pg boundary sample (Dogie Creek, USA). Importantly, the Dogie Creek sample contains a very small ejecta fraction of approximately 0.5% by mass (7) and, as such, a very low Ni concentration that prevented us from effectively isolating and measuring its Ni isotope composition.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed in R (74). Weighted linear regression was made using its built-in functionality. York regression, using a unified algorithm based on the least squares approach, (75, 76) and weighted means (accounting for potential overdispersion of data) were calculated using IsoplotR (77).
