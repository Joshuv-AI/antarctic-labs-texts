The DCC1–DCCR1 phytocytokine–receptor kinase pair activates basal immunity in wheat
Authors: Chuanchun Yin, Siru Shi, Zhaoxi Lu, Hongxu Li, Lijun Wang, Yongjian Zhang, Jinghui Yan, Cuicui Du, Haimiao Zhang, Hongqian Lu, Yaxi Zhu, Xiao Luo, Chao Wang, Guochen Qin, Xinhua Ding, Shuguo Hou
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

To combat pathogen invasion, plants use immune receptors that detect immunogenic molecules to trigger immune responses and confer resistance. Cell-surface-resident pattern recognition receptors activate pattern-triggered immunity through the perception of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), damage-associated molecular patterns, and phytocytokines (PCKs) (Zhou and Zhang, 2020; Jones et al., 2024). PCKs are endogenous immunoregulatory peptides overproduced and released into the apoplast during pathogen challenge. Upon recognition by pattern recognition receptors, PCKs trigger a coordinated immune signaling network characterized by activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades, cytosolic calcium influx, reactive oxygen species burst, and transcriptional reprogramming (DeFalco and Zipfel, 2021). Recent years have brought major advances in identifying PCKs and in understanding the mechanisms underlying their perception and downstream signaling networks. Examples include the PCKs PAMP-INDUCED SECRETED PEPTIDE 1 and SERINE-RICH ENDOGENOUS PEPTIDE 12, which are specifically recognized by the receptor kinases (RKs) RECEPTOR-LIKE KINASE 7 and MALE DISCOVERER 1-INTERACTING RECEPTOR-LIKE KINASE 2, respectively, leading to the activation of convergent pattern-triggered immunity (Hou et al., 2014, 2021; Rhodes et al., 2021). Another example is the PCK SMALL PHYTOCYTOKINES REGULATING DEFENSE AND WATER LOSS 1 (SCREW1), which is perceived by its cognate receptor PLANT SCREW UNRESPONSIVE RECEPTOR (NUT) to enhance resistance by promoting tissue dehydration (Liu et al., 2022). However, the role of PCKs in wheat immunity remains largely unexplored. Here, we characterize a novel wheat PCK, DUAL CYSTEINES IN C-TERMINUS 1 (DCC1). Through genetic and biochemical analyses, we show that DCC1 is specifically recognized by the leucine-rich repeat RK (LRR-RK) DCC RECEPTOR 1 (DCCR1), which activates wheat resistance to Xanthomonas translucens, the causal agent of bacterial leaf streak in wheat.

To identify potential PCKs in wheat, we screened the hexaploid wheat (Triticum aestivum cv. China Spring) genome for genes encoding small (50–100 aa) secreted proteins upregulated by pathogens and PAMPs. We identified a gene family encoding a peptide precursor with a conserved C-terminal double-cysteine motif, which we designated pDCCs. The pDCC family in hexaploid wheat consists of 13 members organized into six homoeologous groups (pDCC1–pDCC6) (Supplemental Table 1). Of these, six homologous genes from the pDCC1 and pDCC2 groups showed significant upregulation in response to PAMPs (flg22 and chitin) (Figure 1A) and diverse pathogen infections (Supplemental Figures 1A–1D). These findings suggest that the pDCC gene family plays a key role in regulating wheat basal immunity and conferring broad-spectrum disease resistance. Subcellular localization analysis showed that pDCC1-GFP and pDCC2-GFP mainly displayed punctate distributions at the plasma membrane, with strong colocalization with SYP41-RFP, a well-established marker of the secretory pathway (Supplemental Figures 3A and 3B). This observation suggests that pDCC1 and pDCC2 are secreted peptides that traffic through the conventional secretory route. However, we did not detect clear apoplastic localization, possibly because of GFP fluorescence quenching in the acidic extracellular environment or the need for additional proteolytic processing for release from the plasma membrane. To identify the mature form of pDCC1, we collected apoplastic fluids (AFs) from leaves of transgenic wheat overexpressing pDCC1. Peptides in the 0.5–3 kDa range from the AFs were analyzed by mass spectrometry, which revealed a high-confidence peptide, designated DCC1, that precisely matched the C-terminal decapeptide of pDCC1 (Figure 1B). We synthesized DCC1 and demonstrated its potent induction of wheat MAPK activation at concentrations as low as 10 nM (Figure 1C). All five DCC1 analogs, corresponding to six pDCC homoeologous groups, consistently exhibited MAPK-inducing activity (Supplemental Figure 4A; Supplemental Table 1). Site-directed mutagenesis replacing one or both C-terminal cysteines with serines in DCC1 (DCC1CC/SS) completely abolished MAPK activation (Supplemental Figure 4B). Consistently, RT–qPCR showed that DCC1, but not DCC1CC/SS, upregulated the expression of the immune-related genes TaWRKY28 and TaPUB23 (Figure 1D). DCC1 displayed remarkable thermal stability, retaining full activity after incubation at 50°C for 6 h or even boiling at 95°C for 1 h (Supplemental Figure 4C). Notably, repeated foliar application of 5 μM DCC1 caused no observable phytotoxicity or growth retardation in wheat (Supplemental Figure 4D), highlighting its potential as a safe and effective resistance inducer. Pre-treatment of wheat leaves with DCC1, but not DCC1CC/SS, significantly enhanced wheat resistance to X. translucens pv. undulosa (Xtu) Kn5 (Figure 1E). Consistently, transgenic wheat overexpressing pDCC1 showed significantly enhanced resistance to Xtu Kn5 infection (Figure 1F and Supplemental Figure 5A). By contrast, dcc1/dcc2 double mutants showed no significant difference in bacterial susceptibility compared with wild-type (WT) plants (Supplemental Figures 5B and 5C), implying functional redundancy among DCC paralogs. In addition, wheat DCC1 and its orthologous peptides (OsDCC1–OsDCC4 and ZmDCC1) displayed species-preferential immune activation profiles (Supplemental Figures 6A–6E). Exogenous application of either TaDCC1 or OsDCC1 enhanced rice resistance to X. oryzae pv. oryzicola (Supplemental Figure 6F).

To further confirm that DCC1 maturation is essential for its activity, we synthesized four peptide fragments of pDCC1 (DCC1-34, DCC1-16, DCC1-9, and DCC1-N20) (Supplemental Figure 7A). DCC1-34, DCC1-16, and DCC1-9 displayed significantly weaker immune-inducing activities, whereas DCC1-N20 completely lost activity compared with the 10-aa DCC1 (Supplemental Figure 7A). These results suggest that DCC1 represents the optimally processed bioactive form of pDCC1, consistent with previous findings in rice (Wang et al., 2023). Furthermore, DCC1-34 induced a delayed MAPK phosphorylation pattern relative to DCC1 (Supplemental Figure 7B), consistent with the requirement for additional proteolytic processing. This hypothesis was further supported by the observation that pre-treatment with wheat AF markedly increased the MAPK-activating capacity of DCC1-34 in wheat (Supplemental Figure 7C). To further verify DCC1 processing, we designed a fluorescence-quenching heptapeptide reporter (LGDLKSG, FQ7) spanning the N-terminal cleavage site of DCC1, with DABCYL (quencher) and EDANS (fluorophore) at opposite termini. In vitro assays showed that the FQ7 peptide was efficiently processed by wheat AFs but not by heat-inactivated AFs. Processing was strongly blocked by the serine protease inhibitor EPI1a (Supplemental Figure 7D), suggesting that a serine protease(s) in wheat AFs is required for DCC1 maturation.

To identify DCC1 receptors, we performed a targeted screen of XI subfamily (subfamily 11) LRR-RK members upregulated by PAMPs (Supplemental Figure 8A; Supplemental Table 2). Several candidate genes were cloned and tested for their ability to mediate DCC1-triggered immunity using Arabidopsis protoplast transient expression assays. One gene, named DCCR1, mediated DCC-induced MAPK activation (Supplemental Figures 8B and 8C). Ectopic expression of DCCR1 in transgenic Arabidopsis conferred DCC1-induced MAPK activation, upregulation of WRKY30 and CYP81F2, inhibition of root growth, and enhanced resistance to Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (Supplemental Figures 9A–9D). Next, we generated wheat dccr1 mutants using CRISPR–Cas9-mediated gene editing (Supplemental Figure 10A). Loss of DCCR1 abolished DCC-induced MAPK activation and defense gene expression while preserving responsiveness to the canonical PAMP flg22, indicating a specific role for DCCR1 in DCC1-triggered immunity (Figures 1G and 1H and Supplemental Figure 10B). Furthermore, DCC1-induced resistance to Xtu Kn5 was compromised in dccr1 mutants (Figure 1I). Notably, wheat dccr1 mutants were more susceptible to Xtu Kn5 than WT plants (Figure 1J), highlighting the critical role of the DCC1–DCCR1 module in wheat resistance. Knockout of the closest DCCR1 homolog in rice also abolished OsDCC1-induced MAPK activation (Supplemental Figures 10C–10E), indicating functional conservation of the DCC–DCCR1 module in Poaceae immunity.

DCCR1 contains an N-terminal signal peptide, an extracellular domain with 22 LRR repeats, a transmembrane domain, and a cytosolic kinase domain (Supplemental Figure 11A). Confocal microscopy showed that DCCR1-GFP localized to the plasma membrane (Supplemental Figure 11B). To further test whether DCCR1 acts as a DCC1 receptor, we synthesized TAMRA-labeled DCC1/2 (TAMRA-DCC1/2), which retained comparable bioactivity to DCC1/2 (Supplemental Figure 12). When co-incubated with Arabidopsis seedlings, TAMRA-DCC1 peptides specifically bound to the plasma membrane of DCCR1-expressing plants but not WT plants (Figure 1K). This binding was competitively inhibited by unlabeled DCC1 (Figure 1K), demonstrating specific DCC1–DCCR1 interaction. We then expressed the extracellular LRR domain of DCCR1 (DCCR1LRR) in insect cells and detected direct DCC1/2–DCCR1LRR binding using isothermal titration calorimetry. DCCR1LRR bound to DCC1 and DCC2 with dissociation constants (KD) of 16 and 28 μM, respectively (Figure 1L). By contrast, DCC1CC/SS failed to bind DCCR1LRR (Figure 1L), indicating that cysteine residues in DCC1 are required for interaction. Because the KD of the DCC1–DCCR1LRR interaction is much higher than the saturation concentration (∼10 nM) for immune activation, we propose that additional factors, such as post-translational modifications of DCC1, may enhance binding in planta. The intracellular kinase domain of DCCR1 (DCCR1KD) displayed autophosphorylation activity in vitro (Figure 1M). A kinase-inactive mutant, DCCR1KDK729E, harboring a lysine-to-glutamate substitution at the conserved ATP-binding site, lacked autophosphorylation activity (Figure 1M). When expressed in Arabidopsis protoplasts, DCCR1K729E failed to mediate DCC1-induced MAPK activation (Figure 1N). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DCCR1 perceives DCC1 through its extracellular LRR domain and mediates immune signaling via its kinase activity.

In summary, we identified the 10-aa PCK DCC1 in wheat and showed that DCC1 and its homologs possess immune-inducing activity. DCC1 activates robust immune responses and enhances resistance against Xtu in wheat and X. oryzae pv. oryzicola in rice. We further identified DCCR1 as a DCC1 receptor, which specifically recognizes DCC1 and mediates DCC1-triggered immunity. This study provides mechanistic insights into PCK-regulated basal immunity in cereals and lays the groundwork for exploiting PCKs to improve broad-spectrum disease resistance in crops.
