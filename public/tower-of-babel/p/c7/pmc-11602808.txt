Emerging Biologic Therapies for the Treatment of Atopic Dermatitis
Authors: José Miguel Alvarenga, Thomas Bieber, Tiago Torres
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a prevalent inflammatory skin disease having a significant impact on patients’ quality of life. Conventional treatments, including topical therapies and systemic immunosuppressants, often have limited efficacy and long-term safety concerns. Emerging biologic therapies target specific immune pathways implicated in AD pathogenesis, offering new therapeutic options in a disease known for its complex immune pathomechanisms. This review focuses on novel biologics under investigation, particularly those targeting specific immune pathways such as interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-13, IL-22, IL-31, thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), and OX40-OX40L axis. Interleukin-4 and IL-13 inhibitors aim to reduce Th2-driven inflammation, while IL-22 inhibitors focus on restoring skin barrier function. Interleukin-31 inhibitors help alleviate pruritus, a major symptom in AD. OX40-OX40L pathway inhibitors can selectively suppress the activity of pathogenic T cells, without inducing significant immunosuppression. Bispecific antibodies targeting both IL-4 and IL-31 pathways are emerging as potential dual-action treatment for AD. Thymic stromal lymphopoietin inhibitors offer a novel strategy to control inflammation. While many of these therapies offer promising safety and efficacy profiles, long-term studies and real-world data are essential to confirm their lasting impact. This review highlights the potential of these emerging systemic therapies to continue transforming AD management and improve patient outcomes.

Key Points

Despite therapeutic advancements, a considerable group of atopic dermatitis (AD) patients remains difficult to manage, underscoring the need for novel therapies that can serve as effective alternatives for non-responders or those with only partial response to existing treatments.Emerging biologic therapies for AD are increasingly focusing on specific immune pathways such as IL-4, IL-13, IL-22, IL-31, TSLP, and the OX40-OX40L axis, which are integral to the pathophysiology of the disease.Clinical trials have shown promising efficacy with new agents, with many patients achieving significant skin improvement, along with favorable safety profiles characterized by mostly mild to moderate adverse events.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is an inflammatory skin disease, with a chronically relapsing course, characterized by eczema skin lesions, pruritus, and pain. AD is an extremely prevalent and complex disease, affecting 2–10% of young adults and up to 20% of children, with a significant impact on patients’ quality of life [1].

The management of AD primarily focuses on restoring the skin barrier, controlling itch, and reducing inflammation. Emollients are essential for maintaining skin integrity [1]. Topical therapies, such as corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, Janus kinase [JAK] inhibitors (ruxolitinib), and phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors (crisaborole and roflumilast) are used to manage mild cases or in combination with systemic therapies to manage moderate-to-severe cases and acute flares, offering relief from itching and inflammation [1]. Phototherapy and conventional systemic immunosuppressive therapies have been used for many years as first-line agents to treat moderate-to-severe cases, although their limited efficacy and long-term toxicity raised the need for the development of new therapies [2]. In the last few years, numerous novel therapeutic strategies have emerged, focusing on specific cytokines, their receptors, or intracellular signaling pathways. Currently approved therapies focus on inhibiting the activity of interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-13 and the JAK pathway. These include dupilumab, which blocks IL-4 and IL-13; tralokinumab and lebrikizumab, both targeting IL-13; abrocitinib and upadacitinib, both targeting JAK1; and baricitinib, which inhibits JAK1 and JAK2 [3].

While these newly approved therapies mark a significant progress in treatment, adequate disease management remains elusive for some patients. This could be due to non-response, partial response or diminished efficacy over time, or side effects to certain drugs. This underscores the necessity for the discovery of novel molecules and the exploration of new mechanisms of action [4].

This narrative review will cover emerging biological therapies in development for the treatment of moderate-to-severe AD, particularly emphasizing inhibitors targeting IL-4, IL-13, IL-22, IL-31, thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), and the OX40-OX40L pathways. A review of the published literature was conducted (up to August 2024) using the PubMed database, published abstracts and virtual presentations from scientific meetings, data from industry press releases, and results published on ClinicalTrials.gov.

Atopic Dermatitis (AD) Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of AD is complex, encompassing epidermal barrier dysfunction, alterations in the skin microbiome, and immune dysregulation [5]. In AD, the epidermal barrier is compromised, partly due to mutations in the gene encoding for filaggrin. Filaggrin is essential for the formation of the skin barrier and hydration, as it aggregates keratin fibers in keratinocytes and facilitates the breakdown into natural moisturizing factors. The deficiency or dysfunction of filaggrin leads to increased skin permeability and reduced capacity to retain moisture [6]. The breakdown of the skin barrier is further exacerbated by alterations in the lipid composition of the stratum corneum, particularly a deficiency in ceramides, which leads to increased transepidermal water loss and dryness of the skin [7]. These phenomena allow allergens, irritants, and pathogens to penetrate more easily initiating inflammatory responses [8].

Changes in the skin microbiome, particularly increased Staphylococcus aureus colonization, contribute to the pathogenesis of AD. This bacterium can exacerbate skin barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation, creating a feedback loop that perpetuates skin inflammation and AD symptoms [5].

Immune dysregulation in AD prominently features a skewing towards T helper type 2 (Th2) immune responses, particularly in the acute phases of the disease [9]. Keratinocytes from the damaged epidermal barrier release cytokines, such as TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33, to initiate immune defenses and promote a type 2 immune response shift [10]. Th2 cells secrete key cytokines such as IL-4, IL-13, and IL-31 [9]. By attaching itself to the IL-4Rα receptor, IL-4 can create the type 1 receptor complex with the common gamma chain, or the type 2 receptor complex with the IL-13Rα1 receptor. The type 2 receptor complex is formed by the recruitment of IL-4Rα by IL-13Rα1, which is bound to by IL-13 [11], promoting the differentiation of Th2 cells and the production of immunoglobin E (IgE) [12]. Immunoglobulin E binds to the surface of mast cells and basophils, leading to their activation and the release of histamine and other mediators that contribute to inflammation and pruritus [13].

Certain drugs currently under investigation for the treatment of Th2-mediated conditions work by either blocking IL-13 signaling through the type 2 receptor alone, or by targeting IL-4Rα and suppressing both type 1 and type 2 receptors [12]. Interleukin-31 has been identified as a major pruritogenic cytokine, directly contributing to the itch experienced by AD patients [14]. The cytokine IL-22, produced by Th22 cells, further contributes to the disease by promoting keratinocyte proliferation and downregulating filaggrin and involucrin, thereby impairing the skin barrier [15]. As the disease progresses, there is an upregulation of Th1 and Th17 responses in the chronic phase, contributing to sustained inflammation. The Th1 cells produce interferon gamma (IFN-γ), which aggravates skin barrier disruption, and Th17 cells secrete IL-17, further fueling inflammation [16]. Besides, Th2/Th22 activation progressively intensified from acute to chronic phases of AD [17].

A key regulator of T cell-mediated immune responses in AD is antigen presentation and the OX40-OX40L pathway. This pathway promotes the survival, proliferation, and memory of T cells, contributing to the chronic and relapsing nature of AD. OX40, a co-stimulatory receptor, is predominantly expressed on activated T cells, including both CD4+ and CD8+ subsets as well as on various T helper cells (Th1, Th2, Th17) and regulatory T cells. It is notably absent in naïve and most resting memory T cells, but its expression is quickly induced upon their activation [18]. The upregulation of OX40 is facilitated by T cell receptor engagement and co-stimulation, notably via CD28, and is further enhanced by cytokines such as IL-1, IL-2, IL-4, and TNF [19]. OX40L, the ligand for OX40, is mainly found on antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells, macrophages, and activated B cells [18]. The interaction between OX40 and OX40L is pivotal for T cell expansion, particularly affecting Th2 cells. This axis is integral to the immune response, with its activation promoting the proliferation of effector and memory T cells, crucial in AD inflammation [19, 20].

Emerging Biologic Therapies for the Treatment of AD

IL-4Rα Inhibitors

Rademikibart

Rademikibart, formerly known as CBP-201, is a next-generation human IgG4 kappa monoclonal antibody directed against human IL-4Rα currently being studied in Th2-driven inflammatory diseases. Preclinical studies demonstrated that rademikibart and dupilumab attach to different epitopes on the human IL-4Rα [21].

Two Phase 1 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies investigated the safety and tolerability of rademikibart. In a single-ascending dose trial, 40 healthy adults were divided in a 3:1 ratio to receive either rademikibart (75, 150, 300 or 600 mg subcutaneously [SC], or 300 mg intravenously [IV]) or a placebo, followed up for 12 weeks. Similarly, in a multiple-ascending dose trial, 31 adults with moderate-to-severe AD were assigned in a 4:1 ratio to receive weekly doses of rademikibart (75–300 mg SC) or placebo for four weeks, with an additional seven weeks of follow-up. In both trials, rademikibart was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events (SAEs). In the healthy adults, two SC injection-site reactions and one event of conjunctivitis were reported, all of which were mild [22].

A Phase 2 multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (NCT04444752) was conducted in adult patients with moderate-to-severe AD not previously treated with anti–IL-4Rα/IL-13 agents. The trial comprised a 16-week treatment period followed by an 8-week follow-up period. Participants (n = 226) were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1:1 ratio to receive SC injections of rademikibart at different dosages (300 mg every two weeks [Q2W], 150 mg Q2W, or 300 mg Q4W) or a matching placebo. The treatment began with a 600-mg loading dose of rademikibart or placebo on the first day. The main objective of the trial was met: rademikibart 300 mg Q2W (−63.0%; p = 0.0007), 150 mg Q2W (−57.6%; p = 0.0067), and 300 mg Q4W (−63.5%; p = 0.0004) compared to placebo (−39.7%) all significantly reduced the least-squares mean Eczema Area Severity Index (EASI) from baseline to Week 16. The 300 mg Q2W and Q4W dosing demonstrated significant efficacy, achieving an EASI improvement ≥75% (EASI-75) in 47.4% (p < 0.0001) and 39.3% (p ≤ 0.001) of patients, respectively, compared to 12.5% in the placebo group, and an EASI-90 in 24.6% (p < 0.05) and 23.2% (p < 0.05) of patients, respectively, compared to 8.9% in the placebo group. Rademikibart and placebo had a similar low incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) (48.2% vs 53.6%), in particular conjunctivitis of unspecified cause (2.9% vs 0.0%), herpes infection (0.6% vs 1.8%), injection-site reactions (1.8% vs 1.8%), and serious TEAEs (1.8% vs 3.6%) [23].

The Phase 2 randomized, double-blind, pivotal trial in China (NCT05017480) produced comparable outcomes in terms of the safety and efficacy of rademikibart in adult patients with moderate-to-severe AD, according to the non–peer-reviewed data. In this study involving 255 adults with moderate-to-severe AD, participants were randomized in a 2:1 ratio to receive either rademikibart (600 mg loading dose, followed by 300 mg Q2W) or placebo. At Week 16, significantly higher proportions of patients treated with rademikibart achieved EASI-75 (62.9% vs 23.4%) and EASI-90 (35.8% vs 6.3%) compared to the placebo group (p < 0.001) [24].

A Phase 2 single-arm, open-label, clinical trial is being developed to further study the safety and efficacy of rademikibart in Chinese adult subjects with moderate-to-severe AD (Table 1).

Stapokibart

Stapokibart, formerly known as CM310, is a humanized monoclonal antibody that specifically binds to IL-4Rα. A recently published multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, Phase 2b trial (NCT04805411) was conducted across 21 study sites in China. Adult patients with moderate-to-severe AD qualified for the study (n = 120) and were assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three groups: a high-dose group receiving 300 mg of stapokibart (with an initial dose of 600 mg), a low-dose group receiving 150 mg of stapokibart (with an initial dose of 300 mg), or a placebo group. The SC treatments were given every 2 weeks across a span of 16 weeks, followed by an additional 8-week period for follow-up observations. Reductions in the least-square mean EASI score at Week 16 were noted in the treatment groups, with the high-dose group showing an 81.9% reduction and the low-dose group a 70.5% reduction, in contrast to a 51.2% reduction in the placebo group. Furthermore, EASI-75 was reached by 70.0% (p < 0.0001) of the high-dose group and 65.0% (p < 0.0001) of the low-dose group versus 20.0% in the placebo group. Eczema Area Severity Index-90 was attained by 40.0% (p = 0.0038) of the high-dose group and 47.5% (p = 0.0004) of the low-dose group, compared to 10.0% in the placebo group. Stapokibart demonstrated favorable tolerability and safety profile. The frequency of TEAEs was comparable across all three groups, with upper respiratory tract infection, AD, hyperlipidemia, and hyperuricemia being the most commonly reported TEAEs [25].

Multiple studies are underway to assess the safety and efficacy of stapokibart in treating moderate-to-severe AD in both children and adults (Table 1).

Manfidokimab

Manfidokimab, previously known as AK120, is a humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody directed against IL-4Rα that is being investigated to treat moderate-to-severe AD.

A recently published Phase 1 randomized, placebo-controlled, clinical study (NCT04256174) investigated the safety, pharmacokinetics, and preliminary efficacy of manfidokimab in both healthy and AD patients. The first part of the study was a single ascending dose in healthy adults, with 40 subjects divided into five cohorts (receiving 15, 50, 150, 300 or 600 mg of manfidokimab). The second part was a multiple ascending dose in adults with moderate-to-severe AD, with 41 patients divided into four cohorts (receiving 75 mg weekly, 150 mg weekly, 300 mg weekly or 300 mg every 2 weeks); the treatment duration was 4 weeks, followed by an additional 8-week period for follow-up observations. In the study, a high percentage of participants in the manfidokimab treatment groups and the placebo group experienced at least one TEAE, with rates of 86.7% and 90.0%, respectively, in part one, and 87.5% and 77.8%, respectively, in part two. The TEAEs were mostly mild or moderate. No SAEs or TEAEs causing discontinuation were reported. Common TEAEs included upper respiratory tract infections, headaches, skin infections, and injection-site pain. Furthermore, there was a greater percentage of participants in the manfidokimab treatment groups reaching EASI-50 and EASI-75 at 4 weeks (64.5% [20/31] and 35.5% [11/31], respectively), compared to the placebo group (12.5% [1/8] and 0, respectively) [26].

Currently, Phase 1b, Phase 2, and Phase 3 clinical trials are underway to continue investigating the efficacy and safety of this drug in AD adult patients (Table 1).

IL-13Rα1 Inhibitors

Eblasakimab

Eblasakimab, previously known as ASLAN004, is a first-in-class, fully human monoclonal antibody that prevents IL-4 and IL-13 signaling through the type 2 receptor by binding to the IL-13Rα1 subunit of the type 2 receptor [27].

The Phase 1a study (NCT03721263) of eblasakimab, administered to healthy male volunteers, explored its safety and ability to inhibit STAT6 phosphorylation through either IV or SC single ascending doses. The study demonstrated the favorable safety profile of eblasakimab across a range of doses up to 10 mg/kg (IV) and 600 mg (SC). There were no SAEs linked to the drug, and no participants withdrew due to adverse effects. Eighteen volunteers (40.9%) experienced at least one TEAE, the most common being upper respiratory tract infection and headache. Furthermore, eblasakimab effectively blocked STAT6 phosphorylation for at least 15 days with single doses as low as 3 mg/kg with IV administration and 300 mg with SC administration [27].

A Phase 1b multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled, multiple-ascending dose escalation trial (NCT04090229) was conducted in patients with moderate-to-severe AD. Fifty-two participants received weekly SC injections of eblasakimab at doses of 200, 400, or 600 mg, or a placebo for 8 weeks. Treatment-emergent adverse events occurred in 47.1% of placebo recipients and 71.4% of eblasakimab-treated patients, with the majority of TEAEs being mild or moderate and not resulting in study withdrawal. Injection-site reactions of mild to moderate severity occurred in 25.7% of eblasakimab patients compared to 11.8% in the placebo group, and 5.7% of patients on eblasakimab developed conjunctivitis compared to 0.0% in the placebo group. Secondary efficacy outcomes showed that eblasakimab led to early and progressive improvements in EASI scores over the trial, with the 400 mg and 600 mg doses eliciting stronger clinical responses than the 200 mg dose. Notably, at Week 8, the 600 mg eblasakimab dose achieved a statistically significant mean percentage improvement in EASI scores compared to placebo (−65.0% vs −27.0%, p = 0.014) [28].

The TREK-AD study, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2b trial (NCT05158023), assessed the efficacy and safety of eblasakimab in adults with moderate to severe AD. Involving 289 patients, the study compared four dosing regimens of eblasakimab (400 mg and 600 mg monthly, and 300 mg and 400 mg every 2 weeks) against a placebo over a 16-week period. According to the non–peer-reviewed data, the primary outcome, percentage change from baseline in EASI scores at Week 16, was achieved with eblasakimab doses of 600 mg every 4 weeks, 300 mg every 2 weeks, and 400 mg every two weeks, showing improvements of 73.0% (p = 0.001), 69.8% (p = 0.005), and 65.8% (p = 0.029), respectively, compared to 51.1% with placebo. These improvements were evident from Week 4. Additionally, the eblasakimab 600 mg every four weeks significantly outperformed placebo in achieving EASI-75 at Week 16 (52.0% vs 24.4%, p = 0.004) and EASI-90 (27.6% vs 7.9%, p = 0.008). Dosing regimens every 2 weeks showed superior results compared to placebo in achieving EASI-75 and EASI-90 scores, with the 400-mg dose showing 43.6% (p = 0.036) and 25.3% (p = 0.018), respectively, and the 300-mg dose showing 51.2% (p = 0.005) and 30.8% (p = 0.003), respectively. The treatment was well tolerated with similar discontinuation rates across treatment and placebo groups, although eblasakimab patients reported a higher incidence of conjunctivitis (5.2% vs 1.8%) and injection-site reactions (4.7 % vs 1.8%) [29].

A Phase 2 clinical trial (TREK-DX; NCT05694884) is currently underway to study the efficacy and safety of eblasakimab in adults with moderate to severe AD previously treated with dupilumab (Table 1). According to the non–-peer-reviewed data from 22 patients, 60.0% of patients treated with 400-mg eblasakimab weekly achieved EASI-90, and 66.7% reached a Validated Investigator Global Assessment (vIGA) score of 0 or 1 after 16 weeks, compared to 14.3% in the placebo group. Rapid efficacy was observed, with over 50% of patients achieving EASI-75 by Week 6 (8/15) and 73% (11/15) by Week 16. Among patients with an inadequate response to prior dupilumab treatment, the mean percent change in EASI score at Week 16 was a 91.0% reduction (n = 6) [30].

IL-22Rα1 Inhibitors

Despite the important role of IL-22 in the pathophysiology of atopic dermatitis, fezakinumab, an anti-IL-22 monoclonal antibody, was discontinued following a Phase 2a trial. More recently, there has been renewed interest in temtokibart, a monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-22Rα1 receptor subunit.

Temtokibart

Temtokibart, formerly known as LEO 138559, functions by blocking the IL-22Rα1 receptor subunit, effectively inhibiting the action of IL-22. Additionally, temtokibart may also partially inhibit the effects of cytokines IL-20 and IL-24, although the extent of this interaction is yet to be fully elucidated [31].

According to the non–peer-reviewed data, the Phase 2a trial (NCT04922021), conducted in 58 patients with moderate to severe AD, demonstrated its efficacy and safety. At Week 4, 27 out of 29 patients treated with temtokibart (SC 450 mg Q2W) showed improvement in their EASI scores compared to 17 out of 29 patients in the placebo group. A higher percentage of temtokibart-treated patients achieved significant EASI score reductions compared to placebo: EASI-50 was reached by 55.2% (vs 13.8% for placebo), EASI-75 by 34.5% (vs 6.9%), and EASI-90 by 13.8% (vs 3.4%). By Week 16, these improvements were even more pronounced with temtokibart treatment: 72.4% of patients reached EASI-50 (vs 31.0% for placebo), 51.7% reached EASI-75 (vs 24.1%), 34.5% reached EASI-90 (vs 10.3%), and 20.7% achieved EASI-100, with no patients in the placebo group reaching this level [32]. Temtokibart was generally well tolerated [31].

A Phase 2b clinical trial (NCT05923099) is currently recruiting patients to evaluate the efficacy and safety of different dosages of temtokibart in moderate to severe AD adult patients (Table 1).

IL-31Rα Inhibitors

Nemolizumab

Nemolizumab, formerly known as CIM331, is a humanized IgG2 monoclonal antibody targeting the IL31Rα receptor subunit. It is currently approved in Japan for the treatment of AD-associated itch in patients aged ≥13 years [33], and has recently received FDA approval for the treatment of prurigo nodularis in adults [34].

A Phase 2 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 12-week trial (NCT01986933) was conducted in hospitals in Japan, Europe and the USA, and included 264 adult patients with moderate to severe AD. Patients were randomized to receive SC nemolizumab at doses of 0.1 mg/kg, 0.5 mg/kg, or 2.0 mg/kg Q4W, placebo Q4W, or 2.0 mg/kg Q8W with placebo dose administered at Week 4. The use of concomitant topical glucocorticoids as a form of rescue therapy was permitted. At 12 weeks, patients receiving nemolizumab every 4 weeks demonstrated a significant, dose-dependent reduction in the least-squares mean percentage change from baseline in pruritus visual analogue scale (VAS) scores, compared to placebo. The percentage reductions were −43.7% with 0.1 mg/kg of nemolizumab (p = 0.002), −59.8% with 0.5 mg/kg (p < 0.001), and −63.1% with 2.0 mg/kg (p < 0.001), compared to −20.9% in the placebo group. The frequency and nature of adverse events associated with nemolizumab were largely comparable to those seen in the placebo group, except for an increased incidence of AD exacerbations (19.4% vs 13.0%) and peripheral edema (5.7% vs 0.0%) in patients treated with nemolizumab [35].

A 52-week extension of the previous trial (NCT01986933) was conducted following the initial 12-week period. After completing the 12-week phase, patients entered a double-blind extension phase, during which they continued to receive nemolizumab at their originally assigned doses for an additional 52 weeks (Weeks 12–64). Patients who had been randomized to placebo in the initial phase were re-randomized in the extension phase to receive SC nemolizumab at doses of 0.1, 0.5, or 2.0 mg/kg Q4W in a 1:1:1 ratio. In total, 191 patients participated in the extension phase. The improvement in pruritus VAS score observed in part A was sustained or further enhanced from Week 12 to Week 64 among patients who continued receiving nemolizumab throughout the 64-week study. The most pronounced improvement was noted in the 0.5 mg/kg nemolizumab group, with a reduction of −89.6%. The proportion of patients who achieved EASI-75 was 65.5% in the 2.0 mg/kg Q4W group, 67.7% in the 0.1 mg/kg Q4W group, 67.9% in the 0.5 mg/kg Q4W group, and 73.7% in the 2.0 mg/kg Q8W group. Nemolizumab was generally well tolerated over the 64-week period, with a safety profile consistent with that observed in part A, and no new adverse events were reported during the extension phase [36].

Another Phase 2 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 24-week trial (NCT03100344) was conducted in hospitals of North America, Europe and Australia. A total of 226 patients with moderate to severe AD were randomized to SC nemolizumab at doses of 10 mg, 30 mg and 90 mg Q4W or placebo, with topical corticosteroids. All doses of nemolizumab demonstrated superior improvement in mean percentage change in EASI score at Week 24 compared to placebo. The most pronounced reduction from baseline at Week 24 was observed with the 30-mg dose of nemolizumab (−68.8%) versus placebo (−52.1%), with a least-squares mean difference that was statistically significant (p = 0.016). Additionally, by Week 24, 45.6% of patients in the 30 mg nemolizumab group achieved EASI-75, compared to 26.3% in the placebo group (p = 0.034), and 29.8% achieved EASI-90, compared to 10.5% in the placebo group (p = 0.011). All doses of nemolizumab were associated with a rapid and significant reduction in pruritus scores, with notable differences from placebo starting as early as Week 1. The greatest improvement in Peak Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (PP-NRS) scores at Week 24 was seen in the 30-mg nemolizumab group compared to placebo (−67.3% vs −35.8%, p < 0.001). Nemolizumab treatment was associated with a slightly higher incidence of both TEAEs and serious TEAEs compared to placebo. Non-skin infections, including nasopharyngitis, upper respiratory tract infections, and gastroenteritis, were more commonly observed in the nemolizumab group compared to placebo, with an incidence of 60.4% versus 21.4%, 8.3% versus 1.8%, and 4.1% versus 0.0%, respectively. A dose-dependent increase in asthma events was observed in patients with pre-existing asthma, with most cases being mild and manageable [37].

A Phase 2 open-label clinical trial (NCT03921411) involving 20 adolescents (aged 12–17 years) with moderate to severe AD yielded results comparable to those observed in adult studies [38].

The first Phase 3 clinical trial (jRCT2080223684) was published in 2020 and involved patients aged ≥13 years with moderate to severe AD in Japan. A total of 215 patients were enrolled and randomly assigned in a 2:1 ratio to receive nemolizumab (n = 143) or placebo (n = 72), while continuing stable AD therapies, including medium-potency topical glucocorticoids, topical calcineurin inhibitors, and oral antihistamines. By Week 16, the least-squares mean difference in pruritus VAS score from baseline was −21.5 percentage points in favor of nemolizumab (p < 0.001). Although a greater reduction in EASI score was observed in the nemolizumab group at Week 16 (−45.9% vs −33.2%), no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons in the analysis of secondary endpoints. Overall, 70.6% of patients in both groups reported adverse events during treatment, most of which were mild to moderate in severity. Severe adverse events occurred in 2.1% of patients receiving nemolizumab, including cases of Meniere’s disease, acute pancreatitis, and AD. The most reported adverse event of special interest was worsening AD, which affected 23.8% of patients in the nemolizumab group and 20.8% in the placebo group [39].

A total of 206 patients who completed the previous study were enrolled in a 52-week open-label extension (jRCT2080223684), continuing treatment up to Week 64. In this extension study, the mean pruritus VAS scores continued to show a sustained reduction over time. In the group receiving nemolizumab throughout the study, the pruritus VAS scores decreased by 65.9% from baseline at Week 68. Notably, 12 weeks after the final dose, only minimal increases in pruritus VAS scores were observed, indicating the durability of nemolizumab's efficacy in reducing pruritus. Additionally, another Phase 3 open-label clinical trial (jRCT2080223838) was conducted in Japan with 88 patients aged ≥13 years. All participants received nemolizumab 60 mg every 4 weeks up to Week 48. Injections at baseline, Week 4, and Week 8 were administered by health care professionals, while from Week 12 onward, half of the patients transitioned to self-injection. No significant differences in outcomes were observed between the self-injection group and those continuing to receive injections from medical professionals. Overall, all patients treated with nemolizumab exhibited a consistent reduction in pruritus VAS scores at every study timepoint. In total, more than 90% of patients receiving nemolizumab across both studies experienced TEAEs, the majority of which were mild in severity. Severe TEAEs were reported in fewer than 5% of patients, with the most frequently reported being nasopharyngitis (33.9%) and AD exacerbations (25.2%) [40].

A Phase 3 randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, parallel-group trial (jRCT2080225289) was conducted in Japanese pediatric patients (aged 6–12 years) with AD and inadequately controlled moderate to severe pruritus. A total of 89 patients was randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive nemolizumab 30 mg or placebo Q4W. The primary endpoint, which was the change in the 5-level itch score from baseline to Week 16, showed a statistically significant improvement in the nemolizumab group (−1.3) compared to placebo (−0.5), with a least-squares mean difference of −0.8 (p < 0.0001). Although the percentage change from baseline in EASI score at Week 16 and the proportions of patients achieving 50% or 75% improvements were higher in the nemolizumab group, these differences were not statistically significant when compared to placebo. Overall, the safety profile of nemolizumab in pediatric patients aged 6–12 years was consistent with that observed in patients aged ≥13 years, with no clinically significant differences reported [41].

Recently published, the ARCADIA 1 (NCT03985943) and ARCADIA 2 (NCT03989349) Phase 3 trials were large, multinational, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies evaluating the efficacy and safety of SC nemolizumab 30 mg Q4W (loading dose of 60 mg) in combination with topical corticosteroids in adolescents and adults with moderate to severe AD. A total of 1728 participants were enrolled across both trials: 1142 were randomly assigned to receive nemolizumab (620 in ARCADIA 1 and 522 in ARCADIA 2) and 586 to placebo (321 in ARCADIA 1 and 265 in ARCADIA 2). At Week 16, nemolizumab demonstrated significant improvements in key efficacy endpoints compared to placebo. In ARCADIA 1, 43.5% of patients in the nemolizumab group achieved an EASI-75 response compared to 29.0% in the placebo group (adjusted difference 14.9%, p < 0.0001). In ARCADIA 2, 42.1% of patients in the nemolizumab group achieved EASI-75 compared to 30.2% in the placebo group (adjusted difference 12.5%, p = 0.0006). Nemolizumab resulted in significant reductions in pruritus scores as early as Week 1, with 42.7% of nemolizumab-treated patients achieving a ≥4-point reduction in the PP-NRS by Week 16, compared to 17.8% in the placebo group in ARCADIA 1 (adjusted difference 24.9%, p < 0.0001). In ARCADIA 2, 41.0% of nemolizumab-treated patients achieved this reduction versus 18.1% in the placebo group (adjusted difference 23.2%, p < 0.0001). In ARCADIA 1, 50.0% of patients treated with nemolizumab experienced at least one TEAE compared to 45.4% in the placebo group, while in ARCADIA 2, TEAEs were reported in 41.4% of nemolizumab-treated patients versus 44.5% in the placebo group. Worsening of AD was the most common TEAE reported in both trials, occurring in 12.2% of nemolizumab-treated patients versus 10.6% in the placebo group in ARCADIA 1, and 7.1% versus 5.7% in ARCADIA 2, respectively. Peripheral or facial edema was observed in 1–2% of nemolizumab-treated patients in both trials, showing no significant differences between the groups. Worsening of asthma occurred in 2–5% of nemolizumab-treated patients, with similar rates in the placebo groups [42].

At present, a Phase 2 trial in pediatric patients and a long-term Phase 3 trial are ongoing (Table 1).

OX40-OX40L Pathway Inhibitors

Monoclonal antibodies targeting the OX40-OX40L pathway offer a novel approach in the treatment of AD by modulating T cell activity, which plays a critical role in the disease's pathogenesis.

Monoclonal antibodies targeting OX40, such as rocatinlimab and telazorlimab, modulate the activity of effector and memory T cells that contribute to the severity and chronicity of AD [20, 43, 44]. Rocatinlimab demonstrated a reduction in the activity and proliferation of Th2 cells, suppression of skin inflammation, and decreased serum IL-22 levels [44]. Telazorlimab showed significant decreases in OX40+ T cells and OX40L dendritic cells, along with a reduction in key Th2 mediators and modulation of Th1, Th17 and Th22 pathways [43].

Anti-OX40L antibodies, such as amlitelimab, target antigen-presenting cells, thereby inhibiting the activation of Th2, Th22, and Th17 cytokines in skin lesions [45]. In a primate model, amlitelimab significantly controlled conventional CD4+ T effector cell proliferation while maintaining the regulatory/conventional T cell ratio [46].

Amlitelimab

Amlitelimab, also known as KY1005/SAR445229, is a non-depleting, fully human, IgG4 monoclonal antibody that specifically binds to OX40L, inhibiting its interaction with OX40.

A Phase 1 trial (NCT03161288) with 64 healthy male participants was conducted to assess the safety, tolerability, and immunogenicity of amlitelimab, with subjects divided into eight cohorts for randomized different doses of IV amlitelimab or placebo. All TEAEs were mild or moderate and self-resolving, with headaches being the most commonly reported symptom, and no significant changes were observed in safety laboratory parameters [47].

A Phase 2a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study (NCT03754309), was conducted in adults with moderate to severe AD who were intolerant or had an inadequate response to topical treatments. Eighty-nine patients were randomized 1:1:1 to IV amlitelimab low-dose (200 mg loading/100 mg maintenance Q4W, n = 29), high-dose (500 mg loading/250 mg maintenance Q4W, n = 30), or a placebo (n = 29) until Week 12. The co-primary endpoints were the incidence of TEAEs and percentage change in EASI from baseline to Week 16, with ongoing evaluations extending to Week 36 for those who did not start AD-influencing therapies or experience a relapse. By Week 16, the amlitelimab groups demonstrated substantial improvements: the low-dose group achieved a change in EASI of −80.1% (p = 0.009 vs placebo) and the high-dose group a change of −70.0% (p = 0.07 vs placebo). In contrast, the placebo group showed a change of −49.4%. Greater proportions of patients treated with amlitelimab achieved EASI-75 (low-dose = 59.0%; high-dose = 52.0%; placebo = 25.0%) and EASI-90 (low-dose = 33.0%; high-dose = 30.0%; placebo = 13.0%). Furthermore, 44.0% of low-dose patients and 37.0% of high-dose patients reached a vIGA score of 0/1, compared to only 8.0% in the placebo group. Amlitelimab was generally well tolerated, with the majority of TEAEs being mild to moderate. Headache, hyperhidrosis, and upper respiratory infections were more common in the amlitelimab groups than in the placebo group. Two cases of conjunctivitis were reported but were not considered related to the treatment. During the extension phase, one death occurred in the low-dose group, which was deemed unrelated to the treatment by the investigator and an independent committee [45].

A Phase 2b multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, dose ranging study (NCT05131477) was conducted in 390 adults with moderate to severe AD. The study encompasses the first phase of a 24-week treatment period, and a second 36-week maintenance/withdrawal period. In the first part, patients were randomized to receive SC amlitelimab Q4W in different doses (250 mg with 500 mg loading dose; 250 mg without loading dose; 125 mg without loading dose; or 62.5 mg without loading dose) or placebo Q4W for 24 weeks. The primary endpoint was percentage change in EASI from baseline at Week 16. Amlitelimab treatment led to significant improvements in EASI scores compared to placebo across all doses studied. The group receiving 250 mg with a loading dose exhibited the highest numerical response versus placebo at Week 16, with a least-square mean change from baseline of −32.1% (p < 0.0001). Similarly, the groups without a loading dose also demonstrated significant responses versus placebo: 250 mg (−27.3%; p < 0.0001), 125 mg (−22.2%; p = 0.0002), and 62.5 mg (−30.2%; p < 0.0001). Amlitelimab was well tolerated across all dose groups, with no safety concerns identified [48].

Several Phase 2 and Phase 3 studies are currently recruiting patients to further evaluate amlitelimab efficacy and safety in moderate to severe AD in both children and adults (Table 1).

Rocatinlimab

Rocatinlimab, previously known as AMG451/KHK4083, is a fully human anti-OX40 IgG1 monoclonal antibody currently under investigation for the treatment of moderate to severe AD [44].

A Phase 1, single-center, open-label trial (NCT03096223) assessed 22 adult patients with moderate to severe AD over a 6-week treatment phase, during which they received three IV infusions of rocatinlimab (10 mg/kg) every 2 weeks, followed by a 16-week follow-up period. The most reported related infusion reactions, which were mild or moderate in severity, included pyrexia (50.0%) and chills (36.4%). Other noted adverse effects were aphthous ulcers, increased blood uric acid, nasopharyngitis, erythema, and hordeolum [44].

A Phase 2b multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (NCT03703102) evaluated the efficacy and safety of different doses of rocatinlimab for the treatment of moderate to severe AD in 274 adult subjects. Patients were randomly allocated (1:1:1:1:1 ratio) to receive SC rocatinlimab every 4 weeks at doses of 150 mg or 600 mg, or every 2 weeks at doses of 300 mg or 600 mg, or a SC placebo up to Week 18. This was followed by an 18-week active-treatment extension and a 20-week follow-up period. The least-squares mean percent reduction in EASI score from baseline to Week 16 was significantly higher in all rocatinlimab treatment groups compared to placebo. Specifically, patients receiving rocatinlimab 150 mg every 4 weeks saw a reduction of 48.3% (p = 0.0003), those on 600 mg every 4 weeks had a reduction of 49.7% (p = 0.0002), the 300 mg every 2 weeks group experienced a 61.1% reduction (p < 0.0001), and the 600 mg every 2 weeks group saw a 57.4% reduction (p < 0.0001), compared to a 15.0% reduction in the placebo group. Similarly, at Week 16, the 300 mg Q2W group achieved the greater results for EASI-75 and EASI-90, with 53.8% of patients reaching EASI-75 (compared to 10.5% in the placebo group) and 36.5% achieving EASI-90 (compared to 3.5% in the placebo group). The incidence of SAEs ranged from 2 to 6% in the rocatinlimab groups, while it was 1.8% in the placebo group. One patient receiving rocatinlimab 150 mg every 4 weeks experienced a SAE of anal abscess, deemed related to the study drug, which led to discontinuation of treatment. This event occurred after an endoscopic mucosal resection for polypectomy and a subsequent diagnosis of rectosigmoid cancer, which was not considered related to rocatinlimab treatment. No serious hypersensitivity reactions or deaths were reported. Pyrexia, chills, headache, aphthous ulcers and nausea were most frequently reported in treatment groups [49].

Numerous studies are currently being developed to further evaluate the efficacy and safety of rocatinlimab in moderate to severe AD in children and adult patients (Table 1).

Telazorlimab

Telazorlimab, also known as ISB830/GBR830, is a humanized anti-OX40 IgG1 monoclonal antibody designed to have a therapeutic role in T cell-mediated diseases, including AD [50].

Three Phase 1 studies were conducted to assess the pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity of telazorlimab. In a single ascending dose study, healthy volunteers received telazorlimab (0.3, 1, 3, and 10 mg/kg) via IV infusion. The drug was well tolerated, with no clinically significant findings reported. It exhibited a comparable pharmacokinetic profile in both healthy volunteers and individuals with AD, characterized by a long half-life, high bioavailability, and no indications of target-mediated disposition [50].

The Phase 2a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, repeated-dose study (NCT02683928) was conducted in moderate to severe AD adult patients with inadequate control with topical therapy. Following 3:1 randomization, 16 participants were assigned to receive a placebo, and 46 participants received two IV infusions of telazorlimab (10 mg/kg) on Days 1 and 29, followed by a 16-week follow-up period. At Day 71, the percentage EASI score change from baseline indicated a greater mean improvement with telazorlimab compared to placebo (56.0% vs 38.0%, respectively). Additionally, 42.3% of patients in the treatment group achieved EASI-75, compared to 25.0% in the placebo group. Treatment-emergent adverse events were reported in 62.9% of participants, with the most frequently reported being headache, AD, nasopharyngitis and upper respiratory tract infection. [43].

A Phase 2b multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled, dose-ranging trial (NCT03568162) involved 462 adults with moderate to severe AD. Participants were assigned to receive SC telazorlimab or placebo across different regimens for a 16-week blinded treatment phase (part 1), succeeded by a 38-week open-label treatment (part 2) and a 12-week drug-free follow-up period. The primary end point was the percentage change from baseline in EASI at Week 16. Participants receiving telazorlimab 300 mg every 2 weeks (part 1) and 600 mg every 2 weeks (part 2) demonstrated statistically significant improvements in EASI score over placebo, with a p-value of 0.008 for both groups. The least-squares mean percentage change in EASI from baseline to Week 16 showed −54.4% for the 300 mg Q2W group (compared to −34.2% for the placebo group) in part 1, and the 600 mg group exhibited a −59.0% change (compared to −41.8% in the placebo group) in part 2. Moreover, the 300 mg Q2W group in part 1 also showed greater improvements on most secondary endpoints over the 16-week phase, including a higher percentage of subjects achieving EASI-75 (23.7% vs 11.3% for placebo). Treatment-emergent adverse events were common, but predominantly mild and occurred at comparable rates between the telazorlimab and placebo groups. Few TEAEs resulted in treatment discontinuation or were classified as serious. Additionally, no new safety concerns were identified during the open-label phase [51].

Currently, there are no new Phase 2 or 3 trials registered for the evaluation of telazorlimab in AD. However, according to a press release by Ichnos Sciences dated October 11, 2023, the development program has recently been acquired by another company [52].

IL-4/IL-13 and IL-31 Pathway Inhibitors

A bispecific antibody (BsAb) is an engineered antibody designed to simultaneously bind two different antigens or epitopes. Unlike traditional monoclonal antibodies that target a single antigen, BsAb have two distinct binding sites, allowing them to engage with two different targets at once. This dual specificity enables various therapeutic applications, such as, blocking multiple disease pathways simultaneously, enhancing the efficacy and precision of treatments in complex immune mediated diseases [53]. BsAb targeting both IL-4R and IL-31R offer a novel therapeutic approach for AD by simultaneously inhibiting two key pathways involved in the disease’s pathogenesis. By blocking IL-4/IL-13 and IL-31 signaling, these BsAbs could potentially reduce inflammation, itch, and skin barrier dysfunction more effectively than targeting a single cytokine [54].

GB12-09

Bispecific antibody GB12-09, is designed to target both IL-4Rα and IL-31Rα, aimed at treating AD. In preclinical studies, GB12-09 demonstrated the in vitro ability to simultaneously bind IL-4Rα and IL-31Rα, effectively blocking the IL-4/IL-13 and IL-31 pathways. In a murine model of AD, GB12-09 reduced inflammation, serum total IgE, and IL-31 levels, highlighting its potential as a novel and effective therapeutic approach for AD [55].

NM26-2198

NM26-2198 is a recombinant, bispecific, tetravalent IgG4 single-chain variable fragment molecule that targets IL-4Rα and IL-31. A preclinical study showed that NM26-2198 independently and simultaneously binds to both IL-4Rα and IL-31. It effectively inhibits signaling through both pathways with a potency comparable to the combined effects of dupilumab and BMS-981164 (an anti-IL-31 monoclonal antibody that was discontinued) [54]. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, single- and multiple-ascending dose study (NCT 05859724) is currently recruiting healthy subjects and patients with moderate to severe AD to assess the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and immunogenicity of SC administration of NM26-2198.

Thymic Stromal Lymphopoietin Inhibitors

Thymic stromal lymphopoietin, an epithelial cell cytokine, is produced in response to proinflammatory stimuli. It has been identified as a potential target for AD treatment [56]. Its blockade using tezepelumab, a fully human antibody targeting TSLP receptor, showed limited efficacy after 12 weeks’ treatment [57]. Nonetheless, new agents targeting TSLP or TSLPR remain highly anticipated, such as bosakitug, which is currently in Phase 2 clinical development.

Bosakitug

Bosakitug, also known as BSI-045B, is a high affinity and high potency anti-TSLP monoclonal antibody in Phase 2 clinical development in AD, severe asthma, and chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps. A Phase 2a multicenter open-label clinical trial (NCT05932654), currently recruiting, aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of SC bosakitug, both as monotherapy and as an add-on therapy with dupilumab, in adult patients with moderate to severe AD. Preliminary, non–peer-reviewed data from 22 enrolled subjects indicate that bosakitug monotherapy demonstrated a favorable safety profile and considerable efficacy. At Week 23, with 18 evaluable participants completing the dosing phase of the study, bosakitug achieved EASI-75 and EASI-90 responses in 88.8% and 44.4% of the subjects, respectively [58].

Discussion

The evolving landscape of biologic therapies for moderate to severe AD is marked by significant advancements in targeting key immunologic pathways involved in the disease’s pathophysiology. While therapies like dupilumab, which targets both IL-4 and IL-13 signaling, tralokinumab and lebrikizumab, which solely target IL-13, and JAK inhibitors have broadened the therapeutic horizon, a substantial subset of patients remains inadequately controlled [4]. These patients often do not achieve full disease clearance, experience relapse, or suffer from treatment-related side effects, underscoring the critical need for novel biologics with alternative mechanisms of action that might offer more durable and tailored therapeutic effects.

Interleukin-4Rα inhibitors, such as rademikibart, stapokibart, and manfidokimab, are emerging as promising options for the treatment of moderate to severe AD. Clinical studies have shown that 40–70% of patients treated with these drugs achieve EASI-75 at Week 16. These agents also exhibit favorable safety profiles, with most adverse events being mild, such as injection-site reactions and mild to moderate conjunctivitis (up to 3% of patients). While long-term safety remains to be confirmed, the early data are encouraging.

Interleukin-13Rα1 inhibitors, like eblasakimab, offer a more selective mechanism of action by targeting the IL-13 receptor while sparing the IL-4 receptor. Studies have shown that approximately 50% of patients achieve EASI-75 by Week 16 with eblasakimab treatment. Inhibition of IL-4 signaling through the type 1 receptor has been proposed to induce Th2 to Th1 polarization, which can lead to IFN-γ-mediated goblet cell apoptosis and reduced mucin production, potentially resulting in dry eye and conjunctivitis [59]. Conversely, blocking the IL-13 receptor is believed to significantly decrease Th2 cytokines without causing a simultaneous increase in Th1 cytokines [60]. In terms of safety, eblasakimab has shown low rates of adverse events, including a small number of cases of conjunctivitis (5–6% of patients) and injection-site reactions. Compared to the results from dupilumab trials, which reported a conjunctivitis rate of 8–22% [61], this suggests a more favorable safety profile.

Interleukin-22 inhibitors, such as temtokibart, have been shown to restore skin barrier function and reduce epidermal hyperplasia, with about 50% of patients achieving EASI-75 by Week 16. These drugs have demonstrated excellent safety profiles, with few significant adverse events reported, making them a promising option for effectively addressing AD with minimal risk.

Interleukin-31R inhibitors, such as nemolizumab, have shown strong efficacy in reducing pruritus and improving skin lesions in AD patients, with approximately 40% of patients achieving EASI-75 in clinical trials. Nemolizumab is particularly effective in addressing the persistent itching associated with AD, and its safety profile is consistent with other biologics, with common adverse events including mild infections and AD exacerbations. This drug is already approved in Japan for the treatment of itch associated with AD in adults and children aged ≥13 years, and given the positive results from multinational trials, it is expected to gain approval for use in Europe and the USA in the future.

OX40-OX40L pathway inhibitors, such as rocatinlimab, amlitelimab, and telazorlimab, present an innovative approach by modulating T cell activity, a crucial factor in AD pathogenesis, minimizing the risk of generalized immunosuppression. Clinical trials have demonstrated that 50–60% of patients treated with amlitelimab and rocatinlimab reach EASI-75 by Week 16, whereas telazorlimab has shown more modest outcomes. Most adverse events were mild to moderate, such as fever and injection-site reactions, indicating that these drugs offer effective long-term treatment options without the risks associated with broad immunosuppression.

Bispecific antibodies targeting both IL-4R and IL-31R, such as GB12-09 and NM26-2198, represent a new frontier in AD treatment. Given that AD is a disease with a complex pathophysiology involving multiple pathways, BsAbs may offer therapeutic advantages. Early results indicate that these therapies may provide dual benefits by reducing both inflammation and pruritus.

Finally, TSLP inhibitors represent a novel strategy for controlling inflammation and alleviating symptoms in AD. Bosakitug, currently in clinical trials, has shown promising efficacy in early studies. With a favorable safety profile and primarily mild adverse events reported, it has the potential to become a valuable addition to the treatment options for AD.

The dosing regimen is a critical aspect that requires attention in the treatment of AD. Currently approved biologic therapies for AD are administered every two weeks. Reducing the dosing frequency can lessen the treatment burden, enhancing patient adherence and leading to better disease management with fewer disruptions to daily life. The monthly administration of drugs, like eblasakimab, amlitelimab and rocatinlimab represents a promising step in this direction.

Safety remains a paramount concern, as the long-term viability of these treatments heavily relies on their tolerability. While clinical trials suggest a manageable safety profile for these new agents, further Phase 3 trials and real-world data are essential to confirm their safety comprehensively. Ensuring robust and prolonged safety monitoring will be critical to validating the long-term use of these therapies in diverse patient populations.

Furthermore, the long-term implications of these therapies are yet to be fully understood. Short-term trials have demonstrated promising efficacy; however, the chronic nature of AD necessitates a deeper understanding of the potential for these therapies to modify disease progression over longer periods. Longitudinal studies and rigorous post-marketing surveillance will be crucial to establish the enduring impacts of these therapies and ensure their continued benefit without unforeseen complications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this article aimed to provide a comprehensive review of emerging biologic therapies for AD, focusing on their mechanisms of action, efficacy, and safety profiles. The ongoing development of biologic therapies targeting specific immune pathways in AD represents a promising advancement for the management of moderate to severe disease. These therapies, including IL-4, IL-13, IL-22, IL-31, TSLP, and OX40-OX40L inhibitors, have shown significant efficacy in improving disease outcomes, with a considerable proportion of patients achieving EASI-75 responses. The safety profiles of these agents have been generally favorable, with most adverse events being mild to moderate in severity. Although further research is needed to confirm long-term effects, the development of these therapies represents a significant step forward in the management of AD, particularly for patients who have not responded adequately to existing treatments. As these biologics advance through clinical trials, they hold the potential to reshape the therapeutic landscape of AD.
