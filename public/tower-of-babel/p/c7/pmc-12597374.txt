Paediatric difficult intubations and the impact of timing of weekend versus weekday cases: an analysis of the Pediatric Difficult Intubation registry
Authors: Christy J Crockett, Judit Szolnoki, Sabina Khan, Angela C Lee, Alexander Kaizer, Patrick Olomu, Melissa Brooks Peterson, Pediatric Difficult Intubation Collaborative
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOff-hours presentation has been shown to be independently associated with tracheal intubation-related adverse events in patients with predicted difficult airway. The comparison of the complication rates of paediatric difficult intubations on weekends vs weekdays is unknown. The primary objective of this retrospective review is to determine the complication rates for paediatric difficult intubation cases that occur on a weekend vs a weekday.MethodsWe performed a retrospective review of data from the Pediatric Difficult Intubation registry, with service dates June 8, 2012 to May 9, 2023, to compare complication rates (total complications, hypoxaemia, non-hypoxaemia complications, and first-attempt success rate) during paediatric difficult intubations that occurred on the weekends vs weekdays. Research electronic data capture was used to standardise data collection across institutions.ResultsWe reviewed a total of 8031 cases of difficult paediatric intubations. Two hundred and eighty-five (3.5%) were performed on the weekend, and 7746 (96.5%) were performed on a weekday. Complication rates with paediatric difficult intubations (total complications, hypoxaemia, and non-hypoxaemia complications) were significantly higher on weekends compared with weekdays. The odds of any complication were more than two times higher on the weekends compared with weekdays (odds ratio 2.13, 95% confidence interval 1.49–3.07, P<0.0001).ConclusionsThis study supports paediatric anaesthetists’ clinical decision-making to postpone nonurgent paediatric procedures in children with a history of a difficult airway from weekend to weekday to decrease risk of airway management complications and improve patient safety and quality of care.Keywords: airway management, difficult intubation, paediatric airway complications, paediatric intubation, tracheal intubation

In patients with a predicted difficult airway, off-hours presentation is independently associated with an increased risk of tracheal intubation-related adverse events.1 Emergency intubations in paediatric patients are twice as likely to occur during off-hours (non-daytime hours) and are associated with a three-fold increase in complications compared with non-emergent intubations.2 In children with difficult tracheal intubation, more than two direct laryngoscopy attempts are associated with higher rates of severe complications and failure.3 While much is known about the risks associated with off-hours, emergency intubation, and multiple attempts during difficult airway management, the differences in complication rates between weekdays and weekends remain unknown.

The primary objective of this retrospective study was to compare the total complication rates associated with paediatric difficult airway management between weekends (Saturday/Sunday) and weekdays (Monday through Friday). Secondary objectives included analysing hypoxaemia incidence (hypoxaemia is defined as oxygen saturation <90% for >1 min), non-hypoxaemia complications, and first-attempt success rates during paediatric difficult airway management on weekdays vs weekends. Non-hypoxaemia complications included airway trauma, pharyngeal bleeding, epistaxis, vomiting, aspiration, oesophageal intubation, bronchospasm, laryngospasm, arrhythmia, cardiac arrest, death, pneumothorax, and other. We hypothesised that the rates of all complications and secondary complication types would be higher on weekends compared with weekdays.

Methods

This study was approved by the multi-institutional data-sharing agreements of the Pediatric Difficult Intubation (PeDI) registry and the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP; Philadelphia, PA, USA) Institutional Review Board (IRB 12-009457), with approval from the Executive Committee of the PeDI registry. The PeDI registry, an international, multicentre, collaborative initiative founded by the Society for Pediatric Anesthesia, includes 47 centres worldwide. This registry collects and shares data on paediatric difficult intubations and serves as a resource for research, quality improvement, and outcomes.3 Inclusion in the registry is limited to paediatric patients <18 yr old with difficult direct laryngoscopy, defined by strict criteria.3 Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) is used to standardise data collection across institutions.4,5 A waiver of consent, assent, and parental permission to pursue this study was granted by the CHOP Institutional Review Board (IRB 12-009457). This paper was prepared by utilising the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.6 Data from the PeDI registry were reviewed for cases between August 2012 and September 2023, focusing on complication rates during paediatric difficult intubations occurring on weekdays (Monday–Friday) vs weekends (Saturday/Sunday).

Statistical analysis/data analysis plan

Data are summarised as mean (standard deviation) for continuous variables and frequency (percent) for categorical variables. Continuous measures are compared using two-sample unequal variance t-tests, while categorical measures are compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. We used logistic mixed effects regression models with a random intercept for each participant to account for individuals with multiple observations using PROC GLIMMIX is found in SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA), of who the manufacturer is the SAS Institute (Cary, NC, USA) with the residual pseudo-likelihood estimation technique and Newton–Raphson with ridging for estimation of outcomes of interest. All analyses unless otherwise noted were performed using R v4.1.0 (Vienna, Austria).

Exclusion criteria for the study included age over 18 yr, no service date documented, and zero attempts at airway management recorded.

Results

A summary of the data characteristics by weekday vs weekend is presented in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, with values expressed as frequency (percentage) for categorical data. We analysed a total of 8031 paediatric difficult intubation events from the PeDI registry over an 11-yr period, of which 96.5% (7,746 events) occurred on weekdays (Monday–Friday) and 3.5% (285 events) on weekends (Saturday/Sunday).

The overall distribution of ASA physical status on weekends varied significantly from those on weekdays (P<0.001). Specifically, compared with weekdays, weekends had higher rates of ASA Class 1 patients (9.6% vs 2.8%,), ASA 4 patients (24.1% vs 12.5%), and ASA 5 patients (3.9% vs 0.3%). Conversely, a higher proportion of ASA 3 patients were seen on weekdays compared with weekends (63.5% vs 44%). There were a similar amount of ASA 2 patients between weekends and weekdays (18.4% vs 21%). Additionally, there were significantly more emergent cases performed on the weekends compared with weekdays (49.8% vs 6.6%) (Table 1).

Reasons for difficult intubation

Weekend difficult intubations were characterised by significantly more cervical spine mobility issues and facial trauma, while weekdays saw significantly more cases of micrognathia, microtia, facial asymmetry, and cleft palates (P<0.05 for each) (Supplementary Table S2). Regarding anticipated difficulty in intubation, there was no significant difference between weekends and weekdays (17.9% vs 15.7%, P=0.096) (Supplementary Table S2). Additionally, no significant differences were found in first-attempt success rate, adjunct used, or all attempts failed rate between weekend and weekday (all P>0.05) (Supplementary Table S3).

Overall complication rate

The overall complication rate (any complications) was significantly higher on weekends compared with weekdays (13.7% vs 6.9%, P<0.001) (Fig. 1). Specific complications observed more frequently during weekends included cardiac arrest (2.1% on weekends vs 0.7% on weekdays, P=0.015), hypoxaemia (12.3% on weekends vs 6.7% on weekdays, P<0.001), and arrythmias (0.7% on weekends vs 0.1% on weekdays, P=0.031) (Table 2).

There was no difference in the use of supplemental oxygen during tracheal intubation attempts between weekdays and weekends (13.6% vs 10.5%, P=0.163) (Supplementary Table S3).

Outcome (with predictors for weekend/weekday status): any complications

The odds of experiencing any complication were 2.13 times higher on weekends compared with weekdays (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.49–3.07, P<0.0001). After adjusting for age, ASA physical status, emergent status, prematurity, and diagnosed syndromes, this odds ratio (OR) remains significant at 1.62 times the odds of complication for weekend cases compared with weekday cases (95% CI 1.10–2.38, P=0.015).

The increased OR of around two times higher for any complication occurring on the weekends vs the weekdays remains present across multiple regression models that adjust for different sets of important variables. This includes accounting for anticipated difficulty of intubation (OR 2.12, 95% CI 1.47–3.05, P<0.0001), adjunct type used (OR 2.15, 95% CI 1.49–3.10, P<0.0001), provider type first attempt (OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.45–3.01, P<0.0001), location of intubation attempt (OR 1.87, 95% CI 1.29–2.72, P=0.001), device group (OR 2.17 95% CI 1.51–3.12, P<0.0001), and ‘box location’ (anatomic location of abnormality) (OR 2.14, 95% CI 1.49–3.09, P<0.0001).

Hypoxaemia

Hypoxaemia outcome

The odds for the outcome of hypoxaemia were two times higher on the weekends compared with weekdays (OR 2.09, 95% CI 1.30–3.38, P=0.003). However, after adjusting for age, ASA physical status, emergent status, premature status, and diagnosed syndrome, this association was no longer significant (OR 1.29, 95% CI 0.77–2.16, P=0.336).

Hypoxaemia causes

There was a significant difference found between the causes of hypoxaemia (prolonged intubation attempts, patient condition, airway activation, and other) on the weekends compared with weekdays (P=0.014) (Table 2). Prolonged intubation was a higher cause of hypoxaemia on the weekend (50.0%) compared with weekdays (40.2%), although it was the most common cause for both. There were also more ‘patient condition’ causes of hypoxaemia on the weekends (37.5%) compared with weekdays (26.9%). More ‘airway activation’ causes of hypoxaemia were reported on weekdays (18.6%) than weekends (0.0%).

All complications other than hypoxaemia

The odds for non-hypoxaemia complications were two times higher on the weekends compared with weekdays (OR 2.11, 95% CI 1.35–3.29, P=0.001). This association was still significant after adjusting for age, ASA physical status, emergent status, premature status, and diagnosed syndrome (OR 1.77, 95% CI 1.09–2.87, P=0.020).

First attempt at intubation

First attempt at intubation and complication rate

There was no significant difference in the first attempt at intubation success rate on the weekend compared with the weekday (P=0.142) (Supplementary Table S3). However, despite similar first-attempt success rates, weekends had significantly more complications during the first attempt at intubation, including hypoxia (7.4% on weekends vs 3.7% on weekdays, P=0.002), vomiting (0.7% on weekends vs 0.1% on weekdays, P=0.023), and arrhythmia (0.7% on weekends vs 0.1% on weekdays, P=0.023) (Table 3).

Provider type: first intubation attempt

The first attempt at intubation was performed by various providers, including trainees, certified registered nurse anaesthetists (CRNAs), anaesthesia assistants, attending anaesthetists, attending otolaryngologists, and attending ‘other’. A trainee performed the first attempt at intubation in most cases for both weekends (178 [62.7%]) and weekdays (4423 [57.2%]), with no significant difference of trainee as first attempt at intubation on weekends vs weekdays (P=0.076). Other providers of first attempt at intubation included CRNAs (eight [2.8%] on weekends vs 1106 [14.3%] on weekdays), anaesthesia assistants (two [0.7%] on weekends vs 69 [0.9%] on weekdays), and attending anaesthetists (74 [26.1%] on weekends vs 1832 [23.7%] on weekdays), attending otolaryngologists (11 [3.9%] vs 250 [3.2%]), and attending ‘other’ (11 [3.9%] vs 49 [0.6%]) (Supplementary Table S3).

Intubation devices

Supplementary Table S3 also reports the weekends vs weekdays comparisons for ‘first device used’ and ‘successful device used’ in difficult intubations. The ‘First device group’ summary compares device used first, regardless of whether it was successful. In this comparison, there was not a significant difference between weekends and weekdays (P=0.165) for what device group was used in the first attempt.

However, the “Successful device group” comparison findings were significantly different between weekdays and weekends (P=0.009). This group is not restricted to the first attempt, rather it summarises the device that was ultimately successful. The significant P-value here suggests that there is a significant difference between weekends and weekdays for what device group is ultimately successful. Video laryngoscopy was the most successful device group and was similar for both weekends (56.5%) and weekdays (54.6%). The percentage of the successful device group being fibreoptic scopes and rigid bronchoscopy was higher on weekdays, whereas the percentage of the successful device group being direct laryngoscopy and surgical airways was higher on the weekends (Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

The overall complication rate (any complications) was significantly higher on weekends compared with weekdays (13.7% vs 6.9%, P<0.001) with the odds of experiencing any complication being two times higher on weekends compared with weekdays (95% CI 1.49–3.07, P<0.0001). Even after adjusting for age, ASA physical status, emergent status, prematurity, and diagnosed syndromes, this OR remains significant at 1.62 times the odds of complication for weekend cases compared with weekday cases (95% CI 1.10–2.38, P=0.015). In addition, despite there being no significant difference in rate of trainee as first attempt at intubation and similar first-attempt success rates, weekends still had significantly more complications during first attempt at intubation compared with weekdays.

This study provides evidence that paediatric difficult airway management, both emergent and non-emergent, carries a higher risk of complications on weekends (Saturday/Sunday) compared with weekdays (Monday through Friday). These findings raise the consideration that non-emergent surgical cases in patients with known difficult airways should safely be postponed until weekday hours to decrease the risk of complications.

We suspect that the observed differences in complication rates may be because of several factors. One significant contributor is the availability of multiple experienced anaesthetists during weekdays, providing additional support and expertise when managing difficult airways.

Additionally, the robust equipment and support personnel present on weekdays, such as anaesthesia technicians, central supply, pharmacy staff, and operating room nursing personnel, are often limited on weekends. The absence of or delay in access to these resources could make complex airway management more difficult, potentially contributing to the higher complication rates observed on weekends.

While the exact causes of the increased complications are not fully understood, our research highlights many potential contributing factors that may explain increased complications on weekends vs weekdays. Regarding hypoxaemia specifically, the odds for the outcome of hypoxaemia were two times higher on the weekends compared with weekdays (OR 2.09, 95% CI 1.30–3.38, P=0.003). However, after adjusting for age, ASA physical status, emergent status, premature status, and diagnosed syndrome, this association was no longer significant (OR 1.29, 95% CI 0.77–2.16, P=0.336). This may be because of the study being underpowered to detect significant differences after these adjustments because the overall incidence of hypoxaemia was relatively low.

In addition, while the primary and secondary outcomes of this study did not include an analysis of practice trends over time, we believe that examining changes in airway management practices is valuable and relevant. The PeDI registry spans over 11 yr, and we recognise the potential it holds for identifying evolving patterns in clinical practice. We have ongoing work exploring these trends. For example, one of our studies reveals changes in patient management and associated improvements in patient outcomes over the past decade. More improvements in patient outcomes may be possible with supplementary efforts to limit direct laryngoscopy in paediatric patients with difficult tracheal intubations.7 The PeDI registry anticipates sharing additional findings in future publications.

Our findings suggest that routine or elective procedures for paediatric patients with known or suspected difficult airways may be safer if scheduled during weekday hours. Delaying such procedures could improve patient safety and overall quality of care, particularly for patients with a known or suspected difficult airway.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, its retrospective nature means that it cannot provide data on long-term outcomes and can only establish a correlation between the timing of cases and complications. Also, while the PeDI registry includes many cases across a variety of locations and institutions worldwide, it is limited in scope and depth. For example, the registry lacks detailed information on preoperative anaesthetic and surgical decision-making, which could provide valuable context for understanding the timing of cases and their associated complications. Furthermore, the PeDI registry does not capture data on the time of day when difficult airway management occurs (e.g. daytime hours vs off-hours during the week).

The PeDI registry is deidentified in that specific institutional characteristics and resource data are not collected. Access to more detailed context regarding the types of participating institutions (e.g. tertiary academic centres vs community hospitals) and the potential influence of resource availability by case would enhance the assessment of generalisability. However, owing to the deidentified nature of the PeDI registry, we are unable to perform this level of analysis within the current dataset, and we recognise this as a limitation. Another possible study limitation to consider is that the registry participation may be biased toward academic institutions with higher reporting diligence and difficult airway interest.

Another important limitation is the lack of data on intensive care unit and hospital floor care for patients with difficult airways, and information about anaesthesiologist consultation and availability. Finally, we note that we report the P-values directly from statistical tests and models without correcting for multiple testing. By reporting the observed P-values, we invite others to use their own correction if of interest.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, our study shows that paediatric difficult airway management on weekends is associated with a higher rate of complications compared with weekdays. This study underscores the potential variability in airway-related complications that may arise from the timing of difficult airway management decisions. A portion of the anaesthesiologist’s role as a perioperative leader is to balance safe practice with practice management concerns such as efficient operating room utilisation and accommodating surgeon availability. Our study provides evidence to support paediatric anaesthetists in making informed clinical decisions regarding the timing of non-urgent procedures in children with a history of a difficult airway. Specifically, the data suggest that deferring such cases from weekends to weekdays (when staffing, resources, and multidisciplinary support are typically more robust) may reduce airway management complications and enhance patient safety. While this recommendation may seem intuitive, in today’s healthcare climate marked by operational, financial, and scheduling pressures, decisions that prioritise safety over convenience require clear, data-driven, and evidence-based justification. Our findings offer this support and are intended to empower clinicians advocating for safer care practices in vulnerable paediatric populations.

We cannot control for the patient with unexpected difficult airway who needs care emergently. However, we can control variables such as the timing of urgent but non-emergent procedures and elective cases within our own institutions. To mitigate the risk of airway management complications, we recommend that the anaesthesia care team conduct a thorough risk–benefit analysis when determining the timing of difficult airway management. Particular attention should be given to the availability of skilled support and institutional resources. When feasible, such cases should be scheduled during weekday daylight hours, when full staffing and support services are more reliably available.

Further research should aim to identify specific features, disease processes, or airway types that pose the highest risk for complications, which would help refine the approach to airway management in this high-risk patient population.
