Differentiating HFmr/rEF from HFpEF using standard 12-lead ECG measurements: an interpretable machine learning study
Authors: Chenxu Zhao, Po Jie Chan, Scott Dougherty, Yiting Fan, Alex Pui Wai Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferentiating heart failure (HF) with mildly reduced/reduced ejection fraction (HFmr/rEF) from HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) guides therapy but echocardiography may be delayed or unavailable. We developed and validated machine learning models using routine 12-lead ECG data to classify HF phenotypes.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort of hospitalised patients with HF, predictors available at or before the index ECG were used. HFmrEF was pooled with HFrEF (left ventricular ejection fraction <50%) for model development. Data were split 70/30 into training and held-out test sets. Random forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting and support vector machine models were trained and tuned using fivefold cross-validation in the training set. Boruta was used to select key ECG features. Test-set performance was evaluated by area under the curve (AUC) and accuracy; AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test.ResultsOverall, 495 patients were included (254 HFmr/rEF; 241 HFpEF). RF consistently performed best. Using ECG features alone, RF achieved an AUC of 0.821 (95% CI 0.752 to 0.890) and accuracy of 75.7%. A parsimonious RF model using 12 Boruta-selected ECG variables achieved an AUC of 0.832 (95% CI 0.766 to 0.899) and accuracy of 76.4%, with no significant AUC difference versus a comprehensive RF model using clinical/laboratory/ECG predictors (AUC 0.804, 95% CI 0.734 to 0.875; p=0.288) or the model using all ECG features (p=0.092). Adding X-ray cardiomegaly did not improve performance.ConclusionA parsimonious RF model based on a small set of standard ECG measurements differentiates HFmr/rEF from HFpEF with good discrimination, supporting ECG as an adjunct for phenotyping when echocardiography is not immediately available.Keywords: Cardiac Imaging Techniques, Heart Failure, Echocardiography

Introduction

The ECG, a diagnostic tool in use for over a century, remains indispensable for evaluating cardiac rhythms, conduction and ischaemia. In parallel, heart failure (HF) represents a critical and growing global health burden, affecting over 64 million individuals worldwide.1 2 Accurate phenotyping across the spectrum of reduced (HFrEF), mildly reduced (HFmrEF) and preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is essential, as these subtypes differ in prognosis and evidence-based treatment strategies.3 However, current classification relies on echocardiography, a modality that is often resource-intensive, operator-dependent and not always immediately accessible, particularly in underserved settings.

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) now offer a paradigm shift. The electromechanical remodelling that occurs in HF often manifests in subtle ECG alterations, which may elude conventional interpretation. AI-enhanced ECG (AI-ECG) can uncover complex, non-linear patterns within the signal, translating them into actionable diagnostic insights.4 Previous studies have demonstrated the capability of AI-ECG to detect left ventricular dysfunction,5 diastolic impairment6 and even predict adverse outcomes.7 Machine learning (ML) techniques such as random forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and support vector machines (SVMs) provide robust frameworks for building such predictive models.8 9

Building on this foundation, we aimed to develop and validate interpretable ML models to differentiate HFmr/rEF from HFpEF using routinely available clinical and ECG data from a single-centre cohort in Hong Kong. By leveraging widely accessible parameters, this approach seeks to provide a practical, scalable tool to support timely and accurate HF classification, especially where echocardiography is delayed or unavailable.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study screened consecutive patients admitted with HF to the Department of Cardiology at Prince of Wales Hospital, Hong Kong, between 1 January 2016 and 30 December 2019. Eligibility criteria were: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) a primary discharge diagnosis of HF (the first qualifying hospitalisation was the index admission); (3) a transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) within 30 days of admission documenting left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and structural parameters (eg, left ventricular hypertrophy, left atrial enlargement, systolic and diastolic function, valvular disease, chamber sizes); and (4) a 12-lead ECG obtained within 24 hours of admission with minimal external noise, a stable baseline and all leads visible and interpretable for QRS morphology, PR interval, ST segments and T waves. During case identification, we restricted the cohort to patients without (1) significant primary valvular heart disease (>moderate); (2) prior heart transplantation; (3) constrictive pericarditis or congenital heart disease, and with (4) adequate-quality TTE images or ECG data. Adequate-quality TTE images were defined as studies in which LVEF could be quantified and the main structural assessments were interpretable, as documented in the official echocardiography report. After eligibility screening, 499 patients were assembled for model development and evaluation.

Patients were classified using guideline-recommended LVEF thresholds10: HFrEF (LVEF ≤40%), HFmrEF (LVEF 41–49%) and HFpEF (LVEF ≥50%). Because only 55 patients had HFmrEF, and to reduce overfitting risk, we combined HFmrEF with HFrEF for analysis. This grouping is consistent with prior work showing HFmrEF shares key clinical characteristics with HFrEF, including age, sex distribution, systolic blood pressure and ischaemic aetiology.11 12 Accordingly, analyses compared HFmr/rEF (LVEF <50%) with HFpEF (LVEF ≥50%).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in this retrospective study.

Data collection

Baseline data were extracted from the Hospital Authority’s Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System. The prediction target was HF phenotype at the index admission (HFmr/rEF group vs HFpEF, defined by LVEF on TTE). All candidate predictors were restricted to measurements available at or before the index ECG to minimise information leakage.

Demographics and clinical characteristics

We collected age, sex, New York Heart Association functional class, admission vital signs (systolic/diastolic blood pressure and heart rate), presenting symptoms (eg, shortness of breath and peripheral oedema), cardiovascular risk factors and comorbidities (eg, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, ischaemic heart disease, prior coronary surgery, chronic kidney disease/renal failure, stroke, dementia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, smoking and alcohol history) and anthropometrics (body mass index and body surface area).

Laboratory parameters

Admission laboratory tests included complete blood count indices (white blood cell count, platelet count, neutrophil count and differential), coagulation profile (international normalised ratio), liver function tests (albumin, alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase, bilirubin), renal function (creatinine) and cardiac biomarkers (troponin T).

Chest X-ray

Presence of cardiomegaly was recorded as a binary variable (present/absent) from reports of chest X-rays (CXRs) performed within 24 hours of the indexed ECG.

ECG characteristics

Structured, device-exported measurements were obtained from standard supine 10-second 12-lead ECGs (MAC2000, GE Healthcare). Candidate ECG variables comprised intervals (P-wave duration, PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval and QT corrected by Bazett (QTeBz)), frontal plane axes (P axis, QRS axis and T axis), voltage/amplitude measures (RV5+SV1, RV6, SV2 and RV6+SV2) and automated ECG diagnostic statements, including heart rhythm categories, myocardial infarction (MI) (ECG-defined MI), conduction block and inverted T waves. If multiple ECGs were recorded within 24 hours of admission, the earliest ECG was selected as the index ECG.

Echocardiographic findings (including LVEF and structural parameters such as left atrial enlargement and valvular disease) and discharge medications were used to describe the cohort but were excluded from model development to avoid leakage and to restrict inputs to information available at the time of ECG. Unless otherwise specified, baseline variables were defined as the measurement closest to, and at or before, the index ECG during the index admission.

Data preprocessing

Variables and observations with ≥50% missingness were excluded.13 Missingness was assessed on the full dataset before model development using missingness indicators only. Device-exported ECG measurements sometimes contained zeros indicating unmeasurable values; these were recoded as missing. The data were then split into training (70%) and held-out test (30%) sets using stratified random sampling to preserve HF phenotype prevalence. Remaining missing values were imputed with missForest (RF-based imputation), fitting the imputer on the training set and applying it to the test set to minimise leakage. Categorical variables were one-hot encoded. For SVM, continuous features were standardised (zero mean, unit variance) using training-set parameters.

Model development and feature selection

We evaluated three algorithms: RF, XGBoost and SVM. Using fivefold cross-validation within the training set, models were trained under four feature configurations: (1) demographics, clinical variables, laboratory results and ECG features; (2) ECG features alone; (3) configuration 1 plus CXR cardiomegaly; and (4) ECG features plus CXR cardiomegaly. Hyperparameters were tuned via randomised grid search with cross-validated area under the curve (AUC) as the primary metric.

For the parsimonious ECG-only model, feature selection used Boruta, an RF-based wrapper method.14 Boruta was run once on the training set; ECG variables labelled ‘Confirmed’ were retained for the reduced-feature RF model. The held-out test set was not used for feature selection or tuning. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed in the held-out test set by sex and age (<65 vs ≥65 years). The 12-feature RF model was applied to each subgroup, with class labels generated by majority vote. Accuracy, sensitivity and specificity were calculated treating HFmr/rEF as the positive class.

To improve interpretability beyond conventional feature importance, we performed to improve interpretability beyond conventional feature importance, we performed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis using model-agnostic Shapley values. For the final model, the predicted probability P (HFmr/rEF=1) was used as the model output. Shapley values were estimated on the held-out test set using a Monte Carlo permutation approach. We summarised global feature importance as the mean absolute SHAP value and visualised the distribution and direction of feature effects using a SHAP summary plot.

Model evaluation and statistical analysis

For each algorithm, the feature configuration and hyperparameters with the highest cross-validated AUC in the training set were selected. The final model was refit on the full training set and evaluated once on the held-out test set. Performance was summarised by AUC, accuracy, sensitivity and specificity; 95% confidence interval (CI) were obtained using 1000 bootstrap resamples on the test set. HFmr/rEF was the positive class for sensitivity/specificity calculations. For confusion matrix-based metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value), predicted probabilities were dichotomised using a fixed threshold of 0.5 (probability ≥0.5 classified as HFmr/rEF) and metrics were computed on the held-out test set. AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test.15 Continuous variables are reported as mean±SD or median (IQR) and compared with Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as counts (%) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was set at two-tailed p<0.05. Analyses were conducted in R (V.4.2.1).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 499 consecutive patients were identified through the data query process after applying the prespecified clinical exclusion criteria. Of these, 4 were excluded due to ≥50% missingness, yielding 495 patients in the final analytical cohort, each contributing one index ECG: 254 (51.3%) were classified as HFmr/rEF (55 HFmrEF and 199 HFrEF) and 241 (48.7%) as HFpEF by LVEF criteria (table 1). The cohort was stratified-randomly divided into a training set (n=344) and a held-out test set (n=151); baseline characteristics did not differ between sets (all p>0.05; online supplemental table S1). The median ECG-to-TTE interval was 5 days (IQR: 3–14). Relative to HFpEF, patients with HFmr/rEF were more often male (74.4% vs 43.8%; p<0.001) and younger (median 69 (IQR 60–78) vs 77 (IQR 69–84) years; p<0.001). They had a higher heart rate (86 (72–108) vs 80 (68–95) bpm; p=0.002) and modest blood pressure differences (systolic 137 (120–156) vs 143 (126–161) mm Hg, p=0.007; diastolic 82 (72–95) vs 78 (67–89) mm Hg, p<0.001). Ischaemic heart disease (42.1% vs 29.0%; p=0.003) and current/prior smoking (33.9% vs 18.3%; p<0.001) were also more common in HFmr/rEF (table 1). Laboratory profiles differed, with higher troponin T and modestly higher bilirubin and alanine aminotransferase in HFmr/rEF (all p<0.001), along with slightly higher white blood cell and platelet counts (p=0.014 and p=0.019, respectively). ECG measurements showed consistent between-group differences. HFmr/rEF had longer QRS duration (101 (94–123) vs 96 (88–103) ms; p<0.001) and QTcB (457 (438–475) vs 443 (427–462) ms; p<0.001), but a shorter uncorrected QT interval (373 (331–405) vs 386 (349–424) ms; p=0.003). HFmr/rEF also had higher SV2 amplitude (1.24 (1.05–1.55) vs 0.86 (0.70–1.14) mV; p<0.001), a more leftward QRS axis (26° (–20 to 63) vs 41° (12 to 84); p<0.001) and a higher T-wave axis (86° (44–135) vs 56° (13–114); p<0.001).

Model development and performance

Three ML algorithms: RF, XGBoost and SVM, were trained and evaluated on the held-out test set (table 2; figure 1A). Using all non-echocardiographic predictors (n=53 input features), RF achieved the best discrimination on the test dataset, with an AUC of 0.804 (95% CI 0.734 to 0.875; bootstrap, 1000 resamples), accuracy of 70.9% (95% CI 63.5% to 78.4%), sensitivity of 66.2% (95% CI 53.9% to 76.6%) and specificity of 75.7% (95% CI 64.8% to 85.1%). XGBoost yielded an AUC of 0.761 (95% CI 0.684 to 0.839), accuracy of 68.9% (95% CI 61.5% to 77.0%), sensitivity of 69.4% (95% CI 59.0% to 79.7%) and specificity of 68.4% (95% CI 57.1% to 78.7%). SVM showed lower performance (AUC 0.662; 95% CI 0.586 to 0.739), with an accuracy of 66.2% (95% CI 58.1% to 73.6%), sensitivity of 62.2% (95% CI 50.7% to 72.9%) and specificity of 70.3% (95% CI 59.7% to 79.7%). Performance with ECG features alone

When models were trained exclusively on ECG-derived features (n=16 input features) (figure 1B), RF achieved the highest discrimination (AUC 0.821; 95% CI 0.752 to 0.890), with an accuracy of 75.7% (95% CI 68.2% to 82.4%), sensitivity of 74.3% (95% CI 63.9% to 84.1%) and specificity of 77.0% (95% CI 67.5% to 86.1%) (table 2). XGBoost achieved an AUC of 0.804 (95% CI 0.732 to 0.876), with an accuracy of 74.3% (95% CI 67.6% to 81.1%), sensitivity of 75.0% (95% CI 65.1% to 84.8%) and specificity of 73.7% (95% CI 63.5% to 83.1%). SVM had lower discrimination (AUC 0.689; 95% CI 0.615 to 0.763), with an accuracy of 68.9% (95% CI 61.5% to 76.4%), sensitivity of 60.8% (95% CI 49.3% to 71.4%) and specificity of 77.0% (95% CI 66.7% to 86.2%). Adding CXR cardiomegaly (n=54 input features) to either the all-variable models or ECG-only models did not produce a statistically significant improvement in discrimination for any algorithm (all DeLong test p>0.05).

Models trained on this optimised 12-feature ECG set (figure 1C) performed comparably to those using the full ECG feature set. On the test set, the 12-feature RF model achieved an AUC of 0.832 (95% CI 0.766 to 0.899), with an accuracy of 76.4% (95% CI 68.9% to 82.4%), sensitivity of 74.3% (95% CI 63.8% to 84.1%) and specificity of 78.4% (95% CI 68.3% to 87.7%). XGBoost showed similar discrimination (AUC 0.805; 95% CI 0.733 to 0.876), with an accuracy of 74.3% (95% CI 66.9% to 81.8%), sensitivity of 73.1% (95% CI 62.9% to 83.1%) and specificity of 75.7% (95% CI 65.3% to 85.5%), whereas SVM had lower performance (AUC 0.669; 95% CI 0.593 to 0.745), with an accuracy of 66.9% (95% CI 58.8% to 74.3%), sensitivity of 60.8% (95% CI 49.4% to 71.7%) and specificity of 73.0% (95% CI 62.1% to 82.7%) (table 2). DeLong’s test showed no significant difference in AUC between the 12-feature RF model and the RF model using all predictors (0.832 vs 0.804, p=0.288) or the RF model using all ECG features (0.832 vs 0.821, p=0.092). Given its consistently strong and balanced performance, the RF model using the 12 selected ECG features was selected as the primary model for subsequent analyses. To provide subject-level interpretability beyond conventional feature-importance measures, we performed SHAP analysis for the final RF model (figure 4) and summarised global importance using mean absolute SHAP values (online supplemental table S2). SV2 amplitude showed the largest contribution to the predicted probability of HFrEF (mean |SHAP|=0.146), followed by QRS duration (0.053) and QTeBz (0.033). Additional contributors included P-wave duration (0.029) and RV6+SV2 amplitude (0.026), with smaller effects for the remaining features. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 495 hospitalised patients with symptomatic HF, standard 12-lead ECG measurements differentiated HFmr/rEF from HFpEF with good discrimination using supervised ML. A parsimonious RF model using 12 Boruta-selected ECG variables performed similarly to the RF model using the full ECG feature set (AUC 0.832 vs 0.821) and to a model incorporating broader non-echocardiographic predictors (AUC 0.804). This suggests that much of the phenotype-relevant signal resides in a small set of routinely reported ECG descriptors, supporting clinical deployment by reducing data requirements while preserving interpretability. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to evaluate ECG-only ML for distinguishing HFmr/rEF from HFpEF in symptomatic patients.

Most prior AI-ECG work has focused on detecting reduced LVEF from raw waveforms. Attia et al16 showed that deep learning applied to ECG signals can identify LV systolic dysfunction with high discrimination (AUC 0.93), reframing ECG as a scalable screening tool for asymptomatic individuals in the general population beyond rhythm and conduction assessment. Choi et al17 similarly demonstrated waveform-based detection of reduced LVEF across cohorts (AUC 0.844). Our results align with these studies in linking electrical remodelling to mechanical dysfunction, but extend them in two ways. First, we used structured, device-exported ECG measurements rather than waveform embeddings while maintaining clinically useful discrimination (AUC 0.832), enabling error analysis using familiar ECG constructs (intervals, axes, voltages) and in subgroups where interpretation is challenging (eg, paced rhythms, bundle branch block, drug-related QT prolongation). Second, we addressed an admission-relevant phenotyping task—HFmr/rEF versus HFpEF, rather than a ‘normal vs low EF’ screening problem, which may better reflect treatment decisions and echocardiography triage in symptomatic inpatients.16 17 Third, unlike black-box deep learning approaches that obscure which ECG features drive their decisions, our Boruta-selected model produces a transparent set of 12 clinically recognisable variables, allowing clinicians to verify and trust the model’s reasoning, facilitating clinical adoption. Fourth, we provide a systematic algorithm comparison. While prior studies focused on single deep learning architectures, we compared RF, XGBoost and SVMs, demonstrating that tree-based ensembles outperform SVM for structured ECG data with non-linear interactions. Fifth, our model is resource-efficient and broadly deployable. Attia et al’s approach requires raw digital signal data, GPU-based deep learning infrastructure and proprietary software.16 By contrast, our model operates on routine ECG report data available from any standard ECG machine, making it implementable in low-resource settings without immediate cardiology support.

Kavas et al18 reported high accuracy for HF decision support using models that combined clinical parameters with ECG information. Although multimodal approaches can be powerful, they may be less portable across sites because data completeness, coding practices and feature definitions vary. By contrast, we evaluated ML models to differentiate HFmr/rEF from HFpEF in symptomatic patients using ECG data alone. With ECG-only modelling and rigorous feature selection, we achieved strong, stable test-set performance while improving scalability, particularly where echocardiography is limited and structured clinical variables are inconsistently available.

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, adding chest radiograph cardiomegaly did not improve discrimination (all DeLong test p>0.05). In acute care, radiographic findings are influenced by volume status, timing of diuresis, portable technique and comorbid lung disease; these factors may indicate decompensation but are not tightly linked to EF category. Prior work shows AI-enabled CXR can help identify acute HF and related conditions,19 20 but EF subclassification is a different goal. ECG, in contrast, reflects electrical remodelling tied to myocardial substrate, which may be more stable and phenotype-specific in HFmr/rEF than in HFpEF.21 Therefore, a single radiographic descriptor plausibly adds little to ECG-based phenotyping.

The prominence of conduction and repolarisation descriptors among selected predictors aligns with HF pathophysiology. QRS prolongation may reflect ventricular dilation, fibrosis and conduction system disease—features more common in reduced EF phenotypes and associated with dyssynchrony and adverse remodelling.22 Repolarisation indices (QT/QTc, T-wave axis) likely integrate effects of structural disease, autonomic tone, ischaemic burden and medication exposure, which may be amplified in systolic dysfunction and advanced remodelling.23 Voltage measures (eg, SV2 and composite precordial amplitudes) may capture geometric and remodelling differences across EF phenotypes; while imperfect alone, they add discriminative value alongside intervals and axes.24 Clinically, this supports an interpretable narrative: the model distinguishes HFmr/rEF from HFpEF using a combination of conduction delay, repolarisation disturbance and remodelling-associated voltage changes. SHAP also suggested heterogeneous and non-linear effects across individuals, and—as a post hoc method—should be interpreted as explaining the model’s behaviour rather than implying causality.

The relative performance of the algorithms also fits the data structure. ECG descriptors are correlated and likely relate to HF phenotype through non-linear effects and interactions, making tree-based ensembles a natural choice. RF and XGBoost can accommodate such interactions and are generally robust to collinearity and measurement noise.25 In contrast, the weaker performance of SVM may reflect limited separability and heterogeneous feature distributions in inpatient ECG data, which may not be fully addressed by kernel choice or tuning.26

Performance differences by sex and age warrant attention. HFpEF is more common in older patients and women, and ECG features vary across these strata (repolarisation intervals, voltage patterns, conduction disease prevalence). This does not negate model utility, but it argues for subgroup-specific calibration and threshold selection before deployment, especially for echocardiography triage in resource-limited settings.

An unexpected finding was that the comprehensive model incorporating all clinical, laboratory and ECG features did not outperform the parsimonious 12-feature ECG-only model (AUC 0.804 vs 0.832, p=0.288), with the latter showing a numerically AUC. Several explanations may account for this observation. First, information redundancy likely plays a role. Many clinical and laboratory differences between HFmr/rEF and HFpEF (eg, high troponin, higher heart rate, male sex, ischaemic aetiology) are likely already reflected in the 12 ECG features. Hence, ECG features may already serve as an integrative biosensor for underlying pathophysiology. Second, non-ECG variables may introduce noise into the model, including measurement errors, collection errors and context-dependent variability of different non-ECG variables, all of which do not produce a proportional signal gain. Third, while we have collected standard demographics, comorbidities and laboratory tests, we may have missed key discriminators such as medication use (eg, beta-blocker use) or HF aetiologies (eg, cardiomyopathies).

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, its single-centre retrospective design and reliance on vendor-specific structured ECG data may limit generalisability; external validation in diverse multicentre cohorts is required.27 Second, although HFmrEF was included, limited sample size required combining HFmrEF with HFrEF, which may obscure an intermediate phenotype and contribute to errors near EF cut-offs; future studies should test three-class or ordinal models when larger HFmrEF cohorts are available.28 ECG and echocardiography were not always contemporaneous (median 5 days), and unmeasured factors such as interim treatment, device therapy, HF aetiology and medication changes could affect both ECG features and EF category. Although NT-proBNP is widely used for HF diagnosis and risk stratification, its incremental value for HFrEF classification in our framework should be assessed in contemporary cohorts where NT-proBNP is routinely measured. Finally, we evaluated diagnostic performance on a held-out test set but not clinical impact, which will require prospective workflow-based evaluation with prespecified thresholds.

Implications and future directions

The RF models could provide timely, resource-efficient decision support for HF phenotype classification. Where echocardiography access is limited, embedding an ECG-based model into point-of-care or telemedicine workflows may help triage patients for confirmatory imaging. Using a compact ECG feature set also reduces computational burden and supports deployment in low-resource settings. Current HF screening guidelines offer limited support for broad screening and instead emphasise biomarkers and multivariable risk approaches.29 ECG-based ML models may complement these strategies by enabling scalable identification of patients more likely to have reduced-EF phenotypes and by improving referral pathways. While our structured-feature model offers interpretability and low resource requirements, raw-signal deep learning approaches may achieve higher discrimination by preserving subtle morphological details. Therefore, head-to-head comparisons between these paradigms for HF phenotyping are warranted to determine whether the advantages of our approach of interpretability justify any potential performance trade-offs. Future work should prioritise external validation, calibration assessment and evaluation of single-lead implementations to enable integration with mobile and wearable devices.30

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that supervised ML applied to routinely available 12-lead ECG measurements can differentiate HFmr/rEF from HFpEF in hospitalised symptomatic patients with HF. A compact, interpretable RF model using Boruta-selected ECG features matched the performance of models using broader non-echocardiographic inputs, supporting a practical ECG-only phenotyping strategy. Multicentre external validation and prospective workflow studies are still needed to confirm generalisability and clinical impact, especially for echocardiography triage in resource-limited settings.
