Dental caries, treatment needs, and oral hygiene status among institutionalised people with disabilities in Thailand: a cross-sectional observational study of oral health equity under universal health coverage
Authors: Nithimar Sermsuti-Anuwat, Tanit Arunratanothai, Itt Assoratgoon, Khunnakrit Ratchatasupak, Ronlada Porntirit, Daophon Ardiam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo describe dental caries experience, treatment needs, and oral hygiene status among institutionalised people with disabilities in Thailand, and to assess age patterns and facility-level differences between two sex-segregated foster homes under universal health coverage (UHC).Materials and methodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted among 301 residents aged 7–59 years in two government-run foster homes selected by proportionate stratified random sampling. Examinations used World Health Organization (WHO) dentition criteria and the Simplified Oral Hygiene Index (OHI-S). Logistic regression estimated adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for untreated decay (dt/DT > 0) and caries experience (dmft/DMFT > 0), adjusting for age and facility. Modified Poisson models with robust standard errors were used to estimate adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) as sensitivity analyses.ResultsThe mean dmft/DMFT index was 9.36 ± 7.24; 89.4% had caries experience and 85.4% had untreated decay. Caries burden increased with age. Adults (19–59 years) had higher odds of untreated decay (aOR 2.80; 95% CI 1.23–6.39) and caries experience (aOR 5.12; 95% CI 1.84–14.24) than children (≤ 12 years). The female facility had higher odds of caries experience than the male facility (aOR 2.60; 95% CI 1.07–6.30); because the institutions were sex-segregated, this should be interpreted as a facility-level difference rather than a biological sex effect. Sensitivity analyses using aPRs confirmed these associations.ConclusionsDespite UHC, institutionalised residents with disabilities had very high levels of untreated caries and poor oral hygiene, indicating persistent gaps between coverage and effective access to care. Observed differences between facilities reflect structural or organisational factors rather than biological sex effects, as the two institutions were sex-segregated.Clinical relevanceInstitutionalised people with disabilities are rarely represented in national oral health surveys, yet they face substantial barriers to care. Our findings demonstrate a very high caries burden and poor oral hygiene despite universal health coverage, underscoring the need for targeted institutional protocols, enhanced caregiver training, and proactive outreach services to bridge the gap between policy and practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08724-6.Keywords: Disabled persons, Dental caries, Foster home care, Residential facilities, Public health, Universal health coverage

Introduction

Oral health is an integral part of general health and quality of life [1, 2]. However, oral diseases remain among the most prevalent non-communicable conditions worldwide, imposing substantial burdens on health systems [1, 2]. Consequently, the World Health Organization (WHO) calls for stronger integration of oral health into universal health coverage (UHC) and the prioritisation of populations facing persistent inequities [2, 3].

Persons with disabilities comprise approximately 16% of the global population [3] and face systemic barriers that lead to higher risks of multimorbidity and avoidable complications [3–5]. Oral health mirrors these inequities: studies consistently report higher levels of dental caries and unmet treatment needs among individuals with disabilities compared to their non-disabled peers [6–9]. Contributing factors include dependence on caregivers for hygiene, communication difficulties, and gaps in provider training [4, 6–10].

While Thailand is a regional example of successful UHC with extensive benefit packages [2, 11–13], evidence suggests that people with disabilities may not benefit equally from these gains [3–5, 8, 12–16]. Crucially, national oral health surveys do not routinely disaggregate data by disability status or include large social welfare institutions [17, 18]. As a result, the oral health of people with disabilities in congregate care settings remains under-documented [3, 4, 6–9, 12–14].

Institutionalised populations face additional risks beyond those experienced in the community [9, 19–22]. Studies in residential facilities report high levels of caries and poor oral hygiene [9, 19–22], often attributed to high resident-to-staff ratios, cariogenic diets, and limited access to professional care [4, 6–10, 19–22]. However, data regarding large government-run foster homes in Thailand are particularly scarce [14].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify caries experience, treatment needs, and oral hygiene status using WHO-based survey methods in large government-run foster homes in Thailand. We aimed to describe these outcomes among residents aged 7–59 years and explore associations with age group and facility. Because each foster home was sex-segregated (male-only vs. female-only), comparisons between males and females are not interpretable as sex effects and are therefore discussed as facility-level differences. We hypothesised that residents would exhibit higher caries burden and unmet needs than national averages, providing evidence to support disability-inclusive oral health policies.

Materials and methods

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations for observational studies.

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional observational study between 1 and 8 October 2025 at two government-run residential institutions in Pak Kret district, Nonthaburi province, Thailand: the Rachawadee Home for Persons with Disabilities Protection and Development (Male) and the Rachawadee Home for Persons with Disabilities Protection and Development (Female). While most foster homes in this area are administered by the Ministry of Social Development and Human Security, these two facilities were specifically selected because they are dedicated to the care of people with disabilities aged 7 years and older. In contrast, other foster homes in the district primarily serve newborns and children under the age of 7 or cater to different populations such as orphans. Both facilities share similar management structures and daily routines but differ in resident demographics and staffing. Residents at both locations primarily access dental services at external public facilities.

At the time of data collection, the male facility accommodated 578 residents with 53 caregivers and 2 nurses (approximately 1:11 caregiver-to-resident and 1:290 nurse-to-resident, respectively). The female facility housed 424 residents with 88 caregivers and 5 nurses (approximately 1:5 caregiver-to-resident and 1:85 nurse-to-resident, respectively). These staffing patterns indicate that daily support is provided predominantly by non-professional caregivers with limited nursing oversight.

Study population and eligibility

The study population comprised residents with documented disabilities residing in the two foster homes during the study period. To be eligible for inclusion, residents had to meet the following criteria: (1) a formal diagnosis of disability; (2) current residence in either the male or female facility; and (3) a classification by facility staff as requiring either no assistance or only partial assistance with activities of daily living. Residents were excluded from the study if they lacked the communication ability necessary to complete the interview and clinical examination, or if written informed consent could not be obtained from the resident or their legal guardian.

The restriction to residents requiring no or only partial assistance was mandated by ethical requirements. Consequently, residents with severe functional and communication impairments were excluded, which likely resulted in an underestimation of the overall oral disease burden.

Sample size and sampling technique

Sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power version 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-University, Düsseldorf, Germany) [23]. The study was powered to compare the proportion of residents with untreated caries (dt/DT > 0) across three age groups (≤ 12, 13–18, 19–59 years) using a chi-square test of independence with 2 degrees of freedom. Based on a pilot study conducted in the male facility (n = 30) among residents who did not participate in the main survey, together with existing literature on institutionalised populations [6], at least a medium effect was anticipated. Assuming a medium effect size (w = 0.30), two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% power, a minimum required sample size of 246 participants.

To account for potential non-response, the target sample size was set at 300. From a total population of 1,002 residents (578 in the male home and 424 in the female home), proportionate stratified random sampling was used. A sampling fraction of approximately 0.30 (300/1002) was applied to each facility based on its proportion of total residents, yielding target quotas of 173 male and 127 female residents (578 × 0.30 and 424 × 0.30, respectively). These quotas and the proportionate sampling design were pre-specified in the protocol submitted to the ethics committee. Although the initial plan included the total resident population, the sampling frame was subsequently refined to exclude fully dependent residents following the committee’s ethical recommendations.

Before random selection, facility staff and the research team screened residents for eligibility and updated the name lists. First, 288 residents were deemed ineligible because they were fully dependent (118 from the male facility and 170 from the female facility) and were removed from the lists in accordance with the ethics committee recommendation. Second, 30 residents from the male facility who had participated in the pilot study were also removed to ensure they could not be recruited into the main survey. After these exclusions, the sampling frame comprised 684 eligible residents (430 from the male facility and 254 from the female facility). Participants were then selected from this sampling frame using simple random sampling with computer-generated numbers. In instances of refusal or unavailability, the next randomly selected resident on the list was invited to participate. Across the selection process, 138 residents refused participation and 245 were unavailable on the days of data collection (e.g., attending medical appointments, at school, or visiting family). A final sample of 301 participants (173 from the male facility and 128 from the female facility) completed both the interview and the clinical examination and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Measurement tools

Data were collected using two instruments: a modified oral health questionnaire and an oral health clinical survey form.

Modified oral health questionnaire

A study-specific questionnaire was developed based on the WHO Oral Health Surveys: Basic Methods [24]. It was adapted for face-to-face administration and simplified to ensure suitability for institutionalised people with disabilities. The questionnaire comprised two sections: (1) general characteristics (age, facility [sex-segregated], disability type, and caregiver assistance status); and (2) oral health-related behaviours and self-reported oral health problems, including toothbrushing frequency, consumption of sugary snacks or drinks, and a history of toothache or chewing pain during the previous 12 months. The full questionnaire is provided as a Supplementary File.

Oral health clinical survey form

The clinical assessment was minimally invasive, utilising WHO criteria and established protocols for special-care populations [24–27]. It comprised two components:

Dentition status

Teeth were examined using disposable mirrors and WHO probes in a designated room at each facility under portable headlamp/LED lighting, with participants seated (bedside when needed). Teeth were dried with gauze, and standard infection control was followed. Teeth were classified per WHO as sound, decayed, filled, or missing due to caries [24]. The dt/DT, mt/MT, and ft/FT components were summed to calculate combined primary and permanent dmft/DMFT (person-level caries experience).

Oral hygiene status

This was assessed using the Simplified Oral Hygiene Index (OHI-S) [25]. Debris and calculus scores were summed (range 0–6.0) and categorised as good (0–1.2), fair (1.3–3.0), or poor (3.1–6.0) [25, 27].

Quality assurance and pilot study

A pilot study (n = 30) was conducted in the male facility, using participants who were not included in the main survey, to assess feasibility and calibrate examiners [24]. The questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory content validity (Item–Objective Congruence index = 0.81) [28] and acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.76) [29]. Two dental assistants were trained as interviewers, and five dentists and five dental assistants were trained as examiners and recorders, respectively, under the supervision of a paediatric dentist. In Thailand, paediatric dentists commonly provide dental treatment for people with disabilities because the specialty of special care dentistry is still in its early stages of development [13].

Examiners and recorders were calibrated against the paediatric dentist, who served as the reference examiner. Both intra- and inter-examiner reliability were formally assessed in the pilot study using Polson’s crossover design [30]. For dentition status, Cohen’s kappa (κ) ranged from 0.612 to 0.787, with percent agreement ≥ 83% across tooth groups and ICC values of 0.803–0.871, meeting or exceeding national benchmarks (κ ≥ 0.70; agreement ≥ 80%). For oral hygiene (OHI-S), intra-examiner weighted kappa (κw) was 0.801 and 0.655 for the two examiners, with buccal surfaces demonstrating higher agreement than lingual surfaces; the intra-examiner ICC for buccal OHI-S was 0.816 (95% CI: 0.784–0.844). Inter-examiner reliability was assessed in a small volunteer subsample (n = 5) to minimise repeated examinations [30]. The inter-examiner ICC for OHI-S was 0.882 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.384–0.980), indicating excellent overall agreement; the wide confidence interval reflects the small subsample size. Dentition calibration covered WHO dentition status categories (sound, decayed, filled, and missing due to caries). Data from the pilot study describing age patterns in untreated caries were used to inform the assumed effect size for the subsequent power calculation.

Data collection procedures

Data collection occurred from 1 to 8 October 2025. Following informed consent, trained interviewers administered the questionnaire face-to-face. Caregivers were permitted to clarify daily behaviours (e.g., toothbrushing habits) but were instructed not to influence responses. Clinical examinations were subsequently performed according to the standardised protocol. Data were recorded on paper forms, checked daily for completeness, and double-entered into the study database. No missing data were encountered; thus, imputation was not required.

Primary outcomes were:

Explanatory variables included facility (male vs. female institution), disability type (intellectual/learning, physical, compound), caregiver assistance status (no assistance, partial assistance), toothbrushing frequency (< 2 vs. ≥ 2 times per day), sugary snack/drink consumption (never, once per day, ≥ 2 times per day) and toothache history (never, sometimes, often). Sex was recorded descriptively but was not analysed as an independent exposure because it was perfectly confounded with facility (sex-segregated institutions). Age was categorised as ≤ 12, 13–18 and 19–59 years to align with national benchmarks [15, 16].

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 30; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For categorical data, results are reported as counts with percentages. For continuous measures, we summarised values as mean and standard deviation (SD) when the distribution was approximately normal, and as median with interquartile range (IQR) when it was skewed. Distributional assumptions were checked visually with histograms and formally using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since dmft/DMFT and OHI-S did not meet normality assumptions, we applied non-parametric tests for group comparisons: Mann–Whitney U for two-group comparisons and Kruskal–Wallis for comparisons across three or more groups. Differences in proportions for caries experience, treatment needs, and OHI-S categories were evaluated using chi-square tests.

The caregiver-to-resident ratio was perfectly confounded with sex and institution; therefore, it was not included as an independent predictor. Because each institution was sex-segregated, sex was also perfectly confounded with institution (male facility vs. female facility); thus, any observed ‘male vs female’ differences were interpreted as facility-level (structural) differences and not as sex effects. Adjustment for clustering was not performed due to the small number of clusters (two institutions), potentially leading to slightly underestimated confidence intervals.

Binary logistic regression was used to examine associations of age group and facility (female vs. male institution) with treatment need (dt/DT > 0) and caries experience (dmft/DMFT > 0). Given the high prevalence of outcomes, adjusted odds ratios were interpreted cautiously as measures of association rather than relative risk. Final models included only age group and facility to provide parsimonious estimates comparable to national survey data [17, 18]. We also evaluated goodness of fit using the Hosmer–Lemeshow statistic and a pseudo R² measure. Collinearity among predictors was examined with variance inflation factors; values above 10 were considered problematic, and no such values were detected. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis: Because odds ratios derived from logistic regression may overestimate associations when outcomes are highly prevalent, we estimated adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) as a sensitivity analysis. We used modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors [31], including the same covariates (age group and facility).

Results

Participant characteristics and behaviours

A total of 301 residents participated (mean age 22.46 ± 11.61 years), slightly exceeding the target sample size (n = 300). Adults aged 19–59 years comprised 47.8% of the sample, followed by adolescents (35.9%) and children ≤ 12 years (16.3%). Males constituted 57.5% of participants, and 90.4% had intellectual or learning disabilities. While 61.1% required no caregiver assistance, 38.9% required partial assistance. Most residents reported brushing ≥ 2 times per day (87.0%) and consuming sugary snacks or drinks ≥ 2 times per day (85.7%) (Table 1).

Caries experience

The overall caries burden was high, with a mean dmft/DMFT of 9.36 ± 7.24 and a median of 8.0. Caries experience increased significantly with age (p < 0.001); median scores rose from 6.0 in children to 12.0 in adults (Table 2). Residents in the female facility exhibited higher mean dmft/DMFT than residents in the male facility (10.47 vs. 8.54; p = 0.042), driven by higher missing and filled tooth components. Residents requiring partial assistance had significantly higher dmft/DMFT scores than those requiring no assistance (10.82 vs. 8.43; p = 0.017).

Treatment needs and caries experience

Over 80% of residents had untreated decay (dt/DT > 0) and nearly 90% had caries experience (dmft/DMFT > 0). In adjusted logistic regression models (Table 3), adults (19–59 years) had significantly higher odds of treatment need (aOR 2.80; 95% CI 1.23–6.39) and caries experience (aOR 5.12; 95% CI 1.84–14.24) compared to children. Residents in the female facility had higher odds of caries experience than residents in the male facility (aOR 2.60; 95% CI 1.07–6.30). This comparison reflects facility-level or organisational differences because sex was perfectly confounded with facility. Treatment need did not differ between facilities (aOR 1.24; 95% CI 0.63–2.44).

In sensitivity analyses estimating prevalence ratios, adults (19–59 years) had a higher prevalence of caries experience (aPR 1.21, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.41) and untreated decay (aPR 1.20, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.44) compared with children (≤ 12 years). The female facility had a slightly higher prevalence of caries experience than the male facility (aPR 1.09, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.17), while untreated decay did not differ materially by facility (aPR 1.01, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.11). Adolescents (13–18 years) did not differ significantly from children for either outcome.

Oral hygiene status

The mean OHI-S score was 3.57 ± 1.71, and 55.1% of residents were classified as having poor oral hygiene. Oral hygiene deteriorated significantly with increasing age (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Although most residents reported brushing ≥ 2 times per day, their mean OHI-S scores were still high; however, those brushing ≥ 2 times daily had significantly lower mean scores than those brushing less frequently (3.47 vs. 4.30; p = 0.005).

Discussion

This cross-sectional observational study provides one of the first WHO-based quantifications of caries experience, treatment needs, and oral hygiene status among institutionalised residents with disabilities in Thailand. The findings reveal a critical public health concern: nearly 90% of residents exhibited caries experience, over 80% had untreated decay, and more than half demonstrated poor oral hygiene. These data indicate a severe burden of disease and unmet need in a population that, while formally covered by universal health coverage (UHC), appears to benefit substantially less from publicly funded dental care than the general population. In sensitivity analyses estimating prevalence ratios for these common outcomes, the direction of associations was consistent with the primary logistic models, supporting the robustness of inferences.

Caries burden and unmet need in the context of UHC

Compared with age-specific data from the Eighth and Ninth Thailand National Oral Health Surveys [17, 18], residents in these foster homes exhibited substantially higher caries experience, particularly among adolescents and adults. This aligns with international evidence that people with disabilities carry a heavier burden of oral disease than their non-disabled peers, even in nations with established UHC [3–8]. Similarly, large-scale studies of adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities report elevated levels of untreated caries and tooth loss [6–9].

In our study, the predominance of the decayed component over the filled component suggests that restorative care has failed to keep pace with disease accumulation. Similar gaps have been identified among Thai children with cerebral palsy and individuals with visual impairments [15, 16]. Our findings extend this concern to a broader age range of residents with predominantly intellectual and learning disabilities residing in large foster homes. The prevalence of untreated decay is comparable to, or exceeds, reports from institutional settings in Taiwan, Lebanon, and India [20–22], as well as psychiatric and long-term care populations [10, 19]. Collectively, these data suggest that the intersection of institutional living and disability creates structural disadvantages that allow disease to accumulate [6–9]. In Thailand, this represents a specific inequity: people with disabilities carry a higher disease burden while deriving fewer effective benefits from UHC than the general population [12–16].

Demographic disparities: age and facility (male facility vs. female facility)

Regarding differences between facilities, residents in the female institution had higher mean dmft/DMFT scores and higher adjusted odds of caries experience than those in the male institution. Because the institutions are sex-segregated, these differences cannot be viewed as biological “sex effects” but rather as facility-level or organisational differences. Caregiver training, daily routines, and dietary provision were reported to be similar in both homes, so these factors are unlikely to explain the disparity. Although the female home had more staff, this did not lead to better oral health outcomes. A possible explanation is that routine dental check-ups were usually offered only to fully independent residents [14]. The slightly higher proportion of residents needing assistance in the female home (51.3% vs. 48.7%) may therefore have faced greater barriers to preventive care [4, 12]. Further research is required to identify the specific organisational factors underlying these disparities. Notably, although the prevalence of untreated caries (dt/DT > 0) was similar across facilities, the female facility had higher missing and filled tooth components, suggesting a higher historical utilisation of dental care among female residents. This pattern is consistent with studies reporting that females tend to seek dental care more proactively than males [32, 33], and may partly reflect different care-seeking trajectories rather than an overall advantage in oral health status.

Oral hygiene, behaviour, and structural constraints

More than half of residents had poor oral hygiene (OHI-S), even though most reported brushing at least twice daily. This mismatch suggests that dexterity or cognitive limitations reduce brushing effectiveness [6, 7, 26]. It should also be noted that toothbrushing frequency was self-reported and may be subject to social desirability bias; residents interviewed in a supervised institutional setting may tend to overreport adherence to recommended hygiene practices, meaning the true frequency of adequate brushing could be lower than recorded. Although staffing ratios varied between homes, the overall resident-to-caregiver load, particularly for those caring for highly dependent residents, limited opportunities for individualised daily support. Evidence indicates that structured caregiver training and supervised toothbrushing are needed to address this gap [19, 20, 26]. Furthermore, the absence of a formal nutrition policy allows frequent access to sugary snacks and drinks in both facilities [6–9, 20–22]. Because residents rely on staff to procure food, including donated items, these dietary patterns reflect organisational constraints rather than personal choice. Therefore, interventions should combine assisted oral hygiene with institution-level policies to reduce free-sugar exposure.

Implications for UHC and oral health equity

Thailand’s UHC reforms have improved general health indicators [2, 11–13]; however, our findings indicate that institutionalised people with disabilities do not experience equitable benefits. Barriers likely include the separation of health and social care sectors, lack of on-site providers, and referral systems ill-adapted to residents’ needs [3, 4, 6, 12–14]. Access to routine care appears concentrated among residents who are fully independent [14], indicating gaps in proactive services for residents requiring assistance. Addressing this gap requires recognising institutionalised people with disabilities as a high-risk group within UHC monitoring [2–5, 8, 11, 13]. Oral health education should also be integrated with other health messages whenever such education is delivered. Implications exist at three levels:

Institutional level: Implement standardised assisted toothbrushing protocols, develop caregiver oral-health competencies, integrate oral health education with other health messages where appropriate, and establish policies to limit sugary intake.

Strengths, limitations and potential sources of bias

Strengths include the use of proportionate stratified random sampling, standardised WHO protocols, and robust sensitivity analyses to validate statistical associations.

However, limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding age, facility context, and oral health outcomes. External validity is limited because the analysis included only two institutions, restricting the extent to which facility-level differences can be interpreted or extrapolated beyond these settings and thereby limiting generalisability. In addition, ethical constraints mandated the exclusion of fully dependent residents and those with severe functional or communication impairments. Because higher dependency is often associated with poorer oral health, the reported burden likely underestimates the true oral disease burden among the most severely disabled institutionalised residents. Self-reported behaviours and symptoms, including toothbrushing frequency, sugary snack/drink consumption, and toothache history, may also be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Comparisons with the general population are also limited, as national surveys do not stratify data by disability status. Finally, analytical precision was constrained by the small number of institutions, which prevented adjustment for clustering and likely resulted in slightly underestimated confidence intervals. Future research should aim to include a broader range of institutions and residents with higher dependency levels, employing multi-level analytical approaches to better capture facility-level effects.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional observational study identified a very high burden of caries, extensive unmet need, and poor oral hygiene among institutionalised residents with disabilities in Thailand. Most participants had untreated decay despite UHC coverage. Caries experience increased with age, and residents in the female facility had higher odds of caries experience than residents in the male facility. Because sex was perfectly confounded with facility, this difference should be interpreted as a facility-level or organisational difference rather than a sex effect.

These findings indicate that people with disabilities in institutional care do not receive dental services in proportion to their needs. National surveys overlook institutions, while this study could not include the most severely disabled residents. Together, these gaps define a major “blind spot” in the oral health system. Addressing this requires disability-inclusive surveillance covering institutions, alongside integrated preventive and restorative services. Institution-based prevention (assisted toothbrushing and caregiver training), nutrition policies that reduce free-sugar exposure, and regular public dental outreach should be prioritised within UHC frameworks. Without such measures, the substantial needs of this population will remain hidden, and health equity commitments will not be fully realised.
