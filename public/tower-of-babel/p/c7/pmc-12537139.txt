The Use of Compression Staples for Scaphotrapeziotrapezoid Arthrodesis in the Treatment of Scaphotrapeziotrapezoid Arthritis
Authors: Stephanie W. Holzmer, Ryan K. Dahlberg, Ogonna N. Nnamani Silva, Christian E. Sampson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:Scaphotrapeziotrapezoid (STT) arthritis is not frequently encountered by hand surgeons and may occur in association with thumb carpometacarpal arthritis or in isolation. The incidence of STT arthritis has been reported at widely varying frequencies and may be addressed using different surgical techniques, depending on other concomitant foci of arthritis. Described treatments for isolated STT arthritis include STT arthrodesis, distal scaphoid excision, interposition arthroplasty, and pyrocarbon arthroplasty, though a consensus regarding the most effective technique is lacking. Early critics of STT arthrodesis note that this technique can be technically demanding and associated with high rates of complication, including nonunion; however, newer data have shown an acceptable complication profile with nonunion rates averaging 6.3%. Fusion of the STT joint has been achieved via the use of Kirshner wires, plates, screws, and staples; however, to our knowledge, no study has described a surgical technique using compression staples as the method of fixation for STT arthrodesis. As such, we present the use of compression staples in our 3-patient case series for the treatment of STT osteoarthritis. All patients achieved complete radiographic union within 4 months and experienced improvement in their radial-sided wrist pain; however, 1 patient subsequently developed central wrist pain secondary to capitolunate arthritis. Range of motion was largely preserved; however, grip strength decreased postoperatively. The average tourniquet time was 69 minutes. In summary, we find the use of compression staples to be a feasible technique for STT arthrodesis in the treatment of STT osteoarthritis.

Takeaways

Question: Can scaphotrapeziotrapezoid arthrodesis be achieved with the use of compression staples?

Findings: The use of compression staples as the method for fixation in scaphotrapeziotrapezoid arthrodesis for our 3-patient case series proved to be technically feasible with acceptable postoperative outcomes, as all patients went on to achieve bony union.

Meaning: The use of compression staples is a feasible technique for scaphotrapeziotrapezoid arthrodesis.

INTRODUCTION

Trapezial arthritis is frequently encountered by hand surgeons and most commonly occurs at its distal articulation with the thumb at the carpometacarpal (CMC) joint. Less frequently encountered is proximal trapezial arthritis located at the scaphotrapeziotrapezoid (STT) joint, which can occur in association with thumb CMC arthritis or in isolation. Similar to thumb CMC osteoarthritis (OA), STT OA may be clinically significant in some individuals, causing debilitating radial-sided wrist or thumb base pain and weakness, prompting these patients to seek surgical evaluation. Both cadaveric and radiographic studies have reported on the incidence of STT OA; however, these have yielded widely varying results ranging from 16% to 84%.1–4 This variation is dependent upon the method of evaluation as the radiographic presence of STT OA has not been shown to accurately correlate with the presence of degenerative changes at the STT joint seen under direct visualization.5,6 Furthermore, as with thumb CMC OA, the presence of degenerative changes at the STT joint does not necessarily correlate with patient symptoms, making the true incidence of clinically significant STT OA unknown.

Various presentations of clinically significant STT OA have been described in the literature, including radial-sided wrist and thumb base pain that may or may not be associated with thumb motion. Obert et al7 specifically noted that volar thumb base pain during resisted extension is characteristic of STT OA; however, Tomaino et al8 reported that in patients with arthrosis of both the thumb CMC and SST joints, they were unable to differentiate between the 2 on physical examination. Generally, patients with radial-sided wrist or thumb base pain and radiographic evidence of STT OA, in the absence of other pathology, are considered to have clinically significant STT OA. These patients warrant evaluation for further treatment, depending on the severity of symptoms.

The true etiology of STT OA is not yet fully understood; however, it is believed to be related to carpal kinematics due to its association with several factors that alter wrist biomechanics. Studies have established the association between isolated STT OA and chronic SLAC wrist as a form of nondissociative dorsal intercalated segmental instability where both the lunate and scaphoid are extended relative to the radius resulting in a normal scapholunate angle.9–11 Interestingly, the presence of a type 2 lunate has also been implicated in the development of STT OA, demonstrated by the study conducted by McLean et al.12 Lunate morphology directly impacts scaphoid motion at the STT joint, as scaphoids adjacent to type 2 lunates exhibit more flexion with radial deviation of the wrist compared with translational and rotational motion seen with type 1 lunates.13 This increased motion at the STT joint with scaphoid flexion may contribute to the development of STT OA over time.

As with the understanding of STT OA etiology, consensus regarding the most effective treatment for isolated STT OA is lacking. Various treatments have been postulated, including STT arthrodesis, distal scaphoid excision, trapeziectomy with or without partial trapezoid excision, interposition arthroplasty, and pyrocarbon arthroplasty.14–19 Watson et al20 produced the largest series of STT joint fusions for various indications, of which 8% were completed to treat degenerative disease. This study reported a nonunion rate of 4%, which was lower than previously reported in the literature, with a favorable range of motion achieving 75% of the arc of the contralateral side.20 These results are similar to those found in the systematic review conducted later by Stephens et al,21 who found a pooled nonunion rate for STT arthrodesis of 6.3%. Since this time, several studies have demonstrated STT arthrodesis as a favorable treatment option for STT OA; however, it should be noted that this technique can be technically demanding and is associated with high complication rates. To date, techniques used for STT arthrodesis include both open and arthroscopic approaches with varying methods of fixation, including Kirschner wires, plates, screws, and staples.15,18,21–24 Recently, the use of nitinol staples has gained popularity for small joint fusions due to their ease of insertion, as the entry points are parallel to the joint, in contrast to compression screws, which require perpendicular access across the joint. Unlike fixation with K-wires, staples provide continuous compression across small surfaces that have little contact for bony union to occur.25 A majority of the literature supporting the use of compression staples exists in studies pertaining to foot and ankle surgery; however, McKnight et al25 recently published a comparative study on the use of compression staples versus headless compression screws for capitolunate fusion, showing equivalent outcomes and demonstrating the applicability of compression staples in the carpus. Though the use of staples for STT arthrodesis has been previously mentioned in the literature, to our knowledge, no study has described a surgical technique using compression staples as the method of fixation for STT arthrodesis. As such, we present the use of compression staples for the treatment of STT OA in our 3-patient case series.

SURGICAL ANATOMY

The wrist comprises several unique articulations, which allow for complex 3-dimensional motion. As such, these articulations act as possible foci for degenerative change, which may lead to significant pain and functional limitation for the patient. The trapezium, which functions as a saddle-shaped bridge between the scaphoid and the thumb metacarpal, is frequently affected, with 39% of women showing radiographic evidence of trapezial arthritis by age 80. However, symptomatic manifestations of these findings are variable.26 Distally, the trapezium articulates with the thumb metacarpal base at the CMC joint and the index metacarpal more medially via a small facet. Medially, the trapezium also articulates with the trapezoid, which, in turn, articulates with the distal scaphoid, index metacarpal base, and capitate.27–29 Degenerative changes may occur at this triad of articulations among the scaphoid, trapezium, and trapezoid known as STT or “triscaphe” arthritis (Fig. 1). This articulation is stabilized by the STT ligament complex, which comprises 4 components: the radiopalmar scaphotrapezial ligament, the palmar scaphotrapezial-trapezoidal capsule, the dorsal scaphotrapezial-trapezoidal capsule, and the scaphocapitate ligament. Of these, the radiopalmar scaphotrapezial ligament is considered the main stabilizer of the STT joint.30

INDICATIONS/CONTRAINDICATIONS

Multiple pathologies have been treated via the use of STT arthrodesis, including degenerative joint disease (which remains the focus of this study), Kienböck disease, STT dislocation, as well as rotatory subluxation of the scaphoid and midcarpal instability.20,31 STT fusion stabilizes the scaphoid, preventing escape from the capitate upon axial loading of the wrist.20 This is thought to redirect axial loading away from the intermediate column, which is useful in settings such as Kienböck disease. We believe compression staples may be used for treatment in these settings. The main contraindication for STT arthrodesis with or without the use of compression staples includes the presence of concomitant symptomatic wrist OA that would not be addressed by STT fusion alone such as in cases of advanced scapholunate advanced collapse wrist or concomitant CMC OA. In these instances, a proximal row carpectomy, 4 corner fusion, or trapeziectomy with proximal trapezoid and distal scaphoid excision should be used, respectively.15

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Setup

The patient is brought to the operating room, positioned supine with a hand table attachment, and placed under regional anesthesia.

Superficial Exposure

Following standard preparation and draping procedures, the arm is exsanguinated, and an upper arm tourniquet is inflated. A zig-zag incision is marked, centered over the STT joint, which is localized with fluoroscopy. The proximal extent of the incision is carried just proximal to the Lister tubercle (Fig. 2A). The skin incision is then made with a 15-blade—with care taken to identify and preserve radial sensory nerve branches during this step.

Operative steps of STT arthrodesis with compression staples. A, A zig-zag incision is marked, centered over the STT joint, which is localized with fluoroscopy. B, The EPL tendon is then retracted radially, and a self-retainer is placed, retracting the ECRB and ECRL tendons ulnarly. A longitudinal arthrotomy is then made, dividing a small portion of the distal radiocarpal ligament, and the STT joint is visualized. C, A 2-mm burr is then used to remove the articular surfaces of the STT joint. A distal radius bone graft, harvested from Lister tubercle is then tamped into the cleared STT articulation. D–F, A 15-mm guide is used to create 4 holes for the staples (2 in the scaphoid and 1 each in the trapezium and trapezoid). Two 12 × 15-mm staples are then deployed and tamped into place. ECRB, extensor carpi radialis brevis; ECRL, extensor carpi radialis longus; EPL, extensor pollicis longus.

Deep Exposure

Once superficial exposure is complete, the extensor pollicis longus tendon is retracted radially, and a self-retainer is placed, retracting the extensor carpi radialis brevis and extensor carpi radialis longus ulnarly. A longitudinal arthrotomy is then made, dividing a small portion of the distal radiocarpal ligament, and the STT joint is visualized (Fig. 2B).

Reconstruction

A 2-mm burr is then used to remove the articular surfaces of the STT joint, allowing room for a distal radius bone graft, which is harvested with the use of an osteotome to remove the Lister tubercle. Cancellous bone graft is harvested from this site with a curette. Fluoroscopy is again used, and the previous STT articulation is carefully inspected to ensure the entire articular surface is obliterated. Bone graft is then tamped into the cleared STT articulation (Fig. 2C). The position of the scaphoid is assessed relative to the lunate, which is corrected with reduction as needed—temporary Kirschner wires may be used if needed during this step. Then, a 15-mm guide is used to create 4 holes for the staples (2 in the scaphoid and 1 each in the trapezium and trapezoid). Two DePuy Synthes BME SPEED 12 × 15 mm staples are then deployed and tamped into place. Stability with range of motion is assessed by direct visualization (Figs. 2D–F).

Closure

The wound is irrigated, and the dorsal radiocarpal ligament/arthrotomy is then repaired with 3-0 Ethibond suture. The tourniquet is released, and hemostasis is obtained. The skin is closed in layers.

Postoperative Management

The patient is placed in a plaster volar resting wrist splint intraoperatively. This remains in place until the first follow-up, which occurs at 2 weeks. At this time, the patient is transitioned to a wrist splint that may be removed for completion of range of motion exercises and hygiene. Active range of motion of the digits is unrestricted at this point. At 6 weeks postoperative, the splint is weaned, and hand therapy is initiated for wrist range of motion followed by the initiation of strengthening at 8 weeks.

EXPECTED OUTCOMES AND REVIEW OF THE LITERATURE

Variable outcomes following STT arthrodesis have been reported in the literature, and little data exist comparing methods of fixation. As mentioned previously, Watson et al20 found a 4% total nonunion rate following STT arthrodesis in 800 patients using Kirschner wires as their method for fixation. This is similar to a study published by Rogers et al32 reporting on 10-year follow-up data on STT arthrodesis in 54 patients using a variety of fixation methods, including K-wires (40), screws (3), staples (6), and plates (5). Outcomes were not stratified by the method of fixation; however, a pooled nonunion rate of 7% was found, with 10% of wrists requiring secondary surgery.32 Few studies report outcomes according to the method of fixation; however, Cholley-Roulleau et al33 specifically reported on outcomes following the use of compression staples for isolated STT OA in 22 wrists. They found an 18% (4) nonunion rate at 8-year follow-up; however, 50% (2) of these were partial nonunions with complete scaphotrapezial consolidation. Recently, a systematic review published by Stephens et al21 found a pooled nonunion rate for STT arthrodesis of 6.3% including all fixation methods. Again, outcomes according to the method of fixation were not stratified; however, this review notes that K-wires were used for fixation in 95.7% of all reported constructs.21 There is a clear lack of data investigating outcomes of STT arthrodesis by surgical method, which remains an area for future studies.

We present 3 cases of STT arthritis treated with STT arthrodesis using compression staples for fixation (Table 1). Type 2 lunates were found in 2 of our 3 patients. Complete radiographic union was achieved in all 3 cases by 4 months postoperatively. All patients had improvement in their radial-sided wrist pain; however, 1 patient subsequently developed central wrist pain secondary to capitolunate arthritis, which was present but asymptomatic preoperatively (Fig. 3). Due to the retrospective nature of this report, pre- and postoperative data pertaining to grip strength and range of motion were available for 2 of the 3 patients. Range of motion was largely preserved; however, grip strength (reported in percentage of contralateral grip strength) decreased in both patients to below the previously published value of 75.9%.21 The applicability of our data is limited not only by low sample size, but also by the timing of postoperative evaluation, as grip strength was measured at 9 months postoperatively for patient 1 during an exacerbation of his capitolunate arthritis, and at 2 months postoperatively for patient 3, at which time the patient was cleared for strengthening with hand therapy (Table 1). All 3 patients were satisfied with the procedure, and the average tourniquet time was 69 minutes (Table 1).

COMPLICATIONS

Several complications have been described following STT arthrodesis, including nonunion, radial styloid impingement, failure of symptom relief, development of arthrosis at an additional site (radiocarpal or trapeziometacarpal), and hardware migration.20,24,34–36 Rogers and Watson36 found that 33% of patients experienced symptoms of radial styloid impingement following STT arthrodesis, suggesting that partial radial styloidectomy should be considered at the time of STT fusion. We use the use of radial styloidectomy based on intraoperative findings of styloid impingement when ranged under fluoroscopy—one should note that the partially excised radial styloid may serve as a bone graft for completion of the arthrodesis. Careful patient selection may help mitigate the risk for major complications; however, patients should be counseled appropriately.

CONCLUSIONS

There remains a lack of consensus regarding the most effective method for STT arthrodesis due to a paucity of literature evaluating outcomes according to surgical technique. Compression staples have long been used in other realms of the body but have more recently been adapted for use in the carpus due to their ease of use and ability to apply continuous compression across small areas. Though staples have been mentioned for use in STT arthrodesis, to our knowledge, no article has described the surgical technique to do so in this setting. As described earlier, we find the use of compression staples for STT arthrodesis to be a reliable and replicable technique for the treatment of isolated STT OA, and suspect continuous compression across the staples may lead to improved union rates when compared with other methods of fixation. However, future comparative studies are required to determine this and remain a future aim of this study.
