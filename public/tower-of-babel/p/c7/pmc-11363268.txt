DNA damage-induced proteasome phosphorylation controls substrate recognition and facilitates DNA repair
Authors: Xiaomei Zhang, Tianyi Zhu, Xuemei Li, Hongxia Zhao, Shixian Lin, Jun Huang, Bing Yang, Xing Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceFor proteins that undergo ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation, the polyubiquitination step is believed to account for substrate selectivity. Now, we found that the 26S proteasome can also accept or reject ubiquitinated substrates based on its own status that can be regulated by DNA damage–induced phosphorylation. Through this previously unappreciated mechanism, proteasome function is well coordinated with the DNA repair process. In a broader sense, these findings suggest that substrate acceptance of the proteasome can vary under physiological conditions and may be an important determinant of the efficacy of targeted protein degradation.

Ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation underlies almost all important cellular processes and is closely related to a variety of pathologies (1). The 26S proteasome usually captures ubiquitinated substrates at its 19S regulatory particle (RP), which consists of six ATPase subunits Rpt1-6 and thirteen non-ATPase subunits (Rpns). Unfolded substrates are then translocated to the 20S core particle (CP, made of homologous α1-7 subunits and β1-7 subunits) for proteolysis (2, 3). Substrate recognition by the 26S proteasome mainly relies on three ubiquitin receptor subunits within the 19S RP, namely Rpn10, Rpn1, and Rpn13 (4, 5). Rpn10, also known as PSMD4 or S5a, was the first discovered ubiquitin receptor of the proteasome (6). It features a N-terminal von Willebrand factor A (VWA) domain and one or more C-terminal ubiquitin-interacting motifs (UIMs). Rpn10 in vertebrates has tandem UIMs (UIM1 and UIM2) that can each recognize monomeric ubiquitin and ubiquitin-like domains (UBLs) but together exhibit much higher affinity for polyubiquitin chains, including those formed by K48, K63, and K11 linkages (7–11). The linker region between UIM1 and UIM2 is structurally flexible, so that the UIMs can adopt different configurations when binding ubiquitin chains in different orientations (4). Complete knockout of Rpn10 or homozygous deletion of its UIMs causes embryonic lethality in mice, demonstrating its irreplaceable functions in higher organisms (12).

A well-established function of the UPS is to control the DNA damage response (DDR). DNA damage can be induced by a variety of chemical, physical, and biological insults (13), and genomic instability caused by deficiency in DNA repair is not only a hallmark of cancer (14) but also a critical target for therapeutic intervention (15, 16). Agents used for radiotherapy (ionizing radiation, IR) and chemotherapy (such as camptothecin, or CPT, a topoisomerase I inhibitor) can inflict the most severe form of DNA damage, double-strand breaks (DSBs). Repair of DSBs is usually carried out through homologous recombination (HR) and nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) (17, 18) and a supplementary pathway known as microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) (19). NHEJ occurs fast but is error-prone, whereas HR utilizes a temple DNA to produce accurate repair through more complicated steps. At the very upstream of these repair pathways lie three master kinases, namely ATM, ATR, and DNA-PK (20), which orchestrate the sensing, propagation, and termination of the damage signals by phosphorylating a myriad of substrates at their S/TQ motifs (21–23). Many of the phosphorylated proteins (e.g., H2AX, RPA2, CHK1) can recruit and/or modify other DDR factors, forming hubs of a vast signaling network.

The essential roles of ubiquitination in DDR, both degradative and nondegradative, have been widely recognized (24–33). Similarly, the proteasome is intimately involved in DDR. First, proteasome complexes are rapidly recruited to the site of damage, either via direct binding to ubiquitin or through other DDR factors (26, 34–42). Second, it has been well established that proteasomal degradation, i.e., the proteolytic activity of the proteasome, is indispensable for DDR. Numerous proteins involved in DNA repair, chromatin remodeling, histone modification, gene transcription, and cell cycle control are subject to ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation in response to DNA damage (43–50). Third, inactivation of the proteasome can sensitize cells to DNA-damaging agents, and combination therapies with proteasome inhibitors (e.g., Bortezomib/Velcade™) and drugs targeting the DNA repair system have shown clinical potential (51–53). However, premature or artificially induced degradation of DDR proteins may limit the duration of their operation, leading to attenuated repair efficiency or altered downstream effects (43, 54–57). Evidence exists that proteasome inactivation can also promote faithful DNA repair (58, 59), and antagonistic effects between proteasome inhibitors and genotoxic drugs have been reported as well (59–61). These variations from the theme described above indicate that the relationship between the proteasome and DDR may be more complicated than usually thought and that proteasomal degradation must be carefully regulated to maintain genomic integrity and wellness of the cell. However, previous studies on proteasome function almost exclusively relied on proteasome inhibitors and/or genetic ablation of proteasome components, which do not truly reflect physiological regulation of the proteasome but inevitably introduce considerable perturbations to the cellular proteome in the first place. As a result, the question of whether and how the proteasome itself is regulated in the DDR remains largely unanswered.

Here, we have characterized the function of DNA damage-induced proteasome phosphorylation at Rpn10-Ser266. Our in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that this phosphorylation regulates Rpn10 binding with ubiquitinated substrates and is required for optimal repair of DSBs. The results have revealed a previously unappreciated mode of proteasome regulation in response to DNA damage and provided an important clue to a better understanding of substrate recognition/selection by the proteasome under physiologically relevant conditions.

Results

DNA Damage Induces Rpn10 Phosphorylation at Ser266.

Our survey of phospho-proteomic data identified Ser266 of Rpn10 as one of the most frequently detected pS/pT sites of the human 26S proteasome (62, 63). Consistent with this site being part of a S266Q267 motif (Fig. 1A) that could be recognized by ATM/ATR/DNA-PK, identification of S266 phosphorylation by mass spectrometry (MS) was often in the context of DNA damage (23, 64–66). This SQ motif of Rpn10 is widely conserved in mammals, present in fish, and substituted by a TQ motif in most avian and some reptilian species (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Table S1). Of note, Rpn10 in mouse (Mus musculus) has an Asn (N266) in place of Ser, although this appears to be an exception since the majority of rodent species possess the SQ motif in their Rpn10 proteins (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Table S1).

Using the anti-phospho-S/TQ motif antibody, we confirmed that CPT-induced DNA damage could trigger Rpn10 phosphorylation, while the phospho-signal was completely abolished by the Rpn10-S266A mutation (Fig. 1B). We further generated an antibody specifically against phosphorylated Rpn10-S266 (Fig. 1C). After CPT treatment, phospho-S266 of endogenous Rpn10 was readily detected from whole cell lysates of various human cell lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) and from affinity-purified proteasomes (Fig. 1D). In addition, several other genotoxic agents such as IR, ultraviolet (UV) radiation and hydroxyurea (HU) all rapidly induced pS266 (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). Moreover, in xenograft tumors formed by MDA-MB-468 human breast cancer cells in nude mice, Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation could be induced by intraperitoneal injection of CPT, confirming that this modification can also occur in vivo (Fig. 1F). Indeed, in several cancer types, the pS266 level was considerably higher in the tumor cells than in adjacent normal tissues, probably due to genomic instability associated with tumorigenesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). S266 phosphorylation in tumor samples also positively correlated with the activation status of ATM/ATR (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). As predicted, small-molecule inhibitors of ATM, ATR, or DNA-PK could each reduce the level of pS266, while simultaneous inhibition of all three kinases completely suppressed S266 phosphorylation induced by CPT (Fig. 1G). Taken together, these results establish Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation as an early and general responder to DNA damage immediately downstream of ATM/ATR/DNA-PK activation.

Rpn10-S266 Phosphorylation Is Required for Optimal DNA Repair.

To study the function of Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation, we made homozygous S266N mutation in human U2OS cells using CRISPR/Cas9 based on the naturally occurring NQ motif found in mouse Rpn10 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). In two independent SN mutant clones that would be used for subsequent studies, Rpn10 phosphorylation upon DNA damage was successfully blocked, while the total abundance, assembly, and overall peptidase activity of the proteasome in these cells remained unchanged as compared to parental cells (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C). Under normal conditions, U2OS-WT and SN cells showed no discernable difference in growth and viability (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). However, the mutant cells were significantly more sensitive to CPT and IR treatment as determined by colony formation assays (Fig. 2 B and C) and showed a more pronounced G2/M arrest following DNA damage (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). Further analysis with aphidicolin-synchronized cells revealed a prolonged G2 phase and hampered mitotic entry in CPT-treated SN cells, as evidenced by delayed phospho-histone H3 (Ser10) induction, unsustained cyclin B accumulation and weakened dephosphorylation of CDK1-pY15 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). These results imply that the SN cells might need more time to repair their genome. Continued CPT treatment led to more evident PARP1 cleavage in the SN cells, indicative of increased apoptosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S2G). Neutral comet assays revealed more severe DNA damage in SN cells by CPT and IR (Fig. 2E), which also persisted for a longer time than in control cells (Fig. 2F). Therefore, phosphorylation of Rpn10-S266 is not only a marker for DNA damage but may also be required for efficient DNA repair.

We further monitored the levels of specific DDR markers/factors. CPT-induced activation of ATM was comparable in WT and SN cells, suggesting that DNA damage sensing was intact (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2G). However, several phospho-signals essential for DNA repair such as γH2AX, p-RPA2, and p-CHK1 were either dampened or more rapidly declined in the mutant cells (Fig. 3 A–C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2G). Nuclear foci formation of BRCA1 and RAD51 (key mediators of HR repair) and of ubiquitin conjugates was all weaker in SN cells as compared to control (Fig. 3 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). BRCA1, which is important for RAD51 recruitment to DNA damage sites (68), appeared to be more rapidly turned over in the mutant cells (Fig. 3A). We therefore replenished BRCA1 in the SN cells by transient overexpression and found that RAD51 foci could be effectively rescued after CPT treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Other DDR proteins, however, were less affected by the lack of S266 phosphorylation, including 53BP1, PCNA, and Ku80 (Fig. 3 A and F). Together, these findings suggest that DNA damage-induced Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation is necessary for the full capacity of DSB repair, especially via the HR pathway.

We examined the HR repair defects of SN cells in greater detail. First, the DR-GFP (69) and EJ5-GFP (70) reporter systems were employed to gauge DNA repair via the HR and NHEJ pathways, respectively (Fig. 4A). In each reporter line, we stably knocked down endogenous Rpn10 and simultaneously re-expressed either HA-Rpn10-WT or HA-Rpn10-S266N (Fig. 4B). Following I-SceI-induced DSB, DNA repair by HR was impaired in cells expressing the SN mutant, whereas the effect on NHEJ was variable (Fig. 4C). Second, we measured DNA end resection, a necessary step for HR repair, using a qPCR-based assay (71) (see SI Appendix for details). The results showed that DNA end resection was less efficient in S266N knock-in cells (Fig. 4D), consistent with attenuated HR repair described above. Third, The SN cells showed hypersensitivity to PARP inhibitors such as veliparib or olaparib (Fig. 4 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and D), which is often seen in cancer cells with BRCA1/2 mutations and defects in HR repair (72). These findings again support the notion that blocking Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation impairs DNA repair via HR.

Phosphorylation of S266 Reduces Rpn10 Binding with Ubiquitin Chains.

To understand how Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation participates in DNA repair, we first examined whether it regulates Rpn10 localization in response to DNA damage. In the above U2OS-WT or homozygous S266N cells, we knocked in a GFP-Flag tag in-frame to the C-terminus of endogenous Rpn10 using CRISPR/Cas9 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–C). Following IR treatment, Rpn10-GFP-Flag in the nucleus formed distinct foci that nicely colocalized with γH2AX signals (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). This result recapitulates previous findings (38) and indicates a direct involvement of Rpn10 and proteasome in the repair process at the DNA damage loci. A similar phenomenon was observed soon after DSBs were induced by laser microirradiation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). Notably, S266N mutation did not prevent Rpn10 recruitment to damaged DNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). Rather, Rpn10-S266N-GFP-Flag appeared at the DSB sites even sooner than WT Rpn10 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F and G).

Ser266 is located in the flexible linker region that has been proposed to modulate Rpn10 binding to ubiquitin chains by affecting the relative configurations of UIM 1 and 2 (7). We therefore examined the role of S266 phosphorylation in Rpn10–ubiquitin chain interaction using in vitro binding assays. Here, we took advantage of the genetic code expansion (GCE) technology (73) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A) and obtained Rpn10 protein genuinely and homogenously phosphorylated at S266, termed Rpn10TAG266 (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B and C). Using this phospho-protein as a reference, it was estimated that 20 to 30% of total endogenous Rpn10 in 293T and U2OS cells became phosphorylated at S266 after treatment with 1 μM CPT for 1 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). Importantly, compared to WT (nonphosphorylated) Rpn10, purified Rpn10 TAG266 protein showed much reduced binding to K48-linked ubiquitin chains, and its binding to K63-linked chains was also weakened but to a lesser extent (Fig. 5B). Phospho-S266 marginally decreased Rpn10 association with linear ubiquitin chains (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). Under our assay conditions, Rpn10 interaction with monomeric ubiquitin was too weak for detection, although the UBL domain of RAD23B bound both Rpn10 proteins equally well, regardless of S266 phosphorylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5F). These results indicate that, the ability of Rpn10 to capture ubiquitin chains, which depends on the cooperation of both UIMs, is attenuated by linker phosphorylation at S266 in a Ub linkage-dependent manner, while the function of individual UIMs is unaffected.

To further demonstrate the structural effect of Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation, we adopted a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay (74). The fluorescent protein Venus was split into two halves (VN155 and VC155, respectively), and the UIM1-linker (WT/SN)-UIM2 region of human Rpn10 (aa. 196–306) was inserted in between. At the very C-terminus of the construct was a biotinylation sequence (TBHA tag) for streptavidin pulldown (Fig. 5C). We found that the VN-WT-VC reporter became spontaneously phosphorylated at S266 when overexpressed in cells, which could be increased by CPT treatment and reduced by an ATR inhibitor. VN-SN-VC, as expected, was not phosphorylated (SI Appendix, Fig. S5G). Therefore, we started by treating cells expressing the reporter proteins with CPT, so that VN-WT-VC had a maximal level of pS266 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5H). After cell lysis, immobilized reporters were measured for fluorescence. Under this condition, VN-WT-VC (maximally phosphorylated at S266) always yielded weaker fluorescent signals than VN-SN-VC (nonphosphorylated) after normalization to their expression levels (Fig. 5D). To confirm the role of phosphorylation, immobilized reporter proteins were treated with λ-phosphatase (SI Appendix, Fig. S5H). Dephosphorylation markedly increased fluorescence from VN-WT-VC to a level similar to that from VN-SN-VC, while the latter could not be further enhanced by λ-phosphatase (Fig. 5D). These data further support our hypothesis that phosphorylation of S266 may distort the linker region and change the configuration of two flanking UIMs (Fig. 5E), leading to weakened binding of ubiquitin chains as seen earlier.

The Rpn10 Interactome Is Regulated by S266 Phosphorylation.

The above results prompted us to assess whether S266 phosphorylation also interferes with substrate binding in cells. In order to capture transient and weak interactors of Rpn10/proteasome, we performed proximity labeling and MS analysis (Fig. 6A). Among the commonly used methods, we chose TurboID due to its relatively fast reactivity (compared to BioID) and independence of H2O2 (required for the APEX system) which may cause DNA damage (75). 293T cells were generated to stably express HA-Rpn10-WT or HA-Rpn10-S266N with C-terminal TurboID, which could properly incorporate into the 26S proteasome and label nearby proteins in the presence of biotin (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–C), including all proteasome subunits and various histone proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). These observations supported the feasibility of Rpn10-TurboID for capturing proteasome-interacting proteins/substrates involved in chromatin biology. After treating these cells with or without CPT, biotinylated proteins (including potential proteasome substrates) were enriched then analyzed by MS (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E).

From five biological repeats, 3,418 and 3,420 proteins were identified from Rpn10-WT-TurboID and Rpn10-SN-TurboID samples, respectively, in at least three out of the five runs (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6F). A total of 427 proteins showed reproducible changes in Rpn10 (WT or SN) binding in response to CPT (P < 0.05, Dataset S1). We ranked these proteins according to their mean Log2FoldChange(±CPT) values. In cells expressing Rpn10-WT-TurboID, the numbers of proteins that showed increased Rpn10 binding (Log2FC > 0) and decreased binding (Log2FC < 0) were comparable, whereas a clear right-shift of the Log2FC profile was observed in the Rpn10-SN-TurboID cells, indicating that more proteins bound to or remained associated with Rpn10 in the absence of S266 phosphorylation after CPT treatment (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6G). We next determined Log2FCdiff (i.e., Log2FCSN − Log2FCWT) for each of these 427 proteins to further evaluate how S266 phosphorylation affected their interaction with Rpn10. Consistent with the results above, 80% of them (342/427) had a Log2FCdiff of positive values, indicating that their binding to nonphosphorylated Rpn10 (SN) was relatively favored over binding to S266-phosphorylated Rpn10 under DNA damage conditions (Fig. 6C). “DNA damage response,” “cell cycle,” and “chromosome organization” were among the top GO (gene ontology) terms associated with these proteins (Fig. 6D). On the contrary, only 20% of proteins (85/427) showed the opposite trend, among which only a handful of proteins were enriched in the same processes (Fig. 6C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6H). These analyses suggest that Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation upon DNA damage could indeed influence the Rpn10 interactome in cells. The MS results of several DDR proteins including BRCA1 were verified by western blot (Fig. 6E). Moreover, CPT markedly reduced the amount of K48-linked polyubiquitin labeled by Rpn10-WT-TurboID, whereas ubiquitin association with Rpn10-SN-TurboID was not affected by the same treatment (Fig. 6F). Therefore, the proximity labeling results echoed those from the in vitro binding assays (Fig. 5B), further supporting the idea that S266 phosphorylation attenuates Rpn10 recognition of ubiquitinated substrates.

Loss of Rpn10-S266 Phosphorylation Leads to Dysregulated Protein Degradation After DNA Damage.

Weakened ubiquitin binding to S266-phosphorylated Rpn10 may slow down the degradation of certain substrates upon DNA damage. To test this hypothesis, we monitored the degradation rates of two commonly used, GFP-based model substrates of the proteasome, GFPu and GFPodc (76, 77). GFPu undergoes ubiquitin-dependent degradation, which was indeed significantly slowed in WT cells in the presence of CPT (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). By contrast, degradation of GFPodc, which is ubiquitin-independent, was not sensitive to CPT (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). Neither reporter was affected by CPT in the SN cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). Given that the peptidase activity of the proteasome was essentially unaltered by either CPT or the S266N mutation (SI Appendix, Figs. S2C and S7C), these results agree well with Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation being a selective control mechanism for proteasomal recognition and degradation of ubiquitinated substrates.

We then quantified the global turnover of endogenous proteins using multiplex tandem mass tag labeling followed by mass spectrometry (TMT-MS). U2OS WT and SN cells were briefly treated with CPT and released for 0, 4, and 8 h with no proteasome inhibitors present (Fig. 7A). We focused on the 2,456 nuclear proteins that could be quantified in all samples (Dataset S2) and analyzed their abundances at 4 and 8 h after CPT washout with regard to their levels at the beginning of recovery (“CPT+0”). Although these proteins exhibited diverse changes during this time course, as a whole they were slightly but significantly more accumulated in WT cells than in SN cells at both 4- and 8-h time points as compared to CPT+0 (Fig. 7B). Using Log2FCWT − Log2FCSN (of TMT-MS intensities) as a gauge for the accumulation difference of each protein, we found hundreds of nuclear proteins that were more stable (or less degraded) in WT cells than in SN cells after CPT treatment, greatly outnumbering those proteins showing the opposite trend of change in WT and SN cells (203 vs. 53 at 4-h and 314 vs. 61 at 8-h post-CPT, Fig. 7C). Western blot analysis confirmed that BRCA1 was one of the proteins that were fairly stable in WT cells but were quickly lost in SN cells during the recovery from CPT (Fig. 7 D and E). Ectopic expression of BRCA1 restored HR repair efficiency in the DR-GFP reporter cells expressing Rpn10-S266N (Fig. 7F), further indicating that premature degradation of particular repair proteins such as BRCA1 was a major cause of DDR impairment. Several other DNA repair proteins identified from the MS results (ARMT1, ABRX1 and NABP2) could be marked with K48-linked polyubiquitin chains (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 D and E) and probably targeted for proteasomal degradation as well, and they were also less accumulated in SN cells (Fig. 7D). Taken together, these data demonstrate the physiologically relevant function of Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation in temporarily limiting substrate access to the proteasome, which is necessary for optimal DNA repair in human cells (Fig. 7G).

Discussion

Ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation is undoubtedly important for DNA repair, which requires proteasome function be finely regulated at the same time. Our functional characterization of Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation has revealed a common mechanism by which the 26S proteasome responds to DNA damage and adjusts the spectrum of its substrates, affecting the fates of DDR proteins and cell survival. Specifically, if Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation is abrogated after DNA damage, multiple key factors of HR repair are more likely to be captured by the proteasome and degraded, leading to subdued DNA repair. Therefore, Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation can be likened to a built-in safety of the proteasome system. Since it is inducible, reversible and occurs only on a fraction of Rpn10 molecules, it does not defy the global action of proteasomal degradation but adds an important layer of regulation to fine-tune the process.

Our findings have provided the first evidence that substrate recognition by the proteasome can be modulated by phosphorylation of a ubiquitin receptor subunit under physiologically relevant conditions. That said, previous studies have reported mono- and poly-ubiquitination of Rpn10 and another ubiquitin receptor, Rpn13, that also reduce substrate binding to the proteasome (78–81). Rpn10 ubiquitination was shown to occur at a distance from the UIM(s) in both human and yeast cells, therefore affecting substrate association indirectly (79–83). By contrast, Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation is located at the linker between UIM1 and UIM2, likely to yield a direct structural impact on ubiquitin chain binding. Exactly how phosphor-S266 influences UIM function in cells is currently unknown, since it may also serve as a docking site for additional proteins that participate in controlling substrate recognition. One of such proteins would be the phosphatase that dephosphorylates pS266, the identity of which remains to be determined. In addition, ubiquitination of Rpn10/Rpn13 often resulted from severe loss of proteasome activity (79–83), whereas Rpn10-S266 phosphorylation does not depend on or cause proteasomal inhibition in general. Nevertheless, a theme can be generalized from these studies that posttranslational modifications of ubiquitin receptors represent a conserved and flexible mode of proteasome regulation important for cell survival under various stress conditions.

Although we focused on DDR proteins that escaped from premature proteasome degradation, we did notice that certain proteins seemed to be better recognized by WT Rpn10-TurboID in response to DNA damage (Fig. 6 B and C). The underlying reason can be many-fold. Some proteins may carry ubiquitin chains with K6, K11, K27, and K33 linkages, all of which have been implicated in DNA damage, and at least some of them can mediate proteasomal degradation (29, 84–90). Branched ubiquitin chains (91) as well as cross talk between ubiquitination and other modifications such phosphorylation and SUMOylation (38, 50, 92) have further complicated the picture. Whether their recognition by Rpn10 is regulated by S266 phosphorylation is currently unknown. Moreover, substrates can access the proteasome independently from Rpn10 (4, 5, 93) but become biotinylated by Rpn10-TurboID in the vicinity. Conversely, close contact with Rpn10 does not necessarily lead to proteasomal degradation. For example, many histone variants were constantly captured by Rpn10-TurboID (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). Among them, H2B and H3 were more rapidly turned over in WT cells than in SN cells after CPT treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 F and G), which may facilitate subsequent repair processes (94, 95). However, histone H2AX appeared stable in both WT and SN cells following DNA damage (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S7G), and the same was true for several H1, H2A, and H4 isoforms (TMT results, Dataset S2). Therefore, caution should be taken when interpreting the TurboID data of individual proteins. Nonetheless, our results do support an overarching concept that, in the UPS, substrate selectivity is not only determined by E3 ubiquitin ligases but can also be achieved at the proteasome level. This may be particularly true for those DDR proteins investigated in the current study, which are modified by K48-linked ubiquitin chains and recognized by Rpn10. Given the ever-increasing enthusiasm in harnessing targeted protein degradation for clinical use (96), a better understanding of proteasome “receptiveness” would be of great biological and practical values.

Materials and Methods

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 9.0. Unless otherwise noted, all experiments have been repeated at least three times. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD, and statistical methods used are described in figure legends. GO term analysis was done by STRING (https://string-db.org/).
