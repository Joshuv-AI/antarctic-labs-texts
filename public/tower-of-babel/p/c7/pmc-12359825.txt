Reproducibility and validity of adapted clinical tests for the assessment of muscle strength in community-dwelling older adults living with Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Marcos Paulo Braz de Oliveira, Letícia Bojikian Calixtre, Natália Oiring de Castro Cezar, Carla Lima-Truax, Brendon David Truax, Anielle Cristhine de Medeiros Takahashi, Tatiana de Oliveira Sato, Paulo Henrique Silva Pelicioni, Paula Regina Mendes da Silva Serrão, Larissa Pires de Andrade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adults living with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) have impaired executive functions and can therefore have greater difficulty following the instructions of assessment tests. Therefore, the objective of this study was to determine the intra- and inter-rater reliability and criterion validity of the adapted Five-Times Sit-to-Stand (5STS), 30-Second Sit-to-Stand (30STS) and Calf-Rise Senior (CRS) tests for the assessment of lower limb muscle strength in older adults living with AD.MethodsA methodological study was conducted. For the adaptation of the 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests, standardized verbal commands were added during the execution to assist the older adults in beginning and ending the tests (5STS and 30STS: “stand up” and “sit down”; CRS: “stand on your tip toes” and “now you can get down”). Reliability was determined using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC3,1), standard error of measurement (SEM) and minimal detectable change (MDC95). Criterion validity of the adapted tests was tested by the correlation with isokinetic measures of the knee and ankle using Spearman’s correlation.ResultsForty-two older adults living with AD were included (Mild-AD: 22; Moderate-AD: 20). Intra- and inter-rater reliability were moderate (ICC3,1: 5STS: 0.60, 0.50; 30STS: 0.55; CRS: 0.68, 0.65), with the exception of inter-rater reliability of the 30STS test which was low (ICC3,1: 0.32). Intra-rater SEM and MDC95 (SEM: 5STS: 3.59, 8.33; 30STS: 0.85, 1.97; CRS: 1.37, 3.18) were lower compared to inter-rater values (MDC95: 5STS: 4.02, 9.32; 30STS: 1.42, 3.29; CRS: 1.76, 4.08). The adapted tests correlated significantly with peak torque, average peak torque and total work of the knee (5STS: extensors and flexors [60°/s] [r: -0.33 to -0.46]; 30STS: flexors [180°/s] [r: 0.31 to 0.61]) and ankle (CRS: plantar flexors and dorsiflexors [total work] [30°/s] [r: 0.32 to 0.37]).ConclusionThe adapted 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests demonstrated moderate reliability and correlated with maximum lower limb muscle strength and endurance in older adults living with AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06269-x.

Introduction

Aging leads to a progressive reduction in muscle strength and endurance [1, 2]. The reduction in lower limb muscle strength is more evident in older adults living with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [3]. The identification of changes in muscle strength requires the precise assessment of physical function. Older adults living with AD have impaired executive functions [4], such as understanding, planning and executing specific tasks, and can therefore have greater difficulty following the instructions of assessment tests. These aspects can interfere with the assessment, performance and interpretation of changes in muscle strength.

AD has three stages: mild, moderate and severe [5]. No studies in the literature have investigated whether impaired executive functions differ among the stages of the disease, especially between the mild and moderate stages, which could determine whether there are differences in following the instructions of tests between the stages. However, as AD is a progressive, irreversible, neurodegenerative condition [5], the impairment of cognitive functions increases with the progression of the disease, as older adults in the severe stage are unable to follow simple instructions.

The reliability of lower limb muscle strength assessment test, such as the Five-Times Sit-to-Stand (5STS), and muscle endurance, such as the 30-Second Sit-to-Stand (30STS) test, has been investigated in older adults living with dementia [6–11]. These studies did not identify the etiology of dementia and there are different clinical characteristics (cognitive and physical) among the types of dementia that can affect reliability [12, 13]. The studies also used the original versions of test that were developed for healthy older adults who do not have the same limitations as those living with AD. The Calf-Rise Senior (CRS) test was developed for the assessment of ankle muscle strength in healthy older adults and is considered reliable and valid in this population [14, 15], but has not been tested on older adults living with AD.

Some reliability studies involving the 5STS and 30STS tests in older adults living with dementia modified the original version of the tests, enabling the participants to use their hands to stand up from the chair [7, 8, 11]. This modification is important to overcoming the physical limitations of the participants, but does not consider the difficulties in following the instructions of tests. Therefore, the reproduction of these tests by clinicians and researchers can be challenging, given the lack of standardization in the form of application.

Despite the lack of previous studies that have specifically investigated whether older adults living with AD have difficulty following the instructions of tests and have only demonstrated the impairment of executive functions, Melo and colleagues (2019) considered these difficulties and adapted the Timed Up and Go test for the assessment of mobility in this population [16]. Based on these researchers, we proposed a similar adaptation of the 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests for older adults living with AD, which consisted of adding standardized verbal commands during the execution to assist these older adults in beginning and ending the tests. The commands for the 5STS and 30STS were “stand up” and “sit down”; “stand on tip toes” and “now you can get down” on the CRS. However, a systematic review identified a lack of standardization in verbal commands during the execution of physical assessment tests in older adults living with dementia [17].

Therefore, the objective of the present study was to determine the intra- and inter-rater reliability and criterion validity of the adapted 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests for the assessment of lower limb muscle strength in older adults living with AD. The stages of AD were considered in the reliability analyses. Criterion validity was tested by correlations between the adapted tests and isokinetic measures of muscle strength of the knee and ankle in these older adults. It was hypothesized that the adapted 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests would be correlated with lower limb muscle strength and endurance in this population.

Methods

Design

A methodological study was conducted at the Multidisciplinary Research in Older Adults’ Health Laboratory and Isokinetic Dynamometry Laboratory of Federal University of São Carlos (UFSCar), São Carlos, São Paulo, Brazil. This study received approval from the UFSCar Research Ethics Committee for Human Beings (certificate number: 88921118.4.0000.5504). Verbal and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The caregivers or legally authorized representatives gave informed consent in name of their care recipients and verbal informed consent was obtained from the older adults living with AD on the day of the evaluations. Moreover, the caregiver or legally authorized representatives of illiterate participants provided informed consent for the study. Data were collected between February and December 2019. The study was conducted following the ethical precepts laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki and the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) were followed was followed [18, 19].

Participants

The following were the eligibility criteria: i) community-dwelling older adults living with AD, ii) ≥ 65 years, iii) both sexes and iv) no musculoskeletal disorders in the knee or ankle (e.g., fractures, pain, osteoporosis, previous surgeries, etc.) determined through inspection by the researchers and the reports of the caregivers. The participants were recruited from the UFSCar Health School Unit, University Open to Older Adults and Family Health Programs in the city of São Carlos. The inclusion criteria were i) a medical diagnosis of AD performed by a geriatrician and/or neurologist based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-V) [20], ii) mild (CDR 1) or moderate (CDR 2) stage of AD based on the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale [21, 22], iii) with a ≤ 78 score on the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-revised (ACE-R) [23, 24], and iv) being clinically stable. The exclusion criteria were i) comprehension difficulties (e.g., inability to say one’s own name, give/receive an object when requested, etc.), ii) dementia of other etiologies (e.g., Lewy bodies, vascular, frontotemporal, etc.), iii) other neurodegenerative diseases (Parkinson’s disease) or non-neurodegenerative diseases (stroke) and iv) diagnosis of depression.

Sample calculation

For reliability, the sample calculation was performed a priori based on the method proposed by Walter, Eliasziw and Donner (1998) [25]. A 5% significance level, acceptable correlation coefficient of 0.3 and an expected correlation coefficient of 0.7 with two repeated measures were considered to obtain the sample size. The sample size for an 80% power was 22 individuals. For criterion validity, the sample calculation was performed a posteriori with the aid of the G*Power 3.0.1.0 power based on the correlations obtained in the study. A 5% significant level, two-tailed tests and an 80% power were considered for this calculation. Considering the obtained correlation coefficients, 18 individuals (highest correlation [r: 0.61]), 29 individuals (moderate correlation [r: 0.50]) and 269 individuals (lowest correlation [r: 0.17]) would be needed.

Adapted 5STS, 30STS and CRS

On the 5STS test, the participant is instructed to stand up from a chair and sit down five times as quickly as possible [26, 27]. On the 30STS test, the participant is instructed to stand up from a chair and sit down as quickly as possible for 30 s [28]. On the CRS test, the participant is instructed to perform the movement of raising and lowering the heels bilaterally as quickly as possible for 30 s [14, 15]. No verbal commands or encouragement are given during the execution of the original version of the tests– the individual only received prior instructions. In the present study, verbal commands were added with the aim of assisting in the execution of the tests. Three verbal commands were added at three different times on each test: i) 5STS: the command “stand up” was given to begin the task and “sit down” was given to end the task on the first, third and fifth repetition, ii) 30STS: the command “stand up” was given to begin the task and “sit down” was given to end the task at the 1 st, 10th and 20th seconds iii) CRS: the command “stand on your tip toes” was given to begin the movement and “now you can get down” was given to end the movement at the 1 st, 10th and 20th seconds. All adapted tests were initiated with the verbal command “Ready, set, go!”, followed by the specific verbal command of each test. During the tests, the verbal commands were given according to the execution speed of the participants. The following steps were obeyed during the test sessions: 1) explanation, 2) demonstration, 3) verbal confirmation that the participant understood the test, 4) one trial for familiarization and 5) execution for analysis (Supplementary Material 1, which presents a detailed description of the sessions of the adapted tests). Isokinetic measures

The concentric isokinetic measures were performed using the Biodex Multi-Joint System 3 isokinetic dynamometer (Biodex Medical Inc., Shirley, NY, USA). Assessments were performed of the knee (extensors and flexors; 60º/s: five maximum repetitions; 180°/s: 15 maximum repetitions) and ankle (plantar flexors and dorsiflexors; 30º/s: five maximum repetitions) on the dominant lower limb. The assessment procedures followed the specifications of the manufacture [29]. The isokinetic measures used in the analyses were peak torque, average peak torque and total work, with the highest values corresponding to the best performance (Supplementary Material 2, which presents a detailed description of the isokinetic assessments). Isokinetic measures are reliable for the assessment of knee and ankle muscle strength in older adults living with AD [30].

Characterization measures

Demographic and clinical characteristics of older adults living with AD were collected from the participants and through interviews with the caregivers. The demographic characteristics were age, sex, body mass, height, body mass index and dominant lower limb. The clinical characteristics were grip strength (assessed using a Jamar® handgrip dynamometer; three maximum repetitions of the dominant hand held for six seconds– the highest value corresponds to the best performance) [31]; cognitive function (assessed using the ACE-R, which has five cognitive domains: attention/orientation, memory, fluency, language and visuospatial function, and a total score ranging from 0 to 100– higher scores indicate higher levels of cognitive function) [23, 24]; depressive symptoms (assessed using the Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia, which has five domains, 19 items and total score ranging from 0 to 38: score > 10 corresponds to greater depressive symptoms– version applied to the older adult and caregiver with regards to the older adult) [32, 33]; physical activity level (assessed using the Modified Baecke Questionnaire, which has three domains and three items– the final score corresponds to the physical activity level [low: ≤ 9.11; moderate: 9.12–16.17; high: ≥ 16.18]– version applied to the caregiver with regards to the older adult) [34, 35]; impairment in activities of daily living (ADLs) (assessed using the Activities of Daily Living Questionnaire, which has six domains, 17 items and a total score ranging from 0 to 100 calculated by the following formula: sum of all ratings/3 × total number of items rated × 100– the final score corresponds to the degree of impairment on ADLs [none to mild: 0–33; moderate: 34–66; severe: 66–100]– version applied to the caregiver with regards to the older adult) [36, 37]; and classification of AD stage (assessed using the CDR, which has six domains and quantifies dementia severity from very mild [CDR 0.5] to mild [CDR 1], moderate [CDR 2] or severe [CDR 3]) [21, 22]. The classification of AD stage using the CDR was performed by the researchers, who have ample experienced proven in previous studies [30, 38–40].

Procedures

Assessments were performed on four different days. Day 1 involved the confirmation of the diagnosis, classification of AD stage and measures of characterization of the participants. Assessments for reliability and criterion validity occurred on Days 2, 3 and 4 by two raters (Rater 1 and Rater 2). Day 2 involved the application of the adapted tests (Rater 1) and the isokinetic assessment (Rater 1). The adapted tests were also applied on Day 3 (Rater 1) and Day 4 (Rater 2). Intra-rater reliability was determined based on the assessments conducted by Rater 1 (Day 2 and Day 3) and inter-rater reliability was determined by the assessments of Rater 1 (Day 3) and Rater 2 (Day 4). Criterion validity was determined based on the assessments of Rater 1 (Day 2). The interval between assessments was one to seven days. Rater 2 had no access to the results of the assessments of Rater 1. Rater 1 was blinded to the results obtained on the isokinetic dynamometer. The raters underwent four hours of previous training for the application of the adapted 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests with the aim of standardizing the assessments. The raters were physiotherapists with four years of experience in physical and cognitive assessments and the treatment of older adults living with AD. Rater 1 had three years of experience with isokinetic assessments of older adults. The test sessions were similar among the assessments: same instructions, communication, setting, period of the day (morning or afternoon) and support from the laboratory staff.

Statistical analysis

Normality and homoscedasticity of the data were analyzed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. The characteristics of the participants were compared using the Student’s t-test (variables with normal distribution) (α = 0.05), Kruskal–Wallis or Mann–Whitney test with the Bonferroni correction (variables with non-normal distribution) (αadj = 0.016) and the chi-squared test (categorical variables) (αadj = 0.016). Comparisons were made between the Mild-AD and Moderate-AD groups.

Reliability was determined using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC3,1), with a single-measure two-way mixed model and 95% confidence interval. Interpretation was based on the classification proposed by Munro (very low: 0.00–0.25; low: 0.26–0.49; moderate: 0.50–0.69; high: 0.70–0.89; very high: 0.90–1.00) [41, 42]. The standard error of measurement (SEM) and minimal detectable change with a 95% confidence interval (MDC95) were expressed in absolute and relative values. Absolute values were calculated using the following formulas: SEM: SD√1-ICC3,1 and MDC95: SEM × √2 × 1.96. Absolute values were used to interpret the reliability for all participants [43]. Relative values– percentage of error (SEM%) and percentage of change (MDC95%)– were calculated using the following formulas: SEM%: SEM/mean of measures × 100% and MDC95%: MDC95/mean of measures × 100%.43 Relative values were used to compare the Mild-AD and Moderate-AD groups, with SEM% ≤ 10% and MDC% ≤ 30% considered acceptable [44].

Criterion validity was determined by correlations between the adapted tests and isokinetic measures of the knee (adapted 5STS with angular velocity of 60°/s and adapted 30STS with angular velocity of 180°/s) and ankle (adapted CRS with angular velocity of 30°/s). Spearman’s correlation (nonparametric) were used for the criterion validity analyses (α < 0.05) and interpreted based on the classification proposed by Portney and Watkins (poor: 0.00 to ± 0.25; fair: ± 0.25 to ± 0.50; moderate: ± 0.50 to ± 0.75; strong: ± 0.75 to ± 1.00) [42, 45]. Data for the reliability and criterion validity analyses were continuous. The data were organized and analyzed using SPSS Statistics (Software version: 25.0; IBM; Chicago, IL, USA) and Excel 2010 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA).

Results

Forty-two older adults living with AD were included (Mild-AD: 22; Moderate-AD: 20). All participants completed the assessment protocol with the exception of two who were unable to perform the 5STS and 30STS tests without assistance and were excluded from the analysis. No harmful or undesirable effects occurred during or after the assessments. According to the reports of the caregivers, the participants remained stable between test sessions (no hospitalizations, abrupt change in routine or any stressful event affecting the participant). Reliability

The data met the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity allowing for reliability analysis using the ICC3,1.

5STS

Intra- and inter-rater reliability among all participants on the adapted 5STS test was moderate (ICC3,1: 0.60 and 0.50). The same result was found considering the stages of AD, with the exception of inter-rater reliability in the Mild-AD group which was low (ICC3,1: 0.46). Intra-rater SEM and MDC95 among all participants were lower (3.59 and 8.33) compared to inter-rater values (4.02 and 9.32). Considering the stages of the disease, the Mild-AD group had lower intra-rater SEM% and MDC95% (10.87% and 24.72%) and the Moderate-AD had lower inter-rater SEM% and MDC95% (11.41% and 26.47%). 30STS

Intra-rater reliability among all participants on the adapted 30STS test was moderate (ICC3,1: 0.55) and inter-rater reliability among all participants was low (ICC3,1: 0.32). Differences were found considering the stages of the disease. Intra-rater reliability was high in the Mild-AD group (ICC3,1: 0.72) and low in the Moderate-AD group (ICC3,1: 0.34); inter-rater reliability was low in the Mild-AD group (ICC3,1: 0.41) and very low in the Moderate-AD group (ICC3,1: 0.16). Intra-rater SEM and MDC95 among all participants were lower (0.85 and 1.97) compared to inter-rater values (1.42 and 3.29). Considering the stage of the disease, the Mild-AD group had lower intra-rater SEM% and MDC95% (11.13% and 25.64%) and the Moderate-AD group had lower inter-rater SEM% and MDC95% (18.16% and 42.10%).

CRS

Intra- and inter-rater reliability among all participants on the adapted CRS test were moderate (ICC3,1: 0.68 and 0.65). The same result was found for intra- and inter-rater reliability in the Mild-AD group (ICC3,1: 0.52 and 0.50). In contrast, intra- and inter-rater reliability were high in the Moderate-AD group (ICCs3,1: 0.81 and 0.81). Intra-rater SEM and MDC95 among all participants were lower (1.37 and 3.18) compared to inter-rater values (1.76 and 4.08). Considering the stages of the disease, the Moderate-AD group had lower intra- and inter-rater SEM% (11.37% and 10.96%) and MDC95% (23.34% and 25.39%) values.

5STS

30STS

The adapted 30ST test presented significant (positive) correlations with isokinetic muscle strength of knee flexors at 180°/s considering all participants. The correlations of this adapted test were moderate with total work (r: 0.61) and fair with peak torque and average peak torque (r: 0.31 to 0.34).

CRS

The adapted CRS test presented significant (positive) correlations with isokinetic muscle strength of ankle plantar flexors at 30°/s considering all participants. Only total work of dorsiflexors at 30°/s presented significant (positive) correlations with this adapted test. All correlations were fair (r: 0.32 to 0.37).

Discussion

This is the first methodological study to determine the reliability of the adapted 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests in older adults living with AD and correlate these tests with the isokinetic dynamometer, which is considered the gold standard for the assessment of muscle strength [46, 47]. Intra-rater reliability was moderate and inter-rater reliability ranged from moderate to low. Significant correlations were found between these adapted tests and isokinetic muscle strength of the knee and ankle. The 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests were adapted by adding simple, easily understood verbal commands during the execution of the tests to assist the older adults in beginning and ending the tasks.

The stages of AD and raters interfered with the reliability of the adapted 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests. Older adults living with AD can present mental confusion, spatial disorientation and comprehension difficulties in the mild and moderate stages of the disease [5, 48]. Therefore, the two stages of AD can negatively affect reliability. Moreover, the change in the rater on Day 4 may have confused the participants, resulting in different performances between test sessions.

The diagnosis of dementia is another important aspect to consider, as it poses a challenge for researchers and clinicians [49]. Part of previous reliability studies in the literature failed to identify the type of dementia or used different criteria for the diagnosis and classification of the stage of dementia. Therefore, the reliability of the tests adapted in the present study should be compared with caution to previous studies involving the original versions of the tests, as such methodological differences can affect reliability.

Intra-rater reliability among all participants of the adapted 5STS and 30STS tests was moderate in older adults living with AD. Previous reliability studies involving older adults living with dementia identified high to very high reliability of the original version of these tests [6–11]. Such differences in reliability may be partially explained by the fact that physical test reliability studies that do not identify the etiology dementia tend to find greater reliability compared to studies that identify AD as the cause of dementia [17]. No previous studies were found that determined intra-rater reliability of the original version of the 5STS and 30STS tests in older adults living with AD.

A previous reliability study also identified moderate reliability of the original version of the 5STS test in older adults living with dementia [10], which is similar to the result of the present study. However, the study used a different type of reliability analysis; the weighted Kappa was used rather than the ICC, which was used in the present investigation. This type of analysis was employed because the study did not use time in seconds on the 5STS test for the analyses; the categorized classification of the Short Physical Performance Battery scale was used.

Inter-rater reliability among all participants of the adapted 30STS test was low in the present study. In contrast, a previous study found very high inter-rater reliability of the original version of the test in older adults living with dementia [9]. However, the study did not identify the etiology of dementia. Moreover, the test was applied a single time and both raters recorded the time simultaneously, which may explain the difference in reliability. No previous studies were found that determined inter-rater reliability of the 30STS and 5STS tests in older adults living with AD.

Considering the stages of the disease, intra-rater reliability of the adapted 5STS and 30STS tests was greater in the Mild-AD group. Older adults living with AD in the moderate stage may feel daunted by challenging tasks and confused in the presence of different people compared to older adults in the mild stage [5], which may partially explain the difference in the performance of the test between sessions in the Moderate-AD group, resulting in less reliability.

Intra-rater SEM and MDC95 among all participants of the adapted 5STS and 30STS tests were lower compared to inter-rater values (Table 2). Therefore, the raters affected the results. Only one previous study determined the SEM and MDC95 of the original version of the 5STS test in older adults living with dementia [10]. However, it was not possible to compare the results of this study and those of the present investigation, as different scores were attributed to the 5STS test.

Previous studies determined the SEM and MDC95 of the original version of the 30STS test in older adults living with dementia [7, 9]. In one study, SEM was 1.26 and MDC95 was 3.49 [7]. These values are larger compared to those found in the present investigation (SEM: 0.85 and MDC95: 1.97 [intra-rater] and SEM: 1.42 and MDC95: 3.29 [inter-rater]). Therefore, we suggest that the adapted 30STS test presented more satisfactory SEM and MDC95 compared to the original version for older adults living with AD or dementia. In another study, SEM and MDC95 were 0 due to the assessment procedures adopted [9], which impedes the comparison of the results.

Considering the stages of the disease, intra- and inter-rater SEM% of the adapted 5STS and 30STS tests were above the cutoff point in the Mild-AD and Moderate-AD groups, intra-rater MDC95% was acceptable in the Mild-AD group and inter-rater MDC95% on the 5STS was acceptable in the Moderate-AD group (Table 3). In general, intra-rater reliability in the Mild-AD group presented lower MDC% values. Therefore, we may suggest that more advanced stages of AD and the raters seem to interfere negatively with the reliability of the adapted 5STS and 30STS tests.

Intra- and inter-rater reliability among all participants of the adapted CRS test was moderate and no difference was found in intra- and inter-rater ICCs. The reliability of the original version of the CRS test in healthy older adults was high [14, 15]. Considering the stage of the disease, the reliability of the adapted CRS test was also high in the Moderate-AD group. This is an important result that demonstrates that, besides being reproducible in older adults living with AD, the adapted CRS test presented similar reliability to that found with healthy older adults.

Intra-rater SEM and MDC95 among all participants of the adapted CRS test were lower compared to inter-rater values, with a difference of 0.39 s (SEM) and 0.90 s (MDC95). The study of the original version of the CRS test in healthy older adults did not report SEM and MDC95 values [14, 15], which impedes the comparison of the results. Considering the stage of the disease, intra- and inter-rater SEM% were above the cutoff point in the Mild-AD and Moderate-AD groups and intra- and inter-rater MDC95% was acceptable in the Moderate-AD group (Table 3). We may therefore suggest that more advanced stages of AD and the raters seem not to interfere negatively with the reliability of the adapted CRS test. No previous reliability studies were found on the CRS test in older adults living with AD.

Regarding criterion validity, significant correlations were found between the adapted 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests and peak torque (maximum muscle strength), average peak torque (average maximum muscle strength) and total work (capacity of the muscle group to maintain maximum muscle strength) of the knee at 60°/s and 180°/s and the ankle at 30°/s [50]. Isokinetic tests performed at low velocities (30°/s and 60°/s) determine maximum muscle strength and at high velocities (180°/s) determine maximum muscle endurance [51]. No previous studies were found that analyzed the criterion validity of lower limb muscle strength assessment tests in older adults living with AD.

The adapted 5STS test was significantly correlated with the isokinetic measures of the knee extensors and flexors at 60°/s (Table 3). The 5STS test is considered an indicator of muscle strength [52], which partially explains these results. Although the task of standing up from a chair and sitting down involves the knee extensors [53, 54], the flexors are also required for the movement, which explains the correlation found between the adapted 5STS test and the flexors.

The adapted 30STS test was significantly correlated with the isokinetic measures of the knee flexors at 180°/s (Table 3). The 30STS test is considered an indicator of muscle endurance [52]. This explains the lack of correlation with part of the isokinetic measures at 180°/s, which are more associated with muscle endurance. However, the 30STS test presented a moderate correlation (Table 3) with total work at 180°/s, which is the capacity of the muscle group to maintain maximum muscle strength [50]. No previous studies were found that correlated the original version of the 5STS and 30STS test with isokinetic measures in healthy older adults, which impedes the comparison of the results. However, it is important to highlight that Piche et al., 2021 identified poor to fair correlations (Spearman’s correlation) between the STS (a single chair rise) and isokinetic measures (e.g., force–velocity and power-velocity parameters: maximum power, optimal force, optimal velocity, and theoretical maximal velocity) in healthy older adults. The r-values in that study were relatively lower than those found in our study [55].

The adapted CRS test was significantly correlated with the isokinetic measures of ankle plantar flexors (Table 3). The CRS test was developed for the assessment of plantar flexor muscle strength [14, 15], which explains these findings. The study of the original version of the CRS test in healthy older adults identified strong correlations with peak torque and the strength development rate analyzing plantar flexors and dorsiflexors together [14, 15]. The adapted CRS test was also significantly correlated with total work of dorsiflexors (Table 3). However, the plantar flexors and dorsiflexors were analyzed separately in the present study.

The largest portion of the significant correlations found in the present study were fair (Table 3). This result may partially be explained by differences in the execution of the adapted tests and the isokinetic dynamometer. Lower limb muscle strength is performed bilaterally on the 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests with the closed kinetic chain and involves other components of physical functioning, such as postural balance [56, 57]. In contrast, lower limb muscle strength is performed unilaterally on the isokinetic dynamometer with the open kinetic chain and does not involve other components of physical functioning, given that the individual is in the sitting position.

Strengths and limitations

A strong point of this study was addressing the performance of older adults living with AD on tests that measure limitations that can compromise physical performance over the years. The description of the tests was detailed, enabling the replication of the results. Moreover, methodological care was taken to confirm the etiology of dementia, the diagnosis and the classification of the stage of AD.

However, this study also has some limitations to the generalization of the results. No restriction was imposed regarding the physical capacity of the participants, which may have interfered with the performance on the adapted tests between test sessions. The seven-day interval between sessions may have enabled greater instability of the participants between assessments. It was not possible to blind the Rater 1 and Rater 2 with regards to the stages of AD; due to the small number of researchers involved in the data collection process, the raters were in charge of applying the CDR.

Conclusion

In general, the adapted 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests demonstrated moderate reliability and were correlated with maximum lower limb muscle strength and endurance in older adults living with AD. The stages of AD and the raters seem to affect the reliability of the adapted tests. Verbal commands during the execution of the tests may be a viable option for attenuating difficulties in understanding the instructions of the tests. Therefore, we recommend that clinicians and researchers use the adapted 5STS, 30STS and CRS tests as complementary tests for the assessment of lower limb muscle strength in this population, preferably without changing the rater throughout the sessions to ensure the reliability of the measures. Standard error of measurement and MDC values should be considered in the interpretation of future clinical and scientific findings.
