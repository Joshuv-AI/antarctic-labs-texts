A Highly Powered Large Outcomes Randomised Controlled Trial Will Usually, but Not Always, Have Balanced Groups at Baseline
Authors: Hertzel C. Gerstein, Zayeeda Labiba, Sibylle Hess, Kamel Mohammedi, Yueci Zhou, Shun Fu Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe importance of randomised trials for discerning an intervention's effect rests on randomisation's ability to create groups with (on average) equal hazards of future outcomes. The frequency with which this does not occur in well‐designed large trials with a high event rate was studied.Research Design and MethodsData from 8215 participants in the Outcomes Reduction with an Initial Glargine Intervention (ORIGIN) trial's biomarker study were used to simulate multiple trials. Participants were repeatedly randomised to two groups. After each randomisation, the hazard of death in people assigned to one versus the other was calculated. A set of 1000 randomisations was repeated 1000 times, and the number of pairs for which the hazard ratio was < 0.85 or > 1.18 was tabulated within each set of 1000. Whether such imbalances in hazard could be detected or mitigated by adjusting for baseline covariates was explored using adjusted Cox models.ResultsIn a median of 3 (IQR 2, 4) of 1000 randomisations (0.3%) the hazard ratio for death of assignment to one group versus the other was < 0.85 or > 1.18 by chance alone. This imbalance was not apparent from baseline data and could not be fully mitigated by adjustment for baseline independent predictors of death using data from four randomisations that yielded imbalanced hazards.ConclusionOne out of every 300–400 large, two‐arm outcomes trials have imbalances in the baseline hazard of the primary outcome that are large enough to misrepresent the true effect of the intervention.

Introduction

In a randomised trial, the purpose of randomisation is to divide the initial study group of individuals into two or more study groups that are, on average, identical to each other and to the overall group [1, 2]. Assuming this has successfully been achieved, members of each of the randomised groups are expected to have the same incidence of outcomes during follow‐up, with any differences in incidence or prognosis being minor and attributable to random variation alone. When some intervention is randomly assigned to one of the groups, any differences in the prognosis of that group compared with the others can therefore be reliably attributed to the effects of that intervention [3].

These principles are widely accepted, and randomised trials have identified hundreds of disease‐reducing and life‐saving therapies over the last 80 years [4]. This foundation of evidence‐based medicine assumes that the randomisation process creates equal groups with respect to future prognosis [1]. It is well known that this assumption depends on both the number of participants who are randomised (i.e., the sample size) and the frequency of the outcome being studied (i.e., the event rate) [5]. The larger the trial and the higher the frequency of the outcome, the greater the probability that the trial will detect a true effect of the intervention (i.e., the greater the power). As most randomised controlled trials of novel therapies target outcomes with annual event rates between 2% and 5%, they typically include thousands of participants who are followed for 1 or more years to ensure a 90% probability (or power) of detecting a true effect of the intervention.

It is well known that chance alone can theoretically undermine these assumptions, and the most carefully executed randomisation process could create randomised groups with different prognoses. Such an occurrence may not be detectable, and the frequency with which this happens in large, long‐term trials has not been well studied. We therefore used real data to simulate 1 million randomisations of a completed outcomes trial to estimate the probability of creating randomised groups with different prognoses. We then scrutinised four simulations which yielded imbalanced group prognoses to determine whether such an imbalance could be detected by carefully scrutinising 254 baseline characteristics. We did this by analysing data from the biomarker substudy of the Outcomes Reduction with an Initial Glargine Intervention (ORIGIN) trial, which included baseline measurements of clinical variables plus 238 different biomarkers.

Methods

The design, baseline characteristics and findings of the ORIGIN trial and its biomarker substudy have been published [6, 7]. It recruited and randomly assigned 12 537 people (mean age 64 years, 35% women) with mild dysglycaemia (mean HbA1c 6.4%) and additional cardiovascular risk factors to either insulin glargine‐mediated normoglycaemia versus standard care and omega‐3 fatty acids versus placebo using a factorial design. During a median follow‐up of 6.2 years both interventions had a neutral effect on cardiovascular outcomes as well as total mortality. A serum sample was collected, stored and analysed for 238 biomarkers in a subset of 8215 individuals. All ORIGIN participants provided written informed consent with additional consent provided by the subset who provided blood for biomarkers.

Study Sample and Outcome

All analyses reported herein were restricted to 8215 people for whom baseline concentrations of 238 biomarkers were available. The only outcome that was analysed herein was total mortality defined as death from any cause.

Biomarkers

All analyses were restricted to the 8215 people for whom baseline concentrations of 238 biomarkers were available. Blood samples for biomarkers were drawn and stored centrally at the Population Health Research Institute Biobank (in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada) at −160°C in nitrogen vapour‐cooled tanks. Aliquots of blood were sent to and analysed by Myriad RBM (Austin, TX) for multiplex biomarker analyses on the LUMINEX platforms without any information related to participant characteristics [8]. High sensitivity Troponin I was measured with the Architect Stat Troponin I assay on the Abbott Architect System [7]. Details regarding these 8215 individuals and the 238 biomarkers that were available in all participants have previously been published. As previously reported, all biomarker results were normalised (if not normally distributed) and standardised to a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1 prior to analysis [8].

Randomisations

The 8215 individuals were randomly assigned (without stratification or blocks) to two groups (X and Y) and the hazard ratio (HR) for death in Group X versus Group Y was calculated and recorded. All 8215 individuals were then repeatedly re‐randomised, and the calculation repeated. After 1000 randomisations, the number of pairs with a calculated HR for Group X versus Y of either less than 0.85 or above its inverse (1.18) was tabulated. This threshold was chosen because it is one that is commonly used for power calculations when designing large, randomised outcomes trials [9]. A set of 1000 randomisations was then repeated 1000 times (yielding a total of 1 million randomised pairs), and the average and median number of randomisations in each set of 1000 with a HR outside these limits was tabulated.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarised as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were summarised as counts and percentages. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for death in people assigned to Group X versus Group Y. The number of pairs within each set of 1000 randomisations for which the unadjusted HR of Group X versus Y was < 0.85 or > 1.18 was plotted using a histogram.

A possible mitigation strategy for dealing with occurrences such as the one described above was explored as follows. First, the clinical characteristics of individuals assigned to each group within those pairs identified after the first set of 1000 randomisations, that was characterised by a HR for one group versus the other of < 0.85 or > 1.18 were summarised and compared. Second, the baseline clinical characteristics of all 8215 participants were used to identify the independent clinical predictors of death using a forward selection model. The p‐value of 0.003 for inclusion of the model was calculated as 0.05 divided by the 16 possible clinical predictors (i.e., age, sex, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, prior cardiovascular event, albuminuria, estimated glomerular filtration rate, LDL cholesterol, metformin, statins, renin‐angiotensin drugs, beta blockers and anti‐platelet drugs). Third, beta coefficients derived from this analysis were used to estimate a clinical risk score for death for every one of the 8215 people in the pair (i.e., people in this lower risk group and in its ‘sister’ higher‐risk group in the pair). Normality of the score distribution was assessed by inspecting the histogram, kurtosis and skewness. Once established, each person's risk score was standardised to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, and between‐group differences in the score were compared using a linear regression model and summarised as a mean (95% CI) difference. Fourth, the hazard of random assignment to one versus the other group was re‐estimated using a Cox model that was adjusted for this baseline clinical score.

This method of deriving a baseline clinical risk score was repeated using the 238 baseline biomarkers that were available for each person. Biomarkers for all 8215 individuals were used to identify the independent biomarker predictors of death using a forward selection model in which each person's clinical risk score was forced in. The p‐value for inclusion of the model was set at 0.05 divided by the 238 biomarkers (i.e., 0.00021). Then beta coefficients derived from this analysis were used to estimate a clinical‐biomarker score for death for all 8215 people. As described above, after assessing the score distribution for normality, it was standardised and between‐pair differences were compared. Finally, the hazard of random assignment to one versus the other group was re‐estimated using a Cox model that adjusted for this baseline clinical‐biomarker risk score.

All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

The 8215 individuals were of mean age 64 and included 2792 (34.0%) women, 6675 (81.3%) with prior diabetes, 6488 (79.0%) with hypertension and 4878 (59.4%) with a prior cardiovascular event. The mean eGFR was 77.5 mL/min/1.73 m2 and 2602 (31.7%) had albuminuria. During a median follow‐up of 6.2 years, 1303 (15.9%) had died (Supplementary Table S1).

The 8215 individuals were then repeatedly randomly assigned to two groups for a total of 1000 times, the 1000 randomisations were repeated 1000 times and the HR of assignment to one versus the alternate group was calculated after each randomisation. A median of 3 (interquartile range 2, 4) of every 1000 randomisations (i.e., 0.3%) yielded two randomised groups for which the HR for death of assignment to one group versus the other was either < 0.85 or > 1.18 by chance alone. As noted in Figure 1, the number of times that 1000 randomisations yielded two groups with HRs as or more extreme than these thresholds ranged from 0 on 42 occasions to 3 on 243 occasions and 10 on 1 occasion. Overall, 3086 of 1 million randomisations (0.31%; 95% CI 0.0, 0.7%) yielded a HR that was as, or more extreme.

The 8215 participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups 1000 different times, and the set of 1000 randomisations was repeated 1000 times. The histogram shows the number of sets of 1000 randomised pairs for which the hazard ratio of the group with the higher number of deaths was > 1.18. This number ranged from 0 on 42 occasions to 3 on 243 occasions, and 10 on 1 occasion.

When an additional 1000 randomisations were done to explore analytic strategies to mitigate the impact of between‐group differences in baseline hazard, the hazard for death of assignment to one group in the pair versus the other was < 0.85 or > 1.18 on four separate occasions.

For these four pairs (labelled AB, CD, EF and GH), the HRs for the group with the higher incidence versus the one with the lower incidence were 1.20 (95% CI 1.08, 1.34), 1.22 (95% CI 1.09, 1.36), 1.19 (95% CI 1.06, 1.32) and 1.26 (95% CI 1.13, 1.41), respectively (Table 1). Despite these hazards, the clinical characteristics of the participants assigned to each member of the pair at baseline were generally similar (Table 1).

Discussion

Simulated randomisation of more than 8000 individuals into two groups with an annual incidence of an event (death) between 2.5% and 3% per year yields prognostically balanced groups 99.7% of the time. Conversely, it results in a clinically relevant imbalance (i.e., a HR > 1.18 or < 0.85) on 3 out of 1000 occasions. Thus, 0.3% of large, randomised two‐group trials with an event rate that is typical of many large modern trials have imbalanced groups with respect to future prognosis by chance alone. These analyses also show that this imbalance cannot always be detected by scrutiny of baseline characteristics (even when there are more than 250 baseline characteristics available for scrutiny). Moreover, adjusting for baseline covariates that predict the outcome in one member of each pair cannot reliably mitigate the chance differences in prognosis between the two randomised groups.

The possibility that imbalances in group prognosis can occur by chance is well‐established by statistical theory. These simulations of a real data concretely demonstrate the frequency with which a typical large, randomised outcomes trial would not reflect the effect of an intervention on the outcome despite high power, and near‐perfect adherence, outcome ascertainment and retention. They also demonstrate the consequences of such imbalances. Thus, a trial of the effect of a drug on death in these 8215 people (in whom the annual death rate was 2.7%), would have had 82% power [10] to detect an effect size of 0.85 and 97% power to detect an effect size of 0.8 (assuming a two‐sided Type 1 error rate of 0.05). But if a drug that truly had a neutral effect (i.e., a placebo) was assigned to Group B in a trial for which the randomisation process created the Groups A and B that were described above, the researchers would have falsely concluded that it was harmful compared with an inert comparator (Supplementary Figure S1). Moreover, if a drug that truly reduced the hazard of death by 20% (i.e., HR 0.8) had been assigned to this group (Group B), the researchers would have falsely concluded that the drug had a neutral effect, because its benefit would have reduced the increased hazard of Group B versus Group A. Alternatively, if that drug increased the hazard of death by 25% (i.e., HR 1.25), the researchers would have overestimated its harm (Supplementary Figure S1). The observation that about 1 out of every 300–400 large trials with a sample size and event rate similar to the ORIGIN trial, contain undetected between‐group differences in the baseline hazard of the primary outcome highlights the risk of relying on just one large outcomes trial to estimate the therapeutic effect of an intervention.

Using statistical techniques to adjust analyses of large, randomised trials for prespecified baseline prognostic covariates can improve power [2, 11]. Such an approach statistically mitigates small chance differences in baseline prognosis across randomised groups, thereby clarifying the effect of the intervention being tested. These analyses provide further support for routinely applying such an approach. They also show that when baseline chance differences in prognosis are large enough to be clinically relevant, adjusting for independent predictors will not eliminate the baseline imbalance completely. However, scrutiny of baseline differences in a prognostic risk score could alert the investigator to chance differences in prognosis in some instances (such as for pair GH above), which could inform interpretation of the trial's findings.

Strengths of these analyses include the use of data from real participants who participated in a randomised controlled outcomes trial, a large sample size with an event rate of the outcome similar to event rates of outcomes typically studied in large outcomes trials, the availability of many baseline variables, the 1 million simulated randomisations that were conducted, and the narrow CIs and interquartile ranges for the mean and median estimates. Limitations include the fact that these analyses were based on only one dataset and that only one effect size was explored. A different dataset with different sample size and event rates would clearly have yielded different findings. A higher (or lower) effect size would have yielded a lower (or higher) proportion of pairs with extreme HRs. Nevertheless, an effect size of 0.85 or 1.18 is typical of the effects that many contemporary outcomes trials are powered to detect. Also, for simplicity the simulations did not use a stratified and blocked randomisation scheme. Nevertheless, with more than 8000 people and more than 1200 events, such a scheme is highly unlikely to have altered the findings.

In summary, randomised controlled trials are the most reliable and effective way to estimate an intervention's effect on health outcomes. The simulations and analyses presented here show that even the perfect randomised controlled trial may, by chance alone, yield results that do not truly reflect the effect of an intervention and highlight the importance of scrutinising the totality of the available evidence when making regulatory or therapeutic decisions.
