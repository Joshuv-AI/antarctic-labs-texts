First-week postoperative caloric intake predicts mortality, complications and functional recovery in elderly patients with hip fracture and compromised nutritional status
Authors: Yan Liu, Lili Sun, Zhiwei Liu, Yannan Ma, Chen Li, Menglin Liang, Qing Zhang, Han Yu, Cong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of caloric deficiency during the first postoperative week on 60-day mortality, complication incidence and functional ambulatory recovery in elderly patients with hip fracture who are malnourished or nutritionally at risk.MethodsA prospective observational study included 120 patients with surgically treated hip fracture aged > 65 years with preoperative malnutrition (Mini Nutritional Assessment – Short Form ≤ 11). Caloric intake was calculated using the Harris–Benedict equation, and patients were stratified into two groups: <60% or ≥ 60% of the caloric goals during the first postoperative week. Outcomes included 60-day mortality, postoperative complications (infectious, cardiovascular, delirium) and mobility levels. Logistic regression adjusted for covariates was used for analysis.ResultsThe ≥ 60% caloric intake group exhibited significantly higher 60-day survival (93.98% vs. 72.97%, p = 0.004) and lower complication rates (38.55% vs. 75.68%, p < 0.001) than the < 60% group. Logistic regression analyses verified the reduced mortality risk (odds ratio [OR]: 0.129, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.02–0.70) and complication risk (OR: 0.225, 95% CI: 0.09–0.59) in the ≥ 60% group. Additionally, sufficient caloric intake was found to correlate with improved mobility (OR: 3.984, 95% CI: 1.54–10.31).ConclusionOur study demonstrates that insufficient caloric intake during the first postoperative week is associated with elevated mortality risk, higher rates of postoperative complications and delayed functional recovery in elderly patients undergoing hip fracture surgery who are malnourished.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01314-w.

Introduction

Hip fracture in elderly patients is a critical public health challenge and is associated with high morbidity, mortality and long-term functional decline [1, 2]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and perioperative care, up to 50% of survivors fail to regain pre-fracture mobility levels, and 10%–20% require permanent institutionalisation [3]. An optimising recovery strategy remains an unmet clinical priority, particularly in frail older adults with diminished physiological reserves.

A growing body of evidence underscores malnutrition as a pivotal risk factor influencing postoperative outcomes in elderly patients with hip fracture [4, 5]. Up to 50% of elderly patients with hip fracture present with pre-existing malnutrition, exacerbated by the catabolic stress of trauma and surgery [6]. Caloric deficiency impairs wound healing, amplifies muscle atrophy and prolongs immobilisation, creating a vicious cycle that hinders rehabilitation.

The impact of caloric deficiency on clinical outcomes tends to accumulate over time, with energy delivery being lowest during the first week [7]. The first postoperative week may represent a decisive phase for nutritional intervention. Animal studies suggest that transient caloric deficits during the first postoperative week exacerbate muscle proteolysis and delay functional recovery [8]. Clinically, the relationship between early caloric intake adherence and functional outcomes in human patients have been investigated. Goisser et al. [9] reported that dietary intake during the 4 days following surgery affected improvements in activities of daily living (ADL) at 6 months after surgery. Inoue et al. [10] revealed that energy intake during the first week postoperatively was an independent factor that impacted patients’ health. These studies collectively support the importance of setting a clear energy attainment threshold in the early postoperative period (the first week). Critically ill patients whose caloric intake has not reached the target for more than 1 week are prone to notable deterioration in their nutritional status, adversely affecting clinical outcomes. Therefore, the guidelines [7, 11] suggest providing supplementary parenteral nutrition (SPN) to critically ill patients whose enteral nutrition has not reached the target (> 60% of the target calories) 1 week after admission to the intensive care unit (ICU). The previous guideline recommended the use of SPN after 7–10 days in patients unable to tolerate > 60% of the goal protein and energy requirements [11]. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the impact of early postoperative caloric deficiency, using a predefined threshold, on critical recovery outcomes. The 60% caloric adequacy threshold was employed as a pragmatic benchmark to operationalize this investigation.

However, no prospective studies have specifically examined how first-week caloric intake influences the objective measures of hip fracture recovery. These measures include ambulatory capacity (e.g. ability to walk independently or with assistance, a core functional marker directly reflecting patients’ ability to return to daily life), 60-day all-cause mortality (a critical short-term prognostic endpoint for evaluating treatment effectiveness in this high-risk population) and postoperative complication incidence (e.g. infections and delirium, which are tangible barriers to recovery and closely linked to nutritional status). This gap is particularly notable because prior studies on geriatric hip fracture nutrition have mostly focused on subjective or non-specific outcomes (e.g. general self-reported functional scores), failing to clarify whether early caloric adequacy can directly drive improvements in these objective, clinically actionable recovery metrics that guide perioperative care decisions for patients who are malnourished.

In this study, we evaluate the impact of caloric deficiency during the first postoperative week on three critical postoperative outcomes – functional ambulatory recovery, all-cause mortality and complication incidence – in elderly patients with hip fracture presenting with preoperative malnutrition or nutritional risk.

Methods

Study population

This study was a prospective observational study including 120 elderly patients with hip fracture who underwent surgical intervention between January 2022 and July 2024. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Jishuitan Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval Number: JLKSZ2022-13-B04). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) elderly patients (age > 65 years) with radiologically confirmed acute hip fractures, (2) ability to walk before fracture (either with assistance or independently) and (3) evidence of nutritional compromise (Mini Nutritional Assessment – Short Form [MNA-SF] score ≤ 11) at initial clinical assessment.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-oral feeding status; (2) concurrent non-hip fractures or open hip fractures; (3) pre-existing fracture-related complications at admission (pressure ulcers, localised infections or deep vein thrombosis); (4) severe cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Examination [MMSE] score < 10 points); (5) severe comorbidities, including chronic heart failure, advanced chronic kidney disease (stage 4/5), cirrhosis (Child–Pugh B/C), chronic pulmonary disease (e.g. chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), active malignancies (excluding non-melanoma skin cancers) and chronic gastrointestinal disorders (e.g. pancreatic insufficiency, Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis); and (6) pre-fracture non-ambulatory status (e.g. bedridden or chair bound) or severe mobility limitations (unable to walk even with help).

Sample size calculation​

Sample size was determined a priori based on the primary outcome of 60-day all-cause mortality, using G*Power 3.1 software for calculation. Previous studies on elderly patients with hip fracture who were malnourished reported a 60-day mortality rate of 25%–30% in patients with insufficient postoperative caloric intake (< 60% of requirements) and 8%–12% in those with adequate intake postoperatively (≥ 60%) [9, 10]. We assumed a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power (1 − β) of 0.80 and an allocation ratio of 1:2 (insufficient vs. adequate caloric intake, based on preliminary clinical observations of nutritional intake distribution in our centre). The calculation yielded a minimum required sample size of 112 patients. Considering the potential dropout rate (e.g. loss to follow-up, withdrawal of consent), we planned to enrol 120 patients, which was consistent with the final sample size of this study.

Rehabilitation protocol and mobilisation​

A standardised rehabilitation protocol, consistent with institutional guidelines for geriatric hip fracture care, was implemented for all patients. The timing and content of the rehabilitation sessions were systematically recorded for each participant. Physical therapy, focusing on early mobilisation, was initiated within 24 h postoperatively unless contraindicated by the surgeon due to acute medical instability. The rehabilitation programme consisted of twice-daily sessions on weekdays and once-daily sessions at weekends, with each session lasting approximately 30 min. The content progressed from initial bed-based exercises (e.g. active range of motion, isometric contractions) to assisted transfers, sitting on the edge of the bed and, finally, gait training with appropriate assistive devices as tolerated. The multidisciplinary intervention team included physiatrists, physical therapists, occupational therapists and clinical nurses. The volume of rehabilitation was quantified by the total number of physical therapy sessions attended by each patient during the hospital stay. The timing of first mobilisation (defined as the first time the patient sat on the edge of the bed or stood with assistance) was documented in hours after surgery. These details were collected prospectively from standardised physical therapy notes and nursing records to account for their potential as confounding variables in the analysis of postoperative complications and functional recovery.

Dietary assessment

Basal energy expenditure or calorie requirement was calculated using the following Harris–Benedict equation formulas [12]: 66.5 + 13.76 × weight (kg) + 5.003 × height (cm) − 6.755 × age (years) for men and 655 + 9.563 × weight (kg) + 1.850 × height (cm) − 4.676 × age (years) for women. To estimate the total energy requirement (TER) accounting for postoperative stress and activity during early mobilization, we applied standardized multipliers to the BEE: an activity factor of 1.3 and a stress factor of 1.2 (for moderate surgical stress). Meals with a set total energy content were provided, and their intake was directly observed. The consumption of each dietary item was recorded as a percentage (expressed in ×10%) of the total amount by trained study staff. With the assistance of a dietitian, the ‘average caloric intake (%)’ was calculated as the percentage of the actual caloric intake divided by the calculated calorie requirement using the weight-based formula over a 7-day period (average caloric intake [%] = [caloric intake in 7 days ÷ calorie requirement for 7 days] × 100), starting from the first day after surgery.

We stratified the enrolled patients into two groups based on the first-week caloric intake: average caloric intake (%) < 60% versus ≥ 60%. Based on the evidence from the perioperative studies [9, 10] and in combination with the consensus in ICU guidelines for critically ill patients, a 60% energy adequacy threshold was selected as a pragmatic, hypothesis-generating benchmark rather than a proposed optimal target. This cutoff was chosen for its clinical feasibility (aligning with existing nutritional monitoring practices) and prior use in similar observational studies, enabling comparison with published data—though it has not been validated as the ideal caloric intake goal specifically for geriatric hip fracture patients in non-ICU settings.

Instruments

Nutritional status was determined by interviewing patients using the MNA-SF. The MNA-SF is a validated multidimensional nutritional screening tool specifically designed for geriatric populations, with scores of 12–14 points judged as ‘normal nutritional status’, scores of 8–11 points judged as ‘at risk of malnutrition’ and scores of 0–7 points judged as ‘malnourished’ [13].

The MMSE, a standardised 30-point cognitive screening instrument (range 0–30), is widely used in clinical neurology and neuropsychological research for quantifying global cognitive dysfunction. Patients were considered to have no cognitive impairment if they achieved > 26 points, mildly to moderately impaired with 10–26 points and severely impaired with < 10 points [14].

The study by Bujakovic et al. [15] have confirmed the possibility of using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scoring system in prognosis of the development of postoperative complications in the elderly with hip fractures. It was employed as a comprehensive, validated instrument to quantify the overall physiological derangement and comorbidity burden at admission, incorporating three validated prognostic components: (1) a weighted acute physiology score derived from 12 critical physiological parameters (including vital signs, oxygenation and metabolic markers), (2) age-stratified mortality risk weighting and (3) chronic health comorbidity assessment [16].

Tsai et al. [17] have indicated that hip fracture is independently associated with increased risk of coronary heart disease. The Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) classification was specifically used to stratify the functional capacity of patients with pre-existing coronary artery disease—a common comorbidity in this elderly population—as their angina severity is a key determinant of cardiopulmonary reserve and a known risk factor for postoperative cardiovascular complications. The CCS grading system employs four grades from I (without limitation of physical activity) to IV (inability to perform any physical activity without discomfort) [18].

The Barthel Index (BI), a classic self-reported questionnaire, was used in this study to assess ADL for each patient. The BI includes 10 items: bathing, toilet action, bowel continence, bladder continence, dressing, feeding, grooming, walking on a surface, ascending and descending stairs, and moving from a chair to a bed and from a bed to a chair. The BI score ranges from 0 points (total dependence) to 100 points (complete independence). The lower the BI score is, the worse the ability to perform ADL [19].

The mobility levels assessment results included patients who were bedridden or chair bound, mobile only with a helper (with or without assistive devices for locomotion) and mobile alone (with or without assistive devices for locomotion). Patients were classified according to their mobility levels as ambulators (mobile only with a helper or mobile alone) or non-ambulators (bedridden or chair bound).

Data collection

Patient demographic characteristics (age, gender, height, weight and body mass index [BMI]) were obtained from patients during personal interviews. The following clinical assessment tools were used within 48 h after surgery: the MNA-SF for nutritional screening, the BI for functional independence evaluation, MMSE for cognitive assessment, APACHE II for disease severity quantification and the CCS grading system for angina classification.

All enrolled participants underwent standardised postoperative surveillance through scheduled clinical evaluations, maintaining a minimum observational period of 60-days after surgical intervention. The primary endpoint of the study was 60-day all-cause mortality. The period from hospital admission to discharge (length of hospital stay), postoperative complications and mobility levels were also recorded. Postoperative complications were categorised as infectious, mental and cardiovascular complications. To establish temporal relationships, daily caloric intake was recorded prospectively from postoperative day 1 to day 7, whereas postoperative complications were monitored and documented starting from postoperative day 3 and adjudicated by two independent clinicians. This ensured that energy intake data for the first 2–3 postoperative days were not confounded by complication-related reductions in intake. For the primary logistic regression analysis (evaluating complication presence/absence), only one complication was recorded per patient (prioritizing the first adjudicated complication to avoid overestimating the exposure-outcome association by counting overlapping events). To capture the cumulative impact of complications, we additionally assessed complication burden (number of complications per patient) as a secondary descriptive measure. Patients’ mobility levels were evaluated at 60 days after surgery. For the patients who died before 60 days after surgery, we considered the mobility level at the last report.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 software. Comparative analysis of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics was performed between patients categorised as having average caloric intake (%) < 60% and average caloric intake (%) ≥ 60%. The categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The continuous variables were compared using a Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. The association between average caloric intake in the first postoperative week and postoperative complications, 60-day mortality and mobility levels was analysed using logistic analyses. The strength of the associations was reported as the odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). The details of confounding variable control in the logistic regression model can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Results

Patient characteristics

In this study, a total of 145 eligible patients were initially included, and 25 patients were excluded for the following reasons: concurrent non-hip fractures or open hip fractures (n = 10), pre-existing fracture-related complications at admission (n = 7), non-oral feeding status (n = 5) and severe abnormal liver and kidney function (n = 3). Finally, 120 (43 men and 77 women) surgically managed elderly patients with hip fracture presenting with preoperative malnutrition or nutritional risk were included for analysis. The patient flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Among the patients, 37 (30.83%) were included in the average caloric intake < 60% group and 83 (69.17%) were in the average caloric intake ≥ 60% group. As shown in Table 1, there were no differences in general patient characteristics (e.g. age, gender, MNA-SF score, MMSE score) between the two groups.

Outcomes

Table 2 summarizes the key 60-day postoperative outcomes stratified by first-week caloric intake, including mortality, complication profiles (number, types), Barthel Index, and mobility levels. The overall 60-day survival rate in our study was 82.50% (99/120). At 60 days after surgery, the number of deaths in the caloric intake ≥ 60% group and the caloric intake < 60% group was 7 and 14, respectively. The 60-day survival rate in the caloric intake ≥ 60% group was significantly higher than that in the caloric intake < 60% group (76 [91.57%] vs. 23 [62.16%], p < 0.001). In the crude model logistic regression analysis, the average caloric intake in the first postoperative week was able to significantly predict 60-day mortality (OR: 0.173; 95% CI: 0.05–0.56; p = 0.003). In the exploratory expanded model, the results revealed an association between an average caloric intake ≥ 60% and a decreased risk of 60-day mortality (OR: 0.129; 95% CI: 0.02–0.70; p = 0.017; Table 3).

In total, 60 (50.00%) patients experienced postoperative complications, with 74 cases of infectious, mental and cardiovascular complications reported, including 19 cases of pulmonary infection, 21 cases of urinary tract infection, 14 cases of deep venous thrombosis, 16 cases of delirium and 2 cases of pressure sores. The caloric intake < 60% group had a much higher postoperative complication rate than the caloric intake ≥ 60% group (75.68% vs. 38.55%, p < 0.001). Among the 60 patients with complications, 42 (70.0%) experienced a single complication, 15 (25.0%) had two complications, and 3 (5.0%) had three complications. The caloric intake < 60% group had a higher median complication burden (1.5 [interquartile range, IQR: 1.0–2.0]) compared to the ≥ 60% group (1.0 [IQR: 1.0–1.0]), with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.008). Specifically, 12 (32.4%) patients in the < 60% group had two or more complications, whereas only 6 (7.2%) patients in the ≥ 60% group experienced multiple complications, further supporting the association between insufficient early caloric intake and more severe postoperative complication profiles. In the crude model logistic regression analysis, the average caloric intake in the first postoperative week was able to significantly predict postoperative complications (OR: 0.202; 95% CI: 0.08–0.49; p < 0.001). After adjustment, the logistic model revealed an association between an average caloric intake ≥ 60% and a decreased risk of postoperative complications (OR: 0.225; 95% CI: 0.09–0.59; p = 0.002; Table 4).

Among the 120 enrolled patients, 91 (75.83%) could walk with or without help during the study period. At the 60-day follow-up, the overall median BI score of the 120 enrolled patients was 65.0 (interquartile range [IQR]: 45.0–80.0). The ≥ 60% caloric intake group exhibited significantly higher BI scores than the < 60% group: the median BI score in the ≥ 60% group was 75.0 (IQR: 55.0–85.0), whereas that in the < 60% group was 45.0 (IQR: 30.0–60.0), with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). In the crude model logistic regression analysis, the average caloric intake in the first postoperative week was found to predict postoperative mobility levels (OR: 5.029; 95% CI: 2.05–12.36; p < 0.001). After adjustment for age and length of hospital stay, the logistic regression model revealed an association between an average caloric intake ≥ 60% and increased postoperative mobility levels (OR: 3.984; 95% CI: 1.54–10.31; p = 0.004; Table 5).

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by redefining caloric adequacy relative to the total energy requirement (TER), which incorporated adjustments for activity and stress (TER = BEE × 1.3 × 1.2). Using the ≥ 60% TER threshold, 35 patients (29.2%) were classified into the adequate intake group, and 85 (70.8%) into the inadequate group. The associations between caloric adequacy and clinical outcomes remained consistent with the primary analysis. The ≥ 60% TER group continued to demonstrate a significantly lower 60-day mortality rate (5.71% vs. 25.88%, p = 0.008) and a lower complication rate (40.00% vs. 74.12%, p < 0.001) compared to the < 60% TER group. In the adjusted logistic regression model, achieving ≥ 60% TER was independently associated with reduced mortality (OR: 0.15, 95% CI: 0.03–0.81, p = 0.027) and fewer complications (OR: 0.24, 95% CI: 0.09–0.65, p = 0.005). Similarly, adequate caloric intake relative to TER was associated with improved mobility outcomes (OR: 3.65, 95% CI: 1.41–9.45, p = 0.008). The robustness of these findings across different definitions of energy requirements strengthens the main conclusion that early postoperative caloric intake is a critical prognostic factor.

A milestone analysis was conducted on day 7 after surgery (at the end of the caloric intake assessment period). Patients who developed complications before day 7 were excluded from this sensitivity analysis to eliminate the influence of early complications on energy intake. The adjusted logistic regression results from the landmark analysis remained consistent with the primary analysis: ≥60% caloric intake was still associated with reduced 60-day mortality (OR: 0.142, 95% CI: 0.03–0.78, p = 0.024) and improved mobility (OR: 3.816, 95% CI: 1.43–10.17, p = 0.007), confirming that the primary findings were not substantially driven by reverse causation.

Discussion

The present study investigated the impact of caloric intake during the first postoperative week on three critical outcomes—60-day mortality, postoperative complications, and objective mobility recovery—in elderly patients with hip fracture and compromised nutritional status. A major strength of this work is the inclusion of mobility level as a clinically meaningful, objective outcome, which directly reflects patients’ ability to return to daily life and independent living—core goals of care for this vulnerable population. Our findings demonstrate that achieving ≥ 60% of the estimated calorie requirements postoperatively was associated with significantly reduced mortality, lower complication rates, and notably improved mobility outcomes, reinforcing the prognostic value of early caloric intake for functional recovery beyond just survival and complication reduction. These results align with prior evidence underscoring the critical role of early nutritional optimisation in mitigating adverse outcomes in this vulnerable population [9].

Caloric deficiency is a pivotal risk factor influencing postoperative outcomes of elderly patients with hip fracture [4, 20]. Studies have shown that perioperative nutritional intervention is associated with an improvement in nutritional status and a greater functional recovery in elderly patients with hip fracture [4]. Williams et al. [21] found that early postoperative nutritional supplementation in a small group of patients with hip fracture was associated with a significantly shorter length of hospital stay and higher quality perioperative improvement [21]. The first postoperative week may represent a decisive phase for nutritional intervention. Our results showed that caloric sufficiency (caloric intake ≥ 60%) in the first postoperative week correlated with a 93.98% survival rate at 60 days, compared with 72.97% in the deficient group (caloric intake < 60%), reinforcing the prognostic value of early nutritional support. Logistic regression analysis further verified that an average caloric intake ≥ 60% was associated with a decreased risk of 60-day mortality. Our findings align with those reported by Taylor et al. [7], who observed that critically ill patients achieving < 60% of the prescribed caloric targets during the initial ICU week experienced significantly elevated mortality rates. The observed association between caloric deficiency and adverse outcomes may be related to potential mediating factors such as compounded catabolic stress, impaired immune function, and delayed tissue repair—consistent with animal models showing that transient caloric deficits exacerbate muscle proteolysis and prolong recovery [8]. However, our observational study cannot establish causal relationships, and these mechanistic considerations remain speculative.

Our data also indicated that caloric deficiency in the first postoperative week was a risk factor for postoperative complications. The reduced complication rate in the average caloric intake ≥ 60% group (38.55% vs. 75.68%) emphasised the systemic benefits of adequate caloric intake. The review conducted by Malafarina et al. [4] identified that malnutrition was a key driver of postoperative infections in patients with hip fracture. A meta-analysis involving 14 eligible selected studies revealed that caloric deficiency significantly increased postoperative complications such as delirium [22]. Caloric adequacy may bolster immune competence by preserving lymphocyte proliferation and mucosal barrier integrity, thereby reducing pathogen translocation and decreasing infectious complications [11]. Additionally, the association between caloric intake and delirium aligns with emerging evidence linking metabolic dysregulation (e.g. hypoglycaemia, electrolyte imbalances) to acute cognitive dysfunction in elderly patients with hip fracture [23].

Furthermore, the inclusion of mobility level as a primary outcome represents a key strength of this study, addressing a critical gap in prior literature. Unlike subjective functional scores (e.g., self-reported ADL scales) used in many previous nutrition-related hip fracture studies [9, 10], mobility level (ambulatory vs. non-ambulatory) is an objective, observable endpoint that directly translates to clinical practice and patient-centered outcomes. For elderly hip fracture patients, regaining mobility is not only a marker of physical recovery but also a determinant of long-term independence, institutionalization risk, and quality of life [3]. In a previous study, Inoue et al. [23] found that energy intake during the first postoperative week independently predicted functional recovery, and Goisser et al. [9] demonstrated that inadequate dietary intake within 4 days following hip fracture surgery predicted poorer functional recovery at 6 months. Consistent with these findings, our study identifies an association between average caloric intake ≥ 60% and improved postoperative mobility levels, reinforcing its utility as a prognostic marker for functional recovery. This may reflect preserved muscle protein synthesis, which is highly energy dependent, particularly in the catabolic postoperative state [12]. This association is particularly meaningful given that up to 50% of hip fracture survivors fail to regain pre-fracture mobility, and identifying modifiable factors (e.g., early caloric intake) associated with improved ambulation provides actionable prognostic information for risk stratification and patient counseling.

First-week postoperative caloric intake (with the ≥ 60% estimated energy requirement threshold) can serve as a practical prognostic marker for risk stratification in elderly hip fracture patients with compromised nutritional status—particularly for predicting objective mobility recovery, a key patient-centered outcome. Tracking this parameter may help clinicians identify patients at elevated risk of 60-day mortality, postoperative complications, and delayed mobility recovery. Patients with < 60% caloric intake represent a high-risk subgroup that warrants closer postoperative surveillance, as their prognostic profile suggests greater vulnerability to adverse outcomes. This risk stratification does not imply direct causal links between caloric intake and outcomes, nor does it mandate specific nutritional or rehabilitation interventions, but rather provides a data-driven reference for prioritizing clinical attention and individualizing monitoring strategies. For subgroups such as patients with preoperative MNA-SF scores < 8 or those with prolonged hospital stays, proactive nutrition interventions targeting the ≥ 60% caloric threshold should be prioritised to reduce the complication risks identified in our study.

Notably, the generalizability of the 60% caloric threshold, which is partly derived from guidelines for critically ill ICU patients [7, 11], to a ward-based hip fracture population may be limited and warrants explicit discussion. Critically ill patients typically exhibit a higher degree of metabolic stress, systemic inflammation, and are under more intensive monitoring, which justifies an aggressive nutritional strategy within the first week. In contrast, elderly hip fracture patients on general wards, while experiencing a significant catabolic response, may have a different physiological state and nutritional tolerance. Therefore, the direct applicability of ICU-derived recommendations to this population remains an assumption. Our use of this threshold was primarily as a pragmatically defined and clinically referenced cutoff to test a hypothesis within an observational framework. It should be interpreted as an initial benchmark that highlights the prognostic importance of early caloric intake rather than a definitive, universally validated target. Future studies are needed to determine the optimal caloric threshold specifically for geriatric hip fracture patients in non-ICU settings.

Undeniably, the present study has several limitations. First, the single-centre design may introduce selection bias and limit the generalisability of the findings. The findings of this study may not apply to centres with different care pathways (e.g. varied nutritional support timing or rehabilitation intensity), potentially weakening the external validity of the conclusions. Second, the relatively small sample size (n = 120) limits statistical power and external validity. Small subgroups, such as the 37 patients in the average caloric intake < 60% cohort, increase the risk of type II errors, particularly in stratified analyses. This may result in the missed subtle associations between caloric intake and specific outcomes (e.g. cardiovascular complications). Furthermore, the exclusion of patients with dementia, severe comorbidities or non-oral feeding status, common in real-world hip fracture cohorts, narrows the applicability of findings to a relatively healthy subset. The findings may overestimate the benefits of adequate caloric intake in real-world cohorts that include more frail patients, as these excluded groups often have less favourable baseline recovery potential. Third, the 60-day follow-up period captures short-term outcomes but overlooks long-term functional decline or mortality. Whether the benefits of first-week caloric adequacy persist long term cannot be verified, nor can long-term risks (e.g. muscle loss recurrence) that may emerge after the acute phase be assessed, limiting the comprehensiveness of our conclusions on sustained recovery. Hip fracture survivors face elevated risks of death for up to 1-year post-injury [3], suggesting that prolonged observation is necessary to evaluate the sustainability of early nutritional benefits. Furthermore, caloric intake quantification via direct observation and percentage estimation, although pragmatic, introduces measurement bias. Self-reported or observed intake often overestimates actual consumption, and the Harris–Benedict equation, derived from healthy populations, may inaccurately estimate energy needs in patients who are metabolically stressed following surgery. Objective measures such as indirect calorimetry could improve accuracy but are too resource intensive for routine use. Finally, protein intake (critical for muscle synthesis) and ONS use (a common means of supplementing caloric and protein intake) may have influenced postoperative mobility outcomes, and the unadjusted association between caloric intake and mobility should be interpreted with this confounding factor in mind. Due to the prospective observational nature of the study and constraints in the initial data collection design, this study was unable to retroactively obtain these data. For future research, randomised controlled trials are needed to validate whether targeted nutritional interventions (e.g. personalised oral nutrition plans with protein-enriched foods) that ensure ≥ 60% caloric intake in the first postoperative week can causally reduce mortality and complications, addressing the observational nature of our current study. Additionally, multi-centre studies with larger sample sizes should incorporate long-term follow-up and collect data on protein intake, ONS use and muscle mass changes (via bioelectrical impedance analysis or computed tomography imaging). Such studies could further clarify whether early caloric adequacy synergises with protein intake to preserve muscle function and whether the 60% threshold applies to more diverse populations. While we initially considered additional covariates based on clinical relevance, we ultimately adhered to the prespecified EPV constraints to ensure model stability. The final models were limited to the variables specified in the Statistical methods section, maintaining EPV ratios of 5.25 for mortality (21 events/4 variables) and 10.00 for complications (60 events/6 variables). This approach prioritizes statistical rigor over model complexity, though it may not fully account for all potential confounders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study indicates that insufficient caloric intake during the first postoperative week is associated with elevated 60-day mortality risk, higher postoperative complication rates, and delayed functional recovery in malnourished elderly patients undergoing hip fracture surgery. These findings highlight the prognostic value of first-week caloric intake as a simple, accessible marker for risk stratification in this vulnerable population. First-week caloric intake (≥ 60% of estimated requirements) may help identify patients at lower risk of adverse outcomes, providing a data-driven reference for clinical decision-making regarding surveillance intensity rather than mandating specific treatment modifications. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these prognostic associations and explore potential causal pathways before any clinical implementation recommendations can be made.
