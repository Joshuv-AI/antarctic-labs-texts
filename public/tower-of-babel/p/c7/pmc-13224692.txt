Human factors validation study of a disposable autoinjector (Bmab 1200 autoinjector) for subcutaneous delivery of Ustekinumab
Authors: Sam Moses K, Rohini Kamaraj, Suryaprakash Konda Ramachary Subramanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUstekinumab is approved for treating moderate to severe plaque psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, Crohn’s disease, and ulcerative colitis. The Bmab 1200 autoinjector is a simple, two-step device for subcutaneous ustekinumab biosimilar delivery. This was an in vitro, nonclinical, simulated-use human factors validation study to evaluate if intended users could safely and effectively use it. Ninety participants (recruited via purposive sampling) were enrolled (mean age: 43 years; range: 12–72 years), including adult and adolescent patients, lay caregivers, and healthcare professionals. Without formal training, participants performed simulated injections into foam pads. Test personnel observed, documented, debriefed, and reported any errors made by participants while completing assigned tasks and sub-tasks. Each participant was observed throughout the session and observational and performance data were collected in real time by data logger. Performance measures included injection success rates, use errors, and 24 knowledge tasks. Across these knowledge tasks, participants were required to locate, identify, and understand 28 distinct measures. Participants also provided feedback on usability and confidence levels. All participants (100%) completed unaided dose injections. A total of 20 use errors were documented: hand hygiene (n = 8), site preparation (n = 5), and device handling (n = 7), none causing device damage or failed delivery. The knowledge task success rate was 99.6% (2,511/2,520 measures). Nine use errors (study artifacts or inattention to instructions) occurred only among caregivers. All participants expressed confidence in safe dosing and found the device user-friendly. The Bmab 1200 autoinjector demonstrated high usability and safety across diverse users for ustekinumab biosimilar delivery in chronic autoimmune conditions.Keywords: Autoinjector, simulated testing, user-centered design, ustekinumab, validation study

Introduction

Ustekinumab is a human monoclonal antibody that targets the p40 protein subunit shared by interleukin-12 (IL-12) and interleukin-23 (IL-23). These cytokines play a central role in driving inflammatory processes. Ustekinumab has received regulatory approval for treating multiple chronic inflammatory conditions, including psoriatic arthritis (PsA), moderate to severe plaque psoriasis (PsO), ulcerative colitis (UC), and Crohn’s disease (CD) (Ghazawi et al. 2021; van Vollenhoven et al. 2018). Because these conditions are chronic, prolonged administration of biologic therapies such as ustekinumab is typically necessary, making treatment acceptability and sustainability essential for optimal patient outcomes (Bressler et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2024). Autoinjectors provide several clinical benefits, including accurate dose delivery, improved user convenience, better adherence to therapy, and a lower incidence of injection site reactions (Weinhold et al. 2018). Additionally, research indicates that patients generally prefer autoinjectors over alternative delivery methods (Vermeire et al. 2018).

The Bmab 1200 autoinjector (Figure 1) is a simple, ergonomic, dry rubber latex-free, citrate-free, fully mechanical, two-step, hand-held, single-dose, prefilled, disposable device for automatic drug delivery. The device employs a 29 G thin needle to minimise injection discomfort and features integrated needle shielding for passive needle stick protection (USFDA 2024). It contains a prefilled syringe with 0.5 mL or 1 mL of medication (45 mg or 90 mg), with the needle shielded by a yellow guard before use to reduce the risk of contamination, damage, or accidental contact. Injections may be self-administered into the thigh or abdomen or administered by a caregiver into the back of the upper arm.

The Bmab 1200 disposable single-use autoinjector.

Bmab 1200 has been extensively characterised using orthogonal physicochemical, analytical, and functional tests to assess critical quality attributes, including peptide sequence, secondary and tertiary structures, and potency. Recently, the phase 3 STELLAR-2 study demonstrated that the biosimilar Bmab 1200 maintained clinical biosimilarity to reference ustekinumab through Week 52, even after treatment switching, with long-term comparable efficacy and safety in patients with plaque PsO (Szepietowski et al. 2025a; Szepietowski et al. 2025b).

The present study aimed at assessing whether the target user population of the Bmab 1200 disposable autoinjector for ustekinumab (patients with moderate to severe pain associated with moderate to severe plaque PsO, PsA, UC, CD, and lay caregivers and healthcare professionals [HCPs]) could demonstrate safe and effective use by meeting usability criteria determined through task risk analysis. This human factors validation study was an in vitro, nonclinical, simulated-use study designed to present each user group with a scenario simulating typical product use. This approach aligns with European Union and United States regulatory guidance, which mandates that the final device undergo a human factors/usability study to validate performance by representative users in the intended use setting (IEC 2021; MHRA 2016; USFDA 2016).

Patients and methods

Ethics

Prior to participant enrolment, the protocol and all associated materials were reviewed and approved by Salus (Biocon.IAA-2220; Salus no. 25028), an independent Institutional Review Board located in Austin, TX, USA. The study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines (ICH 2025) and the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later revisions (World Medical Association 2024). The moderator informed all prospective participants about the study’s nature, objectives, and potential risks, and written informed consent was obtained. Participants under 18 years of age completed assent forms and were accompanied by a parent or legal guardian. Study data were maintained on secure, access-controlled computer systems used solely by the usability vendor’s project team. As the study did not involve any therapeutic intervention, it was classified as a nonsignificant risk study.

Study population

The intended users included both adults (≥18 years) and adolescents (10–17 years old) from the United States who were diagnosed with an autoimmune disorder (PsA, UC, CD, or plaque PsO) and were receiving active treatment for their condition. Purposive sampling was used to recruit study participants representative of the intended user populations, including patients taking US-licensed Stelara. At least 15 participants per user group were included, consistent with Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance on applying human factors and usability engineering to medical devices, which indicates that this sample size is sufficient to identify the majority of use-related problems during summative evaluations (USFDA 2016). Both injection-experienced and injection-naïve participants were included. Caregivers (nonprofessionals, ≥ 18 years of age) were included if they routinely provided care to these patients. The study also included HCPs who prescribed medications for these conditions, as well as licensed nurse practitioners or registered nurses who either administered the medication themselves or trained patients on proper administration. Participants with hand and visual impairments were included in the study as long as their impairments did not limit or inhibit their use of the autoinjector device.

Facilities and equipment

The study was conducted at a usability laboratory in Saratoga, CA, USA, in a simulated-use setting designed to replicate typical home or clinic environments. The setup included a table, chairs, and dimmed ambient lighting, where users would self-inject or receive treatment. The study utilised the final production-line Bmab 1200 autoinjector, supplied in its to-be-marketed version, along with instructions for use and active drug-filled syringes. Materials required for simulated use, such as alcohol wipes, hand sanitisers, cotton balls, gloves (for HCPs), a sharps container, and a waste basket, were provided to all participants. Simulation equipment included an abdominal injection pad and thigh pad with a lap board for patients, as well as manikins fitted with upper arm, abdominal, and thigh pads for HCPs and caregivers. Additionally, the testing room was equipped with cameras that recorded wide-angle footage of the moderator and participant, along with close-up footage of the participant performing the task. This setup allowed the project team to review the sessions to support root cause analysis.

Study procedure

Upon arrival, the moderator greeted all participants and guided them to the training room. Participants were asked to sign an informed consent form and complete a pre-study questionnaire that collected personal details, including their experience with similar devices. No training was provided for using the Bmab 1200 autoinjector; participants were only given access to the instructions for use after familiarising themselves with the device. They were randomly assigned to simulate delivering a dose of medication (90 mg or 45 mg) into the abdominal or thigh pad, with counterbalancing applied to ensure balanced distribution across the two dose conditions. Injection pads were positioned over the participants’ injection sites to replicate real-life handling and device use. All patients attached the pad to their self-selected injection site, while caregivers and HCPs attached the pad to their chosen site on a manikin. Participants were allowed to use the system independently and realistically, without any assistance, coaching, or critique. Additional tasks and knowledge-based questions were included to further evaluate safe and effective use. After completing the task, participants took part in a post-task interview designed to identify the root causes of any errors or close calls and to gather their perspectives on the user interface (Figure 2).

Measures

The primary outcome was the ability of intended users to safely and effectively use the Bmab 1200 autoinjector under simulated-use conditions. This was assessed using two categories of measures: performance measures and subjective measures. Performance measures included user actions related to the correct use of the product and instructional materials, as well as actions that could lead to improper use or demonstrated difficulties. Test personnel observed, documented, debriefed, and reported data on any use errors made by participants while completing tasks or sub-tasks, including instances where moderator intervention was required. A use error was defined as an action or inaction that differed from the manufacturer’s expectations, resulting in an outcome different from what the user anticipated, not solely due to device failure, and one that did or could result in harm. Close calls were also recorded, referring to instances where an individual experienced difficulty or made an error that might have led to harm but successfully recovered and prevented it. Additionally, test personnel noted any user difficulties encountered while performing the intended procedure with the autoinjector, as well as the amount of time an individual held the device during the injection. Time measurements included overall injection time, defined as the duration from the first click to device removal, and injection time, measured from the first click to the second click when the yellow plunger rod stopped moving. After completing one unaided dose administration task, participants were interviewed by test personnel using a structured questionnaire that included 24 knowledge-task questions. Across these knowledge tasks, participants had to locate or identify and understand 28 different measures or pieces of information. These included questions on the reuse of the autoinjector, short-term and long-term storage of the autoinjector, understanding the expiry conditions of the autoinjector, selection of injection sites, and precautions during the injection procedure. Additionally, the questions included two performance task-related queries: one involved locating the expiration date on the product carton and the other required identifying the expiration date on the autoinjector label.

Subjective measures included feedback regarding their confidence in successfully dosing themselves or their patients with the autoinjector. A data logger directly observed each participant during the session and collected all observational and performance data in real time.

Risk control measures

A simulated-use methodology was employed to minimise risk for participants. Although the drug product was present, injections were administered into a simulation pad placed over the intended injection site. Risks were further mitigated by continuous observation from the moderator and study staff, with the moderator able to intervene if necessary. Any intervention was classified as a use error and followed by a debrief and root cause analysis.

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses were used to evaluate the number of use errors and success rates, identify patterns across user groups, and compare findings between injection-naïve and injection-experienced users. This approach supported the assessment of overall usability, consistent with human factors evaluation principles.

Results

Demographics

Overall, 90 participants with varying levels of injection experience were recruited. The mean age of all participants was 43 years (range: 12–72 years), and 63% were female. All adult and adolescent patients were diagnosed with plaque PsO, PsA, CD, or UC and were receiving active medications for these conditions. There was an equal proportion of participants with previous experience using autoinjectors and those who were naïve. The majority of participants did not have any physical or cognitive impairments. All participants were fluent in English (Figure 3).

Injection success rate

All 90 participants successfully performed the unaided dose injection without assistance, achieving a 100% success rate. Table 1 summarises all performance measures recorded during the study. There were no close calls or observed difficulties during the unaided dose injection activity. A total of 20 use errors were documented: eight participants failed to wash their hands before administering the injection and five participants did not adequately prepare the injection site with an alcohol swab. Additionally, four participants recapped the autoinjector before injection. However, none of these instances resulted in device or needle damage, and all participants successfully delivered the dose. Furthermore, one HCP placed her fingers near the needle opening after removing the cap, and two injection-naïve patients did not dispose of the used autoinjector in a FDA-cleared sharps container. No notable differences were identified among adults, adolescents, caregivers, and HCPs, regardless of prior injection experience (Table 2).

Injection timings

The overall injection process averaged 17 ± 11 seconds (median: 14 seconds [range: 5–70]), while the actual injection time averaged 4 ± 1.5 seconds (median: 3 seconds [range: 2–7]) (Table 3). Participants using the 45 mg/0.5 mL Bmab 1200 autoinjector had an average injection time of 3 seconds (range: 2–7 seconds), whereas those using the 90 mg/mL Bmab 1200 autoinjector averaged 5 seconds (range: 3–6 seconds). All participants maintained adequate hold duration to ensure successful injection completion during the dry injection simulation.

Knowledge and performance task findings

All participants completed the 24 knowledge tasks encompassing 28 distinct measures requiring location, identification, and comprehension of critical information from the instruction for use template. The overall success rate for locating and identifying essential information was 99.6% (2,511/2,520).

All participants completed the two performance tasks, with nine use errors documented for the knowledge tasks. All use errors were reported among caregivers (both injection-experienced and naïve) and included knowledge gaps related to storage, appearance, handling, and injection site rotation of the autoinjector (Figure 4a & b). Table 2 summarises all use errors and their associated root cause analyses. No close calls or instances of user difficulty were documented for any of the tasks. No notable differences were observed among adult patients, adolescent patients, caregivers, and HCPs, regardless of injection experience.

Knowledge task and performance task findings.

Root cause analysis of use errors

Root cause analysis of all use errors in the performance and knowledge tasks indicated that none were attributed to the design of the product or the instructions for use template. Instead, these errors were study artefacts or were attributed to participants’ curiosity during their first use of the product or to participants intentionally or inadvertently skipping steps despite having read the instructions for use and feeling that the information was clearly presented (Table 2).

Feedback on user-friendliness of the autoinjector and its instructions for use

All participants expressed confidence in their ability to safely and successfully prepare and administer a dose with the autoinjector. The device was described as ‘quite simple’ and ‘pretty easy,’ and the instructions were easy to follow with large fonts (Table 4).

Discussion

In this validation study, adult and adolescent patients, caregivers, and HCPs achieved a high rate of injection success using the ustekinumab biosimilar Bmab 1200 autoinjector. All participants, regardless of prior injection experience, successfully delivered simulated injections into a foam pad. Although no injuries occurred, some participants made errors in site preparation and medical waste disposal. This aligns with earlier studies, which demonstrated that the most commonly reported use errors with autoinjectors included failing to remove the safety cap before injection, holding the device upside down, and not maintaining the device in position until the injection was fully delivered (Guerlain et al. 2010; Lange et al. 2015).

Most participants in this study indicated awareness of appropriate autoinjector storage, the expected appearance of the liquid, and the correct location for inspection. No use difficulties or close calls were reported during task execution or while answering knowledge-based questions. Very few use errors were documented in the knowledge tasks, and none were attributed to the design of the product or the instructions for use template. Instead, all errors were attributed to study artefacts or participants not reading the instructions carefully. These results support earlier findings that autoinjectors are reliable devices preferred by both patients and caregivers (Dostal et al. 2023). Such devices can be routinely used with support training programs for HCPs and patients on optimal use and maintenance of autoinjectors (Lageat et al. 2021).

There are few studies reporting injection times, and most of these studies have shown that users often have difficulties in maintaining the device position for a few extra seconds following completion of the injection (Arora et al. 2018; Guerlain et al. 2010; Lange et al. 2015). In contrast, all participants in this study maintained an adequate hold duration to ensure successful injection completion during the dry injection simulation. This is an important observation as it contributes to the successful delivery of the full prescribed dose in a manner that ensures safety and effectiveness, minimising the risk of underdose.

Despite the minimal use errors observed during the injection process, participants reported favourable usability, including strong acceptance and ease of use. Feedback regarding handling and user confidence was also highly positive. These findings align with previously published evidence demonstrating comparable levels of ease of use, confidence, and comfort with similar autoinjectors (Berteau et al. 2010; Guerlain et al. 2010; Lange et al. 2015). Additionally, the inclusion of adolescent patients (aged 10–17 years) in this study is particularly valuable as this population represents an important user group for chronic inflammatory conditions such as PsO (Lee et al. 2019).

A limitation of this study is that injections were simulated using a foam pad at a research facility rather than in a clinical or home-use environment. However, health authorities consider simulated-use testing, as utilised in this study, sufficient for evaluating the user interface of most combination products. Accordingly, FDA guidance and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) 62366 standard recommend simulated-use testing to assess safe and effective device use by documenting any use-related errors (IEC 2021). It is also likely that many of the observed errors would be mitigated through appropriate training because users tend to exercise greater care when administering a subcutaneous injection to themselves or others in real-world scenarios.

Conclusions

The intended user populations (adults and adolescents with autoimmune disorders, caregivers, and HCPs) were able to use the Bmab 1200 autoinjector safely and successfully without prior training. They were able to appropriately interact with the associated labelling, packaging, and instructional materials. Performance of critical tasks did not reveal any patterns of use errors or difficulties that could lead to patient harm. Participants reported confidence in safe dosing and found the device user-friendly. These findings support the Bmab 1200 autoinjector as an effective and user-friendly option for subcutaneous self-administration of ustekinumab in patients with plaque PsO, PsA, CD, and UC.
