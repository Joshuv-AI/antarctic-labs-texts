Straw management in sugarcane affects sugarcane billbug (Coleoptera: Curculionidae) dispersal
Authors: Gabriela Franchini, João Rafael Silva Soares, Odair Aparecido Fernandes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prohibition of straw burning for sugarcane harvesting facilitates the accumulation of organic matter in the soil, creating a microhabitat favorable to the survival of pests, such as the sugarcane billbug Sphenophorus levis Vaurie (Coleoptera: Curculionidae). This condition may also modify S. levis behavior, especially dispersal in sugarcane fields. As the inefficiency of the control tactics currently employed has turned this species into a key pest in the crop, straw management could be an alternative to S. levis management. In this study, we evaluated the effect of sugarcane straw management on S. levis dispersal under three conditions: (i) full straw, (ii) windrowed straw in the sugarcane interrow, and (iii) removed straw using the capture-release-recapture technique. The maximum distance traveled by the S. levis varied from 1.5 to 6.7 m in 11 d, depending on straw management, with more restricted mobility when the straw was completely removed. The direction of movement was significantly influenced by straw removal, with a higher number of S. levis recaptured in the same row of sugarcane where insects were released (within-row). Males and females exhibited similar patterns, with greater dispersal activity during the first few days after release. The presence of straw favored the movement of S. levis, whereas its total removal or windrowing restricted its dispersal. Thus, straw removal from sugarcane rows is a potential strategy for managing this pest. These results underscore the importance of cultural management as an integrated pest control strategy.

Introduction

In recent decades, sugarcane cultivation in Brazil has undergone significant transformation (Santos et al. 2015). One of the most impactful changes was the prohibition of pre-harvest sugarcane burning, established by law in 2002 and completed in 2010, as part of a global environmental commitment to reduce carbon emissions into the atmosphere (Aguiar et al. 2011). As a result, mechanized harvesting has become predominant, leaving between 8 and 30 t ha−1 of straw on the soil surface (Carvalho et al. 2017).

Although straw deposition offers benefits such as soil erosion reduction, nutrient recycling, and weed control, it also creates a microclimate suitable for the survival of soil pests, such as the sugarcane root spittlebug Mahanarva fimbriolata Stål (Hemiptera: Cercopidae) and the sugarcane billbug Sphenophorus levis Vaurie (Coleoptera: Curculionidae) (Dinardo-Miranda and Fracasso 2013, Bordonal et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2023). The life cycle of S. levis is closely linked to the soil and the base of sugarcane plants. While larvae cause significant damage by feeding on the rhizomes, adults are responsible for dispersal and colonization (Dinardo-Miranda 2005). These adults are primarily terrestrial, moving on the soil surface to find mates and appropriate sites for feeding and oviposition at the base of sugarcane stalks, where their behavior is highly associated with dark conditions (Degaspari et al. 1987, Urach Ferreira and Ferreira 2023). The thick layer of straw left by modern harvesting practices fundamentally alters this habitat (Castro et al. 2019). This straw cover provides a stable, dark humid microclimate and, crucially, offers protection from natural enemies, extreme environmental conditions (ie, heatwaves and raindrops), and insecticides sprayed (Dinardo-Miranda and Fracasso 2013, Castro et al. 2019, Urach Ferreira and Ferreira 2023). Furthermore, sugarcane is a semi-perennial crop that is usually cultivated for 5 or more years in the same area, consistently offering conditions for S. levis population buildup (Dinardo-Miranda and Fracasso 2013, Santos et al. 2015). The presence of straw creates ideal conditions for billbug adults to thrive and move within plantations.

Given that the straw layer functions as the primary habitat for adult billbugs, their ability to disperse to new feeding and oviposition sites is a critical factor in population dynamics and the spread of infestations throughout the field. A slow dispersal rate of 6-11 m per month has been previously reported (Arthur et al. 1987, Degaspari et al. 1987). However, this rate does not explain how local environmental conditions, such as the presence or absence of straw cover, may influence short-distance movement. Understanding these fine-scale movement patterns is essential, as even minor changes in dispersal ability at the local level can accumulate over time, significantly affecting the speed and pattern of field-wide colonization. To date, no study has linked the dispersal of this billbug species to the influence of different straw management strategies. This knowledge gap limits the development of effective cultural practices. In this study, we investigated the movement of S. levis adults under different sugarcane straw management conditions as a complementary control tool for this key sugarcane insect pest.

Material and Methods

Insects

Sphenophorus levis adults were collected using attractive baits in commercial sugarcane-infested fields in the municipalities of Jaboticabal (21°17′S; 48°14′W) and Guariba (21°18′S; 48°11′W), São Paulo state, Brazil. The attractive bait consisted of a clump of four sugarcane stalk pieces, each obtained by cutting sugarcane culms lengthwise into halves approximately 30 cm in length. One bait (ie, one clump of 4 stalk pieces) was placed at each collection point, positioned with the cut surfaces facing downward at the base of the plants and covered with straw (Precetti and Teran 1983). The baits were distributed along 2 crop rows spaced 6 m apart, maintaining 10 m between baits. Forty-five baits were used and replaced weekly. The traps were inspected twice a week, and the captured insects were transferred to plastic containers (1 liter capacity) containing a layer of soil and a piece of sugarcane bait (10 cm long) for maintenance and transfer to the laboratory. The collections were carried out from September to December 2022 (first growing season, 2022/2023) and February 2024 (second growing season, 2023/2024).

In the laboratory, the insects were sexed (Alencar 2016), counted, and transferred to transparent plastic boxes (40 × 20 × 10 cm) containing a ± 5 cm layer of soil, in which a 10 cm cut sugarcane culm was placed with the cut face down for feeding and oviposition. Fifty pairs of S. levis were kept in each plastic box in a climate-controlled room (27 ± 1 °C, 60 ± 5% RH, and a photoperiod of 0:24 h L:D) (Degaspari et al. 1987).

Laboratory Study

Marking and Survival Assessment of S. levis Adults

Before initiating the dispersal study in sugarcane fields, we conducted a preliminary bioassay to determine whether the marking strategy could potentially affect S. levis lifespan and behavior. At this stage, S. levis adults collected from the field were used to evaluate the survival rate associated with marking under controlled conditions. Field-collected S. levis adults were sexed, and equal numbers of males and females were randomly allocated to each marking treatment to evaluate survival under controlled conditions.

Initially, the insects were quarantined for 3 d to rule out possible natural infections by Beauveria bassiana (Balsamo) Vuillemin (Hypocreales: Cordycipitaceae) and Metarhizium anisopliae (Metchnikoff) Sorokin (Hypocreales: Clavicipitaceae) from the collection sites, which are commonly used in commercial fields, and therefore could bias S. levis potential survival (Vinha et al. 2020). After this period, the insects were cleaned with a fine-bristled brush to remove soil and other impurities that could be present in the pronotum and elytra. Square label tags (3 × 3 mm) were glued to the pronotum of adult insects using instant glue (MULTBOND, 3M). These tags were made using A4 paper, duly numbered, colored, and laminated in plastic to facilitate the subsequent identification of each individual (Butler et al. 2012). After marking, the insects were kept in Petri dishes (100 mm in diameter × 20 mm) and dried at room temperature for 10 min. The marked insects were transferred to transparent plastic boxes that were identical to those used for the maintenance of the insects. The control group had no tagging, but they were handled to ensure the same handling conditions as the marked group (Butler et al. 2012). The plastic boxes containing the insects were kept in a climate-controlled room (27 ± 3 °C, 60 ± 5% RH, and a photoperiod of 0:24 h L:D).

We coupled 30 S. levis pairs in a plastic box (experimental unit), following a completely randomized experimental design with 2 treatments (Tagged and Control) and 3 repetitions for each treatment. The insects were observed daily to confirm mortality. Death was confirmed by observing the reaction of the adults after being touched with a fine-bristled brush on their antennae. Dead insects were removed from the plastic boxes.

Field Study

Sphenophorus levis Dispersal in the Field Under Different Straw Management Conditions

The movement of S. levis was assessed by the release and recapture of previously marked males and females over two crop seasons (2022/2023 and 2023/2024) in a commercial sugarcane field located in the region of Jaboticabal, SP, Brazil (approximate coordinates 21°12′S; 48°06′W; 520 m elevation). In the 2022/2023 season, each plot measured 25 × 18 m (450 m2) and contained 12 rows spaced 1.5 m. Plant height ranged from 1 m at the beginning to 2 m at the end of the experiment, and was measured from the base of the plant to the tip of the tallest leaf (Machado et al. 2009). Based on the results from the first season, the plot area was increased in the 2023/2024 season, where each plot measured 33 × 33 m (1,089 m2) and contained 21 rows spaced 1.5 m apart. The plot size was increased to better accommodate the maximum dispersal distance observed in the previous growing season and minimize edge effects. The plants had an average height of 2 m at the beginning of the experiment, measured from the base of the plant to the tip of the tallest leaf (Machado et al. 2009). Both areas were managed according to conventional agricultural practices, except that no pesticides were applied to control S. levis. Vinasse was applied at a rate of 25,000 liter ha−1 60 d before the onset of the experiment. Vinasse is a liquid byproduct of sugarcane ethanol production and is extensively applied in sugarcane fields as a biofertilizer, mainly due to its relatively high potassium content and organic matter. This practice is standard in commercial production systems in Brazil. Beyond its agronomic function, vinasse contains volatile compounds that may influence insect activity and attraction, particularly for soil-dwelling pests such as S. levis (Martins et al. 2020). Considering that vinasse application is an integral component of sugarcane field management, it was deliberately maintained in the experimental area to preserve realistic agronomic and ecological conditions and to avoid introducing artificial management scenarios. The treatments were distributed according to a randomized block design, and each treatment was replicated 3 times for each year. Three treatments were adopted: (i) straw in the total area -FS, (ii) straw windrowed in the sugarcane interrow - WS, and (iii) straw completely removed - RS (Fig. 1). The windrowing process involved removing straw from the sugarcane rows and moving it to the interrow space at a distance of 20 cm from the base of the sugarcane plants. This operation was performed using a windrower coupled to a 4-wheel tractor. Additionally, the amount of straw in the area was assessed at the beginning and end of the experiment using a 1 × 1 m square frame, with 4 random points selected in the study area. The amount of straw in the field at the beginning and end of the trials was 25,000 kg ha−1 and 810 kg ha−1, respectively, during the 2022/2023 season. During the 2023/2024 season, the recorded values were 27,000 kg ha−1 at the beginning and 840 kg ha−1 at the end of the experiment.

Sphenophorus levis adults were collected from the same fields for laboratory study and marked 24 h before each release, using the methodology described in the previous section. The insects were transported to the study site in plastic containers placed inside a Styrofoam box. At the trial site, the plastic containers containing the insects were opened and kept inverted at the center of each plot (release point [RP]; Fig. 2) for 5 min to allow acclimatization.

Release During the 2022/2023 Growing Season

Five releases were conducted (range 5-100 marked insects per release), totaling 2,745 insects released over 55 d (Table 1). The markings allowed for identification of each insect, treatment, and release date. These quantities represent the collection period. Thus, release was conducted with insects kept in the laboratory for the shortest possible time.

Insects were recaptured using 18 attractive baits per plot. These baits were distributed in grids at distances of 3.0 and 4.5 m from the release point (Fig. 2). After 24 d, the distance of the baits was increased to 6 m, while keeping the 2 previous distances constant and increasing the number of baits to 24. These distances were based on dispersal studies by Arthur et al. (1987). The recapture of S. levis adults was based on attractive baits similar to those used for the collection of insects (Precetti and Teran 1983). The baits were checked weekly for S. levis marked individuals captured and replaced with freshly cut stalks. All recaptured insects were handled according to a strict protocol to accurately measure natural dispersal. Upon finding the marked insect, its location was recorded. The insect was then returned to the exact point of capture, but the bait was removed to ensure that the continuous attractant did not arrest its movement and, consequently, bias the dispersal data. We continued to assess the baits until no further insect were captured after three consecutive observations (ie, three weeks). This criterion was adopted as an indicator of stability in the studied insect populations (Southwood and Henderson 2000).

Release During the 2023/2024 Growing Season

In this study, a one-time release of 50 insects per plot was conducted on 9 February 2024 (9 AM), totaling 450 insects (Table 2). This number was established because of the low abundance of insects in the field during this season. Insects were recaptured using 35 attractive baits per plot. The distribution of the baits followed the methodology of the previous season, but they were positioned at distances of 1.5, 3.0, 4.5, and 6.0 m from the release point. The evaluation period for the study was 30 d, and the assessment method adopted to determine the evaluation period followed the same criteria used for the first season (2022/2023).

Data Analysis

The effectiveness of the markings was evaluated by comparing the proportion of surviving adults among the treatments based on a preliminary laboratory test. This process was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method in the “survival” package (Therneau 2024). The differences in survival data between the 2 tested groups (tagged and control) were subjected to statistical analysis using the Wilcoxon and Log-rank tests.

Data regarding the relationship between the distance traveled by S. levis adults in the different treatments were analyzed using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM), considering the growing season as a random effect variable. A linear mixed model (LMM) with growing season as a random effect was also applied to assess potential differences in the average speed for each treatment. Models were fitted using the “glmmTMB” package (Brooks et al. 2017), and residuals of the models were tested for compliance with assumptions such as the normality and homogeneity using the “DHARMa” package (Hartig 2022). Wald test type III was used to check the significance of the treatments. When significant, multiple pairwise comparisons were performed between the estimated marginal means of the treatments, with the P values adjusted by the Tukey method, using a significance level of 5% using “emmeans” package (Lenth 2024).

As dispersal was similar between the 2 yr of the study, the data from both trials were merged to assess the influence of the treatments on the movement of S. levis adults. To evaluate potential sex-specific patterns, 6 candidate models (Supplementary Table S1) were fitted to each dataset (males, females, and pooled data), and the best-fitting model was selected for each case. For this purpose, the Corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) was adopted, as the sample size of the analyses was small, along with the goodness-of-fit test using Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Chi-square (Proença-Ferreira et al. 2023) (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). Model selection was based on the lowest AICC value, a chi-square significance level greater than or equal to 0.05, and visual curve fitting. The influence of sex was evaluated using the same model (Proença-Ferreira et al. 2023). The data were processed using the “dispfit” package (Proença-Ferreira et al. 2023). Generalized linear regression models (GLM) were also employed to verify the effects of treatments and distance traveled on the recapture rate of S. levis adults in the field. The models were fitted by considering a second-degree polynomial function for the distance variable with a negative binomial distribution.

A second negative binomial GLM model was fitted to verify whether there was an interaction between the direction of dispersal (within-row or accross-row) and the different treatments on the capture of S. levis. For this purpose, data were organized by summing the number of recaptured insects from each plot in the row where they were released (within-row) or in the interrow across (accross-row) all evaluations. One plot from the WS treatment in the second year, where no insect capture was observed under any condition (within-row or across-row), was excluded from this analysis. The number of traps in the row of release and adjacent rows differed, and this unequal sampling effort may have affected the number of recaptured insects. Therefore, prior to fitting the GLM, a preliminary analysis was conducted to test this hypothesis through linear and nonlinear fitting and a correlation between the number of traps and recaptured S. levis adults, which showed no significant relationship (Supplementary Fig. S1). The normality and homogeneity of the model residuals were checked, and a marginal means comparison with Tukey’s adjustment at the 5% level was applied to explore the conditions that resulted in higher recaptures.

To visualize the spatial distribution of S. levis recaptures across recapture dates, data were reorganized by summing trapped insects from the same baits across all release dates for each bait coordinate (X and Y, in meters). These data were then submitted to a geostatistical GLMM with a negative binomial link function model using the “sdmTMB” package (Anderson et al. 2024). All analyses were performed using the R software version 4.2.1 (R Core Team 2022).

Results

Evaluation of Marking on the Survival of Sphenophorus levis Adults

Marking with tags did not affect the survival rate of insects (log-rank test: χ2 = 0.759; df = 1; P = 0.384; Wilcoxon test: χ2 = 2.92; df = 1; P = 0.087) (Fig. 3). Tags remained attached and intact in 73.80 ± 2.90% of the marked individuals, and the maximum longevity of field-collected insects, whose prior age was unknown, was 125 d (Fig. 3).

Movement of Sphenophorus levis Adults Under Different Sugarcane Straw Management Practices

In the first year of the study, out of 2,745 insects released, 74 (2.70%) were recaptured, with only one insect recaptured twice. Among the recaptured insects, 5 were confirmed as marked (0.18%); however, their tags were partially worn, preventing identification of individual tag numbers. In the second year, 33 of 450 released insects were recaptured (7.33%), and none exhibited worn tag.

The maximum distance traveled by S. levis adults in the first and second years of the study was 6.70 m and 6.00 m, respectively, in areas with FS coverage; 4.50 m in both years in areas with complete straw removal; and 6.36 m and 3.00 m, respectively, in areas with straw windrowing. The presence of straw in the field influenced the movement of S. levis adults in both years (χ2 = 14.64; df = 2; P = 0.001). In RS plots, adults traveled shorter distances than in FS or WS plots (Fig. 4A). In contrast, there was no significant difference in the average speed of the insects across the different treatments (χ2 = 5.15; df = 2; P = 0.076; Fig. 4B).

Discussion

The use of laminated tags did not influence the survival of S. levis. Consequently, this marking methodology is ideal for studies on the biology, population dynamics, behavior, and dispersal of S. levis (Hagler and Jackson 2001). In a similar study, no differences were observed in the survival of Nicrophorus americanus Oliver (Coleoptera: Silphidae), with a tag retention rate of 75.5% after 2 weeks (Butler et al. 2012). In our study, most markings (99.84%) remained intact for more than 18 weeks. The stability of markings on individuals is crucial in release-recapture studies to estimate population dynamics, as the loss of these markings prevents the identification of marked insects and their differentiation from cohabiting wild individuals. However, an important limitation in our assessment is that we evaluated only survival and tag retention and did not explicitly test whether the presence of tags influenced movement or dispersal behavior. Although tags were attached to the pronotum to minimize interference with locomotion and flight (Butler et al. 2012), subtle behavioral effects cannot be ruled out. Thus, survival and tag retention were used as indirect indicators of tag suitability. While the tagging method proved suitable in terms of survival and durability, future studies should directly evaluate potential effects of tagging on movement and dispersal using controlled behavioral assays or enclosed arenas.

The average recapture rate in our study was 3.3% (107 of 3,195 released insects), a value that is consistent with and expected in mark-release-recapture studies with low-mobility and cryptic beetles (Pisani Gareau et al. 2020, Delaplace et al. 2023, Paul et al. 2024). The intrinsic difficulty of sampling insects that inhabit the soil and shelter under debris, such as S. levis, naturally leads to lower recovery rates compared to more active flying insects. As with most mark–release–recapture studies, our approach is subject to inherent limitations related to detection probability. Only a small fraction of released individuals was recaptured, and the dispersal of the remaining insects could not be directly observed. Consequently, recaptured individuals may represent a subset of the population with a higher propensity to respond to attractive baits, rather than the full range of dispersal strategies expressed by S. levis adults in the field. In addition, because recaptures were based on fixed bait locations, movements beyond the spatial extent of the sampling design could not be detected. Although adult S. levis are primarily ground-active, occasional flight cannot be excluded and may have contributed to undetected long-distance movements, particularly under reduced straw cover (Matos et al. 2025). Therefore, our results should be interpreted as reflecting relative dispersal responses to straw management under standardized sampling conditions, rather than absolute estimates of dispersal behavior. Despite these limitations, the consistency of treatment effects across 2 growing seasons supports the robustness of the observed patterns and their relevance for understanding how cultural practices shape pest movement in sugarcane agroecosystems.

Our results revealed that S. levis adults traveled greater distances in areas where straw was not managed or where it was windrowed. This strongly suggests that the presence of straw in the field favors the movement of these insects, whereas in areas where straw was completely removed, the recapture rate was higher and for a longer period. The adoption of green mechanized harvesting has resulted in extensive straw deposition in fields (Carvalho et al. 2017). The presence of straw is known to influence soil arthropods in annual crops, especially the predatory beetles of the Carabidae family (Leclercq-Dransart et al. 2020, Pisani Gareau et al. 2020). Straw provides a favorable microclimate in terms of moisture and nutrients as well as protection against predators (Castro et al. 2019). Similarly, there was a greater recapture of Cosmopolites sordidus (Germar) (Coleoptera: Curculionidae) in areas with interrow banana residue cover than in areas without cover in banana plantations (Vinatier et al. 2010).

The presence of straw between sugarcane rows did not limit the movement of S. levis. In contrast, it may have acted as a favorable environment for its protection, a behavior that can be interpreted as a strategy to search for suitable conditions, similar to that observed in plots with total straw coverage (Vinatier et al. 2010, Castro et al. 2019). Windrowing, a practice performed after harvesting, involves moving straw to the interrow, increasing the incidence of light on the ratoon, and disfavoring soil pests such as the root spittlebug M. fimbriolata (Concenço et al. 2017). In contrast, in plots where straw was completely removed, insects traveled shorter distances. This restricted movement can be attributed to greater exposure to light and high temperatures, which increase the risk of mortality and make it difficult to find suitable habitats (Vinatier et al. 2011, Dinardo-Miranda and Fracasso 2013). Although straw removal is detrimental to soil pests, it can have negative impacts on ecosystem services, such as reducing organic matter and soil fertility, thus affecting sugarcane yield (Bordonal et al. 2018). Plant size may also have influenced the movement of S. levis adults. At the beginning of the study, plants were relatively short, allowing greater light penetration between rows; by the end of the study, however, plants reached approximately 2 m in height, resulting in increased shading. Preliminary studies have indicated that S. levis exhibits negative phototaxis and moves to darker locations (Urach Ferreira and Ferreira 2023). This may explain the longer recapture period in areas with complete straw removal in the second season (2023/2024).

Additionally, it is pertinent to consider the influence of natural straw decomposition on insect behavior throughout the experimental period (Castro et al. 2019). Our data confirmed a substantial reduction in straw cover, decreasing from approximately 25,000 to 27,000 kg ha−1 at the beginning to approximately 810 to 840 kg ha−1 at the end of the trials in both growing seasons. However, it is likely that the impact of this gradual reduction on measured dispersal patterns was minimal. Our results indicated that most of the movement activity of S. levis adults was concentrated during the first few days after release. The recapture probability density plot over time corroborated this observation, showing a sharp peak in initial activity. During this critical period, the straw cover conditions were highly representative of the management initially applied to each treatment (FS, WS, and RS). Thus, it is likely that the recorded dispersal patterns predominantly reflect the behavioral responses of insects to the conditions they encounter immediately after release.

The initial movement of insects was high, a behavior classified as subdiffuse, possibly because of the search for suitable microclimates and intraspecific competition. This type of behavior is similar to that of C. sordidus, suggesting that movement is rarely random but guided by the search for resources such as food, shelter, and oviposition sites (Vinatier et al. 2010). The observed behavior was consistent with studies on other beetles, such as Ips typographus (L.) (Coleoptera: Curculionidae) (Doležal et al. 2016, Jones et al. 2019). These bark beetle species are aggressive and exhaust their hosts in just one generation; therefore, they must fly longer distances to find suitable hosts (Doležal et al. 2016). In contrast, less aggressive species can complete several generations on the same host tree and forage close to their place of origin, minimizing the risks associated with long flights (Kees et al. 2017). Finally, the direction of movement of S. levis was influenced by the presence of straw, which appeared to act as a stimulus for dispersal. Differences in movement patterns between males and females were similar across treatments, with areas with total straw cover being more favorable for females, possibly due to the search for oviposition sites (Albrectsen and Nachman 2001, Negro et al. 2008).

In conclusion, pronotal tagging did not affect the survival of S. levis adults, supporting its suitability for mark–release–recapture studies. Our results consistently revealed relative differences in dispersal patterns among straw management strategies under standardized sampling conditions. The presence of straw clearly facilitated adult movement, whereas complete straw removal restricted dispersal, highlighting straw management as a promising complementary tactic for sugarcane billbug management. Because dispersal behavior plays a central role in pest spread and population dynamics, these findings contribute to a better understanding of how cultural practices shape pest movement in sugarcane systems. Future research integrating mark–release–recapture approaches with controlled or semi-enclosed experimental arenas should further refine dispersal estimates, identify potential detection biases, and help define straw management thresholds that balance pest suppression with the maintenance of ecosystem services, supporting more sustainable and profitable sugarcane agroecosystems.
