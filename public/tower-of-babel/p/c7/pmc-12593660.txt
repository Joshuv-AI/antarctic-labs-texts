Multi-omics analysis reveals the regulatory mechanism of shading on quality-related metabolites in Camellia sinensis cv. Lifeng
Authors: Xiaojun Niu, Cun Ao, Ruimin Teng, Hongli Li, Yun Zhao, Jizhong Yu, Haitao Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Shading enhances green tea quality by promoting the accumulation of amino acids and reducing the synthesis of catechin. We hypothesize that the expression of amino acid-related genes is related to the accumulation of amino acids in tea plants after shading. To explore the regulatory mechanism of shading-induced amino acid biosynthesis, we performed RNA-Seq and qRT-PCR analyses to examine the expression of amino acid metabolism-related genes. Our results revealed significant upregulation of genes associated with theanine biosynthesis and transport. In addition, non-targeted metabolomics and biochemical quantification revealed that the content of theanine and other amino acids significantly increased after shading. Furthermore, shading was found to promote coordinated regulation between light signaling components (e.g., UVR8, HY5, COP1) and transcription factors (such as CsMYB1/4/7/12 and CsPIF3). These findings reveal key molecular mechanisms behind shade-induced amino acid metabolism and provides new insights into our understanding of regulatory mechanisms for shading enhanced tea quality.

Introduction

Green tea is highly favored by tea consumers around the world for its unique aroma, taste, color, as well as its many other qualities. The quality of premium green tea is primarily determined by the composition and concentration of its secondary metabolites, which play a crucial role in influencing the tea's coloration, flavor profile, and overall taste characteristics (Zeng et al., 2019). These metabolites exhibit significant cultivar-specific variation and are susceptible to external environmental factors (Shao, Deng, et al., 2022). Light and temperature are recognized as critical environmental factors that impact tea quality (Liu et al., 2016). Currently, shading treatments have been widely adopted in tea cultivation to modulate light and temperature conditions, thereby influencing the morphology, color, and metabolic composition of tea leaves (Fang et al., 2022; Ge et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2011; Ye et al., 2023). However, the regulatory mechanisms by which shade induces metabolic changes in Camellia sinensis to improve tea quality remain incompletely understood.

Numerous studies have revealed that moderate shading promotes the accumulation of amino acids and caffeine, while simultaneously reducing tea polyphenol content, thereby improving the multifaceted quality of green tea (Ji et al., 2018; Ku et al., 2010; Sano et al., 2018; Shao et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2012). However, most of studies have focused on the effects of shading on the composition and content of metabolites in tea leaves, relatively little is known about the mechanisms by how shading regulates these metabolites. Chen et al. (2017) found that the hydrolysis of chloroplast proteins was the main reason for the increase in amino acid content in tea plants after shading treatment. This conclusion is mainly based on the proteomic data of tea plants after shading, and the expression of amino acid-related genes was not analyzed. Moreover, the shading period in this paper was two weeks, and the impact of short-term shading on the amino acids of tea plants was not considered. In recent years, some studies have shown that the expression of genes related to theanine synthesis in tea plants increases significantly after shading (Li et al., 2025; Shao, Jiao, et al., 2022). In reality, theanine—a unique non-protein amino acid predominantly found in tea plants (Camellia sinensis), which accounts for more than half of the total free amino acids in tea leaves (Ashihara, 2015; Yang et al., 2021). Therefore, the mechanism by which shading induces the accumulation of free amino acids in tea remains controversial. Several studies have shown that shading can substantially reduce the expression of genes associated with the biosynthetic pathways of phenylpropanoids and flavonoids within the catechin biosynthetic pathway (Li et al., 2025; Shao, Deng, et al., 2022; Shao, Jiao, et al., 2022; Zhang, Ma, et al., 2020). However, the mechanisms through which shading causes the down-regulation of the genes responsible for synthesizing flavonoids and catechins in tea plants are not well understood. Studies have shown that HY5 is a crucial factor promoting photomorphogenesis, which is influenced by the upstream regulator COP1 in darkness (Osterlund et al., 2000). HY5 directly regulates the promoters of genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis and mediates the UV-B or light-induced activation of MYB12, thereby enhancing flavonoid accumulation (Stracke et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2011). Another transcription factor, PIF3, also plays a significant role in light signal transduction and acts as a positive regulatory element for plant responses to shading (Kim et al., 2003). PIF3 and HY5 coordinate the regulation of light-responsive genes through their interaction with cryptochrome and UVR8 (Pedmale et al., 2016). However, it remains unclear how these factors contribute to flavonoid biosynthesis in tea plants under shaded conditions. Thus, it is crucial to gain a more systematic and in-depth understanding of these regulatory mechanisms, given the pervasive utilization of shading in tea cultivation to enhance the quality of tea.

Metabolomics has recently been applied broadly to explore the physiology of tea plants (Arkorful et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2015), identify key taste and aroma compounds (Dai et al., 2017; Jansen et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2018), and clarify the biosynthetic pathways of key metabolites related to quality (Farag et al., 2023; Li et al., 2020; Rubel Mozumder et al., 2020). Transcriptomic approaches have emerged as an effective method of deciphering key biological mechanisms in tea plants, enabling systematic investigation of metabolite biosynthesis, stress response regulation, functional gene identification and metabolic pathway modulation (Gong et al., 2020; Kong et al., 2022). The integration of metabolomics and transcriptomics facilitates a systematic dissection of the molecular mechanisms that govern the complex metabolic regulatory networks of tea trees in response to different environments. The study aims to: (1) investigate gene expression patterns associated with amino acids, caffeine and catechins biosynthesis in the tea plant variety ‘Lifeng’ under shading treatment, (2) elucidate the mechanisms of changes in the accumulation of amino acids and caffeine and the reduction of catechins during the shading process of tea plants.

Materials and methods

Tea plant cultivation and experimental design

The shading experiments were conducted at the experimental base of Pingyao, the Tea Institute, Hangzhou Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The 9 rows of Camellia sinensis (L.) cv. Li Feng tea plants with 10-years-old from tea field were selected for the treatments. Each planting row is 15 m long and 1.5 m apart. The shade period was from April 12 to 17 in 2023, coinciding with the phenological stage when emerging tea shoots had developed to the characteristic “one bud and one leaf” morphology. Shade experiments were applied through covering the tea plants with either BSN (black shade netting, 85 % shading) or a layer of TPF (transparent plastic PE stretch film, 0.1 mm thick), both of which were installed 0.5 m above the top of the tea plants. Tea trees without any cover were used as a control (CK). Three biological replicates were utilized, with each replicate consisting of a pooled sample of 60 leaves collected from the same position on five different tea plants. Upon completion of the shading treatment, approximately 20 leaves from each treatment group were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C to facilitate subsequent multi-omics analyses, such as transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling. The remaining tea leaves were subsequently stored in a − 20 °C freezer to allow for the determination of chlorophyll content and analysis of biochemical components.

Measurement of chlorophyll content

Fresh tea leaves (0.1 g) were finely sliced and incubated in 10 mL of 95 % ethanol for 24 h in darkness at 25 °C to extract chlorophyll. After centrifuging (2000 ×g, 10 min), chlorophyll content was determined by measuring supernatant absorbance at 665 nm and 649 nm with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (TU-1901, Persee, China). The chlorophyll content was calculated using the formula: Chlorophyll a = 13.95 × A665–6.88 × A649 and Chlorophyll b = 24.96 × A649–7.32 × A665. Each treatment consists of three biological replicates, each of which contains three technical replicates.

Quantitative analysis of tea polyphenols, caffeine, catechins and free amino acids

The sample extraction was performed as follows. Approximately 3 g of dry sample was extracted with 500 mL of water and centrifuged at 246 g for 10 min. Subsequently, the sample was filtered through a 0.22 μm aqueous phase membrane and then reserved for further analysis. The total polyphenol content was determined by the Folin colorimetric method as described by Teng et al. (2024). Briefly, a total of 5 mL of 10 % Folin phenol reagent was added to 1 mL of the supernatant. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and allowed to react for 8 min. Then, 4 mL of a 7.5 % sodium carbonate solution was added, and the resulting mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The absorbance of each sample was measured at 765 nm using a spectrophotometer (TU-1901, Beijing, China), with water used as the blank control. The composition of catechin components and caffeine in the extract were estimated using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent 1100, Santa Clara, CA, USA) system equipped with a Hypsial ODS C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm) according to the methodology described in a previous study (Niu et al., 2024). The quantification of total free amino acids was modified slightly according to the method described by Chen et al. (2017). The specific steps are as follows: Accurately absorb 1 mL supernatant and inject it into 25 mL colorimetric tube, and mixed with 0.5 mL of 2 % ninhydrin reagent and 0.5 mL of pH 8.0 phosphate buffer. The reaction mixture was heated in a boiling-water bath for 15 min. After cooling, dilute the solution to a final volume of 25 mL. Allow to stand for 10 min, then absorbance was recorded at 570 nm by a TU-1901 ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer‌ (Beijing Persee Instruments Co., Ltd., China).

The determination of theanine was carried out by HPLC in accordance with the China National Standard GB/T 23193–2017. Briefly, 1.0 g of tea powder was weighed and transferred into a 200 mL beaker. Subsequently, 100 mL of boiling distilled water was added, mixed, and placed in a thermostatic water bath at 100 °C for 30 min. The tea sample was then filtered and transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask, cooled and fixed to the required scale. After the sample was filtered using a 0.45-mm aqueous filter membrane, it was analyzed by a HPLC system equipped with a Hypsial ODS C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). The liquid chromatography conditions were as follows: The column temperature was set at 35 °C. The detection wavelength is 210 nm. The sample injection volume was 10 μL, and the flow rate was 1.0 mL/min. The mobile phases included A: acetonitrile, B: water. The compounds were determined by gradient elution as follows: 0–10 min, 100 % A; 10–12 min, 100–20 % A; 12–20 min, 20 % A; 20–22 min, 20–100 % A; 22–40 min, 100 % A. The theanine was measured by comparing the area of the peak in the sample to a standard curve made by gradually diluting a theanine standard sample. The other 16 amino acid were evaluated by using an amino acid analyzer equipped with cation exchange chromatography and ninhydrin post-column derivatization (S433D, Sykam, Munich, Germany) according to China National Standard GB 5009.124–2016. The pre-treatment method of the with test samples was referred to the determination of theanine as described above. Subsequent to the filtration of the test solution through a 0.22 μm filter membrane, the filtrate was analyzed. Amino acids were detected at 570 nm and 440 nm, as determined through comparison of retention times and spectra of standard solutions. Quantification was conducted through the utilization of external calibration curves. The experimental data were calculated using three independent biological replicates, each of which included three technical replicates.

UPLC-Q-MS/ MS for metabolomic

The samples were prepared for the metabolic analysis and the data analysis at Shanghai Meiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using standard procedures. Approximately 100 mg of tea sample was placed in a 2 mL centrifuge tube and a 6 mm diameter grinding bead was added. Then 800 μL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1(v: v)) was added to the centrifuged tube to extract the metabolites. Samples were ground using the Wonbio-96c frozen tissue grinder (Shanghai wanbo biotechnology co., LTD) for 6 min (−10 °C, 50 Hz), followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). The samples were left at −20 °C for 30 min, centrifuged for 15 min (4 °C, 13000 g) and the supernatant was transferred to the injection vial for LC-MS/MS analysis.

The LC-MS/MS analysis of metabolites was performed on a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive system equipped with an ACQUITY BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, USA). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1 % formic acid in water: acetonitrile (2:98, v/v) (solvent A) and 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). The metabolites were determined using gradient conditions as follows: 0–0.5 min, mobile phase B was maintained at 2 %; 0.5–7.5 min, mobile phase B was increased from 2 % to 35 %; 7.5–13 min, mobile phase B was increased from 35 % to 95 %; 13–14.4 min, mobile phase B was maintained at 95 %; 14.4–14.5 min, mobile phase B was decreased from 95 % to 2 %; 14.5–16 min, mobile phase B was maintained at 2 %. The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min and the column temperature was 40 °C. The injection volume was 3 μL.

Data analysis was conducted using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://www.majorbio.com/, accessed on 20 February 2025). Model robustness was validated through seven-fold cross-validation. Each treatment was set up with 6 technical replications for metabolomic analysis.

Transcriptome analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the tender tea leaves using RN53-EASYspin Plus kit (Aidlab, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Three independent biological replicates were included for each treatment, with each replicate consisting of a pool of leaves collected from five different plants. High-quality RNA was used for library preparation and sequenced (PE150) on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus system. Raw reads were processed with the default parameters using SeqPrep and Sickle online software, then aligned to the Shuchazao V1 genome (http://tpia.teaplants.cn/download.html, accessed on 15 December 2024) using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2015). The expression level of each transcript was calculated using the Transcripts Per Million (TPM) method, and gene abundances were quantified using RSEM (Li & Dewey, 2011). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using GOATools and SciPy, respectively, with significance thresholds set at Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.05 against the whole-transcriptome background, and results were visualized via heatmaps on the Majorbio Cloud Platform.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

In order to verify the reliability of the gene expression obtained from the transcriptome data, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analyses were performed on a selection of genes. We obtained genes involved in amino acid, caffeine, and catechin synthesis from the TPIA database (http://tpia.teaplants.cn/) and published literature. Expression data for 78 relevant genes were extracted from our transcriptome results. Each gene was assigned a unique ID and sorted by a randomly generated number using the RAND function in Microsoft Excel 2019. To avoid bias, this randomization was performed by an author not involved in the subsequent RT-PCR validation. Finally, 17 genes from key metabolic pathways were randomly selected for expression verification. cDNA synthesis was performed using the HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), with qRT-PCR amplification conducted in 20 μL reactions using the LightCycler® 96 system (Roche, Switzerland). Primers sequences designed using the Premier 3.0 online software (https://primer3.ut.ee/, accessed on 25 February 2025) based on the reference genome of Camellia sinensis cv. Shuchazao genome V1. The CsGAPDH as the internal reference gene. The complete list of primers can be found in Table S1. qPCR amplification was performed under standardized conditions: thermal cycling comprised initial denaturation (95 °C, 1 min) followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C (10 s) and 60 °C (30 s). Three biological replicates (each with triplicate technical runs) were analyzed per gene, with relative expression levels calculated via the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software 19.0 and Microsoft Excel 2019. Student's t-test was used to determine the differences between the different tea samples at a 5 % and 1 % significance level, respectively. The R package ropls Version 1.6.2 (http://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/ropls.html, accessed on 2 March 2025) was used to perform Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Orthogonal Least Partial Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) in metabolomic data analysis. The metabolites with VIP > 1, p < 0.05 were determined as significantly changed metabolites (SCMs) based on the Variable importance in the projeciton (VIP) obtained by the OPLS-DA model and the p-value generated by Student's t-test.

Results and discussion

Effect of shading on the tea leaves and chlorophyll content

To investigate the impact of shading on tea trees, BSN and TPF treatments were implemented on tea plants in the experimental field (Fig. 1A). A marked variation in the appearance of the tea leaves was observed across different treatments (Fig. 1B). In conditions of tea plants with TPF, the appearance of tea leaves exhibited a slight yellowish green, and there was no significant change in chlorophylls when compared to the CK (Fig. 1C). When the tea trees were covered with BSN, the tea leaves exhibited a darker green coloration along with an increase in both chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b, whilst the ratio of chlorophyll a to chlorophyll b decreased significantly compared to CK (Fig. 1C). These results are consistent with the previous studies that found that the greening of shaded leaves is due to an increase in the amount of chlorophyll and the development of the chloroplasts in the leaves (Hu et al., 2024; Yamashita et al., 2021). Effect on biochemical content of tea by shading treatment

As shown in Fig. 2, shaded tea samples showed significantly decreased levels of total polyphenols (TF), eight catechin subtypes, and total catechins, whereas total free amino acids (TAA) and caffeine were markedly elevated relative to the control (CK). These findings align with prior research outcomes (Ji et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2018). Interesting, the study also revealed that the content of C, EC, EGCG, ECG and total catechins in tea samples treated with TPF were significantly downregulated, while levels of amino acids were significantly increased (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the levels of 17 amino acids in differently treated tea samples were quantified. As shown in Fig. S1, BSN treatment was found to significantly increase the levels of L-theanine (L-The), aspartic acid (Asp), threonine (Thr), serine (Ser), glutamic acid (Glu), leucine (Leu), histidine (His), lysine (Lys), and arginine (Arg). Similarly, TPF treatment significantly increased the contents of L-The, Asp, Thr, Ser, Glu, Ala and Arg in tea samples. Therefore, both BSN and TPF treatments can significantly alter the composition and content of amino acids in tea, thereby influencing the quality of tea.Fig. 2Effect of shade treatment on amino acids, caffeine and catechins in tea (C. sinensis cv. It is interesting to consider why tea plants covered with plastic film exhibit increased amino acid content and reduced catechin levels in their leaves. The underlying reason may be that the application of transparent plastic film on tea plant leaves can effectively enhance the temperature and humidity within the microenvironment (de Almeida et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024). This alteration promotes the activity of enzymes involved in nitrogen absorption and amino acid synthesis, thereby facilitating an increase in amino acid production. Furthermore, ultraviolet radiation, particularly UV-B, serves as a stress signal for plants, prompting them to synthesize greater amounts of tea polyphenols (notably catechins) as a protective response against potential damage (Zhou et al., 2025). The use of transparent plastic film can significantly filter out most ultraviolet rays, which consequently results in a decrease in catechin content.

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis

To investigate the effect of shading on the non-volatile metabolites of tea plants, non-targeted metabolic profiling was conducted using UHPLC-Q Exactive MS. Metabolomic analysis annotated 1582 volatile metabolites, comprising major classes: 262 flavonoids, 196 terpenoids, 191 lipids, 114 carbohydrates, 105 phenolic acids, 98 amino acids, 59 organic acids and other metabolites (Fig. S2A). The PCA result of the metabolomic components of the different shade treatments revealed that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 34.90 % and 20.90 % of total variance, respectively (Fig. S2B). Score plot of PCA demonstrated the capacity to distinctly differentiate the three groups of samples, thereby indicating significant alterations in the metabolites of tea leaves after shading. The centralization of all QC samples in the middle of the PCA plots indicates the stability of the instrument during data acquisition and demonstrates its reliability and repeatability in this work.

Based on OPLS-DA with the criteria of VIP ≥ 1, fold change ≥1 and p < 0.05, 459 and 433 significantly changed metabolites (SCMs) were identified in tea leaves under different shade treatments, respectively (Table S2, Table S3). As showed in Fig. S3A, the number of SCMs upregulated and downregulated in CK versus BSN and CK versus TPF were 264 and 195, 226 and 207, respectively. Key metabolite abundance changes were further visualized through a heatmap (Fig. S3B). As shown in Fig. S3B, BSN samples shown significantly elevated levels of nearly all analyzed amino acids relative to the control (CK), encompassing l-glutamine, γ-glutamylphenylalanine, L-histidine, L-theanine, L-aspartic acid, 2-oxoarginine, and N2-acetylornithine. TPF-treated samples also exhibited a substantial elevation in amino acid abundance compared with control groups. These results are consistent with those detected in the biochemical component analysis. In addition, we found that shading treatment significantly reduced the levels of catechins (e.g., gallocatechin and epicatechin) and their methylated derivatives (e.g., epicatechin 3-O-gallate and epigallocatechin-(4β → 8)-epicatechin 3-O-gallate) compared to the control group (Fig. S3B). Most flavonoids, such as quercetin glycosides (Q-glycosides), myricetin glycosides (M-glycosides), and kaempferol glycosides (K-glycosides), also exhibited marked decreases under shading conditions. This phenomenon has also been widely discovered in previous studies (Li et al., 2020; Li et al., 2025). Catechins and caffeine primarily contribute to tea's bitter and astringent taste, whereas amino acids enhance its umami taste of tea (Prasanth et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2018). Flavonol glycosides have a synergistic enhancing effect on the bitterness of caffeine (Scharbert & Hofmann, 2005). Therefore, the improved quality of shaded green tea could be primarily due to a reduction in catechins and flavonol glycosides and an increase in amino acids.

Studies have revealed that aroma compounds in tea exist both in free form and as glycosidically bound precursors (Chen et al., 2019). Specific alcohols (e.g., cis-3-hexenol, benzyl alcohol, phenylethanol, and linalool) form glycosidic conjugates with sugars such as primeverose and glucose. Notably, shaded tea leaves in this study exhibited a marked increase in aroma glycosides, including linalool 3,6-oxide primeveroside and cis-3-hexenyl β-primeveroside (Table S2). Furthermore, lipids serve as primary precursors for aromatic volatiles, which undergo oxidative degradation pathways to generate diverse odor-active compounds. As shown in Fig. S3B, BSN treatment significantly elevated the levels of most lipids and lipid-derived metabolites in tea samples, including key oxylipins such as 13-L-hydroperoxylinoleic acid, 2-hydroxylinolenic acid, and 7-epijasmonic acid. Thus, alterations in these aroma precursor levels may enhance tea aroma quality under shading conditions.

Transcriptomic analysis and qPCR validation

As shown in Fig. S4A, there was clear delineation between the experimental groups, with tight clustering of biological replicates. Transcriptomic profiles are delineated in Fig. S4B through hierarchical clustering analysis, with differential expression patterns of different genes among the individual samples being demonstrated by heatmap color gradients. Fig. S4C listed differently expressed genes (DEGs) under different treatments, with absolute log2 (fold change) ≥1 and p value <0.05 as the critical values. There were 662 (302 upregulated and 360 downregulated) and 1670 (664 upregulated and 1006 downregulated) DEGs in BSN versus CK and TPF versus CK, respectively (Table S4 and Table S5). Compared to the BSN treatment, the TPF treatment exerts a more pronounced regulatory effect on gene expression in tea plant shoots, considering that more DEGs were observed in the comparison between CK and BSN than in that between CK and TPF.

To validate transcriptomic data reliability, qRT-PCR verification was performed on selected candidate genes. Seventeen DEGs were randomly selected from for qRT-PCR validation from some pivotal metabolic pathways, including catechin biosynthesis-related genes (TEA003790, TEA009266, TEA027582, TEA026458, TEA010328, TEA024762, TEA023829, TEA033031, TEA014864, TEA023243, TEA003137), theanine biosynthesis-related genes (TEA032123, TEA031206, TEA003892), caffeine biosynthesis-related genes (TEA006735) and other genes (TEA000344, TEA000238). Among 17 candidate genes validated by qRT-PCR, 15 exhibited expression patterns concordant with RNA-seq data across all experimental groups, while two genes (TEA003892 and TEA014864) diverged specifically in the TPF treatment group. Changes in expression of genes involved in amino acid, caffeine and catechin synthesis pathways in tea plants after shading treatment

Amino acids, caffeine and catechins are important secondary metabolites in tea plants. The expression changes of related genes under shading conditions are complex and involve the regulation of multiple metabolic pathways. The biosynthetic network of theanine and other associated amino acids is illustrated in Fig. 4A. In this study, we identified 26 key genes involved in the amino acid biosynthesis pathway from the transcriptome data (Fig. 4B). Notably, shading treatment induced upregulation of critical genes in the theanine biosynthetic pathway, particularly those encoding rate-limiting enzymes: theanine synthetase (TS), glutamine synthetase (GS), glutamate synthase and glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH). Conversely, principal regulatory genes in the shikimate pathway demonstrated pronounced downregulation compared to control conditions, including D-3-phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (DHAPS-1), 3-dehydroquinate synthase (DHQS), 3-dehydroquinate dehydratase (DHQ), shikimate kinase (SK) and 5-enolpyruvylshikimate 3-phosphate synthase (EPSPS). In addition, genes encoding phosphoenolpyruvate kinase (PEPK) and D-3-phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase 2 (PGDH2) showed upregulation under shading conditions. Chen et al. (2017) study found that the three key enzymes (DHAPS-1, GAPCP1, and PGDH2) in amino acid synthesis pathways were significantly downregulated in tea plants after dark treatment, and suggested that chloroplast proteolysis was responsible for the elevated free amino acid content after shading. In this research, the expression of genes involved in the amino acid synthesis pathways associated with the shikimate pathway also exhibited a downward trend. Notably, these amino acids only constitute a small proportion of the total free amino acids present in tea plants. As a non-proteinogenic amino acid, theanine constitutes 40–70 % of the total free amino acids in tea plants (Juneja et al., 1999). Moreover, studies have found that amino acid permease (AAP) transporters mediate theanine transport and facilitate its root-to-shoot translocation in tea plants (Dong et al., 2020). In this study, transcriptomic profiling (Table S6) revealed significant upregulation of three amino acid permease (AAP) family genes (CsAAP1, CsAAP2 and CsAAP4) in shaded samples compared with controls. In addition, we found that the expression of several genes (TEA028537, TEA026779, TEA026779) related to chloroplast (or precursor) synthesis significantly increased after shading treatment (Table S6). Thus, the accumulation of free amino acids in shaded tea plants observed in this study is primarily due to enhanced biosynthesis—particularly of theanine—rather than chloroplast protein degradation. In summary, the reasons for this controversy may be multifaceted, such as differences in the duration of shading treatments and inherent variations among tea cultivars or others factors, all of which require further verified.Fig. 4The amino acid biosynthesis pathway and related gene expression in tea plants. Catechins are predominantly synthesized via the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthetic pathways. The phenylpropanoid pathway enzymatically converts phenylalanine to naringenin through sequential catalysis by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H), 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL), chalcone synthase (CHS), and chalcone isomerase (CHI), which is subsequently channeled into the flavonoid pathway for catechin biosynthesis via coordinated actions of flavanone 3′-hydroxylase (F3’H), flavonoid 3′,5′-hydroxylase (F3’5′H), dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR), flavonol synthase (FLS), leucoanthocyanidin reductase (LAR), anthocyanidin synthase (ANS), and anthocyanidin reductase (ANR). Subsequently, these catechins were further catalyzed by SCPL1A into gallate catechins (Fig. 6A). Transcriptomic profiling revealed the involvement of 26 key genes in the phenylalanine biosynthesis pathway. Specifically, 7 genes were annotated as PAL, 4 as C4H, 4 as 4CL, 7 as CHS, and 4 as CHI. Among these genes, most exhibited significantly downregulated expression levels in response to shading treatment, with the exception of the PAL (TEA003137, TEA003374) and 4CL (TEA025906, TEA027829) genes (Fig. 6B). In terms of the flavonoid pathway, 43 genes were detected from transcriptomic data, including F3’H (3), F3′5′H (2), DFR (6), FLS (3), LAR (2), ANS (2), ANR (2), and SCPL (23). Consistently, most analyzed genes showed downregulation under shading conditions. Biochemical analysis results showed that the content of catechins and their components in tea plant leaves significantly decreased after shading treatment (Fig. 2), which was consistent with the observed trend of gene expression. These findings align with earlier reports on shading-induced suppression of phenylpropanoid/flavonoid pathway genes (Liu et al., 2018; Zhang, Liu, et al., 2020). Notably, tea cultivars demonstrated species-specific shading responses, resulting in divergent expression patterns of pathway-related genes (Li et al., 2020; Li et al., 2025).Fig. 6The catechin biosynthesis pathway and related gene expression in tea plants. The regulatory mechanism by which shading induces the down-regulation of genes related to the biosynthesis of flavonoids and catechins in tea plant leaves remains unclear. Previous studies have shown that the biosynthesis of flavonoids and catechins in tea plants is mainly transmitted to the downstream core effector MYB12 through the UVR8-mediated signaling pathway (Liu et al., 2018). The research also found that the transcription factor CsPIF3 can be activated under shading conditions, and CsPIF3 can promote the expression of transcription inhibitors CsMYB4 and CsMYB7, thereby regulating the expression of downstream genes related to catechin synthesis (Zhao et al., 2021). Furthermore, recent studies have found that CsMYB1 can form the MBW complex with other regulatory factors, thereby regulates catechin biosynthesis genes (Li et al., 2022). This study, transcriptome data (Table S6) indicated that the expression of genes associated with the light signaling pathway, specifically UVR8 and COP1, as well as the transcription factors HY5, CsMYB1 and CsMYB12 were significantly down-regulated under shading conditions. In contrast, CsPIF3 exhibited a significant up-regulation. Furthermore, the transcriptional repressors CsMYB4 and CsMYB7 were also found to be up-regulated, with CsMYB7 demonstrating a notable increase (P < 0.05). Thus, the molecular mechanisms of reduced catechin content in shaded tea plants may involve three key pathways: (1) MYB12 as a coregulatory factor, the expression of upstream light signaling genes (UVR8, COP1) was downregulated under shading conditions, consequently reducing the transcript levels of their downstream regulators HY5 and MYB12. This response restricts the promotion of MYB12 and suppresses downstream responsive genes (FLS, CHS, and F3’H, etc.) within the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, thereby ultimately reducing flavonoid biosynthesis. (2) CsPIF3 upregulation induces transcriptional repressors (CsMYB4 and CsMYB7) in the flavonoid pathway under shade conditions, thereby downregulating catechin biosynthetic genes and ultimately suppressing catechin production. (3) The expression of CsMYB1 was down-regulated under shade conditions, and CsMYB1 interacted with other proteins to form the MBW complex, thereby down-regulated catechin biosynthesis genes.

In summary, we propose a work model that employs shading treatment to enhance the accumulation of free amino acids and caffeine while concurrently reducing the levels of catechins in tea plants (Fig. 7). Although our research has initially established a correlation between gene expression and the accumulation of amino acids and caffeine, as well as clarifying the regulatory relationship between catechin reduction and upstream transcription factors, further functional verification is needed to determine the causal relationship. For example, this could be achieved using gene knockout/overexpression. Conclusions

This study investigated the regulatory mechanism by which shading treatment increased the levels of free amino acids and caffeine, while reducing the levels of catechins in tea plant. We here demonstrated that the accumulation of free amino acids in shaded tea plants is primarily due to enhanced biosynthesis of theanine, rather than protein degradation. Shading promotes the cooperative regulation of light signaling components (e.g. UVR8, HY5 and COP1) and three key transcription factors (CsMYB1, CsMYB12 and CsPIF3) to reduce the expression of genes involved in catechin biosynthesis. Furthermore, shading enhanced the expression of genes associated with theanine transporters. Finally, we proposed a working model of shading treatment to increase the accumulation of free amino acids and caffeine while reducing the levels of catechins in tea plants. These findings provide valuable insights into the mechanisms of key metabolite changes in tea plants caused by shading and offer a theoretical basis for optimizing shading protocols in precision tea production.
