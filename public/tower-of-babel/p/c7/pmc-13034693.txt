Congenital myelomeningocele containing two hemicords with placodes in a patient with complex split spinal cord malformation: illustrative case
Authors: Ulf Nestler, Tsirimalala Rakotomaro, Audrey Dupont, Mathilde Mayerus, Florence Casagrande, Aurélie Occelli, Nathalie Chivoret
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDThe unified theory of split spinal cord malformation (SCM) comprises type I, with each one of the hemicords in one own dural tube, separated by a bone spur, and type II, with two hemicords in one dural tube, separated by a nonrigid septum. This classification is still evolving, with denominations SCM type 0.5 proposed for incomplete forms, SCM type 1.5 for lesions combining features of SCM types I and II, and SCM type III for the composite lesions occurring on different spinal levels.OBSERVATIONSA newborn male patient presented with a complex SCM with two placodes, one in each hemicord, contained in one myelomeningocele (MMC) sac. In addition, caudal regression of the lumbosacral spine, hydrocephalus, and herniation of cerebellar tonsils down to thoracic levels were detected.LESSONSThis complex case of two asymmetrical nonneurulated hemicords attached to a single MMC sac, opening on different levels of the bone spur and on the right side covered by a rudimentary hemilamina, illustrates the difficulties in classifying SCMs. Since up to 15% of MMC patients additionally have SCM, the case description underlines the role of preoperative MRI in newborns before neurosurgical MMC closure. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE251036 Keywords: Chiari II, diastematomyelia, myelomeningocele, split spinal cord malformationABBREVIATIONS: MMC = myelomeningocele, SCM = split spinal cord malformation, SDS = standard deviation score

In most countries with a health system supported by strong financial resources, detection of a myelomeningocele (MMC) as late as at birth has become a rare event. In these cases, closure of the MMC in the first 48–72 hours after birth is advised.1 If not impeded by the clinical situation of the newborn, MRI diagnostics in view of a Chiari type II malformation with the triad of hydrocephalus, cerebellar tonsil herniation, and MMC should be performed preoperatively. Visualization of the contents and structures of the MMC can be crucial for neurosurgical planning.2

The prevalence of congenital MMC is esteemed to be 0.8–1 per 1000 live births; the combination of MMC with a split spinal cord malformation (SCM) seems to occur in 6%–15% of MMC cases.2–4 In 70%–85% of patients, the division of the cord is found at the level of the MMC, or one to two vertebral levels higher; in about 10%–30%, the SCM is lying distally.3,4 The combination of MMC with SCM has been termed “complex SCM,” and the occurrence of two SCMs on different vertebral levels is described as “composite SCM.”5

In view of embryological considerations, together with clinical findings, a classification consisting of two types of SCM was proposed by Pang in 1992.6 SCM type I describes the presence of two hemicords, each with its own dural tube, and the dural sheets separated by a rigid bony septum. SCM type II features two hemicords in one dural tube, separated by a nonrigid septum.

The detection of complex and composite SCMs reveals the possible existence of a lesion continuum, depending on the time point and the extent of embryological damage, ranging from occult dysraphism to open encephalocele.7 Accordingly, current classification overviews include entities such as SCM type 0.5 for incomplete forms, SCM type 1.5 for lesions displaying characteristics of types I and II in close vicinity to the same vertebral level, and SCM type III for composite (tandem) lesions on different levels.7,8

Although the combination of diastematomyelia with MMC has been well described, there remain a number of cases, especially newborns, in which preoperative MRI is not or cannot be performed.3 SCM is then only discovered during MMC surgery, which is apt to complicate the neurosurgical intervention and requires meticulous attention by the neurosurgeon. This can result in the need for a second untethering operation later in life, addressing the split cord.2–4 According to the literature, detailed diagnosis and subsequent simultaneous neurosurgical repair of SCM and MMC in the immediate neonatal period are only accomplished in about 14% of newborns who have a combination of both pathologies.2,4,9

Sophisticated prenatal screening and promising intrauterine neurosurgical techniques are presently not available worldwide. When available, the possibility of in utero fetal surgery and fetoscopic intrauterine repair leads to decreasing numbers of newborn patients with MMC that have to be treated postnatally by neurosurgeons.10,11 To raise awareness for SCM in congenital MMC lesions, and to add detail to the ongoing discussion about SCM classification, we describe the case of a complex SCM with doubled, asymmetric placodes and distal reunification of the hemicords below the bone spur.

Illustrative Case

Given the context of pregnancy denial, the infant did not have any prenatal care or visits and was born without medical assistance in a nonhospital surrounding, presumably at week 38. The attending paramedics reported Apgar values of 1-5-10 and discovered the lumbar MMC with paraplegia and malformation of the legs (Fig. 1). Adaptation to extrauterine life remained stable without signs of respiratory distress.

Preoperative Findings

Clinical examination disclosed a large, smooth anterior fontanel without bulging. The pupils were small, with good reaction to light. On spontaneous opening of the eyes, no upward gaze palsy was observed. Innervation of facial muscles was symmetrical, and the sucking reflex was present. No swallowing disorder or respiratory distress was disclosed. An axial hypotonia was found. The movements of the upper limbs were symmetrical and strong, with good reaction to stimulation and present grasp reflexes.

Paraplegia was detected beneath the lower thoracic levels, with spastic flexion of the hips and knees, suspicion of hip subluxation, and bilateral club foot deformity. The Babinski sign was absent bilaterally. Micturition and defecation of small amounts of meconium were seen, but an atonic anal sphincter was observed.

Transfontanellar ultrasound depicted ventricular enlargement and a thin callosal body. To further assess the extension of the MMC and to describe the anomalies regarding a Chiari type II malformation, MRI was performed.

Spinal imaging showed an SCM, with two hemicords in one dural sleeve at T8 (Fig. 2). The hemicords were separated by a bone spur at L1–2, and each hemicord was suspected to form a placode. The placodes seemed to be asymmetrically attached to one unique MMC sac with a craniocaudal extension of 37 mm (Fig. 3).

Sagittal (A) and axial (B) T2-weighted MR images. The blue line depicts T11, visualized in panel B. The arrow points to the right hemicord with its origin at T8, and the arrowhead depicts the left placode structure adhering to the meningocele sac.

Parasagittal (A) and axial (B) T2-weighted MR images. The blue line depicts L2, visualized in panel B. The arrows indicate the placode of the right hemicord, and the arrowhead the left hemicord. The star indicates the triangular median bone spur.

Cranial imaging disclosed ventriculomegaly and the descent of cerebellar tissue down to T1 (Fig. 4). As suspected ultrasonographically, ventricular enlargement and a partial callosal agenesia were confirmed. Transependymal edema, hemorrhage, and ischemia were not detected. A 5-mm heterotopic nodule of the lateral wall of the left ventricle and a small posterior fossa were described.

Sagittal T2-weighted MR images showing the crowded posterior fossa and ventriculomegaly (A) and herniation of the cerebellar tonsils (B). The arrows indicate T1.

Whereas distal sacral agenesia was encountered, no associated lesions such as a syrinx, lipoma, dermoid, teratoma, or hemangioma were detected. Apart from discrete hypertrichosis around the myelocele, no cutaneous signs of SCM were found (Fig. 1).

After multidisciplinary discussion of the case concerning prognosis, long-term sequelae, and follow-up, we decided to proceed with neurosurgical closure of the MMC in the first 24 hours of life to prevent infection.

Intraoperative Findings

During surgery, duplication of the placodes was encountered, as confirmed by visualization on MRI (Fig. 5). Under the view of the surgical microscope, the placodes were freed from their attachment to the skin, and the interposed fibroarachnoid adhesions were removed. The proximal spinal canal with one dural tube and two hemicords was inspected (Fig. 5A). While the left hemicord formed a flattened placode, covering a bone spur, the right hemicord passed underneath a bony arch, forming a smaller placode lateral to the bone spur. The right hemicord then joined with the left side below the osteophyte. The monochord reentered distally into the remnants of the caudal spinal canal.

B:The dissecting spatula lies underneath the right hemicord, which is covered by a rudimentary bone lamina. C: After partial resection of the osseus structures, the distal reunification of the two hemicords becomes visible. D:The rudimentary hemilamina has been removed, and the surface of the bone spur is partially reduced. The blue arrow indicates the ventral dura with a slit-like opening proximal to the osteophyte. With the patient head to the left, the white arrows indicate the placode of the right hemicord, the arrowheads the left placode, and the star the bone spur.

After resection of the osseus covering of the right hemicord, the median bone spur was removed, to form a common spinal canal (Fig. 5D). Ventral dural rims were sutured, and watertight closure was achieved with interposed pieces of fibrin glue–coated collagen. During manipulation and coagulation, small abortive movements of the hip and thigh musculature were observed.

No tubularizing maneuver was performed, in order to avoid additional manipulation of neural tissue and prevent subsequent tethering of arachnoidal knots to the reinforced ventral or dorsal dural sutures. The hemicords were separated from arachnoidal adhesions while preserving the vascular supply of each side, and were positioned parallel to each other in the reconstructed dural sleeve. The newly formed spinal canal was filled with physiological saline.

Dorsal closure of the dural tube was achieved by mobilizing the lumbar fascia bilaterally and folding it over in continuity with the proximal and distal opening of the spinal canal. Sutures were secured with interposed fibrin glue–coated collagen pieces, as well as one large covering piece.

The skin was mobilized by subcutaneous dissection, and the incision was prolonged cranially, allowing for a nearly linear, tension-free wound closure (Fig. 1C).

The patient recovered well from the neurosurgical intervention. He displayed neither respiratory problems nor failure to thrive. Movements of the head and the upper extremities were unrestricted, with good contact with caregivers during the awake phases. Pupils were small, with prompt reaction to light, and the anterior fontanel did not bulge. The lower extremities remained plegic, in flexion position, but during manipulation weak movements of the hip muscles were observed. No sphincter tonus was achieved, and the patient developed an anorectal prolapse.

On postoperative day 6, a subcutaneous lumbar fluid collection appeared, with bulging during crying phases, but without wound dehiscence or secretion. Under conservative treatment with more frequent positioning on the back, the collection was resorbed and disappeared by day 14. In the following weeks, an increasing head circumference (1 cm/wk) became obvious, accompanied by bulging fontanels and a vertical gaze palsy.

Finally, a ventriculoperitoneal shunt was implanted 39 days after myelocele closure. Orthopedic treatment of the legs with a harness system was started, and surgical intervention for inguinal hernia was performed at the age of 2 months. Abdominal ultrasonography did not reveal urinary tract malformations. Systematic long-term follow-up in a specialized multidisciplinary rehabilitation center has been started.

Cerebral MRI at 4 months disclosed large ventricles and enlarged subdural spaces, without signs of increased intracranial pressure. The ascent of cerebellar tonsils to C5, together with a flow void signal in the mesencephalic aqueduct, was noted. The head circumference, corresponding to a standard deviation score (SDS) of +2.5 (40.5 cm) at 1 month, had lowered to an SDS of +1.63 (42 cm) at 3 months and 1.09 (43.5 cm) at 5 months.

Discussion

The present report describes the case of a newborn male with SCM, with two hemicords in one dural sleeve at T8, separated by a bone spur at L1–2, and each hemicord with a placode, asymmetrically attached to one unique MMC sac. The clinical picture corresponds to a complex SCM type 1.5.

This is a rare finding since, among the 11 original SCM type 1.5 cases, there was no newborn child with MMC.7 Accordingly, case series dealing with the simultaneous occurrence of SCM and MMC report that most children were treated for the SCM later than in the immediate neonatal phase.2–4,9

Observations

Two hemicord placodes adhering to a unique MMC structure have not been explicitly described in the literature. Little information regarding the occurrence and distribution of nonneurulated hemicords is available. Ansari et al. reported on 4 of 33 patients with hemi-MMC, and Kumar et al. describe that in 4 of 12 patients with SCM type I no placode was found.2,3 Schijman found that of 7 patients, 3 had a hemi-MMC and 1 no placode.5

In the study by Iskandar et al., 6 of 20 patients had an MMC and SCM on one vertebral level, all 6 with an MMC of only one hemicord.4 Higashida et al. reported on 2 patients with involvement of one (the right) hemicord, and a third with the diastema occurring below the level of the MMC.9

The patient presented here had complete paraplegia of the lower limbs, left-sided hip subluxation, bent leg position, and bilateral equinovarus feet, necessitating orthopedic treatment by a Pavlik harness. In older MMC patients, asymmetric hemicords or nonneurulation in only one hemicord can lead to asymmetric weakness of the lower extremities. This observation should then prompt diagnostic workup in view of an additional SCM.2,3

The occurrence of oblique or dorsal bony septa, together with asymmetric division of hemicords, can be technically demanding and needs special neurosurgical attention.6,11 Similar to the intraoperative findings shown here, with the diastematomyelia originating at T8, 1 case (of 16) was described by Kumar et al. with a split cord on a thoracic middorsal level and a bone-encircled smaller hemicord on one side (Fig. 5).2

In the present case, preoperative visualization by MRI prompted anesthetic and technical preparation for an enlarged skin incision and extensive bone removal with rongeurs and high-speed drills. Special care was taken given the risk of increased blood loss and prolonged drainage of CSF, compared to patients with a single MMC repair. During surgery, ventral neural structures, placode connections, and the independent blood supply of each hemicord were respected.

Most authors favor simultaneous MMC repair with SCM untethering in one neurosurgical sitting whenever clinically feasible.2–4 This is to avoid slow deterioration of neurological functions, which can remain undetected and subsequently lead to back pain with scoliosis, paresthesia, and lower limb weakness.4,8 Muscular atrophy, trophic ulcers, pes equinovarus, and urinary incontinence indicate severe deficits.2,3,11 A second neurosurgical intervention, sometimes after years, then has to be performed.4,5 However, postponed simultaneous neurosurgical intervention to repair both lesions has been reported, not earlier than 1 month after birth to lower perioperative risks in the newborn.2

In the present case, untethering combined with resection of the bone spur and reconstruction of a common dural sleeve was undertaken in view of the severe congenital neurological deficit and in order to avoid subsequent surgery. Nevertheless, there are reports describing the safe handling of complex SCM cases with primary MMC repair in the first 48 hours of life, followed by delayed SCM untethering after stabilization of the clinical status of pediatric patients 8–18 months after birth.9

The preoperative MRI assessment revealed concomitant ventriculomegaly with descent of the cerebellar tonsils, which corresponds to the complete clinical triad of Chiari type II malformation. Subsequently, a ventriculoperitoneal shunt system had to be placed. Whereas the prevalence of hydrocephalus in MMC patients has been found to be up to 75%, the reported rate of hydrocephalus in complex SCM lesions is lower.12

In their study, Kumar et al. mention 2 of 16 patients (12.5%) with hydrocephalus, Higashida et al. describe 1 of 3 patients (33%).2,9 On the one hand, the prevalence of hydrocephalus in patients with combined SCM and MMC might be lower than that in patients with MMC alone. Hypothetically, this can be due to altered CSF dynamics on the level of the bifurcation compared to a monochord situation. On the other hand, the combination of hydrocephalus with complex SCM constitutes a severe developmental aberration, presumably leading to premature intrauterine death in a certain number of cases, and thus obscuring the actual prevalence rate.

Lessons

SCM in combination with open MMC challenges the unified theory of SCM, given the doubled frequency of possible neurulation mistakes and repair mechanisms. A continuum of aberrations can arise, and the most frequent aberrations have recently led to the definition of new SCM subtypes. On postnatal detection of an MMC, detailed diagnostic cerebrospinal MRI is crucial to discover further lesions. In cases of a complex SCM, neurosurgical repair of the MMC and untethering of the SCM in one intervention is advocated.
