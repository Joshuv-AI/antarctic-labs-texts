GNN enhanced reinforcement learning for robot navigation in complex topological networks
Authors: Suo Zhang, Xuelin DU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the challenges encountered by intelligent robots in perceiving high-dimensional environmental states and making adaptive trajectory planning decisions in complex topological environments, this paper presents a Graph Neural Network–Reinforcement Learning (GNN-RL) integrated framework, implemented based on the Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm for continuous control tasks. First, leveraging the topological modeling capability of GNNs, environmental entities are abstracted into graph nodes, and their spatial constraints and semantic associations are encoded as edge features. Through multi-layer graph convolution and adaptive edge weighting, high-dimensional structured environmental information is compressed into low-dimensional node-level and graph-level embeddings with rich topological semantics. This provides structured environmental cognition for the subsequent reinforcement learning module, alleviating the curse of dimensionality and enabling efficient action selection. Second, a dynamic collaborative mechanism between the GNN encoder and the SAC-based RL agent is established. The topological features extracted by the GNN are fed as input to the RL agent, which consists of twin Q-networks, a policy network, and a value network. A multi-objective reward function, which integrates safety, progress, and motion smoothness, guides the agent’s trial-and-error exploration. In this manner, static topological representations are transformed into dynamic trajectory policies, while the GNN parameters are jointly optimized end-to-end via the gradient signals from the RL loss function, overcoming the limitations of purely static graph learning. Finally, comprehensive comparative experiments are conducted in simulated complex topological environments, evaluating the proposed GNN-RL approach against DQN, PPO, and A* algorithms. The results show that the GNN-RL method achieves a favorable balance between perception accuracy and decision-making efficiency, providing a reliable and adaptive solution for robot navigation and trajectory planning in structured, dynamic environments.

Introduction

With the advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) and computing technologies, intelligent robots1 have been increasingly deployed in diverse fields, including industrial manufacturing, logistics and warehousing, the service industry, and disaster response. In these real-world applications, the variability and complexity of topological environments place ever more stringent requirements on the trajectory planning of intelligent robots. These robots must not only ensure reachability from the start point to the end point but also maintain a balance between safety, efficiency, and adaptability throughout their movement. For example, in industrial workshops, narrow passages demand high-precision steering control during robot operation. In logistics warehouses, densely arranged shelves require robots to update their path trajectories in real time to avoid collisions and operational conflicts. At disaster sites, collapsed structures and unknown obstacles necessitate trajectories with dynamic adjustment capabilities, enabling stable navigation in unstructured environments.

For trajectory planning of intelligent robots in complex environments, researchers have increasingly focused on intelligent optimization algorithms, with the aim of enhancing both the global optimality of trajectories and the real-time responsiveness of the system. For example, to improve path planning efficiency, an adaptive weighting mechanism was introduced into the heuristic function of the A* algorithm, combined with the integration of parent node concepts2. This approach narrows the node search range of the A* algorithm, thereby reducing the number of search nodes and computational time. In another study3, the Rapidly Exploring Random Tree (RRT) strategy was adopted, which uses random sampling to incrementally approximate the solution space, enabling rapid identification of a feasible path and reducing the computational load during 2D robot navigation. Additionally, based on grid environment modeling, a relaxed Dijkstra algorithm was proposed in4 to address the real-time path planning problem for mobile robots in large-scale, obstacle-dense environments.

However, these methods are typically designed and optimized for specific scenarios, and they exhibit limited generalization capability when applied to unstructured or dynamic environments, making direct transfer challenging. Although the method in2 improves real-time performance by constraining the search range, this improvement is achieved at the cost of sacrificing a certain degree of path optimality. Moreover, balancing optimality and real-time performance remains a persistent challenge; in environments with complex obstacle layouts, path optimization effectiveness may decrease or real-time performance may degrade. The approaches presented in3 and4 are primarily designed for low-dimensional spaces; when applied to high-dimensional spaces, the dramatic expansion of the search space can significantly reduce computational efficiency, thus undermining the real-time advantages of these algorithms. Furthermore, all three methods primarily address static obstacle environments, with no explicit mechanisms for dynamic obstacle perception or real-time path adjustment. Therefore, research on intelligent robot trajectory optimization methods suitable for complex topological environments holds significant theoretical significance and practical application value.

In recent years, advances in neural network and RL technologies have provided new approaches for intelligent decision-making in robotics. Owing to their robust feature extraction and pattern-fitting capabilities, neural networks can learn environmental representations directly from high-dimensional perceptual data, enabling robots to adapt more flexibly to unstructured environments. For example, in5, trajectory optimization for an intelligent medicine-delivery robot was realized by integrating a CNN with the Dijkstra algorithm to identify the shortest path. In6, a 3D-CNN was employed to extract spatial features from real-world scenarios, while a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model captured spatiotemporal dynamics to predict the motion of dynamic obstacles. Furthermore, a VGG19-based defect-detection CNN model was proposed in7, which can be systematically integrated into multi-robot control systems to perform workpiece inspection tasks.

However, certain limitations persist in these approaches. In5, the deployed CNN model is relatively simple, which restricts the accuracy of digital recognition particularly in ward environments characterized by variable lighting, blurred digits, or obstructions. In6, processing high-dimensional data using a 3D-CNN leads to an exponential increase in parameters and computational load, which elevates system pressure and causes delays in obstacle trajectory prediction, thereby increasing collision risks. In7, the deep VGG19 architecture results in a large parameter set and prolonged forward computation time. This delay is particularly pronounced in multi-robot collaboration scenarios that require simultaneous multi-view defect detection. Overall, when CNN models are applied to robot trajectory optimization, their generalization performance relies heavily on large-scale annotated datasets, and they still exhibit limited capability in capturing features for dynamic environments or fine-grained tasks in non-standard scenarios.

GNNs8 offer unique advantages in modeling complex topological environments. They can represent obstacles, robot positions, target points, and their relational edges as graph-structured data, then learn environmental topological features through information propagation and aggregation among nodes. Consequently, some researchers have utilized GNNs to assist in robot trajectory planning. For instance, in9, GNNs were employed to provide structured representations for wind farm layout optimization, enhancing the expressiveness and search accuracy of subsequent algorithms. Nevertheless, literature focusing on the specific application of GNNs to intelligent robot trajectory optimization remains limited. Although GNNs can abstract key environmental elements into nodes and edges, accurate modeling of complex settings is critical for trajectory optimization. When new nodes or edges emerge in the graph, GNNs typically require retraining, which limits their real-time adaptability in dynamically changing environments.

In contrast, RL10 supports autonomous exploration and trial-and-error learning, enabling robots to identify optimal trajectories in complex, dynamic, or unknown environments while balancing multiple objectives such as safety, efficiency, and smoothness. As a result, numerous scholars have applied RL to robot trajectory optimization and navigation tasks. For example, an accelerated RL algorithm based on learned experience replay was proposed in11 to improve the efficiency of path planning. Another study12 developed a local policy-based RL approach, which enhances real-time responsiveness by reducing the computational burden of global path search.

However, limitations persist in traditional RL-based trajectory planning methods. For instance, the experience replay in11 reuses historical samples but lacks adaptive updates, making it difficult to promptly respond to dynamic changes in environmental topology. The local policy design in12 focuses primarily on obstacle avoidance, with insufficient consideration of the coordination between local decision-making and global path optimality. Additionally, the path planning method in13 relies heavily on pre-defined environmental models, exhibiting limited generalization when faced with unforeseen changes in the environment (e.g., sudden obstacles or topological adjustments). These limitations highlight the need for a more robust framework that can adapt to complex and dynamic scenarios.

To address these issues,an intelligent robot trajectory optimization method based on GNN-RL fusion is investigated in this paper . Specifically, the RL agent in this method is built on the SAC algorithm, which serves as the fundamental Actor-Critic RL backbone to ensure the stability and effectiveness of the RL-based decision-making process. First, GNNs convert node relationships, topological constraints, and global features in complex environments into interpretable low-dimensional representations, providing the SAC-based RL agent with accurate environmental perception and overcoming the difficulty of optimal action selection in high-dimensional structured states. Second, the SAC-based RL introduces dynamic decision-making through trial-and-error mechanisms, transforming the static features extracted by GNNs into goal-driven optimization strategies, thereby compensating for GNNs’ lack of autonomous learning and dynamic adaptability.

Experimental results indicate that this design effectively improves the generalization ability and trajectory optimization performance of the model in dynamic and complex topological scenarios.

The GNN—RL framework design

In the task of intelligent robot trajectory optimization, complex topological environments pose two key challenges to robot trajectory optimization. First, complex topological environments contain information such as relationships between numerous nodes and topological constraints, which constitute high-dimensional structured states. However, traditional reinforcement learning strategies cannot accurately perceive the dynamics of environmental changes, nor can they effectively optimize decision-making strategies. Second, the static feature extraction method relying solely on GNNs lacks dynamic decision-making and autonomous learning capabilities, which prevents it from adapting to changes in complex topological environments.

To address the above problems, the core of the GNN-RL framework designed in this paper lies in the collaborative complementarity of these two methods. The reinforcement learning module is constructed based on the SAC algorithm, an advanced actor-critic architecture suitable for continuous control tasks. Specifically, the graph structure feature extraction capability of GNNs is utilized to resolve the environmental perception dilemma of reinforcement learning, while the trial-and-error learning mechanism of the SAC-based agent is employed to compensate for the deficiency of GNNs in dynamic decision-making. This forms a closed-loop optimization system: the robot first perceives environmental information, then makes adaptive decisions based on the perceived data, and finally adjusts its behavior through feedback from decision execution. In this way, trajectory optimization of intelligent robots in complex topological environments is achieved. The GNN-RL framework consists of four core components: Environment Interaction, Graph Feature Encoding, Decision Execution, and Learning Optimization, as illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1The GNN—RL system structure based on the SAC.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the Environment Interaction module serves as the starting point of the overall framework. Following the execution of an action by the agent, the environment provides feedback in the form of a reward, which evaluates the quality of the action and generates a graph-structured state representing the complex topological environment. Trajectory data generated during each interaction is stored in the replay buffer, creating a closed-loop data cycle within the GNN-RL framework that transitions from state to action, then to reward, and finally to the next state. This cycle supplies continuous training data for the reinforcement learning component. Furthermore, by sampling stored transitions across different time steps, the replay buffer breaks the temporal correlation between consecutive samples, effectively stabilizing the training process.

The GNN encoders within the Graph Feature Encoding module plays a central role in feature extraction and structural understanding. For high-dimensional graph states with complex topological correlations, the encoder takes the raw graph-structured state as input and produces node-level embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{v}$$\end{document} and graph-level embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{G}$$\end{document} through separate encoding branches. The node-level embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{v}$$\end{document} capture local topological relationships combined with individual node attributes, while the graph-level embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{G}$$\end{document} aggregate global structural information and overall graph correlations. By converting complex topological structures into low-dimensional, interpretable feature vectors, this module resolves the challenge of handling high-dimensional graph states in reinforcement learning. The resulting embeddings are subsequently fed to the RL sub-network to support decision-making.

In the Decision Execution module, implemented as a SAC-based RL agent, the twin Q-Networks take the node embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{v}$$\end{document} encoded by the GNN as input, and compute the action values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q(s,a)$$\end{document}, where s denotes the encoded features of the graph-structured state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{t}$$\end{document} and a represents the set of candidate actions. This value quantifies the expected cumulative long-term reward obtained by executing action a under the current graph state. The action value provides a direct quantitative basis for the agent’s action selection, and plays a critical role in the objective calculation and network update of the reinforcement learning process. It drives the continuous optimization of the Q-Networks to improve the accuracy of value estimation, ensuring that action selection aligns with the goal of maximizing long-term cumulative rewards.

The Policy Network π takes both node-level and graph-level features encoded by the GNN as input. Through multi-layer network processing, it computes and outputs a probability distribution over the action space, thereby formulating the agent’s action policy. This policy directly guides the agent to select the current action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t}$$\end{document} from the candidate action set based on the predicted probabilities. Meanwhile, the policy output operates in coordination with the value estimations from the Q-Networks and Value Network V. During the policy gradient update, combined with environmental reward feedback and state transition information, the policy is continuously optimized to prioritize long-term rewards. This ensures that the policy adapts to the topological characteristics of the current graph-structured state, and converges toward a near-optimal solution in multi-step decision-making tasks.

The Value Network V takes the global graph features encoded by the GNN as input. It estimates the state value from a global perspective, further assists in the optimization of the action policy, and provides more stable feedback signals for the policy gradient update of the Policy Network. This mitigates optimization oscillations caused by reliance on single-step rewards, and enhances the overall stability of the decision-making process.

In the Learning Optimization module, historical trajectory data generated by the agent is stored in the replay buffer, forming an experience repository for model training. During the training process, mini-batches of transition data are randomly sampled from the replay buffer. This sampling strategy breaks the temporal correlation between consecutive trajectory samples, mitigates training instability, and enhances the convergence stability and efficiency of the model. Based on the sampled data, the RL target is computed to derive the ideal reference values for policy and value estimation, which serves as the optimization objective for network updates. Finally, the discrepancy between the Q-values predicted by the twin Q-Networks and the computed RL targets is quantified via a loss function. Through error backpropagation, the parameters of the GNN encoder, Policy Network π, Q-Networks, and Value Network V are updated end-to-end, along with the adaptive adjustment of the SAC entropy coefficient a. This iterative optimization process continuously improves the quality of graph feature representation, the optimality of the agent’s action selection, and the accuracy of value estimation, ultimately enabling robust trajectory optimization in complex topological environments. Environment interaction module design

In interactions with real-world complex topological environments, the decision-making of intelligent robots depends on the relationships between entities. These relationships are not limited to coordinate information, but also include the distance between the robot and obstacles, the connectivity between different path nodes, and other relevant topological attributes. Such complex correlational relationships cannot be fully characterized by traditional Euclidean space coordinates14 or grid-based representations15. In contrast, graph structures can efficiently capture these topological dependencies by abstracting entities as nodes and quantifying inter-entity relationships as edges. This provides a structured theoretical basis for feature extraction in GNNs and decision optimization in RL. Therefore, for the trajectory optimization of intelligent robots, physical entities such as intelligent robots, obstacles, and target points can be modeled as a graph structure. The modeling process considers the characteristics of complex topological structures, including the dynamics of entity correlations, path connectivity constraints, and the coupling of multi-scale environmental features. (7), the fourth-quadrant arc-tangent function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta_{ij,t} = \arctan 2(dy,dx)$$\end{document} can accurately output angles within the range [− π,π] based on the positive and negative combinations of the differences in horizontal and vertical coordinates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(dy,dx)$$\end{document}. This enables the distinction of directions in different quadrants such as northeast, southeast, northwest, and southwest, avoiding the loss of quadrant information that occurs with the traditional arc-tangent function due to its principal value range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$( - \frac{\pi }{2},\frac{\pi }{2}]$$\end{document}, thus ensuring the uniqueness of the relative azimuth angle.

The threat level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\zeta_{ij,t}$$\end{document} quantifies the potential collision risk between any vertices, where the robot is located, during the robot’s movement with the threat level increasing as the distance decreases.Therefore, the properties of the Gaussian function17 can be used to perform a nonlinear mapping of distance, thereby defining the threat feature in the graph structure as follows:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\zeta_{ij,t} = \exp ( - \frac{{d_{ij,t}^{2} }}{{\sigma^{2} }})$$\end{document}wherein, σ is the scale parameter, which is used to control the rate at which the threat level decays with distance, the smaller the value of σ, the faster the threat level decays as distance increases. Conversely, the threat level will remain at a relatively high value even over longer distances.

The dynamic feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta d_{ij,t}$$\end{document} reflects the changing trend of the distance between vertices and is used to predict the relative movement direction of the intelligent robot from one vertex to another. This feature enables the intelligent robot to capture the movement trend of dynamic obstacles in advance. It can be defined as follows:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta d_{ij,t} = d_{ij,t} - d_{ij,t - 1}$$\end{document}

According to Eq. (9), when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta d_{ij,t} > 0$$\end{document}, it indicates that the distance between the two vertices at time t is greater than that at time t − 1, meaning the intelligent robot and the next vertex are in a diverging trend. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta d_{ij,t} < 0$$\end{document}, it means the distance between the two vertices at time t is smaller than that at time t − 1, indicating the intelligent robot and the next vertex are in a converging trend. If the next vertex is an obstacle, the robot should perform obstacle avoidance operations at this point. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta d_{ij,t} = 0$$\end{document}, it implies the robot and the dynamic obstacle are relatively stationary, and there will be no collision risk in the short term.

Up to this point, through the equations design and dynamic modeling of the aforementioned environment interaction module, entities in the complex indoor environment, including the intelligent robot, obstacles, and target points, have been abstracted into a vertex set Vt with spatiotemporal attributes. Simultaneously, the correlation relationships between vertices, such as distance, direction, threat level, and dynamic trend, have been characterized by the edge set Et. And thus, a topological graph structure that conforms to the actual environment has been constructed. While this structure accurately captures the physical and dynamic characteristics of the environment, the discrete vertex attributes and edge features have not yet been transformed into structured feature representations that can directly support the intelligent decision-making of the robot. When the intelligent robot needs to grasp high-level information, such as the connectivity of the global path and the threat distribution of dynamic obstacles, during the trajectory optimization process, such information cannot be directly extracted from isolated vertex or edge features. Therefore, the topological graph output by the environment module must be in-depth processed through the graph feature encoding module: on the one hand, the original physical attributes of vertices are converted into high-dimensional feature vectors; on the other hand, the topological aggregation capability of GNN is utilized to integrate the correlation information contained in edge features into vertex features. Eventually, structured features incorporating global environmental awareness are generated, which provide detailed precision for the subsequent path planning of the robot—enabling the robot not only to accurately avoid immediate obstacles but also to select an optimal route from a global perspective.

Graph feature encoding module design

As a key link between environmental perception and decision-making planning, the graph feature encoding module’s core task is to convert the environment interaction module’s output, discretized vertex and edge features, into a feature form with structured expression capability via systematic mathematical transformations, thereby providing strong support for the robot’s path planning decisions. In this paper, the GNN encoder adopts a 2-layer graph convolutional network (GCN) with the following detailed configuration: Input node feature dimension: 128. First hidden layer dimension: 64, activation function: ReLU. Second hidden layer dimension: 32, activation function: ReLU. Edge features are fused into the message-passing mechanism via an adaptive weighting layer. All linear layers are initialized with the Xavier uniform initialization.

The primary step is to perform accurate quantitative encoding on the original physical attributes of vertices. Whether it is the real-time coordinates of the robot itself, the type labels of obstacles, or the movement speed information of dynamic entities, all such data need to be converted into structured feature vectors that can be directly processed by GNN via unified encoding rules. Therefore, for any vertex \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{i} \in V_{t}$$\end{document} in Eq. (2), its original attributes can be expressed as follows:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{i,t} = (p_{i,t} ,\tau_{i} ,{\mathbf{v}}_{i,t} )$$\end{document}

In Eq. (10), there are significant differences in the original attributes of vertices, coordinates and velocity are continuous values, while entity types are discrete labels, and these attributes cannot directly participate in unified mathematical operations.Therefore, it is necessary to convert heterogeneous features into numerical vectors of the same dimension through encoding and concatenation, facilitating matrix operation processing by GNN.The attribute concatenation and encoding formula for vertex \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{i,t}$$\end{document} features can be defined as follows:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{i}^{(0)} = Concat(p_{i,t} ,OneHot(\tau_{i} ),{\mathbf{v}}_{i,t} )$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{i}^{(0)} \in R^{{d_{v} }}$$\end{document} denotes the unified-dimensional vector obtained after attribute concatenation and encoding, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{v}$$\end{document} represents the dimension of vertex features; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Concat( \cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the vector concatenation operation, which concatenates coordinates, type encodings, and velocity in sequence; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$OneHot( \cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the one-hot encoding of the type, used to realize the digitization of discrete types.

To this point, through attribute concatenation and encoding, the original attributes of vertices in the graph structure have been converted into vectors of a unified dimension, and the format unification of different types of features has been achieved. Whether they are continuous coordinate and velocity values or discrete entity type labels, all such data have been integrated into directly computable numerical vectors. This process not only preserves the original semantic information of each attribute but also ensures that the correlation relationships between features are not destroyed through a fixed-order concatenation logic, allowing each dimension to correspond to a clear physical meaning. More importantly, a foundation has been laid for subsequent feature aggregation and global awareness construction, enabling these encoded features to serve as effective inputs that support the robot in making comprehensive judgments on environmental details and global correlations during path planning. Eventually, a decision result that balances obstacle avoidance precision and global optimality is output.

In the environment module, there are significant differences in the importance of correlations between different vertices with respect to the robot’s path planning. The correlations involving vertex pairs at close range, high-threat associations, and dynamic relationships showing a converging trend have a far greater impact on obstacle avoidance decisions than those involving long-distance, low-threat, or diverging-trend associations.Therefore, the weights need to be used to clarify the priority of these correlations, enabling the robot to automatically focus on key relationships when processing information and avoid wasting computing power on irrelevant information. In this paper, a weight conversion mechanism is designed for the edge features in Eq. (5). Through linear transformations and nonlinear activation functions, correlation information such as distance and threat level is converted into adjustable weight parameters, enabling key information to gain high influence in feature aggregation. The conversion function can be defined as follows:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{e}}_{ij} = ReLU(\lambda_{e} \cdot {\mathbf{f}}_{ij,t} + b_{e} )$$\end{document}wherein, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{e}}_{ij} \in R^{{d_{e} }}$$\end{document} denotes the adjustable weight vector, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{e}$$\end{document} represents the dimension of edge features; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ReLU( \cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the activation function, which is used to enhance nonlinear expression capability; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_{e} \in R^{{d_{e} \times 4}}$$\end{document} represents the learnable linear transformation matrix; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{e} \in R^{{d_{e} }}$$\end{document} denotes the bias term.

The edge weight vector in Eq. (12) defines the correlation strength between vertices in the graph structure. By quantifying features such as distance and threat level, it clarifies the priority of different correlations and provides a quantitative basis for local feature interaction. However, the features of vertices remain in a relatively isolated state, each vertex’s features only contain its own attributes and lack connectivity with distant target points. When environmental changes, such as obstacle movement or the addition of new entities, are occurred, the change information cannot be quickly transmitted through correlation relationships, resulting in lagging algorithm responses to dynamic risks, and in turn, potential errors in local obstacle avoidance decisions. Therefore, it is necessary to deeply integrate the scattered vertex features and edge weight information in the graph structure, combining local correlation details with the global environmental topology. This upgrades each vertex’s features from isolated physical attributes to structured information that incorporates individual attributes, local relationships, and global constraints.To achieve the in-depth fusion of scattered vertex features and edge weight information, all neighboring vertices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{j}$$\end{document} of a vertex \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{i}$$\end{document} are first expressed as follows:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {v_{j} \in N(i) \, } \\ {(v_{j} ,v_{i} ) \in E_{t} {\text{ or }}(v_{i} ,v_{j} ) \in E_{t} } \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N(i)$$\end{document} denotes the neighbor set of vertex \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{i}$$\end{document}, which is determined by the edge set Et of the graph at time t.

For the neighbor node set in Eq. (13), the feature aggregation formula using Graph GNN can be defined as follows:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{i}^{(k)} = \sum\nolimits_{j \in N(i)} {{\mathbf{e}}_{ij} } h_{j}^{(k - 1)}$$\end{document}where⊙denotes the element-wise multiplication. Among them, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{h} \in R^{{d_{h} \times (d_{v} + d_{e} )}}$$\end{document} denotes the feature update matrix; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{h} \in R^{{d_{h} }}$$\end{document} is the bias term.

In the robot path planning process, the decision execution module serves as the action output layer for robot path planning. Based on the global feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{t}$$\end{document} output by the graph feature encoding module and the dynamic environmental information, it generates specific control commands, thereby enabling the robot to avoid obstacles and track paths during movement.

Design of the decision execution module

In the GNN—RL system, the design of the decision execution module is realized by extracting the structured features of the environment through GNN and combining it with the RL method. Therefore, in the learning state of GNN—RL, the environmental features output by GNN and the robot’s own information need to be fused, so that an input understandable to RL can be formed. Accordingly, the input state of the decision execution module at time t can be defined as follows:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{t} = [g_{t} ,p_{t} ,\theta_{t} ,v_{t} ,p_{goal} ]$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{t} = (x_{t} ,y_{t} )$$\end{document} denotes the robot’s position; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta_{t}$$\end{document} represents the robot’s moving direction angle; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{t}$$\end{document} is the robot’s current moving speed; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{goal}$$\end{document} stands for the target position of the robot’s movement.

During the robot’s state transition process, to provide a larger adjustment range for the robot’s precise control and path optimization, and thereby adapt more flexibly to subtle changes in the dynamic environment, the control commands need to be processed continuously, which necessitates the use of an algorithm tailored for continuous control tasks. In this paper, the decision execution module is implemented based on the SAC algorithm, which serves as the core actor-critic framework for the RL agent and consists of three key components with specific network structures: twin Q-Networks, a Policy Network π, and a Value Network V. Specifically, the twin Q-Networks are 3-layer fully connected networks with dimensions [256, 256, 128] and ReLU activation. They take the encoded environmental features including both node-level and graph-level embeddings from the GNN encoder as input to estimate action values Q(s,a). These values quantify the expected long-term cumulative reward and help mitigate overestimation bias. The Policy Network π is also a 3-layer fully connected network with dimensions [256, 256, 128] and ReLU activation. It processes the fused state st and outputs a Gaussian stochastic probability distribution over the action space, defining the agent’s behavioral policy. Meanwhile, the Value Network V is a 2-layer fully connected network with dimensions [128, 64]. It estimates the state value from a global perspective to provide stable feedback signals for policy gradient updates, together with the entropy coefficient α that balances exploration and exploitation.

Therefore, the control quantity is defined as the action of reinforcement learning, and a continuous action space as shown in the following:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t} = [a_{t}^{lin} ,a_{t}^{ang} ]$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t} \in R^{2}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t}^{lin} \in [ - a_{\max } ,a_{\max } ]$$\end{document} denotes the linear acceleration, which is used to control the increase or decrease of the robot’s speed: when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t}^{lin} \ge 0$$\end{document}, the robot’s speed increases over time; when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t}^{lin} < 0$$\end{document}, the speed decreases over time. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t}^{ang} \in [ - \omega_{\max } ,\omega_{\max } ]$$\end{document} represents the angular acceleration, which is used to control the robot’s steering. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t}^{ang} > 0$$\end{document}, the robot’s steering angular velocity increases, resulting in faster steering.When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t}^{ang} < 0$$\end{document}, the robot’s steering slows down.

Through the optimization of the SAC objective, the agent continuously learns to map high-dimensional graph-structured states to optimal continuous actions, enabling the robot to avoid obstacles and track paths during movement.

As the robot moves, the essence of the state transition model lies in how the robot’s speed, moving direction angle, and position change from those at time t to those at time t + 1 after executing the action specified in Eq. (18). Therefore, the robot’s state transition equation can be defined as follows:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {v_{t + 1} = clip(v_{t} + a_{t}^{lin} \cdot \Delta t,0,v_{\max } )} \\ {\theta_{t + 1} = \theta_{t} + a_{t}^{ang} \cdot \Delta t \, } \\ {p_{t + 1} = p_{t} + v_{t} \cdot [\cos \theta_{t} ,\sin \theta_{t} ] \cdot \Delta t} \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$clip(x.\min ,\max )$$\end{document} denotes the clipping function, which restricts x to the range [min,max]. Δt represents the time step, which is used to define the time interval between two state updates.

In the state update for robot trajectory optimization, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{t}$$\end{document} usually does not change directly with the agent’s action selection. Here, the state update formula for the global feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{t}$$\end{document} can be defined as follows:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{t + 1} = g_{t} + 1$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{goal}$$\end{document} is the final movement target of the robot, which remains unchanged once fixed. Therefore, based on Eqs. (17)-(20), the robot state update equation for the GNN—RL decision execution module can be obtained as follows:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{t + 1} = [g_{t + 1} ,p_{t + 1} ,\theta_{t + 1} ,v_{t + 1} ,p_{goal} ]$$\end{document}

In the decision execution module, the robot can update its state based on the current state information and the state transition model. The selection of an optimal new state needs to be evaluated using a reward function. A good reward function should be based on the trajectory segments generated by state updates: if the new state selected by the robot is closer to the target point and farther from obstacles, positive rewards will be given by the reward function; otherwise, negative rewards will be given. Through this correlation, the physical state changes are converted by the reward function into value signals that guide policy learning, enabling the robot to gradually optimize a trajectory that meets the task requirements during state iteration.

Under the GNN—RL framework, the core of reward function design lies in converting three elements, the risk derived from GNN’s obstacle features, the progress reflected by GNN’s target and topological features, and the action stability indicated by GNN’s dynamic features, into quantifiable mathematical expressions. The total reward function can be defined as follows:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{t} = \beta_{safe} \cdot r_{safe,t} + \beta_{eff} \cdot r_{eff,t} + \beta_{smooth} \cdot r_{smooth,t}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{safe} ,\beta_{eff} ,\beta_{smooth}$$\end{document} are the dynamic adjustment coefficients for the priorities of the three objectives: safety, progress, and stability. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{safe,t} ,r_{eff,t} ,r_{smooth,t}$$\end{document} respectively represent the quantitative safety reward, quantitative progress reward, and quantitative stability reward at time t.

The safety reward function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{safe} (t)$$\end{document} is a core function used to quantify the level of risk faced by the robot during obstacle avoidance and guide the robot to generate a safe movement trajectory through reward signals. Its design relies on the obstacle features extracted from the environment by GNN. Therefore, the quantitative safety reward can be defined as follows:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{safe,t} = - \sum\limits_{m = 1}^{M} {\zeta_{ij,t} \cdot \exp ( - \frac{{d_{ij,t}^{m} }}{{d_{safe,t} }}} )$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{ij,t}^{m}$$\end{document} represents the distance between the robot and the m-th obstacle at time t. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{safe}$$\end{document} denotes the safety distance. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{ij,t}^{m} \ge d_{safe,t}$$\end{document}, a positive reward is granted, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{ij,t}^{m} < d_{safe,t}$$\end{document}, a negative reward is given as the distance to the obstacle decreases, which serves to penalize collision risks.

The efficiency reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{eff,t}$$\end{document} is designed to encourage the robot to quickly reduce its distance to the target point. By quantifying the change in distance between the robot and the target point at the current moment, it grants positive rewards for behaviors that move toward the target and imposes negative rewards for behaviors that move away from the target. The quantitative formula for the efficiency reward can be defined as follows:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{reff,t} = \kappa \cdot (d_{goal,t} - d_{goal,t + 1} )$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document} is the efficiency reward weight; the larger its value, the greater the emphasis placed on efficiency. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{goal,t} ,d_{goal,t + 1}$$\end{document} respectively represent the distance from the agent to the target point at time t and t + 1, and their values can be calculated in accordance with Eq. (6).

The stability reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{smooth,t}$$\end{document} is an incentive mechanism designed to suppress abrupt movements of the robot, the greater the movement amplitude, the larger the negative reward imposed. Conversely, the smoother the movement and the more continuous the change in control quantity, the smaller the negative reward. The quantitative formula for the stability reward can be defined as follows:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{smooth,t} = - \chi \cdot ((a_{t}^{lin} )^{2} + (a_{t}^{ang} )^{2} )$$\end{document}

Among them, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi$$\end{document} is the weight coefficient; the greater the movement amplitude, the higher the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(a_{t}^{lin} )^{2} + (a_{t}^{ang} )^{2}$$\end{document}, and the stronger the negative reward, thereby compelling the robot to adopt a more stable control strategy.

In Eq. (22), the safety reward weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{safe}$$\end{document} is employed to balance the priority of the obstacle avoidance safety objective. It adaptively increases its weight when the robot is close to obstacles, thereby prioritizing the safety of the navigation process. The progress reward weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{eff}$$\end{document} is designed to balance the priority of the target approaching efficiency objective. It adaptively increases its weight when the robot is in an open and safe area, accelerating convergence to the target point. The stability reward weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{smooth}$$\end{document} is utilized to balance the priority of the motion smoothness objective. It adaptively increases its weight when the robot’s actions exhibit significant fluctuations, suppressing trajectory jitter and ensuring motion stability.

These weights can be dynamically adjusted based on the robot’s real-time state in the environment, ensuring that the reward function can adaptively prioritize different objectives according to the specific navigation scenario. In this paper, the specific dynamic adjustment rules are defined as follows:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {\beta_{safe} (t) = \beta_{safe,0} \cdot \exp \left( { - \frac{{d_{\min } (t)}}{{d_{saft} }}} \right) \, } \\ {\beta_{eff} (t) = \beta_{eff,0} \cdot \left( {1 - \exp \left( {\frac{{d_{\min } (t)}}{{d_{saft} }}} \right)} \right) \cdot \exp \left( { - \frac{{\left\| {\Delta a(t)} \right\|}}{{\Delta a_{\max } }}} \right)} \\ {\beta_{smooth} (t) = \beta_{smooth,0} \cdot \exp \left( { - \frac{{\left\| {\Delta a(t)} \right\|}}{{\Delta a_{\max } }}} \right) \, } \\ {} \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\min } (t)$$\end{document} is the minimum distance from the robot to the nearest obstacle at time t, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{safe}$$\end{document} is the predefined safety distance threshold.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\| {\Delta a(t)} \right\|$$\end{document} is the norm of the robot’s action change between consecutive time steps, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta a_{\max }$$\end{document} is the maximum allowable action change.To ensure the stability of the reward scale during training, the coefficients are normalized at each time step to satisfy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{safe} (t) + \beta_{eff} (t) + \beta_{smooth} (t) = 1$$\end{document}.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{safe,0} = 1.0,\beta_{eff,0} = 0.5,\beta_{smooth,0} = 0.8$$\end{document} are the initial constant values of the coefficients, determined through empirical tuning.

Through the design of the reward function in the form of Eq. (22), the direct feedback is provided for the behavioral value of the robot, enabling the optimal decision to be selected by the robot among numerous behaviors. As the core actor component of the SAC framework, the policy network, through learning, converts reward signals into executable action strategies. The environmental structured features output by GNN and the correlations between reward signals can be accurately captured by a well-designed policy network, which can quickly generate action commands that balance safety, efficiency, and smoothness in complex dynamic scenarios. Even when faced with unseen obstacle distributions or changes in target positions, the near-optimal decisions can be output through generalization capabilities, ensuring that the goals set by the reward function are stably achieved.

As the critic component of the SAC framework, the Q-network, which evaluates action value, is responsible for quantitatively assessing the expected cumulative reward of state-action pairs. To mitigate the overestimation bias in value estimation, the SAC algorithm adopts a twin Q-network structure, with two independent Q-networks learning in parallel and taking the minimum value for target calculation. It takes the environmental state encoded by GNN and the action output by the policy network as inputs, and outputs the long-term value estimation of the action in the current state through a multi-layer neural network structure. This estimation not only refers to the immediate reward but also integrates the potential rewards of future states, forming a forward-looking judgment on the quality of the action. When the Q-value is higher, it means the action is more likely to lead to the globally optimal cumulative reward in the current state. The Q-network for evaluating action value can be designed as follows:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q(s_{t} ,a_{t} |\psi^{Q} ) = W_{Q} \cdot ReLU(W_{cat} \cdot Concat(h_{s} ,h_{a} ) + b_{cat} ) + b_{Q}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\psi^{Q}$$\end{document} denotes the parameters of the value network; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{Q} ,W_{cat}$$\end{document} represents the weight matrix, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{Q} ,b_{cat}$$\end{document} stands for the bias; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{s} = ReLU(W_{S} \cdot s_{t} + b_{s} )$$\end{document} is the state feature extraction, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{a} = ReLU(W_{a} \cdot a_{t} + b_{a} )$$\end{document} denotes the action feature extraction;\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Concat( \cdot )$$\end{document} serves as the feature concatenation function.

To address the core issue of unstable value estimation in reinforcement learning and ensure that the Q-network can converge stably and learn accurate action values, consistent with the SAC training mechanism, the target Q-value calculation must be performed after the design of the Q-network for evaluating action values as following:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{t} = r_{t} + \varpi \cdot Q^{\prime}(s_{t + 1} ,\mu^{\prime}(s_{t + 1} |\psi^{{\mu^{\prime}}} )|\psi^{{Q^{\prime}}} )$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{t}$$\end{document} denotes the target Q-value at time t; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{t}$$\end{document} represents the immediate reward at time t; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varpi \in [0,1]$$\end{document} is the discount factor; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q^{\prime}(s_{t + 1} ,\mu^{\prime}(s_{t + 1} |\psi^{{\mu^{\prime}}} )|\psi^{{Q^{\prime}}} )$$\end{document} stands for the optimal value estimation of the next state.

In the definition of the target Q-value in Eq. (28), a comprehensive estimation integrating the immediate obstacle avoidance reward, path efficiency reward, and future state value in robot trajectory optimization is incorporated. Next, the Q-network loss function can be defined. By measuring the difference between the trajectory decision Q-value output by the current Q-network and the target Q-value, the update of Q-network parameters is accurately guided. This enables the robot to more intelligently learn how to select actions during trajectory planning to make the trajectory safer and more efficient, and promotes the convergence of the trajectory optimization task to a better solution.The Q-network loss function is defined as follows:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L(\psi^{Q} ) = E[y_{t} - Q(s_{t} ,a_{t} |\psi^{Q} ))^{2} ]$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E[ \cdot ]$$\end{document} denotes the expectation over the robot’s trajectory distribution.

After the Q-network loss function is defined, through which the value estimation error is accurately quantified by the difference between the target Q-value and the output of the current Q-network, and the Q-network is driven to learn the value of actions for trajectory optimization, the policy gradient update phase is initiated. In the robot trajectory optimization task, the policy gradient update builds an objective function for maximizing trajectory returns based on the action value cognition trained by the Q-network loss function. And by calculating the correlation between the action probability gradient and the Q-value to iteratively optimize the policy network parameters, the robot is driven to actively screen and execute action sequences that approach the optimal trajectory solution, thereby promoting trajectory optimization and enabling the robot to plan safer and more efficient motion paths. The policy gradient update formula is defined as follows:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla_{{\psi^{u} }} J(\psi^{u} ) = E[\nabla_{a} (s,a|\psi^{Q} )|_{a = u(s)} \cdot \nabla_{{\psi^{u} }} u(s|\psi^{u} )]$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla_{{\psi^{u} }} J(\psi^{u} )$$\end{document} denotes the gradient direction of the policy network parameters, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla_{a} (s,a|\psi^{Q} )|_{a = u(s)}$$\end{document} represents the action probability gradient.

Therefore, in the GNN-RL system, based on the expectations derived from robot trajectory data, the optimal action-value is applied to guide the policy gradient update. This approach can thereby increase the execution probability of high-value actions, promote the iterative optimization of the policy network, and enable the robot to plan more optimal trajectories in complex topological environments. The final output of the GNN—RL decision-making and execution module is expressed as:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t}^{*} = u(s_{t} |\psi^{{u^{*} }} )$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t}^{*}$$\end{document} denotes the final control command of the robot at time t. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\psi^{{u^{*} }}$$\end{document} is the optimal policy parameter after training convergence, which refers to the parameter set that enables the policy network to stably output optimal actions upon the completion of its training.

In GNN—RL system architecture, after the design of the decision-making and execution module is completed, which defines how the policy network outputs actions based on graph features encoded by GNN, the design of the learning and optimization module becomes the core link for realizing the iterative improvement of the agent’s capabilities. By systematically processing interaction data, constructing learning objectives, and quantifying optimization errors, the learning module ultimately achieves the collaborative evolution of the GNN encoder, policy network, and value network.

Decision execution module design

The decision execution module is implemented based on the SAC algorithm, a maximum-entropy actor-critic framework tailored for continuous control tasks, which serves as the core of the RL agent in the GNN-RL system. After the actions generated by this SAC-based decision execution module interact with the environment, trajectory data containing graph states, actions, and rewards is produced. Due to the strong temporal correlation of this trajectory data, direct use in training will cause the model to over-fit to recent data and trigger training oscillations. However, the learning and optimization module performs multi-network joint decorrelation processing on the data generated by the SAC-based decision execution module, enabling the robot to learn better strategies from experience. This approach not only improves the GNN’s perception accuracy of graph-structured environments and optimizes the action selection logic of the SAC policy network, but also enhances the ability of the SAC value network to evaluate behavioral consequences. Therefore, an experience replay buffer is first constructed to address the issue of temporal correlation in the data, each trajectory is split into independent transition samples, which are stored in the following:32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D = \{ (s_{t} ,a_{t} ,r_{t} ,s_{t + 1} ,done_{t} )\}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$done_{t} \in \{ 0,1\}$$\end{document} is the termination flag. The task ends when the agent meets the preset termination condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$done_{t} = 1$$\end{document} in the graph environment, otherwise \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$done_{t} = 0$$\end{document}, the preset termination condition is not met.

Diverse data covering different graph structures and task scenarios for graph feature encoding is provided by the experience replay buffer through storing transition samples from multiple episodes and stages. To provide stable training inputs that comply with the independent and identically distributed assumption for the GNN encoder in GNN—RL system, and to ensure the learning of graph feature representations with strong generalization ability, it is necessary to randomly sample mini-batches of samples B from the experience replay buffer D during the learning and training phase, as shown in the following :33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B = \{ (s_{t} ,a_{t} ,r_{t} ,s_{t + 1} ,done_{t} )\}_{t - 1}^{\varphi }$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi$$\end{document} denotes the batch size, which refers to the sample set input during a single model training session, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B \subset D$$\end{document}.

The GNN-RL encoder needs to convert the graph states St and St+1 into low-dimensional vector features that can be processed by the policy network or value network. The key lies in aggregating the node’s own features and the features of its neighboring nodes through the message-passing mechanism of GNN, while explicitly modeling the topological relationships between nodes. Ultimately, it generates feature representations that contain both node attributes and integrated structural information, providing structured inputs for subsequent policy decision-making and value estimation.

Notably, the policy network and value network mentioned above are part of the SAC algorithm for the decision-making module. The feature representations output by the GNN-RL encoder are precisely the input for the SAC-based policy network and value network, ensuring that the generated action strategies are compatible with the robot’s trajectory optimization needs. This integration of GNN’s structural feature extraction and SAC’s decision-making mechanism enables the system to better adapt to the complex topological environment, which is also the key to the GNN-RL system’s stable operation.

The node feature update in the learning module enables GNN-RL to form high-order features by aggregating the features of the neighboring nodes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{j}$$\end{document} of each arbitrary node \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{i}$$\end{document}. The node feature update is as follows:34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{i}^{(L)} (s_{t} ) = GNN(\{ h_{j}^{(k)} (s_{t} )\}_{j \in N(i)} ,\psi^{GNN} )$$\end{document}where L denotes the number of network layers; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\psi^{GNN}$$\end{document} represents the GNN parameters; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{j}^{(k)}$$\end{document} stands for the original feature of node v derived from Eq. (15).

Next, all node features are aggregated into a global graph feature via the Readout function18, which serves as the input to the RL module. In the training process of the GNN—RL method, the training parameters such as the GNN encoder, policy network, and Q-network are iteratively updated using the gradient descent optimization strategy. This is achieved by back-propagating the loss signal, which starts from the loss function and computes the parameter gradients layer by layer. The specific update process can be expressed as:41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{gathered} \psi^{Q} \leftarrow \psi^{Q} - a_{Q} \cdot \nabla_{{\psi^{Q} }} L_{total} \hfill \\ \psi^{\pi } \leftarrow \psi^{\pi } - a_{\pi } \cdot \nabla_{{\psi^{\pi } }} L_{total} \hfill \\ \psi^{GNN} \leftarrow \psi^{GNN} - a_{GNN} \cdot \nabla_{{\psi^{GNN} }} L_{total} \hfill \\ \end{gathered}$$\end{document}

Through parameter updates based on the aforementioned formulas, the loss gradients propagate backward along the forward propagation path of the GNN-RL strategy during the training process. This enables precise adjustment of all parameters involved in graph feature encoding, action decision-making, and value estimation, with the entropy coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} adaptively balancing the exploration and exploitation trade-off throughout the entire training process. Consequently, the GNN-RL model achieves continuous learning and performance iteration in graph-structured environments, allowing the robot to gradually acquire strategies that are adaptive to complex graph topologies, maintain sufficient exploration capability during training, and ultimately achieve higher cumulative rewards.

Up to this point, the detailed design of all GNN-RL functional modules has been fully completed. With the graph structure as the core carrier, each module forms an interlocking logical closed loop, jointly constructing a complete system that spans from basic graph modeling to dynamic optimization, which is built on the maximum entropy reinforcement learning framework.

Simulation and analysis

Simulation scenario and environment setup

In this paper, a scenario such as robotic navigation as shown in Fig. 3 is constructed. All experiments are conducted in a self-developed simulation environment implemented with Python (or MATLAB), which supports basic physical collision detection and environment state updates. The green dot in the figure is the starting point, and the red dot is the target point that the robot needs to reach. The gray shapes represent static obstacles, forming fixed barriers such as narrow channels, cross-shaped structures, and ring-shaped areas, which define the permanent topological constraints of the environment. The red shapes are dynamic obstacles, distributed in multiple regions, adding time-varying challenges to navigation.Fig. 3Complex topological environment.

Complex topological environment.

Each dynamic obstacle is governed by a simple constant-velocity kinematic model as following,42\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p(t) = p_{0} + {v} \cdot t$$\end{document}where p0 is the initial position randomly generated within the experimental workspace, and v denotes the constant velocity vector, which is randomly initialized at the start of each test episode. During navigation, dynamic obstacles maintain a fixed speed and direction without responding to the robot’s motion.

This scenario is used to test the path planning and obstacle avoidance capabilities of navigation algorithms under complex static layouts and dynamic uncertainties, requiring the agent to avoid both fixed and moving obstacles during the process from the starting point to the target point.

To comprehensively and fairly validate the advantages of GNN-RL in terms of overall performance, generalization ability, and environmental adaptability, three algorithms, DQN, PPO, and A*, are selected as comparative baselines in this paper, with the selection rationale described as follows.

First, as a classic heuristic search algorithm in path planning, A* is a widely recognized conventional benchmark. It possesses mature obstacle avoidance mechanisms and accurate shortest path optimization capabilities, and is thus suitable for verifying the basic effectiveness of the proposed framework.

Second, PPO, as a mainstream policy gradient reinforcement learning algorithm, exhibits stable and efficient optimization performance. As one of the most representative state of the art methods for mobile robot navigation and path planning, it enables the evaluation of the competitiveness of GNN-RL in complex dynamic environments.

Third, DQN, as a typical value based reinforcement learning algorithm, has been widely adopted in sequential decision making and path planning tasks, and can represent the performance level of early deep reinforcement learning methods in planning problems. Notably, the original DQN only supports discrete action spaces. To ensure the fairness and rationality of the comparative experiments, targeted modifications are made to DQN in this paper. Through action space transformation and redesigned network outputs, the output layer is enabled to predict continuous action values within a predefined range. The tanh activation function is applied to map the outputs to a bounded continuous interval, which is then scaled to the actual action range of the robot to produce a continuous action vector corresponding to the motion control variables. Meanwhile, the ε greedy exploration strategy is replaced with Gaussian noise injection to generate smooth and continuous exploration actions, allowing fair evaluation within the same continuous action space as GNN-RL.

Ablation study

In this section, an ablation study is conducted to verify the effectiveness of the GNN module in the proposed framework. The experiment uses a vanilla RL agent without the GNN module as the baseline, which only adopts a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) for feature extraction. To objectively evaluate the trajectory smoothness, which is closely associated with the stability reward design in the framework, two standard quantitative metrics are introduced: the integral of curvature and acceleration variance.

Conclusion

This paper presents a trajectory optimization method that integrates GNN and RL. The proposed approach employs GNNs to model complex topological environments and uses the SAC as the core RL module. A carefully designed multi-objective reward function is introduced to guide intelligent robots in continuously learning and optimizing trajectory policies through environmental interaction.Comparative simulation experiments are conducted against DQN, PPO, and A* algorithms. The results show that the GNN-RL method achieves favorable overall performance in terms of average path length, arrival time, collision count, and task success rate. It maintains stable and high success rates in representative complex topological scenarios, including narrow passages, intersections, and circular regions. These results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed method in adaptive trajectory optimization and its strong generalization ability in complex environments. The method thus provides a practical solution for autonomous navigation and trajectory planning of intelligent robots.

Since the reward function weights in the proposed GNN-RL framework are fixed during training, the exploration efficiency in highly complex topological environments may be restricted to some extent. Future work will focus on adaptive parameter tuning strategies tailored to specific environmental characteristics to further improve the robustness and adaptability of the algorithm.
