Always Prioritise Your Priorities, Especially During Pandemics
Authors: María P Lamarca Mendoza
Published: 2024

The severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) first wave forced hospitals worldwide to change their surgical practices by redistributing resources. This prospective observational study from 69 hospitals in 21 countries over a 12 week period investigated how the first wave affected post-operative outcomes, re-interventions, and mortality in patients undergoing vascular procedures.1

The study cohort consisted of 3 150 patients who were prospectively collected from 20 March – 31 August 2020. It revealed that most deaths were in hospital (9.1%), with a 30 day mortality rate of 10.4% and a six month mortality rate of 12.8%, which is significantly higher than pre-pandemic published data. Unfortunately, the authors did not provide pre-COVID outcome data from the participating centres, which could have provided a clearer context about the real impact of COVID-19 on vascular surgical care. Although 1.7% of the patients had confirmed COVID-19, it is probable that the actual number of infected patients was higher, as the authors did not specify the proportion of patients who were tested, and testing was not routinely performed. This could potentially explain the increased mortality rate, along with disruptions in established care pathways.

Notably, despite guidelines recommending intervention only for the most severe presentations of vascular disease, 34% of carotid interventions were performed on asymptomatic patients, and 20% of lower limb procedures were on claudicants, demonstrating clear heterogeneity in the effect of SARS-CoV-2 across countries during the first wave. Additionally, the 30 day mortality rate for carotid interventions was as high as 8%, which is significantly higher than the stroke and death rate recommended by the European guidelines (3% for asymptomatic, 6% for symptomatic).2 This raises the question of whether erratic blood pressure control, limited access to higher levels of care, or undiagnosed COVID-19 cases could explain these findings. The absence of data on the causes of death in these groups is a significant limitation.

Although the results should be interpreted with caution, due to the absence of pre-pandemic data from the centres, the study supports previous findings: an increased mortality rate during the first wave among patients undergoing vascular procedures.3,4 This leads to the conclusion that, in the undesirable event of another pandemic scenario, the thresholds for surgery should probably be higher than during normal practice, postponing non-critical procedures. Additionally, disruptions in established care pathways, such as reduced access to higher level care, should be avoided as they entail a higher mortality rate.
