Retinal Vessel Dysfunction in Cerebral Autosomal Dominant Arteriopathy With Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy
Authors: Yonatan Serlin, Colyn Munn, Elisa A. Ferrante, Lyna Kamintsky, Chantal Cousineau-Krieger, Edmond J. FitzGibbon, M. Teresa Magone, Richard W. J. Lee, Tiarnán D. L. Keenan, Camilo Toro, Sherrell Apugo, Alessandra Brofferio, Beth A. Kozel, M. Luisa Iruela-Arispe, Alon Friedman, Manfred Boehm

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesCerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL), the most common inherited cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD), involves NOTCH3-mediated microvascular pathology. As retinal health remains underexplored, serial ultra-widefield fluorescein angiography (UW-FA) was used to characterize dynamic vessel dysfunction.MethodsUW-FA was performed at baseline and after 13.9 ± 4.5 months. Retinal vessel permeability, blood flow, and perfusion across macular, mid-peripheral, and far-peripheral zones were quantified. Aberrant pixels exceeded healthy volunteer (HV) thresholds. Group differences, longitudinal changes, NOTCH3 variant loci, and stroke-age associations were analyzed.ResultsNineteen patients with genetically confirmed CADASIL (mean age 48.4 years; 52.6% female) and 9 matched HVs (mean age 44.3 years, 55.6% female) were included. Baseline far-peripheral permeability was higher in the CADASIL group vs HVs (p = 0.03; r = 0.41). At follow-up, mid-peripheral permeability increased (p = 0.003; r = 0.56) and far-peripheral permeability remained elevated (p = 0.0007; r = 0.64) in patients with CADASIL. Longitudinal analysis showed reduced blood flow and perfusion in the macula and mid-periphery and increased midperipheral permeability (all statistically significant with substantial effect sizes). Neither genotype nor stroke-onset age predicted alterations.DiscussionSerial UW-FA scans detect progressive, region-specific retinal vessel dysfunction in a modest-sized cohort with CADASIL, potentially serving as a surrogate marker for cSVD progression.

Introduction

Cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) is the most common inherited cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD). CADASIL exhibits a broad clinical spectrum, including migraine with aura, recurrent ischemic strokes, cognitive and mood impairments, and motor disability.1 However, retinal vasculature involvement remains underexplored. Owing to their common embryologic origin, retinal and cerebral vessels share cellular and structural similarities, including analogous blood-brain barrier (BBB) and blood-retina barrier (BRB). Thus, retinal imaging may provide a noninvasive surrogate for assessing cerebral vascular alterations.2 Previous studies using optical coherence tomography and fundus photography identified structural retinal abnormalities (e.g., altered vessel diameter and reduced retinal nerve fiber layer thickness).3 However, these modalities have limited fields of view; cannot quantitatively assess early BRB permeability, blood flow, or perfusion; and lack longitudinal assessments.4 Ultra-widefield fluorescein angiography (UW-FA) enables comprehensive assessment of retinal vessel dysfunction by capturing structural and functional vascular alterations across the entire retina. In CADASIL, cysteine-altering variants in the epidermal growth factor-like repeat domains of the NOTCH3 gene lead to accumulation of Notch3 protein in the cerebral mi- crovasculature.5 The relatively well-defined genetic basis and decades-long progression make CADASIL valuable for elucidating cSVD trajectory and mechanisms.6 We present insights from serial UW-FA scans to advance disease characterization, monitor progression, and support development of retinal end points for clinical trials.

Methods

Study Population

Between 2016 and 2022, a longitudinal observational study enrolled 19 patients with genetically confirmed CADASIL and 9 healthy volunteers (HVs) without retinal or neurologic conditions or systemic vascular risk factors. Adults (≥18 years) were included; participants with incomplete data precluding longitudinal analysis were excluded (eFigure 1). Participants with CADASIL underwent clinical evaluation and UW-FA at each of 2 visits (baseline and follow-up) while HVs had UW-FA once. Demographics, NOTCH3 variant data, and neurologic comorbidities were obtained from medical records.

FA

UW-FA was performed using an Optos UWF-P200DTx camera (Optos Inc., Dunfermline, United Kingdom) with a standardized protocol. Imaging of both eyes was acquired after IV fluorescein injection (capturing >36 frames). The eye with the greater frame-capture rate was selected for analysis.

Quantitative Mapping

UW-FA images were analyzed using RETICAD software (Emagix Inc., Halifax, NS). Automatic preprocessing included image quality filtering, artifact correction, and vessel segmentation. Maps of BRB permeability, blood flow, and perfusion were partitioned into 3 retinal zones (Figure 1A) scaled to optic disc width7: macula (6-mm circular zone centered on foveal avascular zone), mid-periphery (6–20 mm), and far periphery (>20 mm). A retinal template from HVs standardized the field of view. To ensure accuracy, analysis was restricted to retinal pixels unobstructed by artifacts (e.g., eyelashes and eyelids). Thresholds for aberrant values were defined using HV maps. Pixels outside the 0.5th–99.5th percentile of HVs were classified as aberrant. Vascular function maps (Figure 1, B–D) included the following: (1) BRB permeability, quantified by extravascular fluorescein accumulation (recirculation to late phase) and percent-leakage area (pixels >99.5th percentile of HVs/total pixels); (2) blood flow, reflecting intravascular fluorescein wash-in from choroidal flush to arteriovenous phase; (3) perfusion, measured as extravascular fluorescein wash-in over the same interval. Pixel values were expressed in arbitrary units/sec (1/s). The method was validated in rat cortical vessels (correlated with histology)8,9 and clinically in human FA imaging.7,10

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed with SPSS 28.0. Continuous variables are reported as mean (±SD). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences in normally distributed continuous variables were assessed using an independent-samples t test, with effect sizes reported as Cohen d. Most retinal measurements were non-normal, and thus, nonparametric tests were used for analysis (χ2, Mann-Whitney U, Wilcoxon signed-rank). Effect sizes were reported using Cramer V for χ2 and |r| for Mann-Whitney U and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, representing the magnitude of the effect, with direction of change described based on group medians. Median absolute deviation (MAD)–based z-scores were computed for each participant, allowing individuallevel comparisons. Values >|3.5| were considered outliers and excluded.11 Analyses compared single–time point HV data with CADASIL data at FA1 and FA2 and longitudinally assessed FA1-FA2 changes within the CADASIL cohort. Linear regression assessed relationships of age, sex, and UW-FA interval with retinal function measures. Logistic regression estimated odds ratios (95% CI) for associating retinal vessel changes with NOTCH3 variant loci or age at first stroke. Statistical significance was as follows: * < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Standard Protocol, Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the NIH. All procedures adhered to IRB ethical standards, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02821780; NCT03538639).

Results

Study Population

Twenty-eight individuals were included: 19 participants with CADASIL (52.6% female; mean age 48.4 years, range 28–72) and 9 HVs (55.6% female; mean age 44.3 years, range 29–77). The groups did not differ in age (p = 0.97; Cohen d = −0.012, 95% CI [−0.81 to 0.78]) or sex (p = 0.88; Cramer V = 0.027). Comparative data are given in eTable 1. Ophthalmologic examination findings were largely unremarkable and non-progressive. One patient with CADASIL had a poststroke visual field defect, and another was found to have a retinal nerve fiber layer abnormality on optical coherence tomography. In the CADASIL cohort, the mean interval between baseline and follow-up UW-FA was 13.9 ± 4.5 months. Linear regression analyses revealed no significant association between age, sex, or interstudy interval and permeability, blood flow, or perfusion rates in our cohort, excluding these variables as potential confounders (eTable 2).

Baseline and Longitudinal Changes

At baseline, participants with CADASIL had increased BRB permeability in the retinal far periphery compared with HVs (p = 0.03; r = 0.41; Figure 2). At follow-up, participants with CADASIL showed increased mid-periphery permeability (p = 0.003; r = 0.56) and persistently elevated far-peripheral permeability (p = 0.0007; r = 0.64; Figure 2), relative to baseline UW-FA in HVs. No significant intergroup differences were observed in macular permeability, blood flow, or perfusion (across all zones for the latter 2). Accounting for interindividual variability, longitudinal analysis comparing FA1 vs FA2 in the CADASIL cohort (Figure 3) showed reductions in blood flow in the macula (p = 0.01; r = 0.59) and mid-periphery (p = 0.05; r = 0.48) and in perfusion in the macula (p = 0.01; r = 0.59) and midperiphery (p = 0.03; r = 0.54). BRB permeability in the mid-periphery increased at follow-up (p = 0.01; r = 0.89). No new visual symptoms were detected in conjunction with these functional changes.

Genetic and Stroke Age Associations

We assessed the association of NOTCH3 variants in more pathogenic exons (exon 4 or exons 1–8) with retinal vessel dysfunction. No differences were observed in the proportion of participants with these variants among those with significant FA1 to FA2 changes (all p > 0.05). Age at first symptomatic stroke did not predict alterations in permeability, blood flow, or perfusion between FA1 and FA2 (all p > 0.05, eTable 2).

Discussion

Serial UW-FA scans revealed progressive microvascular dysfunction with region-specific vulnerability in participants with CADASIL over a mean follow-up of 13.9 months, considering single–time point HV data. BRB permeability was increased mainly in the mid-periphery and far periphery, while blood flow and perfusion were reduced in the macula and mid-periphery (eTable 3). It is important to note that the observed changes were supported by substantial effect sizes, indicating that the differences were of a clinically meaningful magnitude. These findings are consistent with microvascular endothelial dysfunction, a central feature of CADASIL, characterized by impaired neurovascular coupling, hypoperfusion, and increased BBB permeability, as demonstrated in CADASIL mouse models and humans.12 Given the structural and functional parallels between retinal and cerebral vessels, retinal microvascular changes may reflect underlying cerebral microvascular pathology. Concurrent dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI is set to validate retinal findings as an accurate index of cSVD progression. If retinal-cerebral concordance is confirmed, the retina could serve as a sentinel for monitoring the clinical course of CADASIL and assessing therapeutic responses.13 The observed changes in this study may suggest that UW-FA follow-up intervals could optimally be extended, demonstrating how longitudinal, data-driven adjustments can refine study protocols and guide patient management.

More pathogenic NOTCH3 variants and earlier stroke onset were not associated with greater retinal alterations in this cohort. The modest sample size and the variability of dynamic vascular measures may account for the lack of observed associations. Larger, multicenter studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings. Overall, our results suggest that UW-FA can capture evolving small vessel pathology in CADASIL, particularly in previously unexplored retinal regions, offering a potentially valuable tool for illuminating disease trajectories and informing risk stratification and therapeutic decisions.
