Recombinant rSF toxoid enhances efficacy of bacterin-inactivated vaccine against F18+ Escherichia coli-induced edema disease in swine
Authors: Yuqing Liu, Huawei Wu, Fanghong Zhang, Xiaolin Yang, Tinghua Huang, Bin Wu, Guoping Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEdema disease (ED) and post-weaning diarrhea (PWD) are major health and economic challenges in the swine industry, primarily caused by F18+ Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli (STEC). These diseases typically occur within 1–2 weeks after weaning and are associated with high morbidity and mortality, resulting in substantial economic losses worldwide. The pathogenesis of ED is largely mediated by bacterial adhesion via F18 fimbriae, particularly the FedF adhesin, and systemic toxemia induced by Shiga-like toxin IIe (SLT-IIe). Although passive or active immunization with SLT-IIe toxoids or detoxified variants can provide some protection, their immunogenicity is often limited and may be accompanied by adverse effects. Currently, no commercial vaccines are available, and reliance on antibiotics has accelerated the emergence of multidrug-resistant E. coli. Therefore, the development of safe and effective vaccines targeting both F18 fimbrial adhesion and SLT-IIe-mediated toxemia is urgently needed.ResultsRecombinant rSF toxoid (fusion of SLT-IIe B subunit and FedF adhesin) mixed with Ee strain formed a novel rSF toxoid bacterin-inactivated vaccine. Compared to conventional bacterin-inactivated vaccine, the rSF vaccine group showed significantly higher protection efficacy against F18+ SLTEC challenge, with reduced histological scores, attenuated inflammation, decreased creatinine levels, and minimized kidney damage, alongside increased average daily gain and nitric oxide levels.ConclusionrSF toxoid addition safely enhances protection efficacy against F18+ SLTEC and alleviates renal pathology.

Introduction

Edema disease (ED) and post-weaning diarrhea (PWD) are major health challenges in the swine industry, primarily caused by Shiga-like toxin-producing Escherichia coli (SLTEC), particularly serotypes O138, O139, and O141 [1, 2]. The disease typically occurs 1-2 weeks after weaning, a critical period during which weaned piglets experience substantial dietary and environmental changes. These stressors, along with disruption of the gut microbiota, render weaned piglets highly susceptible to colonization by pathogenic E. coli strains [3]. Clinically, ED progresses rapidly and is associated with high morbidity and mortality, often leading to acute neurological signs and sudden death.

Early studies estimated that PWD alone could cause mortality in over 2% of weaned piglets, particularly in herds with a history of high pre-weaning mortality. A case study from 2000 estimated direct losses of $ 3.13–$ 5.88 per nursery pig due to PWD. With over 70 million pigs produced annually in the United States, even a conservative estimate of $ 3 per head equates to $ 200 million in yearly losses. Globally, the estimated loss exceeds $ 2 billion annually based on a swine population of over 700 million (USDA, 2023). These staggering figures underscore the urgent need for effective preventive strategies against F18+ E. coli-associated diseases [4].

The pathogenesis of ED is mediated by two major virulence factors: F18 fimbriae and Shiga-like toxin IIe (SLT-IIe). F18 fimbriae facilitate bacterial adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells through specific interaction with glycosphingolipid receptors containing ABH blood group determinants on the enterocytes of weaned piglets [5]. The FedF protein is the terminal adhesin of F18 fimbriae and has been identified as the key mediator of this interaction. Notably, antibodies against FedF, but not against FedA or FedE, have been shown to block adhesion of F18+ E. coli to porcine enterocytes, underscoring its potential as a protective antigen [6, 7].

The genetic architecture of the fed operon, which encodes F18 fimbriae, consists of five genes (fedA, fedB, fedC, fedE, and fedF) responsible for structural subunits, chaperone, and usher proteins [8–10]. FedF is highly conserved across F18+ E. coli strains worldwide, with no significant mutations identified between F18ab and F18ac antigenic variants [10]. FedF-based ELISAs have demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity and have been successfully used in sero-surveillance to link F18+ E. coli with outbreaks of diarrhea in weaned piglets in China. These findings reinforce FedF’s value as a rational target for vaccine development.

SLT-IIe, the other critical virulence determinant, is an AB5-type exotoxin composed of an enzymatically active A subunit and five receptor-binding B subunits [11]. While the A subunit is responsible for systemic toxicity, the B pentamer binds to host globotriaosylceramide (Gb3/Gb4) receptors, enabling cellular entry. Immunization studies have shown that the B subunit alone, although non-toxic, is capable of eliciting neutralizing antibodies that prevent toxin-receptor interaction [12, 13]. However, the monomeric B subunit suffers from limited immunogenicity and short-lived antibody responses in vivo [14], reducing its standalone effectiveness as a vaccine antigen.

Both passive and active immunization strategies using SLT-IIe toxoid or genetically detoxified SLT-IIe variants such as E167Q have demonstrated protective efficacy in animal models [15–17]. Nonetheless, these toxoids have been associated with local irritation or systemic reactions post-vaccination, especially in pigs, and their protection is not always sustained [16]. Therefore, combining antigens targeting both bacterial adhesion (FedF) and toxemia (SLT-IIeB) may offer superior protection.

To address these limitations, a recombinant fusion protein composed of SLT-IIe B subunit and FedF-referred to as rSF toxoid—was developed. This strategy is designed to enhance immunogenicity, elicit broader protective responses, and avoid the adverse effects associated with native toxin-based formulations [18].

Currently, there is no commercial vaccine available for ED. In response to the global spread of F18+ E. coli in pig farms, antibiotic treatment has historically been used as a primary control strategy. However, the extensive and often prophylactic application of antibiotics has markedly accelerated the emergence of antimicrobial-resistant pathogens [11]. For instance, studies have shown that in over half of nursery pig herds affected by F18+ E. coli outbreaks, the pathogen persisted despite antibiotic treatment [19]. This highlights the limitations of relying solely on antimicrobials to control such infections. Considering the increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant strains, the high mortality and morbidity associated with edema disease, the substantial economic burden, and the potential risks to public health, it is imperative to develop and implement effective non-antibiotic alternatives—particularly safe and immunogenic vaccines targeting key virulence factors such as FedF and SLT-IIe.Moreover, the widespread use of antibiotics in pig production has led to increasing antimicrobial resistance among porcine E. coli isolates [20], further highlighting the urgent need for effective vaccines. In this study, we evaluated the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of a novel rSF toxoid-based bacterin-inactivated vaccine in mice and weaned piglets. The findings aim to provide a viable strategy for preventing ED by targeting both the adhesion and toxin-mediated stages of SLTEC infection.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, cultivation and animals

The SLTEC Ee strain, a virulent field isolate, was obtained from a pig on a farm in Wuhan during an outbreak of edema disease (ED). The BL21 (DE3) strain was a recombinant E. coli harboring the pKSF plasmid for the production of recombinant rSF toxoid. Genetically modified E. coli cultures were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C, whereas SLTEC Ee cultures were propagated in Tryptic Soy broth or on Tryptic Soy Agar.

This study adopted two animal models. The mouse model was selected for its suitability in evaluating immunogenicity, antibody responses, and toxin neutralization under controlled laboratory conditions. Pigs provided a pathophysiologically relevant model of F18+ E. coli-induced edema disease, allowing direct assessment of vaccine-mediated protection, organ pathology, and practical applicability in the field. Together, these two models provided a comprehensive framework for both mechanistic and translational evaluation of the rSF toxoid bacterin-inactivated vaccine. A total of 132 female Kunming mice (5–6 weeks old, 20 ± 2 g) were obtained from the Hubei Academy of Medical Sciences and randomly assigned to three treatment groups. In each group, four mice were used for serum antibody titration, and 40 mice were used for challenge experiments. Mice were housed 3–5 per individually ventilated cage under a 12 h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C and 50–70% relative humidity, with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Additionally, 60 weaned female weaned piglets (Duroc \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} Landrace \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} Yorkshire hybrids, 28–30 days old, 7–10 kg) were obtained from a commercial breeding facility in Shandong Province and randomly assigned to four treatment groups (n = 15 per group). Within each group, five weaned piglets were used for safety assessment of the subunit bacterin vaccine, and the remaining ten weaned piglets for challenge experiments. Weaned piglets were group-housed in pens of five animals, with identical pen environments across all treatment groups. The stock density (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.2\ \textrm{m}^2$$\end{document} per pig) and the feeder space (30 cm per pig) were standardized to ensure equal access to food and water. Because pen environments were equivalent, pen effects were minimized and were not considered as a blocking factor in statistical analyses. Animals were provided ad libitum access to water and a commercial diet containing 21% crude protein. Ten pregnant sows of the same genetic background were used to evaluate the safety of the subunit bacterin vaccine during gestation. Sows were housed individually in standard gestation stalls under commercial farm management, with unrestricted access to feed and water.

All pigs were pre-screened to confirm the absence of antibodies against Ee somatic antigen (agglutination test) and rSF (ELISA), and were culture-negative for F18+ SLTEC by rectal swab enrichment. Randomization was applied for group assignment, and the safety study was conducted in the same facility under consistent housing and management conditions.

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Huazhong Agricultural University and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and AVMA euthanasia protocols (2020).

Safety of rSF toxoid bacterin-inactivated in pigs

Safety of rBI was evaluated by injecting the vaccine to 28-day weaned piglets, and early-pregnant sows. Ten weaned piglets or ten pregnant pigs were randomly divided into two groups. One was rBI group, and the other was blank control, for a subcutaneous injection (S.C) of 4 ml of rBI (a double immune dose) and 4 ml TSB-inactivated, respectively, either in pregnant pigs or in weaned piglets. Pregnant pigs were dosed 3 weeks before giving birth. After that, weaned piglets were observed continuously for two weeks after vaccination. The injection site and the possible side effects were observed, and the body temperature (rectal temperature) was measured for a week. Pregnant pigs were kept under inspection till to the end of pregnancy and parturition. Observation was made for the incidence of abortion, still birth or birth of weak off-spring.

Immunization and sampling

Five- to six-week-old female Kunming mice were housed in filter-top cages in an air-conditioned animal facility in the Experimental Animal Center of Huazhong Agricultural University (Wuhan, China). A total of 132 mice were randomly divided into three groups, group A (rBI treatment), group B (IVC) and group C (BC) (Table 1). Forty mice per group were employed to evaluate the protective efficacy and another 4 mice were used for monitoring antibody titers. Immunization doses were 0.2 ml. After 21 days, all animals were boosted with the same dose of the corresponding vaccine.

Sixty Duroc \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} Landrace \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} Yorkshire (DLY) hybrid weaned piglets (28–30 days old, all female) were used for evaluation in the natural host. All weaned piglets were confirmed to be antibody-negative by the Ee somatic antigen-based agglutination test and rSF-based ELISA, and culture-negative for SLTEC by enrichment of rectal swabs. The weaned piglets were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 15 per group). In each group, 10 weaned piglets were used for protective efficacy evaluation, and 5 weaned piglets were used for monitoring antibody titers. The fourth group served as the normal control (naïve piglets without vaccination or infection). Twenty-one days post-primary immunization, all weaned piglets were administered a booster dose of the same vaccine.

Sample sizes for both mice and pigs were determined based on preliminary experiments and prior studies on SLTEC vaccine efficacy. For weaned piglets, 5 animals per group was consistent with published swine challenge models and was sufficient to reveal meaningful clinical, biochemical, and histopathological outcomes while adhering to the 3Rs principle.

Blood samples were collected from the retro-orbital sinus of anesthetized mice. Weaned piglet blood samples were obtained from all animals at each sampling points. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were used to measure the antibodies raised the SLT-IIe B or FedF protein. The serum from the immunized mice was added as the first antibody after blocking with 1 % BSA (Sangon, China), with peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Jackson) as the secondary antibody. The absorbance was read at 450 nm. Endpoint titers were recorded as the reciprocal of the highest serial dilution of immune serum at which the OD450 was at least twice that of the control-immune serum from the control immunized mice or weaned piglets. The somatic agglutination method was processed according to standard serotyping to monitor the antibody titers for Ee somatic [22].

Biochemical parameters and Pro-inflammatory-cytokine in serum

The serum samples obtained from pigs 72 h after challenge were for the determination of the levels of Glutamic-Pyruvic Transaminase (GPT), Total Bilirubin (TBIL) and Creatinine (CR) (Huamei, Co., LTD) on the machine AU5800 (Beckman). The nitric oxide (NO) level was detected by an ELISA kit according to its manufacturer’s instructions (Jingmei, Co., LTD).

Histological scores of renal tubule

For light microscopy, kidneys were fixed in 10% formaldehyde solution embedded in paraffin and sections stained with Haematoxylin and eosin. Microscopically histological changes of renal tubule caused by SLTEC were the representative, including lesions of epithelial cells of renal tubule, exudation lesions in renal tubule and diffuse degree of renal tubular lesions. The severity of each kind of the three pathological changes was assessed using a semi-quantification method and classified as normal, mild, moderate or severe on the basis of the following criteria: 0 = normal, no change; 1 = mild, minimal pathological change limited to swelling, degeneration and necrosis of epithelial cells; 2 = moderate, exudation and embolic of red blood cells in renal tubule; 3 = severe, Fibrin exudation or protein like exudation in renal tubule. 4 = diffuse degree of renal tubular injury. The histological score for each pathological change was calculated by multiplying its score by its weight on the basis of the change’s importance in the pathology of renal tubule caused by SLTEC congruent with STEC [13]. The total histological scores for each kidney was obtained by addition of the histological score given for each of the four pathological changes and performed in a blinded fashion.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were designed as completely randomized designs (CRD). The experimental unit was defined as a single animal for in vivo studies and as an individual sample for in vitro assays. Sample sizes were determined based on prior studies and preliminary experiments to achieve at least 80% statistical power to detect biologically meaningful differences between groups. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27 software. The normality of data distribution was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student’s t-test was applied. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to correct for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P < 0.05$$\end{document} and extremely significant at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P < 0.01$$\end{document}.

Results

Safety test in pigs

No pigs showed any systemic symptoms such as breathing, appetite and mental state after vaccination with provided vaccines. No abortion, stillbirth, or weak offspring were observed in pregnant pigs following vaccination.

Protection after challenge infection

At week 3 post-booster vaccination, weaned piglets were challenged with the virulent VTEC Ee strain via intragastric administration, and mice received serial doses of purified SLT-IIe toxin subcutaneously Ee strain via intragastric administration in weaned piglets and the seral dose of purified SLT-II e toxin via S.C route in mice [21]. In the mouse model, rSF-vaccinated animals showed strong protection: as the challenge dose increased from 2.3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}g to 288.8 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}g, only one mouse died at the highest dose (9/10 survived). In contrast, Ee-challenged mice exhibited 100% survival at lower doses, which decreased to 70% survival at higher doses (one or three deaths, respectively), indicating that rSF conferred greater tolerance to high-dose toxin challenge. Control animals had survival rates close to 0%, reflecting the minimal protective effect of adjuvant alone. In the weaned piglet model, all rSF-vaccinated weaned piglets survived (10/10), whereas three weaned piglets in the Ee group (7/10) and only one weaned piglet in the control group (1/10) survived, with deaths occurring within 14 days post-challenge. Neutralization of VT cytolytic activity

To assess whether the rSF toxoid elicits neutralizing antibodies, sera from vaccinated weaned piglets were collected at multiple time points and tested in a VT neutralization assay. Previous data indicated that New Zealand rabbit sera contained IgG against SLT-IIe B at a titer of 1:4096 (ELISA). Figure 3 summarizes the cytotoxicity of SLT-IIe on Vero-E6 cells and the neutralizing effect of rBI-induced sera (Fig. 3). Vero-E6 cells, which highly express SLT-IIe receptors, were used as the assay model. Cells exposed to SLT-IIe without serum or with undiluted mouse anti-GST monoclonal antibody displayed morphological alterations, detachment, and lysis (Fig. 3A). Sera diluted 1:512 neutralized 50% of the cytotoxicity of 20 ng/mL SLT-IIe (Fig. 3B), whereas a 1:48 dilution completely abolished the effect (Fig. 3C). Supernatants from the non-toxin-producing DH5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} strain had no cytotoxic effect, and normal cell morphology was observed (Fig. 3D). No inhibition was observed with ST polyclonal antibody controls. All control groups—including Ee bacterin-inactivated, blank, and naïve piglets—remained negative for neutralizing antibodies. Clinical index after challenge

All the dead mice showed nervous disorder and respiratory trouble before death. The other mice that survived the infection developed clinical signs to a certain extent, including ruffled fur, hunched posture, and decreased activity in the week post challenge. Fever was observed in six days after challenge with a body temperature ranging from 40.5 to 41.1°C (Fig. 4). In total, a significantly higher temperature was observed in non-vaccinated weaned piglets after infection than in other groups. Consequently all weaned piglets in this group appeared ataxia, a classical sign of edema disease and only one pig showed the similar appearance in the bacterin-inactivated group, whereas ataxia didn’t happen to rBI, accompaniment of anorexia and decreased activity in a week post challenge. Compared to naïve piglets (without vaccination and challenge), the other two groups showed a significant elevated body temperature, then returned to normal on day 3 or day 6 after challenge in the rBI group or Ee bacterin-inactivated group, respectively. Histology of kidney

As shown in Fig. 5 and Table 4, the mean histological score given for the saline-treatment group was at a level of 14.75 at the end of the experiment. The rBI treatment, as expected, showed an obvious reduction in histological score. A significant reduction in histological score was also observed in the IVC-treated group, whereas the rBI treatment resulted in a more pronounced reduction, which reached statistical significance in comparison with the IVC treatment. There was no difference between the rBI treatment and naïve piglets. Representative kidney histopathology of the experimental groups is shown in Fig. 5C. The characteristics of kidney in weaned piglets without vaccination treatment (blank control) are renal cortical thickness, intestinal edema, diffuse lesions in glomerular, proliferation of endothelial cells and mesangial cell, infiltration of neutrophil and mononuclear cells and capillary cavity under pressure, also exudation of unknown nature in tubules, embolism of RBC, deformity and swelling of tubular epithelial cells. By comparison, the weaned piglets treated with IVC revealed an obvious reduction in histological changes to the extent of focal lesions in the cortical and fibrin exudation in tubules. Fortunately, a dramatic decrease of histological changes happened in the rBI treatment, which was almost like naïve piglets. Discussion

Although the serotype of E. coli associated with ED are relatively specific, over a dozen known serotypes exist, which complicates disease prevention and control. Traditional inactivated vaccine often fail to provide adequate protection across these serotypes due to antigenic diversity. In contrast, the virulence factors of SLTEC eliciting Edema disease of swine are relatively fixed, which is mainly SLT-II e toxin and F18 fimbriae. Among them, SLT-II e is the direct pathogenic factor causing the symptoms of edema, while its B subunit (SLT-II e B), lacking enzymatic activity, serves as a protective antigen and is highly conserved among global isolates. Previous studies have underscored the potential of SLT-IIe B as a vaccine antigen. For example, immunization with a genetically detoxified mutant (E167Q) of SLT-IIe conferred complete protection in weaned piglets against oral challenge with SLT-IIe-producing E. coli, while unvaccinated controls exhibited typical clinical signs [24]. These findings highlight the cross-protective potential of SLT-IIe B and support its inclusion in vaccines targeting both bacterial and toxigenic components [18].

The large-scale extraction and purification of native SLT-IIe B subunit is technically challenging and cost-prohibitive due to its small molecular weight and poor yield. Recombinant expression systems offer a more practical and scalable approach for vaccine production. In addition to SLT-IIe, the F18 fimbrial adhesin FedF plays a critical role in bacterial colonization and is also recognized as a protective antigen. In our previous work [18], a recombinant fusion toxoid (rSF) combining SLT-IIe B and FedF elicited a stronger immune response than either antigen alone in murine models, demonstrating the benefit of antigen co-delivery. This dual-antigen approach is further supported by studies demonstrating that delivery systems expressing both SLT-IIe B and F18 or FedF adhesin—such as Salmonella ghost vaccines—elicited antigen-specific IgA and IgG responses and conferred approximately 85–92% protection against lethal E. coli challenge in murine models, highlighting the immunological synergy of targeting both toxin and colonization factors [25].Their findings highlight the synergistic immune benefit of targeting both toxin-mediated and colonization-associated virulence mechanisms. Consistent with this strategy, we selected the rSF fusion toxoid as a substitute for native toxin in the formulation of a recombinant bacterin-inactivated vaccine (rBI), using formalin-inactivated E. coli (Ee) as a carrier. Although the inactivated Ee vaccine alone elicited strong humoral responses, inducing antibodies against somatic antigens and F18 fimbriae, it failed to provide full protection against lethal challenge with a homologous strain in either mice or weaned piglets. To clarify the immunogenic contribution of the rSF toxoid, we deliberately reduced the O-antigen content of the inactivated Ee vaccine, which enabled a more precise assessment of protective efficacy conferred by rSF. The limited protection observed with bacterin-only vaccination reflects the underlying pathogenesis of ED: although bacterial clearance can be achieved through antibody-mediated mechanisms [26], the SLT-IIe toxin remains systemically active and induces severe neurological and endothelial damage. Thus, protective immunity must also neutralize circulating toxins, not just clear bacteria. Our results suggest that the culture supernatant of SLT-IIe-producing strains contains sufficient levels of secreted SLT-IIe and F18 to elicit low-level humoral responses, but the antibody titers induced by bacterin alone are insufficient to neutralize toxin activity and prevent disease progression.

A notable observation was the difference in F18-specific antibody titers between groups, with the rBI group displaying higher titers than the inactivated vaccine (IVC) group, despite similar fluctuation trends over time. One hypothesis is that SLT-IIe B enhances antigen presentation via receptor-mediated pathways on vascular endothelialcells, thereby potentiating both specific and nonspecific immune responses [27]. If validated, this would suggest that SLT-IIe B may function not only as an immunogen but also as an immune enhancer or adjuvant.

Compared to Ee inactivated vaccine, the rBI conferred immune response contributed to a higher protection efficacy against homologous challenge whether in the mice or weaned piglets’ infection models, which can intrigue a higher neutralizing antibody level for the SLT-IIe toxin in vivo in animals. Therefore an augmented neutralizing antibody level should be attributed to an addition of the rSF toxoid, which means the recombinant rSF toxoid could be a substitute for the natural toxoid to be an ingredient for the vaccine.

Three days post-challenge, serum nitric oxide (NO) concentrations were significantly elevated in both the rBI and IVC groups compared to the control, likely due to immune complex deposition and endothelial activation. Elevated NO levels can promote vasodilation and mitigate thrombotic risks by activating the cGMP pathway in smooth muscle cells, reducing the pathological consequences of immune complex deposition [28]. These vascular protective effects may contribute to improved tissue perfusion and reduced organ damage.

Liver function analysis indicated that SLTEC infection in the control group caused no significant glutamic-pyruvic transaminase (GPT) elevation, suggesting minimal direct hepatotoxicity. However, in vaccinated groups, GPT levels were slightly elevated, possibly reflecting the burden of increased antigen processing. This phenomenon aligns with reports on STEC O157 infections, where GPT and TBIL levels are sensitive indicators of liver burden and detoxification processes [29]. Changes of three biochemical parameters, including NO, GPT and TBIL, reflected addition of rSF toxoid providing higher protection efficacy. Further, in view of renal damage usually caused by O157 infection in humans [30], the role of this vaccine for protecting the kidney from damage was evaluated by detecting the changes of the renal function after the challenge. CR level was an index of the renal function of the patients with progressive damage or prognosis of renal failure [31]. CR in the control group was higher than that in naïve piglets, suggesting that kidney function of infected weaned piglets was damaged, and histological changes elucidated this point. So it was thought that the kidney was more sensitive to SLTEC infection. CR in the vaccine group was significantly lower than in the control group, indicating that vaccination protected renal function to some extent. Further statistical results showed that CR levels in the rBI group were significantly lower than those in the IVC group, indicating a stronger protective effect on the kidney by the rBI vaccine compared to the single IVC vaccine. In summary, addition of rSF toxoid to the Ee inactivated vaccine contributed to alleviating kidney injury to near-normal levels without damage.

Histological and clinical assessments further validated the superior protection conferred by the rBI vaccine. While the IVC group displayed signs of decreased weight gain, elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, and microscopic kidney damage, these indicators were absent or minimal in the rBI group.This aligns with previous fndings showing that immunization with SLT-IIe-based antigens efectively reduced organ damage following SLTEC infection or purifedtoxin challenge [21]. Moreover, no adverse effects were observed in weaned piglets or pregnant sows vaccinated with rBI, in contrast to reports of irritation caused by native SLT-IIe toxoid [32], reinforcing the safety of this recombinant formulation.

A potential limitation of this study is the lack of endotoxin quantification in the recombinant rSF toxoid preparation. Since lipopolysaccharide (LPS) contamination from E. coli expression systems may influence immune responses or cause toxicity, future studies should include endotoxin assessment using assays such as the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) test. Endotoxin removal steps should also be incorporated into vaccine manufacturing to ensure safety and consistency. However, it is important to note that the E. coli host strain used does not express F18 fimbriae or SLT-IIe toxin, and residual LPS in the purified recombinant protein did not cause observable cytopathology or abnormal immune responses in animal models. Therefore, the impact of endotoxin contamination in this preclinical evaluation is considered limited and manageable.

Regarding hematologic and biochemical safety assessments, we selected representative parameters, including liver enzymes (GPT/ALT), creatinine (CR), and nitric oxide (NO), to evaluate hepatic and renal function as well as vascular responses. These markers are widely accepted and provide reliable insight into organ function and immune activation. The selected parameters adequately address the primary safety concerns of this study. No significant abnormalities were detected, supporting the conclusion that the vaccine does not adversely affect liver or kidney function.

In addition to the absence of endotoxin quantification and incomplete hematologic profiling, several other limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the duration of vaccine-induced immune protection was not assessed beyond the short-term period post-immunization. Evaluating the persistence of antibody responses and memory immunity over an extended timeframe will be critical for establishing the long-term efficacy of the rSF toxoid vaccine. Second, although the rBI vaccine conferred protection against a homologous SLTEC strain, its cross-protective capacity against heterologous strains of diverse serotypes (e.g., O138, O139, O141) remains to be investigated. Future challenge studies using genetically and antigenically diverse SLTEC strains are warranted to confirm the breadth of protection.

From a translational perspective, practical hurdles must be considered for real-world implementation. These include the scalability and cost-efficiency of large-scale recombinant vaccine production in veterinary settings, cold-chain logistics for field distribution, and potential regulatory requirements for licensure. Acceptance by end users, such as pig farmers, will also depend on ease of administration, cost-benefit outcomes, and demonstrated safety under field conditions. Addressing these issues through industrial process development and field trials will be essential for moving the rSF toxoid vaccine toward commercialization.

Overall, the rSF toxoid bacterin-inactivated vaccine demonstrated superior immunogenicity and protection compared to inactivated bacterin vaccine alone. By targeting both bacterial adhesion and toxin activity, and without observable side effects, the rBI formulation represents a promising strategy for safe and effective prevention of swine edema disease. Its efficacy and safety are supported by immunologic, clinical, biochemical, and histological data, and future studies should explore its application in broader field conditions and against heterologous strains.

Conclusion

The addition of rSF toxoid to a traditional bacterin-inactivated ED vaccine significantly enhanced protective efficacy, reduced microscopic histopathological kidney injury, and showed good safety in both weaned piglets and pregnant sows. These findings suggest that the rSF toxoid bacterin-inactivated vaccine is a promising candidate for further development as a safe and effective strategy for the prevention and control of swine edema disease.
