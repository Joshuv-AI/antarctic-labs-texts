Apoptosis-induced translocation of nesprin-2 from the nuclear envelope to mitochondria is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction
Authors: Hila Zohar, Liora Lindenboim, Oren Gozlan, Gregg G Gundersen, Howard J Worman, Reuven Stein
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAccumulating evidence suggests that the nuclear envelope (NE) is not just a target, but also a mediator of apoptosis. We showed recently that the NE protein nesprin-2 has pro-apoptotic activity, which involves its subcellular redistribution and Bcl-2 proteins. Here we further characterize the pro-apoptotic activity of nesprin-2 focusing on its redistribution. We assessed the redistribution kinetics of endogenous nesprin-2 tagged with GFP relative to apoptosis-associated mitochondrial dysfunction. The results show apoptosis-induced GFP-nesprin-2G redistribution occurred by two different modes – complete and partial, both lead to appearance of nesprin-2G near the mitochondria. Moreover, GFP-nesprin-2 redistribution is associated with reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and precedes the appearance of morphological features of apoptosis. Our results show that nesprin-2G redistribution and translocation near mitochondria is an early apoptotic effect associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, which may be responsible for the pro-apoptotic function of nesprin-2.KEYWORDS: Apoptosis, LINC complex, mitochondria, nesprin-2, nuclear envelope, nucleus

Introduction

Apoptosis is a form of programmed cell death characterized by morphological alterations such as cell shrinkage, nuclear fragmentation and the appearance of apoptotic bodies mediated by a series of sequential events that culminate in cell death [1]. Two main protein families regulate and execute apoptosis: Bcl-2 family proteins and caspases. The Bcl-2 protein family comprises pro-survival and pro-apoptotic sub-families that act mainly, but not only, by regulating the integrity of mitochondria (for reviews see [2–6]). The pro-apoptotic sub-family comprises Bax and Bak as well as the Bcl-2 homology domain 3 (BH3)-only proteins such as Bid, Bim and Bad. Bax and Bak are the executioner members of the family. During apoptosis they are activated and form pores in the mitochondrial outer membrane that promote mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), causing release of apoptogenic factors such as cytochrome c and second mitochondria-derived activator of caspase [(SMAC, also known as direct IAP binding protein with low pI (DIABLO)] from the mitochondrial inter-membrane space [7]. This leads to activation of caspases, cysteine aspartate proteases that cleave a subset of essential cellular proteins [8], by cytochrome c-induced assembly of the caspase-9 activation platform, the apoptosome [9], and by SMAC-mediated inhibition of the caspase inhibitor X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein [10]. The pro-survival sub-family proteins, such as Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, bind to and block the activity of the pro-apoptotic proteins and thus are considered as the guardians of mitochondrial integrity. BH3-only proteins can act as direct activators of executioners or function by inhibiting guardian proteins. Activation of the executioners and the subsequent MOMP is also regulated by localization of the Bcl-2 family proteins such as Bax and Bcl-xL through mitochondrial translocation and retrotranslocation [11,12]. In addition to MOMP, apoptosis also induces reduction of the inner mitochondrial transmembrane potential (ΔΨm). Whether loss of ΔΨm induces MOMP or occurs as a result of MOMP is debatable. Nonetheless, reduction of ΔΨm leads to mitochondrial dysfunction because it is required for mitochondrial functions including ATP production, regulation of metabolite transport and protein import [13].

The nucleus is generally considered an end target of the apoptotic process, which includes chromatin condensation, DNA fragmentation and caspase-catalyzed cleavage of certain nuclear proteins including lamin A/C [14] and nucleoporins [15]. However, some data suggest that the nucleus can also act as a mediator of apoptosis [16,17]. The nucleus is separated from the cytoplasm by the nuclear envelope (NE). However, it is physically connected to the cytoplasm through the cytoskeleton via the Linker of Nucleoskeleton and Cytoskeleton (LINC) complex which is composed of Klarsicht/ANC-1/Syne-1 homology (KASH) domain protein. Most KASH proteins in mammals are referred to as nuclear envelope spectrin repeat proteins (nesprins) [18] in the outer nuclear membrane and Sad1/UNC-84 (SUN) domain-containing proteins in the inner nuclear membrane [19]. SUN proteins interact with lamins at the nuclear lamina [19–21]. The KASH domain of nesprins projects into the perinuclear space, where it interacts with the SUN domain of the SUN proteins [22–24]. The N-termini of nesprins are cytoplasmic where they interact with cytoskeletal elements such as actin, intermediate filaments, and microtubules. As SUN proteins bind to lamins, the LINC complex connects the cytoskeleton to nuclear lamina (for reviews see [25–33]). SYNE2, a gene containing more than 100 exons, encodes multiple nesprin-2 isoforms that arise by alternative RNA splicing and multiple transcriptional initiation sites [18,34]. The largest isoform of nesprin-2 is termed nesprin-2-giant (herein Nes2G). Nes2G has an N-terminal actin-binding domain that consists of tandem calponin homology domains, followed by a rod-like structure composed of multiple spectrin-repeats [34,35]. Binding of Nes2G to actin is also facilitated by interactions with the formin homology domain-containing protein FHOD1 [36–38], fascin [39] and mena [40]. It also interacts with microtubules via a carboxyl terminal region that binds to the light chains of kinesin-1 as well as the cytoplasmic dynein through the adaptor protein BicD2 [41,42]. There are also nesprin-2 isoforms that lack the N-terminal actin-binding domain and interact indirectly with microtubules via microtubule motor proteins and vice versa [42,43].

Recently, we showed that nesprin-2 can act as an apoptotic mediator. Accordingly, we showed that nesprin-2 has pro-apoptotic activity via Bcl-2 family proteins [44]. This may be mediated by an interaction with Bax in close proximity with the mitochondria [45]. We also showed that apoptotic stress induces caspase-independent subcellular redistribution of nesprin-2/Nes2G in close proximity to mitochondria [45]. These results suggest that the apoptotic mechanism of action of Nes2G involves its redistribution. To further assess the role of Nes2G redistribution in apoptosis, in particular its role in the mitochondrial gateway mechanism, we characterized the dynamics of the redistribution process and its association with mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptotic morphology.

Materials and methods

Materials

All reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich unless otherwise specified. Quinoline-VaL-Asp(OMe)-CH2-OPH (QVD) was purchased from Apex Biotechnology.

Cell culture

c-Myc immortalized mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) [46] engineered to express GFP-nesprin-2 or GFP‐nesprin‐2 and mCherry-lamin A/C from their endogenous genes [47] (herein GFP-nesprin-2 MEFs and GFP-nesprin-2/mCh‐LAC MEFs, respectively) were kindly provided by Dr. Patricia M Davidson (Institut Curie, France; present address: 4Dcell/BforCure, Montreuil, France). Immortalized wild type (WT) 3T9, Bcl2l1–/–/Bcl-x–/– (herein Bcl-x–/–) and Bax–/– MEFs [48], were provided by Prof. Christoph Borner (University of Freiburg, Germany). Cells were grown in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum (FCS).

mCherry-SMAC transfection

Transfection was performed using TransIT-X2 (Mirus Bio LLC, MIR 6003) reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. One day before transfection, cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per glass-bottom 35 mm plate. Cells were transfected with 0.5 µg mCherry-SMAC expressing vector [49] (provided by Prof. Sarit Larisch, University of Haifa, Israel). QVD (40 µM) was added 4 h after transfection. Twenty-four h after transfection, culture media was changed to phenol red-free DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FCS and 1% L-glutamine and then the cells were treated with 25 µM cisplatin and 40 µM QVD (applied 1 h before cisplatin) for additional 24 h.

Live cell confocal imaging and analysis

For time-lapse analysis, 3 × 105 GFP-nesprin-2 or GFP‐nesprin‐2/mCh‐LAC MEFs or 1.5 × 105 mCherry-SMAC-transfected GFP-nesprin-2 MEFs were grown on glass-bottom 35 mm plates. One day before imaging, culture media was replaced with phenol red-free DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FCS and 1% L-glutamine and the cells were treated with no drug or with 20 µM QVD or with 25 µM cisplatin without or with 20 µM or 40 µM QVD for 24 h. Imaging was performed using an Andor revolution spinning disk confocal microscope with DPSS CW 50 mW lasers (Andor) (excitation: 561 nm and 488 nm, emission: above 641 nm and 510–540 nm, for red and green channels, respectively). Bright field images were also captured. The imaging apparatus consisted of an Olympus inverted microscope (IX81F-ZDC2) with an oil-immersion Plan-Apochromatic 60× objective NA = 1.42 (Olympus) and an ANDOR iXon Ultra EMCCD camera. The microscope was equipped with a 37°C temperature-controlled chamber and a regulator providing 5% CO2 (Okolab). The equipment was controlled by Andor iQ software.

Image analysis was performed using Fiji software. For multiple Z-planes time-lapse series, Z-stack of the images captured (red and green channels) was performed using sum slices projection or maximum intensity projection. The results of the time-lapse experiments are shown as still images from one focal plane of the multiple Z-planes at the indicated time points, after background subtraction was applied and images were cropped and adjusted for brightness and contrast.

Tetramethylrhodamine (TMRM), MitoTracker Red and MitoTracker Deep Red analysis

For TMRM analysis, 3 × 105 GFP-nesprin-2 cells were cultured overnight on glass-bottom 35 mm plates and were then incubated with 100 nM TMRM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min at 37°C. For MitoTracker Red analysis, GFP-nesprin-2 MEFs were incubated with 100 nM MitoTracker Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min at 37°C. Media was then replaced with phenol red-free DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FCS and 1% L-glutamine and the cells were treated with no drug or with 25 µM cisplatin with 20 µM QVD for 24 h, after which time-lapse imaging was performed.

For TMRM and MitoTracker Deep Red analysis, 105 GFP-nesprin-2 MEFs were grown overnight on 18 mm cover slips coated with collagen and then incubated with 100 nM TMRM and 100 nM MitoTracker Deep Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min at 37°C. Media was then replaced with phenol red-free media, and the cells were treated with 25 µM cisplatin and 20 µM QVD for additional 24 h, after which images of live cells were captured using a confocal fluorescent microscope (Olympus I×81 microscope supplemented with Abberior STEDYCON device) using a ×40 NA 0.95 objective lens.

Immunofluorescence staining for assessing cytochrome c release and/or Nes2G redistribution

To assess Nes2G redistribution and cytochrome c release, 1 × 105 cells (WT MEFs) or 2 × 105 (Bcl-x–/– or Bax–/– MEFs) were cultured on 18 mm cover slips coated with collagen. After treatment with no drug or with 25 µM cisplatin and 20 µM QVD, the cells were fixed and incubated with the following primary antibodies: mouse anti-cytochrome c Ab (BD Biosciences, cat. number: 556432, 1:200) or rabbit anti-Nes2G Ab (1:200) [50], and stained with the Hoechst dye 33258, as previously described [45]. Nes2G redistribution was defined as appearance of cytosolic Nes2G and absence of, or substantial reduction of Nes2G from the NE. Cytochrome c release was defined as a diffused pattern (as opposed to punctate) of cytochrome c. The percentage of cells exhibiting Nes2G redistribution or cytochrome c release was manually determined by counting the corresponding cells under a fluorescence microscope (EVOS Cell Imaging Systems, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using X63 NA 1.4 objective lens. Images were captured using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (excitation, 405, 488 and 638 nm; emission 415–485, 498–568 and 657–727, for blue, greed and far-red channels).

Determination of the initiation time of Nes2G redistribution, TMRM signal reduction and SMAC release in the time-lapse series

In each time-lapse video, cells undergoing complete Nes2G redistribution were identified. Then, the first time points from the beginning of the recording when Nes2G redistribution, reduction in TMRM signal, mCherry-SMAC release or apoptotic appearance appeared were identified visually by 3 (for Nes2G redistribution, reduced TMRM signal and SMAC release) or 2 (for apoptotic appearance) independent observes in a blinded fashion to avoid confirmation bias. In addition, the reduction in TMRM signal was also determined according to the intensity curves. Nes2G redistribution was determined as reduction in the presence of GFP-Nes2G in the NE ring and appearance of cytosolic GFP-Nes2G. Loss of TMRM signal was determined by reduction in its signal. mCherry-SMAC release was determined by a change of its distribution pattern from punctate to a diffuse pattern. Apoptotic appearance was determined by cell shrinkage followed by detachment from the matrix and appearance of dynamic membrane blebbing.

Quantification of Nes2G redistribution

To quantify Nes2G redistribution, the intensity of the GFP-Nes2G signal in individual cells from the time-lapse imaging was measured and processed by image thresholding to segment the signal using Python software. The analysis was performed on still images obtained from the time-lapse videos at three time points – at the beginning of the recording (T0), at the first time point when Nes2G redistribution was evident (T1) and at the time when Nes2G was completely redistributed (T2) in cells exhibiting complete Nes2G redistribution. The distances between the center of the nucleus and all Nes2G pixels in the image were then calculated and averaged. For internal control, a similar analysis was performed also on cells that did not undergo Nes2G redistribution, in the same captured fields, at the corresponding time points (herein non-redistributed cells). The results were expressed as a mean of the mean distances obtained for each tested cell at the different time points. Image analysis and calculations were performed using Python software.

Quantification of lamin A/C distribution and SMAC release

To quantify changes in mCherry-lamin A/C and mCherry-SMAC distribution, the mCherry signal intensity was measured in mCherry-SMAC expressing cells and then the standard deviation (SD) of the pixels values in region of interest (ROI) in the individual cells was determined and normalized to the sum of total pixel values in the ROI. The analysis was performed using Fiji software. For mCherry-lamin A/C, ROI was defined as the entire nucleus region and SD values were obtained at two time points of the time-lapse imaging – at the beginning of the recording (T0) and at the time when Nes2G was completely redistributed in cells exhibiting complete Nes2G redistribution (T2). For mCherry-SMAC, a cytoplasmic region was defined as a ROI and the analysis was performed at three time points – at the beginning of the recording (T0), at the first time point when Nes2G redistribution is evident (T1) and at the time when Nes2G was completely redistributed (T2) in cells that exhibited complete Nes2G redistribution. Similar analyses were performed for mCherry-lamin A/C and mCherry-SMAC in GFP-nesprin-2/mCh‐LAC MEFs and GFP-nesprin-2 MEFs that did not undergo Nes2G redistribution (non-redistributed cells) in the same captured fields, at the corresponding time points. A high SD value represents a punctate/discrete distribution, whereas a low SD value represents a diffusive or even distribution. The results were expressed as the mean of the normalized SD values obtained for the individual tested cells at the different time points.

Quantification of TMRM signal loss

TMRM signal intensity was measured and the sum of pixels’ values in an individual cell was determined at different time points using Fiji software. Images taken from time-lapse imaging were analyzed at the following time points in cells exhibiting complete Nes2G redistribution: at the beginning of the recording (T0), at the first time when Nes2G redistribution was evident (T1), the time when Nes2G was completely redistributed (T2) and the time that was three quarters of the way between the beginning of the recording and the time when Nes2G redistribution was first evident (T3/4). Similar analysis at the corresponding time points was performed in cells that did not undergo Nes2G redistribution in the same captured field (non-redistributed cells). The values for non-redistributed cells for each video are the average of three cells from the same imaged field. The results were expressed as the average of the sum of the values of pixels obtained for the tested cells at the different time points.

Quantification of co-localization of mitochondria with Nes2G

We analyzed MitoTracker Red-stained cells that exhibited complete Nes2G redistribution. Still images of the cells were taken from time-lapse series at two time points – at the beginning of the recording (T0) and the time when Nes2G was completely redistributed (T2). Python software was used to determine in individual cells pixels that contain both red (mitochondria) and green (GFP-Nes2G) signals above threshold (co-localization). The portion of mitochondria co-localized with GFP-Nes2G was then determined by dividing the co-localized pixels values with all red pixels.

Immunoblotting

Total cell lysates were prepared and analyzed by immunoblotting as previously described [45]. Proteins were separated on 4–15% Criterion TGX Precast Gradient Gel (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and electroblotted (3 h, 80 V) onto nitrocellulose membranes. Blots were probed with rabbit anti-nesprin-2 K2 (1:1,000) [51] and rabbit anti-GFP (Invitrogen # A11122) (1:2,000) Abs. Uniformity of sample loading was verified based on the Ponceau staining of the blots as internal control.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test, Fisher’s exact test, Chi-square goodness of fit or Permutation test (with a two-tailed p-value) as indicated in the corresponding Figure legends. For the statistical analysis of the data shown in Figure 6b, we employed McNemar’s test [52]. This test assesses 2 × 2 contingency data to determine whether the data display ‘marginal homogeneity’. It compares the discordance of the contingency table (cells that show Nes2G redistribution without cytochrome c release, or cells that show cytochrome c release but do not show Nes2G redistribution) and determines whether the proportion of the discordance is significantly different and in which direction. A significant result in a specific direction indicates a potential direction of causality in the data. For the statistical analysis of the data shown in Figure 3b, after one-way ANOVA, a post-hoc test was applied to compare the two experimental groups to the control group (using linear contrasts) with correction for multiple comparisons using the False Discovery Rate method (two-stage FDR method of Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli) [53]. For the statistical analyses of the data shown in Figures 2c, 5b, and 6c, we used a two-way repeated measures ANOVA. We compared the two conditions in each time-point, as well as the time points within each condition, using linear contrasts, and then corrected all p-values for multiple comparisons using the two-stage FDR method. Values of p < 0.05 or q < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data were expressed as mean values ± S.E.M.

Results

Apoptotic stress triggers Nes2G dislodgment from the NE to cytoplasm by two different patterns

To characterize the apoptosis-induced Nes2G redistribution process and its potential role in the apoptotic pathway, we first examined its dynamics. We used GFP-nesprin-2 MEFs containing a CRISPR‐Cas9 engineered Syne2 gene that encodes chimeric nesprin-2 isoforms, including GFP-Nes2G, with GFP at their N-termini immediately preceding the actin-binding domain [47]. As shown previously [47], GFP-nesprin-2 was mainly present in the NE as indicated by its ring shape morphology around the nucleus. As only the Nes2G isoform contains both the GFP-tagged actin‐binding domain and the KASH domain mediating outer nuclear membrane localization, the discrete NE localization suggests that the corresponding protein is Nes2G. We therefore termed this protein ‘GFP‐Nes2G’. The expression of the GFP-nesprin-2 was further tested by immunoblot using anti-GFP and anti-nesprin-2 Abs. Both Abs detected a similar band of high molecular mass protein (greater than 250 kDa), in the protein extracts prepared from GFP-nesprin-2 MEFs (Supplementary Materials Fig. S1). A band of about the same molecular mass was detected by the anti-nesprin-2 Abs but not anti-GFP Abs in the protein extract prepared from WT MEFs. The high molecule mass and the localization of the GFP-nesprin-2 in the nuclear envelope strongly suggest that the GFP-tagged specie, as previously mentioned [47] is Nes2G, hence we refer to this as GFP-Nes2G. Note that the intensity of the nesprin-2 high molecular band in the GFP-nesprin-2 MEFs was higher than in WT MEFs. This may reflect stabilization of the nesprin-2 by GFP or different expression level between the two MEF lines.

We next used these MEFs to examine the dynamic fate of endogenous Nes2G challenged with an apoptotic stress. We treated GFP-nesprin-2 MEFs with the apoptotic inducer cisplatin, without or with the pan-caspase inhibitor QVD (to attenuate cell death, thereby increasing the number of cells available for the analysis). Twenty-four h later we assessed GFP-Nes2G distribution by live cell time-lapse microscopy for approximately 1 h. Cells treated with cisplatin without QVD exhibited normal morphology at the time when recording began (t = 0) with confinement of GFP-Nes2G to the NE (Figure 1a). During the recording time, in approximately 4% of the cells (n = 110), GFP-Nes2G underwent a rapid and dynamic loss from the NE until it was no longer detectable at the NE. This loss was followed by redistribution of Nes2G to the cytoplasm (Figure 1a). This effect resulted from the cisplatin treatment since it was not observed in control untreated GFP-nesprin-2 MEFs imaged under similar conditions (n = 72). A similar rapid redistribution of GFP-Nes2G was also observed in approximately 4% of cells (n = 1,819) treated with both cisplatin and QVD that exhibited normal appearance and NE-associated GFP-Nes2G at the beginning of the recording (Figure 1b). The redistributed cytosolic GFP-Nes2G exhibited both diffused and punctate patterns (Figure 1b upper and lower panels, respectively). The median duration of this rapid process was 1 min from start to completion (lack of detectable NE-associated Nes2G) (Figure 1c). We quantified Nes2G redistribution in cells that exhibited complete redistribution and in cells that did not (non-redistributed cells) in the same imaged fields (Figure 1b lower panel). Measurements were made in both these cell groups at the beginning of the recording (T0), at the time when the redistribution was first evident (T1) and when it was completed (T2) (according to the cells which underwent the redistribution process). The mean distance of GFP-Nes2G signal from the center of the nucleus was measured in individual cells of both cell groups. While in the non-redistributed cells this distance did not significantly change between the analyzed time points, the distance was significantly increased at T1 and T2 compared to T0 in the cells exhibiting Nes2G redistribution (Figure 1d). With cisplatin and QVD treatment, we also observed that approximately 12% of the normal-looking cells exhibited a different GFP-Nes2G redistribution pattern that was detected already at the beginning of the recording and did not change during the recording period (Figure 1e). This pattern was characterized by a reduction in the presence of GFP-Nes2G in the NE and appearance of cytosolic GFP-Nes2G, mainly in clusters. We termed these two patterns of Nes2G redistribution complete and partial, respectively. Notably, both these distribution patterns were due to the cisplatin treatment, since very few cells exhibited Nes2G redistribution in GFP-Nes2G MEFs treated with QVD alone (0.28% and 0.58% of the cells with complete or partial Nes2G redistribution patterns, respectively, Supplementary Materials Fig. S2A and B).

The relationship between Nes2G redistribution and lamin A/C

Because Nes2G is part of the LINC complex that binds the lamina via SUN proteins [19,20,54,55], we next examined whether cisplatin-induced Nes2G redistribution is associated with changes in lamin A/C. We used GFP-nesprin-2/mCh-LAC MEFs, a line derived from GFP-Nes2G MEFs in which the Lmna gene has also been engineered to express the red fluorescent protein mCherry coupled to lamin A/C [47]. We treated GFP-nesprin-2/mCh-LAC cells with cisplatin and QVD for 24 h and then assessed the distribution of both GFP-Nes2G and mCherry-lamin A/C in normal-appearing cells by time-lapse microscopy for approximately 1 h. Similar to GFP-Nes2G MEFs, complete Nes2G redistribution occurred in the GFP-nesprin-2/mCh-LAC MEFs (Figure 2a). Some cells also showed the partial redistribution pattern that was evident at the beginning of the recording and did not change during the rest of the recording period (Figure 2b). To examine if changes in Nes2G distribution affected lamin A/C distribution, we analyzed cells with complete Nes2G redistribution. We determined the SD values of the mCherry-lamin A/C signal (normalized to signal intensities) in cells where Nes2G was redistributed and in cells where it did not (non-redistributed cells) at the beginning of the recording (T0) and when Nes2G redistribution was completed (T2) (according to the cells which underwent the redistribution process). The mean of the normalized SD values of mCherry-lamin A/C signal in the Nes2G redistributed cells as well as in the non-redistributed cells did not significantly change between the beginning of the recording and the time when Nes2G redistribution was completed (Figure 2c). This indicates that the lamin A/C distribution did not change, even though Nes2G was completely redistributed. Notably, the normalized SD value in cells exhibiting Nes2G redistribution was significantly higher than that of non-redistributed cells already at the beginning of the recording, indicating that the lamin A/C distribution in the cells that are prone to Nes2G redistribution was modified (showed a less discrete pattern) compared to non-redistributed cells. This altered lamin A/C distribution was not further affected by Nes2G redistribution. As expected, when the cells exhibited apoptotic morphology, lamin A/C showed a deformed appearance (Supplementary Video S5).

Bcl-xL and Bax play a role in regulating Nes2G redistribution

We previously showed that Nes2G can bind Bax [45] and that its pro-apoptotic activity is associated with Bcl-xL [44]. We therefore examined if Bax or Bcl-xL are needed for cisplatin-induced Nes2G redistribution using WT, Bax–/– and Bcl-xL–/– 3T9 MEFs. First, we confirmed that Bax–/– and Bcl-xL–/– MEFs express Nes2G by immunoblotting (Supplementary Materials Fig. S3). We then treated the WT, Bax–/– and Bcl-xL–/– 3T9 MEFs with cisplatin and QVD and fixed and stained them with anti-Nes2G antibodies. In WT 3T9 MEFs treated with no drug, Nes2G was associated with the NE in most of the cells, whereas 13% of the cells lacked NE Nes2G. Treatment with cisplatin and QVD increased the percentage of cells lacking NE Nes2G localization to 76% (Figure 3). Cisplatin plus QVD treatment of Bax–/– and Bcl-xL–/– 3T9 MEFs also increased the percentage of cells lacking NE Nes2G localization (29% and 52%) relative to their corresponding untreated controls (2% and 8%, respectively) (Figure 3). However, the loss of Nes2G NE localization (after subtracting the corresponding untreated control values) in both Bax–/– and Bcl-xL–/– 3T9 MEFs was significantly lower than in WT 3T9 MEFs. These results suggest that Bcl-xL and Bax contribute to cisplatin-induced Nes2G redistribution, however, they are not essential for the process.

Redistributed Nes2G translocates to mitochondria during early stages of apoptosis

The punctate pattern of cytoplasmically redistributed GFP-Nes2G in apoptotic GFP-Nes2G MEFs appeared similar to the subcellular localization of mitochondria. We therefore examined the spatiotemporal relationship between the redistributed Nes2G and mitochondria. Redistributed Nes2G that exhibited a punctate appearance was localized in close proximity to mitochondria and in some cases even co-localized with mitochondria labeled using MitoTracker Red (Figure 4). The colocalization with mitochondria was observed for Nes2G that was redistributed either completely (Figure 4a) or partially (Figure 4c). Quantification of the co-localization of GFP-Nes2G with mitochondria in cells before and after complete redistribution, showed that GFP-Nes2G – mitochondria colocalization was significantly increased following Nes2G redistribution (Figure 4b). Notably, after complete GFP-Nes2G redistribution, the cells underwent dynamic changes in their morphology typical of apoptotic cells, including cell shrinkage, detachment from the matrix and plasma membrane blebbing and bubbling (n = 52 as exemplified in the bright field images in Figure 4a; t = 22 min).

Nes2G redistribution is accompanied by loss of mitochondrial membrane potential

The redistribution of Nes2G to the proximity of mitochondria suggests that this process may lead to apoptosis via the mitochondrial gateway. If this is indeed the case, Nes2G redistribution should coincide or precede hallmarks of mitochondrial apoptosis such as a decline in ΔΨm and MOMP. Therefore, we examined the temporal relationship between Nes2G redistribution and these apoptotic mitochondrial events. Reduction in ΔΨm was assessed by staining the mitochondria with TMRM that accumulates only in mitochondria with intact membrane potentials. GFP-Nes2G MEFs were labeled with TMRM, treated with cisplatin plus QVD and the temporal relationship between complete GFP-Nes2G redistribution and reduction in TMRM signal was examined using time-lapse microscopy. At the beginning of the recording when GFP-Nes2G was confined to the NE, the TMRM signal was prominent with a typical mitochondrial distribution pattern (Figure 5a). Nes2G redistribution was associated with a concomitant reduction in the TMRM signal (Figure 5a upper panel), which was not observed in the cells that did not undergo Nes2G redistribution (Figure 5a lower panel). The reduction in TMRM signal was further assessed by quantifying its intensity in cells that underwent complete Nes2G redistribution and those that did not, in the same analyzed fields during the recording period. The TMRM intensity in non-redistributed cells remained nearly constant during the recording period although a small reduction in intensity occurred shortly after T0 (Figure 5b). This reduction probably represented a bleaching effect and was also observed in the cells that underwent complete redistribution with similar kinetics. However, at some point the TMRM signal in the redistributed cells steeply dropped and this decrease was associated with the beginning of Nes2G redistribution (Figure 5b).

Quantification of these results showed that the percentage of cells in which TMRM signal reduction occurred at about the same time (±30 sec) or after (>30 sec) the appearance of Nes2G redistribution (75.8%) was significantly higher than cells in which the TMRM signal reduction was observed before (<30 sec) Nes2G redistribution (Figure 5d). In most cells, the apoptotic morphology appeared after the reduction in TMRM signal and after Nes2G redistribution (Figure 5d). These results show that the loss of ΔΨm is associated with complete Nes2G redistribution and that both these events precede the appearance of apoptotic morphology. An association between Nes2G redistribution and loss of ΔΨm was also observed in cells exhibiting partial redistribution, where the TMRM signal was almost completely abolished (Figure 5e). The reduction in TMRM signal in cells that undergo GFP-Nes2G redistribution was not due to depletion of mitochondria, because despite the absence of TMRM signal, cells that exhibited redistributed GFP-Nes2G showed a typical mitochondrial pattern when stained with the mitochondrial dye MitoTracker Deep Red, which stains mitochondria independent of membrane electrochemical potential [56]. As expected, the MitoTracker Deep Red signal coincided (at least partially) with the redistributed GFP-Nes2G (Figure 5f). Hence, the translocation of Nes2G to the mitochondrial proximity is accompanied by loss of ΔΨm.

Nes2G redistribution is accompanied by MOMP

We examined the release of the apoptogenic protein SMAC from mitochondria to assess MOMP [7]. GFP-Nes2G MEFs were transfected with an expression vector encoding mCherry-SMAC, treated with cisplatin plus QVD and then the temporal relation between mCherry-SMAC release and GFP-Nes2G redistribution was assessed by time-lapse microscopy. In cells with complete GFP-Nes2G redistribution from the NE, mCherry-SMAC was released from the mitochondria as indicated by a reduction in the amount of punctate signal and appearance of a diffused signal (Figure 6a left panel). This change in mCherry-SMAC appearance was not observed in cells that did not undergo Nes2G redistribution (Figure 6a right panel). Quantification of the temporal relationship between visual appearance of Nes2G redistribution and SMAC release showed that the percentage of cells exhibiting mCherry-SMAC release at about the same time (±30 sec) or after (>30 sec) the appearance of Nes2G redistribution (70%) was significantly higher than cells where mCherry-SMAC release occurred before (<30 sec) Nes2G redistribution (Figure 6b). In most cells, the apoptotic morphology appeared after mCherry-SMAC release and after GFP-Nes2G redistribution (Figure 6b). These suggest that MOMP is associated with Nes2G redistribution in cells undergoing complete redistribution and that both events precede the appearance of apoptotic morphology.

We quantified the mCherry-SMAC release by assessing the normalized SD values of mCherry-SMAC signal in individual cells that exhibited complete Nes2G redistribution as well as in cells that did not. In this analysis, a lower SD value relative to non-redistributed cells represents a more uniform distribution pattern, which further indicates that SMAC was released from the mitochondria into the cytoplasm. In cells that exhibited Nes2G redistribution the SD values of the mCherry-SMAC signal at T1 and T2 were significantly lower than in T0 (Figure 6c) whereas in the non-redistributed cells, the mean SD value of the mCherry-SMAC signal did not significantly differ over the course of the recording. These results indicate that in non-redistributed cells mCherry-SMAC was not released, whereas it was released in cells that exhibited Nes2G redistribution. Moreover, the release was observed at the time when Nes2G redistribution was first evident (T1), confirming the temporal relationship between Nes2G redistribution and MOMP. An association between Nes2G redistribution and mCherry-SMAC release was also observed in cells exhibiting partial Nes2G redistribution (Figure 6d). This was observed in 73% of the cells (n = 22), as exemplified by the indicated representative cell.

To further assess the relationship between MOMP and Nes2G redistribution, we treated WT 3T9 MEFs without (control) or with cisplatin plus QVD and assessed the relationship between cytochrome c release (a diffused vs. a punctate cytochrome c distribution pattern) and the appearance of Nes2G redistribution in fixed individual cells (Figure 7a,b). In the vast majority (~93%) of the control cells, Nes2G was confined to the NE and cytochrome c was not released. Treatment with cisplatin plus QVD induced substantial Nes2G redistribution and cytochrome c release. Quantification of the results in the cells treated with cisplatin plus QVD showed that 60.3% of the cells exhibit both Nes2G redistribution and cytochrome c release in the same cell, whereas 23.3% of the cells show only Nes2G redistribution and no cytochrome c release and 4.3% cells show cytochrome c release but no Nes2G redistribution (Figure 7c). The results indicate a significant association between Nes2G redistribution and cytochrome c release (Fisher’s exact test). Assessing the cell population where Nes2G redistribution and cytochrome c release did not occur together in the same cell (Nes2G is redistributed but cytochrome c is not released, or cytochrome c is released but Nes2G is not redistributed) revealed that in most of these cells Nes2G is redistributed while cytochrome c is not released, indicating temporal dependency of cytochrome c release on Nes2G redistribution, but not of Nes2G redistribution on cytochrome c release (McNemar’s Test). Thus, in line with the results obtained for SMAC release assessed by time-lapse microscopy, cytochrome c release assessed in fixed cells was also associated with Nes2G redistribution. This supports the hypothesis that Nes2G redistribution is accompanied by MOMP. However, MOMP is not needed for Nes2G redistribution to occur, supporting the hypothesis that Nes2G redistribution is an early apoptotic event upstream of MOMP.

Discussion

We showed previously that Nes2G has a pro-apoptotic activity mediated via Bcl-2 family proteins [44]. During apoptosis it is displaced from NE [45]. Here, we further explored the mechanism by which Nes2G promotes its pro-apoptotic activity focusing on its subcellular redistribution. Our results suggest that the Nes2G pro-apoptotic effect is mediated by redistribution of Nes2G from the NE to near mitochondria where it may contribute to mitochondrial apoptosis, indicated by a reduction in ΔΨm and MOMP.

Mechanism of apoptosis induced displacement of Nes2G from the NE

How the apoptotic signal induces Nes2G redistribution

The mechanism by which the apoptotic signal converges at the NE and leads to Nes2G displacement is currently unknown. However, our results show that it does not require caspase activity, is independent of cytochrome c release and MOMP-associated mitochondrial alterations that do not proceed to caspase activation, such as reduction in ATP production. Our findings also show that the Bcl-2 family proteins Bax and Bcl-xL play a role in Nes2G redistribution from the nuclear envelope. These findings are in line with our previous results which showed that Nes2G redistribution did not occur in Bax/Bak double knockout MEFs and that re-expression of Bax in these cells restored cisplatin-induced Nes2G redistribution [45]. However, it should be noted that both proteins are not essential for the process and, in their absence, other proteins such as Bak may still promote the redistribution effect.

How Nes2G is dislodged from the NE

A possible mechanism for Nes2G redistribution could be via cleavage of Nes2G and the release of the cleavage product from the NE. However, we previously reported [45] that in presence of QVD, cisplatin treatment of MEFs does not significantly affect the amount of Nes2G detected by immunoblots, using the K2 anti-Nesprin-2 antibody whose epitope is located near the C-terminus of nesprin-2 [51]. These results suggest that either under these conditions Nes2G does not undergo cleavage, or that the cleavage occurs in the N-terminal region and the cleavage product is too big to be separated from the full-length Nes2G. Another potential mechanism could be via disruption of the interaction between nesprin-2 and SUN proteins. However, it should be noted that disrupting the interaction of nesprin-2 with SUN proteins using dominant negative GFP-KASH, which displaces endogenous nesprin-2 from the NE [41], neither increased nor decreased sensitivity to apoptosis of MEFs [44]. In line with these results, treatment of MEFs with cisplatin and QVD did not affect lamin A/C protein level [57], suggesting that the redistribution process does not involve lamin A/C degradation. Of note, the current results also suggest that lamin A/C was altered in cells prone to Nes2G redistributed. The effect of such alteration on Nes2G redistribution is currently unknown, but we cannot exclude the possibility that it could play a role in promoting Nes2G redistribution. Additional studies are needed to unravel the mechanism and signaling that leads to Nes2G redistribution.

Complete and partial redistribution Nes2G patterns

The results show two different displays of Nes2G redistribution: 1) complete, observed with cisplatin treatment both in the absence or presence of QVD, and 2) partial, observed only in the presence of QVD. We propose the following model to explain these patterns: There are two different mechanisms, both caspase-independent, whereby Nes2G redistribution occurs. One is very rapid and is the predominant mechanism. Once it occurs, the cells reach a no return point and die by exhibiting apoptotic morphology, even in the presence of QVD (it is known that morphological apoptosis can occur also in a caspase-independent manner [58]). The second is a slow process and is detected only in cells in which their death was attenuated by QVD. It occurs in only a small fraction of cells and is not sufficient to redistribute all Nes2G from the NE. The slowly released Nes2G accumulates over time and can be detected after 24 h (at the beginning of the recording). Hence, at the beginning of the recording the cells that are available for the time-lapse analysis are live cells that have not yet undergone the rapid Nes2G redistribution as well as live cells that have undergone the slow process and their death was attenuated by QVD. During the recording period the fast process occurs in a small, but detectable number of cells. The slow process may also occur during the recording period, but due to its relatively slowness, it cannot be captured during the short duration of the time-lapse recording we used.

There is an apparent difference in the distribution pattern of mitochondria in cells undergoing complete and partial Nes2G redistribution. Namely, mitochondria in cells exhibiting the partial pattern tend to localize in one side of the cell, whereas in cells exhibiting the complete pattern the mitochondria were scattered all over the cell. This is probably due to the tendency of mitochondria to exhibit perinuclear clustering in apoptotic cells [59]. Thus, in cells where QVD inhibits the progression of the cell death process (i.e., those exhibiting the partial pattern), but not of the mitochondrial accumulation, the appearance of this accumulation is more prominent relative to cells that exhibit the complete pattern that die before exhibiting an apparent mitochondrial accumulation.

The role of redistributed Nes2G in the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway

Translocation of Nes2G to the proximity of mitochondria

Our results show that redistributed Nes2G translocates to the proximity of mitochondria. A low level of mitochondrial-associated nesprin-2 was previously detected in normal C2C12 myoblasts [34]. This finding supports the notion that nesprin-2 can localize near mitochondria. The LINC complex-mediated connection between the nucleus and the cytoskeleton is mediated by, among other things, binding of Nes2G to actin directly via its calponin homology domains, and indirectly by binding FHOD1, fascin and Mena [40,50,60]. Actin filaments have also been shown to interact with mitochondria and to regulate mitochondrial motility and fission (for review see [61]). Whether the redistributed Nes2G still binds actin is not known. Nonetheless, it is tempting to speculate that the translocation of redistributed Nes2G to the mitochondrial region is associated with its interaction with actin and of actin with the mitochondria. Alternatively, translocation of redistributed Nes2G to the mitochondria may be due to its displacement from the NE and from actin and is mediated by an interaction with an unknown mitochondrial receptor. Further studies are needed to unravel the mechanism which leads to mitochondrial translocation of redistributed Nes2G.

The mechanism by which redistributed Nes2G may promote mitochondrial apoptosis

Redistribution of Nes2G to the mitochondrial region is associated with initiation of mitochondrial apoptosis (reduction in ΔΨm and MOMP). This precedes the appearance of apoptotic morphology, implying that Nes2G redistribution is an early apoptotic event that contributes to the subsequent mitochondrial alterations. This assumption is supported by our previous studies showing that the pro-apoptotic activity of nesprin-2 is mediated via Bcl-2 family proteins [44], which are known to regulate MOMP [2]. These results imply that the translocation of Nes2G to the mitochondrial region leads to regional co-localization of redistributed nesprin-2 with Bcl-2 family proteins and thus facilitates the interaction between them; as we previously showed for Bax [45]. This in turn regulates the activation of the executors and/or inhibits the activity of the guardian members of the Bcl-2 family [44].

Translocation of Bcl-2 family proteins such as Bax and Bcl-xL to mitochondria plays a key role in the activation of the mitochondrial gateway to apoptosis [2,6]. We showed previously that nesprin-2 regulates the presence of Bax and Bcl-xL in mitochondria [44]. Although the mechanism of this regulation is unknown, one can speculate that the presence of redistributed Nes2G near the mitochondria contributes to this process.

Although our results show a significant association between complete Nes2G redistribution and loss of TMRM and to SMAC release, in some cells they were observed before Nes2G redistribution. This is likely because Nes2G is not the only factor that regulates the activity of Bcl-2 family proteins and the subsequent reduction in ΔΨm and MOMP. Indeed, we showed previously that downregulation of nesprin-2 partially inhibits cisplatin-induced cytochrome c release [44]. Nonetheless, in most cells Nes2G redistribution was associated with mitochondrial dysfunction suggesting that it is a factor that regulates mitochondrial dysfunction.

The results of our previous and current studies suggest that the pro-apoptotic effect of Nes2G [44] is mediated by its redistribution near the mitochondria, where it participates in the activation of mitochondrial apoptosis by regulating the activity of Bcl-2 family proteins. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that other nesprin-2 isoforms that are not tagged with GFP in our experiments also participate in the pro-apoptotic effect of nesprin-2 as we previously suggested [44]. The findings of the current study support the notion that the NE is part of the apoptotic machinery that acts by releasing apoptogenic factors that in turn regulate mitochondrial apoptosis [16]. Our results are line with other studies showing apoptosis-induced translocation of nuclear proteins to the cytosol and/or mitochondria [16,17,62–64]. However, Nes2G is a unique NE protein that translocates to the mitochondria and facilitates apoptosis, further supporting our hypothesis that the NE can also serve as a mediator of apoptosis.
