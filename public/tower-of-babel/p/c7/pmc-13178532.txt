Machine‐learning classification of motor unit types in the adult mouse
Authors: María de Lourdes Martínez‐Silva, Reuben M Ahorklo, Emily J Reedich, Rebecca D Imhoff‐Manuel, Natallia Katenka, Marin Manuel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractMotor unit diversity arises from differences in the contractile properties of muscle fibres and the intrinsic electrical properties of their motoneurons. In mice, however, this relationship has not been quantitatively defined, and conventional classification often relies on subjective thresholds. Here, we combine in vivo intracellular recordings with supervised and unsupervised machine‐learning methods to test whether motoneuron electrophysiology can predict the physiological identity of mouse motor units. Unbiased clustering identified four groups corresponding to slow (S), fast fatigue‐resistant (FR), intermediate (FI) and fast fatigable (FF) types. A multinomial logistic regression model performed well, with most errors occurring between FI and FF types, which showed substantial overlap. Reducing the task to three classes improved accuracy. Feature selection revealed that four electrophysiological properties (input conductance, rheobase, afterhyperpolarization duration and maximal frequency) were sufficient for high predictive performance. Overall, this study provides a quantitative description of mouse motor unit properties and a framework for incorporating motor unit diversity into future investigations of neuromuscular physiology and disease. Key points Motor units are traditionally classified as slow (S), fast fatigue‐resistant (FR), fast intermediate (FI) or fast fatigable (FF) based on a handful of contractile properties, but in mice this classification has relied largely on subjective thresholds.We used unsupervised clustering of 40 contractile variables recorded in vivo to define motor unit types objectively in the adult mouse triceps surae.Motoneuron electrophysiological properties, including input conductance, rheobase, afterhyperpolarization duration and firing frequency, systematically varied across identified motor unit types.A multinomial logistic regression model predicted motor unit type from motoneuron electrical properties with good accuracy, particularly for slow and fast fatigue‐resistant units.These results establish quantitative criteria linking motoneuron excitability to muscle contractile phenotype in mice, providing a framework for studying motor unit diversity in health and neuromuscular disease. Keywords: classifier, in vivo electrophysiology, motoneuron, multinomial logistic regression, principal component analysis (PCA), spinal cord

Introduction

Motor units (the motoneuron together with all the muscle fibres it innervates) form the fundamental building blocks of motor control (Enoka, 2006). The functional diversity of motor units underlies the ability of muscles to perform both fine, slow, deliberate tasks and powerful, rapid movements (Burke et al., 1971). In vertebrates, motor units were classically grouped into at least three types, slow (S), fast fatigue‐resistant (FR) and fast fatigable (FF) types, which differ fundamentally in contractile speed, force generation and fatigue behaviour (Burke, 1981). Seminal work by Burke and colleagues laid the foundation for this classification by correlating mechanical responses, such as twitch contraction time, force and fatigue indices, with distinct histochemical properties (Burke, 1981; Burke et al., 1971). Slow units are characterized by prolonged contraction, high oxidative capacity and strong resistance to fatigue, crucial for posture and endurance. FF units provide high force production and rapid contraction but are notably susceptible to fatigue due to their reliance on glycolytic metabolism (Enoka, 2006). In addition to the three classical types (S, FR, FF), several studies have described a fourth ‘fast‐intermediate’ (FI) class, characterized by fast contraction with an intermediate level of fatigue resistance that did not match either of the FR or FF ranges (Burke, 1999; de Zepetnek et al., 1992; Dum and Kennedy, 1980; Larsson et al., 1991). The FI classification acknowledges the gradation of fatigue resistance that could not be adequately described by the simple FR/FF dichotomy (Burke et al., 1973; Dum and Kennedy, 1980; Goslow et al., 1977; Jami et al., 1982; Stephens et al., 1973).

Concurrently, on the neural side, motoneurons exhibit electrical properties that correlate systematically with the contractile characteristics of their motor units (Burke, 1981). Thanks to pioneering work in feline triceps surae motoneurons and subsequent studies in rats, it is well established that motoneuron input resistance, afterhyperpolarization (AHP) duration, axonal conduction velocity and recruitment thresholds differ systematically across motor unit types (S, FR and FF) and correlate with twitch contraction time, force and fatigue profile (Bączyk et al., 2022; Manuel et al., 2019; Zengel et al., 1985). Generally, S motoneurons are more excitable, having high input resistance and lower rheobase, while fast units (FF and FR) have lower input resistance and higher rheobase (see Bączyk et al. (2022) for a recent review). This systematic relationship underpins fundamental concepts such as Henneman's size principle governing orderly recruitment (Enoka, 2006).

More recently, we were able to perform recordings in adult mice in vivo and study the contractile properties of TS motor units together with measurements of motoneuron electrical properties in two different mouse models of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. In this work, we classified the recorded motoneurons in the three classical types (S, FR and FF) using hand‐picked thresholds in twitch contraction time and twitch amplitude (Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018). This work was the first of its kind performed in mice and opens the door to studying the properties of mouse motor units in healthy mice, but also in mouse models of various human diseases.

Although the mouse has become a crucial model for the study of human disorders, its motor units display faster contraction kinetics and higher maximal firing frequencies compared to rats and cats, illustrating that it is critical to fully characterize the properties of motor units and their motoneurons in this species (Kanning et al., 2010; Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018). Traditional classification schemes in mice often rely on hand‐picked thresholds, despite the fact that motor‐unit properties form a continuum and show substantial overlap (Burke, 1999; de Zepetnek et al., 1992). Modern computational approaches provide an opportunity to integrate high‐dimensional contractile and electrophysiological datasets in an objective and reproducible manner, but such methods have not yet been systematically applied to mouse motor units.

In this study, we measured a large panel of contractile and electrophysiological properties of individual motor units in the mouse TS, classifying them into physiological types based on their contractile properties using unbiased, unsupervised clustering algorithms. Then, we trained a statistical model allowing the prediction of a motor unit type based on the electrical signature of its motoneuron. This approach deepens our understanding of neuromuscular organization and enables faster, less invasive classification in mice and mouse models of neuromuscular disorders.

Methods

Ethical approval

All experiments done in Paris were performed in accordance with European directives (86/609/CEE and 2010‐63‐UE) and French legislation. They were approved by Paris Descartes University ethics committee (authorization CEEA34.MM.064.12). Experiments done at the University of Rhode Island were performed according to guidelines of the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978) and have been authorized by the University of Rhode Island's Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol AN2021‐018). All animal protocols adhered to the ethical principles of The Journal of Physiology (Grundy, 2015; O'Halloran, 2024a, 2024b).

Animals

Animals were provided with food and water ad libitum in a temperature and light‐controlled facility. Twenty‐six B6SJL mice (age 60 ± 5 days [48–71 days]; weight 25 ± 4 g [17–32 g] n = 26; 16 males and 10 females), bred in house from breeders purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME), were used for this study. At the end of the experiment, animals were euthanized with an overdose of pentobarbital (1000 mg/kg, Euthasol, Covetrus, Portland, ME).

Surgical procedure

The surgical procedure has been described previously (Manuel and Heckman, 2012; Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018). Briefly, atropine (0.25 mg/kg; Covetrus) and methylprednisolone (2 mg/kg; Covetrus) were given subcutaneously to prevent salivation and oedema, respectively. Anaesthesia was induced with an i.p. injection of fentanyl (0.025 mg/kg; Covetrus), midazolam (7.5 mg/kg; Covetrus) and medetomidine (0.5 mg/kg; Covetrus). The heart rate was monitored with an ECG, and the central temperature was maintained at all times between 36.5°C and 37.5°C using a manually controlled infrared heating lamp and an electric blanket. Then, the mouse was artificially ventilated with pure oxygen (SAR‐1000 ventilator; CWE, Ardmore, PA) through a cannula inserted in the trachea. The ventilator settings were adjusted to maintain the end‐tidal PCO2 at ∼4% (Micro‐Capstar; CWE). One catheter was introduced in each of the left and right external jugular veins. The first one was used to deliver supplemental doses of anaesthetic whenever necessary (usually every 20–30 min) by i.v. injection (10% of the dose used for anaesthesia induction). The adequacy of anaesthesia was assessed by lack of noxious reflexes and stability of the heart rate (usually 400–500 bpm) and end‐tidal PCO2. The other catheter was used for a continuous perfusion of saline (100 µL/h, Covetrus). The vertebral columns were immobilized with two pairs of horizontal bars (Cunningham Spinal Adaptor; Stoelting, Dublin, Ireland) applied on the T12 and L2 vertebral bodies, and the L2–L4 spinal segments were exposed by a laminectomy at the T13–L1 level. A custom‐made chamber was fitted around the exposed spinal segments, and silicon sealant (Body Double Fast Set, Smooth‐On Inc., Macungie, PA) was applied to create a recording chamber. This chamber was filled with mineral oil to prevent desiccation of the spinal cord. All branches of the sciatic nerve were cut except for the nerve innervating the triceps suraTS, which was left intact and mounted on a mono‐polar stimulation electrode. The achililes tendon TSwas dissected free and securely attached to an isometric force transducer (Aurora 404C, Aurora Scientific, Aurora, ON, Canada, max force 100 mN, bandwidth 0–4 kHz, sampling rate 10 kHz). The skin of the leg was pulled taught and used to form a pool filled with mineral oil around the muscle. The temperature of the muscle was maintained around 37°C (37 ± 1°C [35–39°C], n = 124) using a recirculating flow of mineral oil heated with an inline heater (SF‐28, Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA) and a temperature probe inserted underneath the muscle.

Intracellular recordings were performed using glass microelectrodes (tip diameter 1.0–1.5 µm) filled with KCl 3 M (resistance 8–15 MΩ). Intracellular recordings were obtained with an Axoclamp 2B amplifier (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) and Spike2 software (CED, Cambridge, UK). All recordings were obtained using discontinuous current clamp (7–9 kHz; Manuel, 2021) and sampled at 30 kHz. At the beginning of the experiment, the initial length of the muscle was set to the length at which the ankle was flexed at 90°. The length was then adjusted in order to record the maximal muscle twitch amplitude. Electromyographic activity (i.e., single motor unit compound action potential, MUAP) was recorded at the same time using fine stainless‐steel wires inserted underneath the muscle fascia (signal amplified 100–1000×, and band‐pass filtered at 10 Hz to 10 kHz; A‐M Systems, Sequim, WA, USA, Model 1700).

Electrophysiological recordings

Upon impalement of a motoneuron, we identified the motoneuron as innervating TS muscle fibres based on the presence of an antidromic spike in response to the stimulation of the nerve and its ability to induce muscle twitches upon injection of current through the microelectrode. A series of tests was conducted to characterize the contractile properties of the motor unit and the electrophysiological properties of the motoneuron. First, a series of ten 1‐ms‐long pulses of current (repeated at 1 Hz) were used to elicit single action potentials and produce individual twitches of the motor unit. These twitches were used to measure twitch amplitude (from baseline to peak force), twitch contraction time (time between twitch onset and twitch peak), half relaxation time (time between twitch peak and point where the force went back to 50% of the peak tension), etc. (Fig. 1A 1 ) (Manuel et al., 2009; Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018). Then, 500‐ms‐long trains of 1‐ms‐long current pulses at 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 and 150 Hz (separated by 30 s pauses) were used to study the unfused and fused tetanus of the motor unit (Fig. 1B ). The intensity of the current pulses was adjusted by hand in order to reliably elicit one action potential on each pulse. Trains where some action potentials failed to be triggered, or where the MUAPs were not constant were excluded from analysis. These tetani were used to measure peak force at each frequency, and sag profiles at 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 Hz. For each of these frequencies, we quantified the sag amplitude (ratio of the maximum force over the last twitch in the train) and the tetanus index (ratio of the last twitch over the first twitch of the train). Finally, a new series of 1 ms pulses at 1 Hz were used to elicit single twitches and quantify the amount of post‐tetanic potentiation (Fig. 1A 2 ). A full description of contractile parameters is provided in Martínez‐Silva et al. (2018). The input resistance/conductance of the motoneuron was measured from the peak and plateau responses generated by small‐amplitude 500 ms square current pulses (Manuel et al., 2009). The membrane time constant was measured on the relaxation of the membrane potential following 1 ms hyperpolarizing current pulses using the ‘peeling’ method (Engelhardt et al., 1995). The motoneurons’ firing characteristics were examined using triangular current ramps applied at a rate of 0.1–2 nA/s. The total ramp duration depended on the ramp speed and the current intensity reached at the top of the ramp, which was adjusted by hand based on the rheobase of the motoneuron. The instantaneous firing frequency was plotted against the injected current intensity at the moment of the spike. The voltage threshold for firing was measured on the first spike of the ramp, and was defined as the point where the first derivative of the voltage reached 10 mV/ms (Sekerli et al., 2004). We measured the recruitment current, corresponding to the intensity of current at the voltage threshold; the derecruitment current, as the current intensity at the time of the last spike; the ΔI values, corresponding to the difference between derecruitment and recruitment currents; the onset firing frequency (frequency of the first inter‐spike interval); the maximal frequency reached during the ramp; the offset firing frequency (firing frequency of the last inter‐spike interval); the ΔF value, corresponding to the difference between the offset and onset frequencies; and the gain, calculated as the slope of a linear regression applied over the ‘primary range’ of the f–I curve (Manuel et al., 2009). We measured the spike amplitude, and width, the fast AHP (fAHP) amplitude (measured from the resting membrane potential) and the post‐depolarization potential (measured from the resting membrane potential) on an average of 5–10 action potentials elicited by short 1‐ms‐long current pulses. The amplitude of the slower ‘medium’ AHP was measured as the difference between the trough of the AHP and the resting membrane potential, and the half‐relaxation time was defined as the time it takes for the voltage to go from the trough of the AHP to half its amplitude (Manuel et al., 2009). The rheobase was measured in response to 20‐ms‐long pulses of current and was defined as the intensity of current necessary to elicit an action potential 50% of the time. Finally, trains of eight pulses of current at 40 Hz, repeated every second, were delivered for 3 min (180 trains in total) in order to test the fatigability of the motor unit (Fig. 1C ). As shown previously (Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018), in some motor units the MUAPs had a tendency to decrease over the course of a single train. Therefore, we measured the amplitude of the first twitch of each train, and the fatigue index was calculated as the ratio of the amplitudes of the first twitch of the first train to the last train (Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018).

Data selection

Only motoneurons with a membrane potential more hyperpolarized than −50 mV, an overshooting action potential, a membrane time constant >1.5 ms, and a muscle temperature in the range 35–39°C (37 ± 1°C, n = 124) were kept for analysis. Overall, our final population consisted of 124 motor units. To preserve the full range of biological variability, we did not perform tests to detect and exclude outliers in our dataset. Nevertheless, the number of tests that could be completed in each cell was variable and unpredictable. For the clustering analysis we first used a sub‐sample of 78 motor units in which the full range of contractile properties were recorded, before extending the classification to the remainder of the units. For building the statistical classification model, we focused on another subsample of 98 motoneurons (out of 124), in which the most important electrical properties were fully characterized.

Motor unit clustering

To group the motor units in one of the four types (S, FR, FI and FF), we used principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset (78 observations with 40 features). First the features were standardized (StandardScaler) and then the first five principal components were computed (PCA, n = 5). We then used K‐means clustering to group the recordings in one of four clusters (KMeans, n = 4). To classify the remainder of the motor units (53 motor units in which some of the features were missing), we used the features available for each of the remaining units to train a k‐nearest‐neighbour classifier in order to find the cluster to which each remaining unit was the closest. We used a value of k = 3 with a Cartesian distance weighting to take into account more variability than simply the nearest observation.

Statistical analysis

Based on the procedure above, each unit could be classified into one of four classes, and we then compared the average properties of the units in each cluster versus the other clusters. Comparisons between the four clusters were performed on the basis of the effect size of the difference of mean and its 95% confidence interval. Since some of the features did not follow a normal distribution, we performed the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis H‐test for independent samples. If this test revealed that the four groups were different, then a pairwise post hoc Mann–Whitney U‐test with Holme's adjustment for family‐wise error rate was performed. Linear relationships were quantified using a linear least‐squares regression, and we reported the value of the Pearson correlation coefficient r and the P‐value for a hypothesis test whose null hypothesis was that the slope was zero, using Wald's test. All values are reported as means ± standard deviation. A summary of each property is provided in Supporting information, Tables S1 and S2. For each cluster, we provided the mean ± standard deviation, the range of the values and the number of observations. We also provided the H‐value and P‐value of a Kruskal–Wallis test of the equivalence of the means. We then performed pairwise comparisons between each cluster. For each pair, we showed the effect size (Hedges’ g, or the mean difference if Hedges’ g could not be calculated) and the 95% confidence interval around the effect size. If the Kruskal–Wallis test showed a difference between groups, we performed a Mann–Whitney test and provided the U‐value and P‐value (adjusted using Holmes's method to control the family‐wise error rate).

To assess whether sex influenced the motor unit properties across the different types, we performed two‐way analyses of variance (ANOVA) with the main effects of sex and physiological type and their interaction. Pairwise group differences were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. Because multiple hypotheses were tested, P‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure, controlling the expected proportion of false discoveries at 5%. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and adjusted P‐values (q‐values) are reported where applicable.

Statistical analysis was performed in Python (v3.12), NumPy (v2.2; Harris et al., 2020), Pandas (v2.3; McKinney, 2011), Seaborn (v0.13; Waskom, 2021), matplotlib (v3.10; Hunter, 2007), Pingouin (v0.5; Vallat, 2018), dabest (v2025.03.27; Ho et al., 2019), and scipy (v1.16; Virtanen et al., 2020).

Motor unit type prediction

Feature selection and preprocessing

To predict motor unit type (S, FR, FI and FF) from intrinsic motoneuron properties, we selected a set of 25 electrophysiological variables that are commonly recorded during intracellular recordings and were consistently available across the dataset. These features included resting membrane potential, input resistance (measured at the peak and plateau; Manuel et al., 2009, 2007), sag ratio, input conductance (calculated as the inverse of the peak input resistance), membrane time constant, current at firing onset, current at firing offset, ∆I, onset firing frequency, ramp maximum frequency, offset firing frequency, ∆F, f–I curve gain (in the primary range), voltage threshold (absolute value), voltage threshold (relative to resting membrane potential), f–I curve profile (clockwise, anticlockwise or symmetrical), axonal conduction velocity, rheobase, spike height, spike width, AHP amplitude and AHP half relaxation time. We were able to measure these 23 properties in 103 motoneurons out of the total population of 131 units, and we used that subsample to train our predictive algorithms.

Model training and hyperparameter optimization

To test whether electrophysiological properties could predict motor unit type, multiple supervised learning classification algorithms were trained on the 103 observations, each with 23 features. The models we evaluated were multinomial logistic regression (LogisticRegression in scikit‐learn), random forest (RandomForestClassifier), gradient boosting (GradientBoostingClassifier), XGBoost (XGBClassifier), support vector machine (SVC) and k‐nearest neighbours (KNeighborsClassifier). Each algorithm was implemented within a scikit‐learn pipeline that involved standardization of the numerical features and one‐hot encoding of the categorical features. Model performance was evaluated using five‐fold stratified cross‐validation, maintaining the relative distribution of motor unit types across folds.

For each model, we performed a systematic grid search to optimize its hyperparameters using the GridSearchCV function from scikit‐learn. Each grid search used balanced accuracy as the primary scoring metric, as it compensates for unequal class frequencies among the four motor unit types. The macro F1‐score was also computed to assess the model's performance consistency across classes. To address class imbalance, all models that supported it (multinomial logistic regression, SVM, random forest) used class‐weight balancing. The final model was refitted using the best hyperparameters determined by cross‐validation.

The final model that was retained for further analyses was a multinomial logistic regression model because of its performance and its interpretability. Odds ratios were calculated as the exponent of the coefficients obtained from the fitted model. To estimate the uncertainty of the coefficients, we performed bootstrapping by resampling the dataset with replacement (1000 iterations), refitting the complete model for each bootstrap sample, and computing the empirical 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the coefficient distributions as 95% confidence intervals. Confidence intervals were then exponentiated to obtain the corresponding intervals for the odds ratios.

Confusion matrices were generated to visualize class‐level performance, particularly for distinguishing between the four motor unit types (FF, FI, FR and S). An additional three‐class variant of the problem – grouping FF and FI together (fast), and treating FR and S as distinct classes – was evaluated to examine whether coarser classification improved separability.

All analyses were conducted in Python (v3.12) using scikit‐learn (v1.7; Pedregosa et al., 2011), NumPy (v2.2; Harris et al., 2020), Pandas (v2.3; McKinney, 2011), seaborn (v0.13; Waskom, 2021), and matplotlib (v3.10; Hunter, 2007).

Results

Motor unit classification

We recorded a total of 124 motor units in vivo from the TS muscle of B6SJL mice and characterized both their contractile and their electrophysiological properties using an anaesthetized but non‐paralysed adult mouse preparation (Manuel and Heckman, 2011, 2012). All electrophysiological recordings analysed in this paper were collected de novo for the present study. No data from our previous publications were reused or reanalysed here. Instead of hand‐picked thresholds as we did previously (Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018), we decided to apply unsupervised clustering algorithms to group motor units that behaved similarly. We used 40 contractile variables, including twitch amplitude, contraction and half‐relaxation times, tetanic force, fatigue index, force–frequency curves, sag amplitudes and tetanus indexes (measured at 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 Hz to capture their known frequency dependence), and many others (see Supporting information, Table S1 for the complete list). Unfortunately, due to the inherent instability associated with performing these recordings in vivo, it was not always possible to record all 40 parameters in all recorded motor units. We therefore focused classification on a subset of 78 units in which these 40 parameters had been recorded.

We first performed PCA (Fig. 1D 1 ) on the standardized contractile variables. The first five principal components explained 75.4% of the total variance (PC1 = 46.4%, PC2 = 14.0%, PC3 = 6.7%, PC4 = 4.2%, PC5 = 4.1%), indicating that the dimensionality of the dataset could be effectively reduced to a few components (Fig. 1D 2 ). PC1 and PC2 primarily reflected differences in contraction speed and force production. PC1, which captured almost half of the variance, correlated positively with twitch and tetanic force, whereas PC2 captured differences in contraction and relaxation kinetics (Fig. 1D 3 ; see also Supporting information, Table S3 for a complete list). Based on the classical grouping of motor units in S, FR, FI and FF types (Burke, 1999), we used K‐means clustering (k = 4) to generate four distinct groups of motor units (Fig. 1D 1 ). The clusters were well separated in the PCA space. To assign the remaining partially characterized units (n = 46) to one of these four clusters, we trained a k‐nearest‐neighbour classifier (k = 3, distance‐weighted) on the complete subset and used it to predict the most likely class of each unclassified observation.

Contractile properties of the identified mouse motor unit types

Our strategy grouped motor units into clusters that matched the expected properties of the different types of motor units described in cats (Burke, 1981), rats (Bakels and Kernell, 1993; Kadhiresan et al., 1996; Turkin et al., 2010) and mice (Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018). One cluster had small twitch amplitudes, contracted slowly, and showed strong fatigue resistance, probably corresponding to type S motor units (Fig. 1A–C , n = 17 units, 14% of the population). On the other hand, another cluster contained motor units with the strongest twitches, a short contraction time and high fatigability, which corresponds to the properties of type FF motor units (Fig. 1A–C , n = 21 units, 17%). A third cluster had small to medium twitch amplitudes, contraction times that were faster than the S group, but more spread than the FF group, and were mostly resistant to fatigue, which fits well with the type FR motor units (Fig. 1A–C , n = 56 units, 45%). Finally, the fourth cluster had medium to high twitch amplitudes, fast contraction times, and intermediate fatigue resistance, which can be classified as an intermediate type FI, between FR and FF units (Fig. 1A–C , n = 30 units, 24%). Figure 1E 1 – 3 shows the distributions of the twitch amplitudes, twitch contraction times and fatigue index in the population. It is important to note that, although the labels S, FR, FI and FF are employed thereafter, their definition is not the same as in previous studies, where fatigue index was the sole factor differentiating between the fast units (Burke, 1981; Dum and Kennedy, 1980; Larsson et al., 1991). Our classification was based on the multivariate structure of 40 features, and units were classified based on their distance in this multidimensional space. Therefore, although some units may appear as an outlier relative to units of a similar type when projected along one particular dimension, they would nevertheless be close to similar units in a different set of coordinates.

Other properties also matched the expected distribution among the different types (see Fig. 2A–I for the most relevant; a full comparison of all the properties is available in Supporting information, Table S1). Maximal tetanic force (Fig. 2E ) was smallest in S, followed by FR, then FI and FF had the largest. Yet, the ratio between twitch amplitude and tetanic force (Fig. 2F ) was the largest in S and FR units, and smallest in FI and FF units, indicating that the summation of contractions during high‐frequency stimulation was more efficient in the slowest units (see also Fig. 3). MUAP amplitudes (Fig. 2G ) were also distributed in the expected order (S ≈ FR < FI < FF). Axonal conduction velocity (Fig. 2H ) was slowest in the S units, followed by FR and FI, but was not different between FI and FF. Post‐tetanic potentiation (Fig. 2I ) was assessed based on the twitch amplitude recorded after a series of unfused and fused tetanic contractions. FF and FI units were potentiated by an average of 126 ± 15% [84–163%] (n = 19) and 131 ± 14% [103–168%] (n = 30), respectively, while S and FR units did not potentiate on average (although there was a correlation between potentiation and twitch amplitude r = 0.43, P = 3.7 × 10−5 such that the largest FR units tended to potentiate more than the smallest).

We studied the stimulation frequency vs. output force relationship of our motor units over the range 20–150 Hz (Fig. 3A ). As expected based on their long contraction time and relaxation times (Fig. 2), the force of S units started to sum at lower frequency and reached peak force at lower frequency as well (Fig. 3B ). The force–frequency curve of the FR units started at the same force level, but started increasing at higher frequencies (Fig. 3C ) and reached a higher force output. Although, on average, the frequency at the curve mid‐point is not different between the FR, FI and FF units (Fig. 3C ), observation of the individual curves show that a number of FR units start increasing at lower frequencies than FI and FF units (compare Fig. 3 B 2 and B 3 – 4), as expected based on the larger range of contraction times and relaxation times in FR units (Fig. 2B and C ).

During unfused tetanic contractions, the force often exhibited a ‘sag’: tetanic tension increased rapidly at the onset of the train, followed by a decrease of tension to reach a lower steady state, particularly in F type motor units (Burke et al., 1973). We observed that the sag phenomenon, like in other species, was frequency dependent and occurred in three distinct forms (Fig. 4A ): either the force profile did not show any sag at all (Fig. 4A 1 ), or it sagged to a steady state level (‘simple’ spike, Fig. 4A 2 ), or, after an initial dip, the force started to increase again (‘complex’ sag, Fig. 4A 3 )(Carp et al., 1999; Kim and Heckman, 2023). As shown in Fig. 4B , sag was mainly observed in fast units, but not in all fast units. In response to a train of action potentials at 20 Hz, none of the S motor units exhibited sag, while sag was starting to be apparent in some FR units. Note that 20% is too low a frequency to allow fusion of twitches in the fastest units, and their sag profiles could therefore not be studied. As the train frequency increases, the proportion of units exhibiting sag increases, and increasingly more units exhibit complex sags, particularly in the FF and FI types (Fig. 4B and C ). In studies in rats, it was suggested that the ratio between the last and first twitch amplitude of the train (‘tetanus index’; Kryściak et al., 2019) could be used to differentiate S from F units. In the mouse, we also found that the tetanus index at 20 Hz and 30 Hz could be used to separate S from F units, although the separation was not very large between the fastest of the S units and the slowest of the FR units (Fig. 4D ).

We wanted to test whether animal sex and/or muscle size affects our classification. For the effect of sex, we separated our population based on sex, and tested whether motor unit forces were different between males and females (Fig. A1). The only difference we observed was that female FF units tended to exhibit smaller twitch amplitudes compared to that of males (Hedges’ g = −1.33 [95% CI −2.0 to −0.52], P = 0.005, Appendix Fig. A1A ), but there were no differences in twitch contraction time (Appendix Fig. A1B ), or tetanic force. A mixed‐effects model including sex, body weight and their interaction as fixed effects, and animal as a random intercept (Fig. A1C ), revealed no significant effects on twitch amplitude. Neither sex (β = 8.57 ± 13.73, P = 0.53) nor body weight (β = 0.04 ± 0.37, P = 0.91) significantly predicted twitch amplitude, and the interaction between sex and body weight was not significant (β = –0.25 ± 0.54, P = 0.65). This observation suggests that, although differences in total force output are to be expected between males and females (Celichowski and Drzymała‐Celichowska, 2007; Drzymala‐Celichowska et al., 2012; Mierzejewska‐Krzyżowska et al., 2019), these differences are minimal at the single motor unit level, and are only observable in the largest FF motor units.

Electrophysiological properties of type‐identified motoneurons

We next examined the electrophysiological properties of the motoneurons belonging to each of the four types of motor units identified above. As expected, there were strong correlations between the electrical properties of the motoneurons and their physiological types (Fig. 5). The passive properties of the motoneurons were different between the types of motor units (Bączyk et al., 2022). S‐type motoneurons had the largest input resistance, consistent with their smaller size (Burke et al., 1982), and a distinct absence of sag in their response to subthreshold current pulses (Gustafsson and Pinter, 1984; Zengel et al., 1985). On the opposite end of the spectrum, FF‐type motoneurons had the lowest input resistance, and their voltage responses displayed a pronounced sag (Fig. 5A and D–F ). The rheobase (the minimum amount of current necessary to elicit an action potential) was directly related to the input resistance (r = –0.62, P = 9.6 × 10−12): S motoneurons had the lowest rheobase, followed by FR, then FI and FF, which had similar values (Fig. 5H ). The AHP following each spike is also known to be different between types (Gustafsson and Pinter, 1984; Zengel et al., 1985). Most spikes were followed by a fAHP, and an after‐depolarization (ADP), before the slower medium AHP (mAHP) (Fig. 5B and C ) (Nascimento et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2024). However, the proportion of motoneurons that did display a fAHP/ADP was smaller in S‐type compared to fast‐contracting motor units (70%, n = 7/10 for S, 83% n = 43/52 for FR, 95% n = 20/21 for FI and 100% n = 16/16 for FF). Furthermore, fast‐contracting motor units tended to have larger (more negative) fAHP and ADP than S‐type motoneurons (S > FR ≈ FI ≈ FF; Fig. 5K ). Unexpectedly, we did not observe any differences in mAHP amplitude between motor unit types (Fig. 5I ). However, we did not control for resting membrane potential of the motoneurons and it is possible that variability in driving forces amongst motoneurons hid this effect. We observed that the time it takes for the AHP to repolarize to half its amplitude (AHP half‐relaxation time; Manuel et al., 2009), was systematically longer in the S‐type motoneurons, followed by FR, followed by FI and FF (Fig. 5B and J ). Interestingly, none of the electrophysiological properties shown in Fig. 5 differed between FI and FF motoneurons (a full comparison of all properties is available in Supporting information, Table S2). Furthermore, we explored whether the electrical properties of the motoneurons differed by sex among the different types of motor units, but we did not find any effect of sex on any of the motoneuron electrical properties, regardless of the motor unit type (all q > 0.05, Fig. A1D–E ).

We studied motoneuron firing behaviour by injecting triangular ramps of current through the microelectrode (f–I curves). As expected from the properties reported above and Henneman's size principle, S‐type motoneurons were recruited with a smaller current than that of FR, which, in turn, were recruited at lower current than FI and FF motoneurons (Fig. 6A–C ). Once recruited, mouse motoneurons generally exhibit a ‘sub‐primary range’ during which the firing frequency climbs steeply and with a high variability, followed by a more linear ‘primary range’ (Manuel et al., 2009). We have previously shown that this subprimary range exists in both S and F motoneurons (Manuel and Heckman, 2011). We did not detect a difference in the slope of the primary range (also called ‘gain’) between the different types of motoneurons. The force–frequency curves (Fig. 6A bottom row) show that the force increased rapidly as the firing frequency increased and tended to reach a plateau before the top of the ramp, where increasing firing frequency no longer translated into an increase of force. This is consistent with our previous observations, which showed that the majority of the force generated by a motor unit is recruited during the sub‐primary range (Manuel and Heckman, 2011), and is most likely linked to the fact that, in adult mice, the duration of the AHP is shorter than the duration of the muscle twitch (Fig. 6D ). Note that we did not attempt to push the motoneurons up to their absolute maximum firing frequency as this leads to spike inactivation that is often difficult to recover from. Therefore, the maximal frequencies reported here are underestimations of the motoneuron maximal firing frequency.

During the descending phase of the ramp, firing either stopped at a de‐recruitment current intensity higher than the recruitment current (clockwise hysteresis, ΔI > 0), stopped at roughly the same current intensity (symmetrical, ΔI ≈ 0), or continued firing until the derecruitment current was lower than the recruitment current (counter‐clockwise hysteresis, ΔI < 0). As shown in Fig. 6B, E and F , there was a systematic difference in firing profile between the different types. The vast majority (80%) of the S‐type motoneurons displayed a counter‐clockwise hysteresis and a negative ΔI. The remaining 20% of the f–I curves were symmetrical (ΔI ≈ 0). About half of the FR units displayed a counter‐clockwise hysteresis, and a small proportion (20%) of FR motoneurons displaying a clockwise hysteresis. However, this clockwise profile was not distributed uniformly among the FR motoneurons. There was a relationship between the amplitude of the twitch of a motor unit and the value of ΔI (r = 0.67, P = 6.95 × 10−14), indicating that the FR motoneurons displaying a clockwise hysteresis belonged to the largest motor units. Accordingly, the FI and FF units tended to display a majority of clockwise hystereses (Fig. 6E–F ).

Physiological type prediction

We next sought to generate a predictive statistical model capable of predicting motor unit type solely based on the electrophysiological properties of its motor unit. We chose to focus on a set of electrophysiological properties that (1) are highly relevant to motoneuron physiology, (2) are commonly recorded during our experiments as well as others', and (3) were measured in a significant proportion of our motoneurons. These features include passive properties (input resistance, time constant, etc.), spike properties (height, width, AHP), and firing properties measured on the response to a triangular ramp of current (see Supporting information, Table S2 for a complete list). We selected a subset of 98 motoneurons with no missing data in this set of 25 features from our original dataset of 124 motoneurons. These motoneurons had been assigned to one of the four motor unit types by our unsupervised clustering approach (see ‘Motor unit classification’). This yielded a class distribution of 9% S‐type motoneurons (n = 9), 54% FR motoneurons (n = 53), 21% FI (n = 21) and 16% FF (n = 16). This distribution was similar to the distribution observed in our full sample (χ2(3) = 1.85, P = 0.60).

We performed a systematic search for the model yielding the best predictive power of motor unit type from motoneuron electrical properties. A multinomial logistic regression classifier was chosen for subsequent analysis as it provided one of the best overall performances, and it is relatively easy to interpret. The model, which included standardized continuous features and one‐hot encoded categorical variables, achieved a mean balanced accuracy of approximately 0.73 across five‐fold cross‐validation, outperforming other tested algorithms such as random forest, gradient boosting, and k‐nearest‐neighbour.

Inspection of the confusion matrix (Fig. 7A ) revealed that the model perfectly classified the S motor units and was able to identify FR units fairly well (F1 score = 0.87), indicating that the features captured the distinctive electrophysiological profile of these more excitable cells (high input resistance, low rheobase, and long AHP). In contrast, the classifier struggled to distinguish between FI and FF units (F1 score = 0.50 and 0.53, respectively). As shown above, the electrical properties of these two types were largely overlapping and were not significantly different, which likely explains their higher rate of mutual misclassification.

Because the electrical properties of FI and FF motoneurons proved indistinguishable (Supporting information, Table S2), we decided to merge them together (FF+FI) while retaining FR and S as separate categories, thereby reducing the task to a three class problem. The model's performance improved substantially (Fig. 7B ). In this situation the classifier achieved a balanced accuracy close to 90%, with clear separation between low‐threshold (S vs. FR) and high‐threshold (FI+FF) motoneurons. This finding suggests that while the electrical properties alone are insufficient to finely discriminate among the two largest motor unit types (Fig. 5), they robustly encode the major functional gradient from slow, FR to fast, fatigable units.

We calculated the odds ratio associated with each feature in order to estimate their contribution to the separation of motoneuron clusters (Fig. 7C ). For the S cluster, the highest odds ratios were found for the AHP half‐relaxation time (OR = 1.28 [95% CI 1.16–1.39]), ADP amplitude (1.16 [95% CI 1.08–1.27]), and fAHP amplitude(OR = 1.16 [95% CI 1.09–1.24]), whereas features such as maximum frequency (0.74 [95% CI 0.69–0.79]) and rheobase (0.85 [95% CI 0.79–0.90]) showed negative associations. These findings indicate that the S cluster was characterized by broader spikes (although we did not detect a statistical difference at the population level; Supporting information, Table S2), less pronounced fAHP and ADP, higher input resistance and lower rheobase, whereas the cluster for the largest units was associated with shorter AHP and higher firing rates. In the FF+FI cluster, the strongest positive associations were observed for rheobase (1.25 [95% CI 1.19–1.30]), input conductance (1.24 [95% CI 1.18–1.30]), and the current at firing onset and offset (OR = 1.18 [95% CI 1.14–1.22] and OR = 1.19 [95% CI 1.14–1.24], respectively), indicating that neurons with larger conductances and higher current thresholds were more likely to belong to this group. A complete list of the odd ratios is provided in Supporting information, Table S4.

To improve the generalization power of the model, we sought to reduce the number of input features required while retaining predictive accuracy. Starting from our initial set of 25 electrophysiological parameters, we applied recursive feature elimination with cross‐validation (RFECV) to identify the minimal subset of features that preserved model performance. The results indicated that the model could be reduced to 13 features without loss of accuracy, and more notably, that almost as good performance could be achieved with just four features: input conductance, rheobase, AHP half‐relaxation time and maximal frequency. The model was retrained with these four predictors, achieving an overall accuracy of 87.8% (Fig. 8A ). The odds ratios showed that the S cluster identity is strongly dependent on the AHP half‐relaxation time (2.68 [95% CI 1.75–4.00]), while larger input conductances, rheobase or maximum frequency have a negative impact on this cluster (Fig. 8B 1 ). On the other hand, input conductance was the largest contributor (3.94 [95% CI 2.76–5.90]) to the large motor unit (FF+FI) group (Fig. 8B 3 ). The FR group was associated with high firing frequencies (3.09 [95% CI 2.17–4.78]) but negatively influenced by high input conductance (0.58 [95% CI 0.39–0.85]) (Fig. 8B 2 ). In their seminal work on cat medial gastrocnemius motoneurons, Zengel et al. (1985) used linear discriminant analysis using combinations of rheobase, input resistance, membrane time constant and AHP half‐decay time to predict motor unit type. Their approach achieved remarkably high accuracy (up to 97%) in distinguishing FF, FR and S units, particularly when using only rheobase and input resistance. We tested whether their approach could be used on mouse motoneurons. We used their predictors and trained three‐class decision‐tree models, and validated their performance using a five‐fold cross validation approach. We observed lower overall accuracies (∼57–81% across models, peaking at 81% using the ratio of the rheobase by input resistance) and less separability among classes. While rheobase and input resistance again provided the strongest predictors, no single or combined pair of features reached the discriminatory power of our complete model. The poorer performance of the simpler decision trees likely reflects a more compact and overlapping distribution of motoneuron properties in the mouse compared with the cat (Manuel et al., 2019).

Python code for reproducing the results presented here, as well as our trained model, are freely available. The model can be used with either the full set of 25 properties, or a reduced number of properties (13 or four). The model outputs the most likely class of a motoneuron given the properties provided.

Discussion

In this article, we provide, for the first time in adult mice, a complete examination of the relationship between the electrical properties of motoneurons and the contractile behaviour of their motor units in the mouse TS. Force recordings followed by unsupervised clustering identified four physiological groups consistent with the classic slow (S), fast FR, FI and FF types. Motoneurons belonging to these clusters differed in rheobase, input resistance, AHP duration and firing adaptation, demonstrating a tight coupling between electrical and mechanical properties. Machine learning models trained on motoneuron features predicted contractile identity with good accuracy for S and FR types and lower accuracy for FI and FF. These results demonstrate a graded but systematic relationship between motoneuron excitability and muscle contractile phenotype.

The electrophysiological properties of mouse motoneurons observed here are consistent with long‐standing expectations derived from Henneman's size principle (Enoka and Fuglevand, 2001). Motoneurons with higher input resistance and longer AHP exhibited lower rheobase and were associated with slower, more FR motor units, while those with lower resistance and shorter AHP were linked to faster, more fatigable units. This orderly relationship between excitability and motor unit contractile properties supports the notion of smooth recruitment, in which force is gradually modulated as progressively larger motoneurons are recruited. Our data show that, in mice, motor unit properties also coevolve in a continuous manner: as rheobase increased, twitch contraction time decreased and fatigue resistance declined. PCA placed the motor units along a slow‐to‐fast continuum rather than into separate clusters with clear boundaries. A continuous organization likely facilitates smooth gradation of force output.

Similarly, the overlap in recruitment currents between motoneuron subtypes also underscores that recruitment is probabilistic rather than strictly categorical. While consistent with size‐dependent recruitment principles, rheobase is not fixed and can be dynamically modulated. For example, selective neuromodulatory (Eleftheriadis et al., 2023; Hellström et al., 2003) or synaptic inputs (Basaldella et al., 2015; Westcott et al., 1995) can preferentially alter the excitability of specific motoneuron subtypes thereby shifting recruitment thresholds. In some conditions, recruitment order can even deviate from classical size‐based predictions. Thus, the subtype‐dependent differences observed here should be interpreted as baseline distributional tendencies rather than rigid recruitment hierarchies.

Comparison with previous studies

Compared to classic methods, we used a slightly different paradigm to measure fatigability in the mouse motor units characterized in this study. Our fatigue test protocol involved eight stimulations at 40 Hz (instead of the classical 13) and lasted only 3 min, because some mouse motor units exhibit decreasing MUAPs during high‐frequency stimulation trains (Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018). We measured fatigue index based on the amplitude of the first twitch in the train, instead of the peak force. For these reasons, the values of fatigue index that we measured in mouse motor units are not directly comparable to the ones obtained previously in cats and rats. Nevertheless, they revealed the existence of distinct populations of motor units that exhibit different fatigability profiles.

The classification of motor units into classical schemes developed in cats and rats typically relied on a small set of parameters – most notably twitch contraction time, twitch amplitude, and fatigue index – to distinguish S, FR, FI and FF types (Burke et al., 1973; Dum and Kennedy, 1980; Larsson et al., 1991). In particular, the FI category was historically defined primarily by an intermediate fatigue index (0.25–0.75 in Burke's original criteria). In contrast, the unsupervised clustering approach used in the present study incorporates 40 distinct contractile variables, including force–frequency behaviour, sag expression at multiple stimulation frequencies (20–60 Hz), tetanic fusion characteristics, and post‐tetanic potentiation. As a result, our clusters correspond to a broader and more physiologically integrated phenotype rather than a single fatigue‐based threshold. This approach captures the well‐documented continuous distribution of motor‐unit properties (Bakels and Kernell, 1994; Bigland‐Ritchie et al., 1998; Burke, 1999) and provides a more robust framework for classifying mouse motor units, whose contractile kinetics differ significantly from those described in larger species (Manuel et al., 2019).

Our dataset also refines conclusions about biological sources of variability, including sex. Although sex differences in whole‐muscle force and fibre‐type composition have been reported in rodents (Celichowski and Drzymała‐Celichowska, 2007; Drzymala‐Celichowska et al., 2012), and emerging human studies have shown sex‐specific variation in motor‐unit firing strategies and neuromodulatory control (Jenz et al., 2026; Piasecki et al., 2021), we observed only minimal sex‐dependent differences in single motor unit contractile properties in the mouse. The only detectable effect was a modest reduction in twitch amplitude in female FF units, with no systematic differences in contraction time, tetanic force or fatigue resistance, consistent with recent rodent‐specific observations that intrinsic motoneuron properties do not differ substantially between sexes (Drzymała‐Celichowska et al., 2022).

Discrete motor unit types versus a functional continuum

The physiological classification of motor units has traditionally relied on grouping them into discrete types. Early work in cats and rats established three or four categories of motor units based on twitch contraction time, maximal force and fatigue index (Burke, 1981; Zengel et al., 1985). This framework has proven useful for describing recruitment order and functional specialization. Yet, both historical and recent studies investigating individual motor unit contractile properties have repeatedly demonstrated that these properties are continuously distributed rather than discretely classified. For instance, Bigland‐Ritchie et al. (1998) reported that human thenar motor units display a continuous spectrum of force output and contraction speed, with no clear separation into distinct types. Similarly, studies involving intraneural motor axon stimulation of single motor units, such as those conducted on human toe extensors and in cat distal forelimbs, have established that the variability in contractile characteristics follows a continuous distribution (Bakels and Kernell, 1994; Bigland‐Ritchie et al., 1998; Burke, 1999; Chan et al., 2001; Kernell et al., 1999). Motoneuron electrical features such as input resistance, AHP duration and rheobase vary smoothly along the same axis, producing an overlapping gradient from the smallest, most excitable neurons to the largest, least excitable ones (Bączyk et al., 2022; Bakels and Kernell, 1993; Gustafsson and Pinter, 1984; Manuel et al., 2019; Zengel et al., 1985). Although motor units are thought to be constituted of muscle fibres with homogeneous properties, there is also considerable variability in the histochemical profiles of muscle fibres (Augusto et al., 2004; Bloemberg and Quadrilatero, 2012; Caiozzo et al., 2003; Pette and Staron, 2000; Sawano et al., 2016; Staron et al., 1999), which will similarly contribute to a continuous profile of force recruitment.

While a continuum may better reflect physiological reality, grouping motor units retains practical value. Discrete categories simplify comparison across muscles, species and experimental conditions. The physiological ranges of contraction time or rheobase differ between muscles such as the soleus and extensor digitorum longus, making direct comparison difficult without standard labels (Dum and Kennedy, 1980; Lewis et al., 1982). Classification into subgroups provides a shared vocabulary that allows consistent reporting of trends. It also supports statistical analyses when sample sizes are limited. Grouping can highlight shifts in population composition, such as the loss of FF units during disease progression (Hegedus et al., 2008; Pun et al., 2006) or training‐induced increases in FR units (Gehlert et al., 2012; Luginbuhl et al., 1984).

Relevance for studying motor units in diseases

Selective vulnerability is a defining feature of many neuromuscular disorders (Kanning et al., 2010). FF motoneurons degenerate earliest in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (de Carvalho et al., 2014; Hegedus et al., 2008; Pun et al., 2006), spinal muscular atrophy (Lee et al., 2025), Kennedy's disease (Molotsky et al., 2022) and normal ageing (Hirofuji et al., 2000; Kanda and Hashizume, 1989). Slow and FR units, by contrast, persist until late disease stages.

Traditional analyses often treat motoneurons as a homogeneous pool, averaging properties across all recorded cells. This approach obscures early, subtype‐specific changes. By defining electrophysiological boundaries that correspond to functionally distinct units, researchers can examine how disease processes shift each subgroup's properties. For example, a change in input conductance and resting membrane potential within the FF cluster might indicate selective stress on the largest motoneurons even before overt degeneration occurs (Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018). The ability to classify cells based on electrical features alone also makes it possible to identify vulnerable subtypes in preparations where contractile measurements are not feasible, such as ex vivo spinal cord preparations (Carp et al., 2008; Husch et al., 2011; Mitra and Brownstone, 2011) or when the use of curare is desirable to improve the stability of the recordings in vivo.

Limitations of the study

One limitation of the present study is that the number of recorded motor units was modest, and some clusters contained fewer observations. This limitation reflects experimental constraints and the natural distribution of motor unit types within the mouse triceps surae. Histological and electrophysiological studies indicate that the gastrocnemius muscles are dominated by fast units, while the soleus contains more slow and FR fibres (Bloemberg and Quadrilatero, 2012; Drzymala‐Celichowska et al., 2012). Although numbers were not available for the triceps surae as a wholeTS, we used counts of muscle fibres (Carroll et al., 2012; Olesen et al., 2021) and proportions of muscle fibre types (Agbulut et al., 2003; Augusto et al., 2004) in the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles to estimate that the distribution over the whole TSmuscle is 5% type I, 14% type IIa, 6% type IIx and 75% type IIb. The relative scarcity of slow units in our sample is therefore expected rather than a sampling artefact, and we actually recorded a larger proportion of S‐type motoneurons than expected from looking at the proportion of fibre types. However, this discrepancy can be explained by the fact that the innervation ratios (i.e. number of muscle fibres innervated by each motoneuron) is larger in FF and FR units than in S units (Chamberlain and Lewis, 1989; Rafuse et al., 1997; Totosy de Zepetnek et al., 1992), which means that the proportion of S‐type motoneuron is larger than the proportion of type I muscle fibres.

Another limitation is that the model was derived from only one muscle group (TS, which includes the two gastrocnemius muscles and the soleus muscle). The muscle is highly physiologically relevant for gait, balance, motor control and clinical research in animal models and humans, and has the advantage of containing all types of muscle fibres in substantial proportions, whereas other muscles may be devoid of one type or another (Martínez‐Silva et al., 2018). Yet, motor unit composition can differ substantially across muscles (Bigland‐Ritchie et al., 1998; Enoka, 2006). These anatomical and functional differences raise the possibility that the quantitative relationships between motoneuron electrophysiology and contractile identity observed here may not generalize identically to muscles with more extreme fibre‐type distributions, such as the predominantly fast tibialis anterior. Determining whether the same clusters emerge in such muscles, and whether electrical predictors retain similar discriminative power, will require future work combining in vivo motoneuron recordings with systematic sampling across multiple muscle groups. Such experiments will be essential for establishing whether the mapping between motoneuron intrinsic properties and muscle physiology is conserved across the motor system or exhibits muscle‐specific specializations.

Conclusion

Despite these limitations, the present study is the first to integrate contractile, electrophysiological and computational analyses of identified mouse motor units within the same preparation. These results have both physiological and translational relevance, establishing a foundation for future work on motor unit diversity, recruitment and degeneration. This framework opens the way for systematic studies of motor unit function in health and disease using unified, quantitative criteria.
