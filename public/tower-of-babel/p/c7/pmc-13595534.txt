Oncologic outcomes after poly-4-hydroxybutyrate scaffold in post-mastectomy breast reconstruction: a retrospective, multi-center cohort study
Authors: Haytham Gareer, Julia Tchou, Neil M Iyengar, Nicolás Tentoni, E Shelley Hwang, Kiya Movassaghi, Andrea McCracken, Kimberly Krugman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoly-4-hydroxybutyrate (P4HB) scaffolds are increasingly used in post-mastectomy implant-based breast reconstruction. Clinical adoption has outpaced evidence regarding oncologic outcomes and recurrence detection in reconstructed breasts.MethodsWe conducted a real-world cohort study via the Guardian Research Network database (2016–2023). Female patients with breast cancer undergoing mastectomy and reconstruction were identified; 269 P4HB cases were compared with 269 stratified, randomly sampled non-P4HB controls. Primary endpoints were LRR and LRRFS; secondary endpoints included disease-specific and overall survival. Cumulative incidence functions accounted for competing risk of non–breast cancer death. Administrative censoring at 3 years (as well as time aligned cohorts) was used to mitigate temporal imbalance.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 2.2 years in P4HB and 5.5 years in controls, LRR was uncommon (4/269 (1.5%) vs. 6/269 (2.2%)). The 3-year cumulative incidence of LRR was 1.6% in both groups. Disease-specific mortality was rare (0.7% vs. 2.6%). No early signal of differences in locoregional recurrence was observed between groups within the observed follow‑up period.ConclusionsIn this real-world, multi-center analysis, no detectable signal of increased locoregional recurrence was observed between cohorts. These findings do not establish evidence of oncologic equivalence, but rather an absence of a detectable early signal of increased risk within the observed follow-up. Larger, time-aligned datasets with plane-stratified surveillance and analyses are warranted to further evaluate oncologic outcomes given the comparator cohort included a greater proportion of earlier-era reconstructions than the P4HB cohort, reflecting more recent clinical adoption of P4HB.Keywords: Breast reconstruction, Locoregional recurrence, P4HB, Real-world evidence, Competing risks

Introduction

Implant-based reconstruction represents the majority of post-mastectomy reconstructions in the US, often with adjunctive matrices or scaffolds for soft-tissue support [1].‍ Poly-4-hydroxybutyrate (P4HB) is a fully resorbable biosynthetic scaffold with increasing clinical use, yet oncologic outcomes specific to this material in the reconstruction setting remain incompletely characterized [2–9].

Two clinical questions motivate this analysis. First, could exposure to a degradable scaffold alter locoregional tumor biology or host responses in a way that affects locoregional recurrence (LRR)? Second, might specific reconstructive planes (prepectoral vs. subpectoral) change the detection of recurrence on physical exam or imaging, thereby influencing time-to-diagnosis rather than true risk? These questions are relevant as P4HB adoption has been recent in prepectoral/direct-to-implant techniques [3, 6, 7, 9].

We hypothesized that P4HB use is not associated with a higher risk of LRR compared with non-P4HB reconstruction over early to intermediate follow-up, and that disease specific survival is not worse with P4HB use. With the relatively recent adoption of P4HB in breast reconstruction and absence of long-term prospective oncologic data, analysis of multi-center real-world evidence provides a pragmatic approach to evaluating early oncologic outcomes and recurrence detection in routine clinical practice. Given the observational design, expected low number of recurrence events, and likelihood of structural differences between cohorts, this study was intended to explore whether any signal of concern could be detected in oncologic outcomes.

Methods

Data source and study design

We conducted a retrospective comparative cohort study using the Guardian Research Network (GRN) database, a multi-institutional health data consortium spanning oncology practices in 32 states. GRN network partners include a variety of care settings spanning university outreach clinics in rural areas to multi-state hospital/clinic systems [10]. GRN’s aggregated and anonymized data includes structured EMR attributes and unstructured elements such as operative, pathology, imaging, or progress notes and impressions. Guardian maintains exclusive access to their aggregated and anonymized dataset; all patient identification, selection, and data curation was performed by their Real-World Evidence team based on inclusion and exclusion criteria defined in the study protocol. Female breast cancer patients undergoing mastectomy followed by reconstruction between January 2016 and December 2023 were eligible. P4HB cases had documented implantation of P4HB into their breast space; non-P4HB controls were selected via stratified random sampling by AJCC stage at initial diagnosis, receptor status, and age.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

P4HB cohort: genetically female; confirmed breast cancer; mastectomy; subsequent reconstruction; P4HB implantation after mastectomy. All women meeting these criteria were included. As breast reconstruction is often an iterative process, cases receiving P4HB during a revision reconstruction were also included; 85.1% received P4HB in their first reconstructive procedure. To focus on clinically relevant exposure to the P4HB material, we planned to exclude device removals prior to expected resorption (12–24 months) for reasons other than recurrence; no such removals occurred and all patients with P4HB-assisted breast reconstruction were retained.

Control cohort: genetically female; confirmed breast cancer; mastectomy; subsequent reconstruction; no P4HB exposure and, preferentially, no adjunctive matrix support. Random sampling of women with a history of breast cancer and reconstruction following mastectomy was used to create the control arm. While the preference for no adjunctive matrix support in breast reconstruction was ideal; to maintain balance in sparse strata for the study years, some controls had ADM exposure (29.4%), acknowledged as a source of heterogeneity. As a result, the comparator group should not be interpreted as a uniform ‘no scaffold’ reference. No adjustments were made to the Control cohort post-random sampling and patient retention provided Inclusion/Exclusion criteria were met.

Index date, outcomes, and follow-up

Cancer characteristics were captured and reported at the time of initial diagnosis for each patient. The index date (time zero) for time-to-event analyses was defined as the first reconstructive surgery following mastectomy to provide a common anchor for longitudinal follow-up across cohorts and to contextualize outcomes within the expected P4HB resorption window (up to 24 months). A minority of patients received P4HB during a revision procedure rather than at the initial reconstruction which could create exposure misclassification and immortal time bias. To address this, a sensitivity analysis was performed restricting the P4HB cohort to patients receiving P4HB at the initial reconstructive procedure. Patient data was reviewed longitudinally for each subject until either the last identified encounter for living subjects or the date of death for patients who passed away during the follow-up period. Locoregional recurrence (LRR) was defined as biopsy-confirmed recurrence in the ipsilateral chest wall/residual breast tissue or regional lymph nodes and was ascertained from structured data elements and curated documentation within GRN’s aggregated database. Patient data was reviewed from their study entry (as described above for eligibility) through follow-up current to December 2024 in both cohorts. LRRFS was time from index to LRR or death. Disease-specific mortality and overall survival were secondary endpoints. Last follow-up was the date of last clinical contact for living patients or date of death for deceased patients (current to December 2024) [10].

Recurrence ascertainment and surveillance modalities

Recurrences were identified from structured pathology/oncology data and curated chart abstraction. Documented detection modalities included clinical examination, ultrasound, MRI, and diagnostic mammography where applicable [11–13]. Because structured capture of modality was incomplete, we report qualitative modality descriptions when available and discuss potential detection bias by reconstructive plane. Locoregional recurrence was classified using a prespecified hierarchy: (1) histologic confirmation on biopsy/pathology; (2) clinician-documented recurrence in oncology or surgical progress notes accompanied by corroborating evidence (e.g., surveillance imaging, reoperation, or initiation of new cancer-directed therapy); or (3) initiation of new cancer-directed therapy consistent with recurrence in the absence of alternative explanatory documentation (excluding endocrine therapy changes attributable solely to menopausal status). Patients were classified as indeterminate for LRR when imaging reports described findings suspicious for recurrence without confirmatory pathology or concordant clinical documentation, when biopsy/pathology results were unknown or inconclusive, or when systemic therapy was discontinued early in a manner that precluded definitive recurrence adjudication.

Surgical planes and device configuration

Reconstructive plane (prepectoral vs. subpectoral) and, when available in the source documentation, P4HB construct configuration (e.g., complete wrap, sling, or anterior coverage) were abstracted. Prepectoral placement predominated among P4HB cases, consistent with contemporary practice patterns and the recent adoption of prepectoral/direct-to-implant techniques [14–16]. P4HB products identified within the dataset included Phasix, GalaFLEX, GalaFLEX 3D, GalaFLEX 3DR, and GalaFLEX LITE. Device-level attributes (e.g., size, thickness, or lot information) were not reliably retrievable from the de-identified data and therefore were not analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively with continuous variables presented as means, medians, standard deviations, and interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables are summarized as absolute frequencies and percentages. LRRFS was estimated with Kaplan–Meier and compared with log-rank tests (exploratory). For LRR, cumulative incidence functions (Aalen–Johansen) accounted for death as a competing risk. Administrative censoring at 3 years mitigated temporal imbalance arising from recent P4HB adoption. Incidence rates per person-time were calculated. Given low event counts, multivariable time-to-event modeling was not attempted. All outcomes are reported and analyzed per patient, not per breast.

Power considerations

The study was not powered to detect small differences in LRR or disease-specific mortality given low event rates (10 LRR events among 538 patients). We therefore interpret between-group differences descriptively and avoid claims of equivalence or non-inferiority.

Sensitivity analysis

Given heterogeneity inherent to real‑world data, we prespecified sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of LRR findings to key sources of potential bias and variability. Because P4HB use was concentrated in more recent years, we evaluated confounding by treatment era by applying administrative censoring at 3 years and by restricting analyses to overlapping surgical eras within eligibility constraints (e.g., reconstructions performed from 2019 onward). Calendar year was additionally examined descriptively to contextualize secular changes in oncologic and reconstructive practice. A conservative classification approach was also applied by treating patients meeting criteria for indeterminate LRR as recurrences to evaluate the upper bound of plausible LRR incidence.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted in which indeterminate LRRs were classified as recurrences to assess the robustness of the cumulative incidence estimates under a conservative outcome definition. Additional prespecified sources of between-group heterogeneity included reconstructive approach (e.g., DTI vs. two-stage; prepectoral vs. subpectoral), completeness of tumor biology variables, and use of other soft-tissue support (e.g., ADM); each was evaluated in separate sensitivity analyses as described below.

Specifically, receptor subtype data were incomplete in both cohorts, consistent with limitations of real-world data capture (e.g., fragmented care across institutions, geographic moves, and payer transitions). Missing values were not imputed because the mechanism of missingness could not be assumed to be at random and event counts were low. We therefore report receptor distributions based on available data and interpret subgroup comparisons cautiously. To evaluate whether differential missingness in HER2 status materially influenced conclusions, we performed a complete-case sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with documented HER2 status; results were directionally consistent with the primary analysis.

Results

Among 9,888 eligible patients identified during the study interval, 269 patients with documented P4HB use were included (all eligible P4HB cases in the dataset) and were compared with 269 non-P4HB controls selected via stratified random sampling. Baseline demographics and tumor characteristics were similar across cohorts overall (Table 1), with the majority of patients presenting with early-stage, hormone receptor–positive disease. Demographic distributions were comparable, with a median age of approximately 50–51 years; most patients were White, privately insured, and never-smokers. Given the observational design and the presence of residual differences in reconstructive approach and treatment era, these baseline summaries are descriptive and are not intended to imply full cohort exchangeability.

Baseline demographics and tumor characteristics

Compared with controls, patients in the P4HB cohort more frequently underwent nipple- or skin-sparing mastectomy and bilateral procedures and were more likely to receive direct-to-implant and prepectoral reconstructions (Table 2). Delayed reconstruction was observed in both cohorts; however, 10.4% of P4HB cases reflected first documented P4HB use during a revision reconstructive procedure. The prevalence of prior cancer treatment and use of neoadjuvant and/or adjuvant therapies was similar between cohorts (Table 3). P4HB adoption was concentrated in more recent calendar years: 77.7% of P4HB reconstructions occurred after 2020 versus 28.5% among controls, resulting in shorter observed follow-up for P4HB (median 2.2 years [IQR 1.3–3.1] vs. 5.5 years [IQR 2.7–8.6]). Given the expected resorption interval for P4HB (approximately 12–24 months), the median follow-up suggests that at least half of P4HB patients were observed through much of the anticipated material resorption period; longer follow-up is required to evaluate later outcomes.

Sensitivity analyses

Confidence intervals for LRR cumulative incidence and recurrence-free survival remained broad and overlapping across all group comparisons and sensitivity analyses. Neither administrative censoring at 3 years nor the use of time-aligned (calendar years 2019–2024 – eligibility through follow-up) cohorts altered the qualitative interpretation of LRR cumulative incidence (Table 8). Results also remained unchanged when classifying indeterminate LRRs as recurrences (P4HB rate of 0.02954 per 1000 person-days [0.01188–0.06087] versus Control rate of 0.01793 per 1000 person-days [0.00860–0.03297]). The indeterminate classification was less frequent than LRR with 3 patients in the P4HB cohort and 4 patients in the Control cohort who met criteria.

Discussion

In this multi-center real-world cohort, we did not identify a detectable signal of higher early-to-intermediate locoregional recurrence or disease-specific mortality associated with P4HB-assisted implant-based post-mastectomy reconstruction within the observed follow-up period. These findings do not establish oncologic safety but reflect the absence of a detectable early signal of increased risk within the available follow-up. The observed 3-year cumulative incidence of LRR was similar between cohorts (1.6%), and disease-specific mortality was rare.

Clinical and oncologic context

Contemporary reviews and cohort studies suggest immediate implant-based reconstruction does not compromise oncologic outcomes versus delayed reconstruction or mastectomy alone, and reported post-mastectomy LRR rates are typically low (single digits at ~ 5 years), driven primarily by tumor biology, stage, and adjuvant therapy rather than reconstructive technique [17–23]. In our cohort, absolute event rates were low in both groups, but limited follow-up and few events constrain inference.

This study was not designed to assess carcinogenicity or mechanism. Available preclinical/translational evidence and the absence of an epidemiologic signal to date do not suggest increased tumorigenic potential from biosynthetic/biologic scaffolds in breast reconstruction [24]. Accordingly, we address a pragmatic question: whether adding a fully resorbable scaffold in contemporary prepectoral/direct-to-implant reconstruction may influence recurrence detection and early oncologic outcomes in routine care; findings remain descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

Detection and surveillance considerations

Comparative studies generally report similar short-to-intermediate oncologic outcomes after prepectoral versus subpectoral implant-based reconstruction, although evidence is heterogeneous by cohort selection, follow-up, and surveillance intensity [25]. While implant position can affect postoperative imaging appearance, post-mastectomy surveillance is typically driven by clinical assessment with targeted imaging, and many local events arise in the skin/subcutaneous tissues rather than deep to the implant [23]. In this dataset, P4HB use was predominantly prepectoral, and time-aligned analyses reflected contemporaneous shifts in practice, with comparators increasingly including autologous or staged alloplastic reconstructions (including ADM-assisted prepectoral cases) [15, 16]. These secular trends mirror broader adoption of prepectoral techniques with adjunctive matrices to reduce animation deformity/pain and improve patient-reported outcomes. Subtle plane-specific differences in detection sensitivity cannot be excluded without standardized surveillance protocols.

In this study, documented LRRs were detected through standard clinical pathways involving physical examination and targeted imaging with histologic confirmation. We did not identify evidence suggesting impaired detection associated with P4HB use (governed by the limitations of RWD). Nevertheless, documentation of surveillance modality could be incomplete, and subtle plane-specific differences in detection sensitivity cannot be excluded. Prospective studies with standardized surveillance protocols and explicit reporting of detection modality will be important as prepectoral reconstruction continues to expand. While a consistent approach was used to determine LRR in both cohorts for this study, real-world data will exhibit missingness in some patient factors and HER2 status had a higher proportion of missingness for P4HB patients; influence of this missingness was explored by sensitivity analyses and the possible impact cannot be completely overcome.

Interpretation of negative findings

Negative oncologic studies warrant careful interpretation. With only ten LRR events among 538 patients, this analysis was unpowered to detect small between-group differences, and findings should not be interpreted as proof of equivalence or non-inferiority. Rather, our results indicate no early signal of oncologic harm associated with P4HB use within early-to-intermediate follow-up. To mitigate bias arising from the more recent adoption of P4HB, we applied administrative censoring at three years, though this approach cannot fully account for secular changes in systemic therapy, radiation techniques, or surveillance intensity over time.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include its multi-center scope, use of a curated real-world dataset with longitudinal follow-up, and analytic methods aligned with the endpoint of interest. In particular, competing-risk cumulative incidence methods were used for LRR to account for non–breast cancer death, and recurrence endpoints prioritized biopsy confirmation and disease-specific mortality to enhance clinical specificity. These design choices support the descriptive objective of the analysis and its relevance to reconstructive surgeons and oncologists.

The study has several important limitations inherent to its retrospective, observational design; causal inference is not possible and residual confounding (e.g., tumor biology and adjuvant therapies) may persist despite stratified sampling. Notably, P4HB-assisted reconstructions were concentrated in more recent calendar years, introducing potential treatment-era and follow-up differences; however, administrative censoring, time-aligned analyses, and prespecified sensitivity analyses were applied to mitigate these secular effects and yielded directionally consistent results. Exposure timing was delayed for a minority of P4HB patients because initial documented implantation occurred after the index reconstruction, which could introduce exposure misclassification and immortal time bias; if present, such bias would be expected to dilute differences and therefore reinforces the need for cautious interpretation rather than implying benefit. The control cohort was heterogeneous, including a substantial proportion with ADM exposure, reflecting real-world reconstructive practice rather than a strict scaffold-versus-no-scaffold comparison; importantly, stratification and subgroup sensitivity analyses did not materially change the descriptive interpretation of outcomes. Event rates were low (10 LRR events total), limiting statistical power and precluding robust multivariable adjustment. Finally, key oncologic variables (particularly HER2 status) were missing for a subset of patients, with differential missingness by cohort that may introduce selection bias; complete-case sensitivity analyses suggested similar recurrence estimates, but findings should be interpreted cautiously pending larger, prospectively curated datasets with standardized variable capture and longer follow-up.

The stability of point estimates across multiple prespecified sensitivity analyses supports the internal consistency of the descriptive results and reinforces the absence of a detectable early signal of increased LRR associated with P4HB use within the available follow-up. Nonetheless, given the observational design, low event counts, and potential residual confounding, findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The intent of this analysis was to evaluate whether any early safety signal could be detected in routine clinical practice; more definitive evaluation will require larger, prospectively curated, time-aligned cohorts with standardized surveillance and longer follow-up. This study also highlights a common challenge in real-world oncology research: key determinants of recurrence risk and detection (e.g., granular tumor biology, operative details, and imaging interpretations) often reside in unstructured clinical documentation. When these elements are incompletely captured or variably recorded, misclassification can obscure true associations and limit comparability across cohorts.

Implications and future directions

Despite these limitations, the findings provide clinically relevant, early real-world context for surgeons and patients considering adjunctive support in contemporary implant-based reconstruction. As reconstructive techniques and materials evolve, stakeholders seek evidence that newer approaches do not introduce an early oncologic safety signal. In this cohort, LRR events were infrequent in the P4HB group, and no detectable signal of increased risk versus the comparator group was observed within the available follow-up; point estimates were directionally consistent across prespecified sensitivity analyses, supporting a hypothesis-generating interpretation. Future studies should incorporate longer follow-up, time-aligned comparator cohorts, and explicit stratification by reconstructive plane and surveillance modality to further evaluate oncologic outcomes and recurrence detection as use of P4HB and prepectoral techniques continues to expand.

Conclusions

In this real-world analysis, no detectable signal of higher early‑to‑intermediate locoregional recurrence or disease‑specific mortality was observed with P4HB‑assisted implant‑based post‑mastectomy reconstruction compared with non‑P4HB reconstruction within the available follow‑up. Findings should not be interpreted as evidence of oncologic equivalence, but rather as an absence of a detectable early signal. Larger, time‑aligned datasets with plane‑stratified surveillance and analyses are warranted to further evaluate oncologic outcomes.
