Mesenteric fat proliferation on magnetic resonance enterography is a predictor of long-term disease complications in crohn’s disease
Authors: Eduard Koifman, Inna Spector Cohen, Rebeca Lopez-Alonso, Dana Ben Hur, Anat Ilivitzki, Matti Waterman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFibrofatty proliferation of mesenteric fat is a common finding on magnetic resonance enterography (MRE) in Crohn’s disease (CD) patients. Mesenteric fat may play a role in inflammation, fibrosis, and stricture formation, but its prognostic value in predicting complications remains unclear.ObjectivesTo assess the association between mesenteric fat proliferation on MRE and the risk of hospital admissions and bowel surgery in CD patients.MethodsIn this retrospective single-center study, CD patients with available MRE data were followed for CD exacerbation-related hospitalizations or abdominal surgeries. An expert radiologist reviewed MRE for intestinal wall thickening, stricture, abscess formation, fibrofatty proliferation, and fat edema. Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to analyze associations between mesenteric fat and outcomes, adjusting for clinical and imaging characteristics.ResultsAmong 175 CD patients mesenteric fat proliferation and edema were noted in 52 (29.7%) and 15 (8.9%) patients, respectively. Other findings included wall thickening (46.9% mild, 29.1% moderate, 7.4% severe), stricture (14.3%), abscess (5.7%), and fistula (6.9%). During a mean follow-up of 95.2 months, 93 (51.3%) patients were hospitalized for CD-related reasons, and 47 (26.9%) underwent CD-related abdominal surgery. Mesenteric fat proliferation was strongly associated with hospitalizations and surgery (p < 0.0001 for both). Multivariable analysis confirmed these associations with adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) of 3.45 (95%CI 1.66–7.19) 3.74 (95%CI) for surgery and 1.92 (95%CI 1.16–3.15) 2.03 (95%CI) for hospitalizations. Fat edema was linked to surgery and hospitalization but lost significance in multivariable analysis.ConclusionsMesenteric fat proliferation on MRE is independently associated with increased risk of hospitalizations and surgery in CD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04085-6.Keywords: Mesenteric fat proliferation (MFP), Crohn’s disease, MRE, Hospitalizations, Abdominal surgery

Introduction

For many years, the primary investigatory and clinical focus in Crohn’s disease (CD) has been on mucosal events, with the prevailing view that CD is predominantly an intestinal mucosal condition [1].

However, there is a growing recognition of the pathophysiological significance of extraluminal factors, including those within the mesentery. Anatomically, mesenteric fat is directly connected to the intestinal serosa and muscularis propria, extending along most of the small and large intestines. The phenomenon of hypertrophic mesenteric fat encircling the intestine, also known as “creeping fat” during surgery is characteristic of Crohn’s disease and was described by Burrill Crohn himself in a landmark report on the disease [2] and can sometimes cover more than 50% of the bowel [3].

Recent studies suggest that mesenteric fat may play a role in the pathogenesis of Crohn’s disease as it contains not only fat cells and their precursors but also immune and non-immune cells, such as endothelial cells and fibroblasts. When activated, these cell make mesenteric fat an active participant in inflammation and immunity [4]. Additionally, mesenteric fat proliferation (MFP) is linked to muscularis propria hyperplasia and stricturing disease [5].

Moreover, a previous surgical study demonstrated significantly lower post-operative recurrence rates when the resection margins included the removal of mesenteric fat in the diseased bowel segment [6].

Magnetic resonance enterography (MRE) is a widely used, non-invasive imaging modality for assessing intestinal and extraintestinal manifestations of CD. While MRE reliably detects active inflammation and structural complications, the role of mesenteric findings—particularly MFP—in predicting disease progression and long-term outcomes has not been fully elucidated.

This study aimed to assess whether the presence and extent of MFP detected on MRE are associated with CD-related hospitalizations and surgical interventions.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a descriptive observational retrospective study based on the electronic medical records and imaging records available for analysis in an IBD tertiary care center. The study was reviewed and approved by a local Ethics Committee.

Patient selection

Patients were eligible for enrollment if they had a confirmed diagnosis of Crohn’s disease, were followed-up at pediatric or adult gastroenterology clinics at our institution between 2007 and 2017 for at least one year, and had at least one available MRE examination. The observation period started after the performance of the index MRE and continued until the last clinic visit or end of 2023.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical variables were extracted from electronic medical charts. They included sex, age at diagnosis, age at the beginning of the follow-up, current and previous medical and surgical treatments as well as reasons for performing MRE scan.

Outcome definitions

Crohn’s disease-related hospitalization and surgery rates were measured as indicators of adverse disease outcomes. Disease-related hospitalizations were defined as admissions for medical flares or intestinal complications, surgical admissions related to CD (e.g. drainage procedures), or admissions for parenteral nutrition due to CD-related malnutrition. Hospitalizations for elective infusions of medications, routine diagnostic procedures, or non-GI-related complications were excluded.

Surgery was defined as any abdominal operation performed for Crohn’s disease-related indications, including bowel resection, strictureplasty, or drainage of intra-abdominal abscesses.

MRE protocol

MRE studies were performed at our center using either a 1.5T (Optima MR450) or 3 T (GE Discovery MR750) MRI scanner, with a multichannel phased array coil. The patient was positioned in a prone position. MRI studies conducted at other institutions were performed on a 1.5T scanner (Philips Achieva or GE Optima MR450).

Patients were required to fast for at least 4 h prior to the examination. Approximately 1.5 h before the scan, patients ingested 1 L of 2.5% Mannitol, taking 125 ml every five minutes until the acquisition time without administration of glucagon or butyl-scopolamine. Intravenous contrast consisted of gadoterate-meglumine, administered at a dose of 0.2 mL/kg body weight as an intravenous bolus injection at a flow rate of approximately 2 mL/second.

The standard MRE protocol at our institution until 2014 included a tri-plane localizer followed by coronal and axial Fast Imaging Employing Steady-State Acquisition (FIESTA), coronal and axial Fast Spoiled Gradient Echo (FSPGR), axial and coronal single-shot fast spin echo (SSFSE), and axial and coronal T1 weighted images with fat suppression before and after contrast administration. T2-FAT SAT and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) were introduced into our routine protocol in 2014, therefore some examinations in our cohort lacked these sequences.

MRE image analysis

The scans were reviewed by an experienced board-certified radiologist specializing in abdominal imaging with over 15 years of experience in interpreting MRE scans (R. L.-A.). The radiologist was blinded to all clinical information. Grading of bowel wall thickness, stricture, pre-stenotic dilatation, presence of wall edema, and pathological enhancement of the bowel was performed according to the Consensus Recommendations for Evaluation, Interpretation, and Utilization of Computed Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Enterography in Patients with Small Bowel Crohn’s Disease [7]: Maximal wall thickness was measured in the axial plane and categorized as mild (3–5 mm), moderate (5–9 mm), or severe (≥ 10 mm). Pathological enhancement of the bowel wall was identified as increased signal intensity on contrast enhanced scans in non-contracted segments compared to nearby normal small bowel segments. Intramural edema was identified as hyper-intense signal on fat-saturated T2-weighted images. Upstream dilatation was classified as negative (less than 3 cm of lumen dilatation pre-stenosis), mild (pre-stenotic dilatation of 3–4 cm), or moderate to severe (upstream dilatation of more than 4 cm).

Mesenteric fat proliferation was assessed on axial non-fat-suppressed SSFSE images as increased fibrofatty tissue, typically on the mesenteric side or concentric around the bowel loop, causing separation from adjacent bowel loops. Due to the lack of criteria quantifying the severity MFP, fibrofatty proliferation was categorized as either present or absent (Image 1) [8].

Presence of mesenteric fat (perienteric) edema was defined as increased signal intensity in T2-weighted imaging of the mesenteric fat adjacent to the affected bowel loops [9].

Statistics

Baseline characteristics were summarized using counts (%) for categorical variables and means (SD) for continuous variables. Comparisons between two groups were carried out using chi-square and students’ t-tests, respectively.

Time-to-event techniques were employed in outcome analysis (hospitalization; surgery) in order to account for variations in the length of observation period. For each patient, the time period (in months) between the MRI and the events of interest or end of follow up date (whichever came first) was calculated.

The time-to-event outcomes were graphically described using the Kaplan-Meier survival diagrams and compared between groups using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to estimate hazard ratio (HR) of effects of interest on each outcome. The model included various MRE variables of bowel wall inflammation (wall thickening, enhancement) and MRE criteria of bowel damage (abscess, pre-stenotic bowel lumen dilatation) as well as clinical prognostic factors, including previous surgeries and penetrating disease. Two-sided p-values ≤ 0.05 were regarded as significant. A board-certified statistician performed all analyses in R software (R version 4.2.1).

Results

We analyzed 175 CD patients with corresponding MRE data. The cohort included 104 males (61.5%), with a mean age at diagnosis of 16.9 years (SD 10.2) and at MRE of 20.5 years (SD 12.2). Thirty patients (17.1%) were current or former smokers. Prior treatments included thiopurines (39%), anti-TNF monotherapy (12%), combination therapy (10.8%), methotrexate (3.6%), and corticosteroids (22.7%). Twenty patients (11.7%) had prior abdominal surgery (Table 1).

During a mean follow-up of 95.2 months (SD 39.5), 93 patients (51.3%) required hospitalization and 47 (26.9%) underwent CD-related abdominal surgery. In operated patients, the primary indications for surgery were obstruction 22 (47%), penetrating complications (fistula or abscess) 15 (32%), and medically refractory disease 10 (21%).

On MRE, mesenteric fat proliferation (MFP) was present in 52 patients (29.7%) and mesenteric fat edema in 15 (8.9%). Bowel wall thickening (any grade) was seen in 82 (46.9%), with contrast enhancement in 137 (79.1%). Strictures, abscesses, and fistulas were present in 14.3%, 5.7%, and 6.9%, respectively (Table 3).

We performed a subgroup analysis, stratifying the cohort by age at MRE (Supp. Table 2), MFP was significantly associated with an increased risk of surgery in both adults (p = 0.003) and pediatric patients (p < 0.00001). However, the association between MFP and hospitalization reached statistical significance only in the pediatric subgroup (p = 0.0015).

Follow-up MRE was available in 28/52 patients with MFP on index MRE, with 22 (78.5%) showing persistent findings. Among the 6 patients with baseline MFP who showed a reduction or resolution of MFP on follow-up MRE: 4 underwent surgical resection and 2 were treated with escalation of biologic therapy. The progression to strictures or penetrating complications (abscess or fistula) is described in Supp. Table 3.

Among 123 patients without MFP, 65 had follow-up MREs, and 8 of them (12.3%) developed MFP. Due to small numbers, fat edema progression could not be analyzed.

Discussion

In our cohort, the presence of mesenteric fat proliferation (MFP) was found to be an independent prognostic factor for adverse outcomes, as reflected by almost twofold increased risk of CD-related hospitalizations and about 3.5-fold risk of CD-related surgery.

Our findings go in line with the results of a previous retrospective study by Althoff [10], which found creeping fat on MRI in 21% (23/90) of patients in the cohort and these patients had a 42% risk of undergoing surgery within two years (HR=3.5). Our study included a larger cohort of patients (N = 175) who were significantly younger at the time of their first MRE (mean age 20y vs 37y), were followed for a longer period, and were additionally evaluated for hospitalization related to disease flare-ups and complications, yet still demonstrated a similar outcome, as mentioned.

According to current guidelines (STRIDE II), the treatment goal for patients with Crohn’s disease is to achieve endoscopic remission, as this is associated with fewer complications, hospitalizations, and surgeries [11]. Over the years, the expansion of our therapeutic armamentariumin CD has indeed led to enhanced symptom control, increased endoscopic remission rate, improved quality of life, and reduced hospitalization and surgical rates. However, irreversible bowel damage remains a significant problem, raising the question of whether current therapies are robust enough. Alternatively, it may be suggested that treatment goals need to be broadened to truly alter the disease's natural course, given that CD is a transmural disease where damage can persist despite the presence of mucosal healing at endoscopy [12]. A recently published prospective pediatric research supports this concept, with about a third of CD patients exhibiting healing of either the mucosa or the bowel wall, but not both [13].

In this regard, MRE is an excellent modality for both diagnosing and monitoring Crohn’s disease, with an emphasis on transmural healing. It provides high diagnostic accuracy for both bowel wall involvement and extra-intestinal complications, with high intra-observer reproducibility (κ=0.8) and 85% accuracy in detecting disease-related complications when compared with surgical findings [14]. It is also capable of accurately detecting a response to treatment by using modified Clermont [14] or MaRIA scores [15]. Since Crohn's disease is characterized by cumulative transmural damage, the Lémann Index was introduced to measure additive structural bowel damage. This is a validated quantitative index based on MRE and previous surgical history, that includes bowel wall thickness, as well as strictures, pre-stenotic dilatations, fistulas, and abscesses [16]. However, neither the Clermont, MaRIA nor Lémann indexes include the mesenteric fat, perhaps due to its tendency to remain stable over time, as was recently shown by Buisson et al [17].

In this context, the results of a recent prospective study are of interest, which demonstrated that the presence of creeping fat on MRE 46 weeks after the initiation of biological therapy was associated with an increased rate of hospitalizations during a two-year follow-up [18]. Moreover, in a prospective study by Rimola [19], mesenteric fat proliferation largely persisted even in patients who achieved endoscopic remission after 1 year of therapy (85%). Mesenteric fat in Crohn’s disease (CD), often referred to as"creeping fat,"actively contributes to disease pathophysiology through several mechanisms. It secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines and adipokines—including TNF-α, IL-6, and leptin—which promote a localized inflammatory milieu and sustain transmural inflammation [12, 13, 20]. MFP also serves as a niche for immune cell infiltration and may disrupt lymphatic drainage, fostering chronic inflammation and impairing mucosal healing. Moreover, creeping fat encases the bowel, engaging in bi-directional crosstalk with the muscularis propria and enteric nervous system, potentially driving fibrosis. Fibroblast activation by adipocyte-derived mediators facilitates extracellular matrix deposition, contributing to fibrotic remodeling and strictures [21].

Emerging evidence highlight the clinical significance of persistent mesenteric involvement in Crohn’s disease. Zhou et al. proposed the mesenteric creeping fat index (MCFI) based on CT enterography, demonstrating that higher MCFI scores correlate with increased transmural inflammation and fibrotic features [22]. Similarly, Wilkens et al. [12] emphasized the value of monitoring mesenteric changes as indicators of ongoing transmural activity, independent of mucosal healing. Together, these findings support the view that mesenteric abnormalities may reflect deep tissue pathology and contribute to irreversible structural damage in Crohn’s disease.

We have observed similar findings in our cohort, with 78% of patients showing MFP on their initial MRE also demonstrating no change on the follow-up scan. Moreover, among patients without MFP on index MRE, fat proliferation appeared in 12.5% on a subsequent examination. The persistence of MFP over time and its association with adverse structural outcomes reinforce the notion that MFP may reflect a chronic, progressive disease process rather than a transient inflammatory phenomenon.

Yet, while our data suggest MFP is a relatively stable imaging feature in most patients over time, this should be interpreted cautiously. There is a need for prospective longitudinal studies with standardized imaging follow-up to establish causality.

The optimal method for assessing mesenteric fat remains a matter of debate. It is not yet clear whether this compartment should be evaluated qualitatively through radiologic features or quantitatively via measures such as the visceral-to-subcutaneous adipose tissue (VAT/SAT) ratio [20, 23]. Furthermore, the lack of a standardized and reproducible definition for mesenteric fat hypertrophy still prevents its inclusion in prognostic criteria. Due to the retrospective nature of our study and the lack of standardized fat quantification sequences, volumetric measurements of VAT and SAT were not performed. Instead, we aimed to capture the disease-specific mesenteric remodeling typically referred to as"creeping fat,"which may not fully reflect generalized fat metrics.

Other MRE parameters in our study, such as increased bowel wall thickness, contrast enhancement, strictures, and ulcerations, were also associated with adverse outcomes in the univariate analysis. However, these associations did not remain statistically significant in the multivariate regression model. We hypothesize that this may be due to multicollinearity among the included variables, leading to a phenomenon known as “statistical masking.” This occurs when the effect of one independent variable on the outcome is diminished or obscured by the inclusion of other interrelated predictors that are themselves correlated with both the outcome and the primary variable of interest.

Alternatively, it may be suggested that while such markers of inflammation were more apt to respond to immunosuppressives, they may not be relevant to the prevention of long-term complications. Data regarding the role of various imaging predictors on MRE in assessing long-term disease outcomes is mainly retrospective [24]. Fernandes et al. found that transmural remission on MRE, as defined by normal bowel wall thickness and absence of contrast enhancement and transmural complications, may represent a more suitable target than endoscopic remission, as it correlates better with long-term outcomes in CD [25]. Castiglione et al. conducted an observational study using intestinal ultrasound, reaching similar results [26]. However, neither group provided information on their patients’ mesenteric fat status. We observed that mesenteric fat edema, which may reflect infiltration of the mesenteric fat by immune cells and inflammatory exudate, was associated with CD-related hospitalizations and surgery on univariable analysis. However, this association did not persist in multivariable analysis, likely due to limited statistical power, as only 15 patients in our cohort exhibited mesenteric fat edema on MRE performed with an appropriate technique.

Importantly, while mesenteric fat edema and MFP may co-occur, they appear to represent distinct pathological processes. MFP is generally thought to reflect chronic, adaptive, and possibly fibrotic remodeling, whereas mesenteric edema likely indicates acute or subacute inflammatory activity. Recent studies support the complementary role of assessing both features to capture a more comprehensive picture of transmural disease activity [12]. As noted above, the low prevalence of mesenteric edema and its potential collinearity with other variables precluded its inclusion in multivariable models, underscoring the need for further prospective validation.

We have also demonstrated that past surgery is a risk factor for future hospital admissions. This observation is consistent with the literature, which identifies prior surgery as a risk factor for adverse outcomes, likely due to its association with penetrating or stricturing phenotypes and a predisposition to recurrence [27].

While this study has included the largest cohort reported to date and observed this cohort over more than 7 years on average, it has several important limitations. First, its retrospective design and single-center setting inherently limit the generalizability of the findings. As a tertiary care center, referral bias may have led to a higher proportion of complex or treatment-refractory cases, which may not reflect the broader Crohn’s disease population.

Second, the study spanned a decade (2007–2017), during which MRE protocols evolved, including the introduction of DWI sequences in 2014. Although all scans were reviewed by an experienced radiologist, the possibility of inter-observer variability and protocol-related inconsistencies cannot be excluded, though current literature report a strong inter-reader agreement for key CD features among experienced abdominal radiologists [28]. Third, the study lacks an external validation cohort. Given the prognostic significance of mesenteric fat proliferation (MFP) demonstrated in our analysis, future studies in independent populations are needed to confirm these findings.

Additionaly, changes in medical therapy during follow-up were not systematically captured. Patients with MFP may have received more intensive or escalated treatment regimens, potentially affecting the rates of surgery and hospitalization, thereby confounding the observed associations. Lastly, the potential for lead-time bias must be acknowledged. It is possible that patients with MFP underwent more frequent or targeted surveillance, leading to earlier detection of complications and influencing time-to-event analyses.

These limitations underscore the need for prospective, multi-center studies with standardized imaging protocols and MPF definition and treatment tracking to validate and extend our findings.

In summary, this retrospective study, the largest to date with long-term follow-up—supports the proposed role of mesenteric fat in the development of cumulative structural damage in Crohn’s disease. MFP appears to be a relatively stable imaging feature, though reduction may occur, particularly following surgical intervention or intensive therapy. Current medical treatments seem ineffective targeting MFP. However, recognizing the prognostic significance of hypertrophic mesenteric fat may catalyze future therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating this distinct pathological process.
