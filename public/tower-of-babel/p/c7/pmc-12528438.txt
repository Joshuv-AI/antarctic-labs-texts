Variation by age, smoking, and menopausal status in the impact of body mass index change on liver cancer risk
Authors: Su Youn Nam, Junwoo Jo, Chang-Min Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe influence of age, smoking, and menopausal status on the association between changes in body mass index (BMI) and liver cancer risk remains largely unexplored. We enrolled cancer-free individuals who underwent national health examinations in 2010 and 2014, following them until 2021. BMI was classified as underweight, normal, overweight, obesity I, and obesity II, with BMI changes categorized into 25 groups. Among 3.8 million, 26,188 developed liver cancers. Persistent high BMI was associated with increased liver cancer risk compared to persistent normal BMI. Among individuals with obesity, further BMI gain (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.17; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.04–1.31) and excessive BMI loss (aHR 1.29; 95% CI 1.14–1.46) raised liver cancer risk. Among individuals experiencing overweight, excessive BMI gain raised liver cancer risk in men (aHR 2.34; 95% CI 1.06–5.19), never smokers (aHR 2.22; 95% CI 1.00–4.94), and age ≥ 60 years (aHR 2.45; 95% CI 1.17–5.10). Among underweight and normal-weight women, excessive BMI gain increased cancer risk in premenopausal women (aHR 7.54; 95% CI 1.05–54.36). Excessive BMI loss increased liver cancer risk in most subgroups except premenopausal women. Introduction

New liver cancer account for 4.7% of all cancers and 8.3% of cancer-related deaths worldwide during 2020, underscoring its significant public health burden1. Obesity has been increasingly recognized as a risk factor for liver cancer, highlighting its growing importance in medical research2 and it is a modifiable risk factor related to liver cancer3. However, the relationship between obesity changes and liver cancer risk remains inconsistent. While weight gain is associated with an increased liver cancer risk4,5, unintended weight loss has also shown similar associations6. Previous studies investigated the effects of overall obesity changes on cancer risk7–9. Recent studies examined the effect of obesity index changes among obese or normal-weight individuals5,10,11. Digestive cancer risk increased across three body mass index (BMI) trajectories compared with persistent normal BMI5,10. A Korean cohort study also found that persistent obesity was associated with liver cancer risk in both men and women11. A cohort study from USA suggested that Adulthood weight gain (≥ 20 kg) comparing to stable weight was associated with an increased liver cancer risk in men but not women12.

Although sex-specific analyses on the association between obesity index change and liver cancer risk have been reported11,12, subgroup analysis by smoking, menopausal status, and age in this association has not been reported. Smoking may alter fat distribution, insulin resistance, and inflammation, potentially modifying the link between BMI change and cancer risk. Despite lower BMI, smokers often exhibit higher visceral fat and inflammation, suggesting that cancer risk may differ by smoking status13. Postmenopausal hormonal changes may modify the metabolic impact of obesity, leading to differential effects of BMI change on cancer risk by menopausal status14. Age-related declines in basal metabolic rate, loss of muscle mass (sarcopenia), and increased visceral fat can lead to differential metabolic effects of the same BMI change across age groups15. The inconsistent findings in the literature may arise from small sample size, methodological limitations (e.g., inadequate control for confounding factors and reverse causation) and heterogeneity in study populations. Addressing these gaps with robust methodologies is crucial to accurately assess the impact of BMI changes and to provide actionable insights for liver cancer prevention.

In this study, we explored the association between BMI change and liver cancer risk using the Korean National Health Insurance Service System (NHIS), a resource widely utilized in cancer research11,16,17. Furthermore, we investigated the variations in effect across sex, age group, smoking status, and menopausal status on this association.

Results

Baseline characteristics

After excluding individuals with a history of cancer within the past five years, a total of 5.389 million individuals underwent both NGHE and cancer screening in 2010, with at least one follow-up NGHE through 2021. Following the exclusion of patients with any cancer diagnosis within 12 months (n = 60,889), 5.328 million individuals were eligible. We further excluded those who did not undergo NGHE at 2014 (n = 1,499,632), absence of follow-up BMI (n = 1,902), and unknown sex type (n = 4,779). Ultimately, 3.8 million individuals (41.5% men; mean age of 54.5 years) were eligible and followed through 2021 (Fig. 1a). Impact of BMI change on liver cancer risk among individuals with obesity

Among individuals with obesity, both further BMI gain (from obesity I to obesity II; aHR 1.17; 95% CI 1.04–1.31) and excessive BMI loss (from obesity I to normal; aHR 1.29; 95% CI 1.14–1.46) were associated with an increased liver cancer risk compared to individuals maintaining obesity I (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 3). Among individuals with overweight, BMI loss (from overweight to normal BMI) slightly increased liver cancer risk (aHR 1.10; 95% CI 1.04–1.17) and marked BMI gain (from overweight to obesity II) had a borderline association with liver cancer risk comparing to individuals maintaining overweight (aHR 1.87; 95% CI 0.97–3.57).

Subgroup analysis by sex and smoking status

Interactions between BMI change and cofactors were observed in relation to liver cancer risk (Supplementary Table 4). A sex-specific effect of BMI change on liver cancer risk was observed (Supplementary Table 5). Marked BMI gain (aHR 1.32; 95% CI 1.08–1.61 [from normal to obesity I] and aHR 2.34; 95% CI 1.06–5.19 [from overweight to obesity II]) increased liver cancer risk in men (Fig. 3a). Individuals maintaining obesity increased liver cancer risk with a dose dependent manner and marked BMI loss among individuals with obesity increased liver cancer risk in both men and women (Fig. 3b).

Variations in effect across smoking status in the association between BMI change and liver cancer risk was observed (Supplementary Table 6). Individuals maintaining low BMI reduced liver cancer risk compared to individuals maintaining normal BMI only in never smokers (aHR 0.77; 95% CI 0.63–0.94). Among individuals with overweight, persons maintaining overweight, those experiencing BMI loss or further BMI gain increased liver cancer risk exclusively in never smokers. Among individuals with obesity II, excessive BMI loss (from obesity II to overweight) increased liver cancer risk in smokers (aHR 2.32; 95% CI 1.01–5.30) but not in never smokers (Fig. 3c, 3 d).

Subgroup analysis by age and menopausal status

The effect of BMI change on liver cancer risk varied by age group (Supplementary Table 7). Among individuals with obesity, both individuals maintaining obesity and experiencing excessive BMI loss (from obesity I to normal) were associated with an increased liver cancer risk in both age groups (Fig. 4a, 4b). Among individuals with overweight, excessive BMI gain (from overweight to obesity II) increased liver cancer risk (aHR 2.45; 95% CI 1.17–5.10) only in those aged ≥ 60 years. Conversely, BMI loss among those with normal BMI (from normal to underweight) and excessive BMI loss among those with obesity II (from obesity II to overweight) were associated with an increased liver cancer risk only in those aged < 60 years.

Although no significant interaction between menopausal status and BMI change was found in the joint test, a previous study suggested the discrepancy by menopausal status in colorectal cancer17. Therefore, we conducted subgroup analysis by menopausal status (Supplementary Table 8). Individuals maintaining high BMI was associated with an increased liver cancer risk regardless of menopausal status. Notably, excessive BMI gain was associated with a markedly elevated liver cancer risk (aHR 7.13; 95% CI 1.00–50.84 [from underweight to overweight] and aHR 7.54; 95% CI 1.05–54.36 [from normal to obesity II]) only in premenopausal women (Fig. 4c, 4 d). BMI loss among those with normal BMI was associated with an increased liver cancer risk only in premenopausal women. The impact of BMI change on liver cancer risk in postmenopausal women was similar with those in overall women.

Discussion

In this large cohort study, maintenance of high BMI, further BMI gain, and even excessive BMI loss among individuals with obesity were associated with an increased liver cancer risk. Marked BMI gain among individuals with normal or overweight was associated with an increased liver cancer risk in men. Among those with obesity II, excessive BMI loss was associated with an increased liver cancer risk in smokers and individuals under 60 years, whereas excessive BMI gain among individuals with overweight was associated with an increased liver cancer risk in those aged ≥ 60 years. Among women with underweight and normal-weight, excessive BMI gain was associated with a markedly raised liver cancer risk in premenopausal women. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the variations in effect across age, smoking, and menopausal status in the association between liver cancer and changes in obesity indices.

We found that a dose-dependent relationship between sustained high BMI and liver cancer risk, consistent with previous cohort studies (Supplementary Table 9)11,18. Additionally, BMI gain among individuals with normal BMI was linked to an elevated liver cancer risk, and these associations remained robust across various sensitivity analyses. Among individuals with obesity, both further BMI gain and excessive BMI loss increased liver cancer risk compared to individuals maintaining obesity I. This suggests that not only sustained obesity but also significant weight fluctuations may adversely impact liver health. Excessive BMI gain among individuals with overweight was associated with an increased liver cancer risk, reinforcing the importance of monitoring weight changes in higher BMI groups. Most previous studies focused on weight changes relative to stable normal BMI4,5,12. One study assessed BMI change relative to those maintaining obesity, finding that weight loss among those with obesity I was associated with an increased liver cancer risk, while weight gain in the same group had no significant effect11. With a large sample size and robust methodology, our findings offer valuable insights into the risks associated with both BMI loss and gain among individuals with obesity.

Our findings that maintenance of high BMI and BMI gain among those with obesity were associated with an increased liver cancer risk in both men and women align with previous studies11,12. However, excessive BMI gain in individuals with normal or overweight significantly affected only men. Excessive BMI loss (from obesity I to normal) was associated with an increased liver cancer risk in both men and women, contrasting with most studies that found no significant association between weight loss and liver cancer risk12,19. One study suggested that weight loss from obesity I was associated with an increased liver cancer risk in men (aHR 1.37) but not in women11. Obesity is known to promote hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress—all of which contribute to liver carcinogenesis20. Conversely, unintentional or excessive weight loss in obese individuals may reflect underlying metabolic dysregulation, sarcopenia, or progression of subclinical liver disease, leading to increased cancer susceptibility. Weight loss is often observed in patients with advanced cancers and may be a consequence rather than a cause of cancer. To address reverse causation, we excluded cancers diagnosed within one year (Model I), two years (Model II), and up to the second BMI measurement (Model III). Across all models, excessive BMI loss among individuals with obesity remained consistently associated with increased liver cancer risk. The consistent association between BMI loss and liver cancer across multiple models, even after excluding early cases, suggests that this may not be merely reverse causation but may also involve biological mechanisms related to metabolic instability or muscle loss.

We incorporated key covariates such as lipid levels, diabetes, and chronic liver disease in our multivariable models. These factors are biologically linked to both BMI and liver cancer risk and may mediate or confound the observed associations. For example, dyslipidemia and type 2 diabetes exacerbate hepatic inflammation and fibrosis21. However, as not all obese individuals have dyslipidemia and lipid profiles are independently associated with liver cancer risk, we treated lipids as confounders. The association between BMI change and liver cancer remained consistent regardless of lipid inclusion in the model.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate how age, smoking, and menopausal status modify the association between liver cancer and longitudinal changes in obesity indices. The association between BMI change and liver cancer varied by age. Maintenance of obesity and excessive BMI loss among individuals with obesity were associated with an increased the liver cancer risk in both age groups. However, excessive BMI gain among individuals with overweight was associated with an increased liver cancer risk only in those aged ≥ 60 years, wherein BMI loss in individuals with normal BMI and excessive BMI loss (from obesity II to overweight) were associated with an increased risk of liver cancer only in those aged < 60 years. The molecular mechanisms underlying the age-specific effects of excessive BMI gain or loss on liver cancer risk remain unclear, but it is likely that sex hormone or other age-related factors may influence obesity-related liver carcinogenesis.

The relationship between BMI change and liver cancer risk varied with smoking status. Maintenance of high BMI was associated with an increased liver cancer risk in both never and current smokers, while maintaining low BMI was protective only in never smokers. Among individuals with overweight, maintenance of overweight, BMI loss or gain were associated with an elevated liver cancer risk only in never smokers, whereas excessive BMI loss (from obesity II to overweight) was associated with an increased liver cancer risk exclusively in smokers. Smoking’s dual effects—weight modulation and carcinogenesis—may obscure or amplify the association between BMI change and liver cancer risk in smokers. Adult smokers tend to weigh 4 to 5 kg less than non-smokers and tend to gain weight after quitting22,23, which may further complicate the relationship between BMI change and liver cancer risk.

Menopausal status further modified the association between BMI change and liver cancer risk. Maintenance of obesity was associated with an increased liver cancer risk in both premenopausal and postmenopausal women, while excessive BMI gain in women with underweight or normal-weight was linked to a significantly higher liver cancer risk in premenopausal women. In contrast, excessive BMI loss among individuals with obesity was associated with an increased liver cancer risk in postmenopausal women. These findings likely reflect the complex role of estrogen, which can both protect against hepatic steatosis24 and increase liver cancer risk through excessive estrogen exposure25. The observed differences emphasize the interplay between hormonal changes and BMI in liver carcinogenesis. In premenopausal women, excessive weight gain may heighten estrogen exposure (overexposure), whereas in postmenopausal women, significant fat loss may reduce peripheral estrogen production from adipose tissue (underexposure), both of which could affect liver carcinogenesis. This underscores the importance of hormonal context in interpreting obesity-related cancer risks.

This study has several strengths. First, the consistent impact of BMI change on liver cancer risk across three models validate the robustness of our results. Second, we are the first to investigate the variations in effect across age, smoking, and menopausal status on BMI change and liver cancer risk, uncovering critical interactions. Third, we clarified the impact of BMI loss or gain on liver cancer risk specifically among individuals with obesity or overweight. Fourth, our large sample size and use of high-quality NHISS data, including direct BMI measurements and comprehensive covariates, enhance the reliability of our conclusions. However, there are some limitations. The 11-year follow-up may be relatively short, but the large cohort provided enough events for meaningful analysis. NAFLD was identified using ICD-10 codes K75.8 and K76.0, which may be subject to some degree of misclassification. Nonetheless, reliance on disease codes is an inherent limitation in large-scale nationwide epidemiological studies. Another limitation is the inability to distinguish intentional from unintentional weight loss, particularly among participants who became underweight (BMI < 18.5). Dietary factors, body composition, Habit changes, like smoking, were not considered, and external validation was not conducted. To minimize the possibility of undetected cancer at baseline, we conducted analyses using three models excluding cases diagnosed within 1 year of baseline (model I), within 2 years (model II), and up to the time of the second BMI measurement (model III). However, it is not possible to completely rule out this possibility. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the complex relationship between BMI change and liver cancer risk and lays the groundwork for future research.

In conclusion, maintenance of high BMI and both further BMI gain and excessive BMI loss among individuals with obesity were associated with an increased liver cancer risk. Excessive BMI gain notably increased liver cancer risk in premenopausal women with underweight and normal weight. Excessive BMI gain among individuals with overweight were associated with an elevated liver cancer risk in men, never smokers, and age ≥ 60 years. The finding that excessive BMI loss was associated with an increased liver cancer risk in smokers and those under 60 years highlights the importance of avoiding rapid weight reduction in these populations. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining a normal BMI and highlights the risks associated with both excessive BMI loss and gain, with variations depending on sex, age, smoking, and menopausal status.

Methods

Data extraction

The Institutional Review Board of the Kyungpook National University Chilgok Hospital approved this study (KNUCH 2021–07-047). Data for this large cohort study were obtained from the Korean NHIS (REQ202204452-004), which includes information from the National General Health Examination (NGHE) and the national cancer screening program. The NGHE data include demographic information (age, sex, economic status), health-related factors (chronic diseases, medications, habits, use of lipid-lowering drugs, physical activity), and BMI (weight/height2 [kg/m2]), blood pressure (mmHg), and fasting serum levels of glucose and lipids (mg/dL). BMI was calculated using body weight and height, which was directly measured by health providers. Sex is a biological sex registered in NHIS and age is calculated from birth year and the year which individuals underwent baseline NGHE. Economic status was based on household income, ranked on a scale from 1 to 20. Missing data for hypertension and diabetes was replaced by using blood pressure (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic pressure ≥ 90 mmHg) and fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL. Missing data for ischemic heart disease and cerebrovascular disease was replaced by using the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code within 1 year (heart disease: I20-I25, cerebrovascular disease: I60-I69).

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease was identified using codes K75.8 or K76.017. Smoking status was categorized as never, past, and current smoker and alcohol consumption frequency was classified into none, 1/week, 2–3/week, 4–5/week, and ≥ 6/week. Moderate physical activity referred to “walking or exercising and feeling mild dyspnea for more than 30 min per day.” National cancer screening data included family history of cancer, women-specific factors (menopausal status, breastfeeding duration, parity, use of oral contraceptives, and estrogen replacement therapy), and liver-related factors (chronic liver disease, chronic hepatitis B and C, and liver cirrhosis). Details on handling missing data are is described in our previous study (Supplementary Method)16,17.

Baseline enrollment and follow-up

We obtained all available data for NGHE and cancer screening for individuals who underwent NGHE and any cancer screening in 2010 and had at least one follow-up NGHE through 2021. From 5.389 million subjects who met these criteria, we excluded patients with any C-codes within 12 months, individuals who did not receive NGHE in 2014, those without follow-up BMI data, and those with unknown sex (Model I, Fig. 1a and 1b). All cancer codes (C codes) of the International Classification of Disease (10th revision [ICD 10]) were extracted until December 2021, with liver cancers defined as C22. Informed consent was waived, as the NHISS provided anonymized data to researchers, ensuring no impact on individual disease courses.

Statistical analysis

Due to the fixed number of available subjects, we did not calculate the sample size, but calculations for the primary analysis are provided.

Framework

Persistent high BMI will increase the risk of liver cancer risk comparing to persistent normal BMI group. Change from obese to normal or overweight will reduce liver cancer risk comparing to persistent obese person. More gain of BMI among obese person will increase liver cancer risk.

We put Alpha error as 0.05 and Power as 80%. If two independent groups, Group 1 incidence of liver cancer: 0.4%, Group 2 incidence of liver cancer: 0.35%

In this study, BMI will be categorized as 25 groups.

The portion of the lowest and highest BMI group is about 3% in previous our study. Combination of middle 3 group produce 9 groups (3 × 3).

Therefore, minimal sample size = 234,582 × 9 + 234,582 × 16 × 0.03 (3%) = 2,223,837.

Summary statistics are presented as numbers (percentages) or median (interquartile range). We calculated person-years from the date of baseline NGHE to the first date of diagnosis of cancer, death, or study end (December 31, 2021). The NHIS conducts health screenings every two years, and this study included individuals who had their BMI measured in both 2010 and 2014. BMI at baseline and follow-up was categorized into five groups based on the Asian pacific guideline26; < 18.5 (underweight), 18.5–22.9 (normal), 23–24.9 (overweight), 25–29.9 (obesity I), ≥ 30 kg/m2 (obesity II). BMI changes were classified into 25 groups using baseline and follow-up BMIs (Fig. 1c).

Development of liver cancer over time by BMI was investigated using a Kaplan–Meier method (Fig. 2a). The assumption of proportional hazards was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, showing no evidence of violation. However, the results based on Schoenfeld residuals test were not separately reported. Liver cancer risk according to BMI change during follow-up through 2021 was estimated using hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with persistent normal BMI set as the reference. Traditional Cox proportional hazards models can yield biased estimates in the presence of competing events. To mitigate this, we treated liver cancer as the event of interest and classified other cancers and death as competing events, employing Fine and Gray’s sub-distribution hazard model. The liver cancer risks by covariates, which are measured at baseline (2010), were also assessed. A multivariate analysis adjusted for significant covariates such as age, sex, economic status, hypertension, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, heart disease, smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity, liver disease, chronic hepatitis B and C, liver cirrhosis, family history of liver cancer, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and the use of lipid-lowering medications. Dyslipidemia may act as mediators as well as cofactors in the association between BMI change and liver cancer risk. The association between BMI change and liver cancer risk remained consistent regardless of whether lipid levels were included. Accordingly, we present only the results from the lipid-adjusted model. Among the final eligible population, missing rates for economic status, physical activity, drinking status (0.3%), smoking status (0.3%), and women-specific variables (0.9%) were under 2%, leading to list-wise deletion of missing data in adjusted analysis (Supplementary Method).

For sensitivity analysis, we excluded all cancers diagnosed within 2 years following the first measurement of BMI (Model II) and those diagnosed before the second BMI measurement to minimize reverse causality from cancer-related weight loss (Model III) (Fig. 1b). Interaction analyses (joint tests) were performed between key cofactors (sex, age group, smoking status, and menopausal status) and BMI change in relation to liver cancer risk. Subgroup analysis was conducted by key cofactors. For women, analyses were further adjusted for women-specific factors (menopausal status, breastfeeding duration, parity, and use of oral contraceptives). All analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), results with a P-value < 0.05 were considered significant. Figures were created using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).
