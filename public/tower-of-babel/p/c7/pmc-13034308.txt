Self-reported sexually transmitted infections among adolescents and adults in Zambia and Zimbabwe: evidence from Demographic and Health Surveys, 2005–2018
Authors: Anke Rotsaert, Tom Smekens, Chido Dziva Chikwari, Mwelwa Muleba Phiri, Primrose Matambanadzo, Bernadette Hensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesGlobally, curable sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are increasing, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, yet epidemiological data remain limited, hindering progress towards the 2030 global STI targets. This study assessed the prevalence of self-reported STIs in the last 12 months among adolescent girls and women (AGW) and adolescent boys and men (ABM) aged 15–59 who ever had sex in Zambia and Zimbabwe at three time points. It also assessed whether observed changes across survey rounds persisted after adjusting for sociodemographic and sexual behaviour characteristics.DesignWe analysed six rounds of Demographic and Health Surveys, three per country, collected between 2005 and 2018, using descriptive statistics and logistic regression, while accounting for survey design.SettingZambia and Zimbabwe.ParticipantsA total weighted sample of 86 366 AGW and ABM was included in the study.Outcome measureSelf-reported STIs in the last 12 months.ResultsOverall, self-reported STI prevalence was higher in Zimbabwe than Zambia. Among Zambian ABM, self-reported STIs increased from 6.2% in 2007 to 7.1% in 2018 (adjOR=1.28, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.57, p=0.005). Among Zimbabwean AGW, prevalence decreased from 11.7% in 2005/2006 to 8.3% in 2015 (adjOR=0.72, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.85, p<0.001). No significant differences between survey rounds were observed among AGW in Zambia (2007: 5.2%, 2018: 5.1%, adjOR=1.01, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.22, p=0.070) and ABM in Zimbabwe (2005/2006: 7.9%, 2015: 8.4%; adjOR=1.13, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.38, p=0.371). Self-reported STIs were associated with marital status, HIV status and number of sexual partners in the last 12 months among ABM and AGW. Among ABM, self-reported STIs decreased with older age at sexual debut. In Zimbabwe, lower educational attainment and wealth were also associated with higher STI prevalence.ConclusionThese findings highlight differences in STI prevalence by sex and country across survey rounds, underscoring the need for tailored STI prevention, diagnostic and treatment strategies, particularly for high-risk groups.Keywords: Public health, Prevalence, Africa South of the Sahara, Sexually Transmitted Disease

Introduction

Globally, the prevalence and incidence of curable sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including chlamydia (CT), gonorrhoea (NG), trichomoniasis (TV), and syphilis, are increasing.1 In 2020, there were an estimated 374 million new cases of these four curable STIs among adolescents and adults aged 15–49.2 The global response to STIs is, therefore, off-track to meet the 2030 target of ending STIs as a public health concern.1 If left untreated, STIs can lead to pelvic inflammatory disease in women, infertility and sterility, increase susceptibility to acquire HIV and adversely affect maternal and child health.3 Additionally, rising antimicrobial resistance (AMR) threatens effective treatment.4 A major challenge in STI management is that STIs are often asymptomatic or present non-specific symptoms, leading to undertreatment and overtreatment, respectively, and risk of complications.3 Despite the growing burden of STIs, prioritisation of STI prevention has lagged.5

Accurate STI epidemiologic data, along with reliable estimates of infection and disease burden, are crucial for informing public health strategies, guiding resource allocation and evaluating the effectiveness of interventions aimed at reducing transmission.6 Although STI data collection has improved, substantial gaps remain, especially in low-income and middle-income countries.6 The available data largely stem from modelling estimates, studies conducted in specific geographic regions, individuals seeking care or specific key populations, such as female sex workers.7 8 Additionally, there is a paucity of data examining long-term trends over time.7 9 10 In response to these gaps, the WHO has identified improving STI epidemiological data as a global research priority, aiming to generate the evidence needed for more effective prevention and control efforts.5 6

According to the Global Burden of Diseases study, the sub-Saharan African region had the highest age-standardised incidence rate (19 973.12 per 100 000 person-years, 95% uncertainty interval (UI) 17 382.69 to 23 001.57) and age-standardised disability-adjusted life years rate (389.32 per 100 000 person-years, 95% UI 154.27 to 769.74).10 Evidence suggests that within the region, adolescents and young individuals aged 10–44 are more likely to be affected by STIs.10,12

In Zambia and Zimbabwe, STIs are a public health concern.13 14 Results from the Population-based HIV Impact Assessments (2015–2016) showed a prevalence of active syphilis of 3.0% in Zambia and 1.8% in Zimbabwe.15 16 In Zambia, a study among female sex workers and single mothers with children aged under five, conducted between 2016 and 2019, found a prevalence of 11% for CT and 6.8% for NG.17 In Zimbabwe, high prevalence has also been reported of CT, NG and/or syphilis among key populations: up to 50% of female sex workers tested positive for any STI,18 55% of young women eligible for HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis had an STI detected at least once,19 and 5.5% of men who have sex with men, transwomen and genderqueer individuals were diagnosed with active syphilis.20 These studies offer important insights into STI prevalence among specific populations; however, there is a need for a better understanding of the population-level burden of STIs in these countries using representative samples, whether there is an increase or decrease over time, and what is driving these trends.

This analysis explored whether there were differences between Demographic and Health Survey rounds in the prevalence of self-reported STIs among adolescent girls and women (AGW) aged 15–49 and adolescent boys and men (ABM) aged 15–54 or 15–59 in Zambia and Zimbabwe. It also assessed the extent to which any changes between survey rounds in self-reported STIs were attributable to differences in sociodemographic characteristics and/or sexual behaviours.

Methods

Data source and study population

We used data collected through the nationally representative Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) from Zambia and Zimbabwe. We included six surveys, three from each country, conducted between 2005 and 2018.21,26 We selected Zambia and Zimbabwe due to the availability of DHS data across multiple survey waves, their comparable HIV prevalence and the research experience and collaborations within these countries. Health services primarily rely on syndromic management for STIs, with syphilis testing most commonly available through antenatal care services.1427,29 After 2018, there have been efforts to strengthen STI surveillance and expand diagnostic testing; however, access remains low and varies by region, gender and socioeconomic status.1427,29 Although national policies support sexual and reproductive health and HIV prevention, service uptake continues to be constrained by limited resources, stigma and gender norms limit service uptake.1427,29

The DHS surveys in both countries used two-stage sampling to allow for estimates that are representative, including by age and sex, at the national, provincial and at urban/rural level within provinces. In the first stage of sampling, geographical areas (enumeration areas) were randomly selected and, in the second stage, households within the enumeration areas were randomly selected.21,26

In both countries, AGW aged 15–49 who were permanent residents or visitors who stayed in the household the previous night were invited to complete the survey, in line with DHS protocols.30 For men, the eligibility criteria for residency were the same as women’s; however, the age limit for participation differed by country: in Zambia, men aged up to 59 were eligible, whereas, in Zimbabwe, men aged up to 54 were eligible. All consenting individuals were asked to complete a questionnaire and offered HIV testing services.

For the purpose of our analyses, we restricted the data to individuals who self-reported ever having had sex, regardless of their age.

Outcome of interest and explanatory variables

Our outcome of interest was self-report of ever being diagnosed with an STI or self-reporting symptoms (ie, a bad-smelling, abnormal discharge from the vagina/penis or a genital sore or ulcer) of an STI (defined hereafter as self-reported STIs) in the past 12 months. The outcome variable was derived from three questions: During the last 12 months, have you had a disease which you got through sexual contact? During the last 12 months, have you had an abnormal discharge from your penis or bad-smelling abnormal genital discharge? During the last 12 months, have you had a sore or ulcer on or near your penis or genital sore or ulcer? For purposes of this analysis, a participant who answered ‘yes’ to any of these questions was defined as having an STI in the last 12 months.

The main explanatory variable under consideration was year, as defined by the survey rounds, namely: 2007, 2013–2014 and 2018 for Zambia; and 2005–2006, 2010–2011 and 2015 for Zimbabwe. Other explanatory variables included sociodemographic factors, namely age (categorised in groups of 5 years), marital status (never married, married, living together, widowed, divorced, not living together), highest educational attainment (no education, primary, secondary, higher) and wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest). The wealth index is the quintile of a standardised score constructed through principal component analysis based on the household’s ownership of certain consumer items and other characteristics related to standards of living and wealth status.21,26 Finally, explanatory variables related to sexual health and behaviours: age at first sex (in four categories: <16, 17–18, 18–19 or 20+ years), number of sexual partners in the last 12 months (in three categories: 0, 1, or 2+), living with HIV (based on the number of individuals testing positive for HIV as part of the DHS survey), and, for men, whether the respondent is circumcised or not.

Data analysis

For both countries, we described the outcome and explanatory variables across survey rounds. Proportions and χ2 tests were performed while accounting for sampling weights, clustering and stratification as part of the complex survey design of the DHS.

To assess differences between survey rounds in self-reported STI prevalence in the last 12 months (as measured by our outcome variable), we used logistic regression to estimate ORs for survey round. To account for potential changes in sociodemographics and sexual behaviours likely to influence STI prevalence between survey rounds, we adjusted for all explanatory variables.

To explore the individual-level sociodemographics and sexual behaviours associated with self-reported STIs, we estimated the crude ORs for each explanatory variable. Subsequently, we assessed the ORs adjusted for potential confounders, using multivariable logistic regression models, namely survey round (in the case of age), survey round and sociodemographic variables (in the case of the sociodemographic variables) and all variables (in the case of the sexual behaviour variables). These analyses were done separately for men and women within Zambia and Zimbabwe and were likewise adjusted for the DHS’ complex survey design. The models were diagnosed for multicollinearity using survey-adjusted variance inflation factors.31 All analyses were performed in R with the survey package.32

Results

Description of study populations across survey rounds

Our analysis included 25 515 ABM aged 15–59 years and 27 999 AGW aged 15–49 years in Zambia and 13 967 ABM aged 15–54 years and 18 885 AGW aged 15–49 years in Zimbabwe (online supplemental appendix tables 1,2).

In Zambia, the percentage of never married individuals increased between 2007 and 2018 (AGW: 15.4% vs 21.0% and ABM: 29.1% vs 32.9%, respectively; p<0.001). Educational attainment improved, with a decline in individuals reporting no education (AGW: 11.4% vs 8.3%, p<0.001; ABM: 5.0% vs 4.2%, p=0.001, respectively). Early sexual debut (<16 years) decreased among ABM between 2007 and 2018 (35.6% to 29.3%; p<0.001), but increased among AGW between 2013 and 2018 (35.2% to 40.8%; p<0.001). In 2018, 30.5% of ABM reported being circumcised compared with 13.1% in 2007 (p<0.001). Fewer ABM were living with HIV in 2018 compared with 2007 (9.5% vs 13.5, respectively; p<0.001; online supplemental appendix table 1).

In Zimbabwe, a higher percentage of ABM reported never being married in 2005/2006 compared with 2015 (31.1% vs 27.1%, p<0.001), while more AGW reported being a widow (2005/2006: 9.2% vs 2015: 5.2%; p<0.001). Fewer AGW and ABM reported no education across survey rounds (AGW: 5.3% vs 1.5% and ABM: 1.6% vs 0.7%, respectively, p<0.001). Early sexual debut (<16 years) declined among AGW from 20.7% in 2005/2006 to 17.4% in 2015 (p<0.001). Reports of having one sexual partner in the last 12 months increased among AGW (82.5% vs 87.1%; p<0.001), while reports of 2+ partners increased among ABM (12.4% vs 18.1%; p<0.001). There was evidence for a small increase in self-reported circumcision (2005/2006: 12.1% and 2015: 13.0%; p<0.001). HIV prevalence declined among AGW (19.6% vs 25.4%) and ABM (13.7% vs 18.8%) between 2005/2006 and 2015 (p<0.001) (online supplemental appendix table 2)

Self-reported prevalence of STIs in the last 12 months across survey rounds

Among AGW in Zambia, the prevalence of self-reported STIs remained stable (2007: 5.2% vs 2018: 5.1%), with little evidence of an association between survey rounds and self-reported STIs in the last 12 months (adjOR=1.01 95% CI 0.83 to 1.22; p=0.070) (online supplemental appendix table 3). Conversely, among ABM, self-reported STIs increased between survey rounds, from 6.2% in 2007 to 7.1% in 2018 (OR=1.01 95% CI 1.00 to 1.02). After adjusting for differences in sociodemographics and sexual behaviours across survey rounds, there remained evidence of an association between survey round and self-reported STIs among ABM (adjOR=1.28 95% CI 1.05 to 1.57, p=0.005) (online supplemental appendix table 3)

In Zimbabwe, self-reported STIs decreased among AGW from 11.7% in 2005/2006 to 8.3% in 2015 (adjOR=0.72 95% CI 0.61 to 0.85; p<0.001), whereas self-reported STIs remained stable across survey rounds among ABM (7.9% in 2005/2006, 7.3% in 2010/2011 and 8.4% in 2015), with little evidence of an association between survey round and self-reported STI diagnoses (adjOR=1.13 95% CI 0.92 to 1.38; p=0.371) (online supplemental appendix table 4)

Individual-level factors associated with self-reported STIs

In Zambia, self-reported STIs were higher among AGW cohabiting compared with those who had never married (11.8% n=19 vs 4.3% n=239, adjOR=2.76 95% CI 1.40 to 5.47; p<0.001). Among ABM, divorced individuals had a higher prevalence of self-reported STIs than those never married (14.6% n=91 vs 6.5% n=548, adjOR=2.45 95% CI 1.78 to 3.38; p<0.001). In Zimbabwe, similarly, cohabiting AGW self-reported more STIs than never-married AGW (16.2% n=97 vs 7.9% n=139, adjOR=1.96 95% CI 1.39 to 2.77; p<0.001) and divorced ABM self-reported more STIs than never-married ABM (14.8% n=47 vs 7.7% n=319; adjOR=2.41 95% CI 1.61 to 3.60; p<0.001) (online supplemental appendix tables 3,4)

In Zambia, there was little evidence that educational attainment or wealth was associated with self-reported STIs. In Zimbabwe, 6.1% (n=259) of AGW in the highest wealth quintile (ie, richest) self-reported an STI compared with 11.9% (n=408) in the lowest quintile (ie, poorest) (adjOR=0.58 95% CI 0.46 to 0.72; p<0.001). Among AGW with primary education, 12.5% (n=714) self-reported STIs compared with 8.7% (n=43) of AGW with no education (adjOR=1.47 95% CI 1.03 to 2.10; p<0.001). Among ABM, there was no association with wealth quintile; however, fewer ABM with higher education self-reported STIs relative to ABM with no education (4.1% n=48 vs 8.5% n=16, respectively; adjOR=0.48 95% CI 0.25 to 0.92; p<0.001).

Among AGW and ABM in both countries, living with HIV was associated with self-reported STIs (p<0.001). In Zambia, among ABM, self-reported STIs decreased with older age at sexual debut; those initiating sex ≥20 years had significantly lower STI prevalence than those starting <16 years (p<0.001). Similarly, in Zimbabwe, later sexual debut (≥20 years) among ABM was associated with fewer STIs than earlier debut (<16 years; p=0.002).

In both countries, a higher number of sexual partners in the last 12 months among AGW and ABM was associated with self-reported STIs. In Zambia, 16.5% (n=78) of AGW who reported 2+ partners in the last 12 months self-reported an STI compared with 4.8% (n=1170) who reported one partner (adjOR=4.53 95% CI 3.10 to 6.61; p<0.001); in Zimbabwe, results were similar at 25.9% (n=67) and 10.0% (n=1506), respectively (adjOR=3.56 95% CI 2.33 to 5.45; p<0.001).

In three of the four fully adjusted models, education level had a variance inflation factor slightly higher than 10, suggesting high multicollinearity with other variables.

Discussion

Our analysis of nationally representative data from Zambia and Zimbabwe showed a higher prevalence of self-reported STIs in Zimbabwe and identified a small, but statistically significant increase across survey rounds in self-reported STIs in the last 12 months among ABM in Zambia and a decrease among AGW in Zimbabwe; we found no evidence of an association between survey round and self-reported STIs in the last 12 months among AGW in Zambia and ABM in Zimbabwe. Self-reported STIs were associated with marital status, HIV status and number of sexual partners in the last 12 months among ABM and AGW. Among ABM, self-reported STIs decreased with older age at sexual debut. Additionally, in Zimbabwe, lower educational attainment and wealth were associated with higher prevalence.

Our analysis is limited by the self-reported nature of the outcome. STIs are often asymptomatic and STI symptoms non-specific, particularly among AGW. As such, self-reported symptoms may not accurately reflect the burden of STIs in the population.33 We consider this likely as population-based surveys of STIs that we conducted estimated a higher burden of STIs among adolescents and young people in Zambia and Zimbabwe. In Zambia, our survey among adolescents and young people aged 15–24 years showed a prevalence of curable STIs (NG/CT/TV) of 18.5%.34 Similarly, in Zimbabwe, prevalence of any curable STI (NG/CT/TV) among 18–24-year olds was 19.8%.35 Considering the difference in prevalence relative to this analysis and the timeframe (ie, surveys measured prevalence at a specific moment in time, while DHS reported over the last 12 months), it is unlikely that the prevalence found in these surveys represents a substantial increase compared with previous levels reported in the DHS. These findings highlight the need to move towards aetiologic testing, using cheap, easy-to-use point-of-care tests, to obtain a more precise understanding of STI prevalence and inform targeted public health interventions. In line with this, Zambia’s and Zimbabwe’s National AIDS Strategic Plans prioritise STI surveillance, research and resource allocation to improve STI programme implementation.27 29

Our findings show differences in self-reported STI prevalence by sex and country across survey rounds. In Zambia, the observed increase in self-reported STIs among ABM is consistent with data from the Health Management Information System, which shows a continued rise in STI prevalence among outpatient attendances from 2017 to 2021.14 However, this trend is not exclusive to ABM and does not cover the same time period.14 A better understanding is needed of whether this increase across survey rounds, found in our study, is attributed to an increase in treatment-seeking behaviour (ie, being diagnosed with an STI) or increased awareness of STI-related symptoms. Data from 2001 to 2016, including the Zambian Population-based HIV Impact Assessment and DHS, demonstrated a decline in active syphilis prevalence, suggesting that trends in STI prevalence may vary across different types of infections, requiring further investigation.15 Additionally, addressing AMR, particularly multidrug-resistant N. gonorrhoeae is critical, as it poses serious implications for reproductive, maternal and newborn health.36 To monitor AMR trends, the WHO has established the Enhanced Gonococcal Antimicrobial Susceptibility Programme, with Zimbabwe as a partner.37 38 Linking epidemiological STI data with AMR information not only enhances surveillance but also facilitates timely refinements of national and international NG treatment guidelines.

In Zimbabwe, we observed a decrease in self-reported STIs among AGW between survey rounds, which may reflect investments in sexual and reproductive health. Between 2010 and 2015, the government implemented an adolescent sexual and reproductive health strategy aimed at reducing risky sexual behaviour among young people and improving STI outcomes, among other goals.39 An impact study found that this initiative led to an increase in STI treatment; however, evidence regarding its impact on condom use and STI prevalence was inconclusive.40 Despite the decline in STI prevalence among AGW, this group had the highest STI prevalence overall, while the lowest was found among AGW in Zambia. The Zimbabwean findings align with AGW’s heightened vulnerability to STIs compared with ABM.41 However, the lower prevalence among AGW in Zambia raises questions about potential under-reporting, due to the asymptomatic nature of STIs in AGW, among other factors. These contrasting findings underscore the need to understand factors influencing STI detection and reporting and for targeted interventions that consider gender-specific vulnerabilities and country-specific contexts to effectively address the transmission of STIs.

Factors associated with self-reported STIs included younger age at first sex, living with HIV, younger age (20–29 years), lower education and wealth, and an increasing number of sexual partners in the last 12 months. These findings are consistent with previous studies among sexually active youth, men and women.9 12 35 41 Research has shown that younger age at first sex may increase the likelihood of condomless sex and multiple sexual partners, both of which contribute to increased likelihood of exposure to STIs.42,45 Similarly, due to commonalities in risk factors and transmission routes, the association found between individuals living with HIV and self-reported STIs is unsurprising.46 These results emphasise the need for targeted sexual health interventions that not only focus on early sexual education and condom use—including condom negotiation skills—but also address broader structural drivers such as poverty and education. Additionally, ensuring that individuals engaging in higher-risk sexual behaviours have equitable access to STI screening, testing and treatment will be essential.

A limitation of our analysis is the lack of diagnostic testing, which restricts our ability to provide an accurate estimation of STI prevalence and identify the specific pathogens responsible for the reported symptoms, as STI symptoms are often non-specific and may, particularly among AGW, be due to other factors (vulvovaginal candidiasis, bacterial vaginosis). Additionally, our analysis was based on DHS data up to 2018 only. More recent data from upcoming surveys could provide valuable insights into the current situation and evolving changes. Finally, the reliance on self-reporting in household surveys may introduce biases, including social desirability bias, and a potential lack of awareness of STI symptoms, which may have affected the accuracy of our findings. This analysis, using nationally representative data, contributes to the body of evidence on STIs in sub-Saharan Africa and highlights important differences in STI prevalence by sex, country and survey round.

Conclusions

Our analysis of self-reported STIs in Zambia and Zimbabwe highlights notable disparities among sexually active adolescents and adults; with a small increase in self-reported STIs among ABM in Zambia and a decrease across survey rounds among AGW in Zimbabwe. The observed associations between sexual behavioural and sociodemographic characteristics and self-reporting STIs emphasise the need for prevention and control strategies tailored to individual and contextual factors. Given the critical sexual and reproductive health consequences of STIs, and the high burden across sub-Saharan African countries, it is essential to prioritise STIs and their diagnosis and management. To address this, there is a need to enhance the implementation of diagnostic testing, awareness and access to treatment, particularly for high-risk populations, such as those with multiple sex partners, living with HIV, and an early sexual debut.
