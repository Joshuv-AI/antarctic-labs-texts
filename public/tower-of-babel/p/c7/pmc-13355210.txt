Tensor network-based gene regulatory network inference for single-cell transcriptomic data
Authors: Olatz Sanz Larrarte, Borja Aizpurua, Reza Dastbasteh, Ruben M Otxoa, Josu Etxezarreta Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDeciphering complex gene-gene interactions remains challenging in transcriptomics as traditional methods often miss higher-order and nonlinear dependencies. This study introduces a quantum-inspired framework leveraging tensor networks to optimally map expression data into a lower dimensional representation preserving biological locality. Using quantum mutual information (QMI), a nonparametric measure natural for tensor networks, we quantify gene dependencies and establish statistical significance via permutation testing. From those values, we construct optimal network where genes are positioned according to their quantum informational relationships that reflects the underlying biological circuitry. To validate the proposed method, we recover two distinct single-cell RNA sequencing datasets: first, a six-gene pathway from over 28, 000 lymphoblastoid cells; second, a 16-gene panel from 47 MCF10A breast epithelial cells. Furthermore, we unveil several triadic regulatory mechanisms. By merging quantum physics inspired techniques with computational biology, our method provides insights into gene regulation, with applications in disease mechanisms and precision medicine.Keywords: gene regulatory networks, inference, tensor networks, scRNA-seq

Introduction

Computational analysis, and more precisely computational biology, has revolutionized the ability to understand complex biological processes.1 This branch of science studies biological systems through data analysis, mathematical modeling, and computational simulations. From the beginning of the XXIst century, advances in high-performance technologies and digitization have led to an exponential growth in biological data.2

A key milestone was the human genome project (HGP), completed in 2003, which initiated mass genomics through the first reference human genome sequence.3 The emergence of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies in 2005 drastically reduced sequencing costs and time, exponentially increasing data generation.4

Simultaneously, the concept of “omics” emerged to describe the systematic study of sets of molecules, such as genes (genomics), messenger RNA transcripts (transcriptomics), proteins (proteomics), and metabolites (metabolomics). In transcriptomics, technologies such as RNA-seq make it possible to measure the amount and sequence of messenger RNA in an organism, tissue, or cell at a specific time.5 These tools not only extract meaningful information, but also enable the creation of predictive models to explore complex biological phenomena beyond traditional experimental capabilities. In this context, inference of functional relationships between genes has emerged as a key area in molecular biology.6

Beyond expression profiling, elucidating regulatory relationships requires identifying transcription factors (TFs)—specialized DNA-binding proteins that control gene expression by binding promoter/enhancer regions. While RNA-seq captures the downstream effects of regulation through mRNA abundance, it provides only indirect evidence of TF → target interactions via co-expression patterns. Direct validation is provided by ChIP-seq, which maps physical TF-DNA binding genome-wide using TF-specific antibodies, serving as the experimental gold standard for computational gene regulatory networks (GRNs) predictions.

Under this framework, inference of GRN has become an important area in molecular biology, providing a formal representation of gene-gene interactions using graph representations, G(V, E), where nodes, V, correspond to genes while edges, E, represent regulatory interactions. These networks are fundamental to understand molecular mechanisms, including gene functions and their regulation in cellular processes.7 However, GRN reconstruction remains challenging because experimental data (e.g., gene expression) only provides indirect evidence of regulatory interactions—such as correlations or perturbation responses—rather than direct observations of causality. Computational methods must therefore contend with these ambiguities, alongside technical noise, and biological complexity.8,9,10

In order to infer GRNs from gene expression data, different methods have been developed (see Figure 1). These methods use statistical models to detect dependencies between gene expression profiles and establish possible regulatory relationships between them. Common approaches include correlation networks,11 information-theoretic scores,12,13 regression-based models,14,15 Gaussian graphical models,16 Bayesian and Boolean networks7,17,18 or dynamic models.19,20 It should be noted that each method has its own assumptions and therefore specific limitations that restrict its applicability to more complex biological systems.21,22,23 For example, many traditional methodologies assume linear or simple relationships between variables, which is not always true, and more importantly, none of these conventional methods fully exploit the simultaneous and inter-regulatory connections between all genes. As a result, new ways for GRN inference are being proposed, including using quantum computers to do so.24

Gene regulatory network inference framework

Circular nodes represent genes; edges indicate regulatory interactions, either activation or inhibition.

Tensor networks (TNs) are compact mathematical representations designed to model high-dimensional systems with complex correlation structures.25,26 Originally, at the turn of the century, these were developed to address problems in quantum many-body systems27 and fluids,28 allowing efficient representation of complex quantum states in high-dimensional systems. Recently, these techniques have found applications in machine learning and data science,29,30 due to their striking ability to handle large and highly correlated data with reduced computational complexity. Mathematically, TNs take advantage of decompositions such as singular value decomposition (SVD) to capture correlations between system components, allowing less relevant dimensions to be efficiently truncated. For example, matrix product state (MPS) and tree tensor network (TTN) models are two common TN structures, with applications in quantum many-body systems.

Traditional GRN inference strategies rely primarily on pairwise statistical dependencies, which implicitly assume that regulatory effects are independent and additive. Such linear or bivariate treatments neglect emergent behavior arising from the collective regulation of multiple genes—a hallmark of complex biological systems. TNs provide a principled alternative rooted in the variational formulation of quantum many-body systems, where the state of the system is represented as a network of interconnected low-rank tensors. This multilinear formalism permits explicit modeling of higher-order dependencies while maintaining computational tractability through tensor factorization. Then, adopting TN representations allows us to bridge the gap between purely statistical correlation models and physically motivated models of information propagation, offering both interpretability and theoretical grounding for GRN reconstruction.

In this work, we propose a TN-based framework to infer GRNs from single-cell transcriptomic data, overcoming the limitations of pairwise interaction models by capturing higher-order dependencies among genes. Unlike traditional approaches restricted to binary correlations, our TN formulation naturally encodes multi-way regulatory relationships through its multilinear structure, preserving biological context while enabling efficient computation. By representing gene interactions as interconnected low-rank tensors, our method maintains the intrinsic high-dimensional structure of expression data, revealing complex regulatory motifs. The following sections discuss the theoretical foundations of this approach, detail the methodology, and present experimental results that highlight the ability of TNs to reveal intricate regulatory mechanisms that may be overlooked by classical techniques. As a result, our TN-based GRN inference method is a flexible tool that can capture gene regulation relationships in an accurate and computationally tractable manner.

To test the tensor network-based approach, we analyzed two single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets: GSE126321 and GSE158275 from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database,31 and the MCF10A molecular deep dive (MDD) level 3 dataset from SigComLINCS.32 MCF10A MDD comprises scRNA-seq profiles across 48 cells, accessible via https://maayanlab.cloud/sigcom-lincs/#/Download.

In the following, we describe the processing of each of the steps required to infer the GRN embedded in said databases and the obtained result. To asses the performance of our quantum-inspired method, we compared the inferred GRNs against several reference approaches: STRING database,33 a comprehensive resource of known and predicted protein-protein interactions, used as a ground truth reference for validated regulatory interactions; ARACNE,8 an information-theoretic algorithm based on mutual information (direct classical counterpart to our QMI-based approach), and qGRN,24 an existing variational quantum algorithm for GRN inference. Beyond pairwise comparisons, we leveraged our method to reveal certain triadic regulatory relationships observed in the literature, further showing the applicability of the tensor network-based method to recover complex correlations among genes.

Analysis of 6-gene regulatory network

NF-κB (nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells) is a family of transcription factors central to the regulation of immune responses, cell survival, and differentiation. In B cells-which are a type of white blood cell-, NF-κB dynamics—particularly the activity of its REL and RELA—play a pivotal role in the transition from proliferation to plasma cell differentiation. This transition is orchestrated through a tightly regulated gene regulatory circuit that involves key transcriptional regulators such as IRF4, PAX5, PRDM1, and AICDA. Understanding the interplay among these genes is essential to decipher the regulatory logic driving B cell fate decisions.

Gene expression states were binarized (inactive = 0, active = 1) using GMM, which automatically determined optimal activation thresholds for each gene while preserving biologically relevant expression patterns.

We evaluated the bimodal structure by comparing GMM via the Bayesian information criterion (BIC),34 criterion for model selection among a finite set of models. For models of one and two parameters, the difference between them for each gene resulted in: ΔBIC12 = 17512.0 for AICDA, (ΔBIC12 = 9479.8 for IRF4, ΔBIC12 = 10587.5, for PAX5, ΔBIC12 = 13051.9 for PRDM1, ΔBIC12 = 9033.9 for REL, and ΔBIC12 = 10315.1 for RELA. Further comparison between two- and three-component models revealed minimal improvement for more complex fits, e.g., ΔBIC23 = 469.0 for PAX5 and ΔBIC23 = 92.8 for RELA), supporting the two-component model as optimal. This aligns with the expected binary nature of gene activation while avoiding overfitting.

The binarized gene expression data were optimally ordered using a Hilbert space-filling curve.35 This metric quantifies how well the 1D ordering maintains the rank correlation structure of neighboring genes. This projection mapped the genes into the order PRDM1, PAX5, AICDA, RELA, REL, and IRF4. Crucially, the strong pairwise neighborhood preservation indicates that the Hilbert curve ordering respects the original high-dimensional structure of the transcriptomic data, enabling faithful representation as a quantum state vector |ψ⟩ for subsequent MPS decomposition and multi-gene interaction analysis.

As described before, we measure the QMI between the tensors of the constructed MPS in order to infer the structure of the GRN in question. The resulting QMI matrix revealed the interaction landscape of the six NF-κB genes, which we depict in Figure 3 (see exact values in Table S1).

To experimentally validate inferred regulatory interactions, we used ChIP-seq,36 a technique that identifies genome-wide transcription factor binding sites. This allows us to determine whether a transcription factor—encoded by a regulatory gene—binds near a target gene, providing evidence of potential regulatory control. For this purpose, we used ChIP-Atlas, a comprehensive public database that aggregates ChIP-seq experiments across multiple species, cell types, and factors. We considered a regulatory interaction as supported if the transcription factor showed significant binding within a 5 kb window around the target gene’s transcription start site, a distance commonly associated with proximal regulatory regions. For each predicted interaction, we quantified the strength of the ChIP-seq signal and classified it into three levels of experimental evidence based on the global distribution of binding signals across all cell lines, using the 25% and 75% quartiles as thresholds. When using STRING alone as ground truth, all three methods exhibited high precision but differed markedly in recall: ARACNE achieved the highest precision (0.90) at the expense of a low recall (0.29), consistent with its conservative, sparsity-biased design. In contrast, our TN approach and qGRN both maintained competitive precision (0.81) while recovering a larger fraction of STRING-supported interactions (recall 0.48 for TNs vs. 0.35 for qGRN), resulting in a higher F1-score for our method (0.60 vs. 0.49).

When we integrated independent ChIP-seq validation into the ground truth definition, the advantage of the TN-based model became even more pronounced. Under this stricter criterion, our method achieved high precision (0.93) and the best balance between sensitivity and specificity (recall 0.50, F1-score 0.65), outperforming qGRN (F1-score 0.52) and ARACNE (F1-score 0.46). These results indicate that tensor-network representations not only preserve biologically validated interactions but also recover additional ChIP-supported regulatory links that may be missed by purely pairwise or information-theoretic approaches, in line with recent observations that higher-order dependency models can improve GRN inference in complex transcriptional programs.

Multi-gene relationships

While our primary analysis focuses on pairwise QMI, gene regulatory systems often involve higher-order dependencies. Triadic interactions can reveal synergistic or redundant control not captured by pairwise metrics.

In line with the above, we have taken an additional step in characterizing more complex relationships by analyzing gene triads. This approach allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of functional interactions between genes that may not be fully apparent when analyzing pairs in isolation. In this context, in the six gene database, one of the most notable triads is that formed by PRDM1, PAX5, and IRF4, whose tripartite interaction was statistically significant (p = 0.0036). This result aligns with the established regulatory circuit in which graded IRF4 expression controls B cell fate: low IRF4 promotes AICDA and class-switch recombination, while high IRF4 induces PRDM1 (encoding Blimp-1), which represses PAX5 to silence the B cell identity program and drive plasma cell differentiation.42,43,44 IRF4 binds directly to the CNS+9 enhancer of PRDM1,45 and Blimp-1/PRDM1 is the master repressor of PAX5 during this fate switch.46 The significant triadic QMI therefore captures this hierarchical module—IRF4 → PRDM1 → PAX5 repression—as coordinated multigenic control.

By contrast, the AICDA-RELA-REL triad shows no statistical significance (p = 0.3881). This is biologically consistent with their roles in parallel NF-κB pathways rather than coordinated triadic control: AICDA mediates antibody diversification downstream of both RELA (canonical pathway) and REL (c-Rel, germinal center maintenance), but without direct three-way synergy.38 The lack of triadic significance supports a divergent signaling model where NF-κB subunits act independently on AICDA rather than through unified multigenic coordination, demonstrating QMI’s ability to distinguish parallel from cooperative regulation.

Discussion

In this article, we have introduced a TN-based quantum-inspired approach to determine GRNs. We have shown that the method is succesful in recovering GRNs consisted of six and sixteen pathway genes from sc-RNA sequencing data for different type of cells and databases length.

The benchmarking analysis confirms that our MPS-QMI framework occupies a unique niche among GRN inference methods, delivering superior F1-scores (0.65 with ChIP-seq validation) through its ability to capture both biologically validated pairwise interactions and higher-order regulatory motifs invisible to classical approaches. While ARACNE maximizes precision through conservative pruning, its low recall (0.30) misses coordinated relationships like PRDM1-RELA activation—interactions recovered by our method and independently validated. This performance advantage stems from tensor networks’ native multilinear structure, which analytically encodes multi-way dependencies rather than approximating them through pairwise decompositions or black-box learning.

Respect to quantum algorithms, our TN approach shows significant advantages over other techniques in the literature, such as variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) for GRN inference, particularly in the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era. First, the VQA based qGRN pipeline proposed in24 requires all-to-all qubit connectivity with circuit depth increasing as On2, with n being the number of genes. Such architectural constraints makes the approach challenging for most qubit platforms in which connectivity is usually restricted to nearest neighbors. Additionally, VQAs are highly susceptible to noise and limited coherence times (typically T1/T2 ∼ 100 μs47,48) in current quantum hardware, which can degrade performance.49,50 This is especially relevant for circuits with increasing depth complexity as the one in.24 In fact, recent studies show that even moderate noise levels can exponentially suppress gradients in VQAs, a phenomenon exacerbated in NISQ devices.50,51,52 In contrast, our classical tensor network implementation avoids these hardware limitations while retaining quantum-inspired efficiency.

Finally, VQAs are known to suffer from the so-called “barren plateau” problem, where gradients vanish exponentially with system size—even in the absence of noise-making optimization extremely challenging.53,54,55 This issue is particularly relevant for the approach proposed in,24 as their cost function is based on the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, which requires computing a probability distribution over an exponentially large configuration space at each optimization step. In contrast, the MPS-based method proposed here operates directly on the data-driven probability distribution, avoiding the need for such costly and unstable optimization. This results in a more scalable and reliable framework for GRN inference at mid-system sizes. Moreover, by relying on classical inference, our method allows for straightforward statistical significance testing, which would be highly non-trivial—and computationally expensive—in the quantum variational context.

Furthermore, we showed how tensor networks allow scalable computation of triadic QMI across large gene sets, capturing these higher-order motifs. In future work, we will incorporate partial information decomposition (PID)56 to separate unique, redundant, and synergistic components. This could provide deeper mechanistic insights, particularly when integrated with single-cell multi-omics data. More complicated gene regulatory mechanisms may also be unveiled by using these techniques.

Information-based methods

Numerous methods have been developed for GRN reconstruction from gene expression data. ARACNE8 estimates mutual information I(Xi; Xj) using kernel density estimation (KDE) on continuous expression data, followed by data processing inequality (DPI) pruning: for each triplet (i, j, k), the weakest edge Imin(i, j; k) is removed as indirect. This algorithm scales O(N3 + N2M2) which can be approximated as O(N2M2)8 when M is large enough. With this method, only pair-like relationships are analyzed since the minimal connections of the triplets are discarded.

PIDC57 extends MI with partial information decomposition, splitting I(X; Y) into unique, redundant, and synergistic components via triplet analysis. This provides richer biological interpretation but scales cubically O(N3)57 due to explicit examination of all possible triplets for each gene pair, limiting applicability to N < 500 genes in practice (for N = 100, approximately 1.7 ⋅ 105 triplets are analyzed; for N = 500, approximately 2.1 ⋅ 107, representing a 125-fold increase in computational cost).

In contrast, our MPS-QMI method offers three key advantages. First, it provides native higher-order detection through triadic quantum mutual information I(A: B: C), capturing coordinated motifs like PRDM1-PAX5-IRF4 (p = 0.011) invisible to pairwise methods. Second, the dominant computational cost of MPS construction scales polynomially as O(Nχ3), where empirically χ ≈ 20–100 for biological correlation structures, far superior to PIDC’s explicit O(N3). Third, it produces integrated geometric embedding via a quantum-information Hamiltonian where spatial proximity directly reflects regulatory strength weighted by permutation test significance.

The GMM-based binarization step, while sacrificing continuous resolution, enhances statistical robustness for downstream QMI analysis.58,59 The MPS bond dimension χ can be adaptively selected via singular value truncation. Our direct ChIP-seq validation demonstrates recovery of experimentally supported interactions missed by ARACNE, particularly those involving higher-order coordination (Tables 2 and 3).

Deep learning-based methods

Deep learning approaches have gained significant traction for GRN inference due to their powerful pattern recognition capabilities. Inside supervised deep learning, DeepSEM60 combines variational autoencoders with structural equation modeling on scRNA-seq embeddings, demanding M ≳ N2 samples for stable convergence, becoming very complicated in cases where M is low.

On the contrary, unsupervised deep learning methods such as SCENIC61 integrate co-expression with motif databases. However, the scaling results in O(N2M). GENIE362 (assuming T random forest regression per gene) remains competitive but operates pairwise without higher-order context with complexity O(NTM⁡log⁡M).

Our method analytically defined higher-order dependencies. Unlike deep networks where multi-way interactions emerge implicitly from learned weights, our tensor network representation explicitly quantifies dependencies of arbitrary order. Apart from that, our method constitutes a functional defined exclusively over the observed expression tensor, requiring no external priors for training. Transcription factor databases or motif collections serve only for validation, not model construction—a critical advantage for novel biological contexts where validated interactions are unavailable.

For moderate χ ≈ 20–100 and network with size N, the computational cost scales linearly in N and polynomially in χ (i.e., complexity O(Nχ3)). Attention-based models and neural networks (NNs) incur at least quadratic O(N2L) message-passing costs, even after sparsification.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge regimes where deep learning maintains theoretical superiority: (1) abundant labeled data (ChIP-seq, perturbation screens) enables end-to-end supervised optimization; (2) massive single-cell datasets support robust representation learning; and (3) highly non-local interactions across thousands of genes benefit from NNs long-range attention without 1D ordering assumptions. However, for bulk expression cohorts with limited samples and absent priors—the realistic setting for translational studies—our method provides explicit, interpretable, higher-order inference grounded in quantum information theory.

Limitations of the study

Regarding the computational complexity of the approach, MPS scales polynomially (O(Nχ3)) where N are the genes with bond dimension χ, making it computationally feasible for moderate-sized gene sets. However, in general, the bond dimension increases exponentially with the system size and, thus, exact MPS representation becomes difficult for large networks and the expressive power of classical MPS implementations depends critically on χ, which controls the amount of entanglement.26 When the required bond dimension grows large, the computational advantages of MPS diminish and exact representations become difficult for large networks.

To mitigate this, adaptive truncation strategies can be employed to retain only the most significant singular values, balancing accuracy and efficiency.26 In regimes where entanglement is genuinely high, more expressive tensor network architectures may be needed. Projected entangled-pair states (PEPSs) generalize MPS to higher-dimensional connectivity and can capture more complex correlation patterns, whereas multi-scale entanglement renormalization ansatz (MERA) TNs provide a hierarchical structure well suited for scale-invariant or critical systems.27,63 Both, however, come at the cost of increased contraction complexity.

Finally, our formulation remains forward-compatible with future quantum hardware. Techniques such as tensor-to-qubit encodings via disentanglers64 could enable hybrid quantum–classical implementations in which tensor contractions are offloaded to quantum devices, potentially eliminating truncation-induced errors and relaxing bond-dimension constraints as quantum technologies mature.

Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) are probabilistic models that are particularly effective for capturing heterogeneous or multimodal distributions, such as those often observed in gene expression data.68 In our context, they are used to model the distribution of expression levels for each gene across multiple patients, under the assumption that the expression follows a bimodal pattern: one mode corresponding to low (inactive) expression and the other to high (active).

Hilbert projection of gene expression

To reduce the dimensionality for the TN analysis, we first apply Principal Component Analysis (PCAs) to the gene expression matrix X∈RN×m or its binarized form Z ∈ {0,1}N×m. Projecting onto the space formed by the two principal components yields 2D coordinates (xj, yj) for each gene j. To preserve spatial locality in one dimension, we map these coordinates using the Hilbert space-filling curve H: (x, y) → h. Although not bijective, the Hilbert curve effectively preserves the neighborhood structure better than other mappings,69 making it ideal for structured representations. This is especially valuable in tensor network models, where locality improves computational efficiency.67 The resulting 1D representation hj maintains key spatial relationships and it is well-suited for downstream analysis. The Hilbert curve projection is done using our TN-GRN inference software.

In the MPS framework, the reduced density matrices for QMI are computed by tracing out parts of the system. This is achieved through tensor network contractions to extract the marginal probability distributions, which correspond to the single-site reduced density matrix ρl, and the joint probability distributions from the two-site reduced density matrix ρl1l2. Having quantified the statistical dependencies between genes via QMI and validated their significance through permutation testing (pleft(i,j), pright(i,j)) from Equation 13, we define a inference weights wij = QMIij(1 − pij), where pij=min(pleft(i,j),pright(i,j)). While these weights define a complete graph of regulatory interactions, visualizing big N genes becomes challenging due to edge cluttering and loss of interpretability.

To achieve this, we introduce a force-directed layout inspired by classical algorithms such as ForceAtlas275 and LinLog energy models.76 In these methods, nodes are treated as physical particles subject to attractive and repulsive forces, and a stable configuration is found by minimizing and energy function. Quantification and statistical analysis

To assess the statistical significance of the observed QMI values that quantify gene-gene dependencies, we applied a permutation test. This is performed by randomly permuting the gene labels across samples a number of times, thereby generating a null distribution of QMI values under the hypothesis of no structured dependency between the gene pair.

Unlike a standard two-tailed test, which evaluates extremeness in both directions, we compute both right-tailed and left-tailed p-values separately. This distinction is particularly informative in the context of mutual information-based measures such as QMI, which are inherently non-negative and often asymmetrically distributed. The +1 correction prevents zero-valued p-values and ensures stability with low Nperm. We employed Nperm = 1000 and Nperm = 300 for the 6 and 16 GRNs, respectively, to obtain the results.

We report right- and left-tailed p-values separately, rather than combining them into a single two-tailed measure. This approach is motivated by the distinct biological interpretations of each tail. A significant right-tailed value indicates a gene pair with stronger than expected dependency.78 Conversely, a significant left-tailed value, indicates a pair with significantly weaker dependency than expected by chance. This often reflects active biological mechanisms enforcing independence—such as mutual repression, compensatory79 regulation, or spatial80 segregation.
