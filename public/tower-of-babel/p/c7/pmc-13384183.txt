Study of TOfacitinib for the treatment of chronic PouchITis (STOPit): a multi-centre open label induction with randomised, double blind, placebo-controlled maintenance trial
Authors: Emi Khoo, Gerald Holtmann, Jakob Begun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStudy of TOfacitinib for the treatment of chronic PouchITis (STOPit) is a national, investigator-led, multicentre, open-label induction with randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled maintenance trial to determine the effectiveness of tofacitinib to induce and maintain clinical remission in chronic pouchitis patients. The primary objective of STOPit is to evaluate the clinical response to tofacitinib, compared with placebo, using the validated modified Pouchitis Disease Activity Index score. Secondary objectives include clinical response and remission rates, endoscopic response and mucosal healing rates in induction and maintenance phases; rates of flare and recapturing response in open label extension phase; the effect of tofacitinib on clinical biomarkers and quality of life; and safety of tofacitinib use for treatment of chronic pouchitis. Study period is up to ten months, with five visits and three pouchoscopies.Methods and analysisAdults with ulcerative colitis who have undergone total proctocolectomy and ileal pouch-anal anastomosis and have chronic pouchitis are eligible. Participants receive open-label 10 mg two times per day tofacitinib for 8 weeks. Clinical responders at the end of week 8 will be randomised to either tofacitinib or placebo in the maintenance phase. Non-responders will receive extended induction dosing for a further 8 weeks. Persistent non-responders exit the study and responders will proceed to maintenance randomisation. Randomised individuals will be followed for 24 weeks. Patients experiencing a flare of symptoms during the maintenance phase will be eligible to receive open-label tofacitinib. Efficacy and safety data will be evaluated at 12 weeks after termination from the study. Target sample size is 72 patients to be randomised into maintenance to achieve sufficient power. Data analysis is planned to commence in October 2026.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval was granted (Project ID 66809) and the trial was notified (CTN: CT-2020-CTN-04866-1; ANZCTR: 381128) prior to commencement. The study will be conducted in accordance with National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) guidelines, and findings will be published in a peer-reviewed journal.Trial registration numberCTN: CT-2020-CTN-04866-1.

Introduction

Chronic pouchitis is a common sequela of a total proctocolectomy and ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (IPAA), a standard surgical intervention for refractory ulcerative colitis (UC) and colitis-associated neoplasia or dysplasia.1 Approximately 10–25% of UC patients ultimately undergo IPAA.13 Despite the curative intent of resection, up to 80% of IPAA patients develop pouchitis, a post-operative inflammatory condition.46 Antibiotic therapy, such as ciprofloxacin and metronidazole, remains the first-line treatment for acute pouchitis, consistent with a microbial contribution to the condition.5 However, about 20% of patients progress to chronic pouchitis, including chronic antibiotic-refractory pouchitis (CARP) who have ongoing symptoms despite antibiotic therapy, and chronic antibiotic-dependent pouchitis (CADP) who require at least one 4-week course of antibiotics during a 12-month period.7 8

Prophylactic IPAA surgery is also indicated for patients with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) to prevent colorectal cancer. While pouchitis is uncommon in FAP patients, it affects nearly 80% of UC patients post-IPAA, suggesting a significant role for genetic and immunological factors in its pathogenesis. Histopathological examination of inflamed pouches reveals similarities to UC, characterised by neutrophilic infiltration, intramucosal abscess formation and ulceration.9 10 Gene expression profiling further supports a shared inflammatory pathway between UC and pouchitis.11 Notably, tissue remodelling towards a colonic phenotype occurs in chronic pouchitis but not in healthy or FAP pouches, implicating immune-mediated tissue alterations in pouchitis development.12

Chronic pouchitis is characterised by urgency, incontinence, nocturnal symptoms and frequent loose stools. These symptoms significantly impact patients’ social lives.5 A substantial proportion of pouch patients experience faecal soiling or leakage, along with perianal discomfort.7 Patients with pouch failure exhibit marked deterioration in health-related quality of life, encompassing social, occupational, mental and physical domains.7 Patients describe this condition as a ‘social handicap’, with detrimental effects on employment, social interactions and overall well-being. The significant disease burden profoundly affects not only patients but also their families, which is often unrecognised or underestimated.

Limited evidence exists regarding effective second-line therapies for chronic pouchitis, including CARP and CADP subtypes.13 Vedolizumab demonstrated efficacy in inducing remission in antibiotic-refractory chronic pouchitis patients within the EARNEST trial, which was a well-powered randomised controlled trial of vedolizumab use in chronic pouchitis.14 While a retrospective study suggested ustekinumab as a potential treatment, the evidence base is limited due to small sample size (n=9) of CARP patients.15 Anti-tumour necrosis factor agents like infliximab and adalimumab have been investigated, but comparative studies with vedolizumab revealed a higher rate of adverse events, primarily related to immunogenicity.16 A randomised controlled trial evaluating adalimumab vs placebo failed to demonstrate efficacy.17 In summary, current medical management options for chronic pouchitis remain limited.

Tofacitinib, a small molecule inhibitor targeting JAK1 and JAK3, modulates multiple immune pathways implicated in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) pathogenesis. Tofacitinib demonstrated rapid symptom improvement and mucosal healing in moderate to severe UC in the Phase 3 OCTAVE trials compared with placebo.18 Given the shared immunological features between UC and pouchitis, and tofacitinib’s efficacy in UC, we propose its potential benefit in treating chronic pouchitis. This hypothesis is supported by a recent case report demonstrating successful tofacitinib treatment for refractory chronic pouchitis, as evidenced by clinical and endoscopic outcomes.19

To date, there is a paucity of robust research on effective treatments for chronic pouchitis. A well-designed, randomised controlled trial with standardised outcome measures is needed to definitively establish therapeutic efficacy. The Study of TOfacitinib for the treatment of chronic PouchITis (STOPit) trial will address this critical gap and provide evidence for regulatory approval. This study will also explore the interplay between the pouch microbiome and immune system, which has been understudied in previous research.20 21 Understanding these interactions is crucial for identifying biomarkers of treatment response and developing novel therapeutic strategies.

The STOPit trial aims (1) to evaluate the effectiveness of tofacitinib for the treatment of chronic pouchitis, (2) to define the predictors of response to tofacitinib for treatment of chronic pouchitis, (3) to assess the impact of tofacitinib on quality of life among individuals with chronic pouchitis, (4) to explore the inter-relationships among host responses and microbiota function and (5) to determine the safety of tofacitinib for treatment of chronic pouchitis.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This study is designed as an Australia-wide open-label induction phase followed by a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled maintenance trial at 13 IBD centres within the Australia New Zealand Inflammatory Bowel Disease Consortium (CTN: CT-2020-CTN-04866-1; ANZCTR—381128) (Protocol V.5.0 dated 1 Nov 2024).

The study comprises two phases: an open-label 8-week induction phase, with an optional 8-week extension for non-responders, followed by a 24-week maintenance phase (figure 1). During the induction phase, all participants will receive tofacitinib 10 mg two times per day (BD) for 8 weeks. At the end of induction, non-responders will proceed to extended induction, receiving a further 8 weeks of tofacitinib 10 mg BD. These induction regimens are identical to those in the registration trials for UC.18 Responders to standard or extended induction will proceed to randomisation (1:1 allocation), to either tofacitinib 5 mg BD or placebo BD (identical appearing oral tablet) during the maintenance phase. Remote double-blind randomisation will be performed by a central pharmacist using the randomisation sequence provided by a statistician, using R Statistical package V.4.3.1 or higher. All personnel involved are blinded to the assigned intervention including participants and treating physicians. Any participants who experience recurrent symptoms of pouchitis during the maintenance phase will be considered a treatment failure and may proceed to an open-label extension study, receiving either tofacitinib 5 mg BD or 10 mg BD, at the treating physician’s discretion. There are a total of 5–6 study visits, where clinical assessment, blood tests, stool tests and questionnaires are performed (table 1). Three mandatory pouchoscopies are involved during the study period. Patients will also be reviewed 12 weeks after entering the open-label extension phase or early termination.

The primary outcome measure of this study is to determine the effectiveness of tofacitinib in treating chronic pouchitis. Specifically, we will assess the proportion of patients with this condition who sustain a clinical response at the end of the maintenance phase of the study. A clinical response is defined as a reduction in the clinical Pouchitis Disease Activity Index (PDAI) of 2 or more points. This will be compared with the proportion of patients receiving a placebo who also meet this response criterion. The PDAI is a well-established tool used to evaluate the severity of pouchitis symptoms, encompassing factors such as stool frequency, abdominal pain, incontinence and endoscopic findings.

Finally, we will conduct exploratory analyses to delve deeper into the potential mechanisms of action of tofacitinib and to identify factors that might predict how well individual patients respond to the treatment. These analyses will investigate the impact of tofacitinib on the gut microbiota (the community of microorganisms in the intestine), gene expression patterns in the gut tissue, and aim to identify predictors of treatment response using various clinical and biological markers collected throughout the study. These exploratory analyses may provide valuable insights for future research and the personalised treatment of chronic pouchitis.

Study population

Up to 120 adult patients aged 18–65 years will be recruited for this study. Participants must have a previous diagnosis of UC and have undergone a total proctocolectomy and IPAA. They must also have chronic or recurrent pouchitis requiring more than 4 weeks of antibiotic treatment in the past year (CADP) or pouchitis resistant to antibiotic therapy (CARP). Additionally, patients must have a total modified PDAI score greater than five and endoscopic evidence of active disease. Participants must have completed pre-screening tests for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, varicella zoster virus, tuberculosis, mumps, measles and rubella.

Consistent with the exclusion criteria from the OCTAVE phase three trial, patients with elevated cardiovascular risk factors, bradycardia, chronic renal or liver disease, active infections, history of organ transplantation, immunosuppressive medication use, lymphocyte-depleting therapies, thromboembolism, hypercoagulable conditions, active or past malignancy, lymphoproliferative disorders or pregnancy or lactation are excluded from the study.

Prohibited medications must be discontinued prior to the study baseline to ensure accurate assessment of treatment efficacy. Antibiotics used to treat pouchitis should be stopped at least 3 weeks prior to baseline. Immunomodulators and biologics require longer washout periods of 4 and 8 weeks, respectively. For interleukin antagonists, a 12-week washout period is necessary. These restrictions are in place to minimise the influence of previous medications on study outcomes.

Specific concomitant medications are permitted for the study. Oral 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) compounds and oral corticosteroids are allowed if on a stable dose for at least 2 weeks or recently discontinued at least 2 weeks prior to baseline. Anti-diarrhoeal medication or probiotics for pouchitis are permitted if on a stable dose for at least 2 weeks, or discontinued at least 2 weeks prior to baseline, and these can be discontinued after the standard induction phase

All responding patients who were taking corticosteroids at Week 0 must begin tapering corticosteroids on entrance to the maintenance phase at Week 8. This tapering is mandatory, unless not medically feasible, and should follow the recommended schedule in table 2. If participants experience worsening of their disease activity while tapering corticosteroids, further dose decreases may be suspended, and/or their oral corticosteroid dose may be temporarily increased if deemed necessary by the investigator, but the subject will be considered to have been a treatment failure for statistical analysis.

Rescue medications are permitted for clinical flares during the induction phase. These include oral corticosteroids at a dose equivalent to no more than 25 mg/day of prednisolone or 6 mg/day of budesonide, and antibiotics such as fluoroquinolones, nitroimidazoles and penicillin. Rescue medication use is limited to 2 weeks. Participants who respond to rescue therapy can continue in the study and proceed to the maintenance phase. However, any use of rescue medication during the maintenance phase will necessitate entry into the open-label extension.

Study procedures

Recruitment and consent of participants

Participants will be prospectively identified from IBD outpatient clinics and be provided a participant information and consent form (PICF) (see online supplemental material 1). All participation is voluntary, and it will be made clear that the patient’s decision to participate, not participate or withdraw from participation will not impact on their medical care. Potential participants will be given time to read the PICF provided and to ask questions. Informed consent will be obtained by an investigator from participants after explaining the study, the study visits and procedures involved, and answering all questions. A written informed consent will be obtained, and a copy will be given to the participant for their records.

Withdrawal of participants from study

Participants may choose to withdraw from the study at any time without prejudice to further treatment. Investigators can withdraw a participant at any time if they believe it is in the participant’s best interest to do so. Once a participant has withdrawn from the study no further information will be collected from the participant. However, information that has already been collected will be retained to ensure that the results of the study are analysed accordingly.

Unblinding process

Should knowledge of participant identity become necessary for safety or regulatory reasons, confidentiality of participants will be maintained. Participants can be unblinded in emergency situations. The unblinding process can be performed by a designated physician at the central site who is not involved in the study. Details of any emergency unblinding shall be documented fully in the investigator site file. This includes date, subject details, reason for unblinding, the results, name and role of the individual requesting the unblinding, and name and role of the individual carrying out the unblinding. The information shall be transmitted to the requesting party. All care should be taken to ensure that the rest of the study team are kept blinded. The details shall be included in the statistical report.

Trial management committee

A trial management committee (TMC) including an external member (who is not involved in the trial) and those individuals responsible for the day-to-day running of the trial, including the coordinating principal investigator, trial coordinator, research nurse and data manager. The TMC oversees individual safety reports, aggregate event rates, data relating to the quality, protocol adherence and patient retention rates. TMC meetings will occur with the enrolment of every 20 patients or ad hoc if urgent review is needed. An interim analysis will be conducted by TMC once 50% of recruitment to the maintenance phase has been achieved to determine if the estimated induction response rate, and hence the maintenance study recruitment rate, is sufficient. This structure keeps the decision process free of conflict of interest while considering cost, resources and the meaningfulness of the project.

Steps to be taken to ensure the accuracy and reliability of data include the selection of qualified investigators and appropriate study sites, review of all protocol procedures with the investigators and study-site personnel before the study and periodic monitoring in accordance with the current study manual of procedures, good clinical practice, relevant regulations and standard operating procedures. An annual investigator meeting will be held during which all study sites will be informed of the trial progress, and any issues will be addressed.

A manual of procedures will be provided and reviewed with study-site personnel before the start of the study. The manual will include a schedule of procedures, guidelines for case report form completion, lab manual procedures (collection, storage and transport) and requirements for the study-site research team.

Sample size and analysis

This is an open-label induction study of tofacitinib in chronic pouchitis with a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled maintenance trial for responders. The proportions used in the sample size calculation were based on the effect size observed in previous studies of tofacitinib in UC, where a sustained clinical response with tofacitinib treatment was achieved in 50% of patients and a 19% response in the placebo group with an initial response rate of 60%. Using a two-sided Z test with pooled variance to test for proportions between two independent groups, a sample size of 36 patients per group (n=72) in the maintenance phase will be sufficient to detect a difference in response of 31% between groups with a power of 80% with an alpha of 5%. Sample sizes were calculated in R using the pwr package and power.prop.test function. Hence, we aim to recruit up to 120 subjects into the open-label induction study in order to recruit 72 patients into the maintenance phase.

Data will be collected using REDCap and statistical software R and R studio will be used to perform data cleaning and statistical analysis. Data cleaning for measurements from validated metrics will be conducted as recommended by its manual. In cases where this is not possible, data cleaning rules will be developed and implemented based on team consensus. For blood work data, where there are censored endpoints, floor endpoints will be replaced with half of the value, while ceiling censored endpoints will be replaced with the ceiling value. Safety analyses will be based on all participants who underwent any induction. Due to limited sample size, where required, purposive variable selection will be employed judiciously to select a small number of variables for statistical adjustment. The analysis of baseline characteristics by randomisation status for the maintenance phase will be used to inform purposive variable selection. Model may be adjusted for cPDAI at end of induction if required.

The primary outcome will be assessed with a two-tailed test and P values of<0.05 deemed statistically significant. The main analysis will be performed following an intention-to-treat approach, defined as: Intention-to-treat population: All participants that satisfy the inclusion criteria are analysed as belonging to the treatment arms to which they were randomised, regardless of whether they received or adhered to the allocation intervention for the full duration of the trial. The Intention-to-treat data set will be used for analysis of the primary and secondary outcomes for the final data analyses.

Missing data during trial is expected to be minimal however participant may withdraw from study due to adverse events experienced or change of mind, or may not attend the final follow-up which is 12 weeks after exit from study. Randomised patients who commenced treatment but do not complete the maintenance phase will be considered treatment failures. Thus, there will be no missing values for the primary outcome. For other outcomes, the proportion of missing values will be presented.

Summaries of continuous variables which are normally distributed will be presented as means and SD, or as medians and inter-quartile ranges for skewed data. Categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages. χ2 test to test for differences in responder status by treatment group (placebo vs 5 mg tofacitinib) or Fisher’s exact test if >20% of expected values are less than 5.

Ethics and dissemination

This study will be conducted in accordance with the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) Guidelines on Human Experimentation based on the Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice and under the provisions of the guidelines of the current version of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval has been obtained from the human research ethics committee at Metro North Health (Project ID 66809). The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) has been notified of the clinical trial (Clinical trial notification (CTN: CT-2020-CTN-04866-1)) and registered online in the Australia New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ANZCTR) before commencement (ANZCTR—381128).

While we anticipate no major health issues for participants in this study, previous trials with tofacitinib revealed common adverse events including flu-like symptoms, headache, nausea and diarrhoea. We will actively monitor participants by asking them to report any significant symptoms during study visits and by closely monitoring their health. As a safety measure, we will also regularly check their full blood count, as well as liver and kidney function for any abnormalities. Fasting serum lipid levels will be monitored as advised by the manufacturer. In the rare event of serious side effects such as cardiovascular issues, reactivation of dormant infections, blood clots or cancer, participants will be withdrawn from the study and receive appropriate medical attention.

The monitoring and reporting of safety events in this study will be conducted in strict adherence to the ‘NHMRC Guidance: Safety monitoring and reporting in clinical trials involving therapeutic goods’, published by the National Health and Medical Research Council in November 2016. This guideline provides a comprehensive framework for ensuring the safety and well-being of participants in clinical trials involving therapeutic goods in Australia, outlining the procedures and responsibilities for identifying, documenting and reporting any adverse events or safety concerns that may arise during the study.

On completion, the study results, including any unforeseen early termination, will be made public on the ANZCTR database. If the study ends prematurely, these results will be posted after data analysis is finalised. The main, secondary and exploratory findings of this research will be published in peer-reviewed scientific journals and presented at academic conferences. All individuals listed as authors on resulting publications or presentations will have significantly contributed to the study’s design, data acquisition, data analysis and/or the drafting of the manuscript. We will not proactively reach out to participants with the findings. Instead, we encourage them to contact us if they wish to receive updates and information about the research outcomes. Alternatively, they can visit the Mater Research Institute website for a study summary (https://www.materresearch.org.au).
