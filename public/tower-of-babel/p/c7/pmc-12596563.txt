Evolution of diversity and inclusion excellence within an academic pathology professional organization: historical perspective, progress, and a model for others
Authors: Lydia P Howell, Mary F Lipscomb, Mel Limson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA diverse and inclusive environment is crucial to recruitment, retention, and success of academic departments, especially in pathology which is experiencing a declining workforce in the United States. The professional association for academic pathology departments, the Association for Academic Pathology (formerly the Association for Pathology Chairs), has evolved from its inception in 1967 as a department chair-centered organization to an inclusive organization that develops and serves all members of an academic pathology department, including non-chair faculty and staff. In 2012, diversity, equity, and inclusion became an intentional programmatic focus. In 2024, AAPath adopted a diversity, equity, and inclusion statement. This manuscript describes the organization’s reflection on its history, commitment and progress toward diversity, equity, and inclusion in order to learn, improve, and serve as an example to others. The authors conducted a Google search to identify political and social contextual events. Fourteen chairs were interviewed regarding the Association of Academic Pathology’s diversity, equity, and inclusion history and members’ experience. Findings were evaluated within established stages of diversity, equity, and inclusion maturity. The Association of Academic Pathology’s diversity, equity, and inclusion progress followed national awareness and progress toward a more equitable and inclusive society. Progress was slow in the Association of Academic Pathology’s first 50 years reflecting low numbers of women and racial and ethnic minorities in medicine. In the last 15 years with the establishment of the Leadership Development and Diversity Committee and intentional diversity, equity, and inclusion -related programming, diversity, equity, and inclusion has become integrated across the organization. The Association of Academic Pathology has made progress toward a mature and sustainable organizational culture of diversity, equity and inclusion and serves as a roadmap for other academic medical organizations.Keywords: Academic leadership; Diversity; Equity, Inclusion; Faculty development

Introduction

A growing shortage of pathologists has created substantial recruitment and retention challenges due to a steady decline of medical graduates choosing pathology as their specialty and an aging pathologist population choosing retirement.1 Attracting and retaining the best and the brightest medical students into pathology careers and into academic practice is, therefore, a critical need. Since diverse teams and leaders increase productivity and creativity,2 they are more effective in their delivery of healthcare and education and are more likely to pursue research pertinent to diverse populations,2, 3, 4, 5 effective programs that enhance diversity and inclusive excellence are critical to address the pathologist workforce gap and achieve high performance in the missions of academic pathology. Political activities have recently challenged diversity initiatives at universities in many states and at the national level.6, 7, 8, 9 In academic medicine, professional organizations that support department chairs and their leadership team have a unique and strategic opportunity to fill this need. Unlike their members’ parent universities, these organizations do not have the challenges and constraints of a reporting relationship with state legislatures or university boards of trustees.

Supporting academic pathology department chairs in their leadership challenges prompted the founding of the Association of Academic Pathology (AAPath) in 1967, then named the American Association of Pathology Chairmen of Medical School Departments. Since that time, AAPath has expanded services beyond chairs to include other department leaders and faculty members, and has facilitated peer education, benchmarking, mentorship, sponsorship, and partnerships. In 2012, AAPath broadened its programming to include the development of future academic pathology leaders when it established its Leadership Development and Diversity Committee (LDDC), which implemented several successful courses and inspired organization-wide activities. In 2024, to highlight its inclusive membership and programming, the organization formally changed its name from the Association of Pathology Chairs (so named in 1994) to the Association for Academic Pathology and developed a diversity statement, a living document that reflects its current culture.

To our knowledge, there have been no published reports describing the evolution of diverse membership and activities within an academic medical professional organization. With AAPath’s new name, inclusive membership, and the national interest in reexamining diversity programs, it is timely to reflect on the organization’s history and efforts advancing diverse leadership in academic pathology and examine these within the context of the societal events which formed the background for this journey. As part of this reflection, we describe the emergence of diverse leaders within AAPath, the establishment of the LDDC and its activities as well as those of other AAPath units, and provide AAPath’s recently developed statement articulating the organization’s commitment to diversity. To add the lived human perspective to the organizational facts, this reflection includes perspectives of AAPath members who have been active in different eras of the organization who share their personal experiences. Finally, we discuss AAPath’s evolution toward maturity along the five established stages for diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI)10 in order to provide a framework for others.

Materials and methods

We searched the Internet via Google to identify historical events related to DEI in the United States from 1967 to the present. For AAPath activities, we reviewed documents from the AAPath executive offices, including a history of the organization available on its website.11

A qualitative analysis using semi-structured interviews was conducted to provide lived personal experience and enrich the historical narrative. This qualitative method is common in health services research and a similar method was employed for this study. “Thematic saturation”, i.e. the point at which interviews do not reveal new information, was used to determine adequacy of the interview pool as is standard for the semi-structured interview method.12 To solicit a broad and representative perspective, 14 former chairs who served in different eras and who had diverse backgrounds were identified for interviews. All were members of AAPath as chairs, and all but one served on its executive council (referred to subsequently as the Council). Four of the 14 had also served as medical school deans and one as an associate dean. The 2 interviewers are also former chairs and co-authors and their responses to the questions are included as 2 of the 14 interviewees. One cohort of interviewees included eight who served as chairs from 1979 through the early 1990s. Four of these eight are white men, including one international medical graduate, and four are women, including three white and one African American woman. The remaining 6 of the 14 total interviewees represent a second cohort who were appointed chairs in the 2000s; five were appointed after 2010. Five of these six continue as active chairs in 2024; one retired one year prior to the interview. Three are white women, one is an African American woman and two are Asian American men, both of the latter are international medical graduates. The interviews were conducted by telephone, video conferencing, and/or e-mail using a standard set of questions about diversity of peers, discrimination observed or experienced personally, and recollections about efforts to advance DEI in AAPath and within their home institutions during their careers.

Results

Historical context and DEI-related milestones

An online search tracked DEI-related national events from the 1960s when AAPath became an organization and in subsequent decades. These were compared by years with AAPath’s events (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3). Based on the historic events identified nationally and within AAPath, we identified three eras to serve as the context for the review of AAPath’s diversity experiences and effort: two 25-year periods and a more recent 15-year period as follows:

•1960–1984 (Table 1): Characterized by the rise of the civil rights movement, affirmative action, and feminism, this time period includes a passage of the Civil Rights Act of 1964,13 which forbids discrimination on the basis of sex and race in hiring and promotion. AAPath establishes its identity with its founding in 1967 as the American Association of Pathology Chairmen of Medical School Departments. Few women or minorities served as pathology department chairs, despite an increased number of women medical students, house staff, and faculty by 1977.14 The first woman chair of an academic pathology department, Nancy Warner MD (University of Southern California), was appointed in 1972 at the University of Southern California, and the first African-American woman chair, Vivian Pinn MD, was appointed in 1982 at Howard University.

•2010–present (Table 3): For the majority of this time period, there was a growing national focus on systemic issues in racism.17 The term “DEI” emerged as the preferred organizational framework and name in lieu of affirmative action. Later in the time period, DEI programs were called into question nationally and many rescinded following President Trump’s Executive orders.18,19 A variety of new programs were developed within AAPath early in this time period, including the Leadership Development and Diversity Committee (LDDC) in 2012. Initially an ad hoc committee established by the University of Colorado chair Ann Thor who served as the chair, the LDDC later became a standing committee in 2014. An LDDC-sponsored lecture at the AAPath annual meeting was also added during this time period. Named in the memory of Dr. Patricia Thomas who was committed to increasing the involvement of women and underrepresented minorities (URMs), this annual lecture continues Dr. Thomas’ legacy through thought-provoking topics and discussion led by nationally recognized speakers (Table 4). The LDDC also developed several leadership programs (Table 5). One of the first programs was the Diversity and Inclusion Leadership Program (DILP), a two-year mentored distance learning program which included mentorship by participants’ department chairs and monthly sessions on leadership topics held by conference call. The program was replaced in 2016 by the larger three-day Pathology Leadership Academy (PLA). The year-long Academic Pathology Leadership Fellows (APLF) program was introduced in 2022. This popular program with mentorship has admitted 25–27 fellows per year and completely replaced the PLA in 2025.

This period of spontaneous growth of leadership programs coincided with accelerated gender diversity in leadership of the AAPath. AAPath’s first woman president (Mary Lipscomb, University of New Mexico) was elected 30 years after the appointment of the first woman pathology department chair nationally, and it was 10 more years before the election of the second woman AAPath president, Ann Thor (University of Colorado). 2018–2024 marked an unprecedented period with three consecutive women Association of Pathology Chairs presidents: Barbara Ducatman (Oakland University, William Beaumont School of Medicine); Lydia Howell (University of California, Davis); and Dani Zander (University of Cincinnati). In 2024, the first Asian American and international medical graduate, Chen Liu (Yale School of Medicine), became president-elect and then served as president in 2025.

In recent years, diversity and leadership development activities have become more integrated across AAPath’s committees and programs. A Diversity Roundtable was created in 2025 to coordinate these activities. AAPath members have analyzed historical data and critically examined its experience to better guide program improvement and development, much of which has been published.20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25 In 2024, the organization created a formal DEI statement26 committing to a more diverse, equitable, and inclusive organization that will support its member departments in their DEI journey. This statement will be regularly revisited for updates and modifications to ensure it addresses the changing definitions of diversity and contemporary issues and needs:

The Association for Academic Pathology is committed to a diverse, equitable, and inclusive academic pathology community that supports and enhances health, education and training, and the discovery and dissemination of new knowledge.

Our goal is to support our members in achieving their maximum potential and enhancing the discipline of pathology.

Interviews

Fourteen planned interviews were conducted, which revealed consistent themes and content. Given the thematic saturation, no additional individuals were interviewed beyond the planned 14 individuals. The following common themes were identified from the interviews.

•Gender, racial, and ethnic diversity in medical schools, pathology residency programs, and pathology departments. The first cohort who became chairs before 1995 began their medical school education in the 1950s to the early 1970s when civil rights were a major issue in the US. The former chairs educated in the United States all noted few or no women or racially or ethnically diverse individuals in their medical school class’s residency programs, or as early career colleagues. By the late 1980s, they noted a gradual increase in the number of women in faculty appointments with little increase in minorities. Most indicated that they were unaware of efforts to increase the numbers of women and minorities in their pathology departments or institution wide. The later cohort of chairs were appointed 2010–present, all but one graduated from medical school in the early to mid-1980s, and all had experiences at multiple medical schools and academic health centers over the course of their careers. All reported little awareness of institutional or department DEI efforts during their training or early faculty careers. Those with education, training or faculty experiences at public universities were more aware of DEI efforts and reported classes that were more diverse as compared to those chairs who had been at private institutions. Several individuals acknowledged academic pathology leaders who served as important DEI role models. The two international medical graduates reported the most awareness of DEI during medical school and noted dedicated efforts to include ethnic minorities and women as medical students in their countries of origin. These chairs considered this experience useful in their own DEI efforts later as department chairs.

•Challenges encountered by women and URMs. All the women and almost half of the men appointed in the 1980s and 1990s related personal stories relevant to the prejudice and discrimination faced by women in academic pathology, often related to an inequitable salary structure or marital and/or parental status (Table 6). These chairs noted that this shaped their awareness of disparities and their work as chairs to achieve equitable treatment of women and minorities. Most were unaware of formal DEI institutional initiatives. One chair initiated a salary review with support of higher level administration to examine equity, one participated in recruitment of Black minorities to their medical school, and one became a founder of Executive Leadership in Academic Medicine, a program designed specifically for women. One former chair reported that major efforts to improve DEI at his institution didn’t occur until the early 2000s when he was no longer a chair.

The majority of the women chairs appointed in 2010 and later all reported discrimination related to salary, children, and opportunity (Table 6). All of these women chairs reported observing or experiencing bias and exclusion in their workplace and noted this motivated them to create more diverse, equitable and inclusive environments in their own departments and within AAPath.

•Departmental priorities and a culture of equity in AAPath. Issues unrelated to diversity, including finances, were top agenda items reported by the chairs in the earlier cohort and reflected changing reimbursement regulations for laboratory services during that time period. Nonetheless, all of the women chairs from this early period noted the welcoming climate of the organization and three of the four were nominated to appear on the ballot for an executive council position. One chair positively recalled a 2005 AAPath survey assessing the number of women in various leadership roles in academic pathology departments, illustrating the organization’s proactive interest in involving women.

One chair positively recalled a 2005 AAPath survey assessing the number of women in various leadership roles in academic pathology departments, illustrating the organization’s proactive interest in involving women.

Discussion

AAPath’s journey toward diverse membership and inclusive excellence has taken place within the context of national events but has been initiated and carried forward largely by the chairs’ personal experiences as medical students, house staff, faculty and as leaders. In the organization’s first 50 years, there were no formal, specific, or targeted activities aimed at increasing representation of women and minorities in the membership made up primarily of academic pathology department chairs. Though the US society surrounding AAPath in its early years was actively advancing civil rights, feminism, and other causes intended to advance diversity and inclusion, the 14 chairs interviewed all reported observing or personally experiencing discrimination and prejudice in hiring and promotion. All noted that these inequities inspired their work as leaders to promote change within their departments and within the programming of AAPath. Thus, both societal and personal experiences shaped AAPath’s journey. There was still a scant pipeline of women and minorities in pathology who might become pathology chairs and members of AAPath. Women and minorities, therefore, took their place slowly among the membership.

Washington has described five organizational stages of DEI maturity10 which we found helpful in evaluating AAPath’s progress Stage 1: Aware, when an organization realizes that DEI can provide advantages but has not yet implemented changes; Stage 2: Compliant, when an organization achieves regulatory or legal DEI requirements; Stage 3: Tactical, when DEI is part of organizational initiatives or plans and some, but not all units, participate; Stage 4: Integrated, when DEI is integrated into the organization and routinely included in all activities; and Stage 5: Sustainable, when DEI activities have a track record of success and are seen as “best in class”, thanks to continuous review and improvement. For most of AAPath’s history, the organization was in the first two stages of maturity, “Aware” and “Compliant.” Not until the current period (2010–present) did activities become more intentional and formal within AAPath. The LDDC was established in 2012 and became an important force moving diversity and inclusion excellence forward within the organization, thanks to its timely and relevant leadership development programming including DILP, the PLA and the APLF (See Table 6). The success of these programs placed AAPath into the third, Tactical stage of DEI maturity. As of 2024, the organization has advanced into the fourth, Integrated stage of DEI maturity since initiatives are woven into the fabric of the organization and are not solely the provenance of the LDDC. Other committees, sections and units within AAPath have created their own programming such as a diversity workgroup within AAPath’s Academy of Distinguished Pathology Educators and a leadership development course for future administrative executives by the Academic Pathology Executives Section. AAPath has also made progress to the fifth and final stage of DEI maturity, Sustainability. Characterized by formal evaluation of programs and outcomes for continuous improvement, several AAPath publications share the outcomes and evaluations which are used to inform program development and update content.21,23, 24, 25

Professional organizations such as AAPath can and should play an important role in creating and enhancing a culture of diversity and inclusion excellence in their members’ home departments and medical schools, first by modeling important principles and practices within the organization itself and second by including pathology departmental staff, faculty, and leaders in programs that will educate, inform, and build skills that members can take home and use within their department. AAPath’s leadership development efforts also build a diverse pipeline of chair candidates and other leaders. Chair organizations like AAPath do not directly control or recruit its membership since medical school deans appoint the chairs of their respective departments, and these appointees and their leadership team automatically become a member of AAPath. Nevertheless, AAPath has demonstrated how such an organization can influence the diversity of the pool from which department chairs are chosen. AAPath expands the diversity of the leadership candidate pool via enrollment in its leadership programs for non-chair faculty members. These programs address an important root cause of the lack of diversity by bolstering the pool of mid-level leaders from which chairs are often recruited.24

It is important to note that most of the progress in diversifying pathology department chairs has chiefly been in gender diversity. Women, as the largest and most visible underrepresented group, frequently serve as the “canaries in the coal mine.” Their success (or lack of it) can serve as a marker for what works and where trouble lurks that might be pertinent to other minority groups. As data and interviews illustrate, there were few women and minorities as medical students and in medical school departments in the early decades of AAPath, but women faculty have progressively increased in the ensuing years as students, house staff, and faculty. Despite academic medicine’s leaky pipeline in which many more women than men leave academia before becoming associate and full professors,27,28 women chairs in all specialties have steadily increased in percentage16 (Fig. 1). AAPath’s success in making diversity and inclusion excellence part of its organization culture contributes to this positive change and is also visible in the unprecedented three consecutive women AAPath presidents (2018–2023)29 and four consecutive years of women recipients of AAPath’s Distinguished Service Award (2021–2024).30 Pathology is also among the top two academic specialties which approach salary equity between women and men, and among the lowest specialties for sexual harassment.31 In addition to AAPath’s proactive endeavors since 2010 to grow diversity and inclusion among academic pathology leaders, these successes may also reflect many other excellent leadership programs for women and minorities sponsored by other large organizations.32, 33, 34, 35

Limitations to this review include the limitations associated with Internet searches based on keywords, and limitations in the interview process related to recollection of past events. The relatively small number of interviewees is also a potential limitation; however, the interview pool was diverse in the era of service as chair, region of the country, gender, race, and ethnicity. Thematic saturation, the standard for interviews in qualitative research, was used for achieving representative findings.12

Despite the progress diversifying academic leadership in pathology, there is still much work to be done. New external challenges have arisen, and AAPath’s future efforts toward diversity and inclusion excellence may need to be less internally focused and more externally focused via advocacy to outside organizations, including state and federal legislators who are pushing to halt or limit these efforts. AAPath supports other organizations such as the Association of American Medical Colleges in their efforts to prevent the passage of the EDUCATE Act, which would prevent medical schools with DEI programs from receiving federal funding.36 The teaching of critical race theory (CRT) has been targeted by state legislatures, often complicated by frequent confusion of DEI with CRT, creating undue animosity toward DEI.37 Even when diversity or inclusion excellence is an accepted element of an educational institution’s policy, pushback can occur because of the added expense and time necessary required to implement and educate faculty, staff, and students on best practices for diversity in recruitment.

We hope that AAPath’s journey toward a diverse membership focused on inclusion excellence inspires all of academic medicine to reflect on their own journeys, and to be thoughtful and intentional in their own program development. AAPath’s experience can be used as a guidepost to facilitate the most hospitable and productive environment that will attract the best faculty talent who will deliver outstanding contributions to the tripartite missions of patient care, education, and research.
