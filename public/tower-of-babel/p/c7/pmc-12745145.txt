The clinical and financial impact of new-onset atrial fibrillation after coronary bypass grafting: From indexed procedure to long-term follow-up
Authors: Niv Ad, Clifford E Fonner, Diane Alejo, Ramon Riojas, Glenn JR Whitman, Terri Haber, Charles Evans, Sari D Holmes, Maryland Cardiac Surgery Quality Initiative
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNew-onset postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is common. Additional information regarding long-term clinical and financial outcomes is important. This statewide analysis compares periprocedural and long-term outcomes between patients with and without POAF.MethodsData on patients who underwent isolated CABG from 2013 to 2021 without atrial fibrillation (AF) history from a statewide Society of Thoracic Surgeons database (N = 18,377) were merged with the state health services database. Patients with POAF (n = 4656) were compared with those without POAF (n = 13,721). Outcomes were incidence and cost of readmissions, examined with χ2 tests and regressions.ResultsThe incidence of POAF was 25% (male = 26% vs female = 24%, P = .002). Patients with POAF had worse risk-adjusted outcomes, more often were discharged on oral anticoagulation (29% vs 7%, P < .001), and incurred greater procedural cost ($52K vs $46K, P < .001). The POAF group had a greater incidence of readmissions for AF (12% vs 3%, P < .001), heart failure (HF) (15% vs 10%, P < .001), and major bleeding (2.4% vs 1.6%, P = .001) but not ischemic stroke (5% vs 4%, P = .100). After risk adjustment, POAF remained at greater risk for AF, HF, and major bleeding readmissions. The proportion of patients’ readmission costs associated with AF (11% vs 3%, P < .001), HF (12% vs 9%, P < .001), and major bleeding (2.1% vs 1.4%, P = .009) was greater for patients with POAF but not ischemic stroke (4% vs 4%, P = .591).ConclusionsPOAF remains common after CABG, with greater cost and risk-adjusted morbidity reported. Follow-up data reveal a significant number of patients continue to be impacted by AF. This study suggests targeted discharge planning and surveillance for patients with POAF to improve long-term outcomes and reduce cost of complications.Key Words: CABG, POAF, atrial fibrillation, readmissions

Central Message

Postoperative atrial fibrillation is shown to have an impact on long-term morbidity and cost beyond the indexed procedure stay as identified in a statewide cardiac surgery database.

Perspective

New-onset postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) remains very common after coronary bypass surgery. It is associated with early increased risk for major morbidity and long-term greater incidence of AF and heart failure readmissions. Considerations should be made to tailor the discharge planning and follow-up for patients who develop POAF.

New-onset postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is a very common complication.1, 2, 3 Its occurrence at early stages of the postoperative period and the high rate of early treatment success led to the notion by most that it may be considered as a self-limited event with no intermediate- and long-term consequences and, therefore, no need for special surveillance and follow-up.4,5 However, it should always be kept in context that POAF may be one of the ramifications of atrial myopathy that exists in many patients at the time of surgery even without a history of atrial fibrillation (AF) and, therefore, would impact patients during the perioperative period and at late follow-up.6,7 Previous studies have examined long-term clinical outcomes in patients who developed POAF,8, 9, 10 but additional information may be of importance, especially financial outcomes and common comorbidities that were found to be associated with POAF such as age, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), obesity, diabetes, and hypertension. These clinical variables are central to several validated risk prediction tools in patients with AF, such as the CHA2DS2-VASc and HAS-BLED.11,12 Therefore, it would be extremely valuable to better understand the potential association between POAF and intermediate and long-term outcome focusing on recurrence of AF, heart failure (HF), stroke, and major bleeding. This statewide analysis aims to compare periprocedural and intermediate/long-term outcomes between patients who developed new-onset POAF after surgery with those patients who did not develop AF as well as the role of moderating factors.

Methods

The Maryland Cardiac Surgery Quality Initiative (MCSQI) database merges data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) database from all 11 cardiac surgery programs in the state of Maryland. The MCSQI collaborated with the Maryland Health Care Commission and the Maryland Health Services Cost Review Commission (HSCRC) to link inpatient clinical data (STS database) to administrative inpatient and outpatient data. Under Maryland's unique health care payment system, hospital charges correlate with costs that include direct and indirect costs. The HSCRC's All-Payer Rate Regulation System establishes rates for all hospital services that are non-negotiable with payers. The charges within each hospital are the same for all payers. The payment system has evolved from an “All-Payer Model” (2014-2018) to a “Total Cost of Care Model” (2019-2028) that includes a global budget revenue base with adjustments for inflation, demographic, quality, efficiency, and value. The outpatient encounter costs only represent patient encounters in HSCRC-regulated space, which is typically an outpatient hospital-based department or clinic. The linked MCSQI and HSCRC dataset provides a unique opportunity to assess intermediate- and long-term outcomes for cardiac surgery patients after discharge throughout the state of Maryland. The WCG IRB determined that this research meets exempt status and approved a waiver of consent (approved January 18, 2024; #1-1730744-1).

The sample for this study included patients who underwent isolated CABG from January 2013 to December 2021 without a history of preoperative AF and with valid data on POAF (N = 18,377). Although the study sample included isolated CABG procedures, there were some cases that included other procedures that the STS does not consider moving the case out of the “isolated” category, such as left atrial appendage occlusions (LAAOs). The sample available for follow-up analyses (N = 18,067) excluded patients with operative mortality. The primary study groups included patients with new-onset POAF after CABG (n = 4656) compared with those without POAF (n = 13,721). The role of moderating factors including CHA2DS2-VASc score, CHA2DS2-VA score, and discharge on anticoagulation was also included as secondary analyses.

Clinical variables for the index CABG procedure were defined according to the STS database except for cost data available from the HSCRC dataset. New-onset POAF was defined per the STS database as the patient experienced new onset of AF/flutter after operating room exit that either required treatment or lasted longer than 1 hour. Clinical outcomes during follow-up were generated from the primary, secondary, and third diagnosis codes reported in the HSCRC inpatient dataset according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions (Table E1). Patients who were alive past the perioperative period and did not have inpatient readmissions captured in the HSCRC dataset per indication were categorized as not experiencing that clinical outcome during follow-up. The primary outcomes were incidence of inpatient readmissions for AF/flutter, HF, ischemic stroke, and major bleeding. Secondary outcomes included perioperative complications, costs during index procedure, and costs during follow-up (inpatient and outpatient encounters). Cost during follow-up and total available follow-up time for each patient included data from the HSCRC outpatient follow-up visit dataset. Given that cost of readmissions for specific indications was only available for those who experienced the outcome, we used a modified claims-based methodology13 to examine cost of readmissions by creating variables for the proportion of patients' total readmission costs associated with readmissions that included the indication of interest. For each patient, the total cost of readmissions that included the indication of interest (ie, major bleeding) was divided by that patient's total readmission costs. However, each readmission could have multiple diagnoses/indications, which could not be separated to determine the precise cost of each diagnosis.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 software (IBM Corp), and the α level was set at P < .05, 2-tailed. Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) on the basis of Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality testing. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage). Missing data were excluded using listwise deletion. Univariable group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted with nonparametric Mann-Whitney U tests and comparisons for categorical variables were conducted with χ2 tests.

In order to adjust for potential confounding in analyses of primary and secondary outcomes, multivariable logistic regressions adjusting for outcome-specific STS risk score (as applicable) or STS predicted risk of mortality were used to determine the association of POAF with perioperative and follow-up clinical outcomes. For example, the STS predicted risk of stroke score was adjusted for along with POAF in analyses for the outcomes of perioperative stroke and follow-up ischemic stroke. In addition, multivariable logistic regression models were conducted to examine the factors that were associated with incidence of POAF and incidence of follow-up inpatient AF readmissions. The factors included in these models were age, sex, body mass index, ejection fraction, preoperative hematocrit, last preoperative creatinine, chronic lung disease, cerebrovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, redo procedure, elective status, and hospital for index CABG. The model for follow-up AF readmissions also included POAF. Multivariable linear regressions were used to examine the effect of POAF on total cost for the index CABG and total follow-up costs (inpatient and outpatient encounters) after adjustment for this same set of covariates. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was also conducted to examine the effect of POAF on freedom from readmissions.

Secondary analyses on the role of CHA2DS2-VASc score, CHA2DS2-VA score, and discharge on anticoagulation were conducted using χ2 tests including results for the overall sample and separately for those with and without POAF. In addition, the effect of CHA2DS2-VASc score and CHA2DS2-VA score groups (<2, 2-3, and ≥4) on incidence of POAF was examined with logistic regression analyses with adjustment for the STS predicted risk of mortality score. In this sample, most patients had a CHA2DS2-VASc and CHA2DS2-VA score ≥2 (Table E2). Therefore, we used 3 groups (<2, 2-3, and ≥4) on the basis of the CHA2DS2-VASc and CHA2DS2-VA scores in our analyses to better understand the relationship of these risk scores with outcomes. We also examined the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc score (congestive HF, hypertension, age categories, diabetes, previous stroke/cerebrovascular disease, vascular disease, and female sex) in a multivariable logistic regression to determine which factors were independently associated with readmission for ischemic stroke during follow-up; POAF was added to the model as a second step to determine the contribution of this factor.

Results

Although all of the patients in this sample were listed as having no history of AF, 1% of the sample had an LAAO. However, the group with POAF had a similar prevalence of LAAO as compared with the group without POAF (1% vs 1%, P = .191). The multivariable model that examined which factors were related to the incidence of perioperative new-onset POAF found that greater body mass index (odds ratio [OR], 1.01), chronic lung disease (OR, 1.06), cerebrovascular disease (OR, 1.27), and hypertension (OR, 1.40) were associated with significantly greater odds for POAF (Table 3). There was also a significant omnibus effect for index procedure hospital (Wald = 512.3, P < .001), indicating that the odds for POAF differed by hospital.

Perioperative Outcomes

The patients who developed POAF after CABG had significantly greater incidence of perioperative complications in univariable analyses and after risk adjustment (Table 4), including stroke (1.9% vs 0.9%, P < .001), renal failure (3.4% vs 1.3%, P < .001), prolonged ventilation (11.1% vs 5.1%, P < .001), reoperation (5.9% vs 3.2%, P < .001), operative mortality (2.6% vs 1.4%, P < .001), and readmission <30 days (9.9% vs 8.0%, P < .001).There were more patients discharged on oral anticoagulation in the POAF group (29% vs 7%, P < .001), and length of stay was longer for the POAF group (6 [5-9] days vs 5 [4-7] days, P < .001). There was also significant variability in the likelihood to be discharged on anticoagulation by index procedure hospital (6.4% to 16.2%, P < .001). The median total cost of the index procedure was significantly greater for the POAF group ($51,726 vs $46,120, P < .001). After multivariable adjustment, POAF remained significantly associated with $10,686 greater total cost of the index procedure on average (t = 15.9, P < .001). Within the POAF group, the female patients had greater total cost of the index procedure compared with the male patients ($56,685 vs $50,352, P < .001).

Follow-up Outcomes

The median follow-up was 736 (IQR, 151-1579) days for the group with POAF and 714 (IQR, 119-1623) days for the group without POAF. The POAF group had a greater incidence of inpatient readmissions for AF (533 [12%] vs 358 [3%], P < .001), HF (649 [15%] vs 1278 [10%], P < .001), and major bleeding (102 [2.4%] vs 206 [1.6%], P = .001), but no difference for ischemic stroke (202 [5%] vs 524 [4%], P = .100; Figure 1). The univariable differences remained significant after adjustment for STS risk score, such that the POAF group had 4.8 times greater risk for AF readmits, 1.5 times greater risk for HF readmits, and 1.4 times greater risk for major bleeding readmits (Table 4). The risk-adjusted analysis for ischemic stroke did not find a significant difference by POAF.

For the POAF group, there were a total of 1138 readmissions for HF (1.8/patient, range, 1-15), 714 readmissions for AF (1.3/patient, range, 1-7), 119 readmissions for major bleeding (1.2/patient, range, 1-4), and 246 readmissions for ischemic stroke (1.2/patient, range, 1-4).

The unadjusted survival analysis found that freedom from AF readmissions (log rank = 340.3, P < .001; Figure 2) and HF readmissions (log rank = 40.7, P < .001) was lower for those with POAF compared with those without POAF, but no difference was found for freedom from major bleeding (log rank = 3.4, P = .064) or ischemic stroke (log rank = 0.7, P = .410) readmissions. The multivariable model that examined factors related to the incidence of AF readmissions during follow-up found that greater preoperative hematocrit (OR, 0.98) was associated with reduced odds for AF readmissions whereas chronic lung disease (OR, 1.20) and POAF (OR, 5.04) were associated with greater odds for AF readmissions. Patients who experienced new-onset POAF after CABG were 5 times more likely to experience a readmission for AF during follow-up. There was also a significant omnibus effect for hospital (Wald = 91.8, P < .001), indicating that the odds for AF readmissions differed by index procedure hospital.

Generally, there was significant variability in the median total cost of follow-up encounters ($9542 to $33,822, P < .001) by index procedure hospital and the median total cost of follow-up encounters was significantly greater for the POAF group ($26,197 vs $18,674, P < .001). After multivariable adjustment, POAF remained significantly associated with $6468 greater total cost of follow-up encounters on average (t = 3.8, P < .001). In addition, patients in the POAF group had a greater proportion of their total inpatient readmission costs associated with AF (11% vs 3%, P < .001), HF (12% vs 9%, P < .001), and major bleeding (2.1% vs 1.4%, P = .009) compared with the group without POAF, but there was no difference for ischemic stroke (4% vs 4%, P = .591; Figure 1).

Secondary Analyses

Patients were more likely to develop new-onset POAF after CABG as CHA2DS2-VASc score group increased (18% vs 24% vs 29%, P < .001). After adjustment for the STS predicted risk of mortality score, logistic regression analysis found that patients with CHA2DS2-VASc score of 2 to 3 (OR, 0.81; 95% confidence interval, 0.75-0.87, P < .001) and CHA2DS2-VASc score <2 (OR, 0.58, 95% confidence interval, 0.51-0.66, P < .001) had progressively reduced odds for POAF (19% and 42% reduction, respectively) compared with patients with CHA2DS2-VASc score ≥4. Overall, patients were more likely to be discharged on anticoagulation as CHA2DS2-VASc score group increased (<2, 2-3, ≥4) from 6% to 10% to 17% (P < .001) and this overall association was significant within the POAF (24% vs 26% vs 33%, P < .001), as well as within the no POAF (2% vs 5% vs 10%, P < .001) groups, which is probably a reflection of overall patient comorbidity for the patients without POAF.

As CHA2DS2-VASc score group increased, patients were more likely to experience readmissions for AF (P < .001), HF (P < .001), major bleed (P < .001), and ischemic stroke (P < .001; Table 5). The effect of CHA2DS2-VASc score group on readmissions for those with and without POAF is shown in Figure E1. Examining the specific variables comprising the CHA2DS2-VASc score in a multivariable analysis for the risk of ischemic stroke found that female sex (OR, 1.68, P < .001), hypertension (OR, 1.56, P = .003), history of cerebrovascular disease (OR, 2.54, P < .001), history of vascular disease (OR, 1.42, P < .001), and diabetes (OR, = 1.51, P < .001) had significantly greater odds for ischemic stroke readmissions during follow-up, but not age categories or history of congestive HF. The addition of POAF to the model was not significant (OR, 1.12, P = .194) and did not alter the associations of the CHA2DS2-VASc score variables with the risk for ischemic stroke readmissions.

Recently, the CHA2DS2-VA score has begun to be used in place of the CHA2DS2-VASc score, with the scoring for sex category removed.14 Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the CHA2DS2-VA score and found no effect on the direction/strength or significance of any of the secondary analyses results, but as expected the maximum score was lower and the prevalence of CHA2DS2-VA score ≥4 was less (36% vs 43%), but CHA2DS2-VA score ≥2 was only slightly reduced (88%; Table E2).

Discussion

This statewide study is based on all 11 cardiac surgery programs in the state, which are a good representation of all different types of centers performing cardiac surgery in the United States. The incidence of POAF reported in this study remained substantial with 25% overall incidence. The variables associated with POAF in our study are not different from previous reports.15,16 and are body mass index, COPD, cerebrovascular disease, hypertension, and lower preoperative hematocrit. Interestingly there was a strong association between the center in which the surgery was performed and the risk for POAF (Wald = 512.3, P < .001).

Our study, like studies published before,15,16 found an association between POAF and major perioperative complications such as operative mortality and stroke and an association with increased length of stay (6 vs 5 days), readmission with 30 days, and greater total cost ($51,726 vs $46,120) especially in female patients.

POAF is a well-documented complication after cardiac surgery.17 The medical community was focused for years on trying to identify patients at greater risk of developing the complication,15 followed by efforts to reduce the risk of developing it, from the introduction of beta blockers18 to treatment with amiodarone.19 More recent studies have focused on the ways to manage POAF when it develops with antiarrhythmic management (medical or cardioversion) to rhythm control and anticoagulation treatment.20,21 Despite being such a common morbidity, the management of it is far from being settled, with variability in the management even within the same institution.22 There is also a significant difference in surgeon attitudes toward treatment of their patients with oral anticoagulation because of the increased risk of bleeding.21,22 Recent publications targeted the variability with suggested pathways and treatment options for patients with POAF.23 Throughout the MCSQI centers we did identify a significant variability in the incidence of POAF (4% to 40%), which may reflect on the variability in the preventive methods and perioperative blood transfusion management between hospitals.24,25

Our study documented the greater incidence of perioperative complications associated with POAF. The significant variability in the incidence of POAF among centers may suggest that a closer look into perioperative management differences among centers may lead to further reduction in the incidence of the arrhythmia but it is well beyond the scope of this study. New-onset AF was also found to be associated with significantly greater periprocedural cost secondary to the complication rates and the length of stay, but it would be very important to also look into the long-term effect of POAF on cost beyond the indexed hospital stay.

This study highlights certain clinical variables that are associated with greater event rates during the follow-up period. The data regarding the long-term outcome of patients who developed POAF are scarce, but existing data suggest that patients with POAF have greater risk of mortality and other complication during follow up.8 In our study it was well documented that the incidence of AF after discharge was significant and long-lasting, as is clearly demonstrated in Figure 2. It was also shown that specifically with inpatient readmission for AF, the CHA2DS2-VASc score may not be the best discriminator to ascertain the risk to be readmitted when patients exhibit a score of greater than or equal to 2 (89% of the patients). That was not the case with readmission for HF, major bleeding, or ischemic stroke in which increased CHA2DS2-VASc score was associated with a significant increase in the incidence of these outcomes.

On the basis of our findings, we recommend to include patients who develop POAF in a special follow-up clinical surveillance probably in conjunction with an electrophysiologist in order to minimize recurrence rate and complications associated with recurrent AF, HF, and major bleeding. Consideration should be given to timing of the postoperative visits (in person or via a video chat), the type of rhythm surveillance, and how to modify the risk-benefit ratio for major bleeding and ischemic strokes. It may be suggested by this study that a CHA2DS2-VASc score ≥2 is not specific or sensitive enough as 89% of the patients operated attained that score.

Limitations

The results of this study are limited by the retrospective nature of these analyses, which may have introduced bias. In addition, using administrative databases for research purposes can present challenges given that these data were collected for a purpose other than research studies and are generally not granular enough to answer some clinical questions. For example, we are unable to assess rhythm status at discharge or during follow-up as this information was not collected in the databases used for this study. We also have few details regarding the follow-up course for patients with and without POAF, such as the status of oral anticoagulation at various follow-up time points.

Conclusions

Our study provides unique information on statewide outcomes of patients with POAF after CABG. The study focuses not only on the indexed procedure outcome but also shares information on the incidence of inpatient and outpatient encounters up to 5 years after discharge. On the basis of the data provided, it is clear that in general patients with POAF should not be discharged from the hospital as every other patient and would probably benefit from special follow-up to focus on minimizing the reoccurrence of AF and HF. Those patients also need to be set up with a follow-up regimen to address anticoagulation management and minimize the risk of stroke. Our study may suggest that certain comorbidities such as COPD, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension should be accounted for in conjunction with POAF. Finally, it would be important to ascertain the importance of the CHA2DS2-VASc score probably focusing on the group of patients with values exceeding 4.
