Longitudinal Evaluation of Static and Dynamic Accommodation in Young Children
Authors: Kristen L. Kerber, Deepa Dhungel, Karen Flores, Serena Beri, Iván Marín-Franch, Fuensanta A. Vera-Diaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate longitudinal (3 years) accommodation responses (ARs) in children at high risk (HR) or low risk (LR) for myopia, including a subgroup of children who developed myopia.MethodsAccommodation was measured in young children (n = 92; 7.54 ± 0.97 years) with functional emmetropia (+0.97 ± 0.54 D) at baseline under static and dynamic conditions using an open-field autorefractor. Linear mixed models assessed group differences and the predictor role of accommodation on myopia onset.ResultsAt baseline and all visits (7 total), the LR group showed greater static AR (P < 0.01) and dynamic amplitude (P < 0.01); these effects remained after adjusting for age (AR P = 0.02; amplitude P = 0.01). After further adjusting for refractive error (spherical equivalent of cycloplegic refraction [M]), risk group differences were no longer significant. Logistic regression indicated AR was a significant predictor of myopia onset when age was included (P < 0.01), but not after accounting for M (P = 0.39), reflecting M's stronger influence. Accommodation was primarily associated with age and M in this cohort with homogeneous ages and M. A Sobel test (P = 0.06) suggested a possible mediating effect for AR on myopia development.ConclusionsA lower AR was associated with a greater myopia risk, but its effect appears secondary to refractive error. Similar patterns were observed for dynamic amplitude. Longitudinal evaluations were minimal, suggesting group differences likely emerged before clinical myopia onset.

Myopia is of significant interest to the vision science community owing to an increasing prevalence worldwide that is associated with increasing rates of high myopia. Myopia, and more so high myopia, also poses a high risk for ocular health sequelae including, but not limited to, retinal detachment, glaucoma, cataracts, and myopic maculopathy.1–3 During early childhood, the human eye undergoes significant growth, mainly in the anterior–posterior axis during a process called emmetropization. If this process is successful, the axial length (AXL) of the eye elongates to precisely match the changes in power that occur in the optical components (i.e., cornea and lens). Unfortunately, for many children, the AXL does not halt at zero refractive error, but continues to elongate excessively, causing myopia. Although the precise etiology of myopia is not yet well-understood, it is known that the signals for eye growth and emmetropization, a failure of which causes myopia, depend on the retinal image content.4–6

The retinal image content is affected by multiple factors, one of the most significant being the defocus blur induced by inaccurate accommodation. Accommodation is necessary to focus at different viewing distances, which occurs through the crystalline lens increasing or decreasing its optical power. Although small accommodation errors are normal, including small leads of accommodation at distance and small lags at near,7 if these errors are too large, they may negatively affect emmetropization in young children as a consequence of excessively blurry retinal image.

The role of near-work has been extensively studied in myopia,8,9 and the accuracy and stability of accommodation during such activities has been found to be a likely important factor in myopia development and progression.10 In general, young children appear to have relatively stable accommodative lag during school age years.11 Previous studies have shown that children with myopia have greater lags of accommodation when looking at real targets and shallower accommodation response (AR) functions with negative lens-induced blur.12 Inaccurate accommodation has also been suggested to be associated with myopia progression in adults and children.13,14 The largest study investigating accommodation in myopia, Correction of Myopia Evaluation Trial (COMET), showed that static lags of accommodation with negative lens induced blur increased with progressing myopia and decreased as myopia stabilized15; the authors also found that greater lags were evident in children who progress the most.16 However, other investigators found no relationship between lag and myopia progression.17–19 Collaborative Longitudinal Evaluation of Ethnicity and Refractive Error (CLEERE) evaluated accommodative lag using a Badal system in premyopes and found that static accommodation lags were greater in children who developed myopia, but only after myopia occurred.20 Badal systems, however, have their limitations, including stimulating proximal accommodation and field of view limitations. The variability of the results between studies may be due to differences in methodology, including target characteristics, methodologies of stimulation, and/or field of view.21 Although studies have shown conflicting results of the role of accommodation in myopes and emmetropes, few studies have longitudinally assessed premyopic children, especially those who are imminently developing myopia.20,22

Most previous studies have tested static accommodation. Our group has shown that the accommodation stability, and consequently the stability of the retinal image, may be relevant to the development or progression of myopia.23 In young children, dynamic accommodation appears to have significant microfluctuations, largest in the first decade of life.24 Seidel et al.25 found reduced blur responses under dynamic conditions in young adults who had late-onset myopia, but not early-onset myopia. Progressing myopes have also shown near work-induced transient myopia, an aftereffect of near work that demonstrates longer time constants when changing focus to distance after a period of close work.26 These and other results argue for evaluation of dynamic accommodative measures that represent more real-world viewing.

To better understand the factors that predispose children to developing myopia, the longitudinal study PICNIC (Preventing myopia: Investigating Contributing factors to Nearsightedness In Children) was designed to conduct a comprehensive longitudinal evaluation of the visual system in young children who had functional emmetropia (emmetropia or low hyperopia not requiring optical correction based on clinical standards) at baseline.27–29 This article presents the results for the longitudinal assessment of static and dynamic accommodation responses in PICNIC.

Methods

Participants

Participants were young children (n = 92; mean age, 7.54 ± 0.97 years) from the Greater Boston area enrolled in the longitudinal study PICNIC between 2020 and 2022. Informed consent for study participation was acquired in the form of written assent from all children and written consent from their parents. The study followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the New England College of Optometry. All participants had functional emmetropia (emmetropia or low hyperopia, not requiring optical correction based on clinical standards) at the baseline visit. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria as well as the PICNIC study design are available elsewhere.27 General inclusion criteria, as determined at the baseline visit, were (1) between 6 and less than 9 years of age; (2) uncorrected distance visual acuity of +0.10 logMAR or better in each eye, and (3) refractive error (spherical equivalent of cycloplegic refraction [M]) of no less than −0.25 D and no more than +2.25 D in the right eye, with less than or equal to 0.75 D of anisometropia or astigmatism in either eye. Refraction was determined by cycloplegic open-field autorefraction (two drops of 1% cyclopentolate instilled 5 minutes apart, using a WAM 5500 autorefractor − Grand Seiko, https://grandseiko.com). General exclusion criteria were (1) a history of ocular disease or surgery that may have visual consequences, (2) known binocular or accommodative dysfunction, (3) seizure disorder, or (4) using ocular or systemic drugs that may affect vision.

After the initial baseline visit, subjects who met all the inclusion criteria were asked to return every 6 months (± 2 weeks) for 3 years (7 visits: V1, V2, V3, V4, V5, V6, V7). Children were classified at either low risk (LR) or high risk (HR) for myopia development, based on their baseline refractive error and parental myopia, as previously published (Supplementary Table S1).27 Among other experimental tests, children underwent static and dynamic accommodation testing (WAM-5500 open-field autorefractor WMT-2 Moving System) (Fig. 1a), optical biometry (Lenstar LS900, https://haag-streit-usa.com), and cycloplegic open-field autorefraction. Other clinical tests performed included a thorough history, cover test, near point of convergence, monocular estimation method, dilated assessment of ocular health, measurements of pupil sizes, height, and weight. Of the children enrolled in PICNIC, data for children (n = 92) who completed a minimum of three visits are included in this report. A total of 14 children developed myopia during the 3-year duration of the study, the remaining children maintained functional emmetropia.

Procedures

Anisometropia was limited per the inclusion criteria, and the monocular accommodation tests were conducted in the right eyes. A table and adjustable chair were placed at an appropriate height for young children (Fig. 1a). Care was taken to ensure the readings were taken with the light target always centered in the eye. “Dead leaves” stimuli (Fig. 1b) were chosen for the target as their range of spatial frequency and luminance mimics natural scenes.23,30,31 The angular size of the target is approximately 39° horizontally and 57° vertically.

Image of the WAM-5500 open-field autorefractor WMT-2 Moving System from the side (a) and the participant's point of view with the image of dead leaves stimuli (b).

For static measures, the average of the spherical equivalent (M) of five readings at 40 cm (2.50 D accommodation demand) was taken. The accommodative stimulus was calculated by adding the accommodative demand (2.50 D) to the cycloplegic M, to account for the subjects’ varying levels of hyperopia. The total static AR were calculated using the difference between the average response (M) provided by the autorefractor at near and the cycloplegic M result at distance (6 m). Lag was then calculated as the difference between the accommodative stimulus and the AR. For the dynamic accommodation assessments, the customized robotic arm holding the stimulus was moved sinusoidally for a duration of 27 seconds (9 sec-cycles), creating an accommodative demand range of 1 to 4 D using the same stimulus as the static measurements. Continuous fixation on the red dot located in the center of the stimulus was confirmed by the examiner during the trials.

Participants wore either no optical correction or their best optical correction, as indicated, during testing. A trial frame with their best subjective refraction results was used for the few participants who developed myopia, causing a visual acuity worse than +0.10 logMAR without correction. A frosted lens (neutral density filter mounted in spectacle frames) was placed over the left eye while measurements were taken on the right eye.

Data Processing and Analyses

The two outcome measures for static accommodation were the total AR and the lag of accommodation, computed as described above. Dynamic accommodation raw data results were fitted with three-parameter (median response, amplitude, and phase) sinusoidal functions (Fig. 2) with the same frequency as the stimulus, using a nonlinear least squares method on Python using SciPy library.32 The frequency was fixed at 1/9, corresponding with the 9-second period of the stimulus (i.e., accommodative demand). Noise, potential eye movements, or blinks resulting in accommodation values outside the range of the median ±2.50 were excluded from the sine fitting analysis. The coefficient of determination (R2) of the raw data and the sine fit were calculated as an estimate of the goodness of fit, and if any data that showed an R2 of less than 0.50 or had a proportion of missing data greater than 33%, then the fits were visually inspected individually. If at least two complete cycles of data were present, the results were included. Outcome measures for dynamic accommodation were the amplitude and phase lag of the sinusoidal fit. For ease of understanding, amplitude in this article is reported as twice the amplitude of the sine wave function (midline to peak or trough) to demonstrate the overall accommodative shift (peak to trough), as shown in Figure 2.

Dynamic accommodation responses were fitted to a sinusoidal function to determine amplitude and phase. The yellow dots represent the stimuli demands and the blue curve represents the best fit line of the accommodation responses (gray dots). Sample of a child at LR for developing myopia (a) with larger amplitude and shorter phase, compared to a sample of a child at HR for developing myopia (b) who demonstrated smaller amplitude and longer phase.

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29.02, IBM Corp., 2020). The normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Univariate analysis of variance was used to calculate differences between groups for monocular conditions at baseline. To evaluate differences in accommodation responses across the entire duration of the study, linear mixed models were used with the accommodation variable (AR, lag, amplitude, or phase) as a dependent variable, age and M (potential confounders) as covariates, and visit number as a fixed effect. Logistic regression with a binomial distribution and logit link were used to evaluate the accommodation metrics as predictive of myopia development. For these, the development of myopia (binary) was the dependent variable, and accommodation measures as well as age, M, and visit number were fixed effects, with a random intercept for subject to account for repeated measures. The sample size was calculated to achieve 80% power at a 0.05 alpha level for detecting a small to moderate effect size (Cohen's d = 0.25) using a linear mixed model with seven repeated measurements and two risk groups. Assuming a within-subject correlation of 0.50, the estimated minimum sample size required was 40 to 45 participants per group. A total of 102 participants were recruited to account for a 10% attrition rate. Of these, 92 who completed a minimum of three visits are included in these analyses.

We present these exploratory analyses to assess associations, consistency with the literature, and to generate hypotheses for future confirmatory study design, which will answer the statistical and scientific hypotheses drawn from the current study.

Results

Participants’ demographic information and ocular characteristics at baseline are presented in Table 1. Cycloplegic refractive error (spherical equivalent, M) and AXL were normally distributed; age was not. Participants in the HR group were significantly younger than those in the LR group (U = 662.50; z = −0.31; P < 0.01). As expected, there was a statistically significant but low correlation between AXL and M in this group of young children (r = −0.26; P = 0.01). When analyzed within risk groups, the negative correlation remained similar in magnitude, but did not reach statistical significance, likely owing to the smaller sample (LR, r = −0.27; P = 0.06; HR, r = −0.28; P = 0.07) (Fig. 3). The strength of the correlation did not differ significantly between groups (Fisher's z = 0.03; P = 0.98), suggesting that the relationship between AXL and refractive error was comparable across risk groups.

Accommodation Throughout the 3-Year Duration of the Study

Refractive error is also known to affect accommodation responses.35 However, the LR and HR groups were defined based on the child's refractive error. Therefore, models were constructed with and without M as a confounding variable, to assess if there is an independent effect of accommodation or if it does not explain variance beyond that associated with refractive error. Linear mixed models were conducted to assess the effect of risk group in each accommodation metric as well as the effect of time (visits, repeated measures), and the random effects used to control for inter-subject variability. Both age and refractive error (M) were used as covariates (fixed effects), as well as the interactions of risk group and M with age. All metrics met the necessary assumptions, including a good linearity for all continuous variables and no problematic multicollinearities among fixed predictors. For each accommodative outcome measure, there was no evidence of heteroscedasticity or nonlinearity, and residuals are centered and well distributed. Although the Shapiro–Wilk test indicated a small deviation from normality in some metrics (all P > 0.03), the Q–Q plot showed that residuals were approximately normally distributed, with only minor deviations in the tails.

In the unadjusted model for monocular accommodative responses, risk group had a highly significant predictor effect (F = 42.04; P < 0.01) for static AR (Fig. 4a), with an age-related trend across risk groups (i.e., older children likely to demonstrate greater accommodation) (F = 2.78; P = 0.10). Therefore, it was no surprise to find a significant effect of age (F = 4.94; P = 0.02). Visit number had no effect. The observed estimated difference in AR between risk groups was 0.28 D, with a wide 95% CI (–0.08 to +0.64; P = 0.13). Even though the AR metric is a composite variable that contains refractive error (M), to separate the influence of M from AR, we added M to the model (multicollinearity diagnostics indicated no concerns between M and AR; variance inflation factor = 1.94; tolerance = 0.51; both within acceptable ranges). After adjusting for M, the association of risk group with AR was no longer significant (F = 1.79; P = 0.18; estimated marginal means of AR for LR: 2.03 D; 95% CI, 1.92–2.13; for HR: 2.04 D; 95% CI, 1.94–2.15), nor were any of its interactions with visit, age, or M significant (all P > 0.17), indicating that the effect of risk group in AR is not independent of M. The effect size was small (Cohen's d ≈ 0.24), and observed power was low (≈27%), indicating that the study may have been underpowered to detect small differences between groups. Therefore, although no significant group differences were found, the possibility of a small effect cannot be ruled out. The model revealed a significant main effect of age (F = 52.32; P < 0.01) and visit number (F = 3.17; P < 0.01). Older age was associated with greater AR (B = 0.114; 95% CI, 0.007–0.221; P = 0.04), and greater hyperopia (higher M) predicted stronger ARs (B = 1.697; 95% CI, 1.221–2.172; P < 0.01). A significant interaction between age and M (F = 18.59; P < 0.01) suggested that the relationship between refractive error and AR was moderated by age (B = −0.109; 95% CI, −0.159 to −0.059).

For lag (Fig. 4b), risk group was not a significant predictor (F = 0.43; P = 0.51), nor was there an interaction of risk group and age (F = 0.04; P = 0.85). The observed difference in the outcome between risk groups was less than 0.01 D (95% CI, −0.26 to +0.28; P = 0.95), corresponding with a very small effect size (d ≈ 0.06). Because the 95% CIs include zero broadly, we conclude that there was no meaningful effect. Lag was significantly predicted by M (F = 7.36; P = 0.01) and visit number (F = 2.98; P = 0.01), suggesting that lag varied over time and with accommodative demand (varying with M).

For dynamic measures, unadjusted models show that risk group was also significantly associated with amplitude (Fig. 4c), with larger amplitude in children at LR for myopia (F = 11.20; P < 0.01; estimated difference 0.17 D; estimated means LR, 2.71 ± 0.03 D; 95% CI, 2.65–2.78; HR: mean 2.55 ± 0.04 D; 95% CI, 2.48–2.62). After adjusting for age, the association is still significant (F = 6.33; P = 0.01; estimated difference, 0.15 D; estimated means LR, 2.72 ± 0.04 D; 95% CI, 2.64, 2.80; HR, mean 2.57 ± 0.04 D; 95% CI, 2.48–2.65). However, after adjusting also for refractive error (M), the association was no longer significant (F = 0.29; P = 0.59), and the interaction of risk group and age was also not significant (F = 0.76; P = 0.39), indicating that the effect of risk group in amplitude of accommodation is not independent of M. The observed difference between risk groups was −0.12 D (95% CI, −0.37 to +0.13; P = 0.34), corresponding with a small effect size (d ≈ 0.24). The results indicate that we likely did not have enough power because the CIs were wide; we had limited power (∼57%) to detect small effects, and the observed effect of risk group was small. This means that, even if there were a real effect, it would probably not be clinically important in this sample. There was an effect in amplitude of the interaction of M and age (F = 6.72; P = 0.01), as expected, and of visit number (F = 5.58; P < 0.01). Pairwise comparisons showed the effect of visit was due to a decreased amplitude at V7.

For phase (Fig. 4d), there was no overall effect of risk group (F = 3.41; P = 0.07); estimated difference −0.08 seconds (95% CI, −0.01 to +0.137; P = 0.08; interaction of risk group and age, F = 3.015; P = 0.08). There was an effect on phase with age (F = 10.58; P < 0.01), with older children showing smaller phase values and a significant effect of visit number (F = 2.25; P = 0.04). There was a small interaction effect between risk group and visit number (F = 2.50; P = 0.02), with no significant differences between any pairs of visits.

Separate LMMs were conducted to assess the effect of M and AXL (separately because these variables are highly correlated) in accommodation responses (Fig. 5), accounting for the effect of age (covariate, fixed) and visit number (fixed factor). There is not a significant effect of AXL in any of the accommodation variables (AR, F = 2.35; P = 0.13; lag, F = 2.95; P = 0.09; amplitude, F = 2.63; P = 0.11; phase, F = 0.47; P = 0.49). The effect of M is significant for AR, as expected, with larger AR in eyes with more hyperopia (F = 63.73; P < 0.01). There is also an interaction of M and age (F = 22.68; P < 0.01), indicating the effect of M in AR depends on age. Dynamic amplitude is also greater in eyes with more hyperopia (F = 3.90; P < 0.01), with a significant interaction effect of M and age (F = 3.83; P < 0.01). There is no significant effect of lag (F = 0.14; P = 0.71) or phase (F = 0.08; P = 0.97) on M. Supplementary Table S2 shows a summary of these results.

Accommodation as a Predictor for Myopia

During the 3 years, N = 14 children became myopic; their baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 3. Logistic regressions with a binomial distribution and logit link were used to examine the association between each accommodation outcome measure and the development of myopia. The models included a random intercept for subject to account for repeated measures, age as covariate (fixed), and visit number as a fixed factor. The risk group was not included in the model because all children who developed myopia were in the HR group, making the variable collinear with the outcome and unsuitable for inclusion.

For static ARs, AR has a statistically significant overall effect on myopia development when age is considered as a covariate (B = 2.73; SE = 0.73; t = 3.73; P < 0.01) (Fig. 4a). For each unit decrease in AR, the odds of developing myopia increased by a factor of 15.36 (95% CI, 3.64–64.81), indicating that lower AR values were strongly associated with greater odds of myopia development. There is a lack of interaction with visit number (all P > 0.05), indicating that AR predictive value is stable over time, rather than concentrated at earlier or later visits. When adding M, the overall model was still significant (F = 2.66; P = 0.10), indicating that the predictors jointly contributed to the risk of myopia onset. Among these, M was a strong and significant predictor (F = 12.70; P < 0.01), indicating that children with lower hyperopia had increased odds of developing myopia, as expected. In contrast, AR was not a significant predictor after adjusting for M (F = 0.72; P = 0.39), suggesting that the observed effect of AR in previous models may have been largely attributable to its correlation with refractive error. Visit number did not significantly influence the likelihood of myopia onset (F = 0.54; P = 0.74). To explore whether the effect of M on myopia was mediated by AR, a Sobel test was conducted. The indirect effect was marginally significant (z = 1.84; P = 0.06), suggesting a possible mediating role for AR in the pathway from refractive error to myopia development, although this did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance. This result supports the idea that accommodation may play a partial role in myopia development, but its influence appears secondary to that of refractive error itself. The effect of lag was not statistically significant (B = 0.92; SE = 0.79; P = 0.24) (Fig. 4b). The estimated odds ratio for lag was 2.51 (95% CI, 0.54–11.79), indicating that, although the trend suggested higher lag might be associated with increased odds of myopia, the result was not statistically reliable.

For dynamic accommodation, the fixed effect of amplitude (Fig. 4c) was not statistically significant (B = 1.31; SE = 0.82; t = 1.60; P = 0.11 ; 95% CI, −0.32 to 2.87). The corresponding odds ratio was 3.72 (95% CI, 0.74–18.49), suggesting a potential, although not statistically significant, trend where greater amplitude was associated with greater odds of developing myopia. However, the wide CIs and the nonsignificant result suggest that the model may have been underpowered to detect a meaningful effect. To further examine the relationship, a Sobel test was conducted to assess whether amplitude mediated the effect of refractive error (M) on myopia. The indirect effect was not statistically significant (z = 0.73; P = 0.46), providing no evidence that amplitude of accommodation mediates the relationship between refractive error and myopia in this sample. Visit number was not a significant predictor (P = 0.99). The effect of phase on myopia was not statistically significant (B = 0.60; SE = 1.86; t = 0.32; P = 0.75) (Fig. 4d), with an odds ratio of 1.83 (95% CI, 0.05–70.77). Visit number was also not significant (P = 0.99). These findings indicate that phase was not associated with the likelihood of becoming myopic.

Discussion

We assessed accommodation responses over a 3-year period in a large cohort of young children who had functional emmetropia at the baseline visit to elucidate whether accommodation plays a role in myopia development. Our findings provide insights into how accommodation, age, and refractive error interact over time, and their respective roles in the development of myopia.

As expected, there was a statistically significant but low correlation between cycloplegic AXL and refractive error (spherical equivalent, M) in this group of young children with homogeneous ages and refractive errors. This relationship was comparable across risk groups, suggesting that ocular structural and optical characteristics were similarly coupled across risk levels. As designed, children in the HR group had lower hyperopia at baseline compared to those at LR. In addition, participants in the HR group were significantly younger than those in the LR group.

Accommodation results were similar at baseline to across the entire study and differed across risk groups. At baseline, children at LR accommodated significantly more under static conditions (higher mean static AR) regardless of age. This finding was expected and likely reflects their higher levels of hyperopia, which demand greater accommodation to maintain clear vision at near. There was also a difference in the interaction of risk group and age, indicating that the effect of risk group in accommodation depends on age. In contrast, because accommodation lag accounts for both demand and response, the observed lags were more similar between risk groups, with borderline significant differences. This finding is consistent with the previously reported CLEERE study that premyopes do not show the same relationship with lag after myopia onset.20 Baseline unadjusted dynamic amplitudes were also larger in the LR group; however, the differences were not statistically significant after adjusting for age. There were no significant effects of risk group in phase at baseline. These findings suggest a potential early difference in accommodative performance between children at LR versus HR, although some of this difference may be attributable to age and underlying refractive error.

Longitudinal models across the full duration of the study, both unadjusted models and models adjusted for age showed significantly higher static AR (estimated difference, 0.28 D [95% CI, −0.08 to +0.64]) and dynamic amplitude (estimated difference, 0.17 D [95% CI, 0.04–0.34]) in the LR group, with no effect of visit number. After adjusting for age and M, the association risk group with AR or amplitude was no longer significant, indicating that the effect of risk group in accommodation is not independent of M. This finding indicates that the relationship between accommodation and myopia risk may largely reflect their mutual association with refractive error, rather than an independent effect of accommodation. However, given the small effect size and the low observed power, these results need to be interpreted with caution because the study may be underpowered to detect small differences between groups. Therefore, although no significant group differences were found, the possibility of a small effect cannot be ruled out. Older ages and greater hyperopia predicted greater ARs and amplitudes, and the significant interaction between age and M suggested that the relationship between refractive error and AR was moderated by age. No significant relationships between risk group and static lag of accommodation or dynamic phase were noted in any models. The differences in AR but not lag suggest that the LR children (who had higher hyperopia, but functional emmetropia) were able to compensate for the cycloplegic M, consistent with previous findings.36 This finding may also indicate that the possible effect of accommodation in myopia may not be mediated via retinal image quality (determined by lag), as previously suggested,37,14 but the accommodation effort itself (determined by AR). Similarly, Luo et al.38 (2023) suggested that greater habitual accommodative tone (difference in spherical equivalent precyloplegia and postcycloplegia) was associated with a protective effect against myopia development.

These findings underscore that age and refractive error are primary drivers of ARs, even in this group of children with a relatively narrow range of ages and refractive error at baseline. Absolute AR—which includes accommodation to offset hyperopic refractive error—was higher in LR children throughout the study duration, consistent with their greater hyperopia, and lower in children at HR for myopia. Similar patterns were noted for dynamic amplitude, but the effects were not significant owing to insufficient power. Hyperopic eyes have shown greater per-diopter change in both lens thickness and anterior chamber depth compared with their myopic counterparts.39 Therefore, it may be the extra AR in the LR children that alters the ocular structure and resultant changes (i.e., aberrations, retinal image quality) that matters for myopia development, because the lag was not significantly different between the groups, despite greater accommodative demands in children with a higher M.

Using data analyses from prior studies, including CLEERE, to create the risk group criteria, the authors predicted that more than 70% of the children at HR would develop myopia and minimal to no LR (<10%). However, fewer children than anticipated developed myopia during the study (n = 14), which may reflect an unintended behavioral effect of participating in the study. Although the study was observational, children and their families received repeated education on visual behaviors associated with myopia risk (e.g., time outdoors, near work).40,41 In addition, regular questionnaires and participation in a light exposure substudy may have increased awareness and altered behavior, consciously or subconsciously, decreasing myopia incidence.

Despite this limitation, and an overall lack of independent effects of accommodative measures on myopia, logistic regression analyses showed that static AR was a strong predictor of myopia development when analyzed alone. Children with lower AR had significantly increased odds of developing myopia (odds ratio, 15.36), although this effect became nonsignificant after adjusting for M, again suggesting that AR's predictive value may stem from its correlation with refractive error. To further explore this potential causal pathway, mediation analyses were conducted. A Sobel test indicated a marginally significant indirect effect of refractive error on myopia via AR, suggesting that accommodation may partially mediate the relationship between hyperopia and myopia development. However, this finding did not attain conventional significance and should be interpreted cautiously. However, for dynamic amplitude, no mediation effect was found, nor was amplitude a statistically significant predictor of myopia onset in adjusted models. Phase, too, showed no association with myopia risk. Notably, lag of accommodation was not a significant predictor of myopia development in any model, aligning with recent studies that question the role of lag as a standalone biomarker for myopia onset.

Taken together, these results indicate that, although accommodative responses differ between children at HR and LR of myopia, their predictive power for myopia development is limited when controlling for refractive error. The data support the notion that refractive error remains the primary predictor of myopia development risk,42 with accommodation playing, perhaps, a secondary or dependent role. Although some trends suggested a potential influence of AR and amplitude, the modest effect sizes and limited statistical power underscore the need for further research with larger sample sizes and more sensitive measurement paradigms to fully disentangle the complex interplay between accommodation and myopia development. The small sample size and narrow range of demographics of the recruited children likely contributed to the small changes and effects noted above. Observed changes in accommodative response over the 3-year period for all subjects were small and not clinically significant magnitudes, suggesting that any group differences found likely occurred early—before the onset of myopia. Additional studies in children before myopia onset are therefore warranted.

Previous studies have reported reduced blur sensitivity in myopes, which could impair accommodation accuracy.43,25 Because accommodation influences the level of retinal blur, future research should explore the relationship between accommodation responses, optical aberrations, and blur perception.

A key strength of this study is its prospective, longitudinal design, which allows for the examination of accommodative function before the onset of myopia. Although a substantial number of participants completed all study visits, longer follow-up with larger samples, particularly a higher number of children who develop myopia, is needed to confirm these results. Because many HR children did not develop myopia within the expected timeframe, our ability to detect prediction effects in myopia was constrained. Nonetheless, these preliminary findings provide important insights into early functional differences in children at risk for myopia before myopia development and lay the groundwork for future confirmatory investigations.
