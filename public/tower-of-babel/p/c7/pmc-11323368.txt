Analysis of heavy metals and minerals in edible vegetable oils produced and marketed in Gondar City, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Lamrot Yohannes, Hailemariam Feleke, Mequannent Sharew Melaku, Dagnachew Eyachew Amare
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNowadays, food safety is regarded as one of the most critical global public health issues. Edible oil, a key ingredient in food processing, is widely used and consumed in every Ethiopian household. However, its safety is often overlooked. Currently, edible oil is produced in Ethiopia from small-scale operations to large industrial levels, as well as imported from other countries.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the levels of heavy metals and essential minerals in edible vegetable oils produced and marketed in Gondar City, Northwest Ethiopia.MethodsA laboratory-based cross-sectional study was conducted from May to July 2021 in Gondar City. Seventeen edible oil samples were collected using simple random sampling techniques. Heavy metal content was determined using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer with the standard procedures and techniques after microwave digestion. The efficiency and validity of the method used were evaluated by determining the limit of detection (LOD), the limit of quantification (LOQ), accuracy, and precision. The collected data were entered into Microsoft Excel and transported to Stata for analysis.ResultA total of seventeen vegetable oil samples were analyzed. The accuracy of the method was evaluated by recovery studies, which ranged from 81 to 115%, and the relative standard deviations were found to be below 15%. The concentrations of Zn, Cu, Fe, Cd, and Pb were in the range of 0.07 to 0.8 mg/l, 0.002 to 0.06 mg/l, 0.01 to 0.8 mg/l, 0.08 to 0.18 mg/l, and 0.003 to 0.27 mg/l, respectively. In general, the lead and cadmium content was higher than other metals in some of the investigated edible vegetable oils. Most values fell within the permissible quality limits for edibility as prescribed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC). However, the levels of Pb and Cd exceeded the reference levels in some locally produced vegetable oils.ConclusionTo address the exceeded levels of heavy metals, it is imperative to implement more careful handling, processing of raw materials, and filtering practices. Producers and marketers should take the necessary precautions to prevent contamination. Strict regulatory control from responsible bodies and stakeholders is recommended to ensure the safety and metal contents of vegetable oils originating from the study area.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19695-0.Keywords: Edible vegetable oil, Essential nutrient, Locally made, Heavy metal, Gondar

Background

Food-borne diseases are becoming more common in both developed and developing countries due to the increased consumption of perishable foods [1–3]. Food consumption is the main route through which humans are exposed to harmful metals and contaminants, more so than through inhalation or skin contact. Edible vegetable oils, which are composed solely of glycerides and fatty acids derived from plants, are among the most widely consumed foods. Popular edible vegetable oils include those from olives, soybeans, sunflowers, niger seeds, sesame seeds, peanuts, and palm [4]. These oils are extensively used in cooking, food processing, and the cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and chemical industries. The presence of essential fatty acids, phytosterols, and alpha-tocopherol enhances the nutritional value of vegetable oils, and their ability to reduce cholesterol levels makes them beneficial [5].

Heavy metals are defined by their relatively high densities, atomic weights, or atomic numbers [6]. The presence and concentration of these elements in the environment and within the human body can lead to various health issues. While some of these elements are essential for human health, their excessive accumulation can pose significant health risks [7]. These metals become toxic when they accumulate in body tissues more rapidly than they can be detoxified [7]. Land contamination with heavy metals and metalloids can result from various sources, including emissions from mines and industrial facilities, burning of petrol and coal, the use of paints, fertilizers, pesticides, wastewater irrigation, and atmospheric deposition [8]. Heavy metals can be classified as either potentially harmful (such as arsenic, cadmium, and lead) or essential to human health (such as copper, zinc, and iron) [9]. Toxic elements can be extremely harmful, even at low concentrations, when ingested over an extended period [10].

Numerous researchers from different parts of the world have determined the presence of heavy metals in vegetable oils [11–20]. Assessing heavy metals in vegetable oils is essential due to the metabolic roles of certain metals and the extensive use of these oils [21, 22]. Metal contamination can originate from the plant's metabolism and agronomic processing techniques, including extraction, crushing, refining, bleaching, and hydrogenation, as well as from pesticide residues, infected or pathogenic oil-bearing plants, or plasticizers [15, 23–26].

In 2019, Ethiopians were terrified by the discovery of unqualified edible oils on the market, as well as the health risks of palm and other vegetable oil use. The Ethiopian Public Health Institute advised consumers against being fooled by unlawful edible oil on the market. There has been no previous stringent quality control and inspection of edible oils, as well as a lack of accountability in disclosing information to the public, aside from the Ethiopian Public Health Institute's (EPHI) announcement in 2019 about an increase in the number of illicit edible oils on the market. In Gondar, there is a huge and rapid local production of edible vegetable oil, ranging from small-scale to big-scale companies, all of which require strict quality control. However, most of these businesses are unable to maintain quality control due to limited technology and production processes, particularly in the refining process.

As reported by the Gondar Trade and Industry Bureau, around 85% of the population has access to edible oil. Both locally produced and commercially marketed vegetable oils remain the primary source for the population. Many of the city's oil production industries are outdated, leading to fundamental problems such as the lack of refining and bleaching processes, corrosion, safety issues, hygiene problems, and metal contamination. These issues are especially prevalent in developing countries like Ethiopia due to outdated extraction and filtration systems, limited knowledge and practices among handlers, and the absence of strict monitoring programs.

To our knowledge, there has been no report on the heavy metal levels in edible vegetable oils manufactured in Gondar, Ethiopia, apart from these studies on microbial quality [27] and rancidity quality [28]. Furthermore, unlike earlier studies, the current study uses locally produced oils. In comparison to previous studies on edible oils in Gondar, this one has a larger sample size and a broader focus. To avoid instilling fear and health safety concerns in customers, it is essential to conduct regular and comprehensive studies on the qualities of edible vegetable oils allowing people to make buying decisions based on health risks and quality in order to protect their health. As a result of the aforementioned reasons, it was necessary to conduct the study on edible vegetable oils manufactured in Gondar city in the hopes of filling the gaps listed. It would also serve as baseline information for constructing health policies about the quality of edible vegetable oils. The study aims to assess the level of some heavy metals and essential minerals in edible vegetable oil produced and sold in the markets of Gondar City.

Materials and method

Study design and period

A laboratory-based cross-sectional study design was conducted from May – July 2021.

Study area

This study was conducted in Gondar City, Northwestern Ethiopia. Gondar City is located 760 km away from the capital city of Ethiopia, Addis Ababa. It is located at 12°36′ north latitude and 37°28′ east longitude, with an elevation of 2,133 m above sea level as shown in Fig. 1. The city serves as the region's economic and cultural center, with six sub-cities and 22 kebeles. It has a total population of 413,000 [29]. According to the information obtained from the Gondar City Trade & Industry Office, there are about 76 wholesalers of edible oil. Among these, 22 are wholesalers of vegetable oil, 43 are whole sellers of locally produced oil and 11 edible oil producers who are serving Gondar and Gondar nearby area.

Map of Ethiopia showing Gondar City

Sample source and study samples

Edible vegetable oils available and accessible in the market areas in Gondar City were the sample sources. Edible vegetable oils that are commonly manufactured, marketed, and used by customers and registered by Ethiopia's food and drug administration authority and Gondar trade and industry bureau were study samples.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study includes edible vegetable oils which are available in the Gondar market, licensed and registered by the Ethiopian Food and Drug Administration Authority (EFDA) and the Gondar Trade and Industry Bureau. This study excluded edible vegetable oils that were not properly labeled or oils that did not have manufacturing or expiration dates or were difficult to read.

Sample size and sampling technique

All available locally-produced vegetable oils and eight non-locally produced edible vegetable oils were included in our study. A simple random sampling technique was used to select the study samples. Samples were purchased from eight sites, specifically Kebele 7, 8, 9, 18, 11, 10, 15, and 20. The sample contained eight brands of mixed oils and one brand of Niger seed oil from locally produced oils. Additionally, one brand of mixed oil, one brand of soybean oil, one brand of sunflower oil, and five brands of Niger seed oil that were commercially available or not locally produced were included in the sample. Grab sampling was used to collect the samples from each site.

Before sample collection, a preliminary assessment was done. As a result, two types of oils (Niger seed oil and mixed oil (Niger, sesame, and sunflower) based on their ingredients) were locally produced. Mixed oil, Niger seed oil, soybean, and sunflower oils were available at the market (Tables 1 and 2).

Instrument, reagents and chemicals

The following instruments and reagents were used to perform the work: Whatman filter papers No.42, refrigerators (RT34M3723S8/HL/2017 Model), microwave (SɅMSUNG), and 210 VGP Atomic Absorption Spectrometer Model instruments equipped with flame atomizers were used in the research work. High-purity analytical grade reagents, including 37% HCL, 69–72% HNO3, and 30% H2O2 was used for the digestion of samples. Certified reference materials of Cd, Zn, Pb, Cu, and Fe were used for the preparation of standard samples. Standard solutions of iron, copper, cadmium, lead, and zinc were prepared using the dilution of certified standard solutions (1000 ppm, Blulux) of corresponding metal ions. The blank sample was deionized water. Chromic acid was used for washing glassware. Tap water and distilled water were taken for washing, rinsing, and preparation of the samples.

Collection, preparation and digestion of oil samples

The collection was done manually. The samples were collected aseptically using a 250-ml Erlenmeyer flask and 250-ml edible packed oil by environmental health professionals for internal quality, official, or regulatory purposes. The samples were transported to the laboratory, being kept at dispensary temperature and within an icebox (4°C) [30]. The transported samples were stored in a refrigerator until heavy metal analysis was conducted. The samples were checked consistently by laboratory technicians for sample quality, adequacy, and the general quality of the sample. In heavy metal analysis, all laboratory ware, including pipette tips and, flasks, cups, were cleaned with detergent and tap water, rinsed with distilled water, soaked in dilute chromic acid, and then rinsed thoroughly with distilled water. Different temperatures, volumes, and digestion times were optimized. All calibration curves were based on seven standards. The element standard solutions used for calibration were freshly prepared by diluting stock standard solutions for each element (1000 mg/l) immediately before use.

Heavy metal Analysis

For the heavy metal analysis, different temperatures, volumes and digestion times were optimized which is summarized in the supplementary file.

The digested samples were clear and transparent, which indicated there were complete digestion of the sample and, therefore, the release of the bound heavy metals from the sample matrix.

Digestion of oil samples

Different digestion procedures were assessed for the digestion of the oil samples using HCL, H2O2, and HNO3 acids. 0.5 g of the oil samples were weighed and transferred to the conical flask and mixed with 5 ml of concentrated nitric acid and 2 ml of hydrochloric acid, left to stay for overnight pre-digestion. On the next day, the oil sample mixed with H2O2, and then the flask was heated in a microwave at 180°c for 55 min until a clear transparent, and colorless solution appeared [31, 32]. A blank digest was carried out in the same way. Then the flask was removed from the microwave and cooled. The vegetable oil digestates were filtered with Whatman No. 42 filter paper. This was then transferred into 50 ml volumetric flasks and diluted to the mark with distilled water. All the diluted samples were stored in a refrigerator at 4°c until analysis. Each sample was digested in triplicate. The digestions of blank reagents were also performed using all the reagents used above except the samples.

Determination of metals in the sample

The metals (Pb, Zn, Cd, Cu and Fe) concentrations were determined by using the 210VGP model of flame atomic absorption spectroscopy (FAAS). The analysis of each sample was carried out three times to obtain representative results. The setup of AAS was fitted and adjusted with a specific lamp of a particular metal, the energy, and wavelength and slit width of each metal according to the instrument manual. The instrumental analysis was calibrated by preparing a series of standard solutions of the target analytes. The samples were examined with FAAS for the presence of heavy metals. The slit width, wavelength, and energy settings of the instrument were optimized to achieve the minimum signal intensity, as detailed in Table 3. Hollow cathode lamps were used as primary line sources, adhering to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Proper flame conditions were maintained by supplying acetylene and air at appropriate flow rates. The metals were detected in absorption/concentration mode, and the output data were manually recorded. The same procedure was followed for analyzing the metals in the spiked samples. Preparation and procedure of standard calibration curves

A calibration curve was generated to establish the linear relationship between absorbance and metal concentration within the specified concentration range. This calibration was conducted initially to verify the proper functioning of the instrument before quantitatively determining the target metals. Each target analyte was calibrated using seven series of standard concentrations. The concentration of standards and the measured response (absorbance) of the instruments were plotted on the calibration curve (i.e., the calibration curves were plotted as a function of absorbance versus concentration of the standard solution) and approved by calculating the corresponding correlation coefficient (r2). Calibration curves were prepared to determine the concentration of the heavy metals in the sample solution. First, 50mgL−1 of an intermediate standard solution for each heavy metal was prepared from stock standard solution containing 1,000 mg L−1. Appropriate working standards were prepared for each of the metal solution by serial dilution of the intermediate solution using distilled water. The analytical wavelengths, energy, and slit widths were adjusted according to the instrument's operation manual to optimize sensitivity. Subsequently, the working standards were aspirated one by one into the flame atomic absorption spectroscopy (FAAS) instrument, and their absorbance readings were recorded. Calibration curves were then plotted for each metal by correlating absorbance values with known concentrations (mg/L) of the standards.

Following the calibration process with the standard solutions, the sample solutions were immediately aspirated into the FAAS instrument, and their absorbance readings were recorded. This allowed for the determination of the concentrations of heavy metals in the sample solutions based on the previously established calibration curves.

Evaluation of analytical method performance validation

Method validation results were employed to evaluate the quality, consistency, and reliability of the analytical outcomes, representing a fundamental aspect of sound analytical practice. The validation encompassed several key metrics, including accuracy, precision, limit of detection, and quantitation [34].

Accuracy and precision

Recovery Studies (Accuracy)

The accuracy of the results was evaluated through recovery studies. For this purpose, a known amount of analyte was added to vegetable oil samples. Standard solutions with known concentrations (1000 mg/L of Pb, Zn, Cd, Cu, and Fe) were used. From these standard solutions, intermediate solutions of 100 ppm for Fe, Zn, Cu, and Cd, and 10 ppm for Pb were prepared in 50 ml. Subsequently, 1000 µl of the intermediate standard solution was added (spiked) to 0.5 g of vegetable oil samples. The spiking concentrations ranged from 0.002 to 16, starting below the maximum residue limits for each heavy metal. These spiked samples were then digested using the same method as the initial vegetable oil samples. After diluting the spiked oil samples to the required volume (50 mL) with double-distilled water, they were analyzed using the same method employed for the analysis of the vegetable oil samples. Relative standard deviation (precision studies)

In this study, the precision of the results was evaluated using the relative standard deviation (RSD) of triplicate samples (n = 3) analyzed under identical conditions. The RSD values obtained were all below the required limit, indicating that the overall error from both the sample and the methods was within the acceptable range (RSD ≤ 15%) [36].

The highest RSD for essential metals was 9.09% in mixed and niger seed oil, while the highest RSD value for soybean, mixed, and niger seed oil was 7.8%. This indicates that the analytical method, encompassing both digestion and instrumental measurement steps, demonstrated acceptable precision, as shown in Table 5.

Determination of detection, quantitation and Instrumental limits

The detection limit (DL) was calculated using the standard deviation of the blank concentration and the slope of the calibration curve: LOD = 3Sb/b. Where Sb is the blank concentration's standard deviation and b is the calibration curve's slope. The slope b was calculated using the analyte's calibration curve [37].

The quantitation limit was used to determine contaminants in particular. The quantitation limit (QL) was calculated using the response standard deviation and the slope of the calibration curve as follows: LOQ = 10Sb / b. Where Sb is the blank concentration's standard deviation and b is the calibration curve's slope. The slope b was calculated using the analyte's calibration curve [35].

Instrumental detection limits were directly obtained from the instrument manual for all the elements under study (Fe, Zn, Cd, Cu, and Pb). It is the lowest concentration (smallest quantity) of an analyte that can be detected (without quantitative determination) using a given measuring instrument (e.g., detector). The IDL should always be below the method detection limit. It is not used for compliance data reporting but may be used for statistical data analysis and comparing the attributes of different instruments [38].

Method Validation

The efficiency of the method used was evaluated by determining the limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) and performing recovery studies. The LOD and LOQ of the method were established based on the concentrations of blanks and their standard deviations. The instrumental detection limit ranged from 0.005 to 0.05, while the method detection limit ranged from 0.16 to 4.95. The instrumental and method detection limit values are provided in Table 6.

Quality control

All the standard stock solutions for heavy metals were certified reference materials. Adequate quality control measures and vigilance were implemented to ensure reliable results. Throughout the experiments, all glassware and equipment were meticulously washed, starting with chromic acid and followed by repeated rinsing with distilled water to prevent contamination. Reagent blank determinations were used to correct the readings. To ensure the accuracy of the reported data, recovery experiments were conducted, and all experiments were performed in triplicate. To avoid contamination, all steps in the sample preparation procedure were carried out in a laboratory equipped for trace element analysis. Reagent blanks were prepared and measured in the same manner as the samples.

Data processing, management and analysis

The raw data were coded and entered into a Microsoft Excel 2016 spreadsheet. Data cleaning was performed to ensure accuracy, consistency, and completeness, eliminating any missing values and variables. Descriptive statistics were calculated using Microsoft Excel. The data were then transferred to STATA version 17 for further statistical analysis. Results were presented using text, tables, and graphs. The non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was employed to examine significant variations in heavy metal concentrations across different vegetable oil constituents.

Result and Discussion

Determining the concentration of metals in edible vegetable oil is crucial since they play significant roles in various metabolic processes in the human body. However, excessive intake of these metals can be toxic. This study analyzed the levels of five metals (Fe, Cu, Zn, Pb, and Cd) in edible vegetable oil samples using Flame Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (FAAS).

Levels of heavy metals in edible oils

The concentration of the selected metals in the edible oil samples was determined using flame atomic absorption spectroscopy (FAAS) after preparing the necessary calibration curves. The microwave digestion system was chosen for its high accuracy in terms of both time and recovery values. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The recovery values for the microwave digestion method were nearly quantitative, and the relative standard deviations for all investigated metals were less than 15%. The results were compared with WHO/FAO and NAFDAC standards and showed good agreement with the certified values, except for lead and cadmium in some oil samples. Copper

In the present study, the sample’s Cu content ranged from 0.003–0.06 mg/l (Fig. 3). Mixed oil has a high concentration of copper metal as compared to soybean and sunflower oils. The value was found to be lower than the maximum allowable concentration limit set by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) and the World Health Organization (WHO) (0.1 mg/l) [39–43]. This implies a reduced health risk and preserved shelf life as well as good sensory quality of the oil for consumption. In addition, this level of concentration will prevent potential acute and chronic toxic effects associated with copper contamination. The result in this study was lower than that was reported in similar studies conducted in Spanish (0.02–0.33 mg/kg) [44], Italy (12.71–50.5 mg/kg) [45], Croatia (0.15–1.5 mg/kg) [46], Turkey (0.05–0.71) mg/kg [14], Saudi Arabia (0.035–0.286) mg/kg [11], Turkey (0.71–0.05 mg/kg) [14] and in China (0.214–0.875 mg/kg) [15]. The copper values in the current investigated oil samples are higher than from a study conducted in Riyadh that range from 0.001–0.081 mg/kg [47]. These differences might occur due to several factors. First, different countries and regions may have varying agricultural practices, including the use of copper-based pesticides or fertilizers, leading to differences in copper levels. Secondly, the methods used to extract and refine vegetable oils may yield oils with different copper concentrations where the current study used oil products processed by both cold-pressed and solvent extraction method. Thirdly, the instrument and solvent used might be the reason for the variation where the current study used an atomic absorption spectrometer (FAAS) for detection of the metal content, whereas other studies use different instruments like inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES), chemometric identification stripping voltammetry, and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) [48]. Although excessive copper intake is toxic, it plays various essential roles in numerous biological systems within the human body [49–51]. Children with copper deficiency may experience osteoporosis, leukopenia, and hypochromic anemia, while chronic copper poisoning can result in Wilson's disease [49–51].

Iron

Fe was found within the range of 0.01–0.8 mg/l with a median value of 0.15 mg/l (Fig. 4). The highest Fe concentration (0.8 mg/l) was observed in locally produced mixed oil. The value was found to be lower than the maximum allowable concentration limit set by FAO and WHO (1–1.5 mg/l) [39–43] implying safe quality for consumption without a health risk. The Fe concentration in this study was lower than other studies in Saudi Arabia (17.3 – 57.8 mg/l) [21], China (16.2–45.3 mg/l) [15] and (7.78–28.93 mg/l) [52]. Fe value has been reported to be higher than our values in this literature (15.31 and 3.39 µg/g) [13, 45, 46]. The possible reason for the variation might be the iron content in the soil itself differs depending on geographical location and soil composition. The use of iron-containing fertilizers or amendments can influence the iron content of plants. Fertilizers may be applied to enhance plant growth and nutrient uptake, potentially impacting the mineral composition of the plants, including iron levels. Additionally, processing conditions, storage and handling are other contributing factors. Iron is a well-known essential mineral, integral to hemoglobin. It aids in the oxidation of fats, proteins, and carbohydrates, helping to maintain body weight, whose deficiency can lead to anemia [53–55].

Zinc

The sample’s zinc content varied from 0.07 to 0.8 mg/l, with a median value of 0.25 mg/l (Fig. 5). Its concentration is acceptable as compared with the range of the maximum allowable concentration limit set by the FAO and WHO (1–1.5 mg/l) [39–43]. Zn concentrations in this investigation were lower than other results reported by numerous researchers: is 0.955–3.10 mg/l, 3.08–1.03 mg/l, 3.58–9.54 mg/l, [14, 21, 44, 52, 56] and inline concentration (0.742–2.56 mg/l) in a study conducted in China [15]. The variations in the concentration of zinc across studies might be influenced by a combination of factors related to plant biology, agricultural practices, processing methods, and storage conditions. Additionally, different geographical sample places, different types of instruments used for metal detection, the varied soil’s Zn content, and human activity might all be related to this difference. Zinc plays a crucial role in most human metabolic processes, and deficiencies can lead to immune issues, growth delays, loss of appetite, and skin changes. Conversely, excessive intake of zinc can cause nausea, vomiting, appetite loss, abdominal cramps, diarrhea, headaches, and weakened immune function [15][57, 58].

Cadmium

Cadmium content was found to be in the range of 0.08–0.18 mg/l (Fig. 6). In our study, 25% of oil samples were higher than national, international, and legal limits recommended by FAO/WHO, which that is 0.05 mg/l (Codex S 2011) [39–43]. This indicates broader contamination issues, which imply the oil might pose serious health risks and regulatory challenges. The cadmium level of this study is lower than studies those conducted in China, Iran, and Nigeria (2.64–8.43 mg/kg, 1.87–8.58 mg/kg, 0.34–2.77 mg/kg) [15, 52, 59]. The difference in concentration may be caused by the process applied in the analysis, the analytical solvents used, the digestion method, or the equipment or instrument used for detection. In addition to being a naturally occurring element in soil, cadmium is also widely distributed in the environment due to human activity. Its poisoning can result in cancer as well as problems with the kidneys, liver, skeletal system, bone demineralization, and respiratory problems [60–63].

Lead

In our study, lead concentrations varied from 0.003 to 0.27 mg/l with a median value of 0.07 mg/l as shown in Fig. 7. The results of this investigation were lower than other findings in Iran (4.56–15.82 mg/l) [52] and reported in Turkey [14] and higher than those from studies conducted in Saudi Arabia, China, Turkey, and Nigeria (0.011–0.017 mg/l, 0.03–0.01 mg/l, 0.01–0.034 mg/l, 0.009–0.018 mg/l) [14, 15, 21, 59]. Lead concentrations in 17% of the oil samples were found to be higher than national, international, and legal limits recommended by FAO/WHO, which is 0.1 mg/l [39, 42, 43, 64]. The majority of the study's oil sample results fall below the permissible limit compared to prior published values. The variation in lead concentration in oil samples can be attributed to several factors. The methods used to extract and refine vegetable oils, instrumentation, and processing techniques, especially refinery, may influence the concentration difference in the oil samples. On the other hand, lead contamination can occur during storage and handling of the oil if it comes into contact with lead-containing materials. Variations in quality control measures during the production of vegetable oils might also be the reason. It can harm the developing fetal neural systems by crossing the placenta [65–67]. Lead poisoning can be acute or chronic, and it damages the kidneys, liver, heart, and vascular and immunological systems [68, 69]. Even at comparatively modest levels, lead can have serious health impacts. It can harm the developing fetal neural systems by crossing the placenta [65, 67].

A significant statistical difference was shown in copper and zinc among the samples based on their ingredients. This might be because of the oil processing method, and the natural content of metals in the oil species may affect the concentration of copper and zinc. Zinc is involved in most metabolic pathways in humans and serves as a cofactor for many enzymes. Copper is an essential trace metal important for both physical and mental development in humans. It is usually found in many food types, particularly vegetarian foods such as nuts, seeds, and grains, unlike lead and cadmium.

The limitation of the present study is that the risk associated with the detected levels of metals was not assessed, and the potential risk factors for the findings were not investigated. Additionally, the study focused exclusively on vegetable oil products, which may make it challenging to generalize the results to other types of products consumed in Gondar City.

The study also has several strengths. Firstly, the data collected was both well-representative and of high quality. Secondly, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive investigation into the levels of heavy metals and essential minerals in the study area. Thirdly, the study examined a diverse range of edible vegetable oils, providing a thorough assessment of their chemical quality.

Conclusion

According to the obtained results, the iron, copper, and zinc levels found in locally produced mixed oils in this study are within safe consumption limits, suggesting no risk of metal overload. However, the presence of cadmium and lead levels exceeding regulatory limits in a significant portion of the oil samples indicates a potential health risk for consumers, which can lead to a range of adverse health effects, including renal, pulmonary, hepatic, skeletal, reproductive, and an increased risk of cancer. Therefore, the findings suggest a need for stricter regulatory measures and monitoring of cadmium and lead levels in edible oils to ensure consumer safety. The levels should be reduced by more careful handling, processing of raw materials, and filtering practices. It is therefore necessary that the producers and marketers take necessary precautions to prevent contamination. Consequently, strict regulatory control from the responsible bodies and stakeholders is recommended regarding the safety and metal contents of vegetable oils originating in the study area.
