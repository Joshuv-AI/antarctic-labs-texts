Synthesis of amino-terminated hyperbranched polysiloxane toughener and its application in CF/EP composites
Authors: Weiwei Yang, Jia Deng, Hui Chen, Haifeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperbranched polymers are well-established for boosting the toughness of epoxy-based systems. In this work, a novel amino-terminated hyperbranched polysiloxane (HBPSi-NH2) was synthesized and then incorporated into carbon fiber/epoxy (CF/EP) composites to improve their mechanical performance. A two-step synthetic route was involved: linear epoxy-terminated polysiloxane (LDP) was initially prepared as the intermediate, and the target HBPSi-NH2 was then obtained via nucleophilic addition between LDP and diethylenetriamine. Structural characterization via FTIR and 1H NMR demonstrated that target HBPSi-NH2 was obtained, while quantitative analysis based on 13C NMR data showed that the degree of branching of the product was 0.78. The influence of adding HBPSi-NH2 as a toughener to bisphenol A epoxy resin (E51) blends was systematically investigated. The incorporation of 3 wt% HBPSi-NH2 marginally reduced tensile and flexural strengths of the toughened epoxy. However, it significantly enhanced the impact strength from 26.7 kJ m−2 to 56.2 kJ m−2. Incorporating the toughener into CF/EP composite fabrication effectively improves tensile strength, flexural strength and short-beam shear strength, especially with low HBPSi-NH2 amounts (3 and 5 wt%). This study offers a facile strategy to simultaneously improve the mechanical strength and interfacial properties of CF/EP composites.

Introduction

Epoxy resins are utilized extensively due to their excellent mechanical properties, weather resistance, chemical resistance, and adhesive qualities, making them essential in the fields of sports, automotives, aerospace, etc.1 However, certain composite materials often suffer from issues like high brittleness or low fracture resistance due to their intrinsic properties.2 Consequently, toughening epoxy resins is of interest for a number of advanced applications. Common toughening methods for epoxy resins involve both physical and chemical paths. Physical approaches typically introduce a second phase, such as rubber,3 plastic particles,4 core–shell polymers5 and nanoparticles (e.g., graphene/MXene6), thereby forming a two-phase structure to improve the toughness. However, poor compatibility between the particles and the resin may arise during the handling process; thus, ensuring the uniformity of the toughened resin system to avoid excessive phase separation remains a challenge.7 Chemical modification normally involves structural polymers to adjust the crosslinking network, such as hyperbranched polymers (HBPs),8 mono-functional linear copolymers9 and ladder-like polysilsesquioxane polymer.10 These modifiers can be uniformly dispersed in the matrix through covalent bonding with epoxy groups, delivering more stable toughening effects.11–14

Unlike some linear toughening polymers with higher intrinsic viscosity, HBPs have been widely used to toughen thermosetting epoxy resins due to their low viscosity, unique three-dimensional (3D) cavity structure, good compatibility and high reactivity, which are beneficial for the energy absorption through impact deformation.15,16 The abundant functional end groups of HBPs can be incorporated into the crosslinking network of epoxy resin to improve the miscibility of the resin matrix. This modification strategy enables effective toughening of thermoset epoxy without causing undesirable phase separation.17–19 While these hyperbranched molecules can significantly improve the toughness of the polymer matrix, the strength or modulus of the modified matrix are typically compromised due to the decreased crosslinking density and higher flexible chain amounts.

Hyperbranched polysiloxane, characterized by its siloxane backbone and diverse terminal functional groups, exhibits unique advantages that make it a suitable candidate for toughening epoxy resins. The integration of specific functional units, including aromatic moieties, heterocyclic rings and common functional groups (e.g. amino, epoxy, hydroxyl or vinyl units), has been demonstrated to effectively improve the mechanical properties, dielectric constant and low-temperature performance of epoxy resins.20,21 Devaraju et al.22 synthesized epoxy-terminated hyperbranched polysiloxane (HBPSiE) and incorporated it into diglycidyl ether of bisphenol A resin (LY 556), achieving a 26% improvement in impact strength. Pan et al.23 prepared epoxy-terminated hyperbranched polysiloxane (EPTS-12) as a toughener, and recorded an increased elongation at break at −72 °C. An amino-terminated hyperbranched polysiloxane (HBPSi) toughened epoxy resin system24 was fabricated and it has been found that the secondary amines on HBPSi molecules improved the uniformity of the epoxy network. Xiao et al.25 synthesized phosphorous/silicon hybrid hyperbranched polymer using aminoethylamino propyltrimethoxy silane and tripropylene glycol, and applied it to modify bisphenol F epoxy resin; increased impact strength and fracture toughness (KIC) were obtained.

In addition to conventional HBPs with Si–O–Si backbone, short-segment Si–O–C heterochain with different terminal groups (HBPSi) has also been investigated for epoxy modification.26 Current studies have revealed that terminal groups of HBPSi (including vinyl, epoxy and amino groups) played a critical role in regulating its aggregation and crosslinking behavior. Optimal balance can be achieved between supramolecular-driven aggregation and covalent-mediated dispersion. Similar approach was also documented in the synthesis of the coupling agent (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (KH550) and functional monomer with –NH2 and –OH.27 It was asserted that the impact strength of the modified material was 2.48 times higher than that of the unmodified specimen. This improvement in toughness was found to be ascribed to three major energy dissipation mechanisms: strong interfacial adhesion between the filler and matrix, shear yielding of the polymer matrix, and cavitation of dispersed modifier particles.28

Despite extensive research on siloxane-containing hyperbranched polymers for epoxy resin modification, few studies have involved siloxane-containing tougheners in carbon fiber-reinforced epoxy systems, a critical end application area. Linear siloxane-containing mono-functional aliphatic epoxy was introduced to improve CF/EP unidirectional and laminated composites.9 The enhanced fracture toughness and flexural strength of the composites at 77 K were detected. This phenomenon was primarily attributed to the release of partial thermal residual stress and the formation of interface debonding. The employment of polyhedral oligomeric silsesquioxane (POSS) sizing agent was introduced to enhanced the surface energy and roughness of carbon fibers, as well as the interface properties of carbon fiber reinforced polymers.29 The impregnation, chemical bonding (with epoxy) and the mechanical interlocking effects between CF and EP were all enhanced.

This work presents a custom-synthesized amino-terminated hyperbranched polysiloxane (HBPSi-NH2) with a designed low-generation topology. Leveraging the unique combination of its terminal amino groups and inherent flexible Si–O–Si segments in the hyperbranched backbone, this design addresses practical needs for carbon fiber-reinforced epoxy (CF/EP) composites: the terminal amino groups can covalently bond to the epoxy network to enhance uniformity, while the flexible Si–O–Si chains provide free volume, improve interfacial adhesion between the modified resin and carbon fibers, and enhanced chain mobility that facilitate impact energy dissipation. The toughener HBPSi-NH2 was then incorporated into the epoxy E51 matrix and carbon fiber-reinforced epoxy composites (CF/EP composites). A comprehensive characterization approach was employed, including thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA), mechanical performance evaluation through tensile strength, flexural modulus, and impact resistance tests. These analyses aimed to discuss the synergistic toughening mechanisms driven by the interaction between hyperbranched architecture and chain flexibility, offering critical insights for enhancing the performance of advanced CF/EP composites.

Materials and methods

Materials

All materials mentioned in this paper were purchased from mainland China without further purification. Terminated hydrogen silicone oil 707-30 (industrial grade, Mw ≈ 740 Da, about 10 Si–O–Si segments), ally glycidyl ether (AGE, A.R., Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd), diethylenetriamine (DETA, A.R., Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd), chloroplatinic acid (A.R., Xilong Chemical Co., Ltd), epoxy resin E51 (industrial grade, Nantong Star Synthetic Materials Co., Ltd), a polyether amine curing agent D230 (industrial grade, Changzhou Huisheng Electronic Materials Co., Ltd), the catalyst chloroplatinic acid hexahydrate H2PtCl6·6H2O (100%, Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd), isopropyl alcohol (A.R., Shanghai Maclin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd), tetrahydrofuran (A.R., Shanghai Maclin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd), deuterated chloroform (CDCl3, 99.80% D, Meryer (Shanghai) Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd), carbon fiber woven (T300, plain weave fabric, Jiangyin Tianji New Material Technology Co., Ltd).

Synthesis of HBPSi-NH2

All experiments were carried out in 250 mL four-neck flasks equipped with mechanical agitators, condensers and thermometers, and all reactions were carried out under air atmosphere with a liquid-sealed apparatus. A synthetic schematic is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Synthetic schematic of amino-terminated hyperbranched polymer.

Synthesis of linear epoxy-terminated polysiloxane

A theoretical molar ratio of 707-30 and AGE was set as 1.0 : 2.3 in this study.30 A detailed discussion of the hydrosilylation reaction conditions (temperature and reaction time) is provided in Fig. S1 and S2 in the SI. In a typical synthetic procedure, the reactant 707-30 (110.0 g, ∼0.149 mol) was charged into a flask and heated up to 100 °C at 350 rpm stir speed. Then a mixture consisting of AGE (39.0 g, 0.342 mol) and catalyst dispersion (0.29 g, 1 wt% chloroplatinic acid in isopropyl alcohol) was added dropwise to the flask. After complete addition of all reactants, the reaction was held for another 3 h and then cooled down to room temperature (r.t.), yielding linear epoxy-terminated polysiloxane (LDP). The crude product was then purified by vacuum distillation at 110 °C to remove any volatile impurity. The conversion of the Si–H group was ≥95%, as determined via iodometric titration.31

Preparation of HBPSi-NH2

A theoretical molar ratio of 1.0 : 1.8 (LDP to DETA) was employed in this step. Reducing the DETA content promotes the formation of a linear polymer, resulting in an excessively high viscosity (see in Fig. S3 of SI). DETA (23.9 g, 0.232 mol) was added into a flask, followed by the dropwise loading of LDP (120.0 g, 0.129 mol) at r.t. After the addition of the intermediate LDP, the reaction was heated up to 80 °C for another 6 h (see the discussion on reaction time-epoxy conversion in Fig. S4 of the SI), yielding a light yellow liquid HBPSi-NH2. At this point, the conversion of the epoxy group was ≥97%, as measured through perchloric acid–tetraethylammonium bromide titration.32

Preparation of the toughened carbon fiber/epoxy resin composites

Preparation of the toughened resins

Epoxy resin E51 and curing agent D230 (or the D230/HBPSi-NH2 mixture) were prepared with calculated mass ratios, as listed in Tables S1 and S2 of the SI. After vacuum degassing for 60 min, the resin mixture was poured into a silicone mold. The mold was then placed in an oven at 90 °C for 30 min, followed by sequential heating to 100 and 110 °C for 10 min intervals. Finally, the cured resin was left at r.t. for another 24 h before any mechanical or thermal characterizations. The curing degree of all toughened resins was ≥98.2%, determined through the acetone extraction method, with the corresponding data provided in Tables S3 and S4 of the SI.

Fabrication of carbon fiber reinforced epoxy resin composites

E51, D230 and the toughening agent HBPSi-NH2 were first thoroughly mixed according to the mass ratios listed in Table S2 of SI, followed by a vacuum degassing procedure. Subsequently, CF/EP composites were prepared via a vacuum assisted molding process, yielding final resin mass contents of 37.5 ± 2.5 wt%. The composites were initially left at r.t. for 24 h before undergoing a staged thermal post-curing process: 90 °C for 30 min, 100 °C for 10 min, and 110 °C for 10 min, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 2. Prior to evaluating the tensile and flexural properties, all specimens were conditioned for 48 h post-cure.

The preparation procedure of the CF/EP composites.

Characterization

Infrared spectroscopy was employed to analyze characteristic peaks of LDP through a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet i50 spectrometer. Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) was used to verify the chemical structure of LDP. Carbon nuclear magnetic resonance (13C NMR) was applied to quantitively measure the amount of varied carbon atoms to calculate degree of branching (DB) of HBPSi-NH2. 1H NMR and 13C NMR were both measured through a Bruker AVANCE III HD 400 MHz spectrometer at 25 °C, with polymers dissolved in CDCl3. The molecular weights of HBPSi-NH2 were performed via gel chromatogram analysis (GPC) on an Agilent PL-GPC50 chromatographic instrument at 30 °C, with THF used as the eluent at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1. It was calibrated with 10 narrow polystyrene (PST) standards with weight-average molecular weight (Mw) from 870 to 355 000 Da. The reported values of HBPS-NH2 were based on PST, without any correction. The thermal stability of the cured resins was measured through a NETZSCH STA 449F5 thermogravimetric analyzer at a constant heating rate of 10 °C min−1, with temperature ranging from 0 to 600 °C under N2 atmosphere. DMA was conducted in a single cantilever mode, with temperature heating from 20 to 230 °C at a frequency of 1 Hz. The dimensions of the specimens were 30 mm × 10 mm × 2 mm for DMA tests.

The tensile strength and flexural strength of the cured epoxy resins were measured with an Instron 5969H universal material testing machine, according to Chinese standard GB/T 2570-2021. The tensile specimens were dumbbell-shaped (200 mm × 20 mm × 4 mm), and the flexural specimens were rectangular-shaped, with dimensions of 150 mm × 15 mm × 4 mm. The impact strength of the cured resins was measured according to Chinese standard GB/T1843-2008 on an MTS Zbc8501-c pendulum impact tester, with a 22 J pendulum dynamic energy. The samples were rectangular-shaped, sizing of 80 mm × 10 mm × 4 mm.

The tensile strength and flexural strength of the CF/EP composites were both determined with an Instron 5969H universal material testing machine, in accordance with Chinese standards GB/T 1449-2005 and GB/T1447-2005, respectively. The dimensions of the tensile specimens were 250 mm × 25 mm × 2 mm, and those of the flexural samples were 150 mm × 15 mm × 4 mm (length × width × height), with the span being 32 times the thickness (h). According to Chinese standard GB/T 30969-2014, the short-beam shear strength (SBSS) of the composites was tested using an Instron 5982 universal material testing machine, with specimen sizes of 35 mm × 15 mm × 5 mm.

The surface morphologies of the cured resins and CF/EP composites after the impact failure and tensile breakage were both analyzed via CARL ZEISS Gemini 300 field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Prior to SEM analysis, the samples were cleaned with ethanol and coated with a thin layer of gold.

Nuclear magnetic resonance

1H NMR spectrum of LDP is shown in Fig. 4. The peaks at 0.14 and 0.5 ppm are assigned to CH3–Si– and –CH2–Si– (α-H connecting with the siloxane groups), respectively. The signal at 1.5 ppm is attributed to the chemical shift of –CH2–CH2–Si– (β-H bonding with the siloxane groups). The multiplet located at 3.3 and 3.6 ppm are assigned to –CH2–O–CH2– in the liner structure, as the peaks at 2.55, 2.74 and 3.1 ppm belong to H atoms in the epoxy groups. The characteristic peaks of Si–H from the reactant 707-30 (typically appear at 4.7 ppm) and H2CCH– from AGE (locate at 5.5 and 6.5 ppm, respectively) are barely present in the spectrum, another indication of the successful hydrosilylation reaction.33–35

GPC

The molecular weights (MWs) of HBPSi-NH2 are listed in Table 2, with the number-average molecular weight (Mn) and the weight-average molecular weight being 1600 Da and 2040 Da, respectively. These values likely underestimate the actual molecular weight, given that Mw of the silicone oil 707-30 is approximately 740 Da. This discrepancy arises because linear PST standard solutions were utilized as a universal calibration prior to GPC runs with THF. Linear standards typically underestimate MWs of hyperbranched polymers because HBPs possess a more compact hydrodynamic volume than their linear counterparts of equal mass. In addition, the molecular weight correction in GPC relies on the Mark–Houwink parameters, namely the proportional constant related to the polymer–solvent K and the empirical constant α. The two critical parameters are not universally available in the literature, as their values are inherently dependent on the HBPSi-NH2 polymeric structure. Consequently, the original MW values obtained from the GPC analysis are presented in this study without correction.

Thermal properties

The thermal stability of the cured resins with varied HBPSi-NH2 amounts was analyzed, as depicted in Fig. 6a and b. The initial decomposition temperature (Td5, representing 5 wt% weight loss of the solidified epoxy) of the TGA samples is almost identical, as only a slight increase (from 332 to 336 °C) was observed (see Table 3). The thermal degradation profiles of the resins within 100–350 °C are predominantly attributed to the breakage of C–H and C–O bonds in the polymeric matrix. Above 400 °C, the dissociation of the C–C bonds in the main-chain and crosslinked structures becomes the dominant degradation segments. When temperature exceeds 500 °C, the residual components primarily consist of the thermally stable Si–O–Si units derived from HBPSi-NH2. Residue char yield gradually rises from 11.6% (the neat sample) to 15.9%. This phenomenon is probably due to the fact that the Si–O groups form a silica protective layer at high temperature, which effectively inhibits the oxygen diffusion and reduces the decomposition rate.40

DMA characteristics

The dynamic mechanical properties of the toughened resins are illustrated in Fig. 7 and Table 4. The decrease in storage modulus (E′) is primarily due to the introduction of numerous flexible segments within the molecule's structure and alterations to the network architecture. HBPSi-NH2 molecules contain Si–O–Si and C–C–O ether bonds. These flexible units have lower rotational barriers and higher segmental mobility than the original epoxy resins. When these flexible segments are incorporated into the epoxy curing network via ring-opening polymerization, they act as molecular-level hinges, reducing resistance to segmental motion throughout the network. Consequently, the E′–T curve shifts downwards.28 The variation in the loss modulus (E″) reflects changes in the material's internal dissipation behavior and relaxation mechanisms. Due to the presence of soft segments within HBPSi-NH2, an increase in segment-coordinated motion and frictional losses is anticipated. Consequently, the primary E″ peak associated with the glass transition occurs at lower temperatures, and the peak shape of the toughened epoxy broadens, as compared with that of the neat sample (see in Fig. 7b). This indicates that the glass transition process covers a wider temperature range, with greater energy dissipation.

Dynamic mechanical properties of the cured epoxy

The shape of the tan δ curve represents the uniformity of the cured resins.41 Unimodal curves are seen for all cured resins in Fig. 7a, showing good compatibility of the toughened epoxy systems, a beneficial result of the incorporation between the terminal amino and epoxy groups. The tan δ curves exhibit a gradual shift toward lower temperature region, an indication of reduced Tg, which is derived directly from the peak value of the tan δ curve. The decrease in Tg is dominated by the plasticizing effect of excessive flexible chain segments, along with the formation of micro-phase separation structures.

In Table 4, below 100 °C, all modified samples retain a high glassy-state modulus (Eg, ranging from 1800 MPa to 2110 MPa), albeit slightly lower than that of the neat resin (2674 MPa). This reduction arises from the hyperbranched cavity topology, which increases the free volume and facilitates the molecular chain mobility, offsetting the crosslinking density improvement. A sharp modulus drop is observed around 150 °C, indicating that the cured products undergo a phase transformation (from the glassy state to the rubbery state). Above 200 °C, the toughened resins exhibit higher rubbery-state modulus (Er) than that of the neat sample, showing improved thermal stability.

Mechanical properties of the cured resins

The mechanical properties of the cured resins are summarized in Table 5. The tensile and flexural strengths of the toughened specimens exhibit an inverse correlation with the HBPSi-NH2 loading. At lower HBP amounts of 3 wt% and 5 wt%, the tensile strength decreases from 53.7 MPa to 50.3 MPa, while the flexural strength drops from 103.7 MPa to 77.6 MPa. Further increasing the toughener amount (8 wt% and 10 wt%) leads to even lower tensile and flexural strengths. Fundamentally, the mechanical performance of cured epoxy systems is governed by the synergistic effects of three core factors: the molecular chain rigidity, molecular spatial conformation within the cured resins, and the overall crosslinking density.42 Flexible Si–O–Si and C–C–O molecular segments introduce a pronounced plasticizing effect. Meanwhile, the intrinsic cavity architecture of HBPSi-NH2 weakens intermolecular interactions within the epoxy matrix. Taken together, these two factors impair the mechanical strength of the toughened resins.

Mechanical properties of the toughened epoxy resins

With the 3 wt% HBPSi-NH2 addition, impact strength of the toughened resin increases to 56.2 kJ m−2, 110% higher than that of the neat sample (26.7 kJ m−2). This remarkable improvement is primarily attributed to two key structural features introduced during modification: flexible Si–O–Si segments covalently integrated into the crosslinked network, and the intrinsic free volume generated by the hyperbranched topological structure. Notably, DMA analysis reveals that a slight increase in crosslinking density upon incorporation of low loadings of HBPSi-NH2 (3 and 5 wt%). A higher ve would create more densely packed crosslinking points that restrict long-range molecular chain mobility, leaving insufficient free volume for chain stretching and energy dissipation; this effect is conventionally understood to increase internal stress and brittleness of the matrix. However, the unique structure of HBPSi-NH2 counteracts this effect: the flexible Si–O–Si backbone and C–O segment provide additional local free volume around the hyperbranched core, which compensates the mobility restriction from increased crosslink density, thereby retaining sufficient molecular mobility for impact energy dissipation. In addition, the intramolecular cavities act as energy-absorbing sites that effectively dissipate mechanical energy through localized deformation and molecular chain relaxation.27 Further increasing the modifier amount in the cured samples leads to a gradual decline in impact strength, yet all formulations with higher HBPSi-NH2 content (≥5 wt%) still exhibit much higher impact strength than that of the neat epoxy.43

Mechanical properties of the CF/EP composites

Although the introduction of HBPSi-NH2 toughener leads to a certain degree of reduction in the tensile and flexural strengths of cured resins, the significantly improved impact strength fully validates its excellent toughening potential, which motivated us to further apply this toughener for the preparation of CF/EP composites. As summarized in Table 6, the incorporated HBPSi-NH2 effectively reinforces both tensile and flexural properties of the resulting composites, which stands in contrast to the strength degradation trend observed in the toughened resin systems. Quantitatively, the tensile strength of the modified CF/EP composites exhibits an upward trend at lower HBPSi-NH2 loadings, with numerical values of CF-3 wt%, CF-5 wt% and CF-8 wt% all surpassing 531 MPa, before decreasing to 429 MPa of CF-10 wt% at a high loading. It is worth mentioning that every HBPSi-NH2 modified composite shows a higher tensile strength than that of CF-Neat (425.5 MPa, with stress–strain curves depicted in Fig. S6 of the SI). The flexural strength of the carbon fiber composites follows a similar profile, which increases from 308 MPa of CF-neat to a maximum of 539 MPa (CF-3 wt%).

Mechanical properties of the CF/EP composites

The strength reinforcement may originate from the unique interfacial property modulated by HBPSi-NH2. The siloxane moieties enhance the interfacial affinity between the toughened epoxy resin and carbon fiber.44 During the composite fabrication process, the uncured epoxy liquid mixture may spontaneously adsorb onto the carbon fiber surface. After curing, this affinity forms a thin layer of organic-inorganic hybrid interface containing Si–O–Si structures and creates micro/nano-scale roughness on the originally smooth carbon fiber surface. Such roughed structure can enlarges the contact area between the fiber and the resin, and generate stronger mechanical interlocking between the two phases, which effectively suppresses interface debonding under external stress. The modified CF/EP composites exhibit significantly improved overall mechanical strength, as the enhanced interfacial interaction enables more efficient stress transfer from the relative ductile epoxy matrix to the high-strength carbon fibers under external loading. Meanwhile, this process promotes the stress distribution across the entire matrix, thereby preventing crack propagation.43 The combination of these mutually reinforcing mechanisms ultimately contribute to the enhancement of the mechanical performances of the CF/EP composites.45

SBSS of the CF/EP composites is summarized in Table 6. A slight increase in SBSS is observed for CF-5wt%, compared to that of CF-neat (from 35.5 MPa to 37.2 MPa). This interfacial strength improvement may be attributed to the unique molecular architecture of the modifier. At lower additions (≤5 wt%), the branched topological structure of the HBPSi-NH2, together with its flexible siloxane segments, may form controllable stress dissipation sites within the resin matrix. These sites dissipate the force by promoting micro-silver streak propagation, as well as triggering the formation of local shear band formation. However, this reinforcing effect seems to be highly dependent on concentration, as a noticeable decline in SBSS is noticed when the HBP content is higher than 8 wt%. This degradation occurs because the excessive flexible chain segments may weaken the interfacial stress transfer efficiency between the matrix and carbon fibers. In addition, the polar characteristics of hyperbranched molecules may trigger local micro-phase separation at high content, eventually forming micron-scale agglomeration defects inside the composite. Ultimately, these defects act as crack nucleation points under shear loading, causing premature interfacial debonding failure of the CF/EP composites.46

Micro-structure of fracture surfaces

The impact fracture morphologies of the cured epoxy are presented in Fig. 8. As illustrated in Fig. 8a, the surface of the neat sample is relatively smooth, a characteristic feature of brittle fracture. In the presence of HBPSi-NH2, the ductility and deformability of the toughened resins appear to be enhanced, resulting in the ductile fracture mode at the micro-scale, as demonstrated in Fig. 8b–f. The impact fracture surfaces of all modified resins show a silver-grain expansion phenomenon, with a river-like pattern formed by crack propagation and migration. Additionally, the scale-like structures with varied sizes may improve micro-phase separation, leading to better energy dissipation capability and consequently enhancing the toughness of the modified resins.10,47

Micro-structure of tensile fracture surface of CF/EP composites

As depicted in Fig. 9a, the tensile fracture morphology of CF-neat composite is relatively smooth, indicating a weaker interfacial adhesion between the carbon fiber and the epoxy matrix. With the increasing loadings of HBPSi-NH2, the fracture surfaces of the toughened composites (see in Fig. 9b–d) exhibit a hybrid interfacial phase, which improves the bonding force between CF and the resin. This optimized interfacial structure facilitates integrated bonding between the fiber and matrix, thereby reducing the extent of fiber damage at the break point, increasing the resistance to fiber pull-out, and generating rougher morphology on the residual fiber surfaces. However, as shown in Fig. 9e, the fiber-matrix debonding phenomenon upon 10 wt% addition deteriorates. This may be due to the micro-phase separation induced by the presence of excessive HBPSi-NH2 content, which disrupts the continuity of the resin matrix and ultimately weakens the interfacial bonding with carbon fibers.48

Conclusions

In this work, amino-terminated hyperbranched polysiloxane was successfully synthesized, with a degree of branching equaling to 0.78. Toughened epoxy resin and carbon fiber/epoxy (CF/EP) composites were both prepared using this modifier. It has been found that a relatively low HBPSi-NH2 content (3 and 5 wt%) was beneficial for both systems. At the optimal 3 wt% loading of HBPSi-NH2, the toughened resin achieved a remarkable 110% increase in impact strength while experiencing slight losses in tensile and flexural strengths. Unlike the minor strength sacrifices observed in the epoxy resin, the introduction of HBPSi-NH2 toughener simultaneously enhances the tensile and flexural strengths of CF/EP composites. This performance divergence can be attributed to the improved interfacial affinity between the modified epoxy matrix and carbon fibers, prompting the in situ formation of a rough, siloxane-rich organic–inorganic hybrid interphase that optimizes mechanical interlocking and stress-transfer efficiency. Overall, this study provides a practical and effective strategy for synchronously improving the strength and interfacial adhesion of CF/EP composites, exhibiting great application potential in advanced structural materials.
