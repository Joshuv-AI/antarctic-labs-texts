The role of sodium in the electrochemical tuning of Li2TiSiO5 anodes across ceramic and glass phases
Authors: Awadol Khejonrak, Amorntep Montreeuppathum, Sumeth Siriroj, Jintara Padchasri, Sarawut Pasee, Narong Chanlek, Soorathep Kheawhom, Pinit Kidkhunthod
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the enhancement of Li2TiSiO5 anode material through Na doping via two routes: melt-quenching (route I) and subsequent heat treatment (route II). A 5 % Na-doped ceramic sample significantly improves Li-ion mobility and discharge capacity (215 mA h g−1 at 10 mA g−1), sustaining 45 mA h g−1 at a high rate of 1 A g−1. However, higher doping levels hinder performance, indicating Li-ion path obstruction and non-conductive impurities. Intriguingly, undoped Li2TiSiO5 glass exhibits superior electrochemical performance, with a discharge capacity of 340 mA h g−1 at 10 mA g−1 and high-rate endurance (81 mA h g−1 at 1 A g−1). This research provides insights into phase-dependent optimization, highlighting the glass phase's inherent benefits for Li-ion diffusion. It addresses a significant research gap, offering critical understanding for advancing high-energy-density anode materials in next-generation batteries.Keywords: Li2-xNaxTiSiO5, Na-doping, Electrochemical performance, XAS

Introduction

The burgeoning demand for energy storage solutions with high energy density and extended cycle life has intensified the search for advanced anode materials for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). While graphite anodes dominate the market, their inherent safety risks and limited capacity have spurred investigations into alternative materials [1,2]. Lithium titanate-based compounds, such as Li4Ti5O12, have emerged as promising candidates due to their exceptional stability [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. However, their low specific capacity and high operating voltage restrict their widespread adoption [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

To address these limitations, Li2TiSiO5 (LTSO) has garnered significant attention [13]. Its unique three-dimensional structure enables efficient lithium-ion diffusion, leading to improved electrochemical performance [14]. Nevertheless, LTSO suffers from low electrical conductivity, hindering its practical application. The incorporation of sodium (Na) as a dopant offers a potential solution to enhance ionic conductivity and overall battery performance [15,16].

Despite its amorphous structure, glass exhibits unique properties that make it a promising candidate for energy storage applications [17]. Its disordered network provides a high density of active sites, enabling efficient lithium-ion intercalation [18]. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of glass-based electrodes for lithium-ion batteries [[19], [20], [21]]. For instance, Zhang et al. [22] reported enhanced cycling stability in vanadium tellurite glass anodes due to the formation of nanocrystals within the glass matrix. Similarly, Jiahui Fan et al. [23] showed that Al2O3 doping in a V2O5-TeO2 glass system improved conductivity by creating a more open structure. Lee et al. [24] highlighted the superior lithium-ion embedding capability of boro-phosphate glass electrodes, achieving high initial capacities.

Another pivotal benefit of glass electrodes, when compared to their crystalline counterparts, is the ease with which the glass structure can be tailored by adjusting the glass composition, thereby enhancing their electrochemical performance [25]. However, a notable challenge in the utilization of glass anodes is their vulnerability to rapid capacity degradation during discharge and charge cycles. This issue is primarily associated with the heightened polarization and sluggish diffusion of both Li+ ions and electrons within the active materials, despite their superior gravimetric and volumetric capacities [[26], [27], [28]]. These concerns present a significant impediment to the viable application of glass anodes in Lithium-ion Batteries (LIBs). Consequently, a promising approach for producing high-performance anodes for Li-ion batteries could involve combining Li2TiSiO5 with its glass phase.

Recently, the utilization of glass ceramics based on Li2TiSiO5 as an anode material has been reported, showcasing a specific capacity of approximately 180 mA h g−1 over 5000 cycles at 5 A g−1 [29]. The open network structure of these glass ceramics empowers them to withstand volume changes and the stresses resulting from the insertion and extraction of Li+ ions. Furthermore, the presence of nanocrystals establishes efficient pathways for rapid ion delivery and electron transport. Despite the promising results, there is a paucity of reports and investigations concerning Li2TiSiO5 glass electrodes, emphasizing the need for further research to unlock the full potential of Li2TiSiO5-based glass and glass ceramics as anode materials. Their exceptional performance and stability hold significant promise for advanced energy storage technologies. Future studies could shed more light on their electrochemical mechanisms, paving the way for practical applications in next-generation batteries and energy storage devices.

This research presents two synthesis methodologies for Li2-xNaxTiSiO5, termed Route I (melt-quenching) and Route II (melt-quenching followed by heat treatment), aimed to enhancing the anodic capacity of Li2TiSiO5 through sodium (Na) doping and glass phase integration. X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) is employed to examine the local atomic structure, while X-ray Diffraction (XRD) is used for analyzing the crystalline structure. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) reveals the microstructure, and electrochemical tests assess the anode potential for LIBs, including capacity, stability, and rate capability. This comprehensive approach provides a deep understanding of Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 materials, contributing to future LIBs. Experimental results confirm the improved Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 anodic performance in Route I, attributed to Na-doping, and the higher performance in Route II, owing to the beneficial glass phase formation. This study advances material engineering for LIBs, offering valuable insights.

Experimental

Materials preparations

A series of Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 (x = 0, 2 %, 5 %, 7.5 %, and 10 %) base glass electrode materials were prepared using the melt-quenching method. The materials consisted of 80 mol% Li2O, TiO2, SiO2, and Na2O mixed in stoichiometric ratios, along with 10 mol% K2O and 10 mol% B2O3 as modified according to the previous study [29]. The batch of Li2CO3, K2CO3, TiO2, H3BO3, SiO2, and Na2CO3 which are analytical reagent-grade, was melted in a platinum crucible at 1400 °C for 1 h in air. Subsequently, the melt was quenched by pouring it into cold water. The pristine and doped glass samples were labeled as 0%Na-LTSO-g, 2.5%Na-LTSO- g, 5%Na-LTSO- g, 7.5%Na-LTSO- g, and 10%Na-LTSO- g, respectively. Then, the glass powder was annealed at 870 °C for 1 h in air, at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. Thus, the ceramic samples were obtained and labeled as 0%Na-LTSO-c, 2.5%Na-LTSO-c, 5%Na-LTSO-c, 7.5%Na-LTSO-c, and 10%Na-LTSO-c, respectively.

Materials characterizations

X-ray diffraction experiments were performed using the Bruker D2 X-ray diffractometer Cu Kα radiation with a 40 kV applied voltage and 30 mA anode current. The XAS experiments, including XANES and EXAFS, were performed at BL5.2: SUT-NANOTEC-SLRI XAS, SLRI, Thailand. The measurements at the Ti K-edge were made in transmission mode using the monochromator employing Ge (220) crystal pairs at room temperature. The SEM images of the prepared Li2TiSiO5 powders were taken by using field emission scanning electron microscope with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (FE-SEM, Carl Zeiss-AURIGA).

Electrochemical measurements

Active materials, conductive acetylene black and polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) binder were dispersed in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP) with a weight ratio of 7:2:1. The slurry of the electrode ingredients were doctor-bladed (set at a height of 300 μm) onto the Cu foil current collector and dried at 80 °C for 12 h. Then the film was cut into 16 mm diameter discs and stored in a dry box for future use as the cathode in CR2032 coil-cell test. The anode was lithium foil with a polypropylene separator and 1 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate (EC): ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) (3:7) was used as an electrolyte. The instruction of Coin cell CR20132 assembly as shown in Fig. 2d. The battery charging and discharging performance in the voltage range of 0.1–3.0 V (vs Li/Li+) was investigated using a battery test system (Neware BTS4000). The electrochemical workstation (SP-300 Potentiostat: BioLogic) was employed to perform electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS).

Results and discussion

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, as presented in Fig. 1, reveals distinct structural differences between the Na-doped and undoped Li2TiSiO5 glass and ceramic samples. The Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 glass samples exhibit broad diffraction peaks, which are characteristic of a highly amorphous phase (see Fig. 1a). This observation unequivocally confirms the amorphous nature of the glass samples. On the other hand, the ceramic samples of Li2TiSiO5 display sharp peaks (see Fig. 1b) that align well with the tetragonal phase of LTSO (Li2TiSiO5), as referenced by the standard card (JCPDS No. 89–6365; space group: P4/nmm). These peaks also indicate the coexistence of minor amorphous and rutile phases within the ceramic structure. The synthesis process, particularly the melt-quenching followed by thermal activation, is successful in yielding a composite structure with both crystalline and amorphous characteristics [13].

X-ray diffraction peak patterns of Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 (a) glass phase and (b) ceramic phase. α = Li2TiSiO5, β = Cu current collector, γ = rutile.

Electrochemical evaluations were meticulously conducted to discern the influence of sodium (Na) doping on the dynamics of lithium-ion intercalation. This study also delved into the ramifications of Na substitution on the diffusion kinetics of lithium ions within the solid-solution Li-diffusion matrix. These evaluations were executed over a potential span of 0.1–3 V. Fig. 2 presents a detailed depiction of the electrochemical behavior of the Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 series, comprising both glass and ceramic matrices, with sodium compositions varying from 0 % to 10 %. The data encompasses a potential window stretching from 0.1 to 3 V, while the materials were subjected to progressively increasing current densities in the range of 0.2–1 A g−1. This figure serves as a testament to the alterations in kinetic properties induced by varying levels of Na doping, providing insights into the potential enhancement of electrochemical performance due to these modifications.

In the comparative analysis of the Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 ceramic samples, delineated in Fig. 2a, the sample with a 5 % sodium doping (5%Na-LTSO-c) demonstrated superior electrochemical performance across all examined rates. Notably, this sample manifested a discharge-specific capacity of 215 mA h g−1 when tested at a high current density of 10 mA g−1. Furthermore, at 1 A g−1, the 5%Na-LTSO-c sample upheld an impressive high-rate performance, delivering a capacity of 45 mA h g−1. In stark contrast, the undoped LTSO-c sample and other Na-modified LTSO-c variants exhibited lower specific capacities, all falling beneath the 200 mA h g−1 threshold, and failed to sustain high-rate capacities above 35 mA h g−1. These findings are corroborated by the work of Yueni Mei et al. [14], which postulates that the integration of sodium can substantially enhance lithium-ion mobility. The strategic incorporation of sodium into lithium sites is conjectured to expand the cellular volume, culminating in the augmentation of ionic channels that facilitate more expeditious lithium-ion migration. Nevertheless, there exists a delicate balance, as an overabundance of sodium ions may inadvertently congest the migration pathways or introduce deleterious impurities, leading to an overall decline in capacity [30].

The open network structure, pivotal for enhanced electrochemical performance, is not exclusively derived from sodium doping; the intrinsic morphology of the glass phase naturally exhibits such a structural configuration [31]. The electrochemical behaviors of the Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 glass variants are illustrated in Fig. 2b. Notably, the undoped 0%Na-LTSO-g sample outperformed its counterparts by delivering an exemplary discharge-specific capacity of 340 mA h g−1 at a current density of 10 mA g−1, and it maintained a high-rate capacity of 81 mA h g−1 even at 1 A g−1. This capacity exceeds that of the 5%Na-LTSO-c sample. Conversely, the specific capacity of the Na-enriched LTSO-g samples declined markedly, aligning closer to the performance metrics of the Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 ceramic iterations. This striking divergence in performance between the glass and ceramic samples merits further examination, which we intend to pursue in forthcoming studies.

The galvanostatic charge/discharge profiles for the Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 series, spanning from undoped to 10 % sodium doping, are depicted in Fig. 2c. In these profiles, a slight decrement in the potential during the discharge process is observed across all doping levels, providing a marked contrast to the constant operational potential of 1.55 V vs. Li+/Li upheld by the Li4Ti5O12 anode. In comparison, graphite maintains an operational potential at the lower verge of 0.1 V vs. Li+/Li [29]. Thus, the Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 matrix emerges as a potential candidate for determining battery capacity by percentage, primarily based on the state of charge (SoC), which depends on voltage and capacity traits.

The characteristics of Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 indicate its compatibility with LiFePO4 (LFP) batteries, wherein the LFP cathode maintains a stable potential of 3.45 V [30] that sharply drops upon nearing depletion of capacity. This property of LFP batteries complements the operational profile of Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 anodes, which can provide a precise measure of the state of charge (SoC) due to their voltage and capacity relationship. Consequently, Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 emerges as a promising candidate for enhancing the accuracy of SoC estimations in LFP-based battery systems.

The elucidation of titanium's local coordination was achieved through advanced spectroscopic techniques: X-ray Near-Edge Spectroscopy (XANES) and Extended X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (EXAFS) spectroscopy. XANES spectra underwent calibration against the Ti foil standard, with alignment predicated upon the pre-edge peak positions. These peaks are indicative of a quantum mechanically forbidden transition from the 1s orbital to the 3d orbital. The pre-edge peak's intensity is reflective of the filling degree of the 3d orbital, which in turn provides insights into the titanium species present and their oxidation states. Furthermore, the coordination environment of titanium influences the intensity of these pre-edge peaks; notably, in an octahedral coordination, the transition from the 1s to the 3d orbital is typically forbidden, hence a negligible pre-edge peak is observed. The white-line peaks, alternatively, correspond to transitions from the 1s to the 4p orbitals [31].

The XANES spectra normalized at the Ti K-edge for various concentrations of Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 glass, as depicted in Fig. 3, indicate that alterations in the Na-to-Li ratio do not significantly perturb the spectra, suggesting the electronic structure of Ti remains unchanged with varying Na doping levels.

XANES spectra of Li2-xNaxTiSiO5-g (x = 0, 2.5 %, 5 %, 7.5 %, and 10 %) samples.

EXAFS spectroscopy provided further insights, as illustrated in the k3-weighted Fourier-transformed EXAFS spectra at the Ti K-edge for Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 compositions (ranging from x = 0–10 %), showcased in Fig. 4. The spectra discern two principal peaks that correspond to Ti-O and Ti-Si bonding interactions. Specifically, the foremost peaks are attributed to the coordination of titanium with one oxygen atom (Ti-O1) and four oxygen atoms (Ti-O2) respectively. The EXAFS fitting results depicted in the figures indicate a discernible contraction in the bond lengths of Ti-O1, Ti-O2, and Ti-Si in Li2−xNaxTiSiO5, with these distances decreasing in tandem with increasing Na concentration. Fig. 5 offers a schematic representation of the LTSO crystal structure, both pre- and post-Na substitution, illustrating the structural changes imparted by Na doping. Contrary to the expected outcome of network expansion, Na incorporation into the Li2TiSiO5 glass matrix seems to induce structural distortion, resulting in the entrapment of Li+ ions within the network. This structural constriction is corroborated by galvanostatic charge/discharge tests, which exhibit a reduction in capacity as the Na content is augmented.

The schematic illustration of the crystal structure of LTSO before and after Na substitution.

These observations present a stark contrast to those reported by Yueni Mei et al., where Na doping was shown to enhance the rate performance of Li2TiSiO5 ceramic electrode materials [14]. Our findings suggest a complex interplay between sodium concentration and the structural dynamics of the LTSO glass matrix, underscoring the nuanced effects of Na substitution on the electrochemical characteristics of these materials.

The morphological characteristics of the Na-doped Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 glass samples are presented in Fig. S1. Examination through field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) has disclosed particle morphologies with a general size distribution ranging from 5 to 20 μm. These primary particles appear to be encased within a matrix of fine carbon black particles. Notably, the Na doping level seems to exert no significant alteration on the morphology, as evidenced by the similarity in shapes across all doped samples.

The compositional analysis, as depicted in Fig. S2 through energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping, affirms the incorporation of Na, Ti, Si, O, and K elements in the 10 % Na-doped Li2TiSiO5 glass sample (10Na-LTSO-g), intermingled with C and F elements deriving from the carbon black and PVDF, respectively. The EDX spectral analysis corroborates the uniform dispersion of these elements within the glass matrix, substantiating the efficacy of the doping process in achieving a homogeneous distribution of sodium within the Li2TiSiO5 glass framework. This uniformity is crucial for ensuring consistent electrochemical performance throughout the material.

Table 2 presents the atomic percentages of the Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 glass samples with varying Na content (x = 0, 2.5 %, 5 %, 7.5 %, and 10 %). The data indicate a proportional escalation in the atomic percentage of sodium corresponding to the incremental levels of doping, which is consistent with the expected outcome of the preparation methodology. However, the 7.5 % Na-LTSO-g sample has a lower Na percentage than the 5 % Na-LTSO-g sample. This suggests an irregular distribution of Na on the surface. Despite these variations, the atomic presence of the B elements in the base glass composition remains undetectable. This absence might imply that the B elements are present in sub-detection limit concentrations or could potentially be masked by the predominance of other elements within the complex glass matrix.

The morphological stability of Li2−xNaxTiSiO5 glass post-35 electrochemical cycles is depicted in Fig. S3. FE-SEM images highlight the retention of surface morphology, despite the apparent residue from the electrolyte. The structural integrity of Li2−xNaxTiSiO5 appears to be preserved across repeated cycling, indicating robustness against morphological degradation. Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy mapping, presented in Fig. S4, confirms the persistent presence of Na, Ti, Si, O, and K elements within the 10 % Na-doped LTSO-glass sample. The surrounding matrix, comprising carbon from carbon black and fluorine and phosphorus originating from the binder (PVDF) and electrolyte, suggests an undisturbed elemental distribution even after substantial cycling. This elemental consistency is indicative of the material's durability and its potential resilience in battery applications.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to elucidate the effects of sodium incorporation on lithium-ion transfer kinetics within Li2TiSiO5 (LTSO) based materials. The EIS measurements, spanning a frequency spectrum from 0.1 Hz to 200 kHz prior to and subsequent to electrochemical cycling, are graphically represented in Fig. 6. Within the Nyquist plots, the discernible semicircles in the mid-frequency domain are indicative of the charge transfer resistance (Rct), a parameter that characterizes the impedance to lithium-ion transport across the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) and at the electrode-electrolyte boundary. This resistance is integral to the overall electrochemical behavior of the cell, influencing both the kinetics and efficiency of the battery's charge-transfer processes. An equivalent circuit model was utilized to fit the impedance data, facilitating the quantification of Rct alongside other pertinent electrochemical parameters. Fig. 6c and (d) show the real part of the resistance at low frequency and the inverse square root of frequency before and after the test, respectively. The slope, determined as the Warburg impedance diffusion coefficient, can be used to calculate the lithium-ion diffusion coefficient [32] (the equation detailed in the supplementary section). The charge transfer resistance (Rct), as quantified through EIS, serves as a pivotal metric for evaluating the kinetics of electrochemical reactions within a cell. An inverse relationship is observed between Rct and ionic transfer kinetics: a reduced Rct signifies more rapid ionic movement, which is synonymous with enhanced electrochemical performance. Table 3 presents a comparative analysis of Rct values before and after 35 cycles, revealing a diminution in Rct post-cycling. The EIS results show that 5-Na-LTSO-g exhibits the lowest Rct and the highest lithium-ion diffusion coefficient during cycling, consistent with the highest capacity observed in battery tests. This aligns with previous research [[33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38]]. This decline is indicative of improved electronic conductivity and is hypothesized to result from the in-situ formation of nanocrystalline domains during the cyclic charging and discharging of the cell. Such structural evolution, as elucidated by Shujiang Liu et al. [29], fosters the creation of conductive pathways that are instrumental in bolstering stability and capacity retention over successive cycles.

Conclusions

The present investigation delves into the ramifications of sodium (Na) incorporation on the electrochemical attributes of Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 glass and ceramic substrates, thereby assessing their viability for integration into energy storage technologies. Synthesized via a melt-quenching technique, samples with variable Na content (x = 0, 2.5 %, 5 %, 7.5 %, and 10 %) were scrutinized for their distinct electrochemical responses. Notably, the 0%Na-LTSO-g glass variant demonstrated a remarkable discharge-specific capacity, underscoring its promise in discharge applications. Concurrently, the 5%Na-LTSO-c ceramic exhibited favorable Li-ion mobility. When considering the potential of Li2-xNaxTiSiO5 in Li-ion battery frameworks, especially in tandem with LiFePO4 cathodes, the findings are particularly promising. Morphological evaluation through FE-SEM revealed that Na doping does not alter the nanoparticle morphology inherent to these materials. Insights gleaned from EIS postulate an augmentation in ionic transfer kinetics upon repeated cycling, a phenomenon potentially ascribed to the emergence of electronic conductivity and nanocrystalline structures. This study lays the groundwork for subsequent exploration aimed at refining the electrochemical performance of these materials, thereby advancing the development of cutting-edge energy storage solutions. An in-depth understanding of the interplay between Na doping levels and the structural and electrochemical characteristics of these materials is crucial for their application in the next generation of Li-ion batteries.
