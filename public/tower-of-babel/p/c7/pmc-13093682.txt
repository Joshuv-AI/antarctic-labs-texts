Pediatric Non‐Lupus Full House Nephropathy: Case Report and Review of Literature
Authors: Mohammad Firoz Anjum, Suraj Sah, Inesh Khanal, Sharmila Gainju, Sri Ram K.C.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLupus nephritis is a severe manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) typically characterized by glomerular “full‐house” immunofluorescence. However, non‐lupus nephropathies may occasionally exhibit similar patterns, creating diagnostic uncertainty. Non‐lupus full‐house nephropathy (FHN) is a recently recognized entity in which a full‐house immunofluorescence pattern occurs in the absence of serological or clinical evidence of SLE. We report a 4‐year‐old girl who presented with generalized edema, hematuria, hypertension, and nephrotic‐range proteinuria. Laboratory evaluation revealed anemia, elevated serum creatinine, and low complement C3 levels but negative antinuclear antibody (ANA) and anti‐double‐stranded DNA (anti‐dsDNA) tests. Renal biopsy demonstrated diffuse proliferative glomerulonephritis consistent with lupus nephritis Class IV, with full‐house immunofluorescence positivity for IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, and C1q. In the absence of systemic or serologic lupus features, a diagnosis of non‐lupus full‐house nephropathy was made. The patient was treated with pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisolone, monthly cyclophosphamide for 6 months, and maintenance therapy with azathioprine and low‐dose steroids. Hydroxychloroquine and antihypertensive agents were added. She achieved clinical and biochemical remission on follow‐up.

Introduction

Lupus nephritis, a severe manifestation of Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), is characterized by glomerular full‐house immunofluorescence and endothelial tubuloreticular inclusions [1]. However, non‐lupus nephropathies can sometimes mimic the glomerular full house immunofluorescence [1]. Childhood SLE (cSLE) is a rare disease with a high disease burden. This condition has been reported with prevalence of 3.3–8.8 per 100,000 children in Asia [2]. A kidney biopsy demonstrating a full‐house immunofluorescence pattern—concurrent positive staining for immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, IgM) and complement proteins (C3, and C1q), in the absence of autoantibodies (such as ANA or anti‐dsDNA) and without the typical clinical features of SLE, despite histological findings consistent with lupus nephritis, is now classified as non‐lupus full‐house nephropathy (FHN) [3]. Non‐lupus FHN presents a diagnostic challenge, requiring further studies for definitive quantification of its incidence and prevalence [2]. Here, we present a case of a 4 year female with non‐lupus FHN.

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenges posed by pediatric FHN without serologic or clinical evidence of SLE. Early recognition and long term follow‐up are crucial as some of these patients may later develop SLE or other immune‐mediated renal diseases.

Case History/Examination

A previously healthy 4‐year‐old girl presented with a 3‐day history of generalized edema and hematuria at a local hospital in Karnali. There was no family history of autoimmune disease, renal disorders, or consanguinity. Her initial labs showed anemia (Hemoglobin: 9.9 g/dL) and azotemia (urea 120 mg/dL, creatinine 2.0 mg/dL). Despite treatment for presumed acute glomerulonephritis with antibiotics, diuretics and prednisolone, she developed worsening edema and respiratory distress requiring transfer to our center. She weighed 22 kg at the time of initial presentation.

On admission to our center, she had marked edema, hypertension (BP in the 95th to 99th centile), and worsening renal function (urea 164 mg/dL). Urinalysis revealed active sediment with 52 RBCs/HPF and nephrotic‐range proteinuria with a protein creatinine ratio of 7.03. Immunology workup showed hypocomplementemia (low C3), normal C4, but negative ANA, anti‐dsDNA, and other lupus serologies. Her Chest X‐ray and echocardiography were normal.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Renal biopsy demonstrated diffuse proliferative glomerulonephritis, pathologically diagnostic for lupus nephritis class IV (Figure 1).

Diffuse proliferative glomerulonephritis with presence of neutrophils with mild acute tubular injury.

Immunofluorescence was done in 23 viable glomeruli which concurrently tested positive for immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, IgM) and complement proteins (C3 and C1q). The Anti‐Streptolysin O (ASO) titer was 130 IU/mL.

This created a diagnostic dilemma as the patient lacked both extrarenal manifestations and serologic evidence of SLE, raising questions about whether this represented early/isolated lupus nephritis, a lupus mimic, or a distinct non‐lupus immune‐complex glomerulonephritis with overlapping features. The case highlights the challenges in diagnosing pediatric renal disease with discordant clinical, serological, and pathological findings.

The patient was treated with IV methylprednisolone 20 mg/kg/day for 5 days followed by oral prednisolone initially at the dose of 1 mg/kg/day which was gradually tapered to a maintenance dose of 5 mg once daily over 6 months. She was also given cyclophosphamide 750 mg/m2 monthly for 6 months. She was also given hydroxychloroquine, amlodipine, and enalapril. Hydroxychloroquine 200 mg once daily was added as a steroid sparing agent. Blood pressure was controlled with amlodipine 5 mg once daily and enalapril 2.5 mg twice daily. Following initiation of therapy, the patient's edema subsided within 2 weeks, hematuria resolved by 1 month, and proteinuria decreased to trace levels by 3 months. Renal function normalized by the end of the third month. The patient was then discharged and kept under close follow up. She achieved complete remission within 5 months period of treatment with normal blood pressure and then kept on maintenance therapy of azathioprine at 1.5 mg/kg/day along with the lowest dose of prednisolone 5 mg. Enalapril was stopped after 8 months of follow‐up after achieving blood pressure control. It was planned for her to continue the remaining medication for at least 2–3 years then management would then be according to the EULAR/Pediatric SLE guidelines.

Discussion

Non‐lupus full‐house nephropathy consists of a renal biopsy with “full‐house” immunofluorescence patterns (deposits of IgG, IgA, IgM, C3 and C1q) in the absence of any clinical or serological evidence of SLE, that is, negative ANA and anti‐dsDNA with normal complement levels without any lupus symptoms [3]. This is in unison with the 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria for SLE which suggests ANA positivity is a mandatory criterion for the diagnosis of SLE [4].

There were several symptoms in our case like edema, hematuria, hypertension, proteinuria, anemia and low C3 that can occur in Lupus Nephritis. However, in this case, these features are renal‐specific immunologic manifestations. There is glomerular injury that leads to glomerular inflammation and nephrotic/nephritic presentation rather than multi‐organ immunological involvement [3]. Anemia could be secondary to anemia of chronic disease and renal dysfunction, and not due to auto‐immune hemolysis [4].

Low C3 level indicates complement consumption due to immune complex deposition leading to glomerulonephritis but does not establish lupus since ANA and dsDNA were normal.

Constitutional symptoms of SLE like fever, weight loss, rash, arthritis, and serositis were absent at presentation and also during hospitalization and all the subsequent follow‐ups. The absence of these constitutional lupus features further supports this case as a non‐lupus full‐house nephropathy. The full‐house pattern can be attributed to the renal‐limited immune complex glomerulonephritis rather than lupus nephritis due to systemic lupus erythematosus [4, 5, 6]. Therefore, a diagnosis of SLE cannot be made even with a lupus‐like renal biopsy due to the absence of ANA and anti‐dsDNA status along with systemic features such as rash, arthritis, or serositis. ENA was not done because the diagnosis was already confirmed by the renal biopsy, and ANCA was not done as there were no respiratory symptoms present.

The full‐house immunofluorescence pattern is classically associated with lupus nephritis but it is not pathognomic [5, 6]. Non‐lupus FHN can either be idiopathic or it can have secondary causes [7]. Various studies have shown that a majority of non‐lupus FHN have identifiable causes notably primary glomerulopathies such as membranous nephropathy, IgA nephropathy, membranoproliferative glomerulopnephropathy, diffuse proliferative glomerulopathy, amyloidosis and C1q nephropathy [6]. It was corroborated by another systematic review which found that nearly half of non‐lupus FHN had a secondary cause, mainly infectious [5]. Other causes included membranous nephropathy, IgA nephropathy and use of various drugs [8]. Viral causes were evaluated in our case. Serologic testing for hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV were all negative. In addition, there was no clinical history or laboratory evidence of preceding or concurrent viral infections, that is, no prodromal symptoms, lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly. The ASO (Anti‐Streptolysin O) titer was 130 IU/mL which was not suggestive of PSGN (Post‐streptococcal Glomerulonephritis). Thus, a viral or post‐infectious cause of FHN was considered unlikely. However, we do acknowledge that other viral agents such as parvovirus B19, EBV or even CMV could potentially have been implicated. But given the lack of other clinical cues that would suggest these etiologies, they weren't tested for and couldn't be fully ruled out due to unavailability of all tests in the lab.

Secondary nonlupus full house nephropathy patients had a worse kidney function at diagnosis compared with the idiopathic ones [8]. Rijnink et al. [7] found that idiopathic non‐lupus FHN patients were more often male and had different biopsy features, more purely mesangial or membranous patterns. However, our patient is a 4 year female with diffuse proliferative glomerulonephritis seen in renal biopsy. These features are similar to Lupus Nephritis Class IV rather than non‐lupus FHN [9]. It cannot, however, be labeled as Lupus Nephritis given the lack of clinical and serological evidence.

Despite all this, the possibility of “seronegative lupus nephritis” cannot be ignored. Full‐house staining is highly suggestive of SLE pathology, and rare cases of ANA‐negative lupus nephritis have been reported [10]. But still such cases had clinical evidence of SLE despite having no serological evidence which was not seen in our case.

Our induction therapy, which consisted of cyclophosphamide monthly for 6 months and pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisolone which was gradually tapered, falls in alignment with the management of lupus nephritis class IV, contributing to the remission [6, 11, 12]. Given the histopathological resemblance with Lupus Nephritis class IV, this therapy was opted in our case. After 6 months, azathioprine and low dose prednisolone were given as maintenance therapy. There is a continuous immunological and inflammatory phenomenon that goes on in SLE and clinical pictures similar to SLE. Use of prednisolone in these clinical pictures prevent the cellular damage by decreasing the immunological and inflammatory phenomenon [5, 6]. In addition, hydroxychloroquine is recommended in all SLE and lupus nephritis patients, reducing renal flares and improving outcomes [13]. So, in accordance, hydroxychloroquine was also added along with amlodipine and enalapril for blood pressure control. After 8 months of therapy, enalapril was stopped and as she attained complete remission, she was kept on maintenance therapy which was also according to the ACR/EULAR guidelines [4, 5, 6].

Rituximab is a great alternative to cyclophosphamide but it was not used in this case due to limited availability and higher cost [4, 5, 6, 7]. Cyclophosphamide was administered at an appropriated dose with close monitoring for adverse effects. The cumulative dose of cyclophosphamide for causing gonadal toxicity is 250 mg/kg [8]. The dose used in this patient was 750 mg/m2 monthly for 6 months. The cummulative dose received was 4500 mg. She weighed 22 kg so the dose per unit kg was 200 mg which is below the toxic threshold. However, the potential gonadotoxicity of cyclophosphamide was still recognized and fertility counseling was undertaken with the parents. Given the young prepubertal age of the patient, the risk of permanent gonadal damage is relatively low, especially with limited cumulative exposure, that is, 6 months. This discussion was documented as part of the treatment consent.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient has responded quite well to this treatment plan. This further deepens the diagnostic dilemma in our scenario as it has been reported that as many as 20% of “seronegative lupus” or non‐lupus FHN have developed positive ANA status after 5–10 years [14, 15, 16]. Therefore, early renal biopsy and aggressive immunosuppression can prevent irreversible organ damage in seronegative disease either in the case of “seronegative lupus nephritis” or non‐lupus FHN [11]. Despite this, 55%–63% of such cases remain seronegative long‐term thus supporting non‐lupus FHN as a separate entity [14, 16]. The risk of seroconversion warrants lifelong monitoring of the patient and also possible exploration of genetic predispositions such as monogenic lupus genes like CFHR3‐CFHR1 deletion or DNASE1L3 deficiency if the patient is not able to maintain remission despite appropriate immunosuppressive therapy [17, 18]. In conclusion, this case highlights the diagnostic complexity of pediatric non‐lupus full‐house nephropathy presenting with lupus‐like histopathology but negative serology. Early aggressive management is justified in cases with severe renal involvement, and long‐term follow‐up is essential to detect possible evolution into systemic lupus erythematosus. This case underscores the need for vigilance in distinguishing between lupus mimics and evolving lupus, particularly in children.
