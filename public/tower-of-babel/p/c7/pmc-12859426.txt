Erythropoietin-Secreting uterine leiomyoma presenting with secondary erythrocytosis: A case report

Introduction

Uterine leiomyomas are the most common benign gynecologic tumors, affecting up to 70 % of reproductive-age women (Stewart et al., 2017, Bulun, 2013). They are typically asymptomatic or associated with symptoms including abnormal bleeding, pelvic pressure, or infertility. Rarely, they can be associated with secondary erythrocytosis, a condition in which erythropoietin is secreted ectopically by the tumor, resulting in elevated hemoglobin and hematocrit levels. This rare presentation has been described as part of the myomatous erythrocytosis syndrome (MES), a triad of erythrocytosis, uterine fibroids, and normalization after surgical resection (Suresh and Rizk, 2020, Mui et al., 2020, Ozsaran et al., 1999).

Only around 60 cases of MED have been reported in the literature to date, making it an exceptionally rare condition among the many women diagnosed with fibroids yearly (Suresh and Rizk, 2020, Ozsaran et al., 1999, Thiangphak et al., 2023). A brief review of the published cases reveals that MES typically occurs in women aged 40–55 years, often with large (>10 cm) leiomyomas, and resolves following surgical removal (Suresh and Rizk, 2020, Mui et al., 2020, Ozsaran et al., 1999, Thiangphak et al., 2023).

This case describes a patient with a very large uterine leiomyoma and secondary erythrocytosis. The resolution of laboratory abnormalities following surgery supports the diagnosis of MES. We aim to contribute to the limited literature and raise awareness of this rare condition and its association to a prevalent gynecologic condition (uterine fibroids).

Case presentation

A 44-year-old woman with a history of known uterine fibroids was referred to Gynecologic Oncology for evaluation after imaging identified a 30 cm pelvic mass with rapid growth and degeneration concerning for malignancy. She reported progressive abdominal distention, early satiety, and occasional shortness of breath but denied vaginal bleeding, weight loss, or night sweats. Her medical history included well-controlled epilepsy.

Preoperative laboratory tests were notable for a hemoglobin of 18.2 g/dL, hematocrit of 56.1 %, red blood cell count of 6.47 × 106/μL, and serum erythropoietin (EPO) level of 107 mIU/mL (reference: 4.1–19.5 mIU/mL). She lived at sea level and had no history of smoking, chronic hypoxia, or exogenous EPO use. A hematology consult was undertaken, and it was recommended that additional testing be performed, including a JAK2 mutation panel, which returned negative. Additionally, CALR and MPL mutation testing were also negative. These genes (JAK2, CALR, and MPL) are commonly tested to rule out myeloproliferative neoplasms such as polycythemia vera, which can cause primary erythrocytosis unrelated to EPO production (Spivak, 2019, Nangalia and Green, 2017).

She was counseled for surgical removal, given the size of her mass and extensive symptoms. Despite her elevated hemoglobin and hematocrit, there was no indication per the Hematology team to pursue preoperative thromboprophylaxis. However, given a planned open procedure, preoperative thromboprophylaxis was given per protocol with 5000 units of unfractionated heparin. She underwent exploratory laparotomy with total abdominal hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and debulking of enlarged para-aortic lymph nodes. The estimated blood loss intraoperatively was approximately 500 mL. Intraoperatively, the uterus was massively enlarged, and the largest fibroid was retroperitoneal and extending into the upper abdomen. Frozen section analysis was consistent with a benign leiomyoma. Final pathology revealed a 30 cm degenerating leiomyoma without evidence of malignancy. Nine benign reactive lymph nodes were identified, all negative for carcinoma, with benign mesothelium-lined fibroconnective and fibroadipose tissue. Immunohistochemistry was positive for smooth muscle actin, desmin, and estrogen receptor, and negative for calretinin and inhibin. Her postoperative hemoglobin decreased significantly, more than would have been expected given intraoperative blood loss. It never returned to levels consistent with polycythemia. Discussion

The association between benign uterine fibroids and secondary erythrocytosis has been described in case reports and termed “myomatous erythrocytosis syndrome” (MES) (Suresh and Rizk, 2020, Mui et al., 2020, Ozsaran et al., 1999). This condition is believed to result from ectopic secretion of erythropoietin by leiomyoma. Although the exact mechanism is not fully understood, some studies have demonstrated EPO mRNA and protein expression in fibroid tissue, suggesting a paracrine or autocrine loop in tumor growth and erythropoiesis (Kohama et al., 2000, Asano et al., 2015, Pollio et al., 2005). According to this theory, the autonomous EPO production by the leiomyoma is thought not to be subject to normal feedback regulation. However, other mechanisms have also been proposed, such as tumor-induced hypoxia or renal compression, as the cause for the endogenous production of EPO (Clark et al., 1994, Abdul Ghaffar et al., 2008). Given the retroperitoneal nature of this fibroid, either mechanism may have contributed.

The mean age at presentation for this type of syndrome has been described as between 45–50 years (Suresh and Rizk, 2020, Mui et al., 2020, Ozsaran et al., 1999, Thiangphak et al., 2023). It has also largely been described in cases of large leiomyomas (>10 cm) (Mui et al., 2020, Ozsaran et al., 1999). The mechanism behind this is thought to result from the overexpression of EPO and its receptors, which then contributes to tumor growth and vascularity (Kohama et al., 2000, Asano et al., 2015, Pollio et al., 2005). Definitive treatment results from the removal of the leiomyoma, most commonly by hysterectomy or myomectomy, which results in rapid normalization of hemoglobin and hematocrit as seen in this case (Asano et al., 2015, Pollio et al., 2005).

In this case, several features supported a diagnosis of MES: a large uterine mass, markedly elevated EPO levels, and normalization of hematologic parameters after surgery. Importantly, workup for other causes of erythrocytosis—including polycythemia vera and hypoxia-related disorders—was negative. Testing for JAK2, CALR, and MPL mutations was appropriate, as mutations in these genes are diagnostic for myeloproliferative neoplasms such as polycythemia vera and essential thrombocytopenia, which can present with erythrocytosis independent of EPO levels (Spivak, 2019, Nangalia and Green, 2017). A similar diagnostic framework was suggested in a 2020 review of MES cases, emphasizing the importance of ruling out more common causes before attributing erythrocytosis to a fibroid (Chen et al., 2022, McMullin, 2014).

This patient’s leiomyoma was notable for both its size and retroperitoneal location with reactive aortic adenopathy. While the clinical presentation initially raised concern for malignancy, final pathology confirmed a benign process. Reports in the literature suggest that EPO-producing fibroids may be more likely to exhibit degenerative or cystic changes, although this association has not been well established (Suresh and Rizk, 2020, Mui et al., 2020, Ozsaran et al., 1999, Thiangphak et al., 2023).

Though rare, MES has important clinical implications. Polycythemia can increase the risk of thromboembolic events, which is especially relevant in the perioperative period. Our patient was managed with intraoperative thromboprophylaxis and extended postoperative anticoagulation, though we acknowledge there are no established guidelines for thromboprophylaxis in MES (Ozsaran et al., 1999, Chen et al., 2022, McMullin, 2014).

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare but important cause of secondary erythrocytosis in women with uterine fibroids. When common causes have been excluded and EPO is elevated, an EPO-secreting leiomyoma should be considered. Surgery is presumed curative for this condition and close monitoring for perioperative thrombotic complications is advisable. Increased awareness may help clinicians avoid unnecessary testing and guide appropriate management.
