ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrioventricular valve regurgitation (AVVR) predicts long-term post-Fontan adverse outcomes. Literature on the measurement and prognostic value of quantitative AVVR assessment using cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) is limited. This study compared echocardiographic AVVR measurements with measurements by CMR, and investigated their association with clinical outcomes using the multi-center Fontan Outcome Registry using Clinical Examination (FORCE).MethodsPatients with an examiner-reported AVVR severity category from CMR (AVVRReport-Cat) and an AVVR from matched echocardiography (AVVREcho) were included. Three quantitative CMR AVVR regurgitant fractions were used as follows: (1) stroke volume and antegrade aortic flow (AVVRStandard), (2) atrioventricular valve flow and antegrade aortic flow (AVVRAVV-AO), and (3) examiner-reported regurgitation fraction (AVVRReport-RF). Moderate AVVR was defined as regurgitant fraction of 20%–40% and severe >40%. The concordance across methods was calculated using Kappa statistics, and associations with outcomes of mortality or listing for/receiving heart transplantation were analyzed using Cox proportional hazard models. C-statistic was used to compare model performance.ResultsExactly 2309 Fontan patients were included, with a median age at CMR of 15.7 years and (971/2309) 42.1% female. Categorical AVVR severity reported by CMR was significantly associated with adverse outcomes (adjusted HR 2.10, 95% CI [1.38, 3.19] for moderate and 4.61 [2.80, 7.58] for severe AVVR), comparable to AVVR reported by echocardiography. AVVRAVV-AO showed stronger concordance with AVVRReport-Cat (Kappa=0.58) and higher hazards of the adverse outcome than AVVRStandard. The subset of patients with examiner-reported regurgitation fraction (AVVRReport-RF) showed the highest hazard associated with AVVR and the highest C-statistic among all methods including echocardiography.ConclusionsThis is the first study evaluating different CMR measurements of AVVR in a large cohort of Fontan patients. Compared with AVVRStandard, AVVRAVV-AO had higher concordance with the reported categorical AVVR using CMR. Moderate and severe AVVR determined by both CMR and echocardiography were associated with comparably increased hazards of adverse clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Atrioventricular valve regurgitation (AVVR) measured by echocardiography has frequently been identified as a predictor of long-term adverse outcomes in patients with single ventricle morphology who underwent the Fontan procedure [1], [2]. However, accurate assessment of AVVR in Fontan patients can be challenging due to their complex cardiac anatomy and valve morphology. Echocardiography is the primary modality used by prior studies for assessing AVVR severity, owing to its availability and ease of use. However, echocardiographic evaluation can be limited by subjective interpretation and suboptimal acoustic windows [3].

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) has many advantages in providing an objective and quantitative assessment of AVVR. The lack of radiation and tolerance of both large body habitus and scar tissues [4] make it an ideal longitudinal surveillance tool. In Fontan patients, CMR has been shown to have advantages in reliably assessing ventricular volume, mass, and function [5]. CMR measurements of AVVR regurgitant fraction do not depend on jet morphology [6] and could characterize the effects of the valve regurgitation on heart chambers [7]. Thus, the quantitative calculation of AVVR using CMR may be well suited for anatomically abnormal valves commonly encountered in single ventricle patients.

The evidence on the prognostic value of quantitative AVVR measurement using CMR in Fontan patients is limited. In anatomically normal hearts, studies have supported the accuracy, objectivity, and reproducibility of CMR measurements of mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, as well as their complementary value to echo [8], [9]. Studies have shown that CMR allows better intra- and inter-observer agreement [10] and is a useful clinical tool in determining the necessity of valve interventions [11]. However, such evidence is not available in single ventricle patients.

This study aimed to compare AVVR measurements obtained by CMR and echocardiography, and investigate the associations between each method and clinical outcomes in Fontan patients.

Methods

Study population

We used data from The Fontan Outcome Registry using Clinical Examinations (FORCE), an international consortium that aims to accelerate scientific research and improve outcomes in patients after the Fontan operation.

Our study included patients who had at least one post-Fontan CMR study with categorical AVVR severity measurement reported from 3/1999 to 7/2024 from the FORCE registry. We then selected patients who have AVVR severity reported from a matched echocardiographic study within 2 years of the CMR study. Patients who underwent an atrioventricular valve procedure between the time of CMR and echocardiography were excluded.

Demographics, clinical and surgical history, CMR test reports, echocardiography test reports, and clinical follow-up data were extracted from the registry. The FORCE study protocol was approved by a reliance institutional review board agreement with Boston Children’s Hospital. The protocol was also approved by the institutional review board of University of Pittsburgh as a participation institution. The study proposal and this final manuscript were approved by the FORCE Data Governance and Publications Committee. The complete list of FORCE Investigator co-authors and affiliations is enumerated in Table S1.

CMR data

CMR studies were acquired using scanners from multiple vendors, including Philips Healthcare (Best, the Netherlands), Siemens Healthineers (Erlangen, Germany), and GE Medical Systems (Milwaukee, Wisconsin). While imaging protocols differed across institutions, standardized guidelines for Fontan CMR have been previously published [12], [13]. Ventricular evaluation was conducted using electrocardiographically-gated cine steady-state free precession sequences in short-axis views, spanning from the apex to the atria, with reconstruction into 20–30 phases per cardiac cycle. Only patients with images of sufficient quality to permit calculation of ventricular volumes and function were included in the FORCE registry dataset [14]. Quantitative CMR parameters were extracted from clinical reports and entered into the FORCE registry by participating centers.

Ventricular volumes were obtained via manual contouring of endocardial and epicardial borders at end-diastole (maximum volume) and end-systole (minimum volume). To normalize by body size, end-diastolic volume (EDV), end-systolic volume (ESV), and stroke volume were indexed to body surface area, calculated using the Mosteller formula.

Ascending aortic (AAo) flow was measured by two-dimensional phase-contrast imaging. For patients with an aortopulmonary connection (e.g., Damus-Kaye-Stansel), flow was measured above the anastomosis or calculated as the sum of native and neoaortic flows [14].

Ventricular morphology was classified as right ventricular dominant, left ventricular dominant, or mixed. Mixed morphology was defined a priori as the smaller ventricle contributing to systemic output and compromising ≥25% of the total ventricular EDV. Systemic ventricular EDV (SVEDV), systemic ventricular ESV (SVESV), ejection fraction, and mass were calculated based on the ventricle(s) contributing to the systemic circulation. If both ventricles contributed, SVEDV, SVESV, and mass were calculated as the sum of left and right ventricular values. If only one ventricle was systemic, the measurements from the corresponding ventricle were used directly.

AVVRReport-RF

Reported numeric AV valve regurgitation fraction (RF) from CMR studies were abstracted from the FORCE database. The RF was calculated and reported by examiners at the respective study centers, and the information regarding the specific calculation method was not available.

The last three methods yielded numeric RFs, which were then categorized into None to mild (if RF < 20%), Moderate (20% ≤ RF < 40%), and Severe (RF ≥ 40%). The first two methods yielded categorical regurgitation, which were then condensed into the same categorization of none to mild (None, trivial to mild, and mild), moderate (mild to moderate, moderate), and severe (moderate to severe, severe).

AVVR severity was labeled as missing based on the following method-specific criteria: if AVVR was reported as missing or unable to determine for AVVRReport-RF, AVVRReport-Cat, and AVVREcho; if key input values such as ventricular volume, heart rate, or aortic flow were unavailable for AVVRStandard; or if aortic flow or AV valve flow data were missing for AVVRAVV-AO. As a result, each AVVR method was assessed in a distinct sub-cohort of patients, with partial overlap between groups.

Clinical outcomes

The primary clinical outcome was defined as the time from the CMR test to the first occurrence of death, listing for, or receiving a heart transplantation. If a patient had multiple CMR tests in the registry, the most recent test was used for AVVR measurement. A secondary composite outcome of time to the first event of death, listing for or receiving heart transplantation, and atrial arrhythmia was used. Additional secondary outcomes including a reported NYHA functional class III/IV at the most recent follow-up and a predicted peak VO2 <60% at a matched cardiopulmonary exercise study within 2 years of CMR were examined.

Statistical analysis

Correlation between numerical measurements was performed using Spearman’s correlation test. Agreement test among categorical variables was performed using weighted Cohen’s Kappa statistics. Cox regression models were used to analyze the association between each measurement of AVVR and the composite outcome. The proportional hazard assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. The multivariable models were adjusted using a priori defined covariates, including patient sex, ventricular morphology, and Fontan type. Model performance was evaluated using Harrell’s C-index.

In sensitivity analyses, we used alternative definitions of the cohort to test the validity of our main analysis. First, a secondary composite outcome using time from CMR to the earliest event of mortality, listing for or receiving a heart transplantation, or atrial arrhythmia was used for the survival analysis. Second, we matched echocardiography within 6 months of CMR for closer resemblance in time of test, and analyzed the echo measurements of AVVR severity and association with clinical outcome. In addition, because the AVVR measurements were not available for all patients, each method generated a distinct cohort with varying sample sizes, which limited direct comparison across methods. To address this, we conducted sensitivity analyses using the overlapping sub-cohorts of the most prevalent method, AVVRReport-Cat and each of the other five methods. For each pair, we repeated the survival models using AVVR by each method separately as the predictor. Lastly, we performed alternative multivariable models, additionally accounting for history of atrioventricular valve procedure prior to the time of CMR as a covariate.

Comparing AVVR measurements

Comparing the two categorical measurements of AVVR, AVVRReport-Cat and AVVREcho, more tests were rated moderate and above by AVVREcho compared to AVVRReport-Cat (21.8% vs 16.8%, Chi-square p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2a). Among the methods that yielded numeric AVVR regurgitant fraction, AVVRStandard was calculated in 973 tests (median AVVR 21.2% [IQR 7.3%, 35.5%]), AVVRAVV-AO was calculated in 504 tests (median AVVR 9.6% [IQR 1.0%, 20.7%]), and AVVRReport-RF method was reported in 604 tests (median AVVR 15.0% [IQR 9.0%, 25.0%], Fig. 2b). Association of AVVR categories with clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes were reported in 149 (6.5%) patients, including 89 patients (3.9%) who died and 75 (3.2%) who were listed or received heart transplantation (Table S3).

Moderate AVVR determined by all methods except for AVVRStandard was significantly associated with increased risks of adverse outcomes, and severe AVVR determined using all methods was significantly associated with the outcomes. AVVRReport-Cat and AVVREcho performed similarly in predicting the outcomes (Fig. 3). The adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) for AVVREcho were 1.74 (95% CI 1.18, 2.57, p = 0.005) for moderate and 5.27 (95% CI 3.09, 9.01, p < 0.0001) for severe AVVR. The aHR for AVVRReport-Cat were 2.10 (95% CI 1.38, 3.19, p = 0.0005) for moderate and 4.61 (95% CI 2.80, 7.58, p < 0.0001) for severe AVVR (Table 3). Sensitivity analysis

We performed survival analyses using a secondary composite outcome (mortality, listing for/receiving heart transplantation, or atrial tachycardia), and the associations were similar when compared to the primary outcomes. Moderate AVVR measurements were less strongly associated with outcomes, with the association being nonsignificant in AVVRStandard, AVVRAVV-AO, and AVVREcho, while severe AVVR measurements were consistently associated with outcomes (Table S4). Second, we matched echocardiography within 6 months of CMR, instead of 2 years in the main analysis. The distribution of AVVR severity, concordance, and association with outcomes were comparable to those in the primary analysis (Table S5). In addition, we performed sensitivity analysis using the sub-cohorts defined by available AVVRStandard, AVVRAVV-AO, and AVVRReport-RF measurements, as mentioned above, and performed multivariable Cox regression models using AVVRReport-Cat and AVVREcho as predictors, to test the validity of primary analysis in the corresponding sub-cohorts. The aHR yielded in the sub-cohorts were very similar to the ones in the primary analysis (Table 4). An alternative multivariable model accounted for the history of atrioventricular valve procedure prior to CMR as an additional covariate. The associations between AVVR identified by each method and the composite outcome were comparable with the primary analysis and are detailed in Table S6. Lastly, secondary outcomes were explored: AVVR was significantly associated with NYHA functional class III/IV across all AVVR measurement methods; however, AVVR severity was not significantly associated with predicted peak VO2 <60% measured in catheterization studies matched within 2 years of the CMR study (Table S7).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to compare methods of CMR and echocardiographic assessments of AVVR in Fontan patients from a large multi-center cohort. A moderate or severe AVVR determined by most methods tested was significantly associated with adverse clinical outcomes, with similar hazard ratios using the reported categorical severity by CMR and by echocardiography. Compared with AVVRStandard (calculated using stroke volume from short axis and antegrade aortic flow), AVVRAVV-AO method (calculated using AV valve flow and antegrade aortic flow) showed stronger agreement with the CMR reported categorical severity and echocardiographic evaluation, and was more strongly associated with outcomes. Examiner-reported AVVRReport-RF method revealed the highest hazards of adverse outcomes associated with moderate AVVR and had the best model performance among all the imaging methods, including echocardiography. Thus, the quantitative AVVR measured by CMR examiners may have added value to the widely used echocardiography surveillance in Fontan patients.

Comparing AVVR measurements

In an anatomically normal heart, CMR has been widely used in the diagnosis and surveillance of valve regurgitation. Studies have shown that CMR produces reliable and reproducible results in measuring mitral and tricuspid regurgitation [6], [8], [10], [15], and serves a complementary role to echocardiography in advising clinical management [7], [9]. The literature in single ventricle populations is limited. One cohort study has shown that among pre-Glenn patients who underwent imaging studies as pre-operative assessment, AVVR determined by CMR reliably predicted the need for valve interventions [16].

In the current study, we compared the echocardiographic evaluation of AVVR (AVVREcho) to categorical report from CMR (AVVRReport-Cat), numeric AVVR reported by the examiner (AVVRReport-RF), and AVVR calculated using two metrics from reported volume and flow data (AVVRStandard, AVVRAVV-AO). AVVREcho showed moderate agreement with most CMR methods.

Among the two numerically calculated methods, AVVRStandard showed lower concordance to other methods than AVVRAVV-Ao. This could be due to the prevalence of aortopulmonary and venovenous collaterals, which may alter the ventricular stroke volume and thus overestimate the AVVR. While this method is the standard in anatomically normal hearts and is widely used in Fontan patients, the number of factors that went into the calculation may impact its accuracy. In comparison, AVVRAVV-AO showed higher concordance with AVVRReport-RF and AVVRReport-Cat, which suggests that although this method is not the most commonly used method for AVVR calculation, when it is performed, the examiners more commonly used the calculated result in the final reported regurgitation fraction and categorical severity. AVVRAVV-AO is routinely used to evaluate mitral regurgitation in patients with structurally normal heart [7]. However, in the Fontan cohort, this method was reported in only 22% (504/2309) of CMR scans due to the high number of missing data as many centers do not perform atrioventricular valve flow as part of their routine CMR protocol.

As expected, AVVRReport-RF had the highest concordance with AVVRReport-Cat, as AVVRReport-RF is often the method that examiners use to reach their final assessment. Interestingly, both AVVRReport-RF and AVVRReport-Cat had the highest concordance with echocardiography. This may reflect the non-blinding of the examiners when interpreting studies at local centers.

Association between AVVR and clinical outcomes

When investigating the association between AVVR and clinical outcomes, we found that a regurgitation fraction categorized as moderate (or 20%) and higher was significantly associated with the adverse outcomes using most methods. AVVRReport-Cat, which took into account multiple measures, performed similar to echocardiographic evaluation. This confirmed the findings of the literature, which all used echocardiographic definition of AVVR, as discussed below. A study using a bi-national Fontan registry showed that AV valve failure (defined as AV valve repair, replacement, development of moderate or greater AVVR) is associated with long-term Fontan failure with HR 2.43 (95% CI 1.74–3.39) [2]. Another study using serial post-Fontan echocardiography studies found that moderate or greater AVVR was associated with the development of ventricular dysfunction after Fontan (OR 4.3 [95% CI 2.7–6.7]) [1]. These findings are largely consistent with what we observed in our cohort. Aside from moderate AVVR identified by AVVRStandard that was not significantly associated with outcomes, we have found that a moderate AVVR is associated with hazard ratios ranging from 1.74–3.05, and a severe AVVR associated with hazard ratios ranging from 2.97–5.27 for the long-term outcome, which aids in risk-stratifying patients with Fontan. Our cohort also consisted of a younger patient population with predominantly preserved systolic function at the time of CMR, and the association might be more prominent with longer follow-up time.

Interestingly, we found that both moderate and severe AVVR defined by the reported regurgitation fraction (AVVRReport-RF) were associated with the highest hazards of long-term clinical outcomes compared to other methods. While AVVRReport-RF was only available in a subset of the patients, they likely represent those with optimal imaging quality, complete sequences that allowed the examiners to measure volumes and flow that was used to calculate AVVR. As discussed in more detail below, although calculating a numeric regurgitation fraction may not be possible due to the intrinsic challenge in the Fontan population, when a number can be confidently generated and reported, it adds additional value to echocardiographic measurements in determining the patients’ risks for severe long-term outcomes.

In this cohort, CMR assessment of AVVR was associated with hazards of downstream adverse outcomes that were comparable to those associated with echocardiographic measurements. Our observations reflect the prognostic value of AVVR measured by CMR in predicting clinical outcomes. CMR is also well established to provide quantitative assessment of ventricular volume and function [17], [18], which may offer additional clinical benefits in using CMR-measured AVVR in patient risk stratification.

Challenges and future directions

Despite growing use of CMR to assess AVVR in Fontan patients, there remain important challenges. AVVR calculation can be technically difficult due to valve in-plane movement through the cardiac cycle, complex anatomy and post-surgical changes, and variable image quality. As a result, AVVR is often assessed using multiple approaches, including AVVRStandard and AVVRAVV-AO, as well as integration of echocardiographic findings. This variability highlights both a limitation and an opportunity to optimize the measurement strategies. In our study, AVVRReport-RF, despite only available in a subset, demonstrated the strongest association adverse outcomes and discriminated risk of patients with moderate and severe AVVR. These findings suggest that when feasible, a quantitative assessment integrated by an examiner may provide incremental prognostic values.

There are also emerging methods like using machine learning [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24] and 4D flow [25], [26], [27], [28] that warrant further exploration. Early studies, including our ongoing work, suggest these approaches may provide robust and clinically meaningful assessments. Further studies are warranted to validate and optimize CMR measurements of AVVR in the Fontan population.

Limitations

As elaborated in the methods and discussion sections, we have missing data either due to the reported value being missing for AVVRReport-RF, AVVRReport-Cat, and AVVREcho, or due to key input values being missing for AVVRStandard and AVVRAVV-AO. This made the direct comparison across different methods challenging. We performed sensitivity analyses using AVVRReport-Cat and AVVREcho as predictors in the sub-cohorts with available data of each of the three numeric methods, to test the validity of our primary analysis. Second, this cohort only included patients who underwent post-Fontan CMR and excludes patients who never underwent a CMR study following their Fontan procedure, including patients with pacemakers. Third, echocardiography was matched within 2 years of CMR to maximize sample size; however, the severity of AVVR could change in 2 years and lead to inaccuracy in horizontal comparison. We performed a sensitivity analysis matching echo within 6 months of the CMR study, with similar results yielded in comparison to the primary analysis. Fourth, study data were entered by participating centers and extracted from clinical reports. Although registry-level quality control was in place, potential data entry errors and variability in data quality may have influenced the results. In addition, the specific methods used by examiners to achieve the calculated values in the reports were not available. Lastly, our study did not evaluate 4D flow, an emerging CMR technique that can be used to quantify AVVR through direct measurement of flow. Future studies are warranted for further investigation of 4D flow in assessing AVVR.

Conclusion

This is the first study evaluating different CMR measurements of AVVR in a large cohort of Fontan patients. Compared with AVVR calculated from ventricular volume and aortic flow, AVVR calculated from atrioventricular valve flow had higher concordance with the reported categorical AVVR using CMR. Moderate and severe AVVR determined by both CMR and echocardiography were associated with comparably increased hazards of adverse clinical outcomes.
