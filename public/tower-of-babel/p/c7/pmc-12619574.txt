Comparative Study of Different Minimally Invasive Aortic Valve Replacement Techniques: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis
Authors: Theresia Feline Husen, Ananda Pipphali Vidya, Samuel Heuts, Alfian Prasetyo, Aqilla Katrita Zaira Nugroho, Roberto Lorusso, Elham Bidar, Bart Maesen, Peyman Sardari Nia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis investigation aimed to compare different minimally invasive techniques, namely, mini-sternotomy (MS), mini-thoracotomy (MT), and totally thoracoscopic (TT) approaches for the surgical treatment of aortic valve disease, emphasizing their respective benefits and limitations to guide clinical decision-making.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in Medline, Web of Science, Scopus, Wiley Online Library, Google Scholar, and ProQuest. Studies were appraised using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. A frequentist network meta-analysis (NMA) with a random-effects model was employed to give reflective ranks and compare outcomes across techniques. Treatment ranking was based on p-scores, with MS as the reference. Higher p-scores indicate greater certainty of superiority over competing interventions. The primary outcome was mortality.ResultsTwenty-five observational studies (n = 34 573 patients) were included. Mortality did not differ between techniques (p-score: MS [0.85] ∼ MT [0.34] ∼ TT [0.31]). TT had longer cardiopulmonary bypass (mean difference [MD]: 41.04 [95% CI, 10.98-71.10]) and cross-clamp times (MD: 30.31 [95% CI, 5.81-54.80]) but offered the shortest intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay (p-score: TT [0.98] > MT [0.51] > MS [0.01]; MD: −16.00 [95% CI, −26.62 to −5.38]), reduced hospital stay (MD: −2.07 [95% CI, −3.77 to −0.37]), and fewer complications, including neurological events (odds ratio: 1.79 [95% CI, 1.03-3.13]), blood loss (MD: 208.85 mL [95% CI, 102.29-315.40]) compared to MS. MT showed similar outcomes to MS, except for longer operative times (MD: 29.84 [95% CI, 8.35-51.32]) and shorter ICU stays (MD: −5.88 [95% CI, −11.10 to −0.67]).ConclusionsTT may offer advantages such as shorter hospital stays, reduced neurological complications, and less bleeding as compared to MS, although it is associated with longer operative times. However, as all included studies were observational, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and further NMA including only randomized trials is warranted.Keywords: aortic valve, mini-sternotomy, mini-thoracotomy, totally thoracoscopic, replacement

INTRODUCTION

Aortic valve replacement (AVR) is the mainstay of the surgical treatment of aortic valve disease.1 While conventional AVR via full sternotomy (FS) remains effective, it is associated with significant postoperative pain, longer recovery times, and increased bleeding risks compared to minimally invasive AVR (MIAVR), particularly mini-sternotomy (MS).2,3 Advances in surgical techniques have also introduced several newer MIAVR techniques, including mini-thoracotomy (MT), and the latest, totally thoracoscopic (TT) approach, each offering potential benefits such as reduced recovery times, and less bleeding with comparable mortality, stroke, and acute kidney failure compared to the FS approach.2,4,5

Previous meta-analyses6,7 have compared MS and MT, showing similar outcomes for mortality, stroke, and postoperative atrial fibrillation, but highlighting differences in secondary endpoints, such as a shorter hospital stay with MT. However, conflicting findings exist regarding operative times, with some studies suggesting longer durations for MT, while in other studies, times were comparable. These analyses also did not include TT, which has shown promise as the least invasive option with benefits such as shorter hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) stays, reduced postoperative pain, lower bleeding rates, and improved cosmesis. Nevertheless, previous studies8,9 indicate that TT’s technical complexity may lead to longer cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and cross-clamp (Xc) times.

Hence, this study aimed to directly and indirectly compare MS, MT, and TT through a network approach, in order to clarify their respective benefits and limitations. Unlike previous studies, this analysis incorporates all 3 techniques, making it the first to comprehensively evaluate these approaches. By doing so, it offers valuable insights for clinical decision-making and advances the understanding of MIAVR options.

METHODS

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was written under the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, version 6.2. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were also followed for reporting. Additionally, we drafted and registered our study in the international Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), adhering to international research ethics standards (CRD42024610128), accepted on 22 November 2024.

Search strategy

Four authors (T.F.H., A.P.V., A.P., and A.K.K.N.) performed a comprehensive literature search up to 20 April 2024, in several databases guided by a medical librarian, including Medline, Web of Science, and Scopus. Additionally, searches were conducted across the Wiley Online Library, Google Scholar, and ProQuest platforms (health & medical collection). The keywords used in the pursuit were MS, MT, TT, and AVR along with their synonyms (Appendix S1). Advanced search techniques in each database were applied where applicable and available to make the search more precise. All findings from databases are processed into Rayyan, a web-based application.10 After the automatic deduplication, the same 4 authors reviewed the full papers based on eligibility. Any conflicts during study selection were resolved by majority vote. In cases where a majority decision could not be reached, the final decision was made by the senior author, who served as the arbitrator.

Study eligibility criteria

Studies were screened based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) observational (prospective [collected in real time] or retrospective [previously recorded data] cohort) or randomized control trial; (2) provides a comparison between MS, MT, and/or TT techniques for AVR. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) inappropriate study designs, such as preclinical studies, commentaries, case reports or case series, letters to the editor, and cross-sectional study, (2) studies with incomplete or only qualitative outcome data, (3) ongoing studies at the time of data retrieval, (4) studies without accessible full-text articles, and (5) studies lacking a control group or involving a single-arm design. Data extraction

We predetermined the outcome sheet in tabular form (MS Excel for Mac; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, 2018) to include the following data to be extracted: (1) author and year of publication; (2) study characteristics, including study design, location, and interval of the study; (3) study population, including sample size, age, gender, follow-up period, New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, concomitant surgery, follow-up type, EuroScore (I and II), ejection fraction (EF), comorbidities, surgery indication/s, and type of valve used; (4) study outcomes.

Endpoints

Our study outcomes are divided into 3 main sections which are operative procedure, hospital stay, and postoperative outcomes. The operative procedure comprises CPB, Xc, and operative time, while the hospital stay section consists of the ICU and the overall hospital length of stay. Both of these sections consist of continuous outcomes. When the study did not provide mean and SD, but only median and range, the size-dependent methods for the estimation of mean and SD were performed.11 The last section comprises the postoperative outcomes, which consist of mortality, neurological complications, reoperation, atrial fibrillation, pacemaker, ventilation time, bleeding, and paravalvular leakage. Of note, the primary outcome will be mortality that was reported during hospitalization until 30 days (short-term). All other outcomes will be considered secondary. For the outcomes, we will perform subgroup analysis based on the number of centres (single vs multicentre) and surgeons (single vs multiple) as well as analysis only for propensity score matched (PSM) studies.

Four authors (A.P.V., A.P., T.F.H., and A.K.Z.N.) extracted qualitative and quantitative characteristics from this data extraction. In addition, 3 authors (A.P.V., A.P., T.H.F.) rechecked the accuracy of all extracted data before performing statistical analysis.

Quality assessment

We used the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale for Cohort Studies to appraise both retrospective and prospective observational studies, assessing potential bias qualitatively. Each score was converted to the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) scale, with ratings categorized as >6: good; 4-6: fair; and <4: poor.12,13 For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), we used the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) 2.0 tool,14 with results visualized using the RoB tool. Four reviewers (A.P.V., A.P., A.K.Z.N., and T.F.H.) conducted quality assessments independently and discussed their evaluations until reaching a consensus.

Risk of bias

We included only PSM populations in the network meta-analysis (NMA) to reduce bias from differences in baseline patient risk profiles. However, all available baseline characteristics, both before and after matching, are listed in the systematic review.

Statistical analysis

Network meta-analysis

For baseline patient characteristics, such as gender, age, EF, and operative risk (EuroSCORE II), a single-arm meta-analysis was performed using inverse-variance weighting and pooled with a random-effects model. For other outcomes, a frequentist NMA was conducted to compare different techniques through direct and indirect comparisons using a common comparator (MS). Single-arm meta-analyses utilized the ‘meta’15 package (version 2.8-2) and the ‘netmeta’16 package for NMA. To address potential heterogeneity, a random-effects consistency model was used, with heterogeneity evaluated using I2 statistics according to the Cochrane thresholds (0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% for insignificant, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively). Sensitivity analyses were performed when heterogeneity exceeded 50% to assess the robustness of the findings for each technique and study.17 All of these analyses employed R software (version 3.4.0; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Publication bias was assessed using both qualitative methods, such as funnel plots, and quantitative approaches, including Egger’s linear regression test.17,18 When quantitative bias was detected, additional bias methods, such as the Thompson-Sharp19 and Begg-Mazumdar20 tests, were applied to enhance the validity of the bias assessment results.18

The CPB, Xc, ventilation, and operative time as well as ICU and length of stay were continuous variables, while the remaining variables were considered dichotomous. Differences between continuous outcomes were presented as mean differences (MDs), whereas dichotomous data were expressed as odds ratios (ORs), both accompanied by a 95% CI. For continuous outcomes, a 95% CI that did not include 0 was deemed statistically significant. For dichotomous outcomes, significance was defined as a 95% CI that did not include 1, unless specified otherwise. Reflective ranking treatment, calculated using the p-score, was utilized to establish the ranking of each technique included.21 The reference treatment is an MS in the reflective rank analysis using a forest plot. A higher p-score indicates a higher certainty that a treatment is better than competing interventions. This corresponds to fewer complications and shorter durations, meaning treatments ranked higher are preferable. However, the significance of these p-scores or reflective ranks will be evaluated using the 95% CI. Furthermore, the node-split method was employed to assess the consistency of the NMA between direct and indirect evidence.22

Meta-analysis

We performed conventional random-effects meta-analysis to assess baseline characteristics (age, gender, EF, and operative risk) (inverse-variance weighting method), and a random-effects meta-analysis to compare 3 (sub)groups in conventional meta-analysis, using Review Manager 4.5.

A total of 34 473 patients who underwent elective and isolated MIAVR were involved in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Most patients were male 54% (95% CI, 49%-58%), with a mean age of 64.6 years (95% CI, 61.6-67.6) and a mean left ventricular EF of 58.9% (95% CI, 57.9-59.9). Regarding operative risk, EuroSCORE II was used, and the mean risk was 4.1% (95% CI, 3.2-5.0). We did not observe significant differences between studies in terms of baseline age (MS: 65.8 [95% CI, 61.8-69.8]; MT: 65.7 [95% CI, 61.8-69.7]; TT: 59.1 [95% CI, 46.8-71.3]), sex (MS: 53% [95% CI, 48-57]; MT: 53% [95% CI, 46-60]; TT: 68% [95% CI, 30-91]), EF (MS: 58% [95% CI, 57-60]; MT: 58% [95% CI, 57-60]; TT: 61% [95% CI, 59-63]), or EuroSCORE II (MS: 4.60 [95% CI, 3.36-5.84]; MT: 4.39 [95% CI, 2.97-5.81]; TT: 2.31 [95% CI, 2.09-2.52]). Among all the studies included, only 1 provided direct comparisons of the 3 techniques. While 17 studies compared MS (4557 patients) with MT (3428 patients) and 3 studies compared TT (450 patients) with MT.

Quality assessment

Each study was assessed for its selection, comparability, and outcome using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies. All studies were categorized as good quality according to the AHRQ (Appendix S2).4,8,9,23–44

For overall operative time, 9 studies compared MS (1920 patients) with MT (1852 patients), and 1 study compared MS with TT (40 patients). For ICU stay, TT (−15.87 [95% CI, −26.26 to −5.47]) was associated with shorter duration than MS (p-score: TT [0.99] > MT [0.51] > MS [0.01]) (Table 3, Figure 2D, Table S1D), while MT and MS were associated with comparable stay. In the subgroup analysis, it was also comparable (Figure 3D, Figure S1-2D). For the overall length of stay, TT had a shorter duration compared with MS (p-score: TT [0.96] and MS [0.00]; MD [−2.04] [95% CI, −3.75 to −0.34]) (Table 3, Figure 2E, Table S1E). In the single-centre subgroup, MT was associated with shorter duration of stay compared to MS, while in the multicentre subgroup, it was comparable, consistent with the results of NMA. The subgroup of the number of surgeons involved also showed comparable results between length of stay of MT and MS (Figure S1-2E). Primary outcome: mortality

The overall mortality was comparable between the 3 techniques (p-score: MT [0.62] ∼ MS [0.56] ∼ TT [0.32]) (Table 3, Figure 2F, Table S1F). The mortality in MS vs MT was also comparable in the subgroup of the number of centres and surgeons involved in the studies (Figure S1-2F) as well as in subgroup analysis including only PSM studies (Figure 3F).

Secondary outcomes

For neurological complications, TT demonstrated fewer events compared to MS (p-score: TT [0.95], MS [0.09]; OR: 0.57 [95% CI, 0.33-0.99]) (Table 3, Figure 2G, Table S1G). MT and MT have comparable outcomes, and the subgroup analyses also showed the same results (Figure S1-2G). For blood loss, TT (MD: −215.73 mL [95% CI, −289.13 to −142.32]) was associated with the lowest rates, followed by MT TT (MD:−76.33 mL [95% CI, −120.61 to −32.04]) and MS (p-score: TT [1.00] > MT [0.50] > MS [0.00]). However, the subgroup analysis of single surgeon (from 1 study) MT has comparable bleeding rate to MS (Figure S2L).

All techniques yielded comparable results regarding reoperation, atrial fibrillation, ventilation time, !permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation, paravalvular leakage, and conversion to sternotomy (Table 3, Figure S3A-G, Table S1H-K).

All subgroup analyses (number of centres and surgeons) showed comparable outcomes in all these outcomes except for ventilation time and atrial fibrillation. The subgroup centre analysis showed that in single centres, MT was associated with shorter ventilation time, but the multicentre result showed otherwise, thus resulting in comparable results for the overall analysis. For atrial fibrillation, the multicentre and multiple surgeons subgroup analyses showed that MT was associated with lower atrial fibrillation events than MS (Figure S1-2H-K, M-N). While the rest of the secondary outcomes are comparable between MS and MT in PSM included studies, except in conversion to sternotomy, with MT has higher conversion events (Figure 3G-N).

Publication bias

In all outcomes, the node-splitting method showed that the network was consistent showing P-value > .05 which means there are no significant differences between the direct and indirect evidence (Figure S4A-N).

For the publication bias, Egger’s test could not assess a few outcomes due to the limited amount of studies (blood loss), but roughly the overall funnel plot showed symmetry. While, for the rest of the outcomes, Egger’s test showed no bias except for mortality. We performed additional quantitative bias analysis as aforementioned. For mortality, Begg-Mazumdar test was also significant (P = .0003), but the Thompson-Sharp detected insignificant bias (P = .38). However, as at least 1 method (Begg-Mazumdar) indicated significant publication bias for mortality, publication bias is likely present and cannot be ruled out for this outcome. The lack of significance in the Thompson-Sharp test does not weaken this result. The observed significance may be influenced by studies with large sample sizes, as the Thompson-Sharp test is less sensitive to heterogeneity arising from sample size disparities (Figure S5A-N).

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis for outcomes with moderate to high heterogeneity (>50%), which included CPB time, Xc time, operative time, ICU and hospital stay, mortality, atrial fibrillation, ventilation time, bleeding, and conversion to sternotomy. This analysis was conducted based on individual studies and treatment groups.

Our sensitivity analysis revealed that no individual study significantly influenced the heterogeneity observed in each outcome, and all studies contributed similarly to the reported results, except for mortality. Studies that have large samples compared to others such as Andreas et al, Ghorheisi et al, and Yilmaz et al, may contribute to heterogeneity (Figure S6-7A-N).

DISCUSSION

Minimally invasive aortic valve replacement (MIAVR) has the potential for shorter recovery times. Enhanced recovery and patient satisfaction are increasingly important in all aspects of surgical care.7 In comparing MIAVR techniques, we did not observe differences in mortality, and a potential benefit of the TT approach for neurological complications. Furthermore, our analysis found that TT was associated with significantly shorter hospital stays than MS. In terms of ICU stay, TT was associated with the shortest duration, followed by MT and MS.

Our findings in these outcomes differ from the previous meta-analysis,7 which reported that MT was associated with a shorter hospital stay. This previous meta-analysis attributed the shorter hospital stay with MT to improved postoperative ventilation and reduced pain due to smaller incisions and the avoidance of sternal access. However, these reasons are debatable from the results of other studies. Indeed, previous studies6,7,45 associated MT with increased postoperative pain as compared to MS, possibly due to greater tissue and rib retraction needed for better visualization during the MT approach, as well as the stretching of intercostal nerves, muscles, and parietal pleura.8,45 The shorter ICU and hospital stays associated with TT in our study are likely due to reduced trauma, muscle preservation, and smaller incisions compared to MS and MT, translating into faster recovery, reduced pain, reduced bleeding rate, and need for intensive monitoring. In MT cases, the use of smaller incisions generally results in less trauma and reduced bleeding. However, the increased postoperative pain associated with MT may prolong hospital stay, leading to an overall length of stay that is comparable to MS. The NMA and most subgroup analyses (multiple surgeons and both single- and multicentre settings) consistently demonstrated decreased blood loss with MT compared to MS. Interestingly, the single-surgeon subgroup analysis showed comparable bleeding outcomes between MT and MS, though this finding was based on only one study. This suggests that the bleeding rate may also be associated with expertise. In addition, our study showed that all 3 techniques showed comparable ventilation times.8,24

For the subgroup analysis of hospital stay, when the single-centre subgroup shows a significant difference in length of hospital stay between MS and MT, but this difference is not observed in the multicentre, single-surgeon, or multiple-surgeon subgroups, the most likely cause is centre-specific factors. In single-centre studies, unique institutional protocols, perioperative care pathways, patient selection criteria, or a particularly high level of expertise with one surgical approach can lead to better outcomes for that technique, resulting in a statistically significant difference. However, when data are pooled across multiple centres or surgeons, these individual advantages are diluted by variability in surgical experience, patient populations, and hospital practices, making the outcomes between MS and MT more comparable. This highlights how institutional experience, standardization, and local expertise can influence results in single-centre studies, but may not be generalizable to broader, multicentre or more heterogeneous settings.

A similar pattern was observed in the subgroup analysis of ventilation time. Single-centre studies demonstrated a shorter ventilation time with MT than MS, whereas the multicentre data (only 2 studies) suggested a shorter ventilation time with MS. When these subgroups were combined, the overall effect was attenuated. These findings suggest that while ventilation time may vary depending on centre-specific factors or surgical protocols, the overall clinical impact between MT and MS remains similar. Further multicentre studies with larger sample sizes are needed to clarify these subgroup discrepancies.6,8,32,46

The technical demands of MIAVR also need to be highlighted. Our analysis found comparable conversion rates across all 3 techniques. Instead, the technical challenges of MIAVR may manifest in other outcomes, such as CPB time, Xc time, and overall operative time. Our study demonstrated that TT is associated with significantly longer CPB and Xc time than MS, while MT was associated to a significantly longer CPB and operative time compared to MS, but similar Xc time. Previous studies have contradictory results, with some findings similar to ours, while others presented different results.6,45 However, these outcomes are likely influenced by the surgeon’s experience, institutional volume, and the type of valve used (sutureless versus traditional). Given the steep learning curve of MIAVR, surgical outcomes can improve with experience, underscoring the importance of aligning technique choice with expertise.47,48

In single-centre studies, outcomes such as CPB, Xc, and operative times for MT and MS approaches were often comparable, suggesting that consistent protocols, standardized perioperative care, and concentrated surgical expertise can minimize procedural differences. In contrast, multicentre studies demonstrated that MT was associated with significantly longer operative times compared to MS. This likely reflects variability in surgeon experience, institutional protocols, and learning curves across different centres, where the adoption and proficiency of minimally invasive techniques may not be uniform. Similarly, when comparing single versus multiple-surgeon subgroups, studies involving multiple surgeons tended to show greater variability and, in some cases, longer operative times. This may be attributed to differences in individual surgeon experience, technique, and familiarity with minimally invasive approaches. In contrast, single-surgeon or highly specialized teams may achieve greater efficiency and consistency. These findings underscore the importance of both institutional and surgeon-specific experience in optimizing outcomes for MIAVR, and they suggest that results from high-volume, specialized centres or surgeons may not be directly generalizable to broader, multicentre or less experienced settings.6,8,32,46

Postoperative outcomes, including mortality, PPM implantation, paravalvular leakage, and reoperation rates, were comparable across all 3 techniques. Mortality similarities likely reflect the minimal impact of incision size or cannulation location on these outcomes. Paravalvular leakage is more likely influenced by prosthesis factors, including type, use of sutures, and positioning, rather than surgical approach. Reoperation rate might be influenced by patient selection, underlying valve pathology, and the surgeon’s experience, suggesting that when appropriately selected, patients undergoing any type of MIAVR can expect similar reoperation rates.6,7,49,50

For neurological complications, TT was associated with a lower complication rate compared to MS, which may be the consequence of cannulation approaches and preventive strategies like CO2 insufflation and de-airing.9,49,51

The similarity of the majority of postoperative outcomes across groups in our main and subgroup analyses was likely not influenced by the differing preoperative characteristics, particularly in terms of age and operative risk (as measured by EuroSCORE II), as these did not differ between studies. These comparable baseline characteristics reduce the risk of confounding bias and support the validity of the observed similarity in outcomes. Additionally, in most studies comparing TT with other techniques, the procedures were performed by the same surgeon, which minimizes the risk of surgeon selection bias related to anatomical considerations or surgical expertise. However, data on anatomical factors influencing the choice of surgical approach were inconsistently reported, limiting our ability to assess their potential impact.

Total costs and cost-effectiveness also need to be considered. Previous studies have associated MIAVR with moderate costs compared to transcatheter AVR (TAVR) and conventional AVR. TAVR, known for its highest upfront expenses, has higher costs than surgical AVR due to advanced technology and specialized equipment, making it less cost-effective for low- and intermediate-risk patients. MIAVR, however, offers a balance by combining reduced invasiveness and shorter hospital stays, potentially offsetting its higher procedural cost compared to conventional AVR. Although conventional AVR typically presents the lowest procedural cost, longer hospital stays can lead to higher long-term expenditures, particularly for older or more vulnerable patients. MIAVR’s potential for faster recovery rates provides a cost-effective alternative, especially for patients who benefit from minimally invasive techniques without the financial burden associated with TAVR.52–54

Future of AVR

The future of AVR lies in tailoring approaches to patient-specific needs, balancing cost, durability, and outcomes. Among surgical options, MIAVR is particularly compelling compared to conventional AVR, especially for younger and lower-risk patients, due to its benefits of faster recovery and reduced invasiveness. Conversely, TAVR is ideal for high-risk or frail patients due to its less invasive nature and shorter recovery times. However, TAVR's higher rates of paravalvular leakage, pacemaker implantations, and vascular complications, alongside its high upfront costs, limit its cost-effectiveness for intermediate- and low-risk populations.53,55–57 Moreover, the long-term durability of TAVR remains uncertain, and long-term survival may even be impaired based on recent pooled analyses of randomized trials.58

For the MIAVR approach itself, the variety of techniques available also influences clinical outcomes, as our study and other previous studies have shown. Future studies should compare all types of MIAVR with TAVR, especially TT as the most novel technique because there are no previous studies that have compared TT with TAVR. As advancements in valve technology and surgical techniques continue, the choice among MIAVR, TAVR, and conventional AVR will depend on factors such as patient age, risk profile, and expected durability. MIAVR stands out as a middle-ground option, providing a balance between the invasiveness of conventional AVR and the high cost of TAVR while ensuring robust clinical outcomes. Future studies with a longer follow-up period are essential to refine patient selection criteria, evaluate long-term durability, and address the economic implications of these evolving strategies.53,55–57 In addition, future NMAs should include more studies that compare TT with other techniques as our current NMA is only able to include a limited amount of studies that compared TT with others.

Strength and limitations

Based on our knowledge, the current study is the first to focus on minimally invasive techniques exclusively. Full sternotomy was not included in the current analyses, as these comparisons were previously reported. Furthermore, by including many studies, we have shown promising representative data.

Despite the challenges and the data we have previously presented, this study has several limitations, including potential bias due to the inclusion of only observational studies. Although we included matched population cohorts wherever available, the number of these matched populations is still limited. In addition, the performance of subgroup analyses aimed at evaluating the impact of surgeon expertise (single vs multiple surgeons) and hospital protocols (single vs multicentre), confounding factors such as pretreatment variables, surgeon expertise (depending on the learning curve), surgeon selection bias, institutional differences (surgeon experience and case volume), mix of valve types (sutureless and conventional), and unmeasured confounders may still have influenced the results.

Additionally, several included studies reported continuous outcomes as medians with ranges rather than means with standard deviations. To include these studies, we converted medians and ranges to estimated means and standard deviations using sample size-dependent methods. While this approach is widely accepted in meta-analyses, it assumes approximate normality of the underlying data and may introduce estimation bias, particularly if the original data are skewed. The lack of individual patient data also prevented us from performing data transformations (eg, log-transformation) or nonparametric analyses that might better accommodate non-normal distributions. These factors may affect the precision of our pooled estimates and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Also, most studies only reported short-term outcomes necessitating future studies to research long-term outcomes. Some studies’ outcomes also exhibited moderate to high heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis revealed that this heterogeneity was partially attributable to treatment-related factors, such as atrial fibrillation, length of stay, ventilation time, and conversion to sternotomy. However, heterogeneity in other outcomes was likely driven by the aforementioned confounding factors. Moreover, the number of studies directly comparing MS with TT (only 3) and MT with TT (only one) was limited. Additionally, subgroup analyses could only be performed for MS vs MT, since all studies comparing TT with other techniques were conducted by a single surgeon at a single centre. We also could not analyse the cost-effectiveness between these 3 techniques due to the data unavailability.

CONCLUSION

Although TT is associated with longer operative times, it was associated with faster recovery and fewer complications, including lower rates of neurological complications and bleeding, compared to MS. In contrast, MT demonstrates similar outcomes to MS in most measures, except for longer operative times. This review only includes observational studies and its results should be interpreted with caution. Future NMAs incorporating only RCTs and more studies that include comparison of TT with other techniques would be necessary to strengthen the evidence base.
