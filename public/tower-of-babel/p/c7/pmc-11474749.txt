Good morning, everyone. I am honored to be speaking to you all today about my past year as the AAST Associate Membership Mentoring Scholarship recipient. I had the privilege to learn from Dr. Sharon Henry, our AAST vice president, over the past year. When I first learned I received the scholarship, I won’t lie…my jaw was on the ground. Of all the scholarships to receive, this felt extra special! The Mentorship scholarship, unlike other scholarships, is more nebulous. It can lack definition, structure, or discrete objectives, but mentorship is something that you know it when you’ve got it. It is more often a feeling than a tangible. This can make it challenging to navigate, but it undoubtedly comes with great personal and professional rewards.

When I think about mentorship, it breaks down into two stages: finding a mentor and keeping a mentor. I bet a lot of us can recall our mentors through various career stages: medical school, residency, fellowship, and jobs. We collect them along the way either through a formal mentorship program or relationships formed organically. Luckily, in my case, the AAST mentoring scholarship made it easy for me to find a mentor. The beginning of a mentorship feels a lot like dating: it evolves over time and is a two-way street. It’s hard to walk up to a stranger and ask them ‘will you be my date?’ just like it can be hard to ask someone ‘will you be my mentor?’. It takes a connection between two people and time for a relationship to form. That’s what’s so great about this mentoring scholarship: it pairs two people who are both game to be a little uncomfortable with the intention of developing something great.

When Dr Henry and I had our first meeting, it was a little stiff. Our conversations were formal, often strictly following an agenda of topics and punctuated with some silences. I can recall the turning point in our connection. As a chief surgery resident, the past year has been a pivotal time in identity formation and career development. This led to fluctuations in my level of confidence. At our February meeting, Dr Henry remarked that I was glowing and seemed to be on a high. I shared with Dr Henry that I finally identified as a surgeon, and not just a surgical resident anymore which was a big moment. I can still see Dr Henry’s knowing smile across the Zoom screen as she recounted her own identity story during her senior years of residency with the development of confidence supported by competence. Our sharing of stories was a bonding experience.

From then on, our Zoom meetings were opened with smiles, excited waves, and catching up on how we’ve been. We not only talked about professional goals or challenges, but about our personal lives. Now there was connection between us and a wide-open two-way street of communication. What makes mentorship special is the human connection. It is two people making time and space for each other. What comes from this is personal growth and a sense of community.

Here comes the hard part: keeping a mentor. Keeping a mentor takes effort and intentionality. Thinking back to our various career stages, it often means our mentors are spread out physically and figuratively. This, compounded by the busy lives of surgeons, makes it a hard task to maintain a mentor–mentee relationship. Some of us are lucky to work in the same institutions or cities as our mentors, where it is easy for impromptu coffee chats or office sit-downs. But for other, our mentors are many states or time zones away. While the activation energy to send the text or email, schedule a call, or meet up in person can feel overwhelming, the reward of connection between people with a vested interest in each other is worth it. At our last meeting in August, Dr Henry and I shared the same sentiment that this relationship was just getting going. But that’s the best part about mentorship: it doesn’t end when the scholarship does. It is fluid, without direction, without a timeline, and it will evolve. It will take effort and intention to keep a mentor, but the reward is so great for all that effort put in.

Receiving this scholarship was truly an honor. Not only did I gain a mentor over the past year, I also gained lifelong skills, a thought process, and a relationship. I am truly thankful for the above tangible and intangible skills and experiences Dr Henry provided me with. Often, what we receive out of mentorship cannot be quantified but the impact is profound.

I charge all of you to celebrate mentorship relationships this week at the AAST: grab a cup of coffee, have a conversation, and foster connection. Use this shared time to pour energy into these relationships. I guarantee you the rewards are limitless.
