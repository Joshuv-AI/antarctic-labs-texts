Pharmacokinetic Comparability Assessment of Tixagevimab and Cilgavimab Developed for COVID-19 Prevention and Treatment Using Standard Exposure Metrics and Partial Area Under the Curve
Authors: Huixia Zhang, Weifeng Tang, Samuel Sadow, Roberta Baronio, Antonella Nadia Tullio, Lauren Hirao, Vitalina Dzutseva, Cecil Chi-Keung Chen, Alexandre Kiazand, Lee-Jah Chang, Taylor S Cohen, Michael Gibbs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveAZD7442 is a combination of extended half-life monoclonal antibodies (mAbs; tixagevimab and cilgavimab), developed to neutralize SARS-CoV-2. This open-label Phase 1 study compared pharmacokinetics (PK) of three AZD7442 formulations in which component mAbs were (1) co-formulated versus separately formulated and (2) derived from clonal cell line or cell pool material when separately formulated.MethodsHealthy adults were randomized 1:1:1 to receive intramuscular (IM) AZD7442 300 mg (150 mg each of tixagevimab and cilgavimab) via one injection of co-formulated tixagevimab and cilgavimab from clonal cell line material (treatment A), or two sequential injections of tixagevimab and cilgavimab formulated separately either from clonal cell line (treatment B) or cell pool material (treatment C). Serial blood samples were collected up to 360 days post-dose to evaluate serum concentrations and anti-drug antibody responses. Standard exposure metrics (Cmax, AUClast and AUCinf) were used for PK comparability assessment between treatments (A vs B, B vs C and A vs C). A post hoc analysis using partial area under the curve (pAUC) was also conducted.ResultsIn total, 224 participants were randomized and dosed. Serum concentration–time profiles overlapped post dosing across treatments. Median time to reach peak concentration occurred within 2 weeks with mean terminal half-life 74–84 days. Pharmacokinetic comparability for tixagevimab, cilgavimab and AZD7442 was demonstrated between treatments, with geometric mean ratios (GMR) and 90% CI within 0.8000–1.2500 using traditional exposure metrics (Cmax, AUClast and AUCinf) and partial AUCs (pAUCs; AUC0–d30, AUC0–d60, AUC0–d90, or AUC0–d180), except for comparison of treatment A versus B for tixagevimab, where the lower 90% CI bound for AUClast GMR (0.7933) was slightly below the pre-defined threshold of 0.8000. Low immunogenicity and acceptable safety profiles were observed across treatments.ConclusionsPharmacokinetic comparability of tixagevimab, cilgavimab and AZD7442 was demonstrated between the different treatments based on analyses using traditional bioequivalence metrics or pAUCs. Partial AUC could serve as an exposure metric when evaluating PK comparability for extended half-life mAbs.Trial RegistryClinical trials registration number: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05166421.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-026-01631-7.

Key Points

Comparable pharmacokinetics of tixagevimab, cilgavimab and AZD7442 was demonstrated when tixagevimab and cilgavimab were (1) co-formulated versus separately formulated and (2) derived from clonal cell line or cell pool material, using traditional bioequivalence metrics and partial area under the curves (pAUCs).Current data support that pAUCs could be used as an alternative exposure metric in lieu of AUC to the last measurable concentration (AUClast) or AUC extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf) in assessing pharmacokinetic comparability for monoclonal antibodies with extended half-lives.

Introduction

AZD7442 (tixagevimab-cilgavimab) is a combination (1:1 ratio) of two extended half-life, human immunoglobulin G (IgG) monoclonal antibodies (mAbs). AZD7442 was authorized for pre-exposure prophylaxis and/or treatment of COVID-19 in individuals aged ≥ 12 years, weighing at least 40 kg [1, 2]. The commercial product of AZD7442 was derived from a clonal cell line (clonal cell line material), initially provided in two separate vials for injection (one for each component mAb). However, due to the rapid development during the COVID-19 pandemic, the clinical development program utilized product derived from non-clonal cell pool (cell pool material) until clonal cell line material was available. The co-formulated product was developed to reduce handling and potential administration errors.

Tixagevimab and cilgavimab were engineered with YTE (M252Y/S254T/T256E) substitution in the fragment crystallizable (Fc) region to increase FcRn binding at low pH, enhancing recycling and extending serum half-life [3, 4]. The TM (L234F/L235E/P331S) substitution was also added to the Fc region to abolish Fc gamma receptor (FcγR) and complement component C1q binding, which is expected to reduce the risk of antibody-dependent enhanced disease [4]. As a product with no endogenous human target, AZD7442 and its component mAbs have exhibited linear pharmacokinetics (PK) across a wide dose range (300–3000 mg of AZD7442) administered intramuscularly (IM) or intravenously (IV) in adults, with a serum terminal half-life (t1/2) of approximately 80–90 days [3–6]. Following IM administration, maximal serum concentration (Cmax) was usually reached within 2 weeks. Population PK analysis indicated that AZD7442 PK were similar across populations, and anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) did not have a clinically meaningful impact on PK [7].

This open-label Phase 1 trial was conducted using traditional bioequivalence metrics (Cmax, AUClast and AUCinf) to assess the comparability of PK, safety and immunogenicity following a single AZD7442 IM dose when: 1) the two component mAbs were co-formulated or separately formulated, and 2) the dosing material of the individual mAbs was derived from clonal cell line or cell pool material. A post hoc analysis was conducted to assess if partial AUC (pAUC) could be used as a metric in PK comparability assessments.

Methods

Trial Design

This randomized, open-label, three-arm, parallel-treatment study assessed the safety, PK, and immunogenicity of a single AZD7442 IM dose in healthy eligible adults when administered as a co-formulated product compared with separately formulated component mAbs, and when derived from clonal cell line material compared with cell pool material. The study was approved by institutional review boards and conducted across 14 US sites. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The trial consisted of a screening period of ≤ 28 days, after which randomization and dosing occurred on Day 1, followed by a follow-up period of 1 year.

Participants were randomized on Day 1 in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three treatments to receive a single IM AZD7442 300 mg dose (150 mg each of tixagevimab and cilgavimab) via the lateral thigh, the gluteal dorsal, or the gluteal ventral, and stratified according to body weight (< 70 kg, ≤ 70 to < 80 kg, and ≥ 80 kg) and COVID-19 vaccination status (unvaccinated or vaccinated against COVID-19). Treatment A was a single AZD7442 dose of co-formulated tixagevimab and cilgavimab derived from clonal cell line material (the planned subsequent commercial product). Treatment B was AZD7442 administered as consecutive doses of separately formulated tixagevimab and cilgavimab derived from clonal cell line material (the initial commercial product during the pandemic). Treatment C was AZD7442 as consecutive doses of separately formulated tixagevimab and cilgavimab derived from cell pool material (the initial clinical trial formulation). When administered separately, tixagevimab was administered first, immediately followed by cilgavimab on the opposite site of the same injection region.

Participants abstained from alcohol and strenuous activity for 72 h and caffeine-containing foods/beverages for 24 h prior to study Day 1 and every outpatient visit. On study Day 1 in the clinical unit participants received a standard diet and rested for 1 h after dosing.

Participants

Eligible healthy participants were aged ≥18 years weighing 50–110 kg and screening BMI of 18–30 kg/m2, and either a documented negative SARS-CoV-2 reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction test within 3 days before Day 1 or a negative rapid SARS-CoV-2 antigen test on Day 1 (pre-dose). Participants could be unvaccinated or vaccinated with ≥1 locally approved/authorized COVID-19 vaccine doses, with vaccinated participants not in receipt of the COVID-19 vaccine ≤14 days of Day 1. These criteria allowed a sensitive, controlled and ethical way to detect true formulation differences in exposure with lower variability and risk.

Ineligible participants were those with a known history of allergy or reaction to tixagevimab or cilgavimab, or previous hypersensitivity or severe adverse reaction following IM administration of a mAb, and those presenting with a significant infection or acute illness (including fever >37.8°C) ≤1 day before randomization. Also excluded were individuals with immunodeficiency due to illness or drugs, a history of SARS or Middle East Respiratory Syndrome infection, clinical signs/symptoms consistent with COVID-19 within 4 weeks of screening, history of malignancy or clinically significant bleeding disorder or of significant bleeding/bruising following IM injections or venipuncture, active hepatitis B or C infection or positive test for hepatitis C or hepatitis B surface antigen at screening, receipt of a mAb within 6 months or five antibody half-lives (whichever is longer) prior to screening, prior receipt of any mAb indicated for prevention or treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infection or COVID-19, or any other significant disease that may greatly increase the participant risk in the study. As the study did not provide prospects of clinical benefits, which is required for studying in pediatric participants, healthy individuals aged 12 to < 18 years were not enrolled.

Blood Sampling and Bioanalysis

Blood samples for serum mAb concentration measurement were collected before the dose on study Day 1, and on study Days 2, 5, 8, 15, 22, 31, 61, 91, 181, 271, and 361 (all cohorts). Blood samples for ADA assessment were collected before the dose on study Day 1, and on study Days 31, 91, 181, and 361 (all cohorts). Serum concentrations of tixagevimab and cilgavimab as well as ADAs were analyzed as reported previously. Briefly, bioanalytical analyses of tixagevimab and cilgavimab in serum were performed by PPD Laboratories (Richmond, VA, USA), using immunocapture to SARS-CoV-2 receptor-binding domain followed by denaturation and detection of AZD7442 specific peptides by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry [4, 8]. The lower level of quantification for both tixagevimab and cilgavimab was 0.30 µg/mL. Serum AZD7442 concentration was the sum of serum concentrations of tixagevimab and cilgavimab. The PK parameters of AZD7442, tixagevimab and cilgavimab were derived using non-compartmental methods in Phoenix® WinNonlin® (Certara, Version 8.1 or higher).

Serum samples for ADA detection were determined using a three-tier testing scheme [5]. Briefly, samples were screened for ADA against tixagevimab and cilgavimab separately using an electro-chemiluminescence (ECL) solution-phase bridging method, validated by PPD Laboratories. Diluted samples were incubated in a solution phase with biotinylated tixagevimab or cilgavimab and ruthenium ([sulfo-TAG]-labeled tixagevimab or cilgavimab). Samples were reported screen positive for ADA in the screening assay if the mean ECL value was at or above the ECL value of the plate-specific cut point factor. Screen positive samples were retested in a confirmation assay, where samples were analyzed both in the absence and presence of excess drug to determine if the sample’s positive response is specific to tixagevimab or cilgavimab. Titers were measured in confirmed positive samples and were reported as the reciprocal of the highest twofold dilution that measured positive in the assay, before returning a negative response. Titers for negative samples were reported as < 80 for tixagevimab or < 40 for cilgavimab; the minimum required dilution of the assay was 1:80 and 1:40, respectively.

Treatment-emergent (TE)-ADA to AZD7442 was defined as either treatment-induced ADA positive (ADA positive post-baseline but not detected at baseline for either or both of tixagevimab and cilgavimab), or treatment-boosted ADA positive (baseline ADA titer that was boosted by ≥ 4-fold following drug administration for either or both of tixagevimab and cilgavimab).

Statistical Analyses

Sample Size Considerations

Preliminary analysis of PK data from the first-in-human study indicated that after an IM dose of 300 mg AZD7442 (two consecutive doses of tixagevimab and cilgavimab), the coefficient of variation (CV%) for Cmax of the individual mAbs, tixagevimab and cilgavimab were 35.6 and 38.5%, respectively, while the CV% for AUCs of both individual mAbs were < 36.4%. Therefore, assuming a mean ratio of one between comparators, 69 participants per treatment were expected to provide at least 95% power to demonstrate comparability (90% confidence intervals [CIs] contained within 0.8000 and 1.2500).

Bioequivalence Evaluation

Statistical comparisons were performed based on linear mixed effects analysis of variance models with the natural logarithms of primary PK parameters (Cmax, AUClast, AUCinf, and pAUCs) for tixagevimab, cilgavimab and AZD7442 as response variables. Treatment, injection site, COVID-19 vaccination status and continuous baseline body weight on the log scale were included as fixed effects, and participant as a random effect. Sex was not included in the model, as previous population PK analysis found no significant effect of sex on the PK of tixagevimab and cilgavimab [7].

One statistical model was fit for each PK parameter. No adjustments for multiple comparisons or multiplicity were made. Transformed back from the logarithmic scale, the geometric least square mean (and their two-sided 95% CIs) of each treatment was calculated and the geometric mean ratio (GMR) as well as their 90% CIs for each pairwise comparison was estimated. Bioequivalence was demonstrated if the GMRs (and their 90% CIs) fell within a pre‑defined limit of 0.8000–1.2500.

Demographics, baseline characteristics, safety and immunogenicity variables were summarized using descriptive statistics.

All analyses were conducted with SAS, Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Correlation Analysis

Pairwise associations among PK parameters were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients computed on the natural log–transformed values of each parameter for each drug component, by treatment group.

Safety Assessments

Safety was assessed throughout the study, including adverse events (AEs), serious adverse events (SAEs), adverse events of special interest (AESIs) and stratified per COVID-19 vaccination status at randomization. Adverse events of special interest were defined as anaphylaxis and other serious hypersensitivity reactions including immune complex disease, injection-site reactions and cardiac ischemia, cardiac failure, and thrombotic events. Adverse events of special interest were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA; version 26.0).

Results

Participants

In total, 224 participants were randomized and dosed with AZD7442, 193 completed the study (treatment A [n = 64], B [n = 67] and C [n = 62]), and 31 withdrew from the study (treatment A [n = 12], B [n = 5], and C [n = 14]). Of these withdrawals, 12 were lost to follow-up (treatment A [n = 4], B [n = 1], and C [n = 7]), 14 were participants’ decisions, and five were for ‘other’ reasons. No withdrawal was due to study treatment. Demographics were similar and well balanced among the treatments (Table 1). The mean age of participants was 42.0 years (94.2% were aged <65 years), mean body weight was 77.0 kg, 57% were male, 79.0% had received one or more previous COVID-19 vaccine doses.

Pharmacokinetics

Overlapping serum concentration-time profiles were observed for tixagevimab, cilgavimab and AZD7442, respectively, across all three treatments following a single IM dose of 300 mg AZD7442 (Fig. 1). Peak serum mAb concentrations were reached within 2 weeks post dose, with a median Tmax of 8.1 to 14.0 days and a range of 3–91 days (Table 2). Thereafter, mAb concentrations slowly declined in an apparent monophasic manner, and remained quantifiable until the last collected sample on Day 360 post-dose. Elimination phase of the PK profiles appeared to be reached for all mAbs from about 30 days post-dose (Fig. 1).

Serum PK parameters were comparable in all three treatment arms for tixagevimab, cilgavimab and AZD7442 (Table 2). Across the groups, geomean terminal elimination T1/2 was in the range of 77.8– 83.7 days and 73.7– 79.2 days for tixagevimab and cilgavimab, respectively. Based on the estimation of the geometric coefficient of variation (gCV%), the variability was comparable for the standard exposure metrics (Cmax, AUClast, AUCinf) and partial AUCs (Table 2). Anti-drug Antibodies

The prevalence of ADA to tixagevimab, cilgavimab, and AZD7442 (proportion of participants who were ADA positive at any time) was in the range of 1.4–12.3% across the three treatment groups, and ADA incidence (the proportion of TE-ADA positive participants) was at 0–1.4% (Table 3). Of the AZD7442 ADA positive participants, the majority were classified as non–TE-ADA positive in all treatment arms. Only one participant in Treatment A showed persistent TE-ADA positivity to cilgavimab. This participant tested ADA positive post-dose on Day 30 (titer 80), Day 90 (titer of 40) and Day 180 (titer of 40). Additionally, one participant in Treatment C was transiently TE-ADA positive to tixagevimab with a titer of 320 on Day 30 post-dose. There was no evidence of temporal patten in ADA incidence across the study groups. Anti-drug antibody-positive participants did not exhibit an increased safety risk (see Sect. 3.4).

Safety

The overall summary of AEs reported by at least 5% or more participants is presented in Table S1. The most common AE in all three treatment arms was COVID-19 (2.6–13.2% participants across treatment groups). Overall, most of the AEs were reported of mild or moderate severity. Adverse events of special interest considered related to AZD7442 by the Investigator were reported in 3 (3.9%) participants in treatment group A (events of hypoaesthesia, palpitations and injection-site pain), 1 (1.4%) in treatment group B (injection-site pain) and 1 (1.3%) in treatment group C (injection-site pain).

During the study, 2 (2.6%) SAEs were reported in two participants in treatment A arm and in 1 (1.3%) treatment C participant. Of SAEs, in treatment group A, one participant with familial type V hyperlipidemia and atherosclerosis reported an event of coronary artery occlusion and one participant had an event of major depression. One participant in treatment group C with previous history of hypertension, angina pectoris and myocardial bridging reported an event of angina pectoris; none of these SAEs were considered related to AZD7442 by the Investigator.

There were no fatal AEs or AEs leading to study discontinuation. The AESIs of injection-site pain were reported in 1 (1.3%) participant in treatment A and 1 (1.4%) in treatment B, respectively. No AESIs of serious hypersensitivity reactions or immune complex disease were reported. No clinically relevant trends were observed for laboratory parameters, vital signs or electrocardiograms. The most frequently reported AE was COVID-19 infection. It was noted that fewer COVID-19 infections were observed in the treatment A group compared with treatments B and C. However, when comparing treatments B and C, which used clonal cell line and cell pool materials respectively, no difference in infection rates were noted between the two groups. For other AEs, no notable differences in occurrence of AEs were reported among the treatment groups or between the previously vaccinated or non-vaccinated participants.

The two participants who were TE-ADA positive had non-SAEs (palpitation and headache, respectively) on Day 2, and nerve compression in one of the participants on Day 274. Both participants had low ADA titers (maximum 80 and 320, respectively). The timing and type of AEs in these two participants did not suggest an association between the occurrence of these AEs and AZD7442 ADA.

Discussion

AZD7442 administered IM or IV has been extensively studied in healthy volunteers and participants with varying degrees of risk of COVID-19 disease [4–6, 9–13]. The safety, ADA and PK findings from the current study are consistent with these previously reported studies.

Analysis using traditional BE metrics (Cmax, AUClast and AUCinf) have shown that AZD7442 and its component mAbs have comparable PK regardless of whether the component mAbs were co-formulated or separately formulated (treatment A vs treatment B comparison). The dosing material (cell pool or clonal cell line material) also had no impact on the PK (treatment B vs treatment C comparison). The same conclusions could be drawn from the pAUC post hoc analysis (Fig. 2). Due to the large number of comparisons in this study, the minor deviation on the lower bound of the 90% CI observed for tixagevimab AUClast when treatment A was compared to treatment B may be expected and may not occur with other study designs involving fewer comparisons. The results suggest that for such molecules with extended half-life, a pAUC approach with significantly shorter follow-up period can reliably assess PK comparability and it offers a practical alternative to the conventional metrics of AUClast or AUCinf.

It is standard to collect blood samples to 3–5 elimination half-lives of a drug to obtain reliable and accurate estimation of PK parameters. In the case of AZD7442 with its half-life of about 3 months, this required our study duration of 360 days, even with a parallel study design. This was burdensome for the participants, which, despite having a completion rate of 86.2% (193/224), the trial presented logistical challenges to participants. Use of pAUC instead of the standard PK metrics could significantly reduce study length, reducing the participant burden and improving study feasibility.

Pharmacokinetic comparability studies evaluate the rate and extent of drug absorption, and the main concern is often whether modifications to the reference treatment affect the absorption phase of the PK profile. In line with this notion, FDA guidance recommends that in determining bioavailability or bioequivalence of a drug product with a long half-life (≥ 24 h [14]), the sample collection time should be sufficiently long enough to ensure completion of absorption of the drug substance. In such cases, Cmax and a suitably truncated AUC are recommended metrics for characterizing peak and total drug exposure, respectively [14]. This recommendation has been adopted in many regulatory submissions, with pAUC being accepted in PK comparability studies as an alternative to AUClast or AUCinf for drugs with long half-lives. For example, AUC0–72h has been accepted as an appropriate exposure metric for BE evaluation for warfarin and lusutrombopag [15, 16]. However, these recommendations are mainly intended for small molecule products and may not be optimal for mAb products. In contrast to small molecules with Tmax and half-life values generally measured in hours, conventional IgG mAbs typically reach Tmax several days after extravascular dose administration, and have much longer terminal elimination half-life, normally in the range of 10–30 days [17].

Use of pAUC as an alternative measure in PK comparability assessment for therapeutic biologics was systemically evaluated in a recent publication [18]. Concordance between pAUC and AUCinf was reported in 15 approved IgG products, administered using either a prefilled syringe or an autoinjector presentation for subcutaneous administration. The reported mean Tmax and terminal half-life for these IgG products is about 5 days (range 2.9–5.5 days) and 15 days (range 4.3–28.0 days), respectively. The analyses identified that pAUCs from time zero to Day 14, 21, or 28 showed 100% agreement with AUCinf across all 15 evaluated products, strongly suggesting that using pAUC can provide reliable comparability assessments for biologics with long half-lives.

The appropriate time to truncate AUC for PK comparability assessments should be based on sufficient understanding of the PK profile of mAb from prior studies where PK, ADA and safety have been well characterized for the relevant administration site. For new formulations or presentations of a drug product that are expected to have an impact on absorption, the cutoff time should cover the complete absorption phase, as well as a portion of the terminal eliminate phase to increase confidence in substituting pAUC for AUCinf. The inter-subject variability in pAUCs compared to that of AUClast and AUCinf should also be considered when selecting the most suitable metric.

Applying this approach, we found all four pAUCs were equivalent to AUClast and AUCinf for PK comparability assessment in our study where the IM injection sites included the anterolateral thigh and gluteal regions. Among these, AUC0–d90 emerged as the optimal substitute for AUClast or AUCinf. Firstly, AUC0–d90 not only fully captures the absorption phase but also contains part of the log-linear terminal elimination phase, as shown by the mean PK profiles transitioning to this phase by Day 30 post dose (Fig. 1). During this period, serum concentrations decline in a predictable and exponential fashion, allowing reliable extrapolation from AUC0–d90 to AUCinf. Importantly, AUC0–d90 accounts for approximately 50% of the total drug exposure (geometric mean of AUC0–d90 compared with AUCinf), and the remaining exposure could reliably be predicted from the first half of the curve. In contrast, AUC0–d30 and AUC0–d60 do not fully cover the absorption phase, particularly in individuals with delayed Tmax (Table 2), and AUC0–d180, although capturing a longer duration does not provide additional critical insight beyond AUC0–d90 for PK comparability assessment.

Secondly, AUC0–d90 demonstrated the lowest inter-subject variability among all AUC estimates (Table 2; Fig. S1). The higher variability observed for AUC0–d30 and AUC0–d60 is associated with variable absorption among individuals, while the increased variability for AUC0–d180 could be due to possible study-related factors like long sampling interval and greater heterogeneity in clearance among participants. Our population PK analysis identified body weight as a significant covariate affecting both volume of distribution and clearance [7]; however, this variable was well balanced across groups (Table 1) and was included as a fixed effect in our statistical model (Table S2) thus minimizing its potential influence on the treatment comparisons.

Thirdly, AUC0–d90 showed strong correlations of greater than 88% with AUClast or AUCinf for all mAbs across treatment arms (Table 4). As expected, earlier truncated time points require more extrapolation to estimate AUCinf, resulting in lower certainty and reduced correlations.

Finally, ADA incidence to tixagevimab, cilgavimab, and AZD7442 was low, ranging from zero to 1.4% across treatments, and with no discernible temporal patterns. This is consistent with previous studies where there was no detectable effect of ADA on PK up to 15 months after administration of a single dose of AZD7442 [7, 9]. Consequently, the presence of ADA is not expected to affect the validity or utility of pAUC, further supporting its application in further studies.

Taken together, these findings highlight AUC0–d90 as the optimal metric by striking a balance between increasing study efficiency, reducing participant burden and maintaining scientific rigor in PK comparability assessment.

One limitation of the study is that the use of pAUC for PK comparability assessment was evaluated post hoc, and future prospective validation is recommended. While our current immunogenicity results and previous studies show low incidence and no ADA effect on PK, relying solely on pAUC in early-phase development could risk missing late-onset ADAs or safety events. Therefore, long-term follow-up studies to fully characterize immunogenicity and safety profiles remain essential to complement PK comparability trials.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that AZD7442 and its component mAbs tixagevimab and cilgavimab exhibit comparable PK regardless of formulation or source material, as assessed by traditional bioequivalence metrics. The ADAs and safety profiles were comparable across all treatment groups and consistent with findings from previous studies. Additionally, post hoc analysis suggested that pAUC, especially pAUC0–d90, can serve as a reliable substitute for AUClast and AUCinf for PK comparability assessment in mAbs with a median Tmax of about 2 weeks and extended half-lives of approximately 80–90 days. This approach may allow for shorter follow-up periods in future studies, but the choice of pAUC timepoint should be guided by a thorough understanding of the PK characteristics and any potential impact of ADA on PK and clinical response.
