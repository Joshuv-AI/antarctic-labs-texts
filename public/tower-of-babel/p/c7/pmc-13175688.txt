Combined Surgical Strategy for Large Cystic Brain Metastases: A Case Series of Fractionated Stereotactic Radiotherapy With Cyst Drainage
Authors: Kei Iwamoto, Jo Sasame, Shigeo Matsunaga, Nagatsuki Tomura, Fukutaro Ohgaki, Shuto Fushimi, Takashi Shuto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStereotactic radiotherapy (SRT) is a less invasive, widely accepted treatment for brain metastases, particularly in patients with poor general condition. However, large cystic metastases can complicate radiotherapy because of their morphology. This study evaluated the efficacy and safety of continuous cyst drainage under natural pressure combined with fractionated SRT (f-SRT) for these lesions.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients treated at Yokohama Rosai Hospital between August 2022 and February 2024. Eligible patients had cystic brain metastases ≥10 cm3 and underwent cyst drainage under local or general anesthesia, followed by f-SRT (35 Gy in 5 fractions). Outcomes included Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), tumor and cyst volumes, local control, and neurological symptoms.ResultsFour patients (one male, three females; median age, 52 years) were analyzed. Primary cancers were lung (n=1), breast (n=1), breast and lung (n=1), and esophageal (n=1). After drainage, median KPS increased from 65 to 70, and neurological symptoms improved in three cases. Tumor volume decreased from 35.6 cm3 to 12.4 cm3 (65.5% reduction). No treatment-related complications or radiation-induced adverse events were observed.ConclusionContinuous cyst drainage under natural pressure combined with f-SRT appears safe and effective for large cystic metastases, enabling continuation of f-SRT in patients who are not suitable candidates for resection and providing a minimally invasive treatment option.Keywords: Cystic brain metastasis, Continuous cyst drainage, Fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy, Gamma Knife, Adaptive radiosurgery

INTRODUCTION

Metastatic brain tumors occur in 10%–40% of patients with cancer [1,2], with a median survival of approximately 1 month if left untreated [1,3,4]. Recent advancements in chemotherapy have improved cancer control and extended patient survival, leading to an increased incidence of metastatic brain tumors [5]. Treatment options for metastatic brain tumors include chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery [1,2,3,4]. However, because many conventional chemotherapies have limited penetration across the blood–brain barrier [6], metastatic brain tumors are often difficult to control with chemotherapy alone. Moreover, many patients with metastatic brain tumors present with poor general health [7,8]; therefore, systemic treatment should be individualized and combined with local therapy. Among local options, Gamma Knife-based stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT) is frequently preferred because it is minimally invasive and provides reliable local control [4,8,9,10].

Fractionated SRT (f-SRT) using frameless Gamma Knife systems has shown favorable tumor control rates for large metastatic brain tumors [8]. SRT using linear accelerators (LINAC) also achieves tumor control rates comparable to Gamma Knife [11], expanding the scope of SRT for metastatic brain tumors. Although surgical resection can provide immediate relief of the mass effect, it carries a risk of tumor dissemination [12] and often requires suspension of systemic therapy [13], potentially delaying treatment of the primary malignancy. Therefore, f-SRT is increasingly prioritized as a non-invasive alternative. However, tumor control in f-SRT is more challenging for cystic-dominant lesions than for nodular-dominant lesions [1,14]. Furthermore, large lesions exceeding 10 cm3 often result in neurological symptoms due to the mass effect, making f-SRT alone insufficient for effective management [10,15,16].

Recent studies have reported favorable tumor control rates for large cystic brain tumors using cyst drainage techniques, such as stereotactic aspiration or Ommaya reservoir placement, followed by radiosurgery [2,3,4,7,9,10,15,17]. Despite these advances, these methods provide only temporary benefits, as residual tumor volumes often remain large and irregular, complicating effective radiosurgery [17,18]. Complications, such as cystic fluid aspiration, tube obstruction, and intracranial pressure fluctuations associated with rapid volume changes, have also been reported [10], posing potential risks to patients.

We propose a novel treatment approach that combines continuous cyst drainage under natural pressure release with f-SRT. This method aims to achieve stable tumor volume reduction through gradual cyst fluid drainage, thereby enabling safe and effective radiation therapy. The objective of this study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of this combined therapy and present a new treatment option for patients with large cystic metastatic brain tumors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Eligibility criteria

Patients with large cystic metastatic brain tumors (>10 cm3 in volume) who underwent f-SRT at Yokohama Rosai Hospital between August 2022 and February 2024 were included in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: predominantly cystic tumor, with a pre-treatment volume exceeding 10 cm3; and extracranial metastases with Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score ≥40 [19].

Patients were excluded if they required continuous oxygen therapy, had advanced cardiovascular morbidity with unstable vital signs despite preserved KPS (Recursive Partitioning Analysis class 3 [20]), had extracranial metastatic disease with associated organ failure at the time of treatment, or had tumors without a dominant cystic component.

This report was prepared in accordance with the CARE checklist to ensure completeness and transparency [21].

Cyst drainage

All patients underwent continuous cyst drainage under local or general anesthesia. Tumor and cyst locations were precisely identified using the Stealth Station S7 navigation system (Medtronic). A burr hole was created at the site closest to the brain surface. The cyst was punctured using a ventricular needle, and a 2 mm-diameter drainage tube (Phycon, Fuji Systems) was inserted under navigation guidance, with the tip accurately positioned in the central portion of the cyst. The procedures were performed by a neurosurgical team under the supervision of a senior neurosurgeon, using standard navigation-guided techniques.

Postoperatively, the drainage bag was secured at the bedside, and continuous drainage under natural pressure release was performed while the patient was maintained in a flat, supine position. Cyst shrinkage was confirmed using contrast-enhanced MRI or CT on the day after the procedure. Owing to continuous cyst drainage, patients were required to remain in the supine position throughout the course of radiation therapy, and the drainage tube was removed after completing the treatment.

Stereotactic radiotherapy

Following the confirmation of cyst reduction, the Gamma Knife treatment plan was developed using the Leksell Gamma Plan system (Elekta Instrument AB). In contrast, the LINAC treatment plan was created using Brainlab Elements (Brainlab AG). In both planning software platforms, treatment planning was based on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (Gd-T1WI) co-registered with planning CT. The residual cyst cavity after drainage was included in the target volume in all cases. The prescribed dose of 35 Gy in five fractions was delivered specifically to the drained cystic lesions. All patients received this treatment using either a Gamma Knife Icon (Elekta Instrument AB) or TrueBeam STx with Novalis Radiosurgery (Varian Medical Systems). Concomitant smaller lesions were treated simultaneously, using single fraction (SRS) with the Gamma Knife or f-SRT with TrueBeam STx. Gamma Knife was selected for patients presenting with multiple small solid brain metastases requiring simultaneous treatment, whereas TrueBeam STx with Novalis Radiosurgery was preferred for those with multiple cystic lesions, owing to its superior flexibility in managing extensive cystic components.

In the initial cases, MRI (Gd-T1WI) was obtained prior to each fraction; however, as these early experiences demonstrated that cyst volume remained relatively stable, subsequent cases underwent an interim MRI evaluation after the weekend (no irradiation was performed during weekends) or after fraction 3. In contrast, lesions treated with TrueBeam STx were monitored using plain CT, as Brainlab Elements enables accurate CT-based comparisons. When morphological changes of the tumor, such as displacement beyond the planned target volume or marked over-shrinkage, were observed, additional contrast-enhanced MRI (Gd-T1WI) was performed and treatment replanning was undertaken as required. Furthermore, all patients underwent contrast-enhanced MRI after the completion of f-SRT to assess changes in tumor size and the cystic component.

Management of perilesional edema

Steroids and glycerol were administered to all patients throughout the treatment course as standard management of perilesional edema.

Endpoints, data collection, and follow-up

The primary endpoints were local control of the cystic lesion, avoidance of radiation-induced complications, and avoidance of neurological death (explored descriptively). Local control was defined as the absence of an increase in lesion volume and the absence of new nodular enhancement within the treated target on serial contrast-enhanced MRI (or CT when MRI was unavailable). Radiation-induced complications were assessed based on clinical findings and conventional MRI/CT findings during follow-up. Secondary assessments included changes in tumor volume after cyst drainage, subsequent volume stability, and the impact on radiotherapy planning.

Collected data included patient demographics (age, sex, and primary cancer type); KPS scores at pre-f-SRT, post-f-SRT, and final follow-up; tumor volumes (pre-drainage, post-drainage, and during irradiation); cyst reduction rates; post-treatment tumor control; and neurological symptom progression. Tumor volumes were measured on contrast-enhanced MRI or plain CT and analyzed using the Leksell Gamma Plan system (Elekta Instrument AB) by an experienced neurosurgeon.

Follow-up consisted of contrast-enhanced MRI every 3–6 months in patients surviving beyond 3 months, whereas those with shorter follow-up were assessed based on neurological symptoms and any available imaging. Tumor recurrence and development of new lesions were also monitored during follow-up.

Ethical approval

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Yokohama Rosai Hospital (approval number: 2024-19). As the study involved retrospective analysis of existing medical records and databases without direct interaction or intervention with participants, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. However, all patients were provided with comprehensive information regarding the study and given the opportunity to raise questions or concerns. Additionally, an opt-out mechanism was implemented to allow individuals to decline participation if they wished.

RESULTS

Case 1 (initial case)

A man in his 50s with metastatic lung cancer presented with progressive left hemiparesis. At the time of presentation, his KPS was 80. MRI demonstrated a large cystic lesion (39.8 cm3) in the left parietal lobe adjacent to the central sulcus (Fig. 1A), with surrounding peritumoral edema. The hemiparesis was attributed to the mass effect of the cystic component. Because the lesion was located near the central sulcus, surgical resection was deemed high-risk owing to the potential permanent neurological deficits, and f-SRT was selected as a safer alternative. To enable safer irradiation, cyst drainage was performed under local anesthesia on day -1, resulting in a 63.0% reduction in tumor volume to 14.8 cm3 (Fig. 1E) prior to f-SRT. The patient’s hemiparesis resolved immediately after drainage, and his KPS improved accordingly. f-SRT for a large cystic tumor using the Gamma Knife Icon system (35 Gy in 5 fractions over days 0–7) started on the day after surgery. Contrast-enhanced MRI was performed before fractions 2, 3, and 4 on days 2, 5, and 6, revealing further cyst shrinkage that required treatment replanning. The large cystic tumor volume decreased to 7.7 cm3 at the final fraction, with shrinkage observed in the cystic and solid nodular components. After f-SRT, the tumor remained reduced, with a volume of 2.6 cm3 at 1 month and 0.73 cm3 at 3 months. The treated lesions showed sustained volume reduction without recurrence or radiation necrosis over 18 months of follow-up. At completion of f-SRT, his KPS had improved from 80 to 90 and was maintained at 90 at the last follow-up.

Case 2

A woman in her 50s with metastatic breast cancer presented with progressive left-sided weakness. At the time of presentation, her primary disease was poorly controlled, with the tumor protruding to the body surface and signs of cancer cachexia. Her KPS was 50, and she was considered unfit for invasive craniotomy. MRI demonstrated a large cystic lesion in the left frontal lobe measuring 68.6 cm3, producing a mass effect with extensive surrounding peritumoral edema (Fig. 1B), with six additional brain metastases. As an initial treatment, single-fraction irradiation with a marginal dose of 20 Gy was delivered to the smaller lesions. After SRS, to enable radiotherapy for the cystic lesion, burr-hole cyst drainage was performed under local anesthesia on day -1, reducing the tumor volume to 27.9 cm3, a 59.3% reduction (Fig. 1F) prior to f-SRT. The patient’s left hemiparesis improved immediately after drainage. Subsequently, f-SRT (35 Gy in 5 fractions) for a large cystic tumor and concomitant small tumors was administered using the Gamma Knife Icon system over days 0–6, starting on the day after surgery. Contrast-enhanced MRI scans performed before fractions 2 and 4 on days 1 and 5 demonstrated gradual cyst shrinkage, necessitating treatment replanning. The large cystic tumor volume decreased to 24.4 cm3 at the final fraction, with shrinkage observed in the cystic and solid nodular components. However, during the treatment course, thoracic spinal metastases were identified, resulting in progressive paraplegia. Given the uncontrolled primary disease and multiple metastatic lesions, the patient was transitioned to palliative care, and follow-up MRI could not be obtained. Her KPS declined from 50 to 30 after completion of f-SRT, after which she was transferred to palliative care hospital.

Case 3

A woman in her 40s with metastatic breast and lung cancer presented with headache and gait disturbance. At the time of presentation, her KPS was 90. MRI revealed a dominant cystic lesion in the left cerebellum measuring 31.4 cm3 (Fig. 1C), which displaced the fourth ventricle and pons without associated peritumoral edema, along with nine additional cystic brain metastases. Owing to the multiplicity of lesions, surgical resection was not considered feasible, and radiotherapy was selected as the primary treatment strategy. To reduce the mass effect of the dominant lesion and enable safer irradiation, cyst drainage was performed under general anesthesia on day -2, reducing the lesion volume to 10.1 cm3, a 67.7% reduction (Fig. 1G) prior to f-SRT. The patient’s headache and ataxia improved immediately after drainage. Subsequently, f-SRT was administered using the TrueBeam STx with Novalis Radiosurgery (35 Gy in 5 fractions) over days 4–8. Treatment planning was performed using contrast-enhanced MRI (Gd-T1WI) obtained on postoperative day 2, and irradiation was initiated on postoperative day 6, targeting all 10 lesions simultaneously. Lesion monitoring with plain CT performed after fraction 3 on day 6 confirmed gradual cyst shrinkage, and no treatment replanning was required. The large cystic tumor volume decreased to 3.5 cm3 at the final fraction, which was attributable to the shrinkage of the cystic component, whereas the solid nodular portion remained unchanged. After radiosurgery, the tumor remained reduced, measuring 1.4 cm3 at 1 month and 2.4 cm3 at 3 months after f-SRT. During the 13-month follow-up, new metastatic lesions developed and resulted in left-sided hemiparesis. Nevertheless, the treated lesions continued to show sustained volume reduction without recurrence or radiation necrosis. Her KPS remained 90 after completion of f-SRT but declined from 90 to 60 at the final follow-up due to progression of new brain metastases.

Case 4

A woman in her 70s with metastatic esophageal cancer presented with gait disturbance and delirium. At the time of presentation, her KPS was 40. MRI demonstrated a dominant cystic lesion in the cerebellar vermis measuring 20.1 cm3, associated with peritumoral edema (Fig. 1D), with seven additional brain metastases. Given her advanced age and multiplicity of lesions, surgical resection was not deemed appropriate, and radiotherapy was selected as the preferred treatment option. To reduce the mass effect of the dominant lesion and allow safer irradiation, cyst drainage was performed under local anesthesia on day -1, resulting in a 68.8% reduction in tumor volume to 6.3 cm3 (Fig. 1H) prior to f-SRT. Despite experiencing transient delirium during the treatment course, f-SRT (35 Gy in 5 fractions) for a large cystic tumor and concomitant seven small tumors were completed using the Gamma Knife Icon over days 1–7, starting on postoperative day 2. A mid-treatment MRI performed on day 5 demonstrated stable cyst morphology without regrowth, and treatment replanning was not required. The large cystic tumor volume was 7.3 cm3 at the final fraction, with shrinkage observed in the cystic and solid nodular components. At the 1-month follow-up, non-contrast MRI showed sustained volume reduction of the treated lesions without recurrence or radiation necrosis; however, additional neurological symptoms were noted. Because the primary cancer progressed, the patient was transitioned to palliative care, and a 3-month follow-up MRI could not be obtained. Her KPS improved slightly from 40 to 50 after completion of f-SRT, after which she was transferred to a palliative care hospital.

Overall results

The study included four patients (one male and three females) with a median age of 52 years. Primary cancers included three radiosensitive histologies (lung and breast cancer) and one presumed radioresistant histology (esophageal cancer). Median KPS improved from 65 (range: 40–90) before treatment to 70 (range: 30–90) after treatment. However, KPS declined by the final follow-up in case 2 and 3, due to progression of the primary disease (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This study introduces a novel approach combining continuous cyst drainage under natural pressure release with f-SRT, thereby extending the clinical applicability of f-SRT to patients who are otherwise ineligible for surgical intervention. This strategy may represent an effective and safer alternative to conventional cyst management methods, reinforcing the role of f-SRT as a cornerstone in the treatment of metastatic brain tumors in complex clinical scenarios. Among the four patients analyzed, the median cyst volume reduction rate following drainage was 65.5%, and no recurrence or radiation necrosis was observed during the 1–18-month follow-up period. Neurological symptoms improved in three patients, and no major treatment-related complications were noted. However, in two patients, new metastatic lesions developed at distant sites despite good local control of the treated brain lesions, resulting in a decline in their KPS. This highlights not only the importance of comprehensive systemic disease control alongside local management of brain metastases but also the need for appropriate patient selection criteria for active intervention, except in compassionate cases where the primary goal is to avoid neurological death. These findings suggest that this combined method is a promising treatment option for managing large cystic lesions.

The treatment of large cystic metastatic brain tumors often involves addressing neurological symptoms caused by tumor volume-related mass effects, with surgical resection frequently being the treatment of choice [1]. However, surgical resection has a risk of tumor dissemination [12] and often requires interruption of systemic therapy [13], which can delay treatment of the primary malignancy. Additionally, many patients with metastatic brain tumors are in poor general health and cannot tolerate invasive procedures, such as craniotomy [1,7]. Consequently, less-invasive radiotherapy has become an increasingly preferred treatment option for metastatic brain tumors. However, because large cystic lesions exceeding 10 cm3 in volume are difficult to control [1], pre-treatment cyst volume reduction is essential to improve the safety and efficacy of radiation therapy.

Previous reports [2,3,4,7,9,10,15,17] have also demonstrated effective cyst control using single aspiration or Ommaya reservoir placement (Table 2). The tumors addressed in these studies had an average volume of 21.8 cm3 to 48.2 cm3, with cyst volume reductions ranging from 19.9% to 77.9% after drainage. Prescribed marginal doses ranged from 13.5–20.2 Gy for single fraction and 30–40 Gy for fractionated treatments, with tumor control rates of 45.8% to 91.3% and 100%, respectively. The current study examined lesions of comparable sizes and achieved similar cyst reduction rates. This suggests that the efficacy of the proposed method is comparable to that of Ommaya reservoir placement or a single aspiration.

Reports on f-SRT following a single aspiration or Ommaya reservoir placement are limited. Noda et al. [17] reported excellent tumor control rates (100.0%) using frameless Gamma Knife f-SRT (marginal doses of 30–40 Gy in 5–10 fractions) combined with Ommaya reservoir placement for large cystic lesions. Similarly, the combination of continuous cyst drainage with f-SRT in the present study showed significant promise as an effective treatment for large cystic metastatic brain tumors.

At the study center, Ommaya reservoirs have traditionally been used for cyst control. However, complications, such as tube obstruction and intralesional hemorrhage, occasionally necessitate emergency craniotomy. In contrast, the current method demonstrated no complications, allowing for safe treatment. Gradual cyst reduction under natural pressure release likely prevented abrupt intracranial pressure changes, and a larger drainage tube (2 mm vs. 1.4 mm diameter Ommaya reservoir tubes) may have reduced the risk of complications. These factors suggest that this method results in fewer complications than Ommaya reservoir placement or stereotactic cyst aspiration, which can cause rapid intracranial pressure changes. Another advantage of this method is the reduced risk of cyst aspiration, observed with intermittent drainage methods using Ommaya reservoirs [7]. In this case series, continuous drainage under natural pressure effectively avoided re-aspiration and enabled gradual cyst volume reduction. Importantly, the simplified interim imaging schedule should not be interpreted as limited efficacy of continuous cyst drainage. Continuous drainage maintained cyst volume stability throughout f-SRT, thereby preventing unexpected cyst re-expansion that could compromise dose distribution or necessitate replanning. This morphological stability supports the clinical utility of continuous drainage and explains why interim imaging could be safely simplified after the initial case. Although the solid components also tended to decrease in size, the cystic components consistently maintained volume reduction throughout treatment. These findings suggest that continuous cyst drainage and f-SRT may have contributed to tumor volume reduction in a complementary manner. In the initial case, daily MRI showed no cyst enlargement beyond the treatment field, indicating stable cyst volume during treatment; therefore, daily imaging was not considered necessary. Stable cyst reduction enhances the safety and efficacy of radiotherapy and is a key benefit of continuous drainage. Although fluid output was not assessed in this study, it may help predict cyst volume trends and warrants evaluation in future studies.

An important rationale for selecting f-SRT rather than SRS in this study was the residual tumor characteristics after cyst drainage. Large cystic metastases often remain unsuitable for SRS because post-drainage lesions frequently exceed the optimal size and have irregular morphology [17,18]. In this series, although continuous cyst drainage substantially reduced tumor volume, several post-drainage targets remained large and irregular, rendering SRS inappropriate. In contrast, f-SRT allowed safe marginal dose delivery with better sparing of surrounding normal brain tissue and enabled adaptive replanning in response to dynamic morphological changes during treatment.

Another key aspect of treatment might have been the type of radiation technique applied adjunctively to drainage, known as adaptive radiosurgery [22,23,24]. One of the patients benefiting from this approach, Case 1, demonstrated sustained local control throughout the 18-month follow-up. MRI-guided replanning based on morphological changes during f-SRT, including volume reduction, can spare surrounding healthy tissue by limiting unintended dose delivery outside the target. With Gamma Knife, replanning also allows dose escalation to be remodulated and the “hot-spot” distribution within the tumor bed to be redesigned relatively easily, in near-real time (i.e., hours before treatment). Consequently, replanning prior to each subsequent fraction is an approach worth thoughtful consideration, particularly with lesions larger than 8–10 cm3, those in a critical location, and those nearby other brain lesions concurrently targeted with SRS/f-SRT [22,23].

This study has some limitations. First, the small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, primary tumor origins and radiotherapy platforms (Gamma Knife and LINAC) varied across cases, introducing heterogeneity. However, the underlying concept—continuous cyst drainage followed by fractionated dose delivery with adaptive replanning—was consistent across platforms, supporting applicability beyond a single device. This heterogeneity reflects real-world clinical practice rather than experimental variability. In addition, because f-SRT itself may contribute to tumor shrinkage, the observed volume reduction cannot be attributed solely to continuous cyst drainage; rather, the favorable volumetric changes likely reflect complementary effects of drainage and fractionated dose delivery. Although no infections or major complications were observed, prolonged drainage tube placement carries potential risks, and infection cannot be fully excluded given the limited number of cases. Furthermore, mobility restrictions during drainage may negatively affect activities of daily living and should be considered in clinical decision-making. Finally, two patients transitioned to palliative care due to systemic disease progression, introducing potential selection bias. Thus, patient selection remains critical, although compassionate treatment aimed at preventing neurological death may still be justified in selected cases. Despite these limitations, favorable cyst reduction rates, absence of major complications, and promising local control suggest that this combination therapy is feasible and potentially effective. Further case accumulation and comparative studies are warranted to clarify its clinical value.

The present study proposes a novel treatment approach combining continuous cyst drainage under natural pressure release with f-SRT for large cystic metastatic brain tumors. This combined strategy may provide temporary neurological stabilization and local tumor control in carefully selected patients who are not ideal candidates for surgical resection. Further case accumulation is required to verify the reproducibility and assess comparative advantages over other treatments.
