Overview and future studies of potential hereditary effects of parental exposure to atomic bomb radiation on their offspring
Authors: Nori Nakamura, Michiko Yamada, Ritsu Sakata, Waka Ohishi, Yoshimi Tatsukawa, Arikuni Uchimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis is a brief review of studies conducted at the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission and Radiation Effects Research Foundation regarding the possible transgenerational effects of atomic bomb radiation. These include clinical, epidemiological, and biological studies on birth defect, sex ratio, chromosome aberration, molecular changes, disease prevalence and incidence, and whole genome analysis. Also, future plans are addressed.Keywords: radiation, transgenerational effects, humans and mice

Background and context (1945–7)

In August 1945, two bombs were dropped in Hiroshima and Nagasaki, leading to the death of over 200 000 people by the end of the year. Those who survived had to suffer not only physical and mental distress, but also faced social discrimination in employment, and difficulties in obtaining food and other daily living necessities. Rumors that radiation could induce mutations, and that offspring of the survivors would tend to be abnormal, also led to discrimination in finding marriage partners. Such rumors led certain survivors to decide not to have children. Many of the genetic studies described below were conducted under these sensitive and difficult circumstances.

In response to concerns about potential long-term health effects of radiation on the survivors, and possible hereditary effects on their offspring, the US government in 1947 established and funded the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission (ABCC) research institute to understand the long-term health effects of atomic bomb radiation. In 1975, the work of the ABCC was transitioned to the binational Radiation Effects Research Foundation (RERF), jointly funded and managed by the governments of Japan and the USA, with the mission of conducting research to help improve the health and well-being of the survivors, their children, and radiation-exposed populations around the world. Here, we summarize ABCC/RERF’s work specifically focused on the potential hereditary effects of radiation in human studies.

Birth defects (77 000 F1 of the survivors: 1948–54)

Birth defects, including malformation, stillbirth, and death soon after birth, can occur spontaneously and, in most cases, the cause is unknown. An estimated 20%–30% of such events are thought to be caused by genetics, 10% by environmental factors, and 60%–70% by interaction of the two [1]. In 1948, the ABCC (predecessor to RERF) initiated a set of studies to understand the effect of radiation on birth defects. At the time of study initiation, certain foods were under a war time ration system, and pregnant women were allotted additional special rations. Using the ration system to identify pregnant women and expected birth date of the offspring, study investigators were able to identify and contact over 90% of pregnancies that exceeded 20 weeks in both cities. Medical doctors dispatched from the ABCC examined 76 626 newborn babies in Hiroshima and Nagasaki to collect information on sex ratio, birth weight, preterm birth, malformation, stillbirth, and death within 6 days after birth. Additional examinations were conducted to collect growth information during the 8–10 months after birth.

As individual radiation doses received from the bomb had not yet been calculated, the collected data were classified into five groups depending on distance from the hypocenter, shielding conditions, and degree of acute radiation sickness. No increase in the frequency of any individual birth defect was observed with the increase of this dose proxy (Table 1). The frequency of malformations which may potentially cause handicap in one’s life was 0.91% (594/65 431), very similar to the population frequency of 0.92% (456/49 645) collected in those days by the Japanese Red Cross hospital in Tokyo [2]. Later studies used DS86 individual radiation doses [3 ] and the most recent DS02R1 doses [4] for the reanalysis of untoward pregnancy outcomes, such as major congenital malformations, stillbirths, or neonatal deaths, but the conclusion of no statistically significant findings of elevated risk remained the same. However, interpretation is challenged given the possibility of being confounded by other factors influencing the development of birth defects.

Sex ratio (140 000 F1 of the survivors: 1948–66)

In any human population, males are born slightly more frequently than females [5]. However, early radiation scientists hypothesized that more daughters may be born than sons following maternal radiation exposure because X chromosomes bearing radiation-induced recessive lethal mutations are expressed in sons but not in daughters, as daughters bear two X chromosomes, and the chromosome derived from unexposed father could suppress the recessive mutations induced by the exposure. In the case of paternal exposure radiation, on the other hand, the theory postulated that irradiated X chromosomes would be inherited by the daughters and the Y chromosomes by the sons—thus, if dominant lethal mutations were induced in the X chromosomes (Y chromosome does not contain genes leading to lethality), more sons would be born compared to daughters. Because initial results were in line with the hypothesis but were not statistically significant, data collection continued until 1966 (the total number of assessed births attained 140 000). However, overall results shifted toward the opposite direction expected from the initial data, and no significant difference was obtained [2].

During the emergence of mammalian genetics in the 1950s to 1960s, it was naively thought that similar physiologic mechanisms were operating between fruit flies and mice as their genetic compositions were XY for males and XX for females. However, in 1961, X chromosomes in female mammals were found to be inactivated at some early stage in embryogenesis, which is quite different from insects. The finding implies that in mammalian female embryos, about 50% of the cells express X chromosome inherited from one parent and the remaining 50% express X chromosome derived from the other parent. Given the unlikelihood that an embryo bearing an X-chromosomal recessive lethal mutation expressed in 50% of somatic cells could survive, this finding suggested that sex ratio as a marker for genetic effect of radiation is not applicable to mammals [6], thus ending this line of inquiry.

Chromosome aberrations (about 8000 F1 each for the control and exposed groups: 1967–85)

A cytogenetic program was initiated at ABCC soon after the establishment of culturing blood lymphocytes in vitro, prior to the availability of more sophisticated banding or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) techniques. Detecting target stable chromosome aberrations such as reciprocal translocations that can persist after many cell divisions was a particularly difficult task using the simple Giemsa staining available at the time. Because the solid Giemsa method cannot detect exchanges of similar sized portions between the two chromosomes, it was estimated that this method could achieve a detection rate of about 70% (i.e. 30% of the exchanges remain undetected), a proportion later confirmed by direct comparisons between simple Giemsa and FISH methods [7].

The study examined about 8000 offspring born to proximally exposed parents (estimated parental doses were 0.01 Gy or larger by the T65DR dosimetry system) and about 8000 offspring born to distally exposed or non-exposed parents. Completion of the project took about 20 years, and the results are shown in Table 2. No effect of parental exposure to radiation was observed in the offspring. The observed frequency was close to the spontaneous frequency of chromosome abnormalities observed in newborns around the world (about 0.6%) (summarized in ref. [9]).

Subsequently, taking note of the possibility that some structural aberrations are not newly induced but inherited, parents of offspring bearing autosomal structural aberrations were also requested to participate in the study. Among the 23 offspring in the proximally exposed group who bore stable-type aberrations, parents of 11 could be examined. A de novo mutation was detected in only one case, and 10 cases were inherited. As for the offspring in the distally exposed group, one case possessed a de novo mutation, and 15 cases were inherited among the 27 families examined [10]. Thus, the frequency of de novo mutation was estimated to be about 0.02%, which is close to 0.018%, the frequency of structural aberrations in the newborns [11].

Biochemical genetics (11 000 F1 born to proximally exposed parents and 12 000 F1 born to distally exposed parents: 1975–84)

The next major study moved to the detection of germline mutations at the molecular level as a shift in the electrophoretic band caused by amino-acid substitution of proteins. A total of 30 different serum proteins were examined for more than 23 000 offspring but only two and four de novo mutations were detected in the proximally exposed and distally exposed groups, respectively. However, growing evidence from other studies indicated that radiation-induced mutations in mammalian cells are mainly deletions and hence this study, which tried to detect base-change type mutations, could not fully evaluate the genetic effect of radiation. Subsequently, an additional plan was raised to detect mutations which led to decrease in the enzymic activity of red blood cells. However, this was abandoned midcourse because only one such mutation was detected after examination of approximately half of the examinees [12].

DNA study (120 F1 for minisatellite and microsatellite mutation: 1985–2010)

With the development of methods to study mutations using genomic DNA, RERF initiated a genomic DNA study in 1985. In this study, blood samples were collected from about 1000 families on a family basis (father–mother–child trio) with the goal of establishing immortalized cell lines for use in future genomic studies. Using some of the blood samples collected here, a study of 61 offspring born to high-dose exposed parents (>1 Gy) and 58 offspring born to control parents were tested for mutations at eight hypervariable minisatellite loci. No evidence for increased mutation frequency was observed [13]. Similarly, negative results were also obtained for mutations at 40 microsatellite loci [14].

Mortality of offspring of atomic bomb survivors (77 000 F1 offspring of the survivors: 1946–ongoing)

Approaching the question of potential hereditary effects of radiation from the perspective of long-term mortality or disease outcome, two long-term human population studies were established. The first was an F1 study of mortality in offspring of atomic bomb survivors. From the information collected for the studies of birth defects and sex ratio described above, with the addition of birth notification information for the period from May 1946 to the start of the birth defect study, which was not included in these studies, a mortality follow-up cohort was extracted according to the distance from the hypocenter of parents or exposed parent. The cohort was expanded to include the children of members of the Life Span Study cohort of the atomic bomb survivors, making up a total population of 76 814 [15].

The most recent epidemiologic study which covers 62 years of follow-up (median follow-up period is 54 years) showed no evidence for the increased risk for cancer or non-cancer death in the offspring born to either exposed fathers or mothers [15, results summarized in Table 3 below]. The mean dose is 264 mGy among the non-zero dose parents. The observed results are in line with animal studies indicating no increased risk in the proportion of tumor death or life shortening in the offspring born to irradiated parents [16–20] except for one study [21]. Cancer incidence study also indicated no evidence for the effect of parental exposure to radiation [22].

F1 clinical study (FOCS) (12 000 offspring: 2002–6 13 000 offspring: 2010–ongoing)

FOCS is a second cohort established for a subset of the F1 mortality study of offspring, focused on clinical outcomes. This cohort consists of those who expressed a willingness to participate in health examinations from the mail survey cohort of 24 673 offspring, selected from the F1 mortality study cohort based on information such as family registry, current address, and parental radiation dose. A total of 11 951 offspring underwent health examinations between 2002 and 2006, and associations between parental radiation exposure and the prevalence of adult-onset multifactorial disease (hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, and stroke) were analysed. This prevalence study observed no evidence of increased prevalence with parental radiation exposure, either for paternal or maternal doses [23, 24]. In the analysis by sex of offspring, a negative association between paternal dose and the prevalence of multifactorial diseases was suggested, but only in male offspring. Because the mean age at the time of examination was relatively young at 48.6 years, and potential bias associated with prevalence study existed, RERF converted the prevalence study to an incidence study for prospective follow-up, initiating a program of FOCS health examinations in 4-year cycles starting in 2010.

Possible reasons for the negative results to date

Given that animal data have clearly shown heritable genetic effects of radiation [25], concerns have occasionally been raised that results of genetic studies of offspring of atomic bomb survivors might not fully reflect the genetic effects of radiation. While such criticisms were difficult to counteract in the past given the lack of large radiation-exposed human populations for comparison, the situation has changed in the past few decades. With a steady increase in cancer cure rates, especially among childhood cancer patients, many cancer survivors have grown to adulthood, married, and had offspring—allowing evaluation of the hereditary genetic effect of scattered radiation used for therapy. As with the offspring of atomic bomb survivors, these therapeutic high doses of radiation do not appear to have induced detrimental effects in the offspring of cancer survivors. For example, there was no increase in the frequency of malformation among the offspring of male ex-patients of Wilms tumors at doses below 25 Gy (cumulative local dose of scattered radiation as a result of therapeutic repeat exposures to the tumors), while, malformation was observed in approximately 10% of female ex-patients who were not treated with radiation (i.e. chemotherapy), but the frequency did not increase with the increase of radiation dose received from the radiotherapy [26]. Similar studies on chromosome abnormalities induced among offspring born to ex-patients who underwent radiotherapy also indicated no evidence for increased risk in offspring of exposed parents [27].

Additionally, there are plausible reasons why transgenerational effects of radiation are observed in mouse studies, but difficult to observe in humans [28]. First, even in the mouse, radiation cannot induce mutations in immature oocytes (resting oocytes or primordial oocytes). Such immature oocytes reside in the cortex of the ovary in both mice and humans, where blood vessels are few and oxygen saturation is low (for more details, see refs. [28–30]). Although resting oocytes are not tissue stem cells, both types of cells require long survival with minimum DNA damage, and thus the hypoxic microenvironment is essential [30].

Second, the seven genes used for the mouse mutagenesis study [specific locus test (SLT)] were hypermutable and do not represent many other genes [31 ]. Large differences exist in the mutation induction rate among the seven genes used for the SLT (Fig. 1, black bars). The mean mutation induction rate for the seven genes was about 2 × 10−5 per Gy while the mean rate for 34 genes was about 1 × 10−5 per Gy one half of the mean of the seven genes [31]. Further, when about 1000 DNA fragments which were end-labelled with 32P were tested for the induction of deletion mutations, the mean frequency further decreased to 2 × 10−6 per Gy one order of magnitude lower than that of the seven-locus test [28, 32]. In short, the estimated genetic risk of radiation based on the seven-locus test was too high and probably needs to be reduced to 1/10.

Third, there are fundamental differences between mice and humans. Mice are multiparous animals, and hence it is not possible to abort malformed embryos selectively. In contrast, humans are essentially uni-parous and malformed fetuses are prone to undergo abortion. One estimate suggests that 30% of fertilized eggs are lost before implantation, another 30% after implantation, and 10% after medical confirmation of pregnancy [33]. Aborted fetuses generated for medical reasons contained external malformation in 9% among early-stage fetuses but decreased to 1% among full-term fetuses [34].

RERF ongoing and planned studies on heritable effects

Trio Genome Study (whole genome sequencing of exposed parents and offspring)

With the remarkable progress in the development of genome analysis over the past few years, it is now possible to examine transgenerational effects of radiation at the base-pair level. Eighty years after the end of the war, we are still unclear whether parental exposure to radiation from the atomic bombs can cause genetic effects, or change the risk of disease, in their children.

Previous whole genome analyses examining mouse trios have shown that parental radiation exposure increases the frequency of certain types of de novo mutations in the offspring's genome [35, 36]. On the other hand, analyses of human populations have so far not observed an increase in the frequency of mutations due to parental radiation exposure [37, 38]. For example, whole genome studies were conducted for three high-dose exposed families in Nagasaki [39] and about 130 offspring born to radiation exposed parents by the Chernobyl nuclear plant accident [40] while the effect of radiation exposure is not detected in either study. RERF plans to conduct a whole genome sequencing study in the near future using blood samples that have been collected since 1985, covering 290 family trios each in the high dose exposed and control groups. In this study, the higher doses and dose rates of the study participants (both paternal and maternal exposure cases are included) and the larger number of participants should allow for a more detailed analysis than has been possible in the past. It seems unlikely, though, that such genome analysis would yield biologic data which contradicts the epidemiological observations hitherto conducted worldwide.

Many survivors have already passed away. Of those who remain, numerous survivors and their offspring have urged that the Trio Genome Study be carried out in their lifetime, so that they can get the best evidence of whether radiation-induced damage is heritable. However, given the past discrimination against survivors for jobs and marriage, the fear of potential future discrimination remains a valid concern [41]. Thus, RERF carries a great responsibility to conduct the study ethically and responsibly. Details of the Ethical, Social, and Legal Issues pertaining to the Trio Genome Study are summarized elsewhere in this journal issue [42].

Updates of mortality and clinical outcomes in offspring of survivors

In the 2002–6 FOCS prevalence study, given that the mean age of the F1 offspring was relatively young (48.6 years), most of their disease experience was anticipated to occur in the future. Accordingly, an ongoing analysis is investigating the association between parental radiation exposure and the incidence of multifactorial diseases in their offspring and its results are awaited. To further assess the long-term effects of parental radiation exposure on the incidence of multifactorial diseases in their offspring, it will be crucial to maintain long-term follow-up with a high participation rate.

Conclusion

Understanding the possible hereditary effects of radiation has been one of the longest-standing concerns for atomic bomb survivors, their children, and both the local and scientific communities. To date, successive studies of birth defects, sex ratio, chromosome aberrations, biochemical genetics, and DNA minisatellites/microsatellites have found no relationship between parental exposure and early genetic/epigenetic effects on their offspring. At the level of disease and mortality, epidemiologic follow up of children of the atomic bomb survivors has indicated no increased risk of multifactorial disease (high blood pressure, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, and stroke) or death based on their parental exposure, to date. However, the cohorts are still relatively young at last follow up (F1 mean age of 63 years at the end of December 2020, and FOCS mean age of 68 years at the end of February 2025). Continued follow-up for the lifetime of the offspring will thus be imperative. Similarly, continued examination of potential effects at the genome/epigenome level as molecular and analytical technologies advance will be essential to informing this critical concern with the best scientific evidence available.
