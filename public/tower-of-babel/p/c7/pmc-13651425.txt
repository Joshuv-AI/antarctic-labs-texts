Background

Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) refer to a range of symptoms, including agitation or aggression, depression, and anxiety, which affect over 90% of people living with dementia [1]. These symptoms place substantial strain on family caregivers, contributing to stress, burden, and depressive symptoms [2–4]. The relationship between BPSD and caregiver outcomes can be understood through established frameworks, such as the Stress Process Model and the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping [5, 6]. BPSD act as primary stressors, and caregiver appraisal and coping capacity influence the resulting stress experience.

One commonly used approach to caregiver-led BPSD management is the ABC (Antecedents-Behaviour-Consequences) behavioural problem-solving framework, which trains caregivers to identify triggers of challenging behaviours (Antecedents), observe and interpret behavioural responses (Behaviour), and modify environmental or interpersonal responses to reduce symptom escalation (Consequences). Within this framework, ABC-based strategies function as a structured, problem-focused coping mechanism that enhances caregivers’ skills, sense of control, and self-efficacy in responding to BPSD. Evidence shows that equipping caregivers with the ABC (Antecedents–Behaviour–Consequences) behavioural problem‑solving skill can reduce the frequency and severity of BPSD, and in turn alleviate caregivers’ stress and improve psychological well‑being and perceived physical health [7–9]. However, meta-analyses show that caregiver-led BPSD management interventions have only small effects on BPSD and on related caregivers’ reactions (SMD = -0.14 and − 0.16, respectively) [10], with no significant benefits observed among caregivers of individuals with moderate to severe dementia [11]. This may be attributable to the highly individualised and fluctuating nature of BPSD [12], which necessitates timely, ongoing support for caregivers to apply the ABC approach effectively.

Personalised coaching has been shown to enhance skill acquisition and adherence and is widely used to support parents of children with special needs in behavioural management [13]. When applied to caregivers of people with dementia, coaching can assist them in interpreting behavioural cues, refining ABC strategies, and adapting responses as symptoms evolve. Guided feedback further strengthens caregivers’ confidence and capacity to manage BPSD.

Mobile health (mHealth) applications (apps) offer a scalable and accessible means of delivering personalised coaching to caregivers; however, their impact is frequently limited by low engagement and high attrition [14]. Sustained participation remains a key challenge, underscoring the need for mechanisms that address periods of disengagement, such as transitioning users to more intensive human coaching or offering booster sessions. Push notifications may prompt engagement [15], but poorly timed or excessive alerts can be burdensome [16]. It remains unclear whether personalised motivational messages and symptom‑specific guidance, particularly for identifying antecedents and consequences, can enhance engagement [17].

Recent advances in machine-learning methods offer an opportunity to address these limitations by optimising the timing, frequency, and content of in-app prompts to better align with caregivers’ needs and contexts. While mHealth apps have proliferated due to their flexibility, accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and potential for personalisation, most existing BPSD-focused apps primarily emphasise symptom monitoring in individuals with moderate to severe dementia rather than actively supporting caregivers in managing BPSD [18, 19]. This gap underscores the need for an adaptive, engagement-responsive mHealth intervention that integrates coaching and strategies to support caregivers in managing BPSD in real-world settings.

Methods

This trial is approved by the Human Research Ethical Review Committee of the Education University of Hong Kong (2023-2024-0387) and registered on the Clinical Trial Registry (NCT07444866). This protocol will follow the SPIRIT guidelines [20].

Methods: patient and public involvement, trial design

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public are not directly involved in the drafting of this protocol.

However, caregiver perspectives informed the development of the intervention content. Feedback obtained from caregivers who participated in a previous, similar trial is integrated into the design of the antecedents of BPSD and corresponding management strategies included in the intervention [9]. Participating sites will support the dissemination of study findings to participants and the wider community through established caregiver networks and service channels.

Study design

This study is a hybrid trial integrating a five-arm randomised controlled trial (RCT), a two-stage SMART, and three MRTs (see Fig. 1). The overall design follows a 2 × 2 + 1 factorial structure across five condition arms and is intended to evaluate both the effectiveness of the BPSD management intervention and the optimisation of coaching and just-in-time support strategies. The trial consists of:

A first-stage 5-arm RCT (Weeks 1–6);

A first-stage 5-arm RCT (Weeks 1–6);

A second-stage SMART targeting non-responders (weeks 7–10); and.

A second-stage SMART targeting non-responders (weeks 7–10); and.

To address research objectives 1–3, eligible participants will be randomly allocated to one of the five arms: (1) BPSD management intervention with both human and app-based coaching; (2) the same intervention with human coaching only; (3) the same intervention with app-based coaching only; (4) the intervention without coaching; or (5) psychoeducation on dementia caregiving as an active control.

For Arms 1–4, the 6-week intervention comprises three sequential components: one week of telephone-based psychoeducation, one week of telephone-based BPSD management training, and four weeks of app-based support. During the final four weeks, participants will receive the coaching format corresponding to their assigned arm (Arms 1–3) or no coaching (Arm 4). The control arm (Arm 5) will receive psychoeducation only.

Following the initial 6-week BPSD management intervention, participants who demonstrate adequate engagement (at least once a week) will continue with their assigned coaching condition for an additional four weeks. Participants who do not meet this engagement criterion will be re-randomised to receive either intensified human coaching or an additional 60-min telephone-based booster session on top of their assigned coaching approach, consistent with a two-stage SMART design. A SMART design is used to develop and evaluate adaptive interventions by randomising participants at multiple decision points, with subsequent treatment assignments determined by prespecified decision rules based on participants’ responses to earlier intervention components [21]. This approach enables systematic testing and optimisation of intervention sequences tailored to individual needs.

To address research objectives 4 and 5, between weeks 3 and 10, participants allocated to the combined human- and app-based coaching (Arm 1) and the app-based coaching (Arm 2) will additionally be randomised into one of the three concurrent MRTs. An MRT is an experimental design used to evaluate the proximal causal effects of intervention components delivered repeatedly over time [22]. MRTs support the development and optimisation of just-in-time adaptive interventions by identifying when, how, and for whom specific intervention components are most effective. In MRT 1, push notifications will be delivered at one of six participant-selected time points with a 50% probability when they are available. In MRT 2, the push notification will or will not contain the suggested antecedents and consequences of different symptoms reported by participants. In MRT 3, the push notification will either contain a personalised motivational message or a standardised motivational message.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Participants

Participants will be community-dwelling family caregivers of persons with dementia recruited through non-government organisations providing elder services, caregiver support groups, and the Hong Kong Alzheimer’s Disease Association over a 24-month period. Eligible caregivers will be recruited if they meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) Hong Kong Chinese residents aged 18 or above; (2) family caregiver of an individual with a confirmed medical diagnosis of any types of dementia who has been residing in the community; (3) having been providing care for activities of daily livings and instrumental activities of daily living for at least 20 h per week for at least one year prior to recruitment; (4) have a smart phone and be confident to use it for the intervention; (5) have mild to moderate stress according to the 10-item Chinese version Perceived Stress Scale (i.e., overall score ≥ 14 out of 40) [23]; and with the individual with dementia presenting at least one BPSD (defined as Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire severity score ≥ 1). The relatively inclusive BPSD threshold is intended to capture the heterogeneous and fluctuating nature of symptoms in real-world settings. To ensure the relevance and measurability of intervention effects, we require caregivers to report at least mild to moderate stress, thereby focusing on those most likely to benefit from support. At the same time, requiring the presence of at least one BPSD helps avoid including caregivers whose stress arises primarily from non-BPSD-related demands, for whom the intervention may be less relevant. Those having participated in any structured mind-body intervention, cognitive therapy, or structured psychosocial intervention in the 6 months prior to recruitment, or having acute psychiatric and medical comorbidities that are potentially life-threatening (e.g., suicidal ideation) or that limit the caregivers’ participation or adherence (e.g. acute psychosis) will be excluded.

First-stage intervention (weeks 1–6)

Psychoeducation

All participants (Arms 1-5) will receive a one-week of telephone-based psychoeducation that provides general knowledge about dementia (e.g., disease trajectory) and information on skills for caring for people with dementia (e.g., communication skills), adapted from our previous study [9].

BPSD management intervention

Participants in Arms 1–4 will receive four 30-minute BPSD management sessions, designed to teach caregivers to implement the BPSD management intervention using the ABC approach. The lesson content covers introduction to BPSD; behaviours and management; psychological symptoms and management; approaches of managing BPSD according to the ABC framework; and case studies of different BPSDs. The BPSD management involves six stages: (1) pinpoint a BPSD and assess when it occurs and how often; (2) identify antecedents, caregivers’ responses, and consequences; (3) identify possible strategies for addressing antecedents and improving consequences by changing caregivers’ responses to the behaviour; (4) select the strategies by assessing their pros and cons; (5) plan and rehearse implementation strategy; and (6) try out the strategy and evaluate it.

Participants will use daily records to track changes in symptoms and, through the app, develop plans to modify antecedents and consequences. The app guides them through each management stage via a user-friendly menu or self-input. Rather than offering fixed solutions, it helps participants review their observations and identify appropriate strategies. The goal is to encourage participants to try new approaches that disrupt entrenched PSD patterns. Once a solution is identified, the app will guide participants through mental rehearsal of the implementation steps to support successful execution. If difficulties arise, the app helps participants identify barriers and develop strategies to overcome them. Participants are prompted to schedule a specific time and place to apply the selected solution. The app then supports ongoing monitoring of the target behaviour by evaluating changes in its occurrence frequency. If the behaviour does not improve, participants are guided to consider alternative solutions. When complete elimination is not achievable, the app highlights partial improvements using recorded data to reinforce progress and sustain participant motivation.

To facilitate the participants in recording the BPSD, a checklist modelled on the Neuropsychiatric Inventory has been developed and clustered into seven subsyndromes [24–26]: psychotic symptoms (hallucination and delusion); affective symptoms (depression, anxiety, and apathy); hyperactivity symptoms (agitation/aggression, disinhibition, and irritability); euphoria/elation; aberrant motor behaviours; sleep and night-time behaviours, and appetite and eating disorders. Coaching conditions (weeks 3–6)

Human-only coaching

Participants in the human coaching group will receive weekly telephone calls lasting approximately 20 min. Calls focus on reviewing daily BPSD monitoring, supporting plan development and implementation, identifying barriers, reviewing progress, encouraging help-seeking, and providing feedback and encouragement. The coach is a registered social worker with coaching experience.

App-based-only coaching

In the app-based coaching-only condition, the app will guide participants through daily monitoring of BPSD, encourage them to develop plans to modify either precipitating events or their reactions to the newly identified BPSD, review their performance of the implementation of their plan each day, and assist them in setting personalised steps and goals for the following day.

App-based coach performs four key roles: (1) guiding participants through an introductory session in which they are taught the six steps involved in managing BPSD within the app; (2) guiding participants through daily check-ins regarding their BPSD management and stress levels, with particular emphasis on helping them develop personalised strategies for identified BPSD, and evaluate the outcomes of the strategies they use during the study period; (3) identifying barriers and facilitators in their BPSD management; and (4) being available 24/7 to answer participants’ questions.

The app-based coach is a natural-language processing virtual coach created with IBM Watson Virtual Assistant artificial intelligence software and will be deployed on Slack, a cloud-based instant messaging platform. Through the app-based coach, the participants will receive: (1) text messages automatically every morning to remind participants about plans for implementing strategies for managing BPSD; (2) text messages when participants achieve positive outcomes of their strategies; and (3) tailored feedback messages for continual management of BPSD once a week.

Human- and app-based combined coaching

Duplication between human and app-based coaching will be minimised, with the human coach providing supplementary support using app-generated data.

Second-stage coaching (weeks 7–10)

During the second-line coaching period, participants in Arms 1–4 who are not adequately engaged (i.e., fewer than once per week) will be randomised to either intensified human coaching or a booster session. Those who engage adequately will continue the assigned coaching condition for four additional weeks.

Intensified human coaching

The intensity of human coaching will be increased through biweekly calls, each lasting approximately 30 min.

Booster session

During the single 60-minute booster session, the core content of the BPSD management intervention will be revisited to strengthen participants’ retention and recall of key concepts and skills. This will be achieved through structured review and repeated rehearsal of the principal constructs and processes. Participants will also receive social reinforcement to support maintenance of acquired knowledge and skills and to prevent erosion of engagement with the intervention over time.

Additional topics to be addressed include challenges encountered in implementing the intervention, information sharing, and the review of short‑ and long‑term goals.

Embedded MRTs (weeks 3–10)

One motivational message and one suggested antecedent and consequence for different BPSD will be sent to participants (i.e., ranging from 0 to 2 notifications of message and suggestion) to minimise burden and reduce the risk of habituation.

Push notifications timing

Participants will receive brief push notifications asking whether they have engaged with the app‑based BPSD management intervention on the current day or within the preceding few days. Notifications will be scheduled at one of six prespecified times selected individually by each participant. On any given day, a push notification will be delivered at the participant’s chosen time with a 50% probability, provided that the participant is available (i.e., they have not already received a prompt that day, their phone is turned on, and they have not engaged with the app‑based intervention in the previous two days).

Suggested antecedents and consequences of BPSD

Push notifications may or may not include suggested antecedents and consequences of BPSD reported by participants. To support the selection of relevant antecedents and consequences, the app will generate and deliver real‑time, personalised suggestions. In addition, suggestions for possible management strategies will be randomly selected and sent to participants who have not used the app for one week. These pieces of information are grounded in the literature [27], our previous study [9], and experts’ input. Antecedents of BPSD

Motivational messages

Personalised motivational messages are adapted from prior literature [17, 28, 29] and categorised into four framing types: promotion‑focused messages emphasising engagement, prevention‑focused messages emphasising engagement, promotion‑focused messages emphasising non‑engagement, and prevention‑focused messages emphasising non‑engagement [30]. Messages are randomised such that participants receive either a standardised motivational message or a framing‑specific message. On each delivery occasion, a standardised message is sent with a probability of 3/7, while each of the four framing‑specific messages is sent with a probability of 1/7.

Outcomes

All outcome measures have been validated in the local context and will be used with all participants.

To address research objectives 1–3

The primary outcome is the caregiver’s stress level, measured with the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale [23]. It consists of six negative and four positive items rated on a five-point scale from 0 (never) to 4 (very often) (α = 0.75, 0.87, and 0.82 for overall, positive, and negative subscales, respectively) [31].

Secondary outcomes

Caregiving burden will be measured with the 12-item Zarit Burden Interview scale [32]. Each item will be rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 4 (very frequently). A higher score indicates a higher perceived burden (α = 0.84) [32].

Psychological well-being will be measured by the 18-item Ryff’s Psychological Well-being scale [33]. Each item is rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). It assesses six dimensions of psychological well-being: self-acceptance, positive relations with others, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life, and personal growth (α = 0.92 for overall and 0.60–0.75 for its six subscales).

Symptoms of anxiety will be measured with the 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale [34]. It assesses key anxiety symptoms, such as excessive worry and restlessness, on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) (α = 0.888) [34].

Depressive symptoms will be measured with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [35], which evaluates the frequency of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks on a 4‑point scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) (α = 0.91) [35].

Sleep quality will be measured by the 19-item Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [36], which generates seven component scores (subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction). Each component is rated on a 0–3 scale, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality (α = 0.75) [36].

Physical and mental health will be measured by 12-item Short Form Health Survey, Version 2 (SF-12v2) [37]. It is a generic measure of health status comprising eight health domains: physical functioning, role limitations, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, emotional role, and mental health. Item responses are aggregated using standard scoring procedures to generate two summary scores: the physical component summary and the mental component summary, with higher scores indicating better health status (α = 0.81 for the physical and 0.83 for the mental component summary) [38] .

BPSD of the care-recipient with dementia will be measured by both the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire [39] and Revised Memory and Behaviour Problems Checklist [40]. The Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire assesses symptoms across 12 neuropsychiatric domains: delusions, hallucinations, agitation/aggression, depression, anxiety, apathy, irritability, elation, disinhibition, aberrant motor behaviour, sleep disturbances, and appetite changes. For each domain, the presence and severity of symptoms are rated, and caregivers also indicate their associated level of distress. Domain scores can be summed to produce a total symptom severity score and a total caregiver distress score (α = 0.756).

The Revised Memory and Behaviour Problems Checklist consists of 24 items grouped into three subscales: memory‑related problems, disruptive behaviour problems, and depressive symptoms [40]. Caregivers rate the frequency of each problem and their emotional reaction or distress to it (α = 0.816 and 0.895 for frequency and reaction scores, respectively) [40].

Caregiving self‑efficacy will be examined as a potential mediator of the intervention effects on caregiver stress and related outcomes, assessed with the Revised Scale for Caregiving Self-Efficacy [41]. It consists of three subscales: self‑efficacy for obtaining respite, responding to disruptive behaviours, and controlling upsetting thoughts related to caregiving, with each item rated on a 0–100 scale. Subscale scores are calculated by averaging the item ratings within each domain (α ≥ 0.89 across the three subscales) [41].

To address research objectives 4–5

The proximal (engagement in the app for managing BPSDs) and distal (stress level) outcomes for the three MRTs will be obtained via the app’s built-in functions and an ecological momentary assessment every evening, respectively. For all three MRTs, the proximal outcome will be engagement with the BPSD management intervention, operationalised as whether the participant engages with the intervention within the subsequent 24 h following a notification. The distal outcome will be the participant’s stress level, assessed via a daily ecological momentary assessment administered each evening.

Harms

The intervention is considered low risk, as it is non‑pharmacological and focuses on caregiver education, skills training, and psychosocial support for managing BPSD.

Possible risks are limited to transient emotional discomfort or distress that may arise when caregivers reflect on challenging caregiving experiences. To mitigate these risks, participants will be provided with information on accessing professional psychosocial support services when needed.

Participant timeline

The participant timeline and flow through the study are visualised in Fig. 1.

Following screening and informed consent, baseline assessments (T1) will be completed prior to randomisation. Participants will receive a 6‑week first‑stage intervention comprising psychoeducation and BPSD management training with their allocated coaching condition, followed by post‑intervention assessment at Week 6 (T2). Based on engagement, participants will either continue their assigned coaching or be re‑randomised to intensified coaching or a booster session during Weeks 7–10, with assessment at Week 10 (T3). Embedded MRTs will be conducted between Weeks 3 and 10 to evaluate just‑in‑time intervention components. Follow‑up assessments will be conducted at 20 weeks (T4) and 32 weeks (T5) after baseline to assess longer‑term outcomes.

Methods: assignment of interventions

The randomisation sequence will be generated using a computer‑based random number generator by an independent researcher who is not involved in participant recruitment, assessment, or intervention delivery. Group allocation will be concealed from participants until completion of baseline assessments. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and facilitators is not feasible; however, outcome assessors and data analysts will remain blinded to group allocation.

Methods: data collection, management, and analysis

Data will be collected at five time-points using the study application with audio‑assisted questionnaires. Assistance will be provided by trained research assistants who are blinded to group allocation when needed. Telephone follow‑up is conducted to minimise missing data. Additional contextual data will be collected to train the machine-learning models, including date and time of day, phone battery level, GPS location, physical activity, and device-interaction metrics (e.g., screen lock/unlock, screen on/off, and Wi‑Fi connectivity).

Data analysis and management

To address research objectives 1–3, linear mixed models will be used to test three main effects: (1) the effectiveness of the BPSD management intervention compared with an active control; (2) whether app-based coaching of human coaching is a superior first-line strategy, comparing groups 2 A-2B-2 C with 3 A-3B-3 C; and (3) among non-responders, whether increasing coaching intensity or adding a booster session is more effective, comparing 1B-2B-3B-4B with 1–2 C-3–4 C. Subgroup 1 A includes Arm 1 participants with adequate app engagement who continue the same coaching approach for an additional 4 weeks (Weeks 7–10). Subgroup 1B includes Arm 1 participants with inadequate app engagement who are randomised to receive intensified human coaching. Subgroup 1 C includes Arm 1 participants with inadequate app engagement who are randomised to receive a booster session. The same definitions apply to Subgroups 2 A–2 C, 3 A–3 C, and 4 A–4 C. Analyses will follow an intention-to-treat approach and will be repeated for secondary outcomes.

Mediation analysis will be conducted to examine whether caregiving self-efficacy mediates intervention effects on stress. Information on participants identified as being at risk of significant distress or crisis during the study period, and who are advised to seek appropriate professional support in line with ethical and safety requirements, will be recorded where possible. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the findings by adjusting for, or excluding, participants who receive such additional support.

To address research objectives 4–5 related to MRTs, linear models will assess the time-varying effects of push notifications (sent/not sent) on next-day engagement duration and frequency. A model with an indicator of the randomised treatment condition (intervention vs. no intervention), time, and their interaction as dependent variables will be considered. Regression parameters will be estimated using weighted least squares, with weights determined by participants’ availability of randomisation at each decision period. Analyses will pool time-varying, longitudinal data across participants. A separate analysis will be conducted for each MRT aim and adjustments for prespecified covariates. The first set of covariates will be used to examine the moderation of the causal effect of sending a push notification with a tailored message vs. not sending a push notification. The second set of control variables will be a set of covariates that are expected to be highly correlated with the participants’ app use in the subsequent two days; these covariates will be chosen to reduce noise when assessing the effect of push notifications with a tailored message vs. no push notifications.

To identify optimal timing and frequency for sending push notifications, the analysis will focus on users’ state of receptivity. Machine-learning models will be trained using both contextual factors (e.g., date and time of day, phone battery level, GPS location, physical activity, and device interaction metrics, such as screen lock/unlock, screen on/off, and Wi-Fi connectivity) and intrinsic factors (e.g., age, gender, and outcome measures). The dataset will be split into training and cross-validation sets. Just-in-time response and engagement with the app will be modelled for binary classification, while response delay will be analysed using regression-based estimation. Random forest and support vector machine algorithms will be applied to both classification and regression tasks.

In addition to static models, adaptive models will be developed, as an adaptive model led to a 40% improvement in receptivity compared with the static one [42].

Mixed findings are reported regarding performance in supervised learning, neural network, and reinforcement learning (RL). Therefore, neural network and RL algorithms will be employed to maximise the effectiveness of push notifications. RL approaches will include Deep Q-Learning, Advantage Actor-Critic, and Proximal Policy Optimisation algorithms.

Real-time personalised suggested antecedents and consequences of different BPSD, as well as motivational messages, will be generated using machine-learning classification algorithms. These algorithms will be informed by participants’ previously selected suggestions, their responses to motivational messages, and the stated or inferred preferences of other participants with similar characteristics. A hybrid recommendation approach combining item-based filtering, user-based collaborative filtering, and demographic filtering will be employed to generate activity recommendations and select motivational messages.

A multi-period activity recommender system will be developed to learn users’ choosing order and their repeated usage patterns using the recurrent neural network (RNN), enabling the system to recommend previously chosen items. Lastly, the RL algorithm will then assess which suggested antecedents and consequences of BPSD, which motivational messages, and which timing and frequency of delivery are most likely to maximise app engagement in the subsequent 24 h while minimising response delay. Algorithms for contextual multi-armed bandit (MAB) problems are employed, as they have been shown to be effective in maximising cumulative rewards in sequential decision-making across various domains [43].

Thompson Sampling is used as a Bayesian method well-suited to limited data settings; it is typically directed toward selecting the activity or message with the highest estimated reward while still allowing occasional exploration of options with greater uncertainty [44]. The RL algorithm will learn from previous data to maximise the probability of users’ receptivity using a linear regression model that is updated every day, and the learning data includes the content of the message, the time of sending the message, the day of the week, and the participants’ well-being captured by ecological momentary assessment (EMA). Short Horizon Policy Improvement (SHPI) [45], which approximates policy-induced drift in participant behaviour across sessions, will also be used. The data obtained from the MRTs will be used to further train the algorithms.

Methods: monitoring

An independent Data Monitoring Committee will be established to oversee participant safety and monitor any adverse effects arising from the intervention. The committee will be informed of outcome data throughout the study and will facilitate referral to appropriate healthcare professionals when additional assessment or support is required. Overall trial oversight will be coordinated by the principal investigator (last author) and research team in accordance with institutional and ethical guidelines. Ongoing monitoring will include regular review of recruitment progress, data completeness, and adverse events. All adverse events and protocol deviations will be documented and reported to the ethics committee in line with reporting requirements.

Ethics

This study will be carried out in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guideline and has received ethical approval from the relevant university committee. Any protocol modifications will be submitted for approval prior to implementation. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants before data collection, after they have been provided with clear, plain‑language information regarding the study aims, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and their right to withdraw at any time. Participants who complete all assessments and attend the intervention sessions will receive a HK$200 supermarket voucher to compensate their time involved in this study. Confidentiality will be strictly safeguarded, with all data anonymised and stored securely on institution‑approved servers accessible only to authorised members of the research team. Participants who experience emotional distress during the study will be provided with information about appropriate professional psychological support services.

Conclusion

This trial addresses an important gap in dementia care by testing an adaptive, technology-enabled intervention to support family caregivers in managing BPSD. A key strength of this study lies in its rigorous experimental approach, which is specifically designed to assess both intervention effectiveness and the optimisation of delivery strategies. By moving beyond conventional static, one-size-fits-all interventions, this design allows for systematic evaluation of adaptive sequences and just-in-time support strategies, generating robust evidence on how caregiver support can be tailored to changing needs. The integration of machine-learning methods further strengthens the scientific contribution by enabling data-driven optimisation of the intervention, using real-world behavioural information.

The findings from this trial have the potential to inform the development of scalable, engagement-responsive mHealth interventions for dementia caregivers. More broadly, this trial protocol may advance methodological standards for intervention optimisation in complex caregiving contexts.
