Water resources sustainability assessment through uncertain supply-demand modeling under scarcity conditions
Authors: Elham Ebrahimi Sarindizaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterconnected factors, including improper water resource management and climate change, escalated with rapid population growth, are worsening the global water crisis. This study bridges human-environment interactions through water performance indicators to transform complex problems into manageable information if implemented within organized frameworks. The DPSIR framework (Drivers, Pressures, State, Impacts, Responses), coupled with detailed causal relationships, can demonstrate pressures of human activities on ecosystems, impacts of changing state, and prompt responses to restore and enhance sustainability by reducing stressors. The indicators that measure different attributes of water resources systems help in understanding the interactions and their dynamics. This study utilizes key indicators, including water stress, productivity, and efficiency, to assess various aspects of water resource sustainability in the Kor-Sivand River Basin, Iran, as a region facing severe water scarcity challenges. The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) method is utilized to develop a composite indicator, avoiding redundant indicators while reducing complexity. Findings demonstrate growing socioeconomic drivers and pressures; the system state undergoes unstable water demands, and subsequent cascading impacts make the system respond inversely. The system’s behavior at a critical tipping point in the year 2008, coinciding with severe drought and dam impoundment, reveals the effects of human-environment interactions. This transferable methodological framework can be readily adapted to other water-stressed regions worldwide, providing a replicable template for informing sustainable water governance and diagnosing human-water interactions. The findings directly inform progress toward Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 6, Clean Water and Sanitation, by providing diagnostic tools for water stress assessment and management, supporting targets related to water-use efficiency (Target 6.4), water quality improvement (Target 6.3), and integrated water resources management (Target 6.5). The framework also holds relevance to SDG 14 through the effects of terrestrial water management decisions on downstream marine systems.Keywords: Sustainability, Water balance, Performance indicator, Supply and demand, ScarcitySubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Hydrology, Water resources

Introduction

Water scarcity is intensifying due to climate change, population growth, and demand-driven development policies1. It is estimated that by 2050, approximately 45% of the population will be located in water-stressed regions worldwide2, emphasizing the crucial role of proper water resource management in sustainable development amid the growing complexity of water balance challenges3,4. Current and future societal needs must be met in sustainable water resource systems, while conserving ecological integrity and hydrological balance5.

The necessity of integrating physical and economic water productivity (output per unit of water consumed and value generated per unit) towards sustainable water management grows as water stress intensifies. Consumer productivity improvement is often aligned with technological solutions to water loss reduction, while allocation productivity comes with broader policy considerations. In this context, indicators serve as powerful tools for human-environment interactions evaluation and sustainability policy information6.

Baseline characteristics evaluation is the primary step toward system-wide sustainability7. Analytical frameworks may prevent overlooking interdependencies by a traditional supply-demand focus, neglecting the economic and social dimensions8. Conceptual models enable systematic analysis of the systems by providing an organized and structured presentation of information9,10. DPSIR framework, as a conceptual model11, facilitates the development of indicator sets to link human activities, environmental issues, and the policymaking process to mitigate pressures and enhance resilience12,13. In this approach, anthropological activities serving as drivers enforce pressures on ecosystems, change the state by prompting impacts that lead to societal responses through feedback mechanisms. They contribute to giving insight into efforts toward vulnerability and risk reduction, adaptability enhancement, and sustainability improvement14.

DPSIR application in water sustainability challenges has been studied in several aspects15,10. Sun,16 employed this framework to identify governing factors affecting water supply-demand dynamics in India. Despite the widespread application of the DPSIR in sustainability studies, isolation in component analysis causes many studies failings to capture the dynamic interactions between drivers, pressures, states, impacts, and responses17. In these kinds of integrations of a conceptual model and a quantitative metric, the DPSIR categories are considered as static, independent compartments, which often lack presenting the associated dynamic feedbacks. Advancing toward practical applications, coupling the causal relationships could enhance the analytical value. Recent studies in the water resource sustainability field; emphasize the efforts to detect the main causes of the water-related challenges that integrate social, economic, and environmental attributes. Such frameworks align with growing recognition that sustainability assessments must investigate human-environment interactions, providing more robust insights into water scarcity. Zare et al.18 implemented DPSIR in the Gorganroud-Qarasu watershed, Iran, emphasizing population, financial activities, water resources, and flood risks within their system dynamics model for analyzing cause-and-effect relationships. To address water management challenges, Ebrahimi and Karamouz,12 combined a water-accounting framework with system dynamics and developed the SWA_SD model. They used the DPSIR approach to assess water security indicators in water-stressed regions. They reported an alarming trend corresponding to a 43% increase in water stress and significant fluctuations due to water balance measures. In another recent study, Khani et al.19 used the DPSIR model Water Resources and Environmental Carrying Capacity (WRECC) in Iran to calculate the systemic impacts of climate change and human activities. They offered different management strategies to improve WRECC.

As understanding water use dynamics is crucial for sustainable water resources management, different sets of indicators are taken into account to quantify the different interconnected attributes of the system. Many studies have focused on performance indicators of water resources systems20,21,12,22–24. There is still a crucial necessity to evaluate the role of humans in water resources systems, challenges, potential actions, and effective policy design15,25, which are region-specific in most cases. Principal Component Analysis (PCA), as a multivariate technique, transforms numerous quantitative indicators into fewer components, while preserving most of the original data’s variance26. While it is primarily considered an analytical tool, PCA can contribute to building composite indices which may lack an empirical basis for assessing different components or validating the indices by historical data. In this context, few studies were conducted including Xu et al.27, in which a DPSIR-PCA model was developed for ecological security assessment, their proposed index quantified pressures of the system and resulted in land-use policy recommendations.

While providing a strong foundation, a research gap remains, as the studies often incorporate DPSIR or System Dynamics (via CLDs) or PCA in isolation. When used sequentially, they often employ a non-iterative and static manner. Usually missing the causal understanding of system behavior from CLDs, in structuring the quantitative aggregation, with no direct, explanatory link back to the system’s dynamic drivers. With this background, a structured, causal integration is required in which a dynamic model serves as a ‘causal filter’ to define the governing variables for quantitative analysis, and then uses the quantitative results to diagnose and validate the system. The present study seeks to bridge these parallel efforts (DPSIR-SD for dynamic conceptualization and DPSIR-PCA for empirical indexing), leveraging the capabilities of both through a closed-loop integration, establishing a diagnostic tool explaining why an index changes. This study aims to bridge this gap by proposing a novel, closed-loop DPSIR-CLD-PCA framework. This structured integration transforms the proposed Sustainability Index from a descriptive dashboard into a diagnostic tool, linking quantitative trends to systemic drivers for actionable governance. The paper has been prepared in four sections: Sect. Materials and method details the materials and methods. After introducing the study area in Sect. Case study: the kor-sivand river basin, Sect. Discussion and Findings provides the integrated discussion on the results and explores policy implications. Finally, a summary and conclusion with a focus on the main contributions and the study’s broader relevance outline for water sustainability assessment are presented in Sect. Conclusion.

Materials and methods

Efficient water use supports economic development and advances the productivity of different agricultural, industrial, and urban sectors. From an ecological point of view, water use aims to ensure balanced water allocation for environmental requirements and conserve ecosystem integrity and sustainability. The dynamics of water resource systems, as a result of their exposure to fluctuations, environmental changes, and external shocks, bring an intensifying need for a clearer understanding of the interconnected components (Fig. 1).

A framework for supply-demand modeling in regions prone to water scarcity.

Feedback loops can define a given system’s behavior as the transparent interaction of the different components. Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs) show the feedback mechanisms through the system dynamics (SD) modeling under specified conditions and contribute to recognizing the solutions28. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is implemented to assess the dataset structure and to propose a composite index by estimating the contribution of different variables and indicators. In this study, the DPSIR framework, performance indicators, and statistical methods are applied to identify the water resource systems’ relationships, prioritize key issues, and conceptualize system behavior for sustainable management.

DPSIR framework

Effective modeling begins with an inclusive understanding of the system’s characteristics and the problem’s dimensions. The DPSIR framework is organized to provide a conceptual approach for cause-effect relationships analysis in socio-ecological systems29,30.

The DPSIR model presents how social, economic, and demographic development can act as Drivers (D), to impose Pressures (P) that change the environmental State (S), and generate Impacts (I). Impacts trigger Responses (R): human interferences that alter complex systems, or affect environmental Pressures (P), State (S), and Impacts (I) directly. This framework is well-known among the diverse stakeholders for its ability to facilitate the understanding of the system and key indicator development in assessing water resource sustainability31. To extend the DPSIR framework, system behavior can be assessed through nonlinear feedback loop analysis and SD modeling. CLDs are the feedback loops that qualitatively demonstrate the system structure via variable relationships. CLDs include reinforcing (R) or balancing (B) loops based on their effects established by positive or negative feedback32.

Performance indicators for water resource systems

An appropriate set of indicators to describe water resource sustainability requires several essential criteria: comprehensiveness (economy, society, ecosystems, and human-water relationships inclusion); minimization of redundancy (indicator independence); data accessibility and methodological feasibility33. Indicators provide the required information for an organized evaluation of water resource status, helping decision-makers measure progress toward sustainable development in a quantifiable manner, changing over time, without the complex analysis12. Composite index development through different methodologies requires concise choices that shape its validity and reliability. A robust theoretical framework definition, representative indicators selection, and data standardization for consistent aggregation are among key factors26,34.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA is the most widely used multivariate statistical method to transform a high-dimensional dataset into a set of linearly uncorrelated variables. PCA aggregates multiple indicators into a single index35–37, and allows efficient data compression while preserving maximal variability.

Data collection and preprocessing

Preparation of the multivariate dataset for PCA is initiated by employing the Z-score method (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) for all indicators to ensure comparability across different units and scale effects removal. Then linear interpolation can be incorporated for (a few) missing data, justified by the reports or continuous temporal trends characteristic of the data. Finally, the correlation matrix of the indicators is derived to assess their intercorrelations. This way, the suitability of the dataset for PCA, which seeks to illustrate the shared variance among variables, can be validated.

Component selection was governed by a dual-criteria approach to ensure the extracted dimensions were statistically and conceptually significant. The primary criterion was the retention of the components based on the proportion of total variance explained. To provide a defensible and reproducible basis for the PCA-based dimensionality reduction within each DPSIR category, this was cross-validated through Kaiser rule, in which all components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were retained as they explain more variance than a single original one. As for the interpretability of components, they should contribute a meaningful variance share with clear pattern of loadings. Specifically, components were recognized and justified by identifying the indicators with high absolute loadings, moving beyond vague statistical constructs.

Integrative framework: linking DPSIR, CLD, and PCA

The development of an integrated framework of DPSIR model, System Dynamics-based CLDs, and PCA, is designed to utilize the capabilities and overcome the associated shortcomings of these tools in isolation (DPSIR as a static checklist, CLDs as a qualitative conceptualization, and PCA as a statistical method). The closed loop of causality and quantification established in this framework (Fig. 2), has three primary stages. Stage 1 (DPSIR→ CLD): The DPSIR model provided the systematic structure of the key components’ identification. A coverage of the Drivers (e.g., population growth, agricultural policy), Pressures (e.g., water withdrawals), States (e.g., reservoir storage, groundwater level), Impacts (e.g., agricultural productivity loss), and Responses (e.g., irrigation efficiency investments). Translating the static diagnostic output into a dynamic hypothesis of system behavior, variables included in DPSIR, emerged as the main nodes in CLDs. The analysis made to define the causalities and the feedback loops (reinforcing and balancing) between these nodes, to transform the DPSIR from a list of categories to a model of how changes in a component (e.g., a Pressure) propagate through and feed back upon others (e.g., State and Response). This way the non-linear, complex interdependencies of the water resource systems can be captured. Stage 2 (CLD → PCA): A theory-driven link to move from qualitative dynamics to quantifiable evaluation was established in which the set of variables included in the CLD constitutes the justified and coherent set of indicators. This way, the data utilized in PCA is not on an arbitrary collection of available data and variables’ interrelationships and their systemic roles has been validated. Stage 3 (PCA → CLD): The PCA outputs were then systematically interpreted through the CLD lens with a diagnostic feedback loop. The principal components and their factor loadings are evaluated to identify the parts of CLD with strong co-variation, statistically validating the prominence of linkages within the historical data. Then a quantitative trajectory of system performance, was provided based on the indices derived from the PCA scores, which can diagnostically be followed within the CLD. For instance, a sharp decline period is interpreted by identifying which feedback loops became dominant during that period due to specific shocks or policies. This closes the loop; the quantitative indices can highlight what happened, and the qualitative CLD can explain why it happened.

Sensitivity analysis of index weighting

The aggregation of the the DPSIR component indices to derive a composite index was conducted by a linear model (). To test the sensitivity to the equal category importance assumption, it was analyzed whether the temporal trajectory of the index and critical system transition points identifications is dependent on this weighting. A variance-proportional weighting scheme was implemented to be compared against the baseline equal-weight model. This way (weight of each category) was set proportional to the total variance explained by the principal components within that category. In this data-driven scheme, a category with a larger share of the statistical variance in the dataset is associated with a higher weight in the final index. The Pearson correlation coefficient was then conducted to assess the sensitivity of the composite index, in which a high correlation would indicate that the robustness of the primary conclusions.

Case study: the kor-sivand river basin

Numerous studies reveal concerning water balance deficits in numerous watersheds in most of the developing countries, especially Iran38–41. With a cumulative groundwater deficit exceeding 143 billion cubic meters, affecting over 420 of Iran’s 609 aquifers (about 90% of the groundwater potential), as prohibited42. Poor management practices have imposed pressures from overexploitation to meet growing demand, climate change, and water quality degradation43. The emphasis of this study is on the Kor-Sivand River Basin, comprising two distinct study areas: the Marvdasht-Kharameh region (3941 km²) and the Zarghan-Beyza region (1738 km²). Figure 2 shows the details of the study area.

The study area covers urban centers including Marvdasht, Kharameh, Izadkhast, Takht-e Jamshid, Emamzadeh Esmail, Beyza, and Zarghan. Between 2007 and 2008 and 2014–2015, the groundwater level drop was three meters greater than the national average44. This complex system experiences the interaction between natural resources and anthropogenic water management infrastructure in southwestern Iran’s arid environment. The basin’s average annual precipitation is 337 mm, while withdrawals from surface water resources exceed 757 MCM; this value is about 1232 MCM for groundwater resources.

Discussion and findings

Long-term sustainability should strike a balance between the competing demands, ensuring water resources meet development requirements and ecosystem resilience maintenance. The feedback mechanism between the governing factors affecting water scarcity in a given study area reveals the key, complex, nonlinear interdependencies within the system. Beyond the impact of climate change pressure, the system’s dynamics and patterns may be affected by policies as exogenous variables. Different sectors’ share in the economy may trigger policymakers and other stakeholders to pursue the same older patterns of development or establish paradigm shifts. The state component reflects the physical condition of water resources quality and quantity. The pressures impose impacts such as resource degradation and socioeconomic challenges. Strategic interventions to enhance the efficiency of water use/reuse and treatment shape the responses to ensure reliable water availability and approach sustainability, which are vital in regions experiencing water scarcity. The interactions of these components and the corresponding variables in the study area help recognize the causal relationships and governing mechanisms of the systems in a given study area (Fig. 3).

Development of the causal loop diagrams

Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs) development is essential to building a shared understanding of the system state and behavior. CLD, as a qualitative SD technique, can be utilized for mapping the problem structure (i.e., variables, relationships, and feedback loops) (Karamouz et al., 2023). A systematic process was undertaken to develop the CLDs for moving from the static DPSIR framework to a dynamic hypothesis in the context of the water resource system. This way, all governing variables and their interconnections were recognized and justified based on the SD principles. The initial step was the derivation of variables from the list of indicators of each DPSIR component (namely Drivers, Pressures, State, Impacts, Responses). Then they were refined through a structured literature review (to identify common causal relationships and feedback mechanisms) and consultation with water resource management experts familiar with the study area. The finalized set of key variables with a clear operational definition are presented in Table A1 in the Appendix. The variables were assessed to establish the feedback processes in a system, and the causal links between the variables were justified based on the literature and expert validation.

In the CLD context, variables are connected by arrows headed by positive/negative signs, to show the causal links. The polarity of links (positive ‘+’ or negative ‘-‘) is assigned based on the standard conventions of SD: A positive sign indicates that cause and effect changes in the same direction, while the negative sign depicts the opposite direction of the changes. Feedback loops, categorized in reinforcing (denoted ‘R’) and balancing (denoted ‘B’) loops, are formed by the combination of positive and negative relationships according to the effect of a given variable’s variation propagating and reinforcing the variable or vice versa32. The CLDs integrate the components of the system, to indicate the qualitative blueprint for understanding the system’s behavior through reinforcing and balancing loops (Fig. 3). It is subsequently quantified through the PCA, described in the following section. Figure 3 captures how water scarcity serves as a critical bottleneck to further expansion.

As shown in Fig. 3, the color-coded arrows denote distinct causal relationship types within the SD model. Reinforcing loops are depicted in green and coded by a clockwise arrow labeled by R, highlighting the positive causal links and a direct proportional relationship between variables. In contrast, the red arrows show the inverse relationship between the linked variables. The established balancing loops, labeled as B with a counterclockwise arrow, indicate overall negative causal connections. The balancing loops (labeled as B1-B10) and reinforcing loops (labeled as R1-R9) are explained in Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

PCA for composite index development

Here, PCA is utilized to analyze the dataset’s structure and suitability to establish a composite indicator by clustering similar indicators and those that can serve as the main part of the composite index. The PCA is utilized to refine the multidimensional system dynamics, captured through the DPSIR qualitative framework and the Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs), into quantitative indices. The variable selection for the PCA was guided by the validated system framework and was not arbitrary. The foundational indicator pool was established by the quantifiable variables representing the key components within the CLD (Table 2). Data for the indicator estimation were collected for the Kor-Sivand Basin from Iran Ministry of Energy, Iran Ministry of Agriculture, Iran Ministry of Industry, Mining, and Trade, and Iran Statistical Center.

The multivariate dataset for PCA, was prepared following preprocessing steps, including missing data imputation, standardization, and correlation check. For the few instances of missing values, linear interpolation was utilized to preserve the dataset’s continuity, based on the relatively smooth temporal trends in the data time series. The Z-score method (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) were applied to all data. This was required as the data had different units and scales (e.g., MCM, ha, dimensionless ratios). Additionally, this eliminated the potential for dominating the PCA components by the variables having large numerical ranges. A correlation matrix was derived to confirm significant inter-correlations of the indicators, and to validate the suitability of the dataset for PCA, as it extracts the components due to the shared variance among variables.

PCA results: identification of latent sustainability dimensions

As detailed in the Sect. Principal component analysis (PCA), PCA method was applied to the indicator sets for each DPSIR category. For each category, two principal components were retained, which explain a substantial share of the system’s variance (more than 70% across categories). Regarding the loadings listed in Table A2, components were defined based on the indicators presenting high absolute loadings.

For instance, within the Driving Force (D) category, the components are defined by Agricultural Water Share and Industrial Water Share, which can be interpreted as Sectoral Water Demand Pressure. Similarly, the dominant component for the Pressure (P) category, was Agricultural Sector’s Share in Water Use, interpreted as Sectoral Water use. As for State (S) category, it is characterized by high loadings on Dependency on Groundwater, leading to Water Resource Availability interpretation. Regarding the Impact (I) component, Agricultural Employment Productivity and Industrial Employment Productivity were among the dominant indicators, naming the category as Economic Productivity. Dominant themes of the basin’s sustainability dynamics across all DPSIR categories were explored based on the highest loadings, that yielded Technical Infrastructural Response for the R category.

Integrated diagnosis: linking trends to causal feedback

The temporal convergence of DPSIR components (illustrated in Fig. 4), coinciding with drought and dam impoundment in 2008, highlights a quantitative tipping point. Moving beyond description, this event is diagnosed through its systemic causes within the integrated framework. The sharp decline is directly attributed to the activation of the limits and the balancing loops in the CLD (Fig. 3). The compounded shocks reduced Water Availability, strengthening this loop where stress applies pressure on agricultural expansion. This causal narrative is empirically grounded. Based on the PCA loadings (Table 5), the Agricultural sector’s share in water use strongly covaries with the same component (Resource Pressure), validating the linkage. On the other hand, the high loadings of Agricultural water use efficiency on the Response Efficacy component validate the hypothesized synergy in the related reinforcing loops. Establishing a closed diagnostic loop, the CLD provides the causal hypothesis, and the PCA provides empirical validation. The feedback loops act as engines (CLDs → Indicator Values → PCA → Index), explaining the trends and complex dynamics shown in the pdfs (Fig. 6). Thus, the framework can explain why sharp changes occur based on the feedback interactions and suggests how to reverse them by leveraging feedbacks that inform the policy pathways outlined.

Policy implications and pathways for adaptive governance

In the context of policy implication, learning cycles are required to pave the way for moving from static water management to adaptive water governance, in which actions are informed by monitoring and causal understanding of the system. The proposed integrated framework contributes to establishing the primary components of this cycle by the composite index, as a robust monitoring metric, and the Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs) as an explanatory model for diagnosing changes and testing interventions. Culminating in a management feedback loop creation: monitoring the → diagnosing cause-and-effect via the CLDs → policy design and test → implementation → monitoring again. The aim is to transition from reactive crisis response to proactive, system-aware management. The outline of how this loop and non-linear interdependencies can be operationalized is as follows.

Policy design strategies should focus on the key identified feedback loops. For instance, to approach sustainable goals, incentives that directly boost irrigation efficiency and water productivity may be prioritized as a short-term modification. Incentives may include subsidies or low-interest loans for adopting modern irrigation technologies (e.g., drip, smart sensors), which in turn may deepen water scarcity in the long term, due to the rebound effect50. Additionally, the extension programs to promote high-value, drought-resistant crops may be incorporated to preserve economic gains from agriculture and decouple production from water withdrawal. On the other hand, to postpone or prevent the overshoot and collapse dynamics, with symptoms revealed by the 2008 tipping point, water resources management should be tied to availability. To do so, initial steps may include dynamic water allocation quotas implementation that adjust annual agricultural water permits based on a pre-season assessment of water availability, and zoning regulations enforcement based on land use planning. This approach demonstrates the limit to growth mechanism in agricultural expansion, as the water availability is the main bottleneck of the expansion and development in water-stressed sub-basins. This institutionalizes the balancing loop and key negative feedback required for system stability.

As the composite Index provides a quantifiable metric applicable in operationalizing adaptive governance. Its integration into practical management may initiate from monitoring and early-warning. Annual calculation and publishing of the , can provide a routine performance dashboard for the basin. Basic evaluations of a sustained change (such as a decline or a breach of a predefined threshold) compared to the metrics in the long term may trigger a formal management response. Overall, a tiered, adaptive response protocol can be established as follows:

With sustained changes monitoring in the values and trends, the underlying decomposed scores of its DPSIR components and the referencing causalities depicted by CLDs can be used diagnostically to precisely target the sub-systems requiring intervention. This way, policy designs are directed to the root causes. Perhaps this model can serve as a basis for policy-testing sandbox before implementation. Planners can simulate the long-term impact of policies on the trajectory of the and its underlying indicators and components. This shift to proactive management can contribute to the risk reduction of unintended consequences, which are barely manageable after implementation.

Overall, a roadmap for transitioning from crisis-driven, supply-side interventions to a preventative, adaptive governance system is provided by targeting governing feedback loops, employing the as a managerial dashboard, and leveraging the model for previously implemented policies evaluation. Comparing this index to previously established indices, often a traditional index answers ‘what is the state?‘; this approach answers ‘why is the state such, and what will change it? As examples, regarding the Water Poverty Index (WPI) or the UN’s Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 6 Index, they provide a valuable standardized snapshot or cross-sectional ranking of water stress. The final product of this study extends this paradigm by the composite score within its causal system. The integrated model provides the systems narrative behind the snapshot, diagnosing the feedback loops determining its trajectory. This explanatory power, which links metric performance directly to systemic mechanisms and behavior, represents an advancement in moving from monitoring to management.

Limitations, generalizability, and future research

While providing a robust diagnostic evaluation, this study has faced a variety of limitations that should be acknowledged to contextualize the findings and convey the future research line. The first limitation is the data existence and reliability, which is one of the major challenges experienced frequently in developing regions. The resolution of available historical data directly affects the precision of the analysis. For instance, the annual data may mask important sub-basin heterogeneity and intra-annual dynamics at the basin scale. Second, the SD-based investigations are holistic, although they capture main socio-hydrological feedback, this, in turn, may be considered as a purposeful simplification, in which the complex processes are presented in a parameterized form. Third, the PCA conducted here and the resulting index are dependent on the indicator set, temporal scale of the investigation, and socio-environmental context of the study area. The relative weightings would likely vary in a region with different economic, environmental, or institutional properties.

With these contexts and case-specific limitations, the core methodological contribution, the sequential integration of DPSIR, CLD, and PCA, remains broadly generalizable as a replicable template for holistic water sustainability assessment in other regions. The major contribution is the integrative approach, not the numerical outputs for a particular study area. The methodology is context-adaptable. To adapt it to a new region, the DPSIR framework should be re-contextualized to identify case-specific Drivers, Pressures, States, Impacts, and Responses. With re-validated Causal Loop Diagrams to modify the feedback structure by adapting variables to reflect local dynamics—whether transboundary water agreements, groundwater recharge patterns, or seasonal variability. The PCA should be re-executed with local available data, drawing from universally accessible categories like economic statistics, hydrological records, and agricultural census data, to establish a sustainability index. This transferability was depicted in the study’s variable selection process (Table A1), which reliance on data categories that exist in most national statistical systems worldwide. Therefore, while the numerical outputs are specific to the Kor-Sivand Basin, the analytical framework offers a defensible methodology adaptable to diverse geographical and developmental contexts facing water scarcity challenges.

On this basis, future studies focus lies on: (1) incorporating high-resolution spatiotemporal data, utilizing remote sensing, citizen science or IoT sensors, to enhance the model’s precision; (2) deepening the social aspects by including stakeholder participatory dimension in the CLD co-creation and policy scenario testing phases; and (3) coupling the SD model with climate projection models to assess the long-term sustainability of the study area. These streamlines can further strengthen the framework’s utility as a decision-support tool for adaptive water governance in an uncertain world.

Conclusion

A closed-loop analytical framework is proposed in this study in which complex quantitative trends are diagnostically illustrated by qualitative causal mechanisms. By applying the methodology to the Kor-Sivand Basin, a critical tipping point around 2008 is captured, which is a result of a variety of responses of core system components quantitatively. Providing a more precise insight, the PCA method was used to reduce the dimension and abstract principal components within each set of indicators that corresponded to the DPSIR framework. Analysis showed that PC(D) experienced a rise in recent years, while PC(P) had a consistent fluctuation. PC(S) inversely mirrored PC(P), and PC(I) peaked before declining. PS(R) showed steadiness with incremental growth. The study area’s experience in 2008 includes an extreme drought event and a dam impoundment on the Kor River. This is obvious in the findings of the study as the year 2008 is introduced as a convergence point (so-called tipping point), highlighting how hydrological shocks propagate through all components (D, P, S, I, and R). Underlining how structural solutions worsen stress, the need to address the main drivers of water scarcity through integrated approaches to address root causes and long-term solutions, rather than mitigating the pressures that alter the state of the system, remains urgent. These nonlinear interactions, particularly during transitional years, emphasize the need to capture nonlinear and uncertain systemic connections beyond deterministic trends. The results of the moving averages and probability density functions exposed distinct trends. The steady increases were detected for D and P. However, S, I, and R exhibit nonlinear fluctuations, reflecting system complexity and showing persistent drivers and dynamic responses; the study area suffers from severe water-related challenges. Probabilistic analysis further clarified this shift, with the multimodal probability density functions (pdfs) of the components reflecting high variability and uncertainty. The integrated CLD-DPSIR-PCA framework diagnoses the system’s behavior regarding the activated feedback loops, and PCA loadings empirically validated the causal links. For instance, agricultural water use loads on Resource Pressure and efficiency loads on Response Efficacy are validated based on this approach. This closed diagnostic loop—from causal hypothesis (CLD) to empirical validation (PCA)— identifies systemic shifts and leverage points for policy making. Applying this framework identifies agricultural imbalance as a primary factor of water resource challenges in the Kor-Sivand River Basin. As findings reveal, historical interventions, based on a misunderstanding of “there is always water to supply”, have favored structural solutions, including: large reservoirs construction; irrigation networks development; and rain-fed lands to irrigated agriculture conversion, confirmed by the post-2008 tipping point, can amplify the pressure on the system. Which in turn, as suggested in some studies12,53,54, results in sectoral shift (from agriculture to service/industrial/tourism sectors) that may serv as a solution, however, such singular approaches may be insufficient for regional development goals, along with the sustainable and efficient resource management. More precise investigations are required to evaluate the effects of the different measures on the system’s diverse components. The findings underscore that immediate, short-term, low-cost decision-making and supply-side solutions can deepen unsustainable subsequences. To address the root causes of water scarcity, a systems-based approach is required to balance multiple development objectives. The dual nature of the economic and ecological water use interaction manifests as either competition or synergy. From a competitive perspective, rapid economic development often encroaches on ecological water allocations; while economic optimization can enhance ecological restoration capacity and environmental sustainability, it enables higher-quality economic development, presenting the synergistic potential. The causalities addressed in this study, particularly the management requirement of the agricultural expansion limited by water resources availability and productivity investment, provide visions for achieving water security while maintaining both economic vitality and environmental integrity. Moving beyond reactive management by targeting causal feedbacks, system-based policies for adaptation in stressed river basins facing similar hydro-environmental uncertainties are suggested. To approach sustainability, the focal point of the future governance should be the proceeding from reactive, structural fixes to proactive management of the system’s inherent feedbacks. Aligning with the broader sustainable development agenda, this study offers actionable insights for SDG 6, Clean Water and Sanitation, particularly Targets 6.3, 6.4, and 6.5 on water quality, efficiency, and integrated management. The framework advances water resources sustainability, indirectly contributing to SDG 14 via land-water interactions, implying that to achieving SDG 14, progress on SDG 6 is foundational.
