α-hederin inhibits cervical cancer progression by inducing DNA damage-dependent cell cycle blockade and apoptosis
Authors: Yanlun Song, Haimei Qin, Shaofeng Huang, Jian Wang, Yuehua Huang, Zongyun Lin, Fenglian Yang, Xin Zhang, Rong Wang, Junli Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer incidence has not decreased significantly despite widespread use of HPV vaccines and screening measures.PurposeThis study explored the potential of α-Hederin in the treatment of cervical cancer.MethodsCCK8, EdU staining assay, flow cytometry were conducted for apoptosis, cell cycle, ROS, and mitochondrial membrane potential. Mechanism effects of α-hederin on cervical cancer cells were investigated using transcriptome sequencing, bioinformatics analysis, and single-cell sequencing. Further in-depth detection of key proteins with western blot, immunofluorescence, overexpression of CHK1 gene, DNA damage inhibitors, and NAC inhibitors were performed to study the regulation of the cell cycle. Inhibition of this action significantly promoted G2M phase block. Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common malignancy worldwide and a common cause of cancer death among women. Despite the widespread HPV vaccination and screening, the incidence of cervical cancer has not decreased significantly [1]. Mainstream treatments for gynecologic malignancies include surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy; however, these treatments are not effective for all patients [2]. For advanced and recurrent cervical cancer, chemotherapy is a standard treatment; platinum-based chemotherapy regimens are widely used as first-line treatment. However, chemotherapy resistance is a key factor affecting the treatment outcome of advanced and recurrent cervical cancer [3]. Therefore, new therapeutic agents and deeper understanding of their molecular mechanisms are key to developing effective therapeutic strategies. Recently, natural compounds have received widespread attention for their potential anticancer properties, and many of these compounds have demonstrated promising results in inhibiting tumor growth and progression.

α-Hederin, a pentacyclic triterpenoid saponin isolated from Fructus Akebia [4, 5], exhibits anti-inflammatory activity, relaxes airway and gastrointestinal smooth muscle, and increases cell permeability. There are no reports of significant adverse reactions or side effects associated with α-hederin [5]. α-Hederin displays significant inhibitory effects on a wide range of tumor cells, suggesting its great potential in therapeutic development [6]. α-Hederin can halt the progression of a variety of cancers, such as breast, liver, gastric, and colorectal cancers [7], through several mechanisms, such as induction of apoptosis and cell cycle arrest; reduction of ATP production; and inhibition of autophagy, cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [8]. However, it has not yet been studied in cervical cancer, so its potential and effectiveness in cervical cancer treatment remain unclear. Further clinical trials and studies are needed to validate its application prospects.

Abnormalities in cell cycle progression are a fundamental mechanism of tumorigenesis that make regulators of the cell cycle machinery rational targets for anticancer therapy. Thus, therapies targeting components of the cell cycle machinery can inhibit cancer cell division and also reverse cancer metabolism and restore cancer immune surveillance [9]. The cell cycle is involved in most stages of cancer progression, as the proliferation of cancer cells is mainly regulated by the cell cycle [10]. In normal cells, the activity of cell cycle proteins is tightly regulated by their cell cycle-specific transcription, protein degradation, and several CDK inhibitor proteins. All these mechanisms are frequently dysregulated in human cancers, leading to aberrant activation of cell cycle proteins. Furthermore, genetic damage within the core cell cycle machinery, leading to its hyperactivation, plays a pathogenic role in the development of most tumor types [11]. The cell cycle consists of four consecutive but non-overlapping phases: G1, S, G2, and M phases. Each phase is characterized by critical checkpoints that regulate cell cycle progression, and suspend the cell cycle and initiate repair mechanisms when necessary to ensure normal cell division and function. Drug-induced cell cycle arrest leads to inefficient repair and, if the damage cannot be repaired, apoptosis [12]. It has been reported that α-hederin induces G2/M blockade of colon cancer cells, resulting in decreased cell viability. Cell proliferation is a highly regulated process controlled by cell cycle progression [13]. However, the role of α-hederin in cervical cancer and its regulation on the cell cycle remain unknown. Therapies targeting cell cycle regulation have gradually gained significant attention in therapeutic cancer studies [12]. In cervical cancer, dysregulation of cell cycle regulatory mechanisms plays a key role. By regulating cell cycle-related proteins, such as cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) and P53, the over-proliferation of cancer cells can be effectively inhibited and apoptosis induced. Certain natural compounds and small molecule drugs can specifically interfere with cancer cell cycle progression, especially at the G1/S and G2/M checkpoints, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of cervical cancer cells. α-hederin and other natural compounds inhibit cancer cell growth by interfering with the key nodes of the cell cycle [13] and are expected to provide a new therapeutic strategy for cervical cancer. Drug-induced cell cycle arrest may also enhance the effects of other therapeutic agents, such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy, further improving the combined effects of treatment. Therefore, cell cycle-related therapeutic approaches can provide new treatment options for cervical cancer and new directions for improving the prognosis of patients with treatment-resistant [14] and recurrent [15] cervical cancer.

The purpose of this study is to explore the potential mechanism of inhibiting cervical cancer cells by studying the effect of α-hederin on cervical cancer cells, and to provide a new traditional Chinese medicine monomer for the treatment of cervical cancer.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human cervical squamous carcinoma cells including SiHa cells and HeLa from pricella (Wuhan, China) were cultured in DMEM medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) and supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Yeasen, Shanghai, China), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. The medium was changed every 2–3 days to maintain optimal conditions for cell growth. For experimental treatments, cells were inoculated at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/mL and cultured to 70–80% fusion, followed by passaging or related experiments.

Chemical reagents

α-Hederin (HY-N0255), methylproamine (MPA, HY-15620), N-acetylcysteine (NAC, HY-B0215) were purchased from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, USA) and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, D8371, Solarbio). The following antibodies were used: Caspase3 (Proteintech, 19,677–1-AP, 1:1000), Cleaved caspase3 (Proteintech, 25,128–1-AP, 1:1000), PARP1 (proteintech, 13,371–1-AP. 1:3000), Cleaved-PARP1 (BOSTER, Clone#HI-16, 1:1000), E-cadherin (Proteintech, 20,874–1-AP, 1:100,000), N-cadherin (Proteintech, 22,018–1-AP, 1. 8000), γ-H2AX (Proteintech, 83,307–2-RR, 1:10,000), ATM (Proteintech, 27,156–1-AP, 1:1000), CHK1 (Biodragon, BD-PT0902,1:1000), P-CHK1 (Proteintech, 28,805–1-AP, 1:4000), P53 (Proteintech, 10,442–1-AP, 1:10,000), P21 (Biodragono, BD-PP1849, 1:1000), CDC25B (Biodragono, BD-PT0799, 1:1000), the CDC25C (Proteintech, 16,485–1-AP, 1:8000), CyclinB2 (Proteintech, 21,644–1-AP, 1:8000), CDK1 (Proteintech, 10,762–1-AP, 1:2000).

CCK8 experiments

To assess the effect of α-hederin on cell proliferation, SiHa and HeLa cells were inoculated in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well, respectively. After culturing at 37 °C for 24 h, the cells were cultured with fresh medium containing different concentrations of α-hederin for treatment over 48 h. At the end of the treatment, 10 μL of the CCK-8 reagent (APEXBIO, USA) was added to each well and continued to incubate at 37 °C for 2 h. The incubation was performed at 37 °C for 1 h. The absorbance values at 450 nm were measured using an enzyme labeling instrument (TECAN, Switzerland). Cell activity was calculated by comparing the absorbance values of the treated and control groups. The results are expressed as the percentage of cell activity relative to the control group.

EdU staining for proliferation analysis

To evaluate cell proliferation, SiHa and HeLa cells were inoculated in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well, treated with different concentrations of α-hederin for 24 h, and then incubated for 2 h at 37 °C with the addition of 10 μM EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine). After incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 solution for 15 min. Subsequently, the EdU staining reaction was performed according to the instructions of the EdU detection kit (Beyotime, China). After the reaction, cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was observed by fluorescence microscopy (Leica, Germany).

Apoptosis assay by flow cytometry

Apoptosis induced by α-hederin treatment was explored. SiHa and HeLa cells were inoculated in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight. After treatment with different concentrations of α-hederin for 24, cells were digested using trypsin, collected, and washed twice with phosphate buffer (PBS), and finally resuspended in 1X binding buffer at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. To detect apoptosis, cells were incubated with the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD pharmigne, USA) for staining, per manufacturer’s instructions. The brief procedure was to incubate 100 μL of cell suspension with 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL of propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature protected from light. At the end of the incubation, 400 μL of 1X binding buffer was added to each sample and incubation was continued. Stained cells were analyzed using flow cytometry (Beckman, USA) to quantify the percentage of apoptotic cells. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, USA) and the percentage of apoptotic cells was calculated.

Clone formation assay

To analyze the effect of α-hederin on the clone formation potential of SiHa and HeLa cells, the cells were first inoculated in 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells/well and cultured overnight. Twenty-four hours after inoculation, the cells were treated with different concentrations of α-hederin for 48. After treatment, the medium was replaced with fresh medium, and the culture was continued for 14 days under conventional culture conditions. Finally, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and were then stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 30 min. Clone formation efficiency was determined by calculating the ratio of the number of clones to the total number of inoculated cells and expressed as a percentage. The experiment was repeated three times and the data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy for mitochondrial membrane potential assay

To assess the effect of α-hederin on the mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) of SiHa and HeLa cells, cells were first inoculated in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight. Twenty-four hours after inoculation, cells were treated with different concentrations of α-hederin) for 24 h. After removal of the medium, cells were stained using the JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential probe (Invitrogen, USA) per manufacturer’s instructions. The intensity of red fluorescence (JC-1 polymer, representing high membrane potential) and green fluorescence (JC-1 monomer, representing low membrane potential) of JC-1 was then analyzed using flow cytometry (Beckman, USA). Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed by calculating the intensity ratio of red to green fluorescence. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, USA). Stained cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany) and imaged using 490/530 nm (fluorescence, JC-1 monomer) and 540/590 nm (fluorescence, JC-1 polymer) excitation/emission wavelengths. After images were acquired, changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were assessed by analyzing fluorescence distribution. The percentage of cells with red or green fluorescence was quantified by randomly selecting the field of view.

Cell scratch assay

Cell scratch experiments were performed to assess the effect of α-hederin on the migratory ability of SiHa and HeLa cells. SiHa and HeLa cells were inoculated in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight. After cells reached 90% confluence, a Linear wound was delineated on the cell monolayer using a 200 μL sterile pipette tip. After washing the cells with PBS and removing the detached cells, fresh medium containing different concentrations of α-hederin was added and the culture was continued for 24 h. To monitor the wound healing process, phase contrast images of the same area were taken at set time points (e.g., 0, 24, 48 h) using an optical inverted microscope (ZEISS Germany, Japan). The wound healing area was measured using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). The experiment was repeated thrice and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Transwell cell migration assay

Transwell migration assays were performed to assess the effect of α-hederin on the migration ability of SiHa and HeLa cells. SiHa and HeLa cells were inoculated in 24-well Transwell chambers at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well using 8 μm pore size membranes (Corning Costar, USA) with 200 μL of serum-free medium. After inoculation, the cells were incubated for 24 h and then treated with different concentrations of α-hederin for 24 h. Complete medium (600 μL) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) as a chemokine was added to the lower chamber. After treatment, the unmigrated cells in the upper chamber were gently wiped away with a cotton swab, retaining the cells that migrated through the membrane to the bottom. Cells on the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and were stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 min. The stained cells were washed with PBS to remove excess dye. Under a light microscope (ZEISS Germany, Japan), five fields of view were randomly selected at 100 × magnification and the number of stained migrating cells was counted. The average number of migrating cells in each field of view was calculated and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Clinical cervical cancer tissue acquisition

The clinical tissue samples of cervical cancer used in this study were obtained from surgical resection specimens of patients approved by the Ethics Committee of Youjiang Medical University of Nationalities (Ethics Review No. 2025010501). Our research strictly adheres to ethical requirements and the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients signed informed consent. Surgically excised tumor tissue samples were sent to the laboratory for processing immediately after surgery. Tumor samples were used for immunohistochemical analysis. To ensure the reliability of the experimental results, we strictly screened patient samples that met the inclusion criteria and excluded patients with a history of chemotherapy or radiotherapy and or other malignant tumors. All samples were processed in accordance with uniform operational specifications during the experimental process to ensure the completeness and representativeness of the samples. For control analysis, normal cervical tissues were collected from the same patients and used as a control group. All tissue samples were handled and used in accordance with ethical requirements and in strict compliance with relevant clinical research norms.

Transcriptome sequencing

RNA sequencing was performed to investigate the effect of α-hederin treatment on the gene expression profile of cells. HeLa cells were inoculated in 6-well plates at a density of: 1 × 10⁶ cells/well and treated with 10 μM α-hederin for 24 h. Control cells were treated with equal amounts of loaded DMSO. Control cells were treated with an equal amount of loaded DMSO. RNA from total samples was isolated and purified using TRIzol (Invitrogen, CA, USA), per manufacturer protocols. The amount and purity of total RNA were quality controlled with a NanoDrop ND-1000 (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA). The integrity of the RNA was examined using Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, CA, USA) and also using agarose electrophoresis. Concentrations > 50 ng/μL, RIN values > 7.0, OD260/280 > 1.8, and total RNA > 1 μg satisfied downstream experiments. The mRNA with PolyA (polyadenylate) was specifically captured by two rounds of purification using oligo(dT) magnetic beads (Dynabeads Oligo (dT), item no. 25–61,005, Thermo Fisher, USA). The captured mRNAs were fragmented using the Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (NEBNext® Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module, Item No. E6150S, USA) at 94 °C for 5–7 min. The fragmented RNA was used to synthesize cDNA by reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase, No. 1896649, CA, USA). cDNA was then synthesized using E. coli DNA polymerase I (NEB, No. m0209, USA), with RNase H (NEB, No. m0209, USA), and RNase H (NEBNext® Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module, No. E6150S, USA). RNase H (NEB, item no. m0297, USA) for two-strand synthesis to convert these composite double strands of DNA and RNA into DNA duplexes. dUTP Solution (Thermo Fisher, item no. R0133, CA, USA) was also doped into the duplexes to complement the ends of the double-stranded DNA into flat ends. An A base was added to each of its ends to enable it to be ligated to a junction with a T base at the end. Fragment size was screened and purified using magnetic beads. The second strand was digested with UDG enzyme (NEB, item no. m0280, MA, US) and subjected to PCR—pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, denaturation at 98 °C for a total of 8 cycles of 15 s each, annealing to 60 °C for 15 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension retained at 72 °C for 5 min—to allow for the formation of a library with a fragment size of 300 bp ± 50 bp. Finally, we used illumina Novaseq™ 6000 (LC Bio Technology CO., Ltd. Hangzhou, China) to perform bipartite sequencing according to the standard operation with sequencing mode PE150. The downstream raw data were formatted as fastq and analyzed using gffcompare (https://github.com/gpertea/gffcompare/) software was used to perform quality control of the downstream raw data, including the removal of junctions, duplicates, and low-quality sequences, with the default parameters. Sequencing data were aligned to the genome (Homo sapiens, GRCh38) using HISAT2 (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2) to obtain the file format bam. The sequencing data were aligned to the genome using StringTie software (https://ccb.jhu.edu/ software/hisat2). Genes or transcripts were assembled and quantified with FPKM (FPKM = total_exon_fragments/mapped_reads(millions) × exon_length(kB)]) using the R package edgeR (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html) to analyze the differential genes between the samples, with a multiplicity of difference > twofold or < 0.5-fold and a p-value < 0.05 defined. Finally, genes were analyzed for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment using DAVID software (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/).

Bioinformatics analysis and single-cell analysis

The cervical cancer transcriptome dataset GSE63514 (containing 24 normal cervical epithelial tissues with 28 cervical cancer tissue samples) was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Data were imported via the `GEOquery` package in R and normalized using the `limma` package to remove systematic biases between different samples. Data preprocessing included removal of low-quality samples and quality control of samples. Differential gene expression was analyzed using the `limma` package with the screening criteria of |log2 fold change|> 1 and p-value < 0.05, and the results were visualized by volcano plots and heat maps. To explore the potential biological functions of the differentially expressed genes, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed on the differentially expressed genes using the clusterProfiler package. The significance threshold for GSEA analysis was set at FDR < 0.05. Subsequently, GO analysis and KEGG pathway analysis, and the results of enrichment analysis were presented by bubble plots. All data analyses and visualizations were performed in the R 4.1.0 environment using major R packages including GEOquery, limma, clusterProfiler, ggplot2, and pheatmap. Potential drug targets were identified by using numerous protocols such as PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm. nih.gov/), PharmMapper (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/), and Swiss Target Prediction (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) databases to accomplish this.

The dataset GSE168652 used for single-cell analysis was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Data preprocessing began with quality control to remove low-quality cells. Specific screening criteria were: the number of genes per cell was between 200 and 6000, and the percentage of mitochondrial genes expressed was less than 20%. Data were normalized using the Seurat package to ensure comparability between cells. Next, high variant genes (top 1500) were selected by FindVariableFeatures function and downscaled using PCA. Based on the PCA results, the cells were clustered using the FindClusters function, and the resolution parameters were adjusted to optimize the clustering results. To further visualize the cell populations, nonlinear dimensionality reduction was performed using RunTSNE and genes with significant differences in expression between different cell populations were screened. In the final step, key genes associated with the most important pathways were selected for molecular docking studies, which were performed using PyMOL 2.4.0 and Autodock software.

Cell cycle determination using flow cytometry

During the logarithmic growth phase of SiHa and HeLa cells, cells were treated with different concentrations of α-hederin or other conditions for 24 h. The control group was only added with an equal amount of DMSO. After treatment, cells were collected by washing with PBS, trypsin digestion, and centrifugation, and were fixed in 70% ice-cold ethanol overnight. After fixation, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with 50 μg/mL RNAase for 30 min at 37 °C to remove RNA. Subsequently, cells were stained with 50 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI) dye, and incubated for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light, to ensure that the dye binds to DNA. After staining was completed, PI fluorescence signals were detected using a flow cytometer (e.g., BD FACSCalibur or BD LSRFortessa), and cell cycle distributions were analyzed using FlowJo software. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. The cell cycle distribution was analyzed using flow cytometry to compare the cell cycle changes between the different treatment and control group and to assess the effect of α-hederin on the cell cycle.

Western blot

During the logarithmic growth phase, cells were treated with different concentrations of α-hederin for 24 h. The control group was only added with an equal amount of solvent. After treatment, cells were washed in PBS, collected, and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (containing 1% protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor), incubated on ice for 15 min, centrifuged to remove cellular debris, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was quantified by BCA method to ensure consistent protein concentration in each sample. Overall, 30 μg of total protein was extracted, which was denatured and treated by adding 5 × SDS loading buffer, and separated by electrophoresis using a 10% SDS-PAGE gel (6% SDS-PAGE gel was used for large proteins). After membrane transfer, PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk powder for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the corresponding primary antibodies diluted at a suitable ratio of 1:1000. Membranes were subsequently incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit or anti-mouse, 1:50,000, proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were detected using ECL luminescence reagent (Epizyme, China) and imaged on a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). The density of protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software, and the results were normalized to the internal reference protein GAPDH. All experiments were repeated at least three times, and the results were statistically analyzed.

Immunofluorescence experiments

During the logarithmic growth phase of SiHa and HeLa cells, the cells were treated with different concentrations of α-hederin and MPA for 24 h. The control group was only added with an equal amount of solvent. A γ-H2AX immunofluorescence staining kit (Beyotime, China) was used and the relevant experiments were completed per manufacturer’s instructions. Observation and photography were performed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). Images were analyzed using ImageJ software to calculate the intensity of γ-H2AX fluorescence staining in each sample. All experiments were repeated at least three times and the data were statistically analyzed.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) experiments

During the logarithmic growth phase of SiHa and HeLa cells, cells were treated with different concentrations of α-hederin and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) for 24 h. The control group was only added with an equal amount of solvent. The treated cells were washed twice with PBS and subjected to ROS detection using 10 μM DHE (dihydroethidium, Invitrogen) or DCFH-DA (2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate, Sigma-Aldrich) dyes, and were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After staining, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound dye and blocked using a blocking solution containing DAPI (Beyotime). ROS fluorescence signals were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). Images were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software to calculate the relative fluorescence intensity of ROS in each sample. All experiments were repeated at least three times and the results were statistically analyzed. Meanwhile, ROS quantification was performed using flow cytometry. DHE fluorescence was detected by excitation (~ 488 nm) and its emission wavelength (~ 530 nm). Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software to record the fluorescence intensity of each cell and to calculate the difference in ROS levels between the treatment and control groups. The relative fluorescence intensity of ROS reflects the amount of intracellular ROS production. All experiments were repeated at least three times and the results were statistically analyzed.

Construction of CHK1 overexpressing stably transfected cell line

To construct a CHK1 overexpressing stably-transformed HeLa cell line, gene transduction was first performed using a lentiviral vector system. We used the CHK1 overexpression plasmid provided by Applied Biological Materials Inc (ABM), which contains the coding sequence of the human CHK1 gene and is driven by the CMV promoter for expression. The CHK1 overexpression plasmid, psPAX2 and pMD2.G plasmids were transfected into HEK-293 T cells at a mass ratio of 2:1:1 according to conventional methods. Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) transfection reagent was used for transfection, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours after transfection, lentiviral particles in the supernatant were collected and concentrated using ultracentrifugation. The concentrated lentiviral particles were used to transfect HeLa cells. At 48 h post-infection, successfully transduced cells were screened using an antibiotic screen (e.g., puromycin-containing medium). A CHK1 overexpressing stably transduced HeLa cell line was finally obtained. The establishment of stably transduced cell lines was confirmed using western blot analysis for CHK1 overexpression.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay(CETSA)

Cervical cancer cells were treated with α-hederin (20 µM) for 24 h, with DMSO used as a control for the Buffer group. After treatment, cells were subjected to thermal denaturation by incubating at temperatures ranging from 40 °C to 65 °C for 3 min. Following heat treatment, cells were lysed, and the stability of CDK1 and CHK1 was assessed by Western blot analysis. The protein band intensities were quantified to evaluate the effect of α-hederin on protein stability.

Animal experiments

During the logarithmic growth period, HeLa cells were harvested and resuspended in saline, and cell suspensions were prepared. Briefly, 6–8-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (Beijing Viton Lever Laboratory Animal Science and Technology Co., Ltd.) weighed approximately 20 g. After one week of acclimatization, nude mice were injected with 1 × 10⁶ HeLa cell suspension subcutaneously into the right axilla. Tumor volume was measured periodically using the formula V = (L × W2)/2 (L represents the maximum diameter of the tumor, W represents the minimum diameter). After tumor establishment, nude mice in the experimental group received daily treatment with different doses of α-hederin at 5 mg/kg, administered intraperitoneally or orally, and the treatment lasted for 21 days. The control nude mice were treated with an equal amount of solvent. During the treatment period, the body weights and tumor volumes of the nude mice were measured periodically. The euthanasia drug we use is sulfothiazol, with a dosage of 150–200 mg/kg (body weight). At the end of the experiment, the nude mice were euthanized and tumor tissues were removed and fixed in 10% formalin solution for subsequent histological analyses, including H&E and immunohistochemical staining, to assess tumor growth, cell proliferation, and apoptosis. All animal experiments complied with international and local ethical requirements and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Youjiang Medical Uiversity of Nationalities(NO.2024062801).

Hematoxylin–Eosin staining and immunohistochemical experiments

After fixation, the tumor tissues, liver, and kidney tissues were routinely dehydrated, made transparent, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 4–5 μm thick sections. Sections were first deparaffinized and hydrated, contrast stained with hematoxylin, followed by staining with eosin, and finally dehydrated and sealed. The stained tissue sections were observed under a light microscope, which was used to assess the morphological changes in the tumors, including cellular morphology, tissue structure, and tumor aggressiveness. For further analysis of molecular markers in tumor tissues, tumor tissue sections were stained for immunohistochemistry. First, antigen repair was performed using 10 mM sodium citrate buffer, followed by closure of endogenous peroxidase activity using 3% hydrogen peroxide solution. Sections were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C in a specific primary antibody (e.g., Ki-67, PCNA, P53) solution. The next day, the sections were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody and visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine as the chromogenic reagent. The stained sections were contrast stained using hematoxylin and finally sealed. Immunohistochemically stained positive cells were quantified using ImageJ.

Metabolomics analysis

Samples stored at −80 °C were thawed on ice and homogenized for 20 s using a grinder (30 Hz). To 20 mg of the ground sample, 400 μL of a methanol:water solution (7:3, V/V) containing the internal standard was added. The mixture was shaken at 1500 rpm for 5 min, left on ice for 15 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (300 μL) was incubated at −20 °C for 30 min, followed by a second centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C. A final 200 μL of the supernatant was collected for LC–MS analysis. Samples were analyzed using two LC–MS methods: one under positive ion mode and the other under negative ion mode. A T3 column (Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3, 1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) was used with solvents A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) following this gradient: 5–20% B (2 min), 20–60% B (3 min), increase to 99% B (1 min), hold for 1.5 min, return to 5% B (0.1 min), hold for 2.4 min. The column temperature was 40 °C, the flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and the injection volume was 4 μL. Data acquisition was performed in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode using Analyst TF 1.7.1 software, with source parameters set to 50 psi for ion source gases, 25 psi for curtain gas, 5000 V (positive) or −4000 V (negative) for ion spray voltage, and a temperature of 550 °C. TOF MS scans covered 50–1000 Da with a 200 ms accumulation time. Product ion scans were performed from 25–1000 Da with a 40 ms accumulation time and a collision energy of ± 30 V. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) were calculated using the R package ComplexHeatmap. Differential metabolites were identified using VIP (VIP >1) and p-values (p-value < 0.05, student’s t-test). For OPLS-DA, VIP values were generated using MetaboAnalystR, and data were log-transformed and mean-centered before OPLS-DA. A permutation test (200 permutations) was performed to avoid overfitting.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Intergroup comparisons were performed using t-test (between two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (among multiple groups). Data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Experimental data were obtained from at least three independent experiments to ensure reproducibility and reliability of results. Results in the data graphs are expressed as mean ± standard error (SEM).

Results

α-hederin effectively inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis of cervical cancer cells

The molecular structure of α-hederin was confirmed using chemical molecular structure analysis (Fig. 1A). We evaluated the effects of α-hederin on the proliferation and apoptosis of cervical cancer cells. The chemical formula of α-hederin was C41H66O12. The proliferation inhibitory effects of α-hederin on SiHa and HeLa cells were evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. α-Hederin significantly inhibited the proliferation of SiHa and HeLa cells at different concentrations (Fig. 1B), suggesting its significant cell proliferation inhibition. To further verify the effect of α-hederin on the proliferation of cervical cancer cells, we used Hoechst and EdU fluorescent staining for proliferation detection. α-Hederin-treated SiHa and HeLa cells achieved significant nuclear chromatin condensation and attenuated fluorescence signal, indicating that it inhibited cell proliferation (Fig. 1C). In both cell lines, the fluorescence area of EDU staining decreased with the increase of drug concentration(Fig. 1D-E), while the fluorescence intensity of Hoechst staining increased gradually(Fig. 1F-G). Immunoblot analysis of the effect of α-hederin on apoptosis of cervical cancer cells revealed a significant increase in the expression levels of apoptosis key proteins cleaved-caspase-3/caspase3, cleaved-PARP/PARP in SiHa and HeLa cells under the presence α-hederin (Fig. 1H). Analysis of the gray values of the immunoblot bands showed that α-hederin treatment significantly up-regulated the expression of these apoptosis-related proteins (mean ± SD (n = 3) (Fig. 1I-L). In particular, the activation of Caspase-3 and Caspase-9 indicated that α-hederin could effectively induce apoptosis in cervical cancer cells. To further validate the occurrence of apoptosis, flow cytometry was used to detect apoptosis in cells after α-hederin treatment (Fig. 1M). Double staining using Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) revealed that α-hederin significantly increased the proportion of apoptosis in SiHa and HeLa cells. Further quantitative analysis revealed that the proportion of apoptotic cells was significantly increased in the α-hederin-treated groups, and the apoptotic effect was enhanced in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1N-O). Finally, assessment of the effect of α-hederin on the clone formation ability of cervical cancer cells demonstrated that clone formation was significantly reduced in SiHa and HeLa cells after α-hederin treatment (Fig. 1P). Quantitative analysis results further confirmed that clone formation was significantly lower in the α-hederin-treated than the control group (Fig. 1Q-R), indicating that it effectively inhibited the self-renewal ability of cervical cancer cells. These results indicated that α-hederin can significantly inhibit the proliferation and promote the apoptosis of cervical cancer cells and its anticancer effect might be realized by up-regulating the expression of apoptosis-related proteins as well as enhancing the process of cell apoptosis.

α-hederin impairs mitochondrial membrane potential and inhibits migration and invasion of cervical cancer cells

Further investigation of the effects of ivy saponin (α-hederin) treatment on the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and migration ability of cervical cancer cells showed that α-hederin significantly altered the mitochondrial function of cervical cancer cells and inhibited cell migration. Flow cytometry analysis showed that α-hederin treatment significantly decreased the mitochondrial membrane potential of cancer cells (Fig. 2A). Flow cytometry histograms of JC-1 staining showed that the distribution of JC-1 aggregates (high MMP) and JC-1 monomers (low MMP) cells in the cells changed significantly after α-hederin treatment. Quantitative analysis showed that the proportion of low MMP cells in the treated groups increased significantly, especially in the high concentration of α-hederin group (Fig. 2B). Further fluorescence microscopy of changes in mitochondrial membrane potential revealed that (α-hederin-treated SiHa and HeLa cells showed a significant decrease in the intensity of JC-1 red fluorescence (Fig. 2D), suggesting that mitochondrial membrane potential was impaired, and cell health status was affected. In the α-hederin-treated group, there was a significant increase in JC-1 monomer (green fluorescence), and the ratio of green fluorescence intensity to red fluorescence intensity increased significantly with the increase of α-hederin concentration (Fig. 2E). These results suggest that α-hederin treatment resulted in impaired mitochondrial function in cervical cancer cells. Next, we evaluated the effect of α-hederin on the migration of cervical cancer cells. Wound healing assays showed that the migration ability of treated SiHa and HeLa cells was significantly diminished (Fig. 2G). Further transwell assay verified the inhibitory effect of α-hederin on cervical cancer cell migration (Fig. 2H). After 24 h, the wound healing area was significantly smaller in the α-hederin-treated group, and the wound healing rate decreased significantly with increased α-hederin concentration (Fig. 2I). This result indicates that α-hederin significantly inhibited the migration of cervical cancer cells. 24 h later, the number of migrated cells was significantly lower in the treated than the control group in transwell, especially in the higher concentration of α-hederin-treated group (Fig. 2K). These findings further indicate that α-hederin could effectively inhibit the migration of cervical cancer cells. Immunoblot analysis evaluating the effect of α-hederin treatment on invasion-related proteins of cervical cancer cells showed that α-hederin significantly down-regulated the expression of invasion-associated proteins E-cadherin and N-cadherin (Fig. 2M). Quantitative analysis further showed that the gray value of E-cadherin protein significantly increased and that of N-cadherin protein significantly decreased after α-hederin treatment, suggesting that it may inhibit the invasion ability of cervical cancer cells by regulating invasion-associated proteins (Fig. 2N-Q).

Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis reveal the key role of cell cycle in inhibition of cervical cancer cell proliferation by α-hederin

To explore the molecular mechanism of α-hederin on cervical cancer cells, we further investigated its potential mechanism of action using transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis in the GEO database. Differential analysis of the transcriptome sequencing results revealed a total of 2185 upregulated genes and 1521 downregulated genes. These differentially expressed genes were further analyzed. GO enrichment analysis of differential genes of drug-treated and control groups of cervical cancer cells (Fig. 3A) showed that the differential genes in the drug-treated group were mainly enriched in biological processes and cellular components, such as cell cycle, DNA division, DNA damage, and DNA repair, suggesting that α-hederin may play an anticancer role by regulating these biological processes. Next, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the differential genes in the drug-treated group were significantly enriched in several cancer-related signaling pathways (Fig. 3B). These included cell cycle, DNA replication, P53 signaling pathway, apoptosis cancer-related transcription, and other pathways, further validating that the effects of α-hederin on cervical cancer cells may be achieved through the modulation of these key cancer pathways. To further analyze the differences between cervical cancer and paracancerous tissues, results of the analysis of the GSE63514 dataset showed that the differential genes in cervical cancer and paracancerous tissues had significant expression differences (Fig. 3C). We identified a total of 8583 differentially expressed genes between cancerous tissues and adjacent non-cancerous tissues. In the volcano plot of differential genes (Fig. 3D), we could observe significantly up-regulated and down-regulated genes in cervical cancer tissues, which might be involved in the occurrence and development of cervical cancer. GSEA analysis of the functional enrichment of differential genes in the GSE63514 dataset showed that differential genes were enriched in several key cancer-related biological processes and signaling pathways, including processes, such as the cell cycle and DNA replication (Fig. 3E). Exploration of co-expression relationship of differential genes, by weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGNCA) identified multiple gene modules associated with cervical cancer (Fig. 3F). The intersection of differentially expressed genes and co-expressed genes (MStan module) revealed a total of 1862 genes. GO enrichment analysis of these intersecting genes indicated that they are primarily involved in key processes such as DNA replication and chromosome segregation (Fig. 3G). KEGG enrichment analysis further confirmed that these genes were also closely related to the cell cycle, DNA replication, and P53 signaling pathway (Fig. 3H). α-Hederin-treated cervical cancer cells with transcriptome sequencing enrichment results and cervical cancer clinical tissues in the GEO database were highly overlapped, suggesting that cervical carcinogenesis may be related to the cell cycle, DNA replication, and P53 signaling pathway, and in the α-hederin-treated cervical cancer cells. Through an analysis of drug target databases, including ChEMBL, SuperPred, and Swiss Target Prediction, we identified a total of 182 potential drug targets for α-hederin. Analysis of the intersection of the disease target genes with the GSE63514 differential genes and Venn diagrams (Fig. 3I) revealed the relationship between the target genes associated with cervical cancer and the differential genes of the drug-treated group. Several target genes overlapped with the differential genes; these target genes may play important roles in the anti-cancer mechanism of α-hederin. Finally, we analyzed the differential expression of 14 key genes in the GSE63514 dataset (Fig. 3J). One differential gene, CDK1, is an important protein in the cell cycle. The cell cycle protein-dependent kinase CDK1 is known for its function as a master regulator of the cell cycle [16] and showed significant differential expression in cervical cancer cells, further supporting that α-hederin exerts its anticancer effects by regulating the expression of cell cycle pathways.

α-hederin inhibits the proliferation of cervical cancer cells by regulating CDK1

To further explore the role of the cell cycle protein CDK1 in cervical cancer and the effect of α-hederin on it, single-cell data analysis and molecular docking analysis were combined to reveal the expression and function of CDK1 in cervical cancer. Based on the GSE63514 dataset, the ROC curve of CDK1 was plotted to assess its predictive value in cervical cancer (Fig. 4A). We observed that the expression of CDK1 in cervical cancer had high diagnostic value. The AUC value of the ROC curve was significantly higher than 0.8, indicating its reliability as a potential biomarker. Single-cell transcriptome data from the GSE168652 dataset were analyzed by t-SNE visualization (Fig. 4B). At the cell type level, we visualized the distribution of CDK1 expression in different cells (Fig. 4C) and observed that CDK1 exhibited high expression in epithelial cells, especially most significantly in tumor cells, suggesting that CDK1 may play a key role in cell proliferation and cycle regulation in cervical cancer.

Visualization evaluation indicated that α-hederin also acted on epithelial cells after α-hederin treatment of cervical cancer cells (Fig. 4D). Immunohistochemical analysis of clinical patients’ tissues showed that CDK1 expression was significantly higher in cervical cancer tissues than paracancerous tissues (Fig. 4E). Quantitative analysis showed that the positively stained area of CDK1 was significantly increased in cervical cancer and its percentage of total tissue area was much higher than that of paracancerous tissues (Fig. 4F), further supporting the potential of CDK1 as a cervical cancer marker. The molecular mechanism of α-hederin and CDK1 was explored by molecular docking analysis. The binding energy of α-hederin was −4.1 kcal/mol, suggesting that α-Hederin may regulate the function of CDK1 at the molecular level through direct interaction, thus affecting the proliferation and cycle progression of cervical cancer cells (Fig. 4G). To further validate this hypothesis, we performed CETSA (Cellular Thermal Shift Assay) analysis. The results showed that (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B), compared to the buffer group, α-hederin treatment significantly increased the thermal stability of CDK1 in cervical cancer cells. In the presence of α-hederin, the stability of CDK1 was enhanced, suggesting that α-hederin may bind to CDK1 and stabilize its structure. Therefore, CDK1 is likely one of the targets of α-hederin, and its regulation of CDK1 stability and function may be a key mechanism through which α-hederin influences cell cycle regulation and inhibits cervical cancer cell proliferation. In summary, high CDK1 expression in cervical cancer is closely related to cell proliferation. α-hederin may play an important role in the treatment of cervical cancer by inhibiting CDK1 expression and function.

α-hederin inhibits cervical cancer cell proliferation by blocking cell cycle G2M phase proteins

To evaluate the effect of α-hederin on the cervical cancer cell cycle, we analyzed cell cycle changes and the expression of related cyclin proteins after α-hederin treatment using flow cytometry and immunoblotting. Cell cycle analysis was performed on SiHa and HeLa cells treated with different concentrations of α-hederin using flow cytometry (Fig. 5A). According to the changes in DNA content, the cell cycle was divided into G0/G1 phase (2n), S phase (DNA synthesis phase), and G2/M phase (4n). We observed that the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase of SiHa and HeLa cells increased significantly with the increase of α-hederin concentration, indicating that it may inhibit cell proliferation by blocking the G2/M phase of the cell cycle. The percentage of cells in each phase of the cell cycle under different treatment conditions was statistically analyzed (Fig. 5B-E). In SiHa cells, α-hederin treatment significantly G2/M phase cell percentage (Fig. 5C). In HeLa cells, similar changes were observed: the proportion of G2/M phase cells was significantly increased by high concentrations of α-hederin treatment (Fig. 5E). However, the proportion of S-phase cells did not change significantly, suggesting that α-hederin may inhibit the proliferation of cervical cancer cells mainly by interfering with the cell cycle in the G2M phase. To further explore the effect of α-hederin on cell cycle proteins, we performed immunoblot analysis (Fig. 5F). The treatment results showed that the ATM expression was significantly down-regulated in SiHa and HeLa cells after α-hederin treatment, while the expression level of P-CHK1/CHK1 was significantly increased. The activation of P-CHK1 further triggers the activation of the P53-P21 pathway while inhibiting the CDC25B/CDC25C-CDK1 signaling pathway. Specifically, P-CHK1 action led to P53 activation, which in turn promoted P21 expression, thereby inhibiting cyclin B2 and CDK1 expression, ultimately blocking the progression of G2/M phase. P-CHK1 activation of inhibited CDC25B and CDC25C activity, This regulatory effect may further contribute to the blockage of the G2/M phase. We further confirmed the specific effects of α-hederin on cyclin expression by statistically analyzing the grayscale values of the immunoblot bands (Fig. 5G).

Inhibition of DNA damage repair attenuates the G2/M phase-blocking effect of α-hederin on cervical cancer cells

To thoroughly investigate the effect of inhibition of DNA damage on the G2/M phase blocking effect of α-hederin in cervical cancer cells, we analyzed DNA damage markers and cell cycle changes in α-hederin-treated cells. Immunoblotting analysis was used to detect the expression of γ-H2AX, a DNA damage marker, after α-hederin treatment of SiHa and HeLa cells (Fig. 6A) and showed that α-hederin significantly increased γ-H2AX expression, suggesting the occurrence of DNA damage. Further statistical analysis of the gray values (Fig. 6B-C) showed that the expression of γ-H2AX was significantly increased in both SiHa and HeLa cells after α-hederin treatment, and this change was concentration-dependent. To further verify the DNA damage, we performed immunofluorescence staining of α-hederin-treated SiHa and HeLa cells to observe the fluorescence intensity of γ-H2AX (Fig. 6D). The immunofluorescence results showed that α-hederin significantly increased the fluorescence intensity of γ-H2AX in the nucleus, further confirming the occurrence of DNA damage. α-hederin significantly increased the γ-H2AX fluorescence intensity in cells under different treatment conditions (Fig. 6F-G), indicating that the expression of DNA damage markers was significantly enhanced by α-hederin treatment. Analysis of cell cycle changes of HeLa cells under different treatment conditions using flow cytometry showed that the proportion of α-hederin-treated HeLa cells in the G2/M phase was significantly increased (Fig. 6E). We treated the cells with MPA (a DNA damage repair inhibitor) in combination with α-hederin, suggesting that MPA may alleviate the G2/M phase block by inhibiting DNA damage. The proportion of G2/M-phase cells after MPA combined with α-hederin treatment was significantly decreased compared to the α-hederin-treated group alone (Fig. 6H), suggesting that α-hederin blockade of G2M phase in cervical cancer cells may be dependent on DNA damage. Further experimental analysis of γ-H2AX and CDK1 protein expression (Fig. 6K) with immunoblotting showed that the induction of γ-H2AX by α-hederin was significantly inhibited by MPA pretreatment, while the expression level of CDK1 was restored to near control level. Gray value statistical analysis further confirmed this change, indicating that inhibition of DNA damage attenuated the effect of α-hederin on G2/M phase block in HeLa cells (Fig. 6L-M). In summary, α-hederin blocks G2/M phase in cervical cancer cells by inducing DNA damage and activating γ-H2AX expression. Inhibition of DNA damage repair could attenuate the G2/M phase-blocking effect induced by α-hederin, suggesting an important role of DNA damage in the mechanism of action of α-hederin.

Overexpression of CHK1 attenuates α-hederin-induced G2/M phase block in cervical cancer cells

Immunoblot analysis (Fig. 7J) was used to explore the role of CHK1 in α-hederin action by overexpressing CHK1 in HeLa cells showed that the expression level of CHK1 was significantly increased in HeLa cells overexpressing CHK1 (Fig. 7K), indicating that overexpression of CHK1 was successfully achieved. Subsequently, cell cycle changes in HeLa cells overexpressing CHK1 under α-hederin treatment were analyzed using flow cytometry (Fig. 7L) which showed that overexpression of CHK1 significantly attenuated the blocking effect of α-hederin on the G2/M phase of HeLa cells. Statistical analyses further demonstrated that overexpression of CHK1 significantly reduced the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase (Fig. 7M-N). Rhe interaction of α-hederin with CHK1 was analyzed by molecular docking (Fig. 7O) which showed that α-hederin could bind to CHK1 with a binding energy of −6.8 kcal/mol possibly regulating its role in the cell cycle through direct interaction with CHK1. To further investigate the effect of α-hederin on CHK1 expression and stability, CETSA analysis was performed (Supplementary Fig. 1C-D). The results showed that after heat treatment, α-hederin significantly enhanced the thermal stability of CHK1 in cervical cancer cells, which validated the molecular docking analysis. α-hederin inhibited G2/M phase progression of cervical cancer cells by increasing ROS levels, and NAC could attenuate this effect. Overexpression of CHK1, however, significantly attenuated the G2/M phase block induced by α-hederin. The important role of CHK1 in α-hederin-mediated cell cycle regulation. In addition, to explore the upregulation and downregulation relationships of key proteins regulating the cell cycle, we conducted a time-course experiment. We investigated the protein changes of γ-H2X, P-CHK1, and CDK1 under the effect of 20 μM α-hederin at 0 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 1E). The results showed that the protein expression level of γ-H2X was significantly upregulated at 6 h (Supplementary Fig. 1F). P-CHK1 showed no significant change at 6 h but increased significantly at 12 h (Supplementary Fig. 1G). CDK1 was downregulated at both 12 h and 24 h, with a more pronounced downregulation at 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 1H). We speculate that α-hederin blocks the G2/M phase mainly by activating CHK1 phosphorylation through DNA damage, thereby inhibiting CDK1 expression.

Antitumor effects of α-hederin in a mouse model of cervical cancer and analysis of its mechanism

To evaluate the inhibitory effect of α-hederin on cervical cancer, we analyzed in vivo experiments using a mouse tumor transplantation model. Figure 8A shows the tumor transplantation images of mice, which visually presented the tumor growth. No significant difference in body weight was observed between mice in the α-hederin-treated and control groups (Fig. 8B), suggesting that α-hederin did not significantly affect the overall health status of mice. Further tumor volume measurements (Fig. 8C) showed that α-hederin significantly inhibited tumor growth. Compared with the control group, the tumor volume of mice in the α-hederin-treated group was significantly reduced, suggesting that α-hederin had an inhibitory effect on cervical cancer tumor growth. Consistent with this, α-hederin significantly reduced tumor weight (Fig. 8D), further supporting the inhibitory effect of α-hederin on tumor growth. HE staining of liver and kidney tumor tissue sections (Fig. 8E) showed that number of tumor cells tumor tissues was reduced in the α-hederin-treated group per microscopy, light staining of cytoplasm and nuclei, and reduced number of nuclei with cell disintegration or loss of structure. Tumor cells in the control group were tightly arranged, with abundant cytoplasm and nuclei. The HE staining of the liver and kidney tissue sections after α-hederin treatment showed no significant changes, indicating that α-hederin did not cause significant damage to the liver and kidney functions of mice and did not cause evident histopathological changes, supporting its safety in the treatment of cervical cancer. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that the tumor tissues in the α-hederin treatment group had significantly fewer PCNA, Ki67 and P53-positive regions, suggesting that α-hederin may play an anti-tumor role in vivo by inhibiting cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis. The results of statistical analysis (Fig. 8F-H) further showed that α-hederin significantly reduced PCNA, Ki67, and P53-positive areas in tumor tissues.

The bar graph (Fig. 8J) shows the specific changes in differential metabolites, with significantly variations in levels of certain metabolites in the tumor tissues of the α-hederin treatment group. Finally, Fig. 8K shows a heatmap of correlation analysis between differential metabolites and key proteins of the cell cycle, which revealed significant correlations between α-hederin-induced metabolic changes and CHK1, ATR, CDC25B, CDC25C, and P53, suggesting that cell-cycle regulation may play an important role in the mechanism of tumor growth inhibition by α-hederin. Metabolic enrichment analysis indicated that α-hederin treatment may have inhibited tumor cell proliferation and growth by affecting multiple metabolic pathways (Fig. 8L). In summary, α-hederin significantly inhibited tumor growth in a mouse model of cervical cancer, and its mechanism of action may be related to cell proliferation inhibition, and cell cycle regulation.

Discussion

We observed that α-hederin significantly inhibits the value-added of cervical cancer cells and promoted their apoptosis. CCK-8 assay assessment of cell proliferation showed that α-hederin had a significant concentration-dependent inhibitory effect on SiHa and HeLa cells, which was further verified by cell cycle and proliferation assays by Hoechst and EdU fluorescent staining. Caspase-3 is a widely expressed conserved protein known for its role in apoptosis through protein hydrolysis activation. As an important regulator of apoptosis, activated caspase-3 cleaves multiple downstream substrates and drives the execution of cell death. Increasing evidence indicates that caspase-3 regulates the survival, proliferation and differentiation of normal and malignant cells, in addition to playing a key role in cell death [17]. PARP-1, a sensor of DNA damage, recognizes damage and promotes repair by recruiting repair mechanisms or inducing cell death by triggering DNA fragmentation [18]. PARP shearing is an apoptotic important hallmark and is usually associated with caspase-3 activation. We found that α-hederin significantly upregulates the expression of apoptosis-related proteins such as cleaved-caspase-3 and cleaved-PARP, suggesting that α-hederin promotes cervical cancer cell death by activating the apoptotic pathway, and may act through the endogenous apoptotic pathway [17]. α-Hederin significantly decreased the expression of mitochondria in SiHa and HeLa cells’ mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), a change suggesting that α-hederin may interfere with cellular energy metabolism and survival by disrupting mitochondrial function. The proportion of low-MMP cells was significantly increased, especially in the higher concentration of α-hederin treatment, suggesting that the cells may have entered a compromised state, further supporting this point. Moreover, α-hederin significantly inhibited the clone formation ability of cervical cancer cells. Clone formation assay revealed that α-hederin could effectively reduce the self-renewal ability of cervical cancer cells, which implies that α-hederin inhibits cell proliferation and breaks down the self-maintenance mechanism of cancer cells, thus, effectively controlling the development of cancer.

The results of cell migration experiments showed that α-hederin treatment significantly inhibited the migration ability of cervical cancer cells. Wound healing experiments and Transwell migration experiments showed that α-hederin could effectively reduce the speed of cell migration and number of migrating cells. Combined with its impairment of mitochondrial function, α-hederin may inhibit the motility of cancer cells by inducing energy deficiency and increasing cellular stress response, thus, weakening the invasiveness of cancer cells. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is critical in tumorigenesis, where the EMT program enhances metastasis, chemoresistance, and tumor stemness, and the hallmark of EMT is the up-regulation of N-calmodulin followed by the down-regulation of E-calmodulin [19]. α-Hederin is also effective in inhibiting EMT through inhibition of EMT, further suggesting that it may regulate intercellular adhesion and cell migration mechanisms to inhibit the invasion of cervical cancer cells. This finding suggests that α-hederin not only interferes with the biological properties of cervical cancer cells by affecting mitochondrial function and cell migration, but may also inhibit tumor invasiveness and metastatic ability by regulating cell adhesion molecules.

We further examined the mechanism by which α-hederin inhibits the cervical cancer cells by transcriptome sequencing of cervical cancer cells, and found that α-hederin inhibited the proliferation of cervical cancer cells by regulating several key cancer-associated [1] pathways such as cell cycle, DNA replication, and the P53 signaling pathway. GSEA analysis further confirmed the cell cycle and DNA replication and other process enrichment, supporting the centrality of the cell cycle in the role of α-hederin. Analysis of cervical cancer tissues and paracancerous tissues in the GEO database revealed that the mechanism of cervical cancer development remains focused on the signaling pathways of cell cycle, DNA replication, and P53 signaling, consistent with the mechanism of cervical cancer inhibition by α-hederin that we sequenced. Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is a major causes of cervical cancer development, with the viral E6 and E7 oncoproteins as key virulence factor [20]. HPV E7 oncoprotein degrades the pRB oncoprotein through cullin 2 (CUL2)-mediated polyubiquitination, which in turn releases the E2F transcription factor that drives the cell cycle into S phase. E7 also promotes cell cycle progression into S phase by up-regulating the cyclineA/CDK2/p107 complex, which regulates G2/M phase and promotes HPV replication. E7 binds to the E3 ubiquitin ligase RN1698, which promotes viral replication, alters the cellular response to DNA damage, and increases genomic instability [21]. These results suggest that alterations in the cell cycle are an important pathogenic mechanism of cervical cancer. The significant differential expression of the CDK1 gene, a key regulator of the cell cycle, in cervical cancer cells suggests that α-hederin may play an anticancer role by regulating CDK1 and related pathways. Cell cycle protein-dependent kinases (CDKs) are serine/threonine kinases that form complexes with cell cycle proteins, a process that is essential for the full activation of their kinase activity [22]. CDK1 is the only mammalian CDK that is essential for cell cycle progression [23]. CDK1 is a well-defined cell cycle checkpoint protein involved in the regulation of a range of basic cellular functions in cell proliferation and growth and a range of essential cellular functions [24]. ROC curve analysis of the GSE63514 dataset demonstrated that CDK expression in cervical cancer has high diagnostic value, suggesting its potential in cervical cancer detection and prognosis. CDK1 is mainly distributed in cervical epithelial cells. Single-cell analysis of drug targets showed that the targets of α-hederin were also mainly located in epithelial cells. We hypothesize that α-hederin regulates CDK1 by acting on epithelial cells, which in turn affects the cell cycle and inhibits the development of cervical cancer.

Our results showed that α-hederin could effectively block the G2/M phase of cervical cancer cells. In the late G2 phase of the cell cycle, elevated levels of cyclin B can form a stable CDK1/cyclin B complex, which triggers cells to enter the M phase of mitosis [25]. Cell arrest in G2/M phase is accompanied by decreased levels of CDK1 and cyclin B, suggesting that the tumor growth inhibition mechanism is mediated by decreased CDK1/cyclin B activity [25]. Lack of CDK1 activity contributes to the initiation of the apoptotic pathway in the context of G2/M phase cell cycle arrest, whereas during mitotic arrest, CDK1 activity is required for the induction of apoptosis [26, 27]. In G2 phase, CDK1 inhibition leads to a decrease in anti-apoptotic proteins and an increase in pro-apoptotic proteins, stimulating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. Lack of CDK1 activity induces cell cycle arrest in G2/M phase, which also leads to apoptosis. In M phase, CDK1 activity inactivates anti-apoptotic proteins by phosphorylating them in small amounts [26, 27].

We found that α-hederin may be used by ATM-CHK1-CDC25B/CDC25C and ATM-P53-P21 to regulate CDK1/cycline B activity by phosphorylating downstream substrates, and ATM and ATR amplify signaling responses by phosphorylating downstream substrates, and checkpoint kinase 1 (CHEK1) and checkpoint kinase 2 (Checkpoint kinase 1 (CHEK1) and checkpoint kinase 2 (CHEK2) are phosphorylated and activated by ATR and ATM, which in turn phosphorylate other substrates. ATR-mediated activation of CHEK1 inhibits cell cycle protein-dependent kinases (CDKs), leading to cell cycle arrest [28]. Cucurbitacin B treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma cells enhanced phosphorylated ATM, increased p-CHK1 levels, and inhibited CDC25C expression mediating cell cycle arrest in G2M phase [29]. We found that ATM expression decreased with increasing α-hederin concentration, suggesting that further phosphorylation of this protein, activation of p-CHK1, and inhibition of CDC25B with CDC25C, CDC25B, and CDC25C act at the G2/M checkpoint (Fig. 9). CDC25B may act before CDC25C, because centrosomal cell cycle protein B/CDK1 activation occurs before nuclear accumulation of cell cycle protein B/CDK1 [30], which in turn further regulates the cell cycle. This arresting effect occurs through the regulation of CDC25 phosphatase, which in turn controls mitotic CDK activity [31].

Diagram depicting the mechanism of α-hederin in blocking the G2M phase in cervical cancer cells

In addition to this mechanism, we focused on the P53 signaling pathway in transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis. Tumor protein P53 (TP53) is the most commonly altered gene in cancer. Loss of its activity is thought to be a failure of the barrier that enables tumor transformation. Among different functions, the TP53 signaling pathway is closely related to cell cycle control, and cell cycle arrest is the main biological outcome observed after P53 activation, which prevents the accumulation of damaged DNA as well as genomic instability [32]. Our results indicated that α-hederin-treated cervical cancer cells activated the expression of P53 and P21. One downstream role of the P53 protein is to inhibit cell cycle protein-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) through P21 activation. This role is lost when the TP53 gene is mutated and the mutation fails to stimulate P21 so that CDK1 is no longer inhibited [25].

The P53 tumor suppressor gene and the gene product ATM (“ataxia-telangiectasia mutated”) mediate cell cycle perturbations in response to DNA damage and play a role in cell death, genetic stability, and cancer susceptibility [33]. ATM protein kinase is a key DNA double-strand break (DSB) response regulator, playing an important role in modulating the P53 pathway. It finely regulates the role of P53 in the DNA damage response by directly phosphorylating P53 and regulating its other post-translational modifications. This mechanism ensures cellular sensitivity and adaptation to DNA damage and maintains genome stability.

We further investigated the role of DNA damage in α-hederin-treated cervical cancer cells using immunoblotting and immunofluorescence staining analysis and found that γ-H2AX expression was significantly increased in SiHa and HeLa cells after α-hederin treatment, a phenomenon that showed an upward trend in concentration-dependence, which further confirmed the occurrence of DNA damage. The presence of MPA, an inhibitor of DNA damage repair, attenuated γ-H2AX expression. γ-H2AX expression γ-H2AX serves as an early marker of DNA DSB damage [34] and is commonly used to assess the extent of intracellular DNA damage. Thus, α-hederin induced significant DNA damage in cervical cancer cells by increasing γ-H2AX expression, suggesting that it induced significant DNA damage in cervical cancer cells. Flow cytometry analysis showed that the proportion of α-hederin-treated HeLa cells in G2/M phase increased significantly, suggesting that DNA damage may lead to G2/M phase block by activating cell cycle checkpoints. We found that MPA attenuated the G2/M phase block effect by co-treating cells with MPA and α-hederin. Inhibition of DNA damage repair attenuated the G2/M blocking effect of α-hederin, suggesting that DNA damage plays an important role in its mechanism of action. The DNA damage-responsive signaling pathway orchestrated by ATM and ATR kinases is a central regulator of this network in response to DNA damage, and both ATM and ATR are activated by DNA damage and DNA replication stress [35], which in turn regulate ROS. ROS are a class of highly reactive chemicals that are usually by-products of oxygen consumption and cellular metabolism. Recently, ROS have attracted attention as extracellular and intracellular signaling mediators. Increasing evidence suggests that reactive oxygen species have dual roles in tumors, involving tumorigenesis, transformation, progression, angiogenesis and metastasis [36]. We explored the mechanism by which α-hederin regulates G2/M phase progression in cervical cancer cells by increasing ROS levels. α-Hederin treatment significantly increased ROS levels in SiHa and HeLa cells, suggesting that α-hederin may activate cell cycle-related signaling pathways by inducing oxidative stress. We further found that NAC (a ROS inhibitor) pretreatment significantly attenuated the G2/M phase blocking effect induced by α-hederin. Statistical analysis also showed that the proportion of α-hederin-induced G2/M-phase cells was significantly reduced by NAC treatment, suggesting that ROS play a key role in α-hederin-induced G2/M-phase block. DNA damage occurs through activation of the ATM/ATR signaling pathway, a process that is largely dependent on the increase of intracellular ROS levels [37]. Further, bortezomib significantly contributes to the blockade of G2/M phase through ROS-induced ATM phosphorylation [38]. These findings reveal the important role of ROS in DNA damage response and cell cycle regulation.

CHK1 is involved in the regulation of downstream effectors that determine cell fate and promote cell death [37]. CHK1 activates checkpoints, slows cell cycle progression, and allows time for DNA repair. CHK1 induces cell cycle arrest mainly by promoting the degradation or segregation of CDC25 phosphatase [39]. We observed that cells overexpressing CHK1 showed a significant decrease in the proportion of G2/M phase cells after α-hederin treatment, suggesting that CHK1 plays an important role in α-hederin-induced G2/M phase arrest. Further verifying the interaction of α-hederin with CHK1, molecular docking analysis revealed that α-hederin was able to bind to CHK1 with a binding energy of −6.8 kJ/mol, which suggests that α-hederin may regulate its function in the cell cycle through direct interaction with CHK1. It may be also regulated by upstream molecules; hence, it is undeniable that CHK1 plays a crucial role in α-hederin-induced cell cycle block. CDC25C is phosphorylated by CHK1, which forms a docking site for 14–3-3 proteins and facilitates the nuclear export of CDC25C, which inhibits the activation of the nuclear cell cycle protein B/CDK1 and prevents mitotic entry [39] there by blocking the G2M phase.

Metabolomics analysis revealed significant changes in tumor metabolic profiles induced by α-hederin treatment. One significantly upregulated metabolite was 4-hydroxytamoxifen, a metabolite of tamoxifen, a selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) commonly used in the treatment of estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer [40]. Estradiol stimulates the growth of human cervical squamous carcinoma cells, and the estradiol-stimulated growth can be inhibited by 4-OH tarnoxifen [41], which may be one mechanism by which α-hederin inhibits the growth of cervical carcinoma in vivo, but further studies are needed for further details.

In summary, this study revealed that α-hederin can significantly inhibit the growth of cervical cancer and induce apoptosis via DNA-damage-dependent cell cycle arrest at G2/M phase. Therefore, α-hederin, as a potential herbal monomer, may be an effective DNA damage inducer and is expected to be a potential drug candidate for the treatment of cervical cancer after future clinical trials. However, its toxicology and specific mechanism need further study. A limitation of this study is that we did not conduct a deeper investigation into the key targets of α-hederin in inhibiting cervical cancer and its molecular mechanisms. This will be a focus of our next phase of research.
