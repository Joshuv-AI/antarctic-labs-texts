Application of Mini-CEX combined with student-standardized patients in rehabilitation medicine residents’ standardized training
Authors: Yaqin Zeng, Ruidong Cheng, Zhouli Shao, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe mini clinical evaluation exercise (Mini-CEX) is widely recognized as an effective measure for standardized training for resident physicians in European and American countries. However, in China, use of the Mini-CEX evaluation is limited to clinical patients. Previous studies have not assessed the combined efficacy of the Mini-CEX with student-standardized patients (SSPs) in rehabilitation medicine education. Therefore, this study explored whether combining Mini-CEX and SSP can improve resident physicians’ clinical and communication skills.MethodsThe sample comprised students who received standardized training for resident physicians from January 2022 to December 2023. Before the training began, the observation and control groups received Mini-CEX evaluation. During the training period, the observation group received Mini-CEX combined with SSP evaluation every 2 weeks, while the control group was not evaluated. Before leaving the department after the eighth week, the resident physicians’ learning outcomes were uniformly evaluated, including theoretical knowledge, Mini-CEX, clinical thinking, and specialized skills. A questionnaire was used to collect students’ evaluations of teaching.ResultsAfter 8 weeks, the Mini-CEX combined with SSP group was superior to the control group in terms of consultation skills, communication skills, clinical judgment, humanistic concern, and overall clinical competency (p < 0.05). Compared with traditional Mini-CEX, resident physicians made greater progress in their clinical comprehensive abilities, especially in clinical thinking, patient reception and case writing (p < 0.05). The survey results indicated that resident physicians were highly satisfied with the Mini-CEX combined with SSP.ConclusionMini-CEX combined with SSP is an effective and reliable evaluation method for rehabilitation resident physician training, with advantages for improving students’ clinical abilities.

Background

Rehabilitation medicine is an important first-level discipline in various clinical branches [1]. Such medicine is designed to treat all patients with various functional impairments [2]. Training in rehabilitation medicine is an important component of standardized training for resident physicians, especially with respect to doctor-patient communication, given that most rehabilitation patients experience significant life difficulties. Owing to the diverse target audience for rehabilitation medicine, patients and their families actively participate and have high expectations of functional benefits. These characteristics further test clinical rehabilitation physicians’ doctor-patient communication abilities.

The development of rehabilitation medicine as a discipline has been relatively recent. Similar to most other medical majors, resident physicians have relatively weak communication awareness and lack effective guidance [3]. This highlights the need to explore new teaching methods to improve teaching effectiveness and ensure that rehabilitation students acquire the necessary skills such as clinical thinking ability, doctor-patient communication, and humanistic care. Given the lack of existing research on the current status of and methods to improve doctor-patient communication skills during rehabilitation medicine residential training in China, this study actively explores new teaching models for doctor-patient communication skills training that align with the characteristics of rehabilitation medicine. The Mini Clinical Evaluation Exercise (Mini-CEX) can improve resident physicians’ medical skills and humanistic communication abilities through timely feedback after testing [4]. As a dual tool for teaching and assessment, it has become a fundamental component of competence-based medical education in North America and Europe, where it is formally endorsed by bodies such as the American Board of Internal Medicine and widely used for direct observation and structured feedback [5]. In contrast, Mini-CEX implementation in China remains localized and often combined with other tools such as Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS) to improve training outcomes in community settings, as demonstrated in a Wuhan study showing significant score improvements among general practitioners after such an intervention [6]. Similarly, the Mini-CEX has proven feasible for both internal medicine and surgical residency assessment in Chinese tertiary hospitals [7, 8]; however, its systematic integration into specialty-specific curricula remains underdeveloped, particularly in rehabilitation medicine.

The application of student standardized patients (SSPs) in teaching centers on training functional communication, empathy, and understanding of patients’ life experiences, especially for patients with disabilities [9]. SSPs not only realistically simulate the clinical symptoms of patients, but also simulate the emotional and psychosocial dimensions that are crucial for rehabilitation [10]. Research has shown that a competency based SSP seminar model significantly improved the case analysis scores of rehabilitation students, demonstrating its educational value [11]. This is consistent with global evidence that SSPs increase disability awareness [12]. The incorporation of SSP as an innovative teaching strategy has recently received considerable attention and promotion [13].

Given the characteristics of rehabilitation medicine and the lack of reports on Mini-CEX combined with SSP in China, this study used Mini-CEX combined with SSP to evaluate medical history collection and physical examination by residents in rehabilitation, assess their clinical abilities, and provide timely evaluation and feedback to strengthen their communication skills.

Methods

Participants

The sample comprised 46 resident physicians who received standardized training in the Rehabilitation Medicine Department of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital from January 2022 to December 2023. A researcher who had no contact with the participants used a random number table generated by SPSS software to randomly assign participants to either the observation or control group, with 23 patients in each group. The observation group comprised nine males and 14 females aged 21–32 years (25.22 ± 2.33 years). The control group comprised 11 males and 12 females aged 21–29 years (24.04 ± 2.01 years). Participants were eligible for inclusion if they had completed at least 1 year of clinical training and provided informed consent. Residents with incomplete records, prior exposure to SSP training, or prolonged absences were ineligible to participate. Comparison of the general information of the two groups of resident physicians indicated no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05).

Procedure

Before the residency physician training, the observation and control groups were evaluated using the Mini-CEX. Over the subsequent 8−week training period, clinical instructors evaluated the observation group three times (every 2 weeks [14]) using Mini−CEX combined with SSPs. To ensure content validity and balanced coverage across core domains of neurological rehabilitation, each assessment employed unique SSP cases drawn from a predefined blueprint encompassing common conditions such as stroke, traumatic brain injury, and peripheral nerve disorders; no resident encountered the same case more than once.The control group received the same core teaching activities as the observation group but did not receive any Mini−CEX combined with SSP assessments; instead, they continued with routine clinical rotation and standardized formative feedback, including only two Mini−CEX evaluations, one at baseline and one at the end of the training period. Following the 8-week intervention, both groups were evaluated using the Mini-CEX and comprehensive clinical ability assessments.

Mini-CEX evaluation

The evaluation was conducted predominantly in terms of seven aspects: consultation skills, physical examination, humanistic concern, clinical judgment, communication skills, organizational effectiveness, and overall clinical competency. It conformed to the requirements of job competence, was suitable for evaluating the actual performance of resident physicians in clinical work, and could improve the effectiveness of clinical teaching [12]. The evaluation form consisted of a nine-point scale divided into three levels. Scores 1–3 were considered to indicate that the resident was unqualified, 4–6 were considered to indicate that they were qualified, and 7–9 indicated excellent performance. The overall duration of each evaluation was 15–20 min, followed by 5–10 min of feedback from the teacher.

SSP training

Four interns from the Rehabilitation Medicine Department of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital were selected as SSPs based on predefined criteria, including strong clinical communication skills, previous performance or role-playing experience, and satisfactory performance in preliminary simulated screening. Each SSP is allocated to present five or six cases of neurological rehabilitation (such as post-stroke hemiplegia, craniocerebral trauma, radial nerve injury, or diabetes peripheral neuropathy). Each SSP received a 6-h structured training within one week, which was led by two certified clinical instructors. The training included case reviews, script familiarization, and standardized patient presentations. To ensure the consistency and reliability of the simulation, all SSPs participated in a pilot test, followed by feedback meetings and performance evaluations using a standardized checklist (rater protocol kappa = 0.85). Only SSPs with a fidelity of ≥ 90% to the case script are approved for use in this study.

Assessment indicators

Mini-CEX evaluation

Mini-CEX evaluations were conducted in real clinical settings using newly admitted patients with common rehabilitation conditions such as stroke, traumatic brain injury, and peripheral nerve injury, following receipt of informed consent from patients and families. Trained assessors are standardized through a unified protocol, all assessors received calibration training using benchmark videos, achieving inter rater ICC > 0.80. Different assessors are required to rate trainees before and after training, rather than being observed by a single assessor. To ensure objectivity and minimize bias, the principle of “separation of teaching and assessment” was strictly applied: assessors were not involved in the trainees’ routine supervision. Mini-CEX was performed during actual outpatient or inpatient encounters to evaluate core competencies including history-taking, physical examination, professionalism, clinical reasoning, communication, organization, and counseling [15]. The duration of each evaluation was approximately 15–20 min, followed immediately by structured feedback lasting 5–10 min, ensuring consistency in timing, setting, and scoring across both groups [16].

Comprehensive clinical ability assessment

The comprehensive clinical ability assessment of the resident training physician groups comprised two parts: basic theory assessment and clinical skill operation assessment. The theoretical written exam (maximum 100 points) was developed by a panel of three senior rehabilitation physicians using neurorehabilitation-focused items drawn from the hospital’s accredited question bank. All questions passed content validation based on national residency training standards and were pilot-tested for clarity and difficulty. The clinical skills assessment (total 100 points) was adapted from the official Zhejiang Province Clinical Practice Skills Graduation Examination blueprint, modified to reflect core competencies in rehabilitation medicine. It included clinical thinking ability, patient reception, case writing, and specialized skills operation modules, each of which was scored out of 25 points to ensure balanced emphasis on cognitive and technical domains. A six-member base assessment team conducted the OSCE-style stations, with each station independently scored by two trained instructors, each accounting for 50% of the total score.

Questionnaire

A questionnaire survey was conducted before the residents left the department [6], which primarily included recognition of self-improvement (clinical thinking ability, practical operation skills, patient communication skills), stimulation of learning enthusiasm, perception of increased burden, interaction with teachers, and overall satisfaction level. Each item was scored on a three-point scale, with categories of improvement or satisfaction (3 points), average (2 points), and no improvement or dissatisfaction (1 point).

Data analysis

SPSS 26.0 statistical software was used for the statistical analyses. Demo graphic characteristics and baseline data were compared among groups using the χ2 test and independent sample t-test. Before and after treatment, the paired sample t-test was used for intragroup comparison for normally distributed data, and Wilcoxon signed rank test was conducted as a nonparametric equivalent test. For between-group comparisons, an independent-samples t-test was used for normally distributed outcomes, while the Mann–Whitney U test served as the nonparametric alternative. All data were summarized quantitatively as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for parametric tests and Pearson’s r (calculated as r = Z/√N) for nonparametric tests to facilitate interpretation of practical significance. For each analysis, p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Discussion

Standardized training for resident physicians is crucial to improving their diagnostic and treatment skills and is a key aspect of cultivating medical professionals [17, 18]. Bibliometric analysis [19] of studies of doctor-patient communication in domestic residency training have indicated that the current standardized training for resident physicians on humanistic care and doctor-patient communication comprises a single training method, lacks evaluation tools, and has unsatisfactory training effects. Therefore, doctor-patient communication education should be improved, and doctor-patient communication skills should be cultivated during residency training [20].

The Mini-CEX is an evaluation tool developed and recommended by the American Board of Internal Medicine to assess students’ physical examination skills, clinical thinking, communication skills, humanistic care, and other aspects. The Mini-CEX focuses on diagnostic and management abilities in clinical work without being limited by time or patients. Timely feedback is included as part of the evaluation, making it suitable for improving clinical abilities [21]. The Mini-CEX has been widely applied and achieved positive outcomes both domestically and internationally [22–24].

The Mini-CEX evaluation results showed that after 8 weeks of training, both the observation and control groups demonstrated significant improvements across most domains, particularly in clinical thinking. This finding aligns with recent work by Xu et al. [25], further supporting the role of the Mini-CEX in promoting higher-order clinical reasoning across health professions. Notably, the observation group outperformed the control group in clinical thinking and communication skills. Unlike traditional assessments that focus primarily on medical knowledge from a physician’s perspective, the embedded SSP plays three roles in clinical teaching, namely as patient, evaluator, and teaching guide [26]. The Mini-CEX evaluation combined with SSP can guide and provide feedback on student’s performance from the patient’s perspective, helping students to learn to communicate with patients. This assessment–feedback cycle not only helps learners recognize their own deficiencies but also actively guides them toward concrete improvement strategies, thereby significantly enhancing both teaching and learning motivation [27].

However, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups in physical examination and organizational efficiency after training. This may be explained by several factors. First, both groups received identical hands-on clinical training in core rehabilitation techniques, leading to comparable technical skill acquisition. Second, physical examinations in rehabilitation medicine often follow highly standardized protocols, such as muscle strength grading and range-of-motion measurement, leaving little scope for intervention-specific variation. Third, organizational efficiency, such as time management and workflow prioritization, are heavily shaped by ward-level systems, team structures, and institutional routines rather than individual educational interventions, making it less responsive to short-term pedagogical modifications. These findings suggest that although Mini-CEX combined with SSP models performs well in cultivating clinical thinking and humanistic care, its impact on program or system-dependent domains may require longer exposure times, repeated cycles, or complementary strategies.

The results of the comprehensive clinical ability assessment indicated that the combined application of Mini-CEX and SSP in rehabilitation residential training resulted in better clinical thinking and decision-making, as well as patient reception, among the observation group’s resident physicians compared with the control group. This reflects the advantages of the Mini-CEX combined with the SSP evaluation system in improving students’ communication skills and clinical thinking abilities. This novel teaching model assists in enhancing the comprehensive clinical ability of rehabilitation resident physicians.

However, there were no significant differences in theoretical knowledge or professional technical skills. This may be because both groups received the same core teaching guidance and practical clinical rotation, with theoretical knowledge typically derived from lectures, textbooks, and departmental teaching courses that remained unchanged between groups. Rehabilitation skills are usually acquired through skill training in a real clinical environment, and both groups had the same experience in this respect. In contrast, Mini-CEX combined with SSP specifically targets clinical thinking and humanistic care, such as how residents can collect information, diagnose, and communicate better with patients, which do not receive a high level of focus in routine clinical teaching. Therefore, the lack of differences in outcomes based on knowledge and skills does not diminish the value of the intervention, but rather highlights its impact in specific domains, consistent with the principle that workplace-based formative assessments, such as the Mini-CEX, are most effective for evaluating and improving behavior during real clinical and patient interactions.

Additionally, this study evaluated the learning outcomes and satisfaction with the teaching of the two student groups. The results indicated that the Mini-CEX combined with SSP group had higher self-rated learning outcomes and satisfaction with teaching than the control group.

Limitations and suggestions

Although this study has value for clinical teaching, it had several limitations. First, this was a single-center, small-sample study, which potentially limited the reliability of the results and the ability to extrapolate the outcomes. Second, the evaluation was conducted over only 2 months, which represents a relatively short observation period. Third, while the study demonstrated improvements in clinical thinking and communication skills, it did not evaluate nonclinical skills beneficial for comprehensive resident training in rehabilitation medicine, such as teamwork, professional ethics and teaching ability. Finally, although SSPs are valuable when simulating clinical scenarios, they may not fully replicate the complexity and unpredictability of real patient interactions [28], particularly in rehabilitation medicine. Future research should include larger and more diverse samples to improve universality, and investigate the retention of skills and clinical comprehensive abilities 6–12 months after training. This would enhance the assessment of Mini CEX combined with SSP evaluation in rehabilitation and resident education. Further, studies could explore other non-clinical skills that are beneficial for comprehensive resident training in rehabilitation medicine. In addition, virtual reality simulations or mixed models of real and simulated patients could be explored to overcome the complexity and unpredictability of real patient interactions and improve the teaching quality of doctor-patient communication courses.

Conclusions

This study found that, compared with traditional Mini-CEX evaluation, novel integration of Mini-CEX with SSPs significantly enhanced the effectiveness of standardized training for rehabilitation medicine residents, particularly concerning clinical thinking and communication abilities, which are critical yet challenging competencies in this specialty. Additionally, this novel evaluation method was recognized as beneficial by resident trainees. Given its demonstrated impact and feasibility, the Mini-CEX combined with SSP model has the potential for wider implementation in residency training programs and warrants further refinement and expansion in clinical education settings.
