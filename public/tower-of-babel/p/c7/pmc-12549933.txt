Surface electromyographic assessment of lower eyelid muscle function in ptosis: investigating the role of the orbicularis oculi in scleral show
Authors: Larysa Krajewska-Węglewicz, Małgorzata Dorobek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this study is to investigate periocular muscle interactions in patients with involutional ptosis and their relationship to inferior scleral show, using a novel electromyographic biomarker of levator–orbicularis coordination before and after ptosis surgery. In this prospective comparative study, 28 patients with bilateral involutional ptosis and 26 controls underwent surface electromyography (sEMG) of the levator palpebrae superioris and orbicularis oculi muscle. Root mean square amplitudes were recorded during maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) and normalized to calculate the OOM/LPS %MVC ratio. Measurements were obtained preoperatively and at 2 weeks and 6 months postoperatively. Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way and repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Preoperative ptosis patients had significantly lower OOM/LPS ratios compared to controls: difference in % MVC ratio=-2.10 (p < 0.0001). Postoperative measurements at both 2 weeks and 6 months showed normalization of this ratio, with no significant difference compared to controls (%MVC = 0.037; %MVC = 0.018, respectively). This study introduces a new functional metric to assess eyelid muscle dynamics. Involutional ptosis is associated with an imbalance between levator and orbicularis activity. Surgical correction restores this balance. Ptosis repair may influence both upper and lower eyelid position and should be regarded as a procedure that restores neuromuscular balance within the periorbital region.Keywords: Orbicularis oculi, Levator palpebrae superioris, Ptosis, Surface electromyographySubject terms: Anatomy, Diseases, Medical research, Neuroscience

Introduction

Scleral show refers to the abnormal visibility of the sclera, typically inferior to the corneal limbus, due to downward displacement or malposition of the lower eyelid. While minimal inferior scleral visibility can be a normal anatomical variant, excessive scleral show is generally considered pathological1.

It may result from multiple etiologies, including age-related laxity, facial nerve palsy, thyroid eye disease, trauma, or iatrogenic changes following blepharoplasty2. Clinically, increased scleral show is associated not only with aesthetic concerns but also with ocular surface exposure, dryness, and discomfort3.

Eyelid position is maintained by a delicate balance between agonist and antagonist muscle groups. In the upper eyelid, elevation is controlled by the levator palpebrae superioris (LPS) and Müller’s muscle, while the orbicularis oculi muscle (OOM) opposes this action, contributing to tone, blink dynamics, and closure4. A similar balance exists in the lower eyelid, where the inferior retractors (including the capsulopalpebral fascia and inferior tarsal muscle) act in coordination with the lower segment of the OOM5. Disruption of this neuromuscular equilibrium—whether from primary levator dysfunction, compensatory mechanisms, or surgical alteration—can lead to changes in eyelid position, including inferior scleral show and lower eyelid retraction (LER).

Inferior scleral show has been observed in patients with blepharoptosis6, and has been attributed to mechanical weakening of the lower eyelid retractors or vertical gaze imbalance7,8. However, these theories may not fully explain the variability seen in clinical practice. Notably, the potential contribution of OOM dysfunction to scleral show in ptotic patients has not been systematically evaluated. Weakness or maladaptive compensation of the orbicularis, particularly in its preseptal and pretarsal segments, could play a critical role in regulating lower eyelid tone and maintaining lid-globe apposition. For example, in facial nerve palsy, paralysis of the OOM leads to sagging of the lower eyelid and marked scleral exposure—underscoring the muscle’s importance in eyelid stability9. Despite the clinical relevance of lower eyelid malposition in ptosis, the pathophysiology of scleral show in this context remains incompletely understood. Much of the literature has focused on anatomical and structural explanations, whereas functional neuromuscular mechanisms have received less attention. Surface electromyography (sEMG) offers a non-invasive means of quantifying periocular muscle activity in real time, allowing for precise evaluation of the functional status of the LPS and OOM before and after surgical intervention10.

The aim of this study is to quantitatively assess changes in lower eyelid muscle function in patients with involutional ptosis, both before and after surgical correction, using sEMG. By evaluating OOM and LPS activity, we aim to characterize dynamic muscle interplay contributing to inferior scleral show. This approach may offer new insights into the neuromuscular basis of eyelid malposition and inform more targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in ptosis management.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective observational study was designed to assess periocular muscle interactions in patients with involutional ptosis compared to healthy controls, with a particular focus on electromyographic patterns of the LPS and OOM. Participants were recruited from an oculoplastic outpatient clinic of the National Institute of Medicine of the Ministry of Interior and Administration in Warsaw and underwent detailed ophthalmologic (LKW) and neurologic (MD) evaluations to confirm eligibility and exclude confounding conditions that could influence eyelid function or neuromuscular activity.

The ptosis group comprised adult patients (≥ 18 years) with bilateral involutional ptosis, defined by a margin reflex distance 1 (MRD1) of ≤ 2.0 mm and intact extraocular motility. Individuals with congenital ptosis, prior eyelid surgery or trauma, neurologic disease, or systemic conditions affecting muscle function were excluded. Surgery was performed on the eyelid with more severe ptosis.

The control group included age-matched adults with no clinical signs of eyelid malposition (MRD1 > 3.0 mm), symmetric levator function, and no history of orbital or periocular disease. Exclusion criteria common to both groups included prior periocular surgery or trauma, congenital eyelid anomalies, current or recent use of medications affecting neuromuscular transmission (e.g., botulinum toxin, neuromuscular blockers), and any condition impairing the ability to complete the sEMG assessment protocol.

Ethics approval

This study involved human participants and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the National Institute of the Ministry of Interior and Administration (Approval No. 45/2024). All procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in this study.

sEMG procedure

The sEMG examination protocol was based on the periocular assessment methodologies that we described previously11, and was conducted at the Electromyography Unit of the Department of Neurology (Warsaw, Poland) using a Keypoint EMG system (Medtronic A/S, Skovlunde, Denmark). This device enabled signal acquisition at a 24 kHz resolution using the Keypoint-Net software. For blink periocular sEMG, sweep speed was set to a 50–800 ms/division range according to interstimulus intervals. Amplitude sensitivity was maintained between 100 and 200 µV/division, with upper and lower frequency filters set from 20 Hz to 10 kHz.

Bipolar silver–silver chloride electrodes (5 × 8 mm) were used for signal acquisition. Prior to electrode placement, the skin was cleansed with 70% isopropyl alcohol to reduce surface impedance, which was maintained below 5 kΩ. The active electrode was placed horizontally in the central portion of the upper eyelid, approximately 5 mm above the lash line, overlying the tarsal plate. For OOM recording, electrodes were positioned along the lower lid margin to target the preseptal segment of the muscle, aligned parallel to the lid margin. A reference electrode was placed on the forehead, and a ground electrode was positioned on the left clavicle. Cables were secured with medical tape to reduce motion artifacts. Conductive gel and adhesive tape ensured stable contact throughout data acquisition.

All recordings were conducted in a dimly lit, with participants seated upright, maintaining a neutral head position and minimal facial movement. The procedure was standardized. Before data collection, the participants were fully instructed and trained in the performance of required motor tasks. Formal measurements were initiated only after task familiarity and consistent performance were confirmed through trial runs.

Each sEMG session included maximal voluntary contraction (MVC). For MVC, subjects performed strong upper eyelid elevation (for LPS assessment) and strong eyelid closure (for OOM assessment), each lasting five seconds and repeated three times with one-minute rest intervals to avoid fatigue. Raw sEMG signals were rectified and smoothed using a moving root-mean-square (RMS) algorithm with a 100 ms window. All measurements were conducted on the eye exhibiting the more pronounced ptosis.

To account for inter-individual variability, all signals were normalized to their respective muscle’s MVC, resulting in %MVC values. These normalized signals were used to calculate the OOM/LPS %MVC ratio, which served as a functional indicator of periocular muscle balance.

Study timeline and data inclusion

Control participants were evaluated in a single 20-minute session, which included informed consent, setup, recording, and post-assessment procedures. Patients with ptosis underwent four assessment sessions: preoperatively, two weeks postoperatively, at three months, and at more than six months following surgical correction. A single sEMG measurement was defined as a five-second trial, and three successful trials were averaged to yield the final signal for analysis. Only patients with complete datasets across all postoperative timepoints were included in longitudinal comparisons.

Surgical procedure

All surgical procedures were performed using the anterior approach levator advancement on the eye with more severe ptosis. After sterile preparation and local infiltration with a lidocaine–epinephrine solution, a skin incision was made along the predetermined upper eyelid crease. A skin–muscle flap was elevated to expose the underlying orbital septum. The septum was opened, and preaponeurotic fat was gently dissected and retracted to identify the levator aponeurosis. The aponeurosis was separated from the underlying Müller’s muscle and carefully dissected free from surrounding tissues. The distal aponeurotic edge was then advanced and secured to the superior tarsal border with nonabsorbable sutures, with intraoperative eyelid position checked in the sitting position. Adjustments were made to achieve an optimal eyelid height and contour. The orbicularis and skin were closed in layers, reestablishing the eyelid crease. Postoperative care included topical antibiotic ointment and cold compresses, with suture removal performed after 7 days.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for each group. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the MVC ratios between control and ptosis groups at each timepoint. Repeated-measures ANOVA was applied to evaluate within-group changes across pre- and postoperative sessions in the ptosis group. Post hoc comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was used for all tests. Statistical analysis was conducted using R software (R Core Team, 2019).

Discussion

This study provides novel evidence supporting a dynamic neuromuscular basis for periocular muscle imbalance in patients with involutional ptosis. By introducing a functional metric—namely, the ratio of the % MVC of the OOM to that of the LPS—we sought to characterize the interplay between antagonistic eyelid muscles before and after surgical correction. Our findings reveal that this ratio is significantly altered in ptotic patients prior to surgery, and that surgical intervention partially normalizes this balance. A key finding of this study is the clinically and quantitatively observable improvement in lower eyelid position following ptosis repair.

Patients with ptosis demonstrated a markedly reduced OOM/LPS %MVC ratio relative to controls, suggesting impaired levator activation or compensatory orbicularis inhibition. Two weeks following surgical correction, this ratio increased substantially and remained elevated at the six-month follow-up, indicating sustained neuromuscular recalibration. Notably, the ratio did not significantly differ from control values at either postoperative time point, suggesting that surgical repositioning of the upper eyelid re-establishes a more physiological distribution of muscle activity. These findings position eyelid ptosis not merely as a static anatomical defect but as a modifiable disorder of periocular motor coordination.

The concept of superior-inferior muscular interplay has long been recognized in the context of extraocular muscle coordination, most notably through the “fixation duress” phenomenon described by Wright and later expanded by Hamed12,13.

In this model, neural and mechanical coupling between vertically acting rectus muscles produces reciprocal adjustments in activity—such that impairment or resistance in one muscle induces compensatory overactivation in its antagonist. Extrapolating this principle to periocular musculature, it is plausible that a similar reflexive neuromuscular feedback loop exists between the LPS and the OOM, particularly in their respective roles as eyelid elevators and sphincters. In the setting of levator hypofunction, chronic compensatory demands may suppress or dysregulate orbicularis activity, especially in its pretarsal and preseptal segments critical to lower eyelid support and blink symmetry. Surgical restoration of levator tone, therefore, may not only correct the mechanical deficit but also relieve this neuromuscular burden, enabling re-engagement of the orbicularis in coordinated eyelid function. Our observed normalization of the OOM/LPS % MVC ratio postoperatively supports this feedback model, suggesting that upper eyelid surgery exerts functional effects beyond the surgical field, potentially through central or peripheral integrative mechanisms. This interpretation aligns with emerging views that the eyelid operates as a coordinated neuromuscular unit, not a set of independent anatomical compartments.

A few studies have demonstrated clinical improvement in inferior scleral show following ptosis repair, often attributed to mechanical lifting of the upper eyelid14. However, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying this phenomenon have remained largely speculative. Our results lend quantitative support to the hypothesis that active neuromuscular dynamics contribute significantly to lower eyelid position. The sustained normalization of the %MVC ratio following levator advancement suggests that reactivation or rebalancing of OOM function plays a key role in the resolution of scleral show and restoration of lid-globe congruity. The traditional view of involutional changes in eyelid position has emphasized factors such as skin laxity, ligamentous weakening, and fat redistribution15. However, the results from our study, suggests that active mechanisms, particularly involving the interplay between the LPS and OOM, may significantly contribute to secondary lower eyelid descent.

These findings align with and extend earlier hypotheses proposed by Matsuo et al., who attributed inferior scleral show to inferior rectus overactivity in the setting of levator weakness7. Their study involved patients with levator aponeurosis disinsertion and their model presumed co-contraction of the levator–superior rectus complex and reciprocal activity in the inferior rectus. However, in our cohort, preoperative EMG demonstrated reduced levator activation, not compensatory overuse. In that case, the theory of co-contraction of levator – superior rectus complex and inferior rectus muscles does not apply and must be another explanation for the presence of scleral show. Our data support a direct neuromuscular link between LPS and OOM as the primary driver of dynamic scleral show. Our results are further supported by prior work from Kashkouli et al., who reported that in unilateral myogenic ptosis, the severity of inferior scleral show correlated with the degree of ptosis8.

In myogenic ptosis, the degenerated levator muscle is incapable of generating sufficient contraction, and thus the co-contraction of the levator–superior rectus complex and the inferior rectus muscle—previously hypothesized as a cause of inferior scleral show—does not occur. This suggests that an alternative mechanism must underlie the presence of dynamic scleral show in such cases. A more plausible explanation may lie in the altered interplay between agonist and antagonist periocular muscles, particularly between the LPS and the OOM. Interestingly, Kashkouli et al. reported no significant association between scleral show and ptosis severity in patients with aponeurotic ptosis. One possible explanation is that in acquired (aponeurotic) ptosis, levator function can vary widely—ranging from preserved to compromised—which may account for the lack of a consistent correlation across patients8.

We propose that the OOM, particularly its lower segments, may undergo functional decompensation in the setting of chronic levator insufficiency. This interpretation is consistent with earlier electromyographic and histological data showing regionally variable fatigue and activity patterns within the OOM16,17. Surgical correction of the LPS appears to relieve this compensatory burden and re-enable physiologic orbicularis activity, leading to improved lower eyelid posture and globe apposition.

From a clinical perspective, these findings have several implications. First, the OOM/LPS % MVC ratio may serve as a sensitive, noninvasive biomarker for neuromuscular imbalance in eyelid disorders, supplementing anatomical assessments. Preoperative sEMG evaluation could help identify patients with subclinical orbicularis dysfunction who might benefit from tailored surgical approaches or adjunctive therapies. Second, the normalization of this ratio following ptosis repair supports the notion that surgical correction restores not only lid height but also functional synergy across the periorbital musculature. Third, our findings suggest a rationale for neuromuscular-based interventions—such as EMG-guided physiotherapy, biofeedback, or botulinum toxin modulation—to complement or enhance surgical outcomes, particularly in patients with persistent imbalance.

In our study, we decided not to use Margin Reflex Distances (MRD2) to quantify eyelid position, following a similar rationale to that described by Matsuo et al.7 The main limitation of MRD2 is that it relies on the corneal light reflex as a reference point. Since the globe can shift vertically within the orbit, particularly depending on the contraction of the extraocular muscles (e.g., the inferior rectus), MRD2 does not represent a stable or reproducible measurement of lower eyelid position. For this reason, MRD2 may introduce variability unrelated to true eyelid changes. Consequently, while our study assessed dynamic scleral show, it was not based on MRD2 values.

Limitations of the study

Despite these insights, our study has limitations. Surface EMG provides only indirect measurements of muscle activity and may not capture deeper structures such as Müller’s muscle or the capsulopalpebral fascia. Additionally, all ptosis repairs were through anterior approach levator advancement. The variations in surgical technique among patients were not accounted for, and these may influence the degree and durability of neuromuscular recovery. The relatively small number of patients with complete follow-up data limits statistical power for longitudinal analysis. Future studies should include larger cohorts, standardized surgical protocols, and correlation with objective functional endpoints, such as blink amplitude, tear film stability, and lower eyelid excursion.

The precise mechanism of postoperative rebalancing—whether mechanical, neurogenic, or adaptive—remains to be elucidated. Multimodal investigations incorporating high-resolution imaging, advanced electrophysiology, and potentially molecular profiling of muscle tissue may offer deeper insight into periocular muscle plasticity and functional restoration.

Conclusion

This study introduces a novel functional metric for characterizing neuromuscular dynamics in eyelid pathophysiology and demonstrates that involutional ptosis is associated with a quantifiable imbalance between LPS and or OOM activity. Surgical correction restores this balance. Patients should be advised that after ptosis surgery, OOM tone and consequently, lower eyelid position may change. Ptosis surgery is not only a visual field intervention but also a periorbital rebalancing procedure.
