Erk inhibitors intercept Erk-mediated negative feedback while imposing cell-cycle arrest and activating p38
Authors: Merav Darash-Yahana, Nadine Soudah, Alexey Baskin, Inbal Levy-Saar, Alexander Levitzki, David Engelberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe MAP kinases Erk1/2 mediate the oncogenicity of the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)-Ras-Raf-MEK cascade. Components of this pathway are often constantly active in cancer, making Erks promising targets for therapy. Yet, while Erks promote cell proliferation, they concomitantly inhibit the upstream pathway’s components. Erks’ inhibition may reactivate, therefore, proto-oncoproteins, increasing the risk of recurrent disease. We studied the effects of Erks’ inhibitors on cells transformed by Erk1R84H or Erk1R84S, and on 9 cancer-derived cell lines. Provision of either BVD523, GDC0994, SCH772984, ASN007, or Temuterkib, led to strong phosphorylation of Erks, suggesting that all inhibitors intercepted Erks-mediated negative feedback activity. All inhibitors caused cell-cycle arrest at G1 and activation of the MAP/stress kinase p38, but some degree of cell viability was maintained even after 72 h of treatment. Exposure to BVD523 caused dramatic changes in the phosphoproteome. Thus, the application of Erk’s inhibitors should be accompanied by agents that prevent pathway re-activation.Keywords: erk, MAP kinase, MEK, p38, BVD523 (Ulixertinib), SCH772984, ASN007, Temuterkib (LY3214996), GDC0994 (Ravoxertinib), cisplatin

Introduction

The two extracellular signal-regulated kinases, Erk1 and Erk2 (Erk1/2), are the downstream targets of the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)-Ras-Raf-MEK signaling cascade.1,2 As many cancers are caused by genetic aberrations in the genes encoding the pathway’s components,3,4,5,6 a plausible conclusion is that in most of these diseases Erks are constantly phosphorylated at the “activatory” TEY motif and are constitutively active.7,8,9,10,11 Furthermore, as the only known downstream components of the cascade, Erks have been considered as critical, perhaps even the sole, mediators of the oncogenicity of the entire pathway.12,13,14,15,16 This notion has been strongly supported by the observation that intrinsically active variants of Erk1 oncogenically transform cells in culture and give rise to tumors in mice and flies.17,18,19

Thus, although Erks themselves are rarely mutated in cancer20 they are considered as promising targets for anti-cancer therapy for every cancer case in which an upstream component is abnormally activated.13,21 Also, although inhibitors of EGFR, MEK, and of mutated Raf and Ras are successfully applied in the clinic,22,23,24,25,26,27,28 patients develop resistance quite fast,20,22,29,30 in some cases by activating Erks.31 Erk inhibitors should also be useful, therefore, against tumors that have acquired such resistance.

These ideas led to the development of an array of Erk inhibitors, some of which are in advanced phases of clinical trials.32,33,34,35,36,37 Understanding the effects of these potential drugs on the biochemistry and physiology of cells is thus important and timely. The need to explore the effects of Erks’ inhibitors is reinforced by the emerging complexity of the pathway. The notion that there exist exact positive proportions between the rate of activity of upstream components and that of Erk, and between the degree of Erk activity and the severity of cancer, is clearly over-simplified. In various cancer models, as well as in patients carrying activating mutations in EGFR, Ras, or Raf, the levels of active Erks are not at all high. In some cancer cells and in some cancer models, the level of active/phosphorylated Erks is barely measurable12,18,38 (see also Figures 1 and 7 in this paper). Namely, in these cancers the transformed phenotype is maintained with the most minimal Erk’s activity. Part of the explanation for the counterintuitive low Erk’s activity in cells in which the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK cascade is activated is the powerful negative feedback inhibition imposed by active onco-proteins, including by Erk.18,39,40 Suppression of this feedback inhibition must be taken into consideration when designing therapeutic strategies, because the inhibition of Erk would relieve the negative feedback and re-activate proto-oncogenic upstream components. This concern is equivalent to that exemplified in tumors carrying the BRAFV600E mutant treated with BRAFV600E inhibitors.18,41,42 The complexity of the pathway and its disparate response to inhibitors calls for a detailed understanding of the effects of Erk inhibitors.

The direct impetus for this study was our recent observations that in cells and animals transformed by an oncogenic Erk (Erk1R84S or Erk1R84H) the negative feedback machinery was so efficient that the phosphorylation of the oncogenic Erk could not be measured; and that exposure of Erk1R84S- or Erk1R84H-transformed NIH3T3 cells to the Erks’ inhibitor BVD523 (Ulixertinib) resulted in dramatic re-phosphorylation of Erks.18 Importantly, BVD523 had a similar effect on HeLa, A549, PC3, MCF7 and BT474 cells,18 suggesting that the strong negative feedback and its removal by the inhibitor are not restricted to cells that are experimentally transformed by an active Erk.

Important questions remained open with regard to the effect of BVD523. For example, how long does BVD523-mediated Erk’s phosphorylation persist? Are the re-phosphorylated Erk molecules catalytically active or not? What are the effects of the inhibitor on the physiology of the cells (i.e., whether it impacts growth rate by causing cell-cycle arrest, or perhaps necrosis or apoptosis)? And, which of the effects are specific to BVD523, and which may be shared with other Erk inhibitors? As the various inhibitors are different with respect to the chemical structure, and some also with respect to the mechanism of action,16,43,44,45 they may behave differently from BVD523.

Finally, a critical open question is whether indeed re-phosphorylation of Erks in response to BVD523 is a consequence of abolishment of the negative feedback loop.

Considered to be highly specific against Erks, Erks’ inhibitors belong to the “targeted drugs” category. “Targeted drugs” have been developing since the understanding of the biochemistry of cancer has advanced, primarily after the discovery of oncogenes.46,47 Prominent examples of targeted compounds are the BCR-ABL inhibitor imatinib (Gleevec), the EGFR inhibitors Iressa and Tarceva (Erlotinib) and the BRAFV600E inhibitor Vemurafenib (PLX4032).48,49,50,51,52,53,54,55 Being specific to their targets, not damaging critical cellular machineries, targeted drugs do not commonly evoke cellular stress responses, such as the induction of the MAP/stress kinases JNK and p38.56,57 This is in contrast to the effects of “non-targeted therapy,” which includes radiotherapy, chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, and immunotherapy.58,59,60 Chemotherapy is in fact a “cytotoxic therapy” that interferes with fundamental cellular processes such as cell division, DNA synthesis, and cytoskeleton establishment. These drugs affect both cancerous and healthy cells and are activators of the cellular stress responses,58,61 which are more prone to activation in cancer cells.62,63 Intriguingly, “non-targeted compounds,” such as cisplatin, doxorubicin, and etoposide also induce the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK-Erk pathway.64 The effect, if any, of BVD523 and other Erk’s inhibitors on the cellular stress response has not been reported.

Here we investigated first the mechanism through which BVD523 causes the dramatic elevation of Erk phosphorylation and tested if it impacts cell cycle or cell death. Then, we checked whether SCH772984, ASN007, Temuterkib (LY3214996), and GDC0994 (Ravoxertinib) cause similar effects. These inhibitors are ATP-competitive and vary in structure and binding.34,65 GDC0994 completed phase I clinical trials for the treatment of metastatic solid tumors.66,67,68 SCH772984 has a “dual mechanism” of action,35 inhibiting both Erks’ catalytic activity and Erks’ phosphorylation by MEK.35,69 ASN007 and Temuterkib show improved pharmacokinetic profiles, demonstrating efficacy in preclinical RAS-mutant models.70,71

We report that despite structural differences, all five inhibitors induced dramatic and sustained Erk and MEK phosphorylation in a variety of transformed cells. Using co-treatment with Erk and MEK inhibitors, we show that the drug-dependent re-phosphorylation of Erk is a result of the interception of the negative feedback machinery. All inhibitors induced cell-cycle arrest at the G1 phase, as well as the elevation of some apoptotic markers; but some degree of viability remained for at least 72 h. We further report that the Erk inhibitors triggered the phosphorylation of the p38 MAP/stress kinase, reminiscent of the effect of non-targeted agents. Finally, proteome and phosphoproteome analysis of BVD523-treated cells disclosed large-scale effects of the drug on cellular components, most prominent on the Rho signaling pathway.

Results

The Erk1/2 inhibitor BVD523 induces Erk1/2 phosphorylation in Erk1R84S- and Erk1R84H-transformed cells

In a recent study, we observed that exposure of Erk1R84S- and Erk1R84H-transformed NIH3T3 cells to BVD523 for 2 h caused elevation of Erk1/2 phosphorylation at the TEY motif.18 Notably, the effect was more dramatic on the endogenous Erk1/2, which also became phosphorylated in response to BVD523 in NIH3T3 cells harboring an empty vector, or expressing Erk1WT, that served as control. This observation was reproduced here (Figure 1). Please note, as explained in “Materials and Methods,” that there are minor differences in experimental conditions, mainly cell density, between the experiment shown in Figure 1A and that described in ref.18 Figure S1 shows the comparison of the conditions. It was found that in sparse cultures, as used in ref.18 (0.25 × 106 cells/well of a 6-well plate), the response to BVD523 is more intense than in more dense cultures (1 × 106 cells/well of a 6-well plate) used here (Figure S1).

To further characterize the phenomenon of BVD523-induced Erk phosphorylation, we extended the treatment time and observed that the phosphorylation of Erk1R84S and Erk1R84H on the TEY motif was more apparent after 16 h of exposure to BVD523, and was further elevated after 72 h (Figure 1, middle and lower panels). BVD523-induced phosphorylation of endogenous Erk1/2 was stronger throughout the experiment in NIH3T3/empty and NIH3T3/Erk1WT cells than in NIH3T3/Erk1R84S and NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells (Figure 1A). Notably, the steady-state levels of 3xflag-Erk1WT in NIH3T3/Erk1WT cells are low, as reflected in the western blot with either anti Erk or anti Flag antibodies (Figures 1 and S1). As already shown in ref.18, expression of Flag-Erk1WT is downregulated about 10 days after transfection, while that of Flag-Erk1R84S and Flag-Erk1R84H remains high in the stable clones obtained.18 It seems that the cells cannot tolerate overexpression of native Erk1, so that the stable clone obtained includes cells that were selected against high expression levels. Native, not activated Erk1 molecules may titrate and insulate pathways’ components critical for proliferation. In any case, it is clear that once Erk’s activity is inhibited (and the negative feedback is probably abolished), Erk molecules in non-transformed cells are readily phosphorylated, while in cells transformed by active Erk, the kinetics of phosphorylation are slower and incomplete (as judged by comparing the phosphorylation levels of endogenous Erk1/2).

To check whether the slower kinetics of phosphorylation in Erk1R84S- and Erk1R84H-transformed cells may be associated with slower kinetics of Erk inhibition, we measured directly, Erks’ catalytic activity per se in a kinase assay, using the Erk-specific AssayQuant kit72 (see details in Materials and Methods). We found that following 2 h of exposure to BVD523, Erks’ activity was already significantly downregulated in all clones (Figure 1B; raw data are shown in Figure S2).

Erk1R84S and Erk1R84H are known to autophosphorylate not only the TEY, but also another phosphoacceptor, T207, that probably controls the permissiveness of the TEY motif for phosphorylation.17,18,73 Interestingly, although for phosphorylating T207 Erk1R84S and Erk1R84H employ a mechanism that is different than that responsible for substrate phosphorylation,17,74 exposure of NIH3T3/Erk1R84S and NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells to BVD523 caused downregulation of T207 phosphorylation (Figure 1A), providing another indication that BVD523 efficiently inhibited the Erk molecules. To check whether the effect of BVD523 is reversible, cells were exposed to the drug for 2 h, and then the growth medium was replaced with a fresh medium, not supplemented with the drug. Erk phosphorylation on the TEY motif, which was dramatically elevated following 2 h of drug treatment, was downregulated after drug removal, while the level of T207 phosphorylation, which was reduced in response to BVD523, was high again 2 h after drug removal (Figure 1C).

It is concluded that Erk molecules, native or active mutants, are immediately and efficiently inhibited by BVD523, but become phosphorylated on the TEY motif. This phosphorylation occurs fast in non-transformed, NIH3T3/empty, and NIH3T3/Erk1WT cells, and slower in NIH3T3/Erk1R84S and NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells. The slow phosphorylation of Erks in the latter suggests that the negative feedback machinery is powerful in these cells, so that a longer time is required for rewiring the relevant biochemical activities that re-activate the machinery responsible for the phosphorylation [the (RTK)-Ras-Raf-MEK cascade, see Figure 2B]. Importantly, the high TEY-phosphorylation levels of Erk molecules, which are catalytically inactive, reinforce the notion that TEY phosphorylation levels cannot be automatically taken as a marker for the catalytic status of Erks.

Phosphorylation of Erk molecules in response to BVD523 in the NIH3T3-derived clones is mediated via MEK

Active Erk could induce negative feedback via several mechanisms, such as the induction of phosphatases (mainly DUSP675,76), activation of Sprouty, the pathway’s inhibitor,77,78 or direct inhibition of MEK, Raf, and EGFR.39,40,79,80,81,82 Given the strong and fast elevation of TEY phosphorylation in response to BVD523, we presumed that the major mechanism is re-activation of the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK pathway. As MEK is a dual specificity kinase, phosphorylating both the Thr and the Tyr residues of the TEY motif, we first checked whether indeed both are phosphorylated (there are reports of conditions under which Erk molecules are singly phosphorylated83,84,85,86). For that we reacted lysates prepared from cells treated with BVD523 for 72 h with three different antibodies: anti-pTEpY/pT, which reacts with dually phosphorylated Erk1/2 and also with Erk1/2 molecules mono-phosphorylated on T202/183, anti-pTEpY/pY, which reacts with dually phosphorylated Erks and monophosphorylated on Y204/185, and anti-pTEpY, which reacts specifically and uniquely with dually phosphorylated proteins (for the characterization of the antibodies see ref.73,87). The three antibodies reacted similarly with the lysates (Figure S3), suggesting that in cells treated with BVD523, the Erk1/2 molecules are phosphorylated on both T202/183 and Y204/185, most probably by MEK.

We therefore next monitored the levels of MEK phosphorylation in cells treated with BVD523 and observed that it is indeed elevated (Figure 2A). Elevated MEK phosphorylation is directly associated with the inhibitors as it is promptly downregulated upon drug removal (Figure 1C, 5th row). Notably, however, in the Erk1R84S- and Erk1R84H-transformed cells, prominent MEK phosphorylation was observed only after 72 h of treatment, and its levels were lower than in similarly treated NIH3T3/empty and NIH3T3/Erk1WT cells (Figure 2A). This observation supports the notion that the feedback machinery is more robust in cells expressing the active Erks. To assess whether MEK re-activation is indeed an important factor in BVD523-induced Erks’ phosphorylation, we treated cells with BVD523 and the MEK inhibitor Trametinib together. Trametinib (commercially known as Mekinist) is a highly potent MEK inhibitor, FDA-approved for the treatment of melanoma and glioma, with IC50 of 0.92 nM and 1.8 nM toward purified MEK1 and MEK2, respectively.88 If Erks’ phosphorylation in response to BVD523 is achieved by MEK, then it should not be elevated in cells exposed to the two drugs. This presumption seems to be correct as Erk’s phosphorylation was very slightly elevated in cells treated with both drugs (Figure 2B).

It is concluded that the inhibition of the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK cascade is the major mechanism employed by Erk1R84S and Erk1R84H in the negative feedback process, and interception of this negative loop by BVD523 is responsible for the dramatic Erk’s phosphorylation. Other mechanisms (phosphatases, sprouty) may still be involved, but given that Erks’ phosphorylation is so low in cells exposed to BVD523+Trametinib, their effect is clearly minor.

BVD523 causes a reversible arrest at the G1 phase of the cell cycle

What is the cell response to BVD523 at the physiological level? Previous studies showed that exposure of UACC-62 melanoma-derived cells, expressing the BRAFV600E mutant, or thyroid cancer cells, to BVD523 imposed cell-cycle arrest at the G1 phase.89,90 Whether cells are viable after this treatment was not reported. To assess whether BVD523 affects the cell cycle of the NIH3T3-derived clones, we monitored the status of the treated cultures with respect to the cell cycle process via FACS, following PI staining (Figure 3A). After 16 h of exposure to BVD523, most cells of the cultures accumulated in the G1 phase. Depending on the clone, 77–87% of the cells accumulated in G1, with the most significant effect on transformed cells (Figure 3A; Table S1).

Having observed the accumulation of BVD523-treated cells in G1 phase, we checked whether the drug induced the expression of components associated with G1 arrest, such as Klf4, p21Cip/Waf, and cyclin D1 (Figure 3B). Klf4 is a transcription factor whose expression is downregulated in various cancers and may be considered as a tumor suppressor.91,92 Cyclin D1 expression is commonly high in G1 phase and its activity is critical for promoting entry of cells from G1 to S phase via CDK and retinoblastoma, while the function of p21Cip1/Waf1 is to inhibit cell cycle at G1 phase by inhibiting CDK.93,94,95,96 Also tested were proteins associated with apoptosis, γH2AX, and cleaved caspase 3. We observed that BVD523 induced p21Cip1/Waf1, cyclin D1, and Klf4 expression in accordance with the FACS results (Figure 3B). Intriguingly, expression of γH2AX and cleaved caspase 3 was elevated as well (Figure 3B). Notably, in NIH3T3/Erk1WT cells, the level of the proteins tested was high in untreated cells, a phenomenon that is probably associated with the low expression level of Flag-Erk1WT itself (Figures 1 and S1). The results of the FACS analysis and the western blots combined suggest that the major effect of BVD523 is on cell cycle, while some apoptotic-associated activities are also evoked. In agreement with these observations, cultures did not proliferate in the presence of the inhibitor, and microscopic inspection showed that the cells remained attached, did not disintegrate, and looked bright and vital, even after 3 days of treatment. However, as the expression of γH2AX and cleaved caspase 3 was also elevated, perhaps cell death also occurs. The question of whether cells die is of importance because if the drug does not eliminate the ability of all transformed cells to re-grow, its use in the clinic may be questionable. We therefore performed a clonal viability test in which cells were exposed to 5 μM of BVD523 for 72 h, and then samples of cells were re-plated in a drug-free medium, and colonies that appeared were counted. Somewhat unexpectedly, the viability rate of cells exposed to DMSO was quite low in the clonal viability assay (Table 1). Viability of cultures treated with BVD523 was lower, particularly that of the NIH3T3/Erk1R84H, but nevertheless, still monitored (0.35%; Table 1). Namely, BVD523 does affect cells’ viability, in particular of Erk-transformed cells, but even 3 days of treatment (with a daily drug replenishment) were not sufficient to totally eliminate cells in the culture.

In summary, by inhibiting Erk’s activity in various NIH3T3-derived clones, BVD523 intercepts the negative feedback imposed by active Erks and causes the elevation of MEK activity and, in turn, the elevation of Erks’ phosphorylation at the TEY motif. But the Erk proteins remain catalytically inactive. Consequently, cells arrest at the G1 phase of the cell cycle and lose viability, but even after 72 h of exposure, a portion of the cells is still viable, capable of re-proliferation.

The Erk1/2 inhibitors GDC0994 (Ravoxertinib), SCH772984, ASN007, and temuterkib (LY3214996) induce Erk1/2 and MEK phosphorylation, similarly to BVD523, but at different levels

To check whether the effects observed with BVD523 might be unique to this compound or are also caused by other Erk inhibitors, we exposed the NIH3T3-derived clones to GDC0994, SCH772984, ASN007, and Temuterkib. We found that all four inhibitors induced elevation of Erk’s and MEK’s phosphorylation (Figures 4A–4D). Thus, regardless of their chemical formula, structure, and manner of binding to Erk, all inhibitors tested caused elevation of phosphorylation of Erk and MEK in transformed or non-transformed NIH3T3 cells. The effect is more rapid in the non-transformed cells and is maintained throughout the experiment.

Notably, however, although the inhibitors’ effects are similar in principle, there seem to be differences in their kinetics and strength, particularly in Erk1R84S- and Erk1R84H-transformed cells.

As it is difficult to compare the effects of the inhibitors when each is tested in a different experiment, we carried out an experiment in which cells were treated simultaneously with the 5 inhibitors for 16 h, and lysates prepared were tested side-by-side on the same SDS-PAGE gels (Figure 5A). This analysis revealed quantitative differences between the inhibitors indeed. In NIH3T3 cells expressing the wild type or active Erks, Erk phosphorylation was elevated more significantly in response to BVD523, ASN007, and Temuterkib treatment than in response to GDC0994 or SCH772984 (Figure 5A). Also, interestingly, Erks phosphorylation increased significantly in response to Temuterkib only in non-transformed cells (Figure 5A). MEK phosphorylation was strongly elevated in response to all inhibitors in control cells (NIH3T3/empty; Figure 5A), while in Erk1R84S- and Erk1R84H-transformed cells, only SCH772984 imposed a significant MEK phosphorylation (Figure 5A). These results were further confirmed with all three anti-phospho-Erk antibodies available (Figures S3 and S4). Note that proper inhibition of Erk by SCH772984 was confirmed by a direct kinase assay (Figures 5B and S5A), and inhibition by all inhibitors was substantiated by the downregulation of the Erk’s target c-Jun (Figure 5A, row 6) and of phospho(T207)-Erk (Figure 5A, right panel, row 3). These observations, which reveal quantitative differences in the effects of the inhibitors on the Erk pathway, point to GDC0994, and to some extent at SCH772984, as inhibitors that are less effective in relieving the Erk-mediated negative feedback. This may be an advantage for using these molecules in the clinic.

Similarly to BVD523, the inhibitors GDC0994, SCH772984, ASN007 and Temuterkib impose cell-cycle arrest at the G1 phase

We next asked whether the effect of GDC0994, SCH772984, ASN007, and Temuterkib is also similar to that of BVD523 with respect to cell cycle and viability. FACS analysis and western blot assays showed that all compounds caused cell accumulation in the G1 phase of the cell cycle, just like BVD523 (Figures 6A–6D; Table S2). To assess whether cells exposed to GDC0994, SCH772984, ASN007, and Temuterkib also maintain some viability, as they do when exposed to BVD523, cells of all NIH3T3-derived clones were re-plated and provided with drug-free media after 3 days of exposure to the inhibitors. When plating 10,000 or 100,000 cells, a layer of cells appeared a few days later, showing that there are vital cells among those arrested for 3 days in the presence of Erk inhibitors.

Erk inhibitors induce Erk and MEK phosphorylation in a variety of cancer-derived cells

To check whether the effects of the Erk inhibitors might be specific to the laboratory model which is based on NIH3T3 cells, or are more general, the five inhibitors were provided to cancer-derived cell lines. We tested the following lines that carry genetic aberrations in upstream components of the Erk pathway (the oncogene carried by each line appears in parentheses): A549 (KRasG12S), MDA-MB-231 (KRasG13D and BRafG464V), MEL1617 (BRafV600E), HepG2 (NRasQ61L and mutation in CTNNB1/β-catenin), NCI-H1975 (EGFRL858R,T790M,R273H), and BT474 (amplification of HER2). Also tested were three lines, mutated in other components: HeLa (harboring an integrated HPV type 18 genome), MCF7 (expressing PIK3CAE545K), and PC3 (harboring a homozygous loss of PTEN and mutation in TP53).97,98,99,100,101,102,103,104,105,106 Testing this array of lines allows assessing the generality of the response to Erk’s inhibitors and to check whether cancer cells mutated in the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK-Erk cascade respond differently than those mutated in other components.

It was observed that in many of these cell lines, including those not mutated in the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK-Erk pathway, the effect of the inhibitors on Erk and MEK phosphorylation was similar to their effect on the Erk-transformed NIH3T3 cells. Namely, most cells responded by the elevation of Erks and MEK phosphorylation (Figure 7A; Table 2). Yet, some cell lines are unique in these respects. In PC3 cells, for example, none of the drugs caused Erk’s or MEK’s phosphorylation, and in NCI-H1975 cells MEK was strongly phosphorylated in response to all drugs, but Erk’s phosphorylation was not elevated in response to GDC0994 or SCH772984 (Figure 7A; Table 2). In fact, in all cell lines tested, SCH772984 did not cause a significant elevation of Erk’s phosphorylation (Figure 7A; Table 2). These observations are reminiscent of those made in the NIH3T3-derived clones (Figure 5A), supporting the notion that perhaps using GDC0994 and SCH772984 (or a drug with similar effects) reduces the risk of acquiring drug resistance and disease recurrence by the activation of the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK cascade.

In summary, the effects of the inhibitors on cancer-derived cell lines, at the biochemical, mechanistic and cell cycle aspects were commonly similar to their effects on the NIH3T3-derived clones tested above. Some effects, however, were cell line-specific and inhibitor-specific.

The Erk inhibitors caused transient activation of the p38 MAPK

Non-targeted drugs, such as cisplatin, etoposide, and doxorubicin, were reported to activate p38, as part of the mechanism that induces cell death.107,108,109,110,111,112 It was unexpected, therefore, to observe that upon 16 h of treatment with Erk inhibitors, p38 phosphorylation increased in both the NIH3T3-derived clones and in cancer-derived cell lines (Figures 9A and S6). p38 phosphorylation is directly associated with the presence of the drugs, as, at least for the case of BVD523, it is downregulated when the drug is washed away (Figure 9B). SCH772984 was commonly a less potent p38 activator than the four other inhibitors (Figure 9A). p38 activation in response to the inhibitors was more prominent in transformed NIH3T3cells, compared to non-transformed (Figure 9A, upper panels). In HeLa and A549 cells, all five inhibitors induced p38 phosphorylation. In all cell lines tested, ASN007 was a more efficient inducer of p38 than other compounds (Figure 9A).

Two explanations may account for the p38α activation. One, that it is a consequence of Erks’ inhibition.113,114 Second, that the Erks’ inhibitors cause, directly, damage or stress, which in turn ignites the stress/MAPK, similar to the case of non-targeted drugs,109,111,115 not related to the Erk’s inhibition. A way to address this question is to test whether p38 phosphorylation is induced in response to the inhibition of Erks by inhibitors that target upstream components of the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK-Erk cascade. We thus exposed the various NIH3T3 clones to the EGFR inhibitor Tarceva or to the MEK inhibitor Trametinib. We also tested the B-RafV600E inhibitor Vemurafenib. Indeed, the Vemurafenib target does not exist in the NIH3T3-derived clones studied, and it was applied as a control for an off-target effect of a targeted inhibitor. Treatment of the NIH3T3-derived clones with Tarceva, Trametinib, or Vemurafenib did not cause elevation of p38 phosphorylation (perhaps even some downregulation; Figure 10A). On one hand, these observations could support the notion that Erk’s inhibition per se is not the trigger of p38 activation in response to Erk’s inhibitors. But on the other hand, Tarceva, Trametinib, or Vemurafenib may be impotent p38 activators because they do not efficiently inhibit Erk, because in the NIH3T3 system their targets are expressed at low levels (i.e., EGFR), or are not very active. We thus tested whether these compounds would evoke p38 activity in cancer-derived cell lines, particularly in cells where their targets are overactive. Indeed, treating NCI-H1975 cells (in which the EGFR is abnormally active) with Tarceva did induce p38 activation (Figure 10B). All three drugs induced p38 in HeLa cells (Figure 10B), and Tarceva (but not Vemurafenib) caused p38 induction in Mel1617 cells (Figure 10B).

It seems that various targeted drugs may activate p38 in particular cell types, perhaps more so in cells in which their targets are active (see discussion). Although the mode of p38 activation in response to Erk’s inhibitors is not clear yet, the fact that it is activated should be taken into consideration when designing therapeutic plans.

Cisplatin induces p38 and Erk phosphorylation in oncogenically transformed cells

Having observed that Erk inhibitors cause activation of p38, similarly to some non-targeted drugs, we compared their effects to those of the non-targeted drug cisplatin. It was observed, unexpectedly, that in the NIH3T3 system, the effects of cisplatin and BVD523 on Erk, MEK, and p38 were quite similar (Figure 11A, upper panel). As we already reported, transformed cells are more prone to cisplatin effects,62 and therefore the responses are less pronounced in parental NIH3T3 cells (Figure 11A, lower panel).

Unlike the similar effects of cisplatin and Erk inhibitors on Erk, MEK, and p38, their effects on cell cycle and cell death components are significantly different. Cisplatin induces s dramatic elevation (significantly stronger than the effect of BVD523) of p21Cip1/Waf1, γH2AX, and p53 (Figure 11A). Cisplatin was also tested on HeLa, Mel1617, and NCI-H1975 cells, and the effects were essentially similar to those monitored in the NIH3T3-derived clones (Figure 11B).

Proteomic and phosphoproteomic analysis of Erk1R84S- and Erk1R84H-transformed cells exposed to BVD523 revealed significant effects on the Rho GTPases pathway

To gain a wider insight into the effects of Erk inhibition at the molecular level, we determined the proteome and phosphoproteome of NIH3T3/Erk1WT, NIH3T3/Erk1R84S, and NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells, exposed or not exposed to BVD523 (5 μM for 2 h, in triplicates).

Protein lysates were analyzed via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), and the complete datasets of proteins and phosphorylation sites are provided as Supplementary data items Data S1 and Data S2, respectively.

Data were subjected to multiple rounds of hierarchical pairwise comparisons, aimed at identifying differentially expressed (DE) proteins (Figure 12). At the first hierarchy, we examined the effect of BVD523 on the proteome of each clone. Only 43 proteins were DE between NIH3T3/Erk1R84S cells treated or not treated with the drug (light blue oval shape in Figure 12A). Similarly, 41 proteins were DE between treated and not treated NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells (pink oval shape in Figure 12A), with only four of them, STAT5A, CIRBP, EPPK1, and MMAB being also DE in NIH3T3/Erk1R84S cells (Figure 12A). The effect of the drug on NIH3T3/Erk1WT cells was somewhat more significant, with 136 DE proteins (Figure 12A, green oval). The small effect of BVD523 on the proteome could be explained, in part, by the short treatment time, just 2 h. In contrast, the impact of BVD523 on the phosphoproteome was substantially more pronounced (described in the next paragraph; Figure 13). To identify proteins affected by BVD523 specifically in the transformed cells, we compared the proteome of BVD523–treated NIH3T3/Erk1R84S and NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells to that of BVD523-treated NIH3T3/Erk1WT cells (Figure 12B). This analysis identified 273 DE proteins expressed in NIH3T3/Erk1R84S-transfomed cells and 299 proteins in NIH3T3/Erk1R84H-transformed cells versus NIH3T3/Erk1WT (Figure 12B). Out of those, we focused on 179 DE proteins that are shared between NIH3T3/Erk1R84S and NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells treated with BVD523 and not detected in NIH3T3/Erk1WT-expressing cells. This list likely includes baseline differences between the transformed and non-transformed cells, unrelated to BVD523 exposure. To exclude proteins that are DE regardless of BVD523 exposure, we used the proteome of untreated samples as a baseline. In the absence of BVD523, 91 DE proteins were common in cells expressing Erk1R84S or Erk1R84H and not in cells expressing Erk1WT (Figure 12C). Comparing these to the 179 DE proteins shared between NIH3T3/Erk1R84S and NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells treated with BVD523 revealed 43 proteins common to both conditions, leaving 136 BVD523–specific DE proteins present only in cells transformed by active Erks and not in NIH3T3/Erk1WT under inhibitor treatment (Figure 12D). We confirmed that the 43 baseline proteins exhibited consistent expression directionality in both untreated and treated conditions (Table S4).

Pathway enrichment of BVD523-specific phosphosites in Erk1R84S- and Erk1R84H-transformed NIH3T3cells

In summary, the analyses of the proteome and phosphoproteome of NIH3T3/Erk1R84S and NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells treated with BVD523 revealed minor alterations in the proteome and a more pronounced effect on the phosphoproteome. These changes affect multiple pathways, most prominently the Rho GTPases signaling. These results are not unexpected as we have observed changes in the levels of KLF4, p21, p27, and c-Jun, known targets of the Rho GTPase pathway (see discussion).

Discussion

The response of cells and tissues to non-targeted anti-cancer drugs is unusually intricate, and commonly results in cell death, because of the damage they cause to essential cellular components. Targeted drugs are expected to have milder off-target effects, but may still impose significant changes on the cells when they target a molecule that controls a plethora of substrates and physiological processes. Erk1/2 are clearly such targets. A basic premise of this study was, therefore, that Erk1/2 inhibitors would have dramatic effects on cells, so that the cells would not tolerate them for longer than a few hours and would lose viability. Another prediction was that each inhibitor would manifest particular effects, related to its specific off-target activity and its mechanism of inhibition. The observations with the cell lines tested were, however, that viability was maintained to some degree even after 3 days of exposure, and that the inhibitors showed, in principle, similar effects on the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK cascade, p38, and the cell cycle.

Erk1/2 inhibitors caused strong TEY phosphorylation, although Erks’ catalytic activity remained suppressed. Therefore, while pTEY abundance is widely used as a readout for Erks’ catalytic activity, it should be taken into consideration that it does not necessarily reflect Erks’ activity. The measure of Erk1/2 catalytic activity, in a direct and specific kinase assay, should be the sole bona fide readout for the status of Erks’ catalytic capabilities.

Because they are strongly phosphorylated, although inhibited, Erk molecules are probably prone to a rapid re-activation that would consequently promote cell proliferation (once the inhibitor is dissociated or degraded). Strategies to prevent this situation should reduce Erks’ phosphorylation and enhance the death of the treated cells. A possible approach toward this goal, proposed in other systems,119,120,121 is that treatment with Erks’ inhibitors be accompanied by inhibitors of upstream components of the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK pathway. Such a mode of treatment is already becoming a practice in melanoma treatment with BrafV600E inhibitors such as Vemurafenib, that is provided together with MEK inhibitors.122,123,124,125,126,127 This approach has been proposed for Erk inhibitors, too, in other models.119,120,128 Alternatively, to avoid exposure of patients to too many drugs, one can consider the development of compounds that affect Erks’ oncogenic activities without relieving their negative feedback functions. A starting point could be SCH772984, and perhaps GDC0994 too, whose effect on the feedback system is milder than that of the other inhibitors, while they still fully inhibit Erk’s catalytic activity. Further investigation is required to address whether a reduced effect on the feedback machinery would be associated with a lower likelihood of disease recurrence.

Another cellular response to the Erk1/2 inhibitors, which should be taken into consideration, is the induction of p38. Stress/MAP kinases are known to be activated by non-targeted drugs62,112,129,130,131, and it is currently not clear to us what exactly leads to their activation by the targeted Erk inhibitors. This matter seems complex to reveal, as the mechanism may be cell-specific. Indirect inhibition of Erk, by EGFR and MEK inhibitors, did not cause p38 activation in the NIH3T3-derived clones, but did in some cancer-derived cell lines. It could be that these inhibitors would not activate p38 in cells in which their targets are not very active, such as NIH3T3 cells. This notion cannot be taken as a general rule, however, because, for example, Vemurafenib did not induce p38 phosphorylation in Mel1617 cells that express BRafV600E. Another puzzle with respect to p38 activation is its effect on the cell. It seems not to lead to apoptosis when induced by Erk inhibitors, as it does in response to non-targeted drugs.62 Perhaps it contributes to the activation of cell cycle components that impose the G1 arrest observed in cells treated with Erk inhibitors.132,133 Some cancer cells, mainly of late tumors, may be protected from p38-induced cell death, even when non-targeted drugs are used, due to the elevated expression of heat shock proteins (HSPs).134 It would be interesting to assess the status of the classical stress response (induction of HSPs and other stress-associated proteins) in response to Erk inhibitors, because, if elevated, it may neutralize the effect of p38 in these cells as well.

Our proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses consistently identified modifications in the Rho GTPase signaling as prominent and specific responses to BVD523 treatment in NIH3T3/Erk1R84S and NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells. A straightforward explanation for the effect on the Rho pathway would be that once BVD523 lifts the negative feedback imposed by active Erks on the RTK–Ras–Raf–MEK cascade, re-activated components, mainly Ras, would regulate Rho.135 The observed changes in the Rho GTPase module in response to Erk’s inhibition are in accordance with the results of Klomp et al.136 They found that the Erk-dependent phosphoproteome in KRAS-mutant pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cell lines is enriched in cell-cycle and Rho GTPase pathways.136 The observations presented here and those of Klomp et al.,136 calls for further investigation of the Rho GTPase cascade in response to anti-cancer treatment.

The enrichment of phosphoproteins and genes (e.g., ANLN, ARHGAP28, MAPK3, CLASP1, SH3PXD2A) that are associated with actin remodeling, cytokinesis, and focal adhesion dynamics further points to functions that are regulated by Rho signaling. This could indicate a phenotypic shift toward cytoskeletal plasticity and adhesion remodeling in response to Erk inhibition.

In summary, this study emphasizes the effect of Erk inhibitors on the negative feedback machinery, the lack of total cell death by these inhibitors, and activation of the stress-responsive MAPK p38. It further emphasizes the necessity to monitor directly Erk catalytic activity and not to rely on phosphorylation levels when assessing Erk’s status in cells or tumors. The Erk-specific AssayQuant method seems optimal for this purpose.

Limitations of the study

The study presented here was performed on cell lines, and the findings must be assessed in vivo. It should be noted, however, that the initial drive of the study was observations in mice in which intrinsically active Erk caused hepatocellular carcinoma and suppression of Erk phosphorylation, suggesting some degree of equivalence between the in vitro and in vivo models. With the availability of the highly specific and accurate kinase assay used here, AssayQuant, it should be possible to monitor Erk activity in vivo, in the course of tumor development, and also to compare the activity to that in the tumor environment and in the healthy tissue. Another limitation is the lack of a mechanistic explanation for the status of Erk in some cancer cell lines. For example, why Erk’s phosphorylation and activity are so low in HeLa cells (regardless of the presence of an inhibitor) and so high (even at the basal level) in MDA-MB-231 cells.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and cell culture

The following NIH3T3 clones were used throughout the study: NIH3T3/empty, NIH3T3/Erk1WT, NIH3T3/Erk1R84S and NIH3T3/Erk1R84H cells. These clones were developed in our laboratory, and relevant plasmid construction, transfection and isolation of stable clones were previously described.18 The human cell lines HeLa, Mel1617, A549, NCI-H1975 and HepG2 were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium high glucose with sodium Bicarbonate and L-glutamine (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich D5796). MDA-MB-231, MCF7, BT474 and PC3 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute Medium with L-glutamine (RPMI, Gibco) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum Heat Inactivated (FBS HI, Gibco), Sodium Pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich), Penicillin-Streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) and Plasmocin prophylactic (InvivoGen). All cells were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2 and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination. HeLa cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), while all other cells from the collection of Levitzki’s laboratory.

Method details

Drug testing on cell lines

24-h post seeding of 1 million cells per plate of 6 well plates (Greiner) cells were treated with either fresh medium or medium containing DMSO or a drug, as specified in each experiment, for 2, 16, or 72 h. Note that in Soudah et al.18 only 250,000 cells were plated prior to treatment with drugs, hence some differences in the degree of the cells’ response to the drug (see ref. 18 and Figure S1). Drugs were purchased from Selleckchem: BVD523 (catalog number S7854), GDC0994 (S7554), SCH772984 (S7101), ASN007 (S9975), Temuterkib (S8534), Trametinib (S2673), Tarceva (S7786), Vemurafenib (S1267) and Cisplatin (S1166). After incubations with drugs, cells were either lysed with sample buffer for western blot analysis, or collected after Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) treatment for cell cycle analysis. Experiments were performed at least 3 times. For clonal viability assay following treatment with BVD523 cells were exposed to the drugs for 72 h, counted with hemocytometer, and 1,000, or 5,000 cells were re-plated on fresh plates and grown in drug-free medium. Number of colonies that appeared was counted 10 days later.

Cell cycle analysis

One million cells were washed twice with PBS then incubated at 4°C for 30 min in 2 mL of 70% ethanol for fixation and then washed again with 2 mL PBS. Cells were then treated with 50 μL of 100 μg/mL RNase A for 30 min at 37°C. Propidium iodide (50 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, P4864) was added to each sample before running flow cytometer analysis (FACS) using CellStream analyzer flow cytometer sorter (BD Biosciences). Voltage setting for forward and side scatter, and fluorescence channels were kept constant for all experiments. Data were recorded for up to 10,000 cells from each sample and analysis with FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences). Experiments were performed 3 times and a representative experiment is shown. t test was done in Excel - 2-tailed - type 2- Two-sample equal variance.

Western blots

Protein concentration in lysates was determined using Protein Quantification Assay (MACHEREY-NAGAL). 5 or 10 μg of protein lysates were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad) using Trans-Blot Turbo transfer system (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST, washed with TBST then incubated with primary antibodies (diluted in 5% BSA in TBST) overnight in 4°C. Day later blots were washed with TBST then incubated with secondary antibodies in 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST. Antibody-Protein interaction was detected using SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher). Signals were visualized by Fusion pulse imaging system and densitometry was quantified using ImageJ. Antibodies used in this study are provided in the key resources table above.

Kinase assay

Erk1/2 kinase activity was measured using a continuous, fluorescence-based PhosphoSens assay (PhosphoSens ERK1/2 Cell Lysate Kinase Assay; AssayQuant Technologies; catalog #AQT-AQT1076-KL-100), which directly monitors phosphorylation of a sensor peptide substrate in real time throughout the enzymatic reaction. Cell lysates were prepared according to manufacture instructions: Cells were collected in Cell Extraction Buffer (CEB) containing 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EGTA, 30 mM NaF, 10 mM Na4P2O7, 1% Triton X-100, Sigma Protease Inhibitor cocktail (#P8340) 10%; Sigma Phosphatase Inhibitor cocktail (#P285) 1%. 100 μL of CEB was used for each well in 6 well plates. Cells were sonicated on ice for 5 s and clear cell lysate was obtained after centrifuging at 6000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Cell lysate samples were monitored for concentration (Protein Quantification Assay kit, Macherey-Nagel, Germany) and immediately assayed for kinase activity.

Final reaction conditions: Protein kinase activity was determined in 54 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 1 mM ATP, 1.2 mM DTT, 0.012% Brij-35, 0.52 mM EGTA, 10 mM MgCl2, 1% glycerol, 0.2 mg/ml BSA, and 10 μM Sox-based sensor. A protein kinase sensor (AQTx) was used to measure the kinase activity of ERK1/2 (AQT0376). All components except enzyme were equilibrated to 30 °C prior to setting up reactions in 96-well half-area, white, flat and round-bottom NBS microplates (Corning #3824). For each reaction (50 μL final volume), 5 μL Sox-based substrate (10×) was first mixed with 35 μL Reaction Mix containing ATP & DTT (1.43×), followed by a 5 min preincubation (all components except buffer control or cell lysate) at 30 °C. This was followed by addition of 10 μL CEB (1×) or crude cell lysate (5× in CEB) or 40 μg of crude cell lysate per reaction. Actual final reaction concentrations containing CEB were 10 mM TRIS, pH 7.5, 30 mM NaCl, 6 mM NaF, 2 mM Na4P2O7, 20 μM Na3VO4, 0.2% Triton X-100, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, Sigma Protease Inhibitor cocktail (#P8340) 2%, and Sigma Phosphatase Inhibitor cocktail (#P285) 2%. Plates were sealed using optically clear adhesive film (TopSealA-Plus plate seal, PerkinElmer, applied with a roller) to eliminate evaporation and resulting drift and then fluorescence intensity measurements were read kinetically every 2 min from the top for up to 120 min at 30 °C, with excitation and emission wavelengths of 360 nm and 485 nm, respectively, using a Synergy H1 multimode plate reader (Biotek Instruments, Winooski, VT).

Data analysis: Fluorescence, determined with identical reaction conditions was subtracted from the total fluorescence signal for each time point, with both determined in duplicate, to obtain corrected relative fluorescence units (RFU). Corrected RFU values then were plotted vs. time and the reaction velocity for the first ∼40 min (initial reaction rates) were determined from the slope with units of RFU/min or RFU/min/μg.

Proteomics, mass spectrometry

Sample preparation, Phosphopeptide enrichment and Liquid chromatography were performed as previously described.18 Data processing and analysis followed the same protocol with a few modifications, as detailed below.

In total, 5467 proteins and 17,383 phosphorylation sites (across 3789 proteins) were identified and quantified. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the Limma package in R,138 following logarithmic transformation of the data. Missing values were imputed with a bounded minimum value. Differentially expressed proteins were identified using a cutoff of absolute fold change >2 and p-value <0.05, with a requirement of at least two peptides detected by mass spectrometry. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using enrichR116,117,118 to identify significantly relevant biological pathways. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE139 partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD069633.

Quantification and analysis of western blot, FACS and kinase assay results are described in each relevant methods above.
