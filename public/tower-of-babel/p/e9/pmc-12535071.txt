Obstruction of the pharyngeal airway space after correction surgery in the cervical spine for dropped head syndrome
Authors: Hiroshi Miyamoto, Terumasa Ikeda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative dysphagia has been reported to occur as the result of narrowing of the pharyngeal airway space (PAS) due to a change in the alignment of the cervical spine. This study aimed to investigate potential risk factors for postoperative PAS obstruction following corrective surgery for dropped head syndrome (DHS) by analyzing clinical and radiographic parameters.MethodsThis retrospective design included 42 patients (7 men, 35 women; mean age, 71.5 years) who underwent corrective surgery for DHS. The following measurements were obtained: narrowest PAS (nPAS), cervical vertebra 2 (C2)–7 angle, occipito (O)–C1 angle, C1–2 angle, and swallowing line (S-line). The correlations between the postoperative nPAS and clinical and radiographic factors were assessed. Logistic regression analysis was used to analyze the specific factors for the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia. ROC analysis was also used to determine the cut-off values.P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultsThe mean preoperative nPAS was 15.0 ± 4.1 mm, which significantly decreased to 11.4 ± 3.6 mm postoperatively. The mean preoperative O-C1 angle was 7.8 ± 7.1°, which decreased to −7.2 ± 6.6° postoperatively. The mean pre- and postoperative C1–2 angles were 38.9 ± 5.8° and 34.8 ± 5.8°, respectively. Among this cohort, the mean nPAS of patients with S-line(-) was significantly smaller (8.5 ± 2.5 mm) than that of those with S-line(+) (11.7 ± 3.6 mm). Postoperative nPAS was correlated with O–C1 angle (r = 0.55, P < 0.015). Forward stepwise multivariate logistic regression showed that C0-1 angle (odds ratio (OR: 5.774) was the most impactable factor for the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia. ROC analysis revealed that the cut-off value for the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia was nPAS of 10.78 mm.ConclusionThe reduction of the O–C1 angle as a reciprocal change after the surgical correction of mid/lower cervical spine kyphosis for DHS may be a key factor in the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia due to airway obstruction.Keywords: Dropped head syndrome, Surgery, Dysphagia, Complication, Risk factor

Background

Corrective surgery for dropped head syndrome (DHS) is a challenging procedure requiring extensive realignment of the cervical spine and is associated with a high rate of complications [1]. Postoperative dysphagia is a devastating complication that threatens life support, similar to bladder and bowel disturbances, although its occurrence is a well-known complication after anterior approach procedures of the cervical spine [2–5]. Potential risk factors for postoperative dysphagia after anterior approach procedures include intraoperative esophageal traction [2], longer surgical time [3], multilevel surgery [2], soft tissue swelling [4], cervical plate use [3], and recurrent laryngeal nerve damage [5]. Most cases of postoperative dysphagia or airway-related complications were observed in patients treated for DHS with combined (anterior and posterior) approaches [1].

Postoperative dysphagia may also occur in patients who have undergone a posterior realignment after occipitocervical fusion (OCF) [6, 7]. The reduced postoperative occipito (O)–cervical vertebra 2 (C2) angle compared to the preoperative value is a risk factor for postoperative dysphagia caused by narrowing of the pharyngeal airway space (PAS) between the tongue root and the posterior pharyngeal wall [7]. The protrusion of the apex of cervical lordosis anterior to the S-line, which passes through the center of the C1 anterior arch perpendicular to McGregor’s line, is another risk factor for postoperative dysphagia due to the narrowing of the PAS [6].

Sakai et al. [8] reported that the inhibition of neck motion due to the surgical fixation of the mid/lower cervical spine, either the anterior or posterior approach and alignment correction were risk factors for the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia. However, they did not mention the narrowing or obstruction of the PAS.

Miyamoto [9] reported the case of a 68-year-old female patient with DHS who suffered from severe postoperative dysphagia after a massive corrective surgery for mid/lower cervical kyphosis and was rescued by surgically downgrading the cervical lordosis from 20 to 10°. He reported that patients who suffer from a long duration of DHS with rigid cervical kyphosis are more likely to be contracted at C1–2 in the hyperlordotic alignment and that the O-C1 angle reduction due to postoperative reciprocal changes can cause narrowing of the PAS, subsequently resulting in postoperative dysphagia.

Thus, there are two hypotheses to cause postoperative dysphagia after correction surgery for DHS: the contribution of the anterior procedure and/or alignment alteration.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore potential risk factors for postoperative PAS obstruction after corrective surgery for DHS by analyzing clinical and radiographic parameters obtained from patients with DHS who underwent surgery at our institution.

Methods

The study protocol was approved by our institutional ethics committee and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A single surgeon (H.M.) performed corrective surgeries for DHS on 42 consecutive patients (7 men, 35 women; mean age, 71.5 years) at our institution from 2013 to 2023. The mean disease duration was 17.8 months (1–120 months).

The therapeutic indication was difficulty achieving horizontal gaze, causing a severe disruption of quality of life. Conservative treatment was first attempted, which included the application of a soft collar, active range of motion exercises, and the administration of analgesics for 2–3 months. A consultation with a neurologist was also conducted, and when neuromuscular diseases were identified, the initial treatments for these diseases were implemented. One patient was diagnosed with multiple system atrophy, one with myositis, and one with Parkinson’s disease; however, the medication did not affect the spinal malalignment. The remaining 39 patients were idiopathic. Ankylosing spondylitis, infection, neoplasm, and those who had prior history of cervical spine surgery and head and neck radiation therapy were excluded [10]. Surgery was only considered if the conservative treatment failed and written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the intervention. The extent of fixation was C2–7 or C2-thoracic vertebra 1 (T1) in 29 patients (short fusion) and involvement of the cervicothoracic spine (long fusion) in 13 [11], while no OCF was required. The surgical procedure used a combined anteroposterior (AP) approach in 19 patients and a posterior-only (P) approach in 23 [12]. Briefly, in patients where kyphosis was reducible in the extension position, posterior surgery was performed. The cervical pedicle screws were inserted as appropriate. Decompression was not necessary without spinal stenosis, but in cases of myelopathy or spinal stenosis, posterior decompression was performed before kyphosis correction using a Cantilever although accompanying with cervical myelopathy is not so many in DHS. Conversely, when kyphosis was irreducible, an anteroposterior combined procedure was essential; anterior release and cage insertion, mostly at two disc levels of kyphosis beak, was firstly performed in the prone position, and secondly, cervical pedicle screw insertion, and posterior correction using Cantilever were performed in supine position with Mayfield skull cramp [12]. All 42 patients were able to gaze horizontally after surgery.

Videoendoscopic evaluation (VE) of swallowing was performed in the recent 25 of 42 patients in this case series. Briefly, the VE scoring system (Hyodo score) consisted of four parameters: (1) salivary pooling at the vallecula and piriform sinuses, (2) glottal closure reflex induction by touching the epiglottis or arytenoid with the endoscope, (3) swallowing reflex initiation assessed by “white-out” timing, and (4) pharyngeal clearance after swallowing blue-dyed water. These four parameters were each scored as 0–3 on a 4-point scale (0, normal; 1, mildly impaired; 2, moderately impaired; 3, severely impaired). The total score was expressed as the sum of scores for each of the four parameters, ranging from 0 to 12. Patients with a score < 5 were determined to have normal swallowing function, whereas patients with a score >8 were determined to have a severe disturbance in swallowing function [13, 14]. There was no difference between VE and non-VE groups in terms of age, gender, duration of disease, and surgical procedure. VE was re-examined in patients who complained of postoperative dysphagia at the same day as onset in the same fashion. The narrowest PAS (nPAS) was measured pre- and postoperatively on the standing lateral radiograph [15], from which the pre- to postoperative change in nPAS was calculated. The pre- and post-operative and transition (post-minus-pre-operative) C2–7, O–C1, C1–2, and O–C2 angles and C2–7 distance, as well as S-line positive (+) or negative (-), were determined from the lateral upright whole spine. The C2–7 angle was the angle formed by the lines of the caudad endplates of C2 and C7; the O–C1 angle was that formed by McGregor’s line and the line passing through the middle of the anterior and posterior arches of the axis; the C1–2 angle was that formed by the line passing through the middle of the anterior and posterior arches of the axis and the line of the caudad endplate of C2; and the O–C2 angle was that formed by McGregor’s line and the line of the caudad endplates of C2. The C2–7 distance was the distance between the antero-caudad edges of C2 and C7.

The correlations between the postoperative nPAS and clinical parameters such as age, sex, duration of disease, and surgical approach (AP combined or P) were evaluated, as were those between the postoperative nPAS and the aforementioned radiographic characteristics. The pre- and postoperative nPAS values were statistically compared using the student t-test, as were the nPAS values between groups based on the following: sex (man/woman), surgical procedure (AP/P), extent of fixation (short/long), and S-line (+/-). Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the comparisons between postoperative nPAS and the clinical and radiographic parameters. Data were shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Logistic regression analysis was used to analyze the specific factors for the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia amongst postoperative O-C1 angle, C1-2 angle, C2-7 angle, age, duration of disease, and surgical procedure. ROC analysis was also used to examine the postoperative nPAS for the the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia and to determine the cut-off values. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The initial series of patients in the present study in whom VE had not been performed did not complain of dysphagia either pre- or post-operation. All 25 patients who underwent preoperative VE were also categorized as having normal swallowing function. However, four of them complained of postoperative dysphagia, with VE scores of 11 (3-2-3-3), 10 (3-2-2–3), 7 (1-1-2–3), and 7 (1-1-2–3) points. One patient who underwent the combined AP approach fully recovered after a revision surgery 2 months after the initial surgery in which cervical lordosis was downgraded from 20 to 10° [9]. Another patient who underwent P approach needed tracheotomy and gastrostomy due to severe dysphagia. She refused the downgrading surgery, and the situation was the same in 2 years after surgery. Two patients suffered transient dysphagia after the combined AP approach but made a complete recovery within a month without further intervention.

Demographic data are shown in Table 1. The mean preoperative nPAS was 15.0 ± 4.1 mm, which decreased significantly to 11.4 ± 3.6 mm postoperatively. The pre- to postoperative nPAS transition was − 3.8 ± 3.2 mm. The mean preoperative C2–7 angle of −38.4 ± 29.1° was corrected to 15.1 ± 11.3° (51.6 ± 23.2° correction) postoperatively. The mean preoperative O–C1 angle was 7.8 ± 7.1°, which decreased to −7.2 ± 6.6° postoperatively, with a mean transition of −15.0 ± 10.2°. The mean pre- and postoperative C1–2 angles were 38.9 ± 5.8° and 34.8 ± 5.8°, respectively, with a transition of −3.7 ± 5.3°, indicating that the C1–2 angle was still in a hyperlordotic position, likely due to the contracture [9]. The mean preoperative O–C2 angle was 45.5 ± 15.5°, which decreased to 27.9 ± 9.2° postoperatively, with a transition of −17.1 ± 16.8°. The mean preoperative C2–7 distance was 65.9 ± 10.3 mm, which increased to 77.9 ± 6.0 mm postoperatively, with a transition of 14.1 ± 13.3 mm.

Discussion

Most cases of postoperative dysphagia or airway-related complications for DHS treatment were reported to be related to the anterior approach, not the change in alignment [1]. However, the alignment after corrective surgery for DHS was certainly related to the occurrence of dysphagia, as evidenced by the 69-year-old woman with DHS who experienced the immediate relief of severe dysphagia after the downgrading surgery, which decreased the cervical lordosis from 20 to 10° [9]. This study’s results indicated that the preoperative nPAS value in patients with DHS was wider than the postoperative value, and no dysphagia was found. However, nPAS decreased by a mean of 4 mm after correcting the kyphosis surgically in DHS treatment.

The craniovertebral junction provides a compensation mechanism when attempting to obtain a horizontal gaze by allowing the upper cervical spine to become extremely hyperlordotic if the patient is unable to gaze horizontally [16]. However, whether O–C1 or C1–2 is more important in this compensatory mechanism remains unclear. The results of the present study indicated that the mean preoperative C1–2 angle was large (38.9°), which remained even after surgery (34.8°, Table 1). Miyamoto [9] reported that the specific radiographic findings in DHS implicating contracture between C1 and C2 were observed, such as osteophyte formation between the C1 anterior arch and dens, C1 anterior arch extending past the tip of the dens, and pseudoarthrosis formation between the C1 posterior arch and C2 lamina. Contrarily, the present study revealed that the O–C1 angle shifted to kyphosis with a mean range of 15° before and after surgery. That is, reciprocal change likely occurred at O–C1 rather than C1–2 in patients with DHS who underwent the surgical correction of kyphosis in the mid/lower cervical spine (Fig. 2). Moreover, the present study revealed that the postoperative O–C1 angle was correlated with postoperative nPAS (Fig. 1). That is, a reduction in the O–C1 angle can cause a decrease in nPAS, likely related to the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia. Cut-off value of nPAS causing dysphagia was 10.78 mm.

The present study also revealed that S-line (-) was a risk factor causing narrower nPAS, although none of the S-line (-) cases exhibited postoperative difficulty in swallowing in this cohort. The S-line crossed over the anterior margin of the C2 vertebra in all negative cases (Fig. 3). This is not the same as the “pure” S-line (-), which crosses over the protrusion of the apex of cervical lordosis in the mid/lower cervical spine [6]. These S-line (-) findings of the present series indicate that reducing the O–C1 angle and the contracture at C1–2 in hyper-lordosis may have resulted in the S-line crossing over the anterior margin of the C2 vertebra.

This study has some limitations. This study was a retrospective design, with a small sample size (42 patients, VE in 25) and a single-surgeon approach, which may have introduced bias and underpowered the study, thereby limiting its generalizability. A prospective, multi-center randomized control trial with larger cohorts and extended monitoring is recommended to validate findings and enhance clinical applicability.

The mechanism of postoperative dysphagia in the cervical spine is no doubt multifactorial. The patients with postoperative PAS narrowing occasionally did not complain of dysphagia. This may be explained by the fact that older adults habitually adjust their neck position, such as neck protrusion and/or chin-down [17], although such compensatory mechanisms are limited because surgery fixes the range of motion of the neck. However, the author believes that airway obstruction can be a significant cause of postoperative airway complications in DHS treatment. Understanding the risk factors that cause PAS narrowing is essential to avoid such catastrophic complications.

Conclusions

In conclusion, reduced nPAS, along with a decreased O–C1 angle resulting from realignment at the mid/lower cervical spine, may be a cause of postoperative dysphagia after corrective surgery for DHS. Overcorrection of the cervical alignment should be avoided, especially in cases with contracture at C1–2 in the hyperlordotic alignment.
