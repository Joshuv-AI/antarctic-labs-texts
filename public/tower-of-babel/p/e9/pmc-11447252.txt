Deep learning-derived optimal aviation strategies to control pandemics
Authors: Syed Rizvi, Akash Awasthi, Maria J Peláez, Zhihui Wang, Vittorio Cristini, Hien Van Nguyen, Prashant Dogra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 pandemic affected countries across the globe, demanding drastic public health policies to mitigate the spread of infection, which led to economic crises as a collateral damage. In this work, we investigate the impact of human mobility, described via international commercial flights, on COVID-19 infection dynamics on a global scale. We developed a graph neural network (GNN)-based framework called Dynamic Weighted GraphSAGE (DWSAGE), which operates over spatiotemporal graphs and is well-suited for dynamically changing flight information updated daily. This architecture is designed to be structurally sensitive, capable of learning the relationships between edge features and node features. To gain insights into the influence of air traffic on infection spread, we conducted local sensitivity analysis on our model through perturbation experiments. Our analyses identified Western Europe, the Middle East, and North America as leading regions in fueling the pandemic due to the high volume of air traffic originating or transiting through these areas. We used these observations to propose air traffic reduction strategies that can significantly impact controlling the pandemic with minimal disruption to human mobility. Our work provides a robust deep learning-based tool to study global pandemics and is of key relevance to policymakers for making informed decisions regarding air traffic restrictions during future outbreaks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73639-7.Keywords: COVID-19, Pandemic, Deep learning, Graph neural network, Artificial intelligence, Aviation policySubject terms: Computational science, Viral infection

Introduction

In December 2019, the first COVID-19 cases were detected in the Wuhan region of China, from where the SARS-CoV-2 virus rapidly spread worldwide, infecting people and causing the World Health Organization to declare COVID-19 a pandemic in March 20201. Since neither vaccines nor therapeutic drugs were available at the onset of the pandemic, governments around the world responded by implementing stringent public health policies to control the spread of infection. These measures included, but were not limited to, social distancing, use of face masks, and travel restrictions.

In response to this, researchers sought to explore the impact of public health policies and human mobility on pandemic dynamics, leveraging data analytics and modeling-based studies. Kraemer et al. analyzed the effectiveness of public health interventions in China during the early stages of the pandemic, concluding that the interventions significantly mitigated the early spread of COVID-192. International channels of human mobility were also examined to explain early pandemic dynamics; Adiga et al. utilized international air traffic data as a mobility indicator and assessed the effectiveness of flight cancellation policies on the time of arrival of the pandemic to other countries3.

Further works used mechanistic modeling, machine learning, and deep learning-based approaches to forecast pandemic dynamics and evaluate the impact of public health policies on infection incidence. Chang et al. and Yang et al. integrated population mobility data into epidemiological models to account for the role of population movement in driving the evolution of the pandemic, with the goal of guiding public policy4,5. Chimmula et al. utilized a deep learning-based approach, applying LSTM6 networks to forecast Canadian COVID-19 cases and predict the possible stopping points of the pandemic7. In a novel approach, Dogra et al. leveraged the Elliott Wave principle of financial mathematics to explain and forecast the trends in global COVID-19 cases based on human emotion as a driving factor of the pandemic8. However, a major limitation of the above works is that they do not incorporate the complexity of spatial relationships and interactions between different geographical locations in determining the outcomes of the pandemic.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) provide a deep learning-based framework for representing and capturing rich relational information among elements in a network or graph. Because of its flexibility, this framework is suitable for studying the influence of global population mobility on COVID-19 infection dynamics. Spatiotemporal GNNs are a special class of GNNs that simultaneously consider spatial and temporal information, and have been widely applied to problems such as traffic forecasting9–17 and intelligent networking solutions18. Kapoor et al. examined county-level COVID-19 forecasting within the United States by constructing 100 large-scale graph snapshots of US counties, with nodes representing counties and edges representing human mobility between nodes on each day19. Other works20,21 constructed models with GNN and LSTM layers to capture both spatial and temporal dependencies in data and predict future cases. Sesti et al. devised a GNN and LSTM-based architecture that operated over a static adjacency graph derived using geographical proximity, with edge attributes representing the social connectivity between different countries20. Panagopoulos et al., on the other hand, applied a Message-Passing Neural Network (MPNN) to each graph snapshot of an input window, and used a concatenation-based skip connection of input features at each timestep along with LSTM layer outputs to predict future cases21. We find this approach results in a model which depends heavily on input features rather than spatial information to predict future cases. To create a model that utilizes more structural information, we instead use a skip connection based on structural embeddings output from GraphSAGE layers. This results in an architecture more sensitive to changes in edge weights reflecting flight connections.

In this work, we present the Dynamic Weighted GraphSAGE (DWSAGE) architecture, a deep learning-based modeling approach for analyzing mobility-driven regional impact on pandemic dynamics. Our work differs from previous works in that we utilize aviation data as an indicator of international human mobility in the COVID-19 pandemic, using edge weights to model dynamic flight connections between regions. We propose a modified GraphSAGE layer which, in contrast to approaches with static edge features, incorporates dynamically changing edge weights which allow our architecture to directly accept dynamic flight information that varies on a day-to-day basis. In addition, we propose a sensitivity analysis framework to quantify the impact of nodes with high aviation traffic on global infection dynamics. Existing explainability methods22,23 for GNNs are defined over static graphs, however we define our perturbation framework over spatiotemporal graphs with dynamic edge features. We quantify sensitivity by measuring the reduction in forecasted COVID-19 cases in other regions when a given region has its flights reduced. We then use these insights to simulate and identify tangible strategies for limiting aviation to reduce the impact of highly influential regions.

Methods

DWSAGE model development

To address the challenges of modeling dynamically changing edge attributes in spatiotemporal graphs, we introduced a novel deep learning architecture, referred to as Dynamic Weighted GraphSAGE (DWSAGE), which utilizes the GraphSAGE message-passing framework24 to learn spatial relationships between nodes in our graph. As shown in Fig. 1, DWSAGE is a recurrent graph architecture composed of GraphSAGE and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) layers, which exploit the spatial and temporal information present in the data. The input for DWSAGE at timestep t is a fully connected, weighted, directed graph Gt, which comprises of ten nodes representing the partitioning of the globe into ten geographical locations of interest in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. The ten nodes are North America, South America, Oceania (i.e., Australia and neighboring island nations), Africa (Egypt excluded), Middle East (includes Egypt), Eastern Europe, Western Europe, Central Asia, South Asia, and Southeast Asia. DWSAGE uses timeseries data for daily COVID-19 infections and international flights for the ten nodes, obtained from public databases25–27. For the one-day graph input at timestep t, the infections count represents each node’s feature, while the number of flights between geographical regions are used to weigh the edges. We start with a fully connected graph to ensure that messages are always received from neighboring nodes. Weighing edges with flight weights allows our architecture to incorporate edge features that are based on real-world phenomena into learned node representations. This increases the expressive power of the architecture, allowing it to distinguish nodes from one other. We denote the cost matrix of edge weights between all pairs of nodes and as .

DWSAGE model architecture. A one-day graph Gt is input into the model, consisting of a set of node features Xt (daily COVID-19 cases) and adjacency information At (daily flight data). Two sequential GraphSAGE layers process the data to learn structural information about the input graph, which is then fed into two stacked LSTM cells that learn structural relationships over time. After processing seven days of input, the LSTM cell hidden state outputs (, ) are concatenated with output structural embeddings from the first GraphSAGE layer and passed to the final output layer to predict the next day cases for each node ().

We chose to use directed graphs in our DWSAGE model to accurately capture the directionality and asymmetry of flight traffic between regions. While flights operate in both directions, factors such as differing passenger loads, flight schedules, layovers, and operational asymmetries can create significant differences in the movement patterns. Directed graphs enable us to model these nuances effectively, providing a more precise representation of the potential spread of infections. This approach allows for better-targeted intervention strategies based on the specific dynamics of each flight route.

Additive GraphSAGE operator

In contrast to transductive approaches28, GraphSAGE computes node embeddings in an inductive manner by performing an aggregation operation (such as mean or max) on a sampled set of neighboring nodes and passing the results through a learned nonlinear transformation24. This allows for new nodes to be introduced into the graph by applying the learned aggregator function on neighboring nodes, opening possibilities for scalability to large graphs and networks.

On a given GraphSAGE layer , GraphSAGE works by sampling a neighborhood of connected nodes around the current node , and aggregating their embeddings using a permutation-invariant pooling operation , e.g., mean. The aggregated neighbor embedding is then concatenated with the node’s own embedding from the previous layer , and fed through a learned embedding function to obtain the node embedding for the th layer. For the current application of capturing flight-driven pandemic dynamics, we needed to account for both direct and long-distance connecting flights. This is accomplished by using two GraphSAGE layers, allowing for node information to propagate beyond the immediate (or, nearest) neighborhood of a node. The original GraphSAGE operator does not incorporate edge weights into the learned node representations. Previous works building upon the GraphSAGE operator20,29 propose to incorporate edge weights in a multiplicative fashion during message passing. We find that this leads to dying messages when multiplying with edge weights close to zero. The GraphSAGE framework includes an optional L2-Normalization step to prevent the magnitude of node embeddings from becoming too large. We omit L2-normalization from DWSAGE in favor of using a separate normalization layer, as described next, since we observed that it improves training convergence.

GraphNorm

Internal covariate shift31 can slow down the training convergence of deep neural networks, due to the changing input distribution for neural layers as the parameters of previous layers are updated. Proposed normalization layers for deep neural networks31–33 address this problem by shifting and scaling the distribution of activations by its mean and standard deviation and introducing learned parameters to shift and scale output activations, allowing the operation to possibly assume the identity transform. Normalization layers for GNNs have been proposed34–38, aiming to address the internal covariate shift and oversmoothing challenges in GNNs.

In DWSAGE, we use GraphNorm34 layers on the output activations of each GraphSAGE layer. GraphNorm is a modification of Instance Normalization33 which uses a learnable shift rather than subtracting out the total mean statistics of node embeddings, with the intuition that some information may be contained in the statistics of the node embeddings. LSTM

To exploit temporal dependencies across a sequence of one-day graphs, we utilize LSTM cells6 and learn about spatial relationships in daily infections from previous timepoints. Specifically, we process the output embeddings of the two GraphSAGE layers (containing spatial information) for each daily timestep using two stacked LSTM cells (Fig. 1). LSTMs are a class of recurrent neural networks that control the flow of information using gates. A cell state and hidden state are maintained across the sequence, which capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of the pandemic. Parameter updates in recurrent networks are done using the backpropagation through time (BPTT) algorithm39 or truncated BPTT40 in the case of long sequences where information in error gradients would become diluted. We use two LSTM layers in a stacked fashion to learn more abstract features in the input series of graphs, and empirically find that including a second LSTM layer improves recursive predictions from the model.

Although Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), with their simpler architecture of two gates (update gate and reset gate), are faster to train and more computationally efficient, they may sacrifice some expressiveness in modeling long-term dependencies compared to LSTM41. Transformers also offer robust performance for time series forecasting but generally require larger datasets for effective training42. Given the constraints of our current dataset, which is not sufficiently large for Transformers, LSTMs presented a more practical solution. Additionally, LSTMs are more suitable than CNNs in this context, as CNNs excel in spatial feature extraction but are less adept at managing long temporal sequences. Thus, to capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of the pandemic effectively, we employed LSTM cells and learned from the spatial information provided by GraphSAGE layers across daily timesteps.

Skip connections

After multiple iterations of message passing, GNNs often suffer from a problem known as oversmoothing43–45, where the embeddings for different nodes become too similar. This occurs when the receptive fields (K-hop neighborhood) of different nodes become highly overlapped46. To prevent oversmoothing in DWSAGE, we make two design choices: (i) we set the number of GNN layers equal to the most common graph diameter (two) found on daily input graphs in our dataset47, and (ii) introduce a concatenation-based skip connection48 from the first GraphSAGE layer output to the LSTM, so that both one-hop and two-hop information are passed to the LSTM Cells. Including a skip connection here effectively results in learning a mixture of one and two-hop models49,50, and has been successfully applied in other works to prevent over smoothing in GNNs19,51.

As embeddings pass through the LSTM layers, the effect of structural information from earlier layers may be reduced48. To ensure that our model is sensitive to the structural information in input graphs, we introduce a second skip connection in DWSAGE, passing structural information from the first GraphSAGE layer to the final multilayer perceptron (MLP) classifier as shown in Fig. 1. Output structural embeddings on each day () are concatenated along with the output of the two LSTM cells ( and ) at the last timestep and used to predict the next-day cases. This skip connection results in more direct gradient updates for the first GraphSAGE layer. Additionally, in our application, it alleviates the distribution shift between LSTM cell outputs which are in range [−1, 1] and COVID-19 cases (> 0), which need to be non-negative numbers and can span across several orders of magnitude.

Data curation

To train our model, we used data from public databases involving daily COVID-19 infections and international flights. Flight data was obtained from the “Crowdsourced air traffic data from the Open Sky Network 2019–2020”27 which included data of over 41,900,660 flights from 13,934 airports in 127 different countries. The daily infection numbers across the various countries were obtained from “Our World in Data – Coronavirus Pandemic”26 which included various longitudinal data of deaths, cases, tests, vaccinations, hospitalizations, among others. For the node feature timeseries data, we summed-up the daily infection numbers across various countries belonging to a given node (Fig. S1); to obtain the dynamic, bidirectional edge weights, we aggregated daily international flights departing from or arriving to the various airports across a country (Fig. S2) and summed the numbers across countries in a given node (Fig. S3).

For data pre-processing, we first aligned the timespan of both data sources and deleted any days where information was missing or erroneous (negative values), resulting in a timeframe from March 1st, 2020, to September 30th, 2021, with intermittent gaps. We then smoothened the infection data using a 7-day moving average, after which we performed a log10 transformation to reduce the skewness in the dataset (Fig. S4). The flight data was not smoothened but was log10 transformed. A 64%/16%/20% split was used for the training/validation/test dataset, respectively, with the validation and test data lying subsequent to the training data in the timeseries.

To assess the impact of different aviation strategies on global case trends, we must be able to forecast COVID-19 cases over a period of time into the future. We train a many-to-one architecture and obtain a long-term forecast of length L by iteratively predicting one day into the future L times while feeding model predictions back to itself to make the input for predicting future timesteps. We refer to this strategy as recursive prediction, and define the long-term forecast of length L made in this fashion as a recursive prediction window.

Local sensitivity analysis

We first obtained 30 days of recursive predictions for all (W = 523) data input windows in the dataset, referred to hereafter as unperturbed recursive predictions. Perturbed recursive predictions were then obtained by setting the incoming and outgoing flight weights from a chosen node to zero, one node at a time, and again obtaining 30 days of recursive predictions. This yielded 10 sets of perturbed recursive predictions corresponding to the 10 nodes in our graph having their flight weights perturbed. Zeroing out flight weights effectively isolates or “locks down” the node, analogous to restricting flights to and from a geographical location. We define the impact that a geographical region has on global infections - hereafter referred to as its sensitivity - as the reduction in predicted cases observed across the other nine nodes and summed over 30 days of recursive predictions when that node’s flights are perturbed. Results

Model training, predictions, and bias correction

Our model aims to learn the dynamic spatiotemporal patterns in the global evolution of the COVID-19 pandemic, based on human mobility assessed through international air traffic data. Deep learning models exhibit stochasticity in their predictions due to differences in parameter initialization and training. We therefore train 100 DWSAGE models and consider average predictions for our numerical experiments and analyses. As shown in Fig. 2A, loss curves from the training of a representative DWSAGE model show convergence within 400 epochs, thereby minimizing the error between model predictions and ground truth. The tight coupling observed between the train and validation loss shows that the model generalizes well to new data, predicting COVID-19 cases accurately on validation data which comes chronologically later in the dataset than the training data. We use MPNN21 as described by Panagopoulos et al. as a reference for comparing the performance of the DWSAGE model, and provide corresponding training figures for MPNN in Fig. S9. MPNN consists of LSTM (16) and GCN (37) layers which process graph inputs representing a week of input data; each node contains the previous 7 days of COVID-19 cases as features, and an averaged graph structure over the 7 input days is input into MPNN as the adjacency information. We note that this is not as flexible as the dynamic modeling of international flights in DWSAGE, which can change by day to better model human mobility indicators. As shown in Fig. S10, MPNN models exhibit more variability in predictions over a 30-day recursive window, making sensitivity score calculations based on recursive predictions less reliable. For this reason, we do all further analysis using DWSAGE.

Sensitivity analysis with spatiotemporal edge weight perturbation

To obtain explainable predictions from our DWSAGE model, we need explainability methods that are well-defined over spatiotemporal graphs. Existing explainability methods, however, provide post-hoc explanations on static graphs, and do not extend to the case of spatiotemporal graphs with dynamically changing information on edges. To address this shortcoming, we define a novel edge feature perturbation framework on spatiotemporal graphs which perturbs edge weights and quantifies the relationship between edge features and node features in the graph.

Using the forecasting ability of our model, we predict future cases based on aviation trends and employ it to identify the impact that the international flights to and from a geographical region have on the infection trends in the rest of the globe. For this, we used sensitivity analysis where a “perturbation” of a single node in the network is imposed to quantify the total effect on other nodes in terms of reduction in predicted cases. We used the predictions from the previously trained 100 models to calculate a sensitivity score for each node across the duration of study (see Methods), with highly negative sensitivity scores indicating a larger reduction in predicted cases.

As shown in Fig. S6, the sensitivity score timeseries from 100 DCSAGE models indicates that more models predict more highly negative sensitivity scores for Western Europe and the Middle East, suggesting greater influence of these regions on infection trends around the globe. We verified this by performing increasingly large flight reductions on Western Europe as a representative node, which yielded higher sensitivity scores as more flights were reduced (Fig. S7). To quantify the average sensitivity score for nodes on each recursive prediction window, we fit an Extreme Value distribution to the predicted sensitivity scores of the 100 models on each recursive prediction window and estimated the parameter, which represents the mode of the distribution (Fig. S8). The value is then used to rank order the nodes in terms of their sensitivity. As shown in the area bump chart in Fig. 4A, for a major portion of the studied timeframe, Western Europe ranked the highest, closely followed by the Middle East. North America, Southeast-East Asia, and Eastern Europe follow in 3rd to 5th place during the pandemic, succeeded by South Asia, South America, and Oceania. Africa and Central Asia were at the bottom of the ranking list, suggesting low impact of these two nodes on the evolution of the pandemic. The timeseries results are summarized as violin plots in Fig. 4B, which rank ordered the distribution of values across the timeseries based on the median of the distribution. The observations are consistent with the trend observed in the timeseries plot ( values normalized between 0 and −1). We provide a world map with geographical regions colored by their averaged sensitivity score across the entire dataset in Fig. 4C. This gives a visual indication of which geographical regions are connected in proximity and have a large influence over other regions. We can see from the map that Western Europe and the Middle East have large influence over infection patterns due to their central location, reflected in their high sensitivity score.

Outgoing flights as a correlate of node impact

To understand the impact of human mobility and infection load on the global evolution of the pandemic, we evaluated the correlation between mobility and daily cases versus model-derived sensitivity scores. As shown in Fig. 5A, nodes with larger number of outgoing flights tend to have a more highly negative sensitivity score, while that is not necessarily true for the daily case count (Fig. 5B). For instance, Oceania (red) and South America (green) despite a huge disparity between their case count distributions over 567 days (Fig. 5B), have comparable sensitivity scores which can be attributed to their similar outgoing flight counts (Fig. 5A). Alternatively, Central Asia with larger number of daily cases than Oceania has a less negative sensitivity scores due to its lesser number of outbound flights affecting other nodes compared to Oceania. Similarly, South Asia has one of the largest numbers of daily COVID-19 cases at certain timepoints, however it is still superseded in terms of reductions in cases caused in other nodes by Europe, North America, and the Middle East due to the higher number of outgoing flights emanating from those regions.

With these observations, we can establish outgoing flights as a correlate of node impact or sensitivity. This is further corroborated by a strong Spearman correlation between outgoing flights and sensitivity scores for all the ten nodes pooled together (Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient = −0.95; Fig. 5A). This nonlinear correlation is characterized by a power law that is in good agreement with the raw data (Pearson correlation coefficient R = −0.87; Fig. 5C). It can thus be inferred that aviation trends hold the key to controlling the global evolution of the pandemic, and attention must be given to geographical regions that are aviation hubs, irrespective of the magnitude of their infection load. With the ability of our dynamic network model to use real-time human mobility data and identify the most influential geographical regions driving the pandemic, we can propose adjustments in policies relevant to international flights necessary to reduce forecasted cases.

Forecasting flight reduction effects on pandemic control

Based on our findings from the local sensitivity analysis (Fig. 4), we explored creating sets of flight reductions which, when applied to highly sensitivity nodes, would efficiently reduce predicted global cases. As a tangible proof-of-concept, we tested policies (363 total) that involved reductions in incoming and outgoing flights at different levels (25%, 50%, and 75%) in up to the five most sensitive nodes (Western Europe, North America, Middle East, Eastern Europe, and Southeast Asia). 10 out of the 100 previously trained models were used for this analysis (see Methods). (C) Scatter plot of policies including a 75% reduction in flights to and from the Middle East. (D) Scatter plot of policies including a 75% reduction on Eastern Europe. (E) Scatter plot of policies including a 75% reduction on Southeast Asia. (F) Scatter plot of policies that do not place a 75% reduction on any of the five most sensitive nodes in the graph. Policies of interest in Q3 are demarcated by the red dotted box, highlighting policies that cause larger reductions in global cases while not making 75% flight reductions on any node.

As shown in Fig. 6A, when a 75% reduction is applied to at least Western Europe, with or without reduction in the other four nodes, the outcomes mostly end up in Q4. This indicates that policies including stringent reduction in the number of Western European flights are predicted to cause larger reductions in global COVID-19 cases. Stringent flight reductions on North America (with other node reductions combined) are also predicted to cause large reductions in global cases, i.e., mostly Q3 and Q4-type response (Fig. 6B). Of note, several policies in the Q3 region (demarcated in the dotted square) cause larger reductions in global COVID-19 cases with smaller daily flight reductions, only reducing around 1500 daily flights on average. Through this we identified that simultaneously perturbing the most sensitive nodes (e.g., 75% Western Europe, 50% North America) is one approach for achieving large reductions in global cases with flight reduction policies. This is also evidenced by the results shown in Fig. 6C–E where policies with 75% reduction in the lesser sensitive nodes (Middle East, Eastern Europe, and Southeast Asia) caused less reduction in global COVID cases with similar or larger flight reductions. On Fig. 6F, we note that with no max reductions on highly sensitive nodes, a number of policies also achieve higher reductions in global cases while reducing less flights (Q3, demarcated in dotted square). This indicates that a second approach for reducing global cases might be to spread flight reductions among multiple, less sensitive nodes.

Conclusions

In this work, we investigated the relationship between human mobility in the form of international flights and global COVID-19 pandemic dynamics. We developed a novel GNN-based model known as DWSAGE, capable of processing one-day graph inputs and learning the relationship between edge features and node features. A perturbation methodology was designed on our spatiotemporal GNN model to identify the impact of geographical regions on global pandemic dynamics, from which we identified that Western Europe, North America, and Middle East have the highest impact on fueling the pandemic, which correlates with their larger volumes of outgoing international flights. We use these insights to set up and search through tangible restriction policies on air traffic for controlling the pandemic, identifying policies and strategies that reduce predicted global COVID cases effectively with lesser air traffic reductions. Our work represents a novel usage of perturbation analysis on spatiotemporal GNNs to gain insight on pandemic forecasting. Our method, however, can be extended on by integrating more node and edge features into the model, for example by including socioeconomic or development factors as node features or including geographical proximity and political relationship as edge features. Additionally, the architecture could be improved on by using transformers to model long-range dependencies in longer sequences and extend the architecture to multi-step prediction.
