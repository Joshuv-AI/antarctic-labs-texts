Introduction

ASPS is a rare, slow-growing soft tissue tumor of unclear etiology. It is among the least common sarcomas, representing 0.2–1 % of soft tissue sarcomas. It tends to affect 15–35 years old patients. Women outnumber men, especially those under 25 years. A characteristic nonreciprocal translocation is found in nearly all cases, involving the ASPSCR1 (previously called ASPL) gene on chromosome 17 and the TFE3 gene on the X chromosome. The resulting ASPSCR1-TFE3 fusion gene is postulated to be involved in the pathogenesis of this tumor. [1]

ASPS occurs in the lower extremities with 39.5 % located in the buttock or anterior thigh. Additional rare locations include the mediastinum, breasts, urinary bladder, gastrointestinal tract, bone, head, and neck region especially the orbit in children, and the female genital tract [2]. Our aim is to report an ASPS of the descending colon and to review and summarize the available literature on this rare condition.

Case report

Ms. S.A is a 29 years-old nulliparous female, previously healthy, who presented with lower abdominal pain for few months, with no other gastrointestinal or genitourinary symptoms. She had an unremarkable surgical and family history.

Examination revealed a large abdominopelvic mass reaching 4 cm above the umbilicus.

MRI showed a large necrotic abdominopelvic mass, measuring 20 × 23 × 19 cm, separate from the uterus and ovaries as well as few suspicious peritoneal nodules. Computed tomography (CT) confirmed the above findings, in addition, there were smaller similar characteristic nodular enhancing lesions on both sides of the abdomen measuring up to 2 cm. CA-125 was 216.2 units/mL and LDH 271 IU/L.

Multidisciplinary tumor board recommended surgery with debulking, so she underwent a laparotomy which revealed a large nodular lobulated mass with areas of necrosis and relatively smooth outer surface covered by thin capsule, it measures around 31 × 25 × 9 cm arising from the distal part of the descending colon close to the upper sigmoid and left lateral wall of the uterus and filling the whole cul-de-sac and adherent to the medial peritoneum. Moreover, there were several nodules in the mesentery and the surrounding peritoneum. Inspection of the rest of abdomen and pelvis was negative. She underwent a resection of the mass and involved adjacent peritoneal and mesenteric surface nodules en-block with a left colectomy with primary anastomosis. The specimen with the mass weighed 452 g (Fig. 1).

At the end of surgery, the patient had no gross residual disease. Her postoperative course was uneventful.

Histopathological examination revealed an ASPS involving the outer serosal layer of the large bowel wall, mesenteric fat, greater omentum, and peritoneum with negative margins (Fig. 2).

The tumor cells are polygonal with well define cell borders). (B) TFE3 immunohistochemistry stain shows diffuse nuclear positivity (TEF3 IHC 20×).

No further treatment was received in our facility nor elsewhere, as the patient went back to home country shortly after surgery.

A year later, she presented with shortness of breath and cough. Computed tomography revealed recurrence of the tumor in liver, lung, peritoneum. The multidisciplinary tumor board recommended palliative chemotherapy or targeted therapy after biopsying the tumor to confirm ASPS recurrence. However, her clinical status was deteriorating rapidly, and after extensive counselling the patient and her sister opted for palliative supportive care only. She passed away three months later.

Discussion and conclusion

Involvement of the gastrointestinal with ASPS is very rare. A review of the literature revealed seven such patients as summarized in (Table 1) to which we have added our current patient [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. However, only two of the patients had involvement of the large intestine. The seven patients were presented with various symptoms including, abdominal pain, gastrointestinal bleeding, intestinal obstruction, or intussusception. Three of the seven patients had metastatic disease and underwent surgery with invariably a poor outcome since all three of them passed away from their disease [[6], [7], [8]]. The remaining patients were lost to follow up. To note that none of these patients received targeted therapy.

Treatment of early-stage ASPS involves radical surgical excision of the primary tumor [10]. Traditionally, patients with advanced ASPS have limited therapeutic options because of the resistance of the tumor to conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, however, potentially it is a targetable type of sarcoma [11]. The ASPSCR1-TFE3 fusion gene leads to aberrant transcription of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), which upregulates proangiogenic factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and hepatocyte growth factor receptor (MET/HGFR), and induces immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment. The response rate of metastatic ASPS to targeted therapy like tyrosinase kinase inhibitor is clinically meaningful, and comparable to that of first-line chemotherapy. Most patients who received targeted therapy achieved disease control. Patients who had refractory or progressive disease to one targeted agent demonstrated a response to other agents. A retrospective study revealed that ASPS is sensitive to Anlotinib and Pazopanib monotherapy. Compared to pazopanib, Anlotinib yields longer progression free survival and lower incidence of grade 3 to 4 toxicities. Patients on Pazopanib were more likely to have their dose reduced and may have affected the control of their disease [14].

In view of the rarity of the disease, especially large intestine involvement, multi-center, and multi-national collaborative trials are needed to expand the treatment options [12,13].
