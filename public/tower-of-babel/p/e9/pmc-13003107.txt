Neural predictors of treatment outcome through emotion regulation in internalizing disorders: a narrative review
Authors: Heide Klumpp, Delaney Davey, Scott A. Langenecker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Internalizing disorders such as depressive and anxiety disorders are prevalent, disabling, and characterized by difficulty managing emotions particularly in the context of negative information. Despite the availability of empirically supported treatments, response to these treatments remains heterogeneous. Accruing data indicate neural predictors of treatment response have the potential to contribute to precision medicine to improve treatment outcomes. This review evaluates functional magnetic resonance imaging studies that examined explicit (e.g., effortful) and implicit (e.g., automatic) emotion regulation involving negative stimuli and treatment response. Results showed treatments mostly consisted of cognitive behavioral therapy, exposure therapy, and/or pharmacotherapy. Explicit regulation findings were predominantly based on cognitive reappraisal, which showed pre-treatment activity in medial and lateral prefrontal cortices frequently served as predictors of treatment response. Regarding directionality, greater symptom improvement was generally associated with lower baseline activity in these regions. Results suggest patients with less baseline explicit regulation capacity may benefit more from treatment. For implicit regulation, most studies utilized emotional interference tasks. Predictors frequently involved prefrontal cortical regions and anterior cingulate cortex; here, the direction was largely that of more baseline activity predicting greater symptom improvement. Findings suggest treatment may leverage greater pre-existing implicit regulatory capacity. While baseline activity in other regions during explicit and implicit regulation were reported, including regions central to emotion processing (e.g., amygdala), results were less consistent. Despite these insights, substantial gaps in the literature were observed. For explicit regulation, studies predominantly focused on cognitive reappraisal, an adaptive regulation approach. Furthermore, the majority of studies consisted of major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, and posttraumatic stress disorder with insufficient representation of other internalizing disorders. Findings underscore the relevance of neural predictors of treatment outcome through emotion regulation in internalizing disorders. However, further study is needed to determine their contribution in precision medicine.

Introduction

Internalizing disorders such as depression and anxiety are highly prevalent as about a third of individuals will experience an internalizing disorder in their lifetime [1]. These disorders are also frequently comorbid. For example, nearly half of individuals with lifetime major depressive disorder have a lifetime history of one or more anxiety disorders [2]. The prevalence is all the more striking given these disorders are a major public health problem--they are impairing [3, 4] and contribute to significant economic, physical, emotional, and social costs [5–7], as well as excessive mortality [7]. Effective treatments have the potential to reduce this burden.

Well-studied empirically-supported treatments for internalizing disorders include cognitive behavioral therapy and selective-serotonin reuptake inhibitors. However, response to these treatments is heterogeneous and many do not fully respond to treatment (e.g., [8–11]). An aim of precision medicine is to utilize biomarkers that reflect meaningful individual differences in treatment response to aid in tailoring treatments to increase the likelihood patients will receive the greatest therapeutic outcome with the least adverse effects [12]. Highlighting the relevance of brain activity in biomarker development is evidence baseline brain activity predicts treatment response in internalizing disorders more robustly than clinical or demographic information alone (e.g., [13–19]). These findings align with decades of neuroimaging research that indicate internalizing disorders are ‘brain disorders’ [20, 21]. Thus, it stands to reason measures of brain activity would be more sensitive in predicting treatment outcome than distal measures (e.g., clinical observation).

Emotion regulation

An important construct in biomarker development for internalizing disorders is emotion regulation, including for detection and treatment targeting. Emotion regulation (hereafter ‘regulation’) encompasses the management of motivationally-relevant stimuli or events that commonly occur in daily life. Negative information is particularly salient as it is evolutionary advantageous to attend to, or be distracted by, cues such as angry faces in a crowd [22] given that it permits a rapid, appropriate response to threat. However, without adaptive regulation, monitoring and response to negative information can interfere with higher-order goals [23, 24] and adversely impact mood. Indeed, failure of adaptive regulation is proposed to contribute to the development and maintenance of internalizing disorders [25–27].

Regulation strategies vary in their adaptiveness and span explicit (i.e., volitional, effortful) and implicit (i.e., automatic, effortless) domains. An example of an adaptive explicit regulation approach is cognitive reappraisal, which involves interpreting or reframing negative information in a way that inhibits the genesis of negative emotions or reduces them [28]. In contrast, it is generally considered maladaptive to intentionally inhibit one’s emotions through expressive suppression [26, 28]. Implicit regulation encompasses behaviors that are automatically governed by executive functions (e.g., flexible, adaptive responding to shifting contingencies) [29] in addition to experience-dependent information filtering (potentially enhancing or diminishing effectiveness). Adaptive implicit regulation includes effectively carrying out task-relevant goals in the face of emotionally salient task-irrelevant information [30], and dampening negative affective state by labeling emotions [31]. On the other hand, examples of maladaptive implicit regulation are repetitive negative thinking tendencies such as rumination and worry [26, 32], which are associated with reduced cognitive control [33] and a persistent self-focus that is negatively-toned [26, 32].

Neurobiology of emotion regulation

At the neurobiological level, successful regulation involving negative information (e.g., aversive images) is characterized by recruitment of regions central to top-down control functions (e.g., dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, inferior frontal gyrus, inferior parietal cortex) and conflict monitoring/resolution (e.g., anterior cingulate cortex) [34–38] coupled with relatively low activity in regions critical to emotion processing such as the amygdala [34, 39]. See Fig. 1 for key regions implicated in regulation. Put another way, amygdala responsivity is a purported index of emotional reactivity. Therefore, successful regulation is associated with relatively low amygdala activity and engagement of regions that inhibit amygdala activity [34–36, 40]. For example, a review of cognitive reappraisal studies consisting of different clinical populations (e.g., mood disorders, anxiety disorders) and healthy controls showed less neural activity in ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (PFC) and dorsolateral PFC across disorders relative to controls [42]. Thus, in addition to evidence of diminished activity in regions that support control functions during reappraisal, findings were transdiagnostic. With regard to emotional reactivity, greater amygdala responsivity was observed in mood but not anxiety disorders indicating a disorder-specific subcortical effect [42]. In a meta-analysis of cognitive reappraisal consisting of different disorders that also encompassed mood and anxiety disorders, less activity in prefrontal regions (e.g., ventrolateral PFC, dorsomedial PFC) was found across different disorders relative to controls in addition to relatively more activity in other cortical regions (e.g., precentral gyrus, supramarginal gyrus) [41]. While aberrant amygdala activity was not detected, greater activation in various regions, including the anterior insula, which is responsible for interoceptive awareness [44] was found across clinical cohorts compared to controls [41]. Taken together, clinical populations were characterized by less activity in top-down regions during reappraisal relative to controls. Evidence of elevated emotional reactivity was mixed though reports of greater amygdala activity in mood disorders [42] and anterior insula activity in clinical populations in general relative to controls [41] points to abnormal emotional reactivity during reappraisal.

Beyond explicit regulation, a meta-analytic study that evaluated control mechanisms relevant to implicit regulation, for example, studies that evaluated conflict monitoring, set-shifting, and/or inhibition in various clinical populations such as depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, and substance use [45]. Results revealed transdiagnostic aberrant activity in (pre)/frontal areas (e.g., dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, anterior mid-cingulate/presupplementary motor area) as well as the insula [43], a key part of the salience network [45]. Findings indicate that control functions in clinical populations are associated with transdiagnostic disruptions in both the multiple-demand and salience networks [43].

The neurobiology of regulation as it pertains to repetitive negative thinking is less evident. For instance, since rumination and worry are associated with self-focus, regions in the resting-state default mode network (DMN) (e.g., precuneus, posterior cingulate cortex), which supports self-referential and introspective processes [46, 47], would be expected to underlie these forms of repetitive negative thinking. However, neuroimaging findings indicate resting-state activity [48] and the DMN [49] are not synonymous with rumination and worry. Therefore, more research is needed to characterize the neurobiology of ‘successful’ regulation concerning repetitive negative thinking.

In summary, emotion regulation plays a critical role in day-to-day functioning and mental well-being and task-based neuroimaging study findings encompassing negative stimuli indicate regulation probes regions central to control processes and emotional reactivity. Moreover, accumulating data indicate effective regulation is characterized by the recruitment of regions key to control and monitoring functions, which are aberrant and largely transdiagnostic in clinical populations and may contribute to elevated emotional reactivity.

Emotion regulation and individual differences

An important aspect of this work are individual differences, which are integral to biomarker development and cannot be adequately assessed with overall brain activity during regulation (e.g., group-based analysis). Evidence of regulation-related variance has been observed in individuals with and without internalizing disorders. For example, in an emotional conflict study comprising healthy participants, higher self-reported reappraisal corresponded with more dorsolateral PFC/dorsal anterior cingulate cortex activity when inhibiting negative faces [50]. The same study also showed higher subjective suppression, a maladaptive regulation strategy [28], was associated with more amygdala activity [50]. Additionally, a transdiagnostic study comprising patients with depressive and/or anxiety disorders showed that patients with better real-time subjective reappraisal when reappraising negative images exhibited more (pre)/frontal activity (e.g., dorsolateral PFC, superior frontal gyrus) [51]. While a comprehensive review of neuroimaging studies concerning regulation-related variance is beyond the scope of the current review, findings suggest individual differences play a role in the neurobiology of regulation. The specific reasons for variability in the neural underpinnings of regulation remain unclear, though they are likely multifactorial. For instance, variance may stem from the type of strategy an individual engages in to complete a task [52], environmental factors [53, 54], and genetics [55]. Consequently, such factors are important considerations in the development of neural predictors of treatment outcome.

In summary, the delineation of neural predictors of treatment outcome through regulation in internalizing disorders has important clinical and scientific inferences. However, there remain substantial gaps in the literature. To date, review and meta-analytic studies have mainly concentrated on the neurobiology of regulation in individuals with and without internalizing disorders [37, 38, 41–43]. Also, despite important review and meta-analytic studies on neural predictors of treatment outcome in internalizing disorders (e.g., [56–63]), we are not aware of a review or meta-analysis that examined neural predictors of treatment outcome in the context of explicit and implicit emotion regulation in different internalizing disorders. Therefore, the objective of this narrative review is to evaluate previous emotion regulation studies to advance understanding of neural predictors of treatment outcome and identify important knowledge gaps. Findings have the potential to assist in the development of emotion regulation-based neural predictors of treatment response in internalizing disorders.

Methods

The current review focused on functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) as it is a frequently used non-invasive method to evaluate brain activity. We searched PubMed and PsycINFO databases for articles up to June 2024. Only peer-reviewed articles in English were included and all trial designs were included (e.g., open label, randomized control). FMRI studies were constrained to regulation paradigms that probed control processes (e.g., executive functions) and encompassed negative stimuli. Therefore, fMRI studies that exclusively involved resting-state, symptom provocation, or emotion processing were excluded. To identify studies for the current review, search terms were: “neuroimaging” (OR “fMRI”) AND “emotion/emotional” (OR “affect/affective”) AND “regulation/regulate” (OR “implicit” OR “control” OR “Stroop” OR “interfere/interference” OR “inhibit/inhibition”) AND “predictor” (OR “biomarker”) AND “treatment” (OR “response” OR “outcome”). Studies in previous review and meta-analytic studies of neural predictors of treatment response in internalizing disorders involving task-based fMRI were also evaluated.

Studies were also required to involve patients with internalizing disorders. Therefore, studies of depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, trauma- and stressor-related disorders, and obsessive-compulsive and related disorders were included and externalizing and psychotic disorders were excluded. Regarding study design, studies were required to encompass a baseline symptom measure and related symptom change measure following treatment. Since the current review focused on pre-treatment neural predictors following treatment, studies of early treatment response (i.e., response prior to completing full treatment trial) and those restricted to mechanisms (e.g., pre-to-post treatment change in brain activity) or predictors solely based on mechanisms of change were excluded as neural activity that underlies therapeutic change may not necessarily serve as baseline predictors of change. Lastly, only studies that involved treatments known to be efficacious, effective, or non-inferior to an empirically-supported treatment were included based on valid resources (i.e., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, American Medical Association, American Psychological Association). Treatments used for treatment-resistant populations (e.g., brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression; [64]) were excluded.

Prefrontal cortices (i.e., anterior portion of frontal lobes)

The majority of predictors comprised prefrontal cortex (PFC) regions, which may be due in part to the use of ROI approaches that focused on regions central to control processes. Specifically, ventrolateral PFC (vlPFC), vlPFC/inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), dorsomedial PFC (dmPFC), and dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC) were observed. With regard to directionality, more symptom improvement was predicted by less baseline activity in vlPFC [66], vlPFC/IFG [69], dmPFC [68], and dlPFC [70]. Reinecke and colleagues [65] showed symptom improvement following exposure-based CBT was predicted by dlPFC though in the direction of more baseline activity predicting symptom improvement. However, since the finding was based on a contrast that compared an emotion processing condition with a regulation condition, we surmise less baseline dlPFC activity would have predicted more symptom improvement if comparing the regulation condition with the emotion processing condition.

For the few studies where patients were randomized to different treatment modalities, one reported differential activity and the other did not. Specifically, in a study in which depressed or anxious patients were randomized to CBT or ST, more symptom improvement following CBT was predicted by less baseline vlPFC activity [66]. Yet, more symptom improvement following ST was predicted by more baseline vlPFC activity [66]. In a study in which patients with PTSD were randomized to exposure therapy, with or without an SSRI, or SSRI with enhanced medication management, symptom improvement was predicted by less baseline dmPFC activity regardless of treatment arm [68].

In summary, symptom improvement was mostly predicted by less baseline activity in lateral and medial prefrontal cortices in different studies.

Other regions

Symptom improvement was also predicted by baseline activity in an array of cortical and subcortical structures though this effect was largely driven by studies that used data-driven approaches. Specifically, more baseline activity in superior frontal gyrus following a trial of SNRI or CBT predicted symptom improvement [13, 67] and more baseline activity in middle frontal gyrus predicted symptom improvement following a trial with an SNRI [67]. More baseline activity in transverse temporal gyrus, anterior insula, superior temporal gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, precentral gyrus, and substantia nigra also predicted more symptom improvement following CBT [13].

In the direction of less baseline activity predicting greater symptom improvement, regions consisted of amygdala following exposure-based CBT [19], middle cingulate and paracentral lobule following treatment with an SNRI [67], and rostral anterior cingulate cortex following CBT [16].

In summary, neural predictor regions outside prefrontal areas comprised frontal, temporal, parietal, and subcortical structures and the direction of activity predicting symptom improvement was mixed. Additionally, regions central to emotion processing were infrequently observed, and the direction was inconsistent as greater symptom improvement was predicted by more baseline anterior insula activity and less baseline amygdala activity.

Prefrontal cortices, frontal regions, anterior cingulate cortex

Most predictors consisted of prefrontal cortices, frontal regions, and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), which may be due in part to the use of ROI approaches as these regions are implicated in implicit regulation. Evidence of prefrontal cortex (PFC) involvement included reports that greater symptom improvement was predicted by more baseline activity in the following regions: ventromedial PFC (vmPFC) regardless of treatment arm [75]; vmPFC following exposure therapy [73]; and inferior frontal triangularis, medial frontal gyrus, frontal superior medial gyrus, and superior frontal gyrus after completing CBT [76, 77]. A study that evaluated functional connectivity with amygdala as seed regions found more inverse ventrolateral PFC-amygdala connectivity at baseline predicted more symptom improvement regardless of treatment arm, which comprised CBT or acceptance and commitment therapy [78].

There was also evidence symptom improvement was predicted by less baseline activity in the following regions: dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC) regardless of treatment arm [75], dlPFC following an SSRI trial [72], and superior frontal gyrus, which encompassed dorsal ACC and dorsomedial PFC, regardless of treatment arm [79].

For the ACC, symptom improvement was predicted by more baseline activity in the rostral ACC regardless of treatment arm [74] and after completing CBT [16]. Greater symptom improvement was also predicted by more baseline activity in the dorsal ACC following CBT [77]. Without specifying ACC division, higher baseline activity in ACC predicted more symptom improvement after completing CBT [76] and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy [71]. Yet not all studies demonstrated positive relationships as greater symptom improvement was also predicted by less baseline activity in the dorsal ACC following an SSRI trial [72] and regardless of treatment arm [79].

Brain structures central to emotion processes were also observed. Greater symptom improvement was predicted by higher baseline amygdala activity following CBT [16], more baseline anterior insula activity regardless of treatment arm [75], and higher baseline anterior insula activity following mindfulness-based cognitive therapy [71]. Without specifying division, more baseline insula activity also predicted symptom improvement following CBT [76]. In contrast, less baseline insula activity predicted symptom improvement following an SSRI trial [72]. It is unclear whether certain divisions of the insula explain divergent effects or if the difference in direction relates to treatment modality.

Other regions

Two studies reported an assortment of other neural predictors. Findings from a study that manipulated perceptual load (low vs. high) to modulate attentional resources, and used different task-irrelevant threatening facial expressions (i.e., fearful, angry) suggest these factors may interact with neural predictors [76]. Namely, more regions that served as neural predictors were identified when comparing high perceptual load against low perceptual load (e.g., parietal regions, precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus) following CBT and greater symptom improvement was largely predicted by more baseline activity in these regions [76]. Though most neural predictors did not appear to differentially relate with type of task-irrelevant expression, some regions did, for example, greater symptom improvement was predicted by more baseline activity in postcentral gyrus to task-irrelevant angry but not fearful faces.

Greater symptom improvement was also predicted by less baseline activity in the following regions: frontopolar cortices, hippocampus, visual cortex, and cerebellum following treatment with an SSRI [72] and paracentral lobule following CBT [76]. Of note, when using a machine-learning approach to examine symptom improvement at the individual level following treatment with an SSRI, findings included, but were not limited to, swaths of activity in cortical, subcortical, and cerebellar areas [72]; the approach did not permit interpretation of directionality—that is, whether more or less baseline activity predicted improvement [72].

In summary, baseline activity in prefrontal cortices, frontal regions, anterior cingulate cortex, and key emotion processing regions (e.g., amygdala, insula) predicted symptom improvement in different studies. Also, the direction of activity was that of mostly higher baseline activity predicting better treatment outcome. Results from a study that manipulated perceptual load and task-irrelevant threat faces suggest neural predictors may interact with attentional resource capacity and the signal of threat ‘distractor.’ In a machine learning study, activity in multitudinous regions predicted individual-level treatment response.

Discussion

In this review, fMRI studies reporting neural predictors of treatment outcome through baseline (i.e., pre-treatment) explicit and implicit emotion regulation comprised patients with depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, and posttraumatic stress disorder. For both explicit and implicit regulation, neural predictors in different studies frequently consisted of (pre)/frontal regions. Among studies in which patients were randomized to different treatment arms, some of which included a control-only arm, most showed neural predictors did not interact with treatment arm [68, 74, 75, 78, 79], which may reflect downstream treatment effects. Alternatively, the studies may not have been adequately powered to detect interactions with treatment arm. Even so, several studies demonstrated differential effects. More specifically, studies consisting of a placebo or waitlist arm showed predictors were driven by the treatment arm as expected [72, 73]. Yet, a study that compared cognitive behavioral therapy against supportive therapy [66] showed symptom improvement differentially related to baseline activity in a prefrontal region. Below, we elaborate on findings using the framework that successful regulation is characterized by engagement of regions central to control functions [34–38] coupled with relatively low activity in regions critical for emotion processing [34, 39].

Explicit emotion regulation

For explicit regulation, studies predominantly used cognitive reappraisal involving negative images. Despite inconsistent findings that may relate to methodological differences between studies, certain patterns emerged. Most predictors consisted of lateral and medial prefrontal cortices. Since prefrontal cortex (PFC) structures consistently engage during reappraisal [37, 38], the frequency of PFC predictors may be partly due to approaches that focused on PFC regions (e.g., regions of interest approach). Specifically, symptom improvement was predicted by baseline activity in the ventrolateral PFC (vlPFC), vlPFC/inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), dorsomedial PFC (dmPFC), and dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC). The vlPFC is involved in response selection and inhibition [80]. Also, as reappraisal necessitates interpreting a negative stimulus in a way that hinders or reduces negative affective state, vlPFC may also reflect its role in semantic processes [81, 82]. As for dmPFC, functions include emotional awareness, monitoring, self-referential processing, and voluntary attention [83–85]. The dlPFC is a domain-general cognitive control region [86] and is also implicated in volitional attention [84, 85]. The IFG is involved in semantic processes [87, 88] and inhibition [89, 90].

Regarding directionality, there was evidence that patients with less baseline activity in PFC areas during regulation benefitted more from CBT or exposure therapy [65, 66] or antidepressants with or without exposure therapy [68, 69]. Findings point to a ‘deficiency’ model [91] as lower baseline activity in regions associated with control processes foretold a more positive outcome. Additionally, for studies in which patients were randomized to different treatment arms, most results indicate neural predictors were not treatment specific potentially reflecting downstream treatment effects though it will be important to replicate results.

Concerning emotional reactivity, relatively few core emotion processing regions predicted symptom improvement and findings were mixed. In one study greater improvement was predicted by less baseline amygdala activity [19] and in another study symptom improvement was predicted by more baseline activity in the anterior insula [13]. The amygdala is a key emotion processing region [39] and the anterior insula plays an important role in interoceptive awareness [44]. Symptom improvement was also predicted by less baseline activity in rostral anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) [16], which is involved in evaluative functions (e.g., assessing salience of stimulus; [92, 93]). Collectively, results suggest baseline variance in emotional reactivity during explicit regulation may not necessarily be a consistent predictor of symptom improvement in internalizing disorders.

Within-study findings showed most studies were consistent in the directionality of results. That is, they either found that more or less baseline activity, but not both, predicted symptom improvement. However, a few studies reported differential effects. In late life depression, remission following treatment with an SNRI was predicted by more baseline activity in superior and middle frontal gyrus in addition to less baseline activity in middle cingulate and paracentral lobule [67]. It is possible that findings reflect dissociable functions as they pertain to treatment outcome. To increase understanding of the effect, it may be useful to evaluate whether findings reflect phenotypes within the disorder that interact with mechanisms of action. In a psychotherapy study where patients were randomized to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) or supportive therapy (ST), symptom improvement was predicted by less baseline activity in vlPFC following CBT yet more baseline activity in vlPFC following ST [66]. Results indicate baseline vlPFC activity may interact with different psychotherapy-related mechanisms of action.

In summary, studies that reported neural predictors of treatment outcome via explicit regulation largely involved cognitive reappraisal, an adaptive regulation strategy [28]. Prefrontal recruitment is essential for successful reappraisal [34] and studies comprising different clinical populations and treatments generally showed symptom improvement was predicted by less baseline activity in PFC areas during explicit regulation. Findings suggest patients with lower pre-treatment PFC activity, possibly indicative of diminished PFC engagement, were more likely to benefit from treatment. Symptom improvement was also predicted by activity in regions integral to emotion-related functions (e.g., amygdala, insula). However, these regions were not as frequently observed and the direction of activity linked to symptom change was more mixed. Thus, variance involving emotional reactivity during explicit regulation may not be a robust treatment outcome predictor though it will be important for future studies to replicate effects before drawing firm conclusions.

Implicit emotion regulation

For implicit regulation, studies reporting findings mainly utilized emotional interference tasks involving task-irrelevant emotional faces that included negative expressions. Similar to explicit regulation results, findings were mixed, which may be due to methodological differences between studies. Even so, certain patterns emerged. Most predictors comprised prefrontal cortices, frontal regions, and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), which may relate to the use of region of interest approaches. More specifically, symptom improvement was predicted by baseline activity in dlPFC [72, 75], vmPFC [73, 75], vlPFC [78], inferior frontal triangularis [76], frontopolar cortex [72], superior frontal gyrus [76, 79], medial frontal gyrus [76], superior frontal medial gyrus [76, 77], rostral ACC [16, 74], dorsal ACC [72, 77], and ACC without specifying division [71, 76].

Regarding function, these regions support goal-directed behavior that align with executive functions (e.g., inhibition, decision-making, response regulation). More specifically, inhibitory processes are largely managed by the vmPFC [94]; inferior frontal gyrus [89, 90], which encompasses inferior frontal triangularis [95, 96]; and superior frontal medial gyrus and medial frontal gyrus [97–100]. Other regions fundamental to goal-directed behavior are superior frontal gyrus (SFG) and frontopolar cortex. Among functions, SFG is involved in attentional control [101] and the frontopolar cortex permits the monitoring of multiple goals to enable appropriate actions [102]. Response control and regulation of emotional reactivity are facilitated by the dlPFC dorsolateral [103] and vlPFC [104]. The ACC has connections to many of these regions and plays a key role in regulating both cognitive and emotional processes [93, 94, 105]. Reports of baseline activity in ACC predicting symptom improvement were observed for the dorsal area, which is integral to conflict monitoring and deployment of cognitive control in addition to the rostral area, which is involved in the assessment of salient information and conflict resolution [35, 36, 92, 93].

Regions critical to emotion processing were also detected though only one study showed baseline amygdala activity predicted symptom improvement [16]. Insula was more frequently observed; specifically, symptom improvement was predicted by baseline activity in anterior insula [71, 75], and insula without specifying division [72, 76].

Regarding directionality, studies largely showed that more baseline activity was predictive of greater symptom improvement [16, 71, 73–77]. Since many of the predictor regions are involved in executive functions, it may be that treatment ‘capitalizes’ on greater pre-existing processes responsible for goal-directed behavior [91]. In support, the one study that examined functional connectivity during an affect label task comprising emotional faces showed more inverse vlPFC-amygdala connectivity at baseline predicted more symptom improvement indicating better regulation capacity corresponded with better treatment outcome [78]. Even so, evidence symptom improvement was also predicted by more baseline activity in emotion processing regions (e.g., amygdala, anterior insula; [16, 71, 75]) suggests patients with higher emotional reactivity may also benefit more from treatment. While more reactivity may represent ineffectual regulation capability, it may also reflect ‘bottom-up’ processes that influence control functions. For example, one pathway in which insula contributes to control functions is via the detection of salient information which is proposed to facilitate transient control signals to recruit brain regions that mediate attentional, working memory, and control functions [45]. Collectively, findings suggest more baseline activity during implicit regulation may be a promising marker of treatment success in internalizing disorders. However, further study is needed to disambiguate the extent to which better treatment outcome is explained by baseline activity indicative of high control facility versus high emotional reactivity.

Similar to explicit regulation findings, within-study findings generally showed consistency in the directionality of predictors. Yet, two studies demonstrated that both less baseline activity and more baseline activity in different regions predicted symptom improvement [75, 76]. Again, future studies may want to examine whether findings reflect dissociable functions that relate to phenotypes.

Limitations

Our review needs to be considered in light of important limitations. First, there are concerns regarding the rigor and reliability of fMRI findings (e.g., task design, analytic strategies, measurement variance), and we did not review the relative variability in stability and reliability of such metrics here. Also, if the different internalizing disorders vary in their baseline neural activity, findings may not translate to consistent predictors of treatment response. Second, we did not focus on methodological details that may have contributed to inconsistent findings. This includes the determination of what is considered to be significant baseline brain activity which may have differed between studies. Third, brain-based predictors of treatment outcomes may not necessarily reflect mechanisms of symptom change. For example, baseline ACC activity that predicts treatment response may reflect a trait that is not altered with treatment. Fourth, most study samples were small, which may have reduced the power to detect predictors or interactions with different treatment modalities. Fifth, only studies that reported significant results were included, which reduces rigor. Sixth, reactivity to motivationally-relevant stimuli in the absence of explicit or implicit regulation demands may serve as more robust predictors of treatment outcome than regulation-related predictors. Indeed, meta-analytic studies of treatment response frequently involve neural predictors that do not rely on regulation (e.g., passively viewing emotional faces or scenes; [58, 59, 62, 106]). Thus, additional study is needed to determine which paradigms yield the strongest predictors. Finally, findings were predominantly correlational in nature. Therefore, results are prone to overfitting, an important limitation that has been raised by other researches (e.g., [107]).

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, emotion regulation is an important treatment target in internalizing disorders and this review revealed important themes and gaps. Specifically, neural predictors of treatment outcome through explicit emotion regulation were predominantly based on cognitive reappraisal, an adaptive regulation strategy [28] and most predictors involved lateral and medial prefrontal cortices where less baseline activity generally predicted greater symptom improvement. Regarding implicit regulation, neural predictors were largely based on emotional interference tasks. Baseline activity in prefrontal cortices, frontal regions, and anterior cingulate cortex during implicit regulation frequently served as predictors and generally in the direction of more activity predicting greater symptom improvement. In contrast, fewer studies found regions central to emotional reactivity (e.g., amygdala, anterior insula) predicted treatment outcome. Therefore, baseline variance in cognitive control and/or aspects of executive functions and attentional processes may play a larger role in treatment success than emotional reactivity [108]. Even so, there is evidence that data-driven approaches identified an array of neural predictors, including but not limited to, numerous cortical and subcortical regions. This indicates that such approaches could delineate novel neural predictors, potentially reflecting ‘nodes’ in large scale brain networks that underlie emotion regulation [109].

With regard to gaps in the literature, the focus on reappraisal may have reduced generalizability of findings. While reappraisal is an important explicit regulation strategy, accruing data indicate internalizing disorders are more strongly associated with maladaptive regulation strategies than difficulty employing adaptive regulation strategies or under-utilizing them [26, 110]. Therefore, it would be useful for studies to evaluate neural predictors of treatment outcome with maladaptive strategies, for example, expressive suppression [28] or repetitive negative thinking [26, 32]. Another gap is the limited representation of internalizing disorders. For example, most depression studies focused on major depressive disorder and we were not able to identify studies that consisted of patients with obsessive-compulsive and related disorders. Additionally, evidence that certain findings cut across studies comprising different disorders suggests neural predictors may be transdiagnostic, though this would need to be tested. Further study is also needed to determine the reliability of regulation-based predictors of treatment response at the individual level as the utility of neural predictors is contingent on acceptable reliability. While fMRI data has been shown to have good to excellent reliability in a number of instances, reliability is affected by the approach used, the domain assessed, the amount of data acquired, and attention to the signal and noise characteristics of the neural signals captured in the blood oxygenation level dependent contrast (e.g., [111–113]). In summary, fMRI studies of neural predictors of treatment outcome though emotion regulation in internalizing disorders have yielded promising candidate biomarkers. However, more research is needed to determine their role in precision medicine. For example, more standardization in the field and the utilization of advanced data-driven techniques (e.g., machine learning; [114, 115]) would likely aid biomarker development.
