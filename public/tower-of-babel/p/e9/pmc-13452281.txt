Distinct goal-directed perfusion signatures in donation after brain death versus circulatory death heart transplantation1
Authors: Chen Chia Wang, Awab Ahmad, Mark Petrovic, Kevin McGann, Aaron Williams, John Trahanas, Swaroop Bommareddi, Tarek Absi, Eric Quintana, Aniket Rali, Kelly Schlendorf, Matthew Bacchetta, Ashish Shah, Brian Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContinuous oxygen extraction ratio (O2ER) monitoring during cardiopulmonary bypass is an emerging strategy to support goal-directed perfusion (GDP) during heart transplantation (HT). Because donation after circulatory death (DCD) introduces additional warm ischemia, we examined whether post-reperfusion O2ER dynamics differ from donation after brain death (DBD).MethodsAdult HT at a single center (10/2021–06/2025) with continuous perfusion data were retrospectively reviewed. O2ER was calculated as oxygen consumption/oxygen delivery index (DO2i). The primary endpoint was O2ER after graft reperfusion (cross-clamp removal), quantified as the area above O2ER of 0.25. Weighted regression after adjusting for baseline characteristics identified the association of DCD status, pre-reperfusion O2ER, and post-reperfusion DO2i on post-reperfusion O2ER.ResultsAmong 322 HT, 193 (59.9%) were DBD and 129 (40.1%) were DCD. Pre-reperfusion DO2i and O2ER, and post-reperfusion O2ER were similar between DBD and DCD cohorts. Weighted regression analysis demonstrated that DCD donor status (mean difference 0.36, 95% CI 0.11–0.60), pre-reperfusion area above O2ER 0.25 (mean difference 0.39, 95% CI 0.29–0.50), and post-reperfusion time below DO2i 280 mL/min/m² (mean difference 0.05, 95% CI 0.03–0.07) were all independently associated with post-reperfusion area above O2ER 0.25. The interaction term between DCD donor status and pre-reperfusion O2ER was also significantly associated with post-reperfusion O2ER (mean difference −0.27, 95% −0.41 – −0.14).ConclusionsDCD grafts exhibit persistently higher O2ER profiles and reduced metabolic responsiveness to pre-reperfusion optimization. These findings underscore the need for individualized, real-time O2ER-guided GDP to dynamically match perfusion to graft and patient-specific metabolic demands.

Introduction

Goal directed perfusion (GDP) is a strategy for meeting specific perfusion targets that has been studied in elective cardiac surgery, and has been shown to decrease complications such as acute kidney injury and reduce hospitalization length.1, 2, 3 The goal of GDP and perfusion targets in heart transplant is an emerging frontier. Early work has identified that prolonged durations with elevated oxygen extraction ratio (O2ER), defined as fraction of oxygen extracted during cardiopulmonary bypass, is associated with increased post-transplant morbidity and mortality.4

Donation after circulatory death (DCD) donors have expanded the heart transplant donor pool in recent years, and has yielded comparable outcomes to traditional donation after brain death (DBD) donors. 5, 6, 7 The DCD recovery process exposes the allograft to an additional period of warm ischemia, and extended warm ischemic insult has been shown to increase the rates of primary graft dysfunction and mortality.8, 9. It is possible that after the allograft is implanted and exposed to ischemic-reperfusion injury following exposure to the recipient systemic circulation after cross-clamp removal, an O2ER increase could be a result of the metabolically stressed heart failing to meet the oxygen demands on the body.

The objective of this study is to compare trends in O2ER following graft reperfusion between DBD and DCD transplants. We hypothesize that DCD transplants would show a higher post-reperfusion O2ER compared to DBD transplants, reflecting a more metabolically stressed state. Furthermore, we aim to correlate pre-reperfusion O2ER and post-reperfusion oxygen delivery index (DO2i) to provide insight into potential real time management strategies.

Methods

Study population

All adult heart transplants at a single center from October 2021 to June 2025 were retrospectively reviewed. The following exclusion criteria were applied: multiorgan transplants, recipients with adult congenital heart disease, allografts stored using machine perfusion, allografts stored on 4 – 8°C static storage, DCD recovery strategies other than thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion (NRP), and cases missing continuous perfusion data. The cohort was stratified into a DBD group and a DCD group. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Vanderbilt University Medical Center approved the study protocol and publication of data, under protocol #241909. Patient written consent for the publication of the study data was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective, chart review nature of the study.

Cardiopulmonary bypass data processing

Continuous minute-by-minute perfusion data were captured and extracted from our institutional perfusion system (Quantum Perfusion System, Spectrum Medical). Missing values were imputed using a rolling median method. Bypass time was divided into pre-reperfusion (when cross-clamp is applied) and post-reperfusion (after cross-clamp removal) periods, or before and after the cardiac allograft was reperfused and exposed to the recipient systemic circulation. As post-reperfusion graft dysfunction could prolong total bypass duration and confound the calculated perfusion parameters, post-reperfusion bypass duration was capped at the cohort median to avoid reverse causality. The first 5 min of cardiopulmonary bypass was also excluded due to variability during bypass initiation.

Data definitions and outcomes

The primary exposure of interest was intraoperative O2ER after graft reperfusion. O2ER was calculated as the ratio of oxygen consumption index to DO2i. An O2ER value greater than 0.25 was prespecified as “high”, based on prior publications in which an O2ER of 0.20 is around the physiologic normal.10 The degree of high O2ER was captured by calculating the area above the O2ER 0.25 threshold, a composite measure that takes into account both the magnitude above 0.25 and duration above 0.25.

DO2i pre- and post-reperfusion, and O2ER pre-reperfusion were also captured. For DO2i, the average, minimum, and time and area below prespecified targets of 260, 280, 300, 320, and 340 mL/min/m² were calculated during both the pre-reperfusion and post-reperfusion intervals. These targets were chosen based on various prior studies on GDP during adult cardiac surgery, which cited DO2i targets ranging from 270 – 300 mL/min/m2.11, 12 For O2ER, the average, maximum, and time and area above prespecified targets of 0.15, 0.20, 0.25, and 0.30 were calculated during both the pre-reperfusion and post-reperfusion intervals.

Clinical outcomes were also compared between the DBD and DCD cohorts. These included intraoperative ventricular dysfunction on transesophageal echocardiogram, severe primary graft dysfunction (PGD) as defined by the International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) consensus statement in 2014,13 postoperative cardiac indices, postoperative vasoactive inotrope scores (VIS), postoperative need for mechanical circulatory support (MCS), LVEF at postoperative day (POD) 7, duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU and hospital length of stay, and 30-day and 90-day survival. VIS was calculated as dopamine (µg/kg/min) + dobutamine (µg/kg/min) + 100 * epinephrine (µg/kg/min) + 100 * norepinephrine (µg/kg/min) + 15 * milrinone (µg/kg/min).

Statistics

Unadjusted donor and recipient characteristics, perfusion data, and outcomes were compared between the DBD and DCD cohorts. Categorical variables were reported as number (percentage) and compared using Fisher exact test. Continuous variables were reported as median (quartile 1 – quartile 3) and compared using Wilcoxon rank sum test. Kaplan Meier survival curves and log-rank tests compared survival between the study cohorts. LOESS curves were used to visualize post-reperfusion DO2i and O2ER trajectories between DBD and DCD transplants. A weighted regression model with the area above post-reperfusion O2ER of 0.25 as the outcome was built using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to create balanced cohorts by adjusting for covariates that could affect post-reperfusion O2ER. These covariates included: donor sex, donor age, and donor COD, SCS type, total ischemic time, recipient sex, recipient age, recipient BMI, ischemic HF, recipient diabetes, pre-transplant hospitalization, pre-transplant IABP, pre-transplant Impella, pre-transplant LVAD, pre-transplant ECMO. The weighted regression model examined the effects of DCD donor status, pre-reperfusion O2ER, and time below DO2i of 280 mL/min/m² post-reperfusion on post-reperfusion O2ER area above 0.25, with an interaction term between DCD status and pre-reperfusion O2ER. The time below DO2i of 280 mL/min/m² post-reperfusion covariate was chosen as the threshold to account for oxygen delivery as 280 mL/min/m² was the median minimum DO2i value in both DBD and DCD cohorts. A post-hoc linear hypothesis test assessed whether baseline O2ER remained a significant predictor in DCD hearts by evaluating the sum of its main effect and interaction term. A subgroup analysis of the DCD cohort examined the relationship between various warm ischemic times during the DCD withdrawal process and post-reperfusion O2ER area above 0.25 during the subsequent transplant, while adjusting for pre-reperfusion O2ER and time below DO2i of 280 mL/min/m² post-reperfusion. Warm ischemic intervals included total donor warm ischemic time (DWIT; withdrawal of life support to initiation of TA-NRP), functional warm ischemic times (FWIT) defined as time from oxygen saturation <80% to initiation of TA-NRP, and asystolic warm ischemic time (AWIT; initial declaration to start of TA-NRP).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 322 adult heart transplants met inclusion criteria, of which 193 (59.9%) were DBD donors and 129 (40.1%) were DCD donors. DCD donors were less likely to be female (20.2% vs 40.9%, p < 0.001) and there were less female donor to male recipient mismatches within the DCD cohort (7.8% vs 19.7%, p = 0.004) (Table 1). The distribution of donor causes of death were also different, with a higher proportion of blunt trauma (28.7% vs 15.6%) and lesser proportion of hypoxic brain injury (30.2% vs 42.7%) among DCD donors compared to DBD donors (p = 0.043). DCD donors were also less likely to have diabetes (1.6% vs 7.0%, p = 0.031). All other donor variables, including proportion of static storage on ice or 10°C, total ischemic time, and predicted heart mass ratios were comparable between the cohorts. Recipients in the DCD cohort were less likely female (18.6% vs 29.0%, p = 0.036). Perfusion data

Pre-reperfusion DO2i and O2ER were similar between the DCD and DBD cohorts. Before allograft reperfusion, the average DO2i was 305 mL/min/m² within the DCD group and 304 mL/min/m² within the DBD group, with minimums of 247 mL/min/m² in the DCD group and 248 mL/min/m² in the DBD group. Average pre-reperfusion O2ER was 0.20 in both cohorts, with a maximum of 0.26 in the DCD group and 0.25 in the DBD group.

Post-reperfusion DO2i were similar between the cohorts, with an average of 307 mL/min/m² in the DCD group and 312 mL/min/m² in the DBD group. The minimum post-reperfusion DO2i were 278 mL/min/m² in DCDs and 282 mL/min/m² in DBDs. The average post-reperfusion O2ER was 0.27 in DCDs and 0.26 in DBDs, with a maximum of 0.30 in DCDs and 0.29 in DBDs. The time above an O2ER of 0.15 (median 29 [IQR 26 – 29] DCDs vs 29 [23–29] DBDs, p = 0.029) and above 0.30 (median 3 [IQR 0 – 22] DCDs vs 1 [0 – 15] DBDs, p = 0.049) were greater in the DCD cohort. Discussion

In this single center, exploratory analysis of continuous cardiopulmonary bypass data during adult heart transplantation, we observed distinct O2ER signatures between DBD and DCD allograft recipients that could reflect a more metabolically stressed state in DCD allografts, potentially offering findings that may guide real time management. The principal findings were: (1) pre-reperfusion oxygen delivery index (DO2i) and O2ER were similar between donor types; (2) following cross clamp removal (post-reperfusion period), DCD cases demonstrated greater O2ER burden despite comparable DO2i trajectories; and 3) DCD allografts were independently associated with higher post-reperfusion O2ER burden while adjusting for baseline covariates, pre-reperfusion O2ER, and post-reperfusion DO2i, and 4) the association between pre-reperfusion O2ER and post-reperfusion O2ER was less pronounced in DCD transplants compared to DBD transplants.

This study adds important information to the current literature landscape around DCD heart transplantation. Several previous studies have investigated the effects of warm ischemic intervals during DCD allograft recovery with outcomes, such as correlating prolonged functional warm ischemic time with increased primary graft dysfunction and prolonged asystolic warm ischemic time with increased mortality.8, 9. We believe our current work adds to these studies by introducing O2ER as a measurement of real time metabolic stress and demonstrating its clinical relevance in terms of real-time monitoring and management strategies. The time of cross-clamp removal when the allograft is first reperfused on cardiopulmonary bypass represents a period of stress where the allograft is most vulnerable to ischemia-reperfusion injury, and is simultaneously exposed to abrupt temperature shifts and inflammatory activation.14 Since O2ER provides a more physiologically accurate representation of the energy supply-demand balance by integrating both DO2i and oxygen consumption index,10, 15 its measurement after cross-clamp removal and graft reperfusion can provide surgeons with real time information on the cumulative metabolic stress. Both increased duration and magnitude of O2ER elevation above the norm, represented in this study by area above 0.25, plausibly indicates increased oxygen debt and metabolic stress. This mismatch between energy supply and demand can worsen endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory pathways implicated in early graft dysfunction.16, 17 Further work correlated warm ischemic intervals during DCD heart recovery and intraoperative O2ER is necessary to clarify the relationship between these variables, and may offer valuable insight that can guide both preoperative risk stratification and planning, and real time intraoperative adjustments.

The findings of our study provide important implications for what GDP may entail for DCD heart transplants. First, the higher post-reperfusion O2ER in DCD transplants compared to DBD transplants, despite similar pre- and post-reperfusion DO2i, suggests that reliance on a single oxygen delivery target may not fully characterize physiologic adequacy during early reperfusion. While further validation is necessary, this adds credence to the idea of O2ER being a more accurate reflection of a patient’s metabolic state. Second, it is important to recognize that pre-reperfusion O2ER was significantly associated with post-reperfusion O2ER in both DBD and DCD transplants, albeit to a smaller degree in DCD transplants. This demonstrates that optimization of pre-reperfusion O2ER may prevent large, sustained elevations in O2ER after the allograft is reperfused. Preoperatively, treatment of anemia and iron deficiency, optimizing volume status, and nutritional prehabilitation have been shown to improve the body’s ability to meet the increased metabolic demand during cardiac surgery.18 Intraoperatively, optimizing DO2i during the pre-reperfusion phase may help lower the pre-reperfusion O2ER and translate to reduced metabolic stress after cross-clamp removal and graft reperfusion. For example, in our cohort it is evident that DO2i in the pre-reperfusion phase was lower than DO2i in the post-reperfusion phase. While it is unclear whether this difference is due to more cognizant efforts to increase DO2i in the post-reperfusion phase or due to other reasons, strategies to increase pre-reperfusion DO2i could potentially lower the pre-reperfusion O2ER. We also found that longer post-reperfusion time below DO2i of 280 mL/min/m² was significantly associated with higher post-reperfusion O2ER. While the 280 mL/min/m² threshold was chosen for our study cohort since it’s the median minimum value in the post-reperfusion phase, and establishment of a threshold will require further validation in larger studies, the idea of optimizing post-reperfusion DO2i to prevent O2ER elevation is still supported by our findings. We acknowledge that this was an observational study where no specific interventions were applied, so future studies with interventions will be necessary to establish the feasibility of targeting specific O2ER thresholds. Specifically, we acknowledge that the relationship between systemic O2ER and allograft-specific metabolic status remains inferential, and systemic O2ER could plausibly be influenced by other factors uncontrolled in this study such as inflammation, temperature, and anesthetic management. Overall, this study provides the foundation for future work to build upon, where a prospective protocol could entail preoperative risk stratification based on DCD status and warm ischemia exposure, preoperative patient optimization, intraoperative optimization of O2ER prior to allograft reperfusion, and strategies to optimize DO2i and lower O2ER after allograft reperfusion.

Importantly, we acknowledge that this difference in post-reperfusion O2ER in DCD transplants compared to DBD transplants did not results in differences in clinical outcomes. While our previous study demonstrated that higher O2ER was associated with increased morbidity and early mortality in all adult heart transplant (DBD and DCD included),4 this current study found no differences in outcomes in DCD transplants despite a higher O2ER profile in the post-reperfusion phase. Several possible explanations exist. One, we could be underpowered to detect differences in clinical outcomes, but we believe this is less likely given the abundance of studies demonstrating comparable outcomes between DBD and DCD heart transplants. Second, it is possible that the O2ER elevation inherent to DCD transplantation is of a low enough magnitude that does not by itself translate to clinical differences. From this perspective, we could consider DCD status as a risk factor (the way diabetes and obstructive sleep apnea have been shown to be risk factors for high O2ER in coronary artery bypass grafting) when planning for the transplant and establishing perfusion goals.19 It is also plausible that other factors such as inflammation, temperature, and anesthetic management may also contribute to each patient’s O2ER trajectories during the transplant. Lastly, it may also be plausible that monitoring and optimizing O2ER is most important among a subset of DCD transplants where the allograft was exposed to longer durations of warm ischemia, a subset that has been shown to have higher rates of graft dysfunction and mortality. However, data from our subgroup analysis did not demonstrate an association between warm ischemic intervals during the DCD recovery process and post-reperfusion O2ER. Further validation with larger cohorts is necessary to clarify the relationship between warm ischemia during DCD recovery and post-reperfusion O2ER during the subsequent transplant.

This study has limitations inherent to its single center, retrospective nature. The perfusion thresholds chosen, such as O2ER of 0.25 and DO2i of 280 mL/min/m² may not be generalizable to patients from other transplant centers with different perfusion and surgical protocols. However, as a large volume heart transplant center, we believe the perfusion parameters and findings from our institutional experience provide valuable information for other transplant centers and lay the groundwork for multi-institutional studies. Another limitation is that other donor and recipient characteristics, organ recovery details, and intraoperative factors may influence O2ER. While we created balanced cohorts to adjust for covariates that we suspect could influence a patient’s cumulative metabolic stress, we acknowledge that other confounders could exist. Lastly, our study does not establish whether real time adjustments in DO2i or other intraoperative factors could actually lower O2ER, and the strategies proposed in our discussion require prospective studies to validate. Future mechanistic studies are necessary to fully understand the physiological underpinnings of these findings, and prospective trials are needed to establish if intraoperative O2ER can be effectively optimized by other perioperative strategies.

Conclusion

Elevated O2ER during heart transplant reflects an energy supply and demand mismatch and cumulative metabolic stress. DCD transplants exhibit persistently elevated post-reperfusion O2ER profiles and reduced metabolic responsiveness to pre-reperfusion optimization compared to DBD transplants, consistent with a more deleterious metabolic insult during the organ recovery process. Optimization of pre-reperfusion O2ER and post-reperfusion DO2i represent potential opportunities to prevent O2ER elevation after the allograft is reperfused. These findings underscore the need for individualized, real-time O2ER-guided GDP to dynamically match perfusion to graft and patient-specific metabolic demands.
