The burden of malaria and associated factors among children aged 6–59 months in Mozambique: a nationwide cross‑sectional survey
Authors: Jovinary Adam, Isabelle Munyangaju, Syabo Mwaisengela, Hawa Mbawala, Pankras Luoga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality among children under five years of age in Mozambique despite ongoing prevention and control interventions. Understanding the sociodemographic and household factors associated with malaria is essential for designing targeted public health strategies. This study aimed to determine the burden of malaria and identify its associated factors among children aged 6–59 months in Mozambique.MethodsThis study used secondary data from the nationally representative 2022–23 Mozambique Demographic and Health Survey. A weighted sample of 4,016 children aged 6–59 months who underwent malaria rapid diagnostic testing (mRDT) was included in the analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ characteristics and estimate malaria prevalence. Associations between malaria and independent variables were assessed using modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation. Adjusted prevalence ratios (APRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.ResultsThe overall prevalence of malaria among children aged 6–59 months was 32.3% (95% CI 29.1–35.7), with marked regional variation ranging from 0% in Cidade de Maputo to 54.7% in Nampula Province. In the multivariable analysis, malaria was independently associated with older child age (24–59 months) (APR = 1.58; 95% CI 1.42–1.76), rural residence (APR = 1.36; 95% CI 1.10–1.68), maternal primary education (APR = 2.06; 95% CI 1.52–2.81), informal maternal education (APR = 2.04; 95% CI 1.49–2.78), unimproved roof materials (APR = 1.29; 95% CI 1.07–1.56), middle wealth status (APR = 1.97; 95% CI 1.43–2.70), poor wealth status (APR = 2.41; 95% CI 1.73–3.37), anemia (APR = 2.89; 95% CI 2.42–3.45), malnutrition (APR = 1.10; 95% CI 1.02–1.21), residence in the central zone (APR = 1.92; 95% CI 1.41–2.64), and residence in the northern zone (APR = 3.06; 95% CI 2.24–4.19).ConclusionMalaria remains highly prevalent among Mozambican children and is strongly associated with socioeconomic and household conditions. Bold, integrated, and equity-driven action that strengthens health systems, addresses social determinants, and protects the most vulnerable children are urgently required.

Introduction

Malaria remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among children under-five years of age globally, with the greatest burden concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa. According to the World Malaria Report 2025, there were an estimated 282 million malaria cases and 610,000 malaria-related deaths worldwide in 2024 [1]. Children younger than five years continue to account for the majority of malaria mortality due to limited acquired immunity and high susceptibility to severe disease manifestations [2, 3]. Beyond mortality, malaria contributes substantially to anaemia, impaired growth, neurocognitive deficits, poor educational attainment, and long-term developmental consequences, making it a persistent threat to child survival and development. [2–5]. Although expanded access to insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying (IRS), rapid diagnostic testing, and artemisinin-based combination therapies has contributed to reductions in malaria burden in many settings, progress has recently stalled because of factors such as insecticide resistance, drug resistance, climate variability, humanitarian crises, and insufficient financing [1, 6, 7]. Consequently, malaria continues to pose a substantial threat to child survival and development, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.

The burden of malaria is overwhelmingly concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), which accounts for approximately 95% of global malaria deaths and the vast majority of malaria cases [1]. Numerous studies across SSA have demonstrated persistently high malaria prevalence among children under five years of age despite widespread implementation of control interventions [8–10]. The region continues to face challenges related to poverty, inadequate housing, unequal access to healthcare services, limited health literacy, and environmental conditions that favor transmission [9, 11, 12]. These factors have contributed to marked geographical and socioeconomic inequalities in malaria burden across and within countries in SSA.

Mozambique remains one of the highest malaria-burden countries in the world and contributes substantially to malaria morbidity and mortality in the African region [1, 13–15]. Malaria is endemic throughout the country, although transmission intensity varies considerably across provinces and ecological zones [14, 16]. The disease is a major cause of outpatient consultations, hospital admissions, anemia, and deaths among children younger than five years [13, 17–20]. Despite considerable investments in malaria control, including nationwide distribution of ITNs, expansion of rapid diagnostic testing, IRS, seasonal malaria chemoprevention in selected high-risk areas, and the recent introduction of the R21 malaria vaccine; malaria transmission remains persistently high in several provinces, particularly in northern and central Mozambique [21–24].

Evidence from previous studies has identified multiple determinants of malaria among children under five years of age. Child age is consistently associated with malaria risk, with older children generally exhibiting higher infection prevalence than younger children due to increased exposure to infective mosquito bites and changing immunity profiles [9, 11, 25, 26]. Socioeconomic factors such as household wealth and maternal education have also been repeatedly linked to malaria burden, with poorer households and children of less educated mothers experiencing significantly greater risk of infection [9, 12, 27, 28]. In addition, housing characteristics including unimproved roofs, walls, and floors have been associated with increased exposure to malaria vectors by facilitating mosquito entry into households [29–31]. Studies have further identified rural residence, limited access to health information, poor healthcare accessibility, climate-related factors, and geographical location as important drivers of malaria transmission and vulnerability in both Mozambique and other SSA countries [9, 12, 14, 18, 28, 32, 33].

Despite the availability of malaria surveillance systems and previous household surveys, important knowledge gaps remain in understanding the current burden and determinants of malaria among children in Mozambique. Much of the available evidence is based on older datasets, specific geographical areas, or analyses that do not fully capture the complex interactions between socioeconomic, household, and geographical factors influencing malaria transmission [14, 15, 34, 35]. Furthermore, changes in malaria control strategies, including expanded intervention coverage and the introduction of new preventive tools such as malaria vaccines, necessitate updated analyses using recent nationally representative data.

Therefore, there is a need for contemporary evidence to quantify the burden of malaria and identify the factors associated with malaria infection among children in Mozambique. Understanding these determinants is essential for identifying populations at greatest risk, guiding equitable allocation of resources, and informing integrated malaria control strategies. Using nationally representative data from the 2022–23 Mozambique Demographic and Health Survey, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of malaria and identify associated sociodemographic and household factors among children aged 6–59 months in Mozambique. Because malaria prevalence among children in Mozambique remains high, analytical methods that directly estimate prevalence ratios provide more interpretable measures of association than odds ratios for informing public health policy. The findings provide nationally representative evidence to guide the prioritization of malaria control strategies, inform equitable allocation of resources, and support targeted interventions for populations and geographical areas at greatest risk, thereby contributing to accelerated progress toward malaria reduction in Mozambique.

Methodology

Study design and data source

This study used a secondary analysis of nationally representative cross-sectional data obtained from the 2022–23 Mozambique Demographic and Health Survey (MZDHS). The survey was implemented by the Instituto Nacional de Estatística (INE) in collaboration with the Ministry of Health and ICF under the DHS Program [17]. The survey collected data on a wide range of demographic, socioeconomic, and health indicators, including malaria testing among children aged 6–59 months.

The DHS employed a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design to ensure national representativeness across all provinces and urban–rural areas of Mozambique. In the first stage, enumeration areas (EAs) were selected from the national sampling frame based on the most recent population census. In the second stage, households were systematically selected within each cluster. Detailed information regarding the survey design and sampling procedures is provided in the MZDHS final report [17].

Study population and sampling

The study population consisted of children aged 6–59 months who underwent malaria rapid diagnostic testing (mRDT) during the survey. Children with missing malaria test results or incomplete information on key study variables were excluded from the analysis. A total weighted sample of 4,016 children aged 6–59 months was included in the final analysis.

Study variables

Dependent variable

In this study, the dependent variable was the malaria rapid diagnostic test results in children aged 6–59 months. Children aged 6–59 months were classified as having malaria based on the results of the RDT. The variable was defined in a binary manner, with a positive malaria result coded as “1” and a negative result coded as “0”.

Independent variables

Independent variables included child age (6–23 months and 24–59 months), place of residence (urban or rural), maternal education level (secondary/higher, primary, or informal education), sex of the household head (male or female), household wealth status (rich, middle, or poor), anemia status (anemic or not anemic), nutritional status (malnourished or normal nutritional status), geographical zone (southern, central, or northern), and sleeping under an insecticide-treated net (ITN) the night before the survey (yes or no) [17]. Household housing characteristics were also assessed, including main wall material, floor material, and roof material, each categorized as improved or unimproved. Housing materials were classified according to DHS standard definitions, whereby improved materials included finished materials such as cement, tiles, bricks, concrete, and metal roofing sheets, while unimproved materials included rudimentary or natural materials such as earth or sand floors, mud walls, thatch roofs, bamboo, palm, and other unfinished materials [17].

Data management and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata version 18. Sampling weights, clustering, and stratification were accounted for throughout the analysis using DHS complex survey design procedures to ensure nationally representative estimates. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ characteristics, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The svy command in Stata was used to apply sampling weights and adjust for clustering and stratification. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). No evidence of problematic multicollinearity was observed, as all VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold of 5, ranging from 1.03 to 3.54 (mean VIF = 1.80).

Bivariate analysis was conducted to assess the association between malaria prevalence and each independent variable using the Rao–Scott chi-square test. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in the bivariate analysis were included in the modified Poisson regression model. Because malaria prevalence was relatively high in the study population (32.3%), modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation was used to identify factors associated with malaria infection and to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) and adjusted prevalence ratios (APRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). This approach was preferred because logistic regression would have produced odds ratios that increasingly overestimate the true prevalence ratio. Therefore, modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation was employed to directly estimate prevalence ratios, an approach recommended for cross-sectional studies with common binary outcomes [36, 37]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

A total weighted sample of 4,016 children aged 6–59 months who underwent malaria rapid diagnostic testing (mRDT) was included in the analysis. Most children were aged 24–59 months (68.0%), and nearly three-quarters resided in rural areas (72.6%). Almost half of the mothers had primary education (48.5%), while 32.2% had informal education and 19.3% had secondary or higher education. Most households were headed by males (74.6%). Regarding housing conditions, 63.2% of households had improved wall materials, whereas the majority had unimproved floor materials (72.3%) and unimproved roof materials (60.0%). Nearly half of the children belonged to poor households (48.5%), and 72.5% were anemic. In addition, 39.8% of the children were malnourished. The study population was almost equally distributed between the central (42.5%) and northern (42.2%) zones, while 15.2% resided in the southern zone. Furthermore, 41.0% of the children had slept under an insecticide-treated net (ITN) the night before the survey. Detailed characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. Results of the modified Poisson regression analysis of factors associated with malaria among children aged 6–59 months in Mozambique

Maternal education was strongly associated with malaria prevalence after adjustment. Children whose mothers had primary education (APR = 2.06; 95% CI 1.52–2.81; p < 0.001) and informal education (APR = 2.04; 95% CI 1.49–2.78; p < 0.001) had about twice the prevalence of malaria compared with children whose mothers had secondary or higher education.

Compared with children from rich households, malaria prevalence was higher among children from middle-income households (APR = 1.97; 95% CI 1.43–2.70; p < 0.001) and poor households (APR = 2.41; 95% CI 1.73–3.37; p < 0.001). Children with anemia had substantially higher malaria prevalence than non-anemic children (APR = 2.89; 95% CI 2.42–3.45; p < 0.001), while malnourished children had a modestly higher prevalence than children with normal nutritional status (APR = 1.10; 95% CI 1.02–1.21; p = 0.040). Compared with the southern zone, malaria prevalence was higher in the central zone (APR = 1.92; 95% CI 1.41–2.64; p < 0.001) and northern zone (APR = 3.06; 95% CI 2.24–4.19; p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study aimed at determining the prevalence and socio-economic and geographical factors associated with malaria burden among children aged 6–59 year. The analysis found that nearly one third of children aged 6–59 months in Mozambique tested positive for malaria, confirming that malaria remains a major public health challenge among under-five children in the country [8, 13]. The prevalence observed in this study is consistent with recent national DHS findings and highlights the persistent high malaria burden despite ongoing prevention and control interventions [14, 34]. Mozambique continues to rank among the countries with the highest malaria burden globally, contributing substantially to malaria morbidity and mortality in sub-Saharan Africa [13, 14, 34].

The study demonstrated substantial geographical disparities in malaria prevalence, with the highest burden observed in northern and central provinces such as Nampula, while Cidade de Maputo reported almost no malaria cases [14]. These regional differences likely reflect variations in climate, rainfall patterns, vector density, housing conditions, urbanization, and access to preventive interventions. Northern provinces of Mozambique are characterized by higher rainfall, tropical climatic conditions, and prolonged mosquito breeding seasons, which favor sustained malaria transmission. In contrast, southern urbanized areas such as Maputo City benefit from improved housing, better sanitation infrastructure, lower vector density, and greater access to healthcare services [14, 16, 18, 19].

The higher malaria prevalence observed among older children aged 24–59 months compared with younger children is consistent with findings from other African settings. Several studies have shown that malaria risk increases with age among children under five, with those aged 36–59 months having significantly higher odds of infection than younger children [9, 11]. This pattern has been consistently observed across sub-Saharan Africa, where age remains an important predictor of malaria prevalence [8]. The increasing risk with age may partly reflect the waning of maternal immunity and the gradual increase in children’s exposure to infective mosquito bites [25]. Behavioral factors also contribute, as increased outdoor exposure has been associated with higher malaria risk in children [26]. Furthermore, preventive practices such as sleeping under insecticide-treated nets significantly reduce malaria risk, suggesting that younger children may benefit from greater protection due to closer supervision and more consistent use of preventive measures [26].

Residence in rural areas remained strongly associated with malaria infection after adjustment for confounding variables. Studies based on Mozambique survey data have shown that malaria prevalence is significantly higher in rural areas compared with urban settings, even after accounting for socioeconomic factors [14]. Similar findings across sub-Saharan Africa confirm that place of residence remains an independent predictor of malaria infection [9]. Rural communities often face constraints related to healthcare access and diagnostic capacity, which influence treatment-seeking and case management [38]. In addition, environmental and household-level factors such as poor housing conditions have been shown to increase malaria risk in rural Mozambique [29]. Barriers to achieving optimal coverage of vector control interventions, including indoor residual spraying, further contribute to persistent transmission [39]. Agricultural activities common in rural settings have also been associated with increased malaria risk, particularly through environmental modifications that favor mosquito breeding [40, 41]. These findings highlight persistent rural–urban inequities in malaria burden and underscore the need for geographically targeted interventions.

Maternal education emerged as one of the strongest predictors of malaria prevalence in this study. Previous research has consistently demonstrated that maternal education is a significant determinant of malaria risk among under-five children, with higher levels of education associated with substantially lower odds of infection [9]. Similarly, children whose mothers have secondary or higher education are less likely to test positive for malaria compared to those whose mothers have only primary or informal education [42]. Maternal education is closely linked to health literacy and healthcare-seeking behaviour, with more educated caregivers more likely to seek prompt treatment for febrile illness [27]. Furthermore, education is associated with increased uptake of preventive interventions, including insecticide-treated nets, as well as improved knowledge of malaria symptoms and prevention strategies [11, 43, 44]. These findings reinforce the broader importance of female education as a key determinant of child health outcomes in malaria-endemic settings.

Poor housing conditions, particularly unimproved floor and roof materials, were independently associated with malaria prevalence [30]. These findings align with growing evidence that improved housing can significantly reduce malaria transmission by limiting mosquito entry into households. In many rural Mozambican settings, poorly constructed houses with gaps in roofing and walls create favorable conditions for mosquito exposure during nighttime hours when malaria vectors are most active [29, 31, 45]. Housing improvement interventions may therefore represent an important complementary strategy alongside conventional vector control approaches [30, 45, 46].

Household wealth status remained a strong predictor of malaria, with children from poorer households experiencing substantially higher malaria prevalence than those from wealthier households. This is consistent with evidence from Mozambique and other sub-Saharan African countries showing that poverty increases malaria risk through poorer housing conditions, reduced access to preventive interventions and healthcare, and greater exposure to mosquito breeding environments [9, 12, 18, 28]. Similarly, children living in the northern and central regions had significantly higher malaria prevalence than those in southern Mozambique, reflecting well-documented geographical differences in climate, vector ecology, transmission intensity, and health service access [14, 16, 18, 19]. These findings reinforce the importance of geographically targeted and equity-oriented malaria control strategies [9, 14, 47].

Anemia was the strongest independent predictor of malaria in this study, highlighting the close biological relationship between malaria infection and anemia in young children. Malaria contributes to anemia through hemolysis and impaired erythropoiesis, while repeated infections may further worsen child health [7, 20]. In addition, malnourished children had a modest but significantly higher prevalence of malaria, supporting evidence that malaria and undernutrition interact in a vicious cycle, whereby poor nutritional status increases susceptibility to infection and repeated malaria episodes contribute to impaired growth and nutritional deficits [7, 8, 48]. Together, these findings underscore the need for integrated child health programmes that combine malaria prevention and treatment with nutrition and anemia interventions [10, 13, 20, 48].

The findings of this study are particularly important within the evolving malaria control landscape in Mozambique. In 2024, Mozambique introduced the WHO-recommended R21 malaria vaccine into its routine immunization programme, initially targeting high-burden districts in Zambézia Province, with plans for expansion to provinces including Nampula and Cabo Delgado [23, 24, 49]. The introduction of malaria vaccination represents a major milestone in child health and malaria prevention efforts in Mozambique. Evidence from a phase 3 clinical trial demonstrates that the R21 vaccine provides high protective efficacy against clinical malaria in African children, with efficacy estimates ranging from approximately 68% to 75% [50]. Modelling studies further suggest that widespread vaccine deployment could substantially reduce malaria cases and deaths across endemic settings [51]. However, malaria vaccines provide only partial protection, and their impact is maximized when integrated with existing interventions such as insecticide-treated nets, indoor residual spraying, and effective case management [52, 53]. Consequently, vaccination alone is unlikely to eliminate malaria transmission, particularly in settings characterized by socio-economic vulnerability and high transmission intensity, underscoring the need for sustained, integrated malaria control strategies.

Climate change is increasingly recognized as an important driver of malaria epidemiology in Mozambique and across sub-Saharan Africa. Temperature and rainfall are key determinants of malaria transmission, influencing mosquito breeding, survival, and parasite development [54, 55]. Rising temperatures, flooding events, and changing rainfall patterns have been shown to expand mosquito habitats and alter transmission dynamics across Africa [56]. Mozambique is particularly vulnerable to climate-related disasters, including cyclones and floods, which can damage infrastructure, displace communities, and increase malaria risk [57]. Extreme weather events have also been associated with disruptions to healthcare delivery and reduced access to preventive services, further exacerbating disease risk [58]. These findings indicate that climate variability is a growing contributor to malaria persistence, underscoring the need for climate-resilient malaria surveillance and control strategies.

Armed conflict and population displacement in Cabo Delgado Province may also contribute to worsening malaria vulnerability [48, 59, 60]. Evidence from sub-Saharan Africa shows that violent conflict can increase malaria risk by disrupting local environments and increasing population exposure to transmission [61]. Conflict also significantly reduces healthcare service coverage, including access to diagnosis and treatment of common childhood illnesses [62]. Systematic reviews further demonstrate that humanitarian crises promote infectious disease transmission through displacement, overcrowding, poor sanitation, and limited access to healthcare services [63]. Disruptions to malaria control interventions, such as treatment access and preventive campaigns, can lead to substantial increases in malaria morbidity and mortality [64]. These findings highlight how insecurity and displacement in northern Mozambique may undermine malaria control efforts and contribute to sustained transmission in affected populations.

Although Mozambique has made progress in scaling up malaria interventions, several challenges remain, including insecticide resistance, limited healthcare access in remote areas, health system constraints, funding gaps, and persistent socioeconomic inequalities [10, 65–67]. Global malaria control efforts have also experienced stagnation in recent years due to climate change, conflict, drug and insecticide resistance, and inadequate financing [6, 7]. Strengthening integrated malaria control approaches that combine vaccination, vector control, improved housing, maternal education, surveillance, and community engagement will be essential for reducing the malaria burden among Mozambican children [68].

Study strength and limitations

This study has several strengths. It used nationally representative DHS data with standardized data collection procedures and incorporated complex survey design adjustments, enhancing the generalizability of findings to the national population. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference between exposures and malaria infection. Additionally, important variables such as seasonal variation, environmental exposures, nutritional status, and insecticide-treated net utilization were not included in the present analysis. Residual confounding may therefore remain. Despite these limitations, the study provides important updated evidence on malaria epidemiology and associated factors among children in Mozambique using recent nationally representative data.

This study has several important strengths. It draws on recent nationally representative survey data, allowing robust estimates of malaria prevalence among children aged 6–59 months across Mozambique. The analytic approach accounted for the complex survey design and used adjusted prevalence ratios, which strengthened the assessment of independent associations between malaria and key sociodemographic and household factors. The findings also have practical value because they help identify population groups and settings that may benefit most from targeted malaria control efforts.

This study also has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Because the analysis was based on cross-sectional data, the observed relationships cannot be interpreted as causal. Malaria status was determined using rapid diagnostic tests, which may misclassify some infections, particularly in children with low parasite density or recent treatment. In addition, some potentially relevant variables were not fully captured in the dataset, including detailed environmental exposures, seasonal variation, and localized transmission conditions.

Conclusions and recommendations

Malaria remains highly prevalent among children aged 6–59 months in Mozambique and is strongly associated with socioeconomic disadvantage, rural residence, poor housing conditions, maternal education, anemia, malnutrition, and geographical location. These findings highlight the need for integrated, equity-oriented malaria control strategies that prioritize high-burden provinces and vulnerable populations while addressing the social and environmental conditions that sustain malaria transmission. Targeting these determinants alongside existing prevention and treatment interventions will be essential for accelerating malaria control and reducing childhood morbidity in Mozambique.
