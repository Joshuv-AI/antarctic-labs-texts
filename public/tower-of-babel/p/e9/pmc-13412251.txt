Beyond the Neutrophil Count: Functional Profiling and Targeted Modulation of Neutrophils in Paediatric Care
Authors: James Trayer, Lynne A. Kelly, Eoghan Dunlea, Dearbhla Byrne, Nicoleta Barbu, Eleanor J. Molloy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeutrophils are the most abundant leukocytes in childhood and form a critical component of innate immunity, particularly in early life when adaptive responses are still maturing. In paediatrics, both quantitative and qualitative disturbances in neutrophil number or function shape susceptibility to infection and inflammatory tissue injury. Emerging data from neonatal, sepsis, and allergic cohorts show that paediatric neutrophils exhibit distinct effector profiles and regulatory roles compared with adults, positioning them as central mediators of child health and disease.SummaryThis review synthesises current understanding of neutrophil biology across key paediatric settings, spanning sepsis, benign transient neutropenia, severe congenital neutropenia, neonatal brain injury, and allergic disease. In paediatric sepsis, quantitative and functional neutrophil abnormalities, including impaired trafficking, excess immature forms, dysregulated pattern-recognition signalling and excessive NETosis, contribute both to failure of pathogen control and to organ injury. In childhood neutropenia, transient post-viral marrow suppression contrasts sharply with congenital defects of granulopoiesis, in which granulocyte colony-stimulating factor therapy has transformed survival. Experimental and clinical data in hypoxic-ischaemic neonatal brain injury highlight biphasic neutrophil recruitment, with the initial phase responsible for tissue damage while the later reparative phase is beneficial. Beyond infection, neutrophils emerge as key players in asthma, allergic rhinitis, anaphylaxis, and food allergy, where neutrophilic endotypes, IgG-neutrophil pathways and degranulation signatures link environmental exposures, disease severity, and treatment response.Key MessagesNeutrophils in children display age- and context-dependent plasticity, acting as indispensable defenders against infection yet potent drivers of tissue damage when dysregulated. Paediatric care should move beyond simple neutrophil counts towards functional and phenotypic profiling to distinguish protective from pathogenic activity and to guide therapeutic management. Future therapies should focus on selectively modulating neutrophil trafficking and neutrophil extracellular trap formation, particularly in sepsis, neonatal encephalopathy and severe allergic disease, while preserving host defence in this uniquely vulnerable population.

Introduction

Neutrophils are the most abundant white blood cell and play a critical role in the innate immune system. In children, especially neonates and infants, neutrophils are critical for defending against bacterial and fungal infections [1]. Their rapid response to pathogens is the first line of immune defence but their function and regulation in early life are uniquely complex and still under active investigation. Although neutrophils are often viewed as short-lived, terminally differentiated cells, evidence now shows substantial developmental neutrophil plasticity, with dynamic changes in phenotype, transcriptional profile, and effector function across gestation, the neonatal period and childhood [1]. These developmental adaptations shape how neutrophils respond to pathogens in early life and help explain why their behaviour cannot simply be extrapolated from adult studies.

A growing area of interest is neutrophil heterogeneity. In paediatrics, increased proportions of immature neutrophils, including band forms and promyelocyte-derived subsets, are frequently observed during infection, inflammation and in neonatal physiology. Low-density neutrophils, often associated with chronic inflammation, sepsis and autoimmune conditions, also appear to be more prominent in early life compared with adults. These heterogeneous populations differ in their capacity for phagocytosis, degranulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation [2]. Early life is also characterised by features of trained immunity, where epigenetic imprinting induced by infections, microbiome interactions, or vaccines alters neutrophil function long term. Such epigenetic programming influences activation thresholds, cytokine responses, and antimicrobial capacity [3].

Paediatric neutrophils differ fundamentally from adult neutrophils across multiple biological domains. Neonatal and infant neutrophils show altered surface marker expression, reduced chemotaxis and a higher activation threshold for oxidative burst. Metabolic pathways also differ, with early-life neutrophils exhibiting reduced glycolytic reserve and distinct mitochondrial responses compared with adults [4]. Together, these developmental differences contribute to unique functional limitations and strengths, making direct extrapolation from adult neutrophil biology problematic and often misleading.

In paediatrics, neutrophil disorders such as neutropenia can lead to increased susceptibility to infections, particularly in the lungs, skin and gastrointestinal tract. Children with congenital or acquired neutropenia often require careful monitoring and, in severe cases, treatment with granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) to stimulate neutrophil production [5].

Children with severe neurological impairment demonstrate abnormal immune function compared to healthy controls. Severe neurological impairment is associated with reduced circulating monocyte counts, blunted neutrophil response to LPS and altered inflammasome transcription both at baseline and in response to LPS compared to matched healthy controls [6].

In summary, neutrophils play a crucial role in maintaining health, particularly during early life when the immune system is still maturing. Ongoing research continues to reveal their complex involvement in inflammation, infection control, and developmental processes. Their heterogeneity, epigenetic programming and distinct paediatric biology underline their complex involvement in inflammation, infection control and neurodevelopmental processes. Understanding neutrophil function and regulation is essential for identifying early markers of immune dysregulation and guiding therapeutic strategies aimed at improving outcomes across diverse paediatric populations. This review aims to highlight the important role of neutrophils as age-dependent, context-sensitive, and phenotypically plastic cells across a diverse array of common childhood conditions, including sepsis, neutropenia, allergy, and neonatal brain injury, with a view to identifying common themes for clinical intervention and improving outcomes in paediatric populations.

Sepsis

Sepsis is a major cause of morbidity and mortality amongst children, with almost half of all estimated sepsis cases worldwide occurring in children under 5 years of age [7]. The WHO has declared sepsis a global health priority given its incidence, mortality rate and risk of long-term neurodevelopmental sequelae, especially in the neonatal population [8]. The Phoenix sepsis criteria for sepsis and septic shock in children identify children with potentially life-threatening organ dysfunction secondary to infection [9]. Neutrophils play a vital role in our first response to sepsis and infection; this is especially true for the paediatric population and neonates. The neonatal population rely heavily on innate immune function for protection against pathogens [10]. Differences exist in the innate immune responses amongst paediatric patients and adult patients [10], as seen during the COVID-19 pandemic [11]. There are both quantitative and qualitative differences in neutrophils between adults and children [1]. These differences affect how children fight infection.

Neutrophils are essential for normal immune system function and are the most plentiful immune cell in the body. Neutrophils are complex cells, mounting immune responses via various mechanisms such as phagocytosis, bactericidal enzyme production, reactive oxygen species and NETs [12]. Neutrophils are key to an optimally functioning innate immune system, and work with adaptive immunity to modulate responses [13]. During infection, neutrophils migrate to sites of infection through complex mechanisms including margination, rolling, adhesion and transmigration to arrive at their intended destination [14]. The role of neutrophils in fighting infection and sepsis has been described as a double-edged sword as although essential for immune function and repair but excessive neutrophil responses can result in tissue damage [15] (Fig. 1). Their importance in the paediatric population can be highlighted by diseases where neutrophils are deficient or their function affected such as congenital neutropenia or chronic granulomatous disease where children are predisposed to severe, often life-threatening infections [16].

Despite their importance, hyper-activation of this immune cell is associated with tissue damage, particularly during acute inflammatory responses [17]. Dysregulated neutrophil activity is implicated in sepsis in both adults and children [18]. During sepsis, the normally well-coordinated process of neutrophil activation and migration becomes disrupted. Proinflammatory cytokines during sepsis drive increased production of neutrophils in the bone marrow, resulting in a higher proportion of immature neutrophils entering the circulation. These proinflammatory cytokines also impair migration of neutrophils due to altered expression of adhesion molecules by vascular endothelial cells and neutrophil cytoskeletal dysfunction, leading to increased rigidity and impaired transmigration [18]. Reduced margination and rolling of these neutrophils leads to sequestration in blood vessels and ultimately ischaemia and end-organ damage [19]. Additionally, originally protective functions such as NETosis can cause significant tissue damage [20]. NETs may help trap pathogens but also drive endothelial activation, glycocalyx shedding, barrier failure and a procoagulant switch that promotes microvascular thrombosis and organ hypoperfusion [21, 22].

In paediatric and neonatal sepsis, this process is shaped by developmental endothelial and innate immune differences: neonatal endothelial cells express lower levels of adhesion molecules and have weaker antioxidant capacity, while neonatal neutrophils show reduced recruitment and, in many settings, diminished NET formation, especially in preterm infants [23]. Despite this relative hyporesponsiveness, severe neonatal sepsis is still characterised by activation of the endothelium, release of von Willebrand factor, platelet and monocyte recruitment, tissue factor exposure, and thrombosis, all of which can amplify disseminated intravascular coagulation and multiorgan dysfunction [24].

During sepsis, the basic functions of these first-line responders are altered; one such reason is abnormal pattern recognition receptor (PRR) function. PRRs, including toll-like receptors, NOD-like receptors and others, recognise pathogen-associated molecular patterns on invading pathogens and activate the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, triggering pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Excessive early PRR activation may contribute to cytokine storm and associated tissue damage. As the infection progresses, these receptors may become saturated, leading to a blunted neutrophil response and immunoparalysis [25, 26]. Similarly, neutrophil exhaustion occurs during periods of prolonged immune system activation such as sepsis. Although circulating neutrophils may remain numerically elevated and phenotypically activated, they often exhibit reduced chemotaxis, dysregulated oxidative burst, delayed apoptosis, and abnormal NET release, which together compromise bacterial clearance while contributing to tissue injury and immune dysregulation [19]. This biphasic model of sepsis/inflammation with an early hyperinflammatory phase followed by a later immune suppressive phase is mirrored in hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE) and will be discussed in more detail below [27]. Neutrophils in patients with sepsis also show higher levels of programmed cell death ligand 1 with a positive correlation with disease severity [28].

As neutrophils are consumed while fighting infection, G-CSF and IL-6 signalling trigger emergency granulopoiesis leading to the release of immature neutrophils into the circulation. While this may maintain circulating neutrophil numbers, these immature cells may display aberrant function, impaired migration or altered effector activity leading to a maladaptive response during sepsis [29]. In children with severe infection caused by respiratory syncytial virus there is a marked reduction in the adaptive immune response with an activation of emergency myelopoiesis [30]. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio has been shown to correlate with disease severity [30].

Neonatal sepsis is characterised by a qualitatively and quantitatively immature neutrophil response that differs from that of older children or adults. Neonates have a limited marrow storage pool, reduced capacity for rapid neutrophil mobilisation, and a greater tendency to peripheral neutropenia, while their circulating neutrophils also show impaired chemotaxis, adhesion, phagocytosis, oxidative metabolism, and bacterial killing [1]. A recent study suggests that morphological changes in neutrophils can serve as a biomarker for early diagnosis of neonatal sepsis [31]. These morphological changes can be measured using analysers routinely available in hospital laboratories and highlight the importance of translational neutrophil research in children, although further investigation is required to see if this can be applied to clinical practice. In contrast, older children more often mount a more mature granulocytic response, with stronger neutrophil recruitment and functional effector activity, although severe sepsis can still lead to secondary neutrophil dysfunction. As a result, neonatal sepsis often reflects immune immaturity rather than the later exhausted or dysregulated phenotype more typical of prolonged critical illness in older patients [1]. Understanding the complex function of neutrophils and their dysfunction during sepsis is key to developing immunomodulatory therapies in the future.

Neutropenia in Childhood

Neutropenia is a common finding in children [32]. In many cases, neutropenia is a transient phenomenon usually caused by viral illness [33, 34] (Fig. 1). Many different viruses have been implicated and are thought to cause neutropenia through transient suppression of myelopoiesis, as well as peripheral destruction of neutrophils [35]. Neutropenia is generally graded as either mild (1–1.5 × 109/L), moderate (0.5–1 × 109/L), or severe (<0.5 × 109/L) [36]. Symptoms of neutropenia are reflective of neutrophils key role in host defence against bacterial and fungal pathogens, as well as wound healing, and include mouth ulcers, gingivitis, bacterial and rarely, invasive fungal infections [37]. Neutropenic sepsis remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality in children on chemotherapy treatment for cancer, where disruption of the gut mucosal lining in combination with severe neutropenia can result in invasive bloodstream infections [38].

Remarkably, most children with neutropenia are asymptomatic, even with severe neutropenia [39]. The likelihood of symptomatic neutropenia is largely down to the underlying aetiology [35, 40, 41]. Only ∼5% of the body’s neutrophils are measurable in blood samples [42]. Most of the total body neutrophil mass are stored in the bone marrow or the marginated pool in the lungs, liver and spleen, ready to be released in the event of inflammation or infection. If production of neutrophils in the bone marrow is normal, but they are being destroyed peripherally, then there are usually adequate neutrophils to respond to bacterial infection or tissue damage [43]. An illustrative example of this is primary autoimmune neutropenia of infancy. This rare condition is usually diagnosed in children aged 6–12 months and often causes severe neutropenia. Antibodies to neutrophil antigens such as CD16, CD11b (also known as human neutrophil antigens 1 and 4, respectively) are usually detectable, and are thought to cause antibody mediated destruction of neutrophils. This condition usually resolves after an average of 2 years [44]. While a propensity to mild bacterial infections is seen, severe or invasive infections are rare, and many children are largely asymptomatic. These children can form abscesses and usually have normal tissue healing and repair [41, 44, 45].

In contrast, children with congenital neutropenia are severely affected and at risk of life-threatening invasive bacterial infection [16, 40]. In congenital neutropenia, pathogenic mutations in various genes result in defective neutrophil production [16]. Defects in neutrophil function in some forms of congenital neutropenia are also documented, for example, in Shwachman Diamond Syndrome [46]. The most common cause of isolated congenital neutropenia is heterozygous mutations in the ELANE gene [47]. In this condition, accumulation of defective neutrophil elastase in the endoplasmic reticulum results in activation of the unfolded protein response mechanism, ultimately leading to apoptosis of neutrophil precursors at the promyelocyte stage of maturation and severe neutropenia [48].

Historically, many children with severe congenital neutropenia would die in childhood from overwhelming infection, but G-CSF therapy has dramatically improved outcomes by reducing neutropenic infections [16]. With most children now surviving to adulthood, there is a risk of acute myeloid leukaemia which occurs in approximately 20–30% of patients with severe congenital neutropenia [16]. While there was concern that G-CSF therapy might be leukemogenic, this has never been demonstrated in clinical studies, and G-CSF is not thought to increase the risk of leukaemic transformation [49, 50]). Leukemia risk is instead thought to be related to the underlying genetic lesion, and the mechanism of leukemogenesis is described by the concept of somatic genetic rescue. This is where certain acquired DNA mutations in oncogenes of haematopoietic stem cells actually improve the replicative potential of the haematopoietic stem cell by ameliorating the underlying defect. For example, in severe congenital neutropenia there is frequent development of premature truncating mutations in the CSF3R gene (the G-CSF receptor) [51], leading to defective internalisation and constitutive activation of G-CSF receptor signalling with resultant reduced apoptosis of neutrophil precursors and enhanced neutrophil production [52]. While some mutations can be advantageous, ameliorating the phenotype of the underlying disorder, mutations in certain oncogenes, for example TP53 or CSF3R, can lead to the development of acute myeloid leukaemia [53].

Recent advances in the care and monitoring of patients with neutropenia include the publication of international consensus guidelines on the diagnosis, monitoring and treatment of neutropenia patients [36]. Careful periodic monitoring using cytogenetics and NGS panels can detect pre-leukemic somatic genetic changes, allowing pre-emptive treatment with bone marrow transplantation prior to the onset of leukemia [54]. While G-CSF therapy has been revolutionary, novel therapies are also being developed. Mavorixafor, an oral CXCR4 antagonist, is effective for the treatment of neutropenia in Warts, Hypogammaglobulinaemia, Infections and Myelokathexis syndrome, and has shown promise in other forms of congenital neutropenia [55, 56].

The Role of Neutrophils in Allergic Disease

As well as their primary role in fighting bacterial infection, there is a growing body of evidence that neutrophils play a role in allergic conditions. This includes conditions such as asthma, food allergy, drug allergy, and allergic rhinitis (Fig. 1). In childhood, these conditions are highly prevalent and clinically important, and the contribution of neutrophils appears to vary across age, phenotype, and disease severity. Taking paediatric asthma as an example, neutrophilic inflammation is more often linked to severe or difficult-to-control disease and reduced corticosteroid responsiveness, whereas allergic asthma in younger children is more commonly type 2/eosinophilic [57, 58].

Asthma is reported to affect over 350 million people worldwide, making it one of the most common chronic diseases [59]. Rather than a single condition, asthma presents with different phenotypes that can be differentiated by the predominant cell type in sputum. In children, the most common phenotype is allergic asthma (Th2-mediated), where a high proportion of eosinophils are present in the sputum. There is a smaller cohort of patients with neutrophilic asthma, which is Th2 low and predominantly driven by IL-17 from Th17 cells [57]. These patients are typically older and frequently have more severe disease requiring frequent courses of oral steroids and hospitalisation [60, 61].

Airway epithelial cells can detect inhaled allergens, pollutants and infections through pattern-recognition receptors and alarmin pathways, then release CXCL8, IL-33, TSLP, and other mediators that recruit and activate neutrophils [62, 63]. Downstream intracellular signalling in neutrophils includes TLR4, FPR, PKC, and MAPK-linked pathways, together with NF-κB activation, which promote adhesion, chemotaxis, degranulation, survival and ROS generation [64]. Neutrophilic asthma is typically less responsive to corticosteroids due to defective glucocorticoid receptor α and impaired histone deacetylase 2 [58], which normally work to reduce inflammation by suppressing genes coding for pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-8 and TNF-α [65].

The development of neutrophilic asthma is associated with environmental pollution. Risk factors include living near to major roads [66], smoking [67] and geographic variability in epidemiological studies maps high rates to countries with significant air pollution [68, 69]. Infancy and childhood is a time of rapid growth and development. Exposure to environmental pollutants including black carbon, nitrogen dioxide, and fine particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10) in early life is associated with increased risk of developing asthma [70, 71]. NETosis is one mechanism through which these factors contribute to lung inflammation. NET production can be induced by inflammatory cytokines such as IL-33 [72], IL-17 [73], IL-1β [74], as well as infectious and environmental triggers including LPS [75], viruses [76] and diesel exhaust particles [77]. Sputum extracellular DNA and NET components are elevated in asthmatics and correlate with disease severity [74, 78].

Neutrophil-derived mediators appear to be important effectors of tissue injury in allergic airway disease. Elastase promotes mucus secretion and epithelial activation; ROS contribute to epithelial damage and oxidant stress; lipid mediators such as LTB4 can support neutrophil recruitment and survival; and NET-associated products may worsen barrier dysfunction and airway obstruction [62, 79]. Neutrophil-derived extracellular vesicles and exosomes are also emerging as potential mediators of airway remodelling, including effects on airway smooth muscle proliferation and inflammatory amplification, although their role in paediatric allergic disease remains incompletely defined [80].

In allergic rhinitis, the immediate phase response is mediated by mast cell release of preformed mediators such as histamine. The late phase reaction is characterised by localised inflammation with an increase in neutrophil numbers within the nasal mucosa and nasal lavage fluid with a higher proportion of activated neutrophils [81]. As well as inflammation, there is evidence that neutrophils can act as antigen presenting cells in people with birch pollen allergy, inducing proliferation of allergen specific T cells [82, 83]. Immunotherapy has been shown to reduce this neutrophil driven late phase response, in addition to secondary recruitment and activation by mast cells [84, 85]. House dust mite, one of the most common inhaled allergens, can directly activate neutrophils via protein components including Der p2, Der f2, and Der p38, binding to TLR4 [86]. Similar neutrophil activation is seen when stimulated with grass pollens [85].

In mice, IgG-neutrophil-mediated allergy is common. Evidence for a similar pathway in humans is less clear, though a paper by Jonnson et al. [87] demonstrated that in patients with an allergy to neuromuscular blocking agents, there is an IgG-neutrophil-mediated pathway that may coexist and amplify IgE-mediated allergy to neuromuscular blocking agents.

Food allergy is common in paediatrics, affecting 3–10% of children [88]. Most food allergies are IgE-mediated, with symptoms due to degranulation of mast cells and basophils. There is some evidence that neutrophils may play an amplifying role in the allergic reaction to foods. Much of our understanding of the mechanisms underlying anaphylaxis comes from the study of mice. In these models, neutrophil depletion serves to reduce anaphylaxis severity [89]. In human studies, anaphylaxis is associated with neutrophilia, increased myeloperoxidase and platelet-activating factor, all of which correlate with reaction severity [90–92]. In a recent paediatric oral immunotherapy study, suppression of neutrophil-related transcriptomic signatures in the treatment group further supported a role for neutrophils in food allergy [93]. Overall, paediatric allergic disease likely reflects a dynamic balance between epithelial activation, granulocyte cross-talk and neutrophil effector functions, with the dominant pathway varying by age, disease stage and environmental exposure.

The Role of Neutrophils in Neonatal Brain Injury

Neutrophils serve as critical mediators of neuroinflammation in neonatal brain injury, exhibiting distinct behavioural patterns compared to adult systems [27, 94, 95]. Following a hypoxic-ischaemic insult, injured brain tissue rapidly upregulates neutrophil chemokines, including IL-1, IL-6, and CXCL1, triggering systemic neutrophil activation and recruitment within hours [94, 95]. Remarkably, neonatal neutrophils accumulate in brain vasculature as early as 4 h post injury but demonstrate delayed extravasation into brain parenchyma, remaining intravascular for 24–48 h – a pattern substantially different from adult response [95, 96]. Studies reveal biphasic neutrophil recruitment: an early pro-inflammatory wave producing elevated ROS and inflammatory cytokines, followed by a later reparative wave expressing arginase-1 and transforming growth factor-beta [27].

Neutrophil-mediated injury occurs through multiple mechanisms. NETs form rapidly following hypoxic-ischaemic injury and disrupt the blood-brain barrier through activation of the NLRP-3 inflammasome [97–99]. Peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) is essential for NETosis in neonatal mouse HIE models. Pharmacological PAD4 inhibition (preclinical rodent studies) significantly reduces inflammatory cytokine levels, brain tissue loss, and improves survival [97, 99]. Oxidative stress from NADPH oxidase-mediated ROS production directly damages neurons and activates inflammatory pathways; neonatal brains are particularly vulnerable due to underdeveloped antioxidant defences [100, 101]. Matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), secreted by neutrophils, degrades basement membrane components and facilitates BBB breakdown – plasma MMP-9 levels in neonates with encephalopathy (441.7 ± 91.5 ng/mL) substantially exceed adult stroke levels [102]. Inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 amplify neuroinflammation and recruit additional immune cells [103–105]; elevated IL-8 correlates with HIE severity and mortality [104, 106].

Therapeutic targeting of neutrophils shows promise: prophylactic neutrophil depletion reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines and brain tissue loss [95, 107], though post-insult depletion proves ineffective, indicating a critical early therapeutic window [95]. Therapeutic hypothermia, the current standard treatment in neonatal encephalopathy caused by HIE, modulates peripheral immune responses but paradoxically enhances NET formation, suggesting combination therapies targeting NET-mediated inflammation may optimise outcomes [98]. This occurs through hypothermia-amplified NLRP3 inflammasome activation and Cit-H3 expression in neonatal rodent brains (rat model), peaking early post-TH despite overall neuroprotection. Emerging strategies include selective suppression of pro-inflammatory neutrophil infiltration, promotion of earlier transition to reparative phenotypes, and modulation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 signalling pathways [27]. Understanding neonatal-specific immune responses is essential for developing age-appropriate neuroprotective interventions targeting neutrophil-mediated injury while preserving endogenous repair mechanisms.

Most mechanistic insights (e.g., neutrophil timing, NETosis, depletion effects [27, 94–99, 107]) derive from neonatal rodent (primarily mouse/rat) HIE models, with limited direct human data beyond biomarkers like MMP-9 and IL-8. Key hurdles include species differences in brain maturation (human gyrification absent in rodents), immune ontogeny (human neonatal neutrophils less responsive), and BBB development, complicating therapy translation, such as PAD4 inhibitors from preclinical success to human trials.

Discussion

Neutrophils occupy a central yet paradoxical position in paediatric health: they are simultaneously indispensable defenders against infection and potential mediators of tissue injury (Fig. 1). This review summarises our current understanding of neutrophil function across diverse paediatric conditions, revealing common themes that warrant both clinical attention and further investigation.

As the most numerous circulating leucocyte, neutrophils play a key role in innate immunity. In children, neutropenia is common [32], usually secondary to viral infections [33, 34], and in the majority of cases, it remains asymptomatic [39]. In contrast, children born with inborn errors of neutrophil production or function have historically died from overwhelming sepsis. The advent of G-CSF therapy has dramatically improved outcomes for children with congenital neutropenia. Recent advances in the treatment and monitoring of congenital neutropenia patients will hopefully continue to improve outcomes for these life-threatening conditions.

The innate immune system, and neutrophils in particular, differ between children and adults [1, 10], and death from sepsis remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide [7]. Despite their vital role in protecting the body from infection, neutrophils are also implicated in tissue damage. This can be due to overactivation during the acute immune response [11], impaired migration [19], or excessive NETosis [20].

Similar dysregulation leading to tissue damage is seen in neonatal brain injury caused by a hypoxic-ischaemic event. Neutrophils demonstrate delayed extravasation and biphasic recruitment patterns distinct from adult responses [95, 96]. Early pro-inflammatory neutrophils produce damaging reactive oxygen species and matrix metalloproteinases that disrupt the blood-brain barrier, whilst later reparative phenotypes may support tissue repair [27]. Notably, therapeutic hypothermia, the current standard neuroprotective intervention, paradoxically enhances NET formation, highlighting the need for combination therapies that selectively suppress early inflammatory responses whilst preserving later reparative functions.

Beyond their role in acute infection, there is growing evidence that neutrophils are involved in allergic diseases. Neutrophilic asthma phenotypes, associated with environmental pollution and steroid resistance, represent a particularly challenging clinical entity requiring novel therapeutic approaches. The identification of IgG-neutrophil pathways in drug allergy [87] and the correlation between neutrophilia, myeloperoxidase, and platelet-activating factor with anaphylaxis severity provides mechanistic insight and potential biomarkers for risk stratification [90–92]. The decrease in transcription factors associated with neutrophil degranulation in peanut-allergic children treated with oral immunotherapy further supports the role played by neutrophils as well as their potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target [93].

This review highlights that neutrophils exhibit remarkable phenotypic plasticity across developmental stages, disease states and tissue microenvironments. The neonatal immune system’s heavy reliance on innate immunity, coupled with unique neutrophil behavioural patterns, necessitates age-appropriate therapeutic interventions rather than extrapolation from adult studies or animal models. There are additional factors that need to be considered in paediatric neutrophil research. Preterm infants have a circulating blood volume of 70–100 mL/kg, which in an 850-g infant would be just 60 mLs [108]. These infants are often critically unwell, so research sample volumes are often incredibly small. Future research should prioritise (1) identifying biomarkers that distinguish protective from pathogenic neutrophil activity, (2) developing therapies that modulate specific neutrophil phenotypes whilst preserving host defence, and (3) understanding how genetic, environmental and developmental factors shape neutrophil function.
