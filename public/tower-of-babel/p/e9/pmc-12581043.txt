Tracking real-world persistence and adherence to anti-VEGF intravitreal therapy with prefilled syringes: a German prescription sales analysis
Authors: Patrizia Brunner, Prateek Havanur, Steffeni Papukchieva, Benjamin Friedrich, Focke Ziemssen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIntravitreal antivascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy is the standard treatment for neovascular age-related macular degeneration and other retinal vascular diseases. Despite proven efficacy in clinical trials, real-world data suggest suboptimal persistence and adherence. This study aimed to assess therapy continuity in routine practice using pharmacy-based dispensing data in Germany, focusing on treatment with prefilled syringes (PFS).Methods and analysisThis retrospective cohort study used anonymised nationwide pharmacy sales data from Germany to evaluate patients who initiated anti-VEGF therapy with PFS between 2020 and 2023. Patient trajectories were followed from treatment initiation for up to 12 months. 46 546 therapy beginners with 297 454 anti-VEGF PFS purchases were included. Discontinuation of treatment was defined by not purchasing a relevant medication for >6 months.ResultsMean time on therapy for all patients was 8.1±8.2 months (median=5.5) with 6.9±6.1 purchases (median=5). 20.5% of patients initiating anti-VEGF treatment stopped therapy before completing the loading phase (first three injections), and more than half of all patients discontinued treatment after 6 months. 49.4% of the patients who completed the loading phase (n all=34 895) adhered to the recommended monthly regimen. Non-adherence significantly decreased the number of injections later on (average of 7.5 vs 8.7 injections). Further, patients who stayed longer on therapy bought more PFSs within the first 6 and 12 months.ConclusionsReal-world adherence and persistence to intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy in Germany are suboptimal, even with the use of PFS formulation. The high rate of early treatment discontinuation, especially during the loading phase, underscores the need for improved patient support, education and flexible treatment approaches to sustain long-term outcomes. A ‘breaking point’ or sensitive phase already seems to exist right at the beginning of therapy.Keywords: Macula, Macular Degeneration, Treatment Medical

Introduction

Intravitreal therapies (IVTs) with biologics/active substances against vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) or angiopoietin 2 are the gold-standard treatment for several sight-threatening retinal conditions, including neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), diabetic macular oedema (DME) and retinal vein occlusion (RVO).1 2 By delivering medication directly into the vitreous cavity, these therapies inhibit abnormal vascular proliferation, stabilise the blood–retina barrier and help preserve or improve visual acuity in affected patients.3 4

Despite their clinical efficacy, anti-VEGF therapies require frequent, long-term administration as a life-long repeated invasive procedure—often monthly or on a pro re nata (PRN) basis, or following a Treat and Extend (T&E) regimen, representing a significant burden for patients.5 This treatment burden presents significant challenges to patients and healthcare systems. Adherence and persistence to IVT are critical to achieving optimal outcomes, as demonstrated in randomised controlled trials (RCTs),6,9 where patients typically receive regular follow-up and adhere closely to injection schedules. However, real-world data consistently show that treatment continuity is substantially lower outside controlled trial settings.10

In Germany, healthcare is primarily organised under a statutory health insurance system, which covers the majority of the population. It ensures that all insured individuals have access to necessary medical services, including ophthalmologic care and intravitreal injections, largely without direct out-of-pocket costs. Several claims-based studies have shown that up to two-thirds of patients discontinue anti-VEGF therapy within 6 months of initiation.11,13 While specific adherence rates may vary, studies have consistently shown that a substantial proportion of patients receive fewer injections than recommended by standard treatment protocols (typically three initial loading injections followed by ongoing PRN or T&E regimens), indicating early undertreatment in clinical practice.14,17 Delay and undertreatment lead to an avoidable loss of visual acuity and reduce the overall health-related economic benefits associated with effective anti-VEGF therapy.18 Multiple factors contribute to this drop-off, including fear of injections, logistical burdens, poor visual outcomes and lack of caregiver support.19,22 (1) Patients experience a high treatment/follow-up burden and have to show long-term commitment.23 (2) Patients suffer from fear of injections and/or side effects.24 (3) Adherence is often affected by visual acuity and outcome. The lack of a perceived improvement or high expectations can lead to disappointed patients and a lack of motivation to continue a demanding treatment. (4) Patient demographics and structural factors contribute to lower adherence rates, particularly among older patients and those with comorbidities, greater travel distances to treatment centres or limited social support from caregivers and their environment.

Therapy regimens with less burden for the patient (such as ‘treat-and-extend’) and newer agents designed to prolong injection intervals, including faricimab, aflibercept (8 mg) and the port delivery system, are continuously being developed to minimise visit frequency and improve long-term adherence.25 26

Prefilled syringes (PFSs) offer a more convenient and potentially more standardised approach to anti-VEGF administration. The risk of disruptive air inclusions and the probability of contamination (and therefore very rare endophthalmitis) can be minimised compared with vials.27,31 A faster and more efficient procedure in the immediate preparation of the injection under sterile conditions is supported. However, it remains unclear whether the use of PFS affects or improves patient persistence or adherence in real-world settings. This real-world data analysis uses prescription sales data from Germany to assess patterns of persistence and adherence to intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy, independent of ophthalmic indication, delivered via PFS. Specifically, we investigate (1) therapy discontinuation rates and timing, (2) adherence to the recommended loading phase and (3) the association between early adherence and long-term treatment continuity. Other real-world analysis projects in Germany were restricted to the differences in medication usage32 or focused solely on specific agents (eg, brolucizumab33). Our findings aim to inform strategies that support sustained patient engagement and optimise visual outcomes in chronic retinal diseases.

Materials and methods

Data source and collection of German prescription sales data

The data for this analysis were provided by the Permea platform (Temedica), comprising patient-level pharmacy transactions from 8246 community pharmacies across Germany. The dataset covered approximately 44% of the retail pharmacy market and included all relevant intravitreal anti-VEGF injections dispensed between January 2020 and November 2023. PZN numbers corresponding to PFSs only were used for analysis, to be able to correlate each purchase with a single injection. The PFSs analysed here included the active substances aflibercept, brolucizumab and ranibizumab. All three were approved on the German market for the whole observation time frame (2021–2023). Brolucizumab was first approved for nAMD and its use got expanded for treating DME in April 2022 (EMA/130586/2022). While aflibercept and ranibizumab were both approved for nAMD, DME, RVO and other causes of choroidal neovascularisation (CNV) for the whole observation time frame (EMA/885871/2022 and EMA/554038/2018). It is important to note that the PFS of aflibercept only includes the low dose on the market (injected dose: 2 mg) and not the high dose (8 mg), which was only available as solution in vials at the time of this study. Brolucizumab was only available as a PFS on the market and no corresponding solution in a vial was existent.

The data were stored on the Permea platform in a General Data Protection Regulation compliant manner and no personal information, which might allow identification of individuals, was revealed. As this was a retrospective analysis based on prescription sales data, patients were not involved in this study.

Data processing and analysis

A washout period of 12 months was used to identify new therapy initiators. Patients were included if they received their first anti-VEGF PFS between January 2021 and July 2023 and had no IVT prescriptions in 2020. From a total of 854 386 transactions related to the relevant IVT injections from 85 711 unique customers, specifically, we excluded all individuals in the observation period of 2021–2023 who had filled a prescription for an IVT injection in the year 2020 (n=34 403). Individuals who cashed more than 100 prescriptions within the time frame (n=1) as well as individuals who cashed more than 1 prescription on the same date or with the same prescription (n=4761) were excluded. This was necessary to be able to correlate each purchase with one injection and calculate therapy adherence and injection intervals. We further excluded purchases cashed >6 months after the last purchase (n=20 459), since we defined >6 month break in therapy as discontinuation of therapy, according to other studies.34 This also led to an exclusion of patients who started therapy in the last 6 months of the time frame (n=5494) for the time on therapy and discontinuation analyses. These patients were included in the analysis of purchase intervals of the first three injections as well as the purchases over time on therapy analysis, as here the results were not influenced by the start date within the time frame or the absolute time on therapy.

This resulted in 46 546 unique individuals and 297 454 transactions overall and 41 052 unique individuals with 284 572 IVT injection purchases for the time on therapy analyses. The data were further processed and analysed with Python V.3.9. Statistical tests were conducted with Prism V.9.3.1. Welch t-test or Welch analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed and a p<0.05 deemed as significant.

Time on therapy and drop-off

Therapy discontinuation was defined according to the interval without any further treatment, for example, 3–12 months.9 17 34 35 Therefore, persistence was defined as the duration from first to last prescription without a treatment gap exceeding 180 days. Patients were grouped based on therapy initiation into half-year cohorts to account for variable follow-up duration. Drop-off rates were calculated at 6, 12 and 24 months.

Adherence

Adherence was assessed via the timing of the first three injections (loading phase). According to guideline recommendations, monthly injections (≤33 days between purchases) are expected during this phase. Patients with ≥3 purchases were categorised as:

Monthly loading: all intervals ≤33 days.

Monthly loading: all intervals ≤33 days.

Irregular loading: at least one interval >33 days.

Irregular loading: at least one interval >33 days.

Injection frequency

Total number of prescriptions over time on therapy was analysed in monthly intervals. To explore the relationship between early adherence and longer-term persistence, patients were stratified by minimum therapy duration (≥6, ≥12 or ≥24 months), and average injections in the first 6 or 12 months were compared across groups.

Patients who started therapy within the last 6 months of the data frame for indicated analyses were excluded. We further grouped customers according to their first purchases in five half-yearly start of therapy groups covering the first or the last 6 months of a year: 2021–1, 2021–2, 2022–1, 2022–2, 2023–1.

The minimum time on therapy was defined as the date between the first prescription and the date of the last prescription within the observation period.

Therapy regimen and intervals

Adherence was assessed based on the first three injections (‘loading phase’). To analyse the individual purchase intervals of these injections each patient adhered to, the time between purchases was calculated in days and then collected in defined buckets, creating time intervals of weeks to months. Intervals were collected in buckets of ≤4 weeks (1–33 days), 6 weeks (34–47 days), 8 weeks (48–61 days), 10 weeks (62–75 days), 12 weeks (76–89 days) and >12 weeks (90–180 days). All patients who bought ≥3 PFSs were included in this analysis, no matter their therapy start or time on therapy (n=34 895). They were then analysed for their loading pattern and grouped according to performing the loading phase either regular (monthly) or irregular (at least one injection purchased after >4 weeks after the last). The two groups were analysed regarding the overall number of purchases during therapy. A Sankey diagram was created using SankeyMATIC.

Descriptive statistics are reported as means±SD or medians with IQR, where appropriate. Between-group comparisons were performed using Welch’s t-tests and Welch ANOVA. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among 41 052 patients initiating anti-VEGF therapy with PFS between January 2021 and July 2023, the majority (85.3%) were older than 66 years. Younger patients were rare, with only 9.5% aged 56–65 years, 3.1% aged 46–55 years and less than 2% aged below 45 years. The mean time on therapy was 8.1±8.2 months (median: 5.5) and the mean number of injections was 6.9±6.1 (median: 5) (table 1). The range between the extremes was pronounced: The minimum number of purchases was one with a time on therapy of 0 days, because no further PFS was bought. The maximum number of purchases in the dataset was 66 injections within 1022 days of treatment (1 customer) and the maximum time on treatment 1055 days with 29 bought injections (1 customer).

Early drop-off and time on therapy

Early drop-off was frequent: 20.5% of patients initiating anti-VEGF treatment stopped therapy before completing the loading phase (first three injections), and 13.1% of patients (n=5368) discontinued after a single injection (ie, time on therapy=0 days). The most common discontinuation timepoint was at 2 months (17.2%, n=7057). Overall, 55.4% of patients discontinued within the first 6 months of therapy (figure 1A,B). Stratification by start cohort showed no meaningful differences in early discontinuation trends (online supplemental figure 1). Injection frequency by therapy duration

We then analysed whether patients who stayed on therapy longer (24 vs 12 vs 6 months) showed differences in therapy adherence within the first 6 or 12 months of therapy. Here, we grouped patients into the minimum total time on therapy and calculated their average number of purchases within the first months. Patients who persisted for ≥24 months had significantly more injections in the first 6 months compared with those who discontinued at 6 months (mean difference: +0.3 injections; p<0.05, figure 2B). A similar association was seen at 12 months (figure 2C).

Impact of loading phase adherence

To gain a better understanding of the therapy journey of patients on anti-VEGF medication, we analysed the purchase intervals of the first three purchases.

We collected the purchase intervals in buckets ranging from ≤4 weeks (recommended) to >12 weeks (max. 180 days as defined with the discontinuation criteria). Important to note here is that purchasing the injection has to take place before actual administration of the injection; therefore, we limited the ≤4 weeks interval to a max. of 33 days, including a buffer for weekends and holidays.

Of the 34 895 patients with ≥3 injections, 68% received their second injection within 4 weeks, but only 49.4% maintained monthly timing through the third dose (figure 3A). Thus, just under half adhered to the recommended monthly loading schedule.

According to these purchases, patients were then grouped into ‘monthly loading’ and ‘irregular loading’ and analysed regarding their total number of purchases on therapy (independent of time on therapy and also including therapy starters in 2023–2). Therapy beginners who performed the loading according to the recommended monthly regimen bought significantly more injections over the whole therapy (8.7) compared with patients who performed the loading irregularly (7.5; figure 3B).

In summary, real-world persistence and adherence to intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy using PFSs in Germany were markedly suboptimal. The average time on therapy was 8.1 months with 6.9 injections per patient, and 55.9% of patients discontinued within the first 6 months. The most frequent drop-off points occurred after the first, third and sixth injections, with 13.1% receiving only one injection and 19.0% stopping after three. Nearly half of all patients (49.4%) completed the loading phase at monthly intervals; those who adhered to this schedule received significantly more injections overall. Patients who remained on therapy for at least 6, 12 or 24 months had already received more injections during the initial 6 months, underscoring the importance of the early treatment phase as a critical window for long-term engagement and a key opportunity for targeted patient support.

Discussion

Our analysis reveals that persistence and adherence to intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy using PFS in Germany are suboptimal. Over half of patients discontinued within 6 months, with marked drop-off points after the first, third and sixth injections. Early treatment behaviour—particularly adherence to the loading phase—was strongly associated with longer-term persistence and higher injection counts. Our findings show no relevant differences compared with earlier studies based on vials, off-label anti-VEGF use or historical cohorts. Although PFSs offer theoretical advantages in terms of safety and efficacy,27,30 this factor did not appear to influence early treatment discontinuation. Furthermore, the introduction of PFS formulations may have initially delayed the adoption of biosimilars that were not yet available in this format; however, this had no measurable impact on early treatment discontinuation.

The findings suggest that the initial treatment period is a critical leverage point for improving long-term outcomes in real-world practice. Persistence in routine clinical care remains consistently lower than in RCTs, where adherence is closely monitored.5 9 According to a more recent evaluation, only 16% of nAMD patients did not complete 2-year treatment with aflibercept or faricimab in a phase 3 RCT,36 while a meta-analysis of non-persistence in the real world depicted rates of 30% patient-led discontinuation at 12 months due to a combination of dissatisfaction with treatment results, comorbidities, motivation loss and commuting burden.37

Our findings are comparable to recent German claims-based studies, which reported non-persistence rates of 51.4% for nAMD and 62.6% for DME after 12 months.13 34 While absolute persistence estimates were similar, our study provides additional insights by analysing early injection dynamics based on national pharmacy dispensing data. Only one in three patients with sufficient follow-up remained on therapy for 1 year, and fewer than one in five continued for 2 years. It is particularly disconcerting as to how many patients discontinued therapy right after the first injection (13.1%) or did not even complete the loading phase (20.5%). We purposely did not differentiate between the three different active substances (aflibercept, brolucizumab, ranibizumab) available in PFSs as we did not want to analyse medication specific differences. As we did not distinguish between indications, the third as well as the fifth or sixth injection could represent the end of the loading phase for some patients. Certain indications, such as secondary CNV or myopic CNV, may clinically require only one or two injections, whereas chronic diseases such as AMD and DME typically necessitate ongoing treatment. It is likely that the majority of patients in this dataset were treated for these common chronic conditions; however, without indication-level information, interpretation of the observed prescription pattern should be made with caution, as lower injection numbers could also reflect appropriate treatment rather than non-adherence. A flat learning curve has taken place over a longer time horizon. Specifically, in the early years of anti-VEGF therapy, almost two decades ago, real-world data showed very low injection frequencies per therapy year. Studies from the USA and Denmark presented 5 and 6 injections within the first therapy year,38,40 while a small German study observed an average of 12–13 injections after 24 months on therapy.41 In line with the use of PRN regimens during that period, another Swiss claims-based analysis from 2013 reported an average of 4 injections within the first 6 months of therapy.42 More current studies, such as a US claims analysis from 2021 showed very similar results to our study, with an average of 7.7 months on therapy with six injections.43

A key contribution of this study is the characterisation of early treatment patterns. Discontinuation was most common within the first three injections: 13.1% of patients received only one injection, and 19.0% stopped after three. These findings suggest that the loading phase represents a vulnerable period for drop-out, likely due to early discomfort, lack of perceived benefit, failed communication or logistical barriers. For a subset of patients, one or two injections might also have stabilised their disease and therefore resulted in discontinuation. Patients who completed the loading phase on a monthly schedule were significantly more likely to remain on treatment, aligning with previous evidence that early adherence predicts visual outcomes and long-term persistence.10,12

Strengths and limitations

This study offers several strengths. The use of a large, national, pharmacy-based dataset provides an objective view of injection behaviour across a broad patient population, avoiding documentation or coding bias. By focusing exclusively on PFS formulations, each dispensed unit could be directly linked to an injection event, representing the dosage administered. Injection frequency varied widely, which likely reflects clinical heterogeneity, bilateral treatment or patient-specific needs. Studies showed that ~20%–30% of patients will develop nAMD in the fellow eye, requiring bilateral treatment.44

The large cohort of 46 546 therapy beginners and the stringent way of cleaning for pretreated patients and discontinuations (12 months and 6 months of no purchase, respectively) constitutes a very robust dataset. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. First, the data did not include diagnostic codes, laterality or visual outcomes, which limits disease-specific interpretation. Second, discontinuation was inferred from a gap in prescription activity and may not distinguish between clinical pause and true drop-out. Third, injection timing was estimated on dispensing dates, which may not reflect exact administration dates. Lastly, hospital-dispensed and solution-in-vial formulations were excluded, potentially underrepresenting certain patient segments. A non-negligible proportion of patients showed prescription intervals shorter than 4 weeks. This likely reflects discrepancies between the prescription date and the actual injection date. Additionally, access to specific anti-VEGF medications in the German healthcare system is influenced by regionally differing selective contracts, which often favour cost-efficient alternatives over higher-priced ready-to-use syringes. This economic framework shapes patient access and prescribing behaviour and may contribute to variability in persistence patterns. In contrast, claims datasets do not necessarily represent reality completely realistically. The lack of additional information, especially reasons for discontinuation, limited us from gathering further insights. Specifically, the death of the patient, a switch to a pharmacy not contributing to our data set or a switch to a medication not covered in our data set (solutions in vials, including overall bevacizumab, overall faricimab and a subset of aflibercept and ranibizumab medications) is seen as drop-offs in our analysis. The used dataset covers 44% of community pharmacies, hereby limiting the generalisability of our results to all German patients and to other healthcare models outside of Germany.

Conclusions

In summary, the early months of anti-VEGF therapy appear critical for determining long-term engagement. Early drop-out is common, and failure to complete the loading phase strongly predicts lower treatment persistence. Targeted support during this early period—including patient education, counselling and appointment coordination—may help reduce premature discontinuation and support sustained treatment outcomes in everyday practice. Funders and doctors should be aware of this cliff that needs to be jumped over. While some factors of this retrospective prescriptions sales analysis might contribute to the overestimation of non-persistence, other factors such as purchase of the injection not necessarily happening immediately before the injection but a few days or even weeks before, could even underestimate the depicted non-adherence.

The findings of this study underscore the significant discrepancy between prevalent treatment recommendations for anti-VEGF IVT and their application in real world practice, which could potentially affect both optimal clinical outcomes for patients and cost–benefit outcomes for funders. In terms of the economical use of medication, the aim must be to keep more patients on treatment and to maintain their vision despite stresses and challenges such as old age.
