Factors associated with mental health of Chinese international students in the global context: a systematic review
Authors: Peixin Zuo, Chandra Ramamurthy, Ann Gowing, Anurika De Silva, Harry Minas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChinese international students (CIS) form the biggest cohort in popular host countries such as the United States, Australia, and the United Kingdom, but research shows that their mental health is challenged by multifaceted stressors. Despite this, we are unaware of any previous systematic review that has synthesised both quantitative and qualitative findings on factors associated with their mental health across different countries, and no existing review has included studies done during or after the COVID-19 pandemic. This systematic review aims to answer the following questions: What are the factors associated with the mental health of CIS across different countries? Among identified factors which factors emerged during the COVID-19 pandemic, and which were exacerbated during the pandemic?MethodsThis review follows PRISMA guidelines. Six English and three Chinese databases were searched in November 2023: PsycINFO, MEDLINE, Embase, ERIC, Scopus, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP. All types of empirical studies were eligible. Quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Data were extracted and findings were narratively synthesised using a convergent approach.ResultsThirty-nine English language papers and one Chinese language paper were included. The mental health of CIS is associated with various factors, including academic issues, parents and family, language proficiency, social support, discrimination, acculturative stress, the COVID-19 pandemic, and other factors. Among these, academic-related issues are their main concern, intertwined with family expectation and Confucian cultural values. Language plays a fundamental role in their daily life. During COVID-19, other than pandemic-related fear, there was an increase in experiences of discrimination and social isolation, associated with poorer mental health. Satisfaction with online learning is related to better mental health. Concerns about face, self-esteem, perfectionism, physical health, green space usage, and other factors were also reported.ConclusionsThe mental health of CIS is associated with various factors, and it worsened during the pandemic. Universities, and professionals in education and mental health could provide resources for students to enhance language abilities, academic skills, and social networks. CIS could be more prepared academically and mentally. Suggestions on future research directions were also provided.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24400-w.Keywords: Chinese international students, Mental health, Systematic review, Mixed methods appraisal tool

Background

Chinese international students (CIS) are Chinese citizens studying abroad. The number of CIS has increased rapidly over the recent decades [1]. In 2019 alone, there were 1.6 million CIS studying abroad [2]. Despite that the number has been decreasing after the outbreak of the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, China remains the leading source of international students [3–6]. The top three destinations for CIS are the United States of America (USA), Australia, and the United Kingdom (UK). Latest figures show that more than 310,000 CIS are in the USA [7], over 190,000 are in Australia [8], and 176,000 are in the UK [9]. One in four international students in these three countries is from China [7, 8, 10]. There is a significant presence of CIS in universities worldwide.

Studying abroad is costly [11–13]. Despite the fact that the expense of studying abroad is six to ten times more than that of studying in China [14, 15], CIS are willing to study abroad, and 90% of them are self-funded [16]. This might be attributed to economic, cultural, and systemic factors. First, the increasing affluence of Chinese families has made studying abroad more financially accessible. The per capita disposable income of urban households increased nearly seven times in the past twenty years [17–19]. Second, academic achievement is highly valued in Chinese culture and directly linked to the honour of the entire family, or the Chinese concept of face [20, 21]. Hence, parents are willing to invest in education. Third, domestic university admission in China is extremely competitive. In 2023, of the 12.9 million students who sat the College Entrance Examination, also known as Gaokao [22], only 7% were offered a place at top universities [23]. Out of 10,000 students less than 6 are accepted by Peking University and Tsinghua University, the top two universities in China [24]. Thus, either to avoid fierce competition of Gaokao or to make up for the unsatisfactory outcome of it, a significant number of students and families switch their attention overseas. Students and their families view studying abroad as an enhancement of their educational qualifications and credentials of elite identity which also gives them a better chance in the Chinese job market [25–27].

Host countries have recognised the value of international students, as they bring substantial economic revenue, cultural diversity, social benefits, and a high-quality workforce [28–30]. CIS contribute over 10 billion annually in each of USA, UK, and Australia, which has provoked warnings that universities in those countries are over-reliant on the Chinese market [31–33]. While CIS make significant contributions to their host countries, it is worth asking whether their mental health is being adequately understood by these countries.

CIS face great challenges when they leave their families and social networks in China and enter a country with different culture and language. Factors such as discrimination, language barriers, social isolation, academic pressure, and financial concerns are related to psychological distress and mental health problems among them [34, 35]. Students with mental health problems may fail their exams or perform poorly in the workplace after graduation [36]. Mental health problems could also result in the least desirable consequences. A coronial inquest into the suicide death of a Chinese international student in Melbourne found that there had been 27 international student suicides between 2009 and 2015 in Victoria [37].

Another critical concern is that the COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in negative impacts on the mental health of CIS worldwide [38]. Experiences of discrimination were common [39–41] and many CIS felt nervous and unsafe [40, 42]. Meanwhile, the shift from face-to-face to online learning exacerbated social isolation [39]. Multiple challenges emerged simultaneously, leading to unprecedented mental health hardships [43], and CIS reported higher prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress than their counterparts in China [41, 44, 45].

To the best of our knowledge, no previous systematic review has synthesised factors associated with mental health problems among CIS across countries. Existing literature include two reviews that focused on international students from various Asian countries [46, 47]; two reviews reported the experiences of CIS in the USA [48, 49]; and one that investigated mental health risks for CIS in the Australian context [50]. These reviews, either did not investigate CIS as a separate cohort, or only researched CIS studying in a certain host country. Whether CIS encounter common issues in different host countries has not been researched, and none of the reviews included articles published during the pandemic. To address these gaps, our review included research articles on mental health of CIS across different host countries and also included those studies conducted during the pandemic.

This systematic review aims to answer the following questions: What are the factors associated with mental health of CIS across different countries? Among identified factors, which factors emerged during the COVID-19 pandemic and which factors were exacerbated during the pandemic?

Methods

This review follows PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) Guidelines [51]. The protocol was developed a priori and registered on PROSPERO (No. CRD42023402503).

Reflexivity

As a Chinese international student in Australia, the first author herself experienced several difficulties and witnessed what other families and friends experienced. During the pandemic, some students were forced to purchase high-priced flights to return to China; some travelled to Thailand or Dubai first before they could enter Australia to continue their studies; some struggled in the host countries dealing with online learning and social isolation; and some had to change their study plans or even give up studying abroad. Observations such as these have motivated the first author to systematically explore factors related to the mental health of CIS.

Eligibility

This systematic review includes three types of study designs, quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods. This is because we think it would be valuable to explore whether evidence from different study designs may complement each other, as quantitative data suggests general patterns, while qualitative data brings in deeper insights and voices of participants. Studies investigating factors associated with the mental health of CIS in universities and vocational institutes were considered eligible. Studies were excluded if their participants were under 18 years, were visiting scholars or foreign-born Chinese who are permanent residents/citizens of the country in which the study was conducted. Different inclusion and exclusion criteria apply to different study designs. Quantitative studies must have measured at least one mental disorder as an outcome, including depression, anxiety, stress, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), eating disorders, or suicidal thoughts/behaviours. Qualitative studies had to report results on factors contribute to depression, anxiety or stress, identified by CIS. Mixed methods studies had to have a quantitative and/or qualitative component that met the above criteria. Studies written in English or Chinese were eligible for inclusion, with no restrictions regarding the publication date. English studies were eligible if published in peer-reviewed journals, while Chinese studies were eligible if published in journals included in the Chinese Social Sciences Citation Index (CSSCI) or the Peking University (PKU) core journal list [52, 53]. Academic journals in China adopt different practices from the internationally recognised peer review procedure [54]. Therefore, we only include journals in the CSSCI and PKU core journal list, as these are recognised and accepted by Chinese academia. To ensure a rigorous evidence base, grey literature was not included as quality cannot be confidently assessed.

Search strategies and screening process

The search was conducted on November 3rd, 2023. Nine online databases were searched, including six English databases (PsycINFO, MEDLINE, Embase, ERIC, Scopus, and Web of Science) and three Chinese databases (China National Knowledge Infrastructure [CNKI], Wanfang, and VIP Database for Chinese Technical Periodicals [VIP]).

Search strategies were developed for each English database with the help of a university librarian in the field of health science. A combination of keywords, synonyms, and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms were discussed, and three groups of key elements were generated: mental health outcomes of interest (depression, anxiety, self-harm, stress, suicide thoughts, PTSD); Chinese or China; and international/overseas/exchange students. Then following a same logic, the first author (PZ) developed search strategies for Chinese databases. See supplementary materials for line-by-line search strategies for each database.

English records yielded by the search were imported to EndNote 20 for screening. Duplicate records were removed automatically by the software. Any remaining duplicates were removed manually by PZ. Chinese records were screened manually using Excel. Title and abstract screening was then completed by PZ and a fellow Chinese PhD student (ZZ) in the same research field. The two reviewers screened a random 10% of the records against the inclusion and exclusion criteria independently and achieved an agreement of 91.5%. After discussion, a 100% agreement was achieved, and PZ completed the remaining 90%. Then, PZ and ZZ reviewed a random 10% of the full-text records independently and reached a 94.1% consensus. Conflicts were resolved by a senior researcher (HM) in this field and a 100% consensus was achieved after discussion. PZ screened the remaining full-text records and manually searched the reference lists of the included studies.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (PZ and CR) extracted data from each included article independently using an Excel form generated by PZ. Extracted information includes year, first author, study context, sample size, data collection methods, associated factors, outcomes, COVID-19 relatedness, and key findings. Upon completion, PZ compared the two forms and had a discussion with CR to finalise it. There were only very few minor discrepancies and again, the senior research (HM) was invited to resolve them by comparing the excerpt with the original text.

Quality assessment

The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT), tailored for assessing a combination of different study designs [55] was used in our review. The MMAT consists of six components: one screening section and five appraisal categories corresponding to different study designs, which are qualitative research, randomised controlled trials, non-randomised studies, quantitative descriptive studies, and mixed methods studies. Each category contains five questions to appraise the methodological quality of the study. The screening section has two questions determining whether the study is empirical, and if a study fails to obtain a Yes for both questions, it will not be processed further. Studies passing the screening section are assessed, and reviewers respond Yes/Can’t tell/No to the five criteria questions under the corresponding category. Can’t tell means there is no or insufficient information to be assessed. A study only earns one star when received a ‘Yes’, and the maximum is five stars. For mixed methods studies, reviewers respond to a set of 15 questions: 5 for qualitative research component, 5 for quantitative research component, and 5 for mixed methods component. All three components are weighted equally. For more detailed instructions please check the MMAT documents available to the public online [55]. PZ and CR independently assessed all the included studies. Minor disagreements were resolved by a senior researcher (HM) after discussion.

Synthesis method

We searched existing literature to determine a suitable synthesis method. Eventually, we found a systematic review conducted by Hong and colleagues [56]. Their review investigated the synthesis methods of 459 systematic reviews that reported both quantitative and qualitative data. Then two methods of synthesis were identified, “convergent” and “sequential” [56]. The convergent method means that, “all included studies are analysed using the same synthesis method and results are presented together” (p.7) [56], while the sequential method means that researchers synthesise findings from one study type first, and then synthesise findings from another study type. Given that we would like to examine whether quantitative and qualitative findings may complement each other in terms of the various factors associated with the mental health of CIS, it is more reasonable to compare both types of findings of a certain factor. Thus, the convergent method was adopted in our review. Findings were synthesised narratively. No statistical synthesis methods were applied given that the study designs were diverse. Further, heterogeneity was not explored. Research findings were grouped according to the factors, and findings from high-quality studies were weighted more in our synthesis.

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 shows the results of the eligibility screening process applied to the 1,144 records generated from English Language databases, with 36 articles found to be eligible for inclusion. After citation searching, three more articles were included. Among the 305 records yielded via Chinese databases, only one article was included. Half of the records were excluded as they were not published in journals included in the CSSCI and PKU core journal list. See the supplementary file for the records yielded via Chinese databases. In total, 40 studies were included, 39 in English and 1 in Chinese. Articles were excluded if: (a) the participants were under 18 [57] or they were not CIS [58, 59]; (b) the outcome being measured was not of interest to this review [60, 61]; (c) the findings were not relevant to the questions of this review [62, 63]. Reviews and meta-analyses were excluded [46–48, 64] but their reference lists were manually searched.

Study characteristics

Of the 40 included studies, 60% were published after 2021, indicating a growing trend of mental health research on CIS. Nineteen studies recruited CIS from the USA, four from Australia; three each from the UK and South Korea; two each from Canada and Japan; and one each from Germany and Ireland. The remaining five studies recruited CIS studying in multiple countries.

Twenty-nine were quantitative studies, eight were qualitative, and three were mixed methods studies. The included studies investigated a wide range of factors that were potentially related to mental health, including academic issues, parental and family factors, language competency, social support, discrimination, acculturation, COVID-19-related factors, face concern, personality, physical health, leisure, and other factors.

Depression was the most common outcome explored by twenty-five studies. Then it was followed by anxiety and stress. Three studies reported on PTSD, and only one measured self-injury ideation and suicidal attempts. None of the studies investigated eating disorders or sleeping disorders. One-third of the studies were conducted during the pandemic. Please see the Supplementary File for detailed information on the characteristics of the included studies.

All eight qualitative studies obtained the maximum five stars.

Of the ten quantitative non-randomised studies, all except one [65] provided insufficient information on criterion 3.1 whether the sample was representative of the target population. All three mixed methods studies [69–71] received a quality rating of four stars but had unclear sampling information and an absence of a strong rationale for using a mixed-method design. In terms of the link between the quantitative and qualitative analyses, one study used interview data to further explain the positive association between social interaction and psychological stress (r = 0.409, p < 0.01) [69]. One study measured the mental health of CIS using validated scales and collected qualitative data to understand the causes behind [70]. One study explored the association between green space usage and mental health, and obtained qualitative information to know more details of their usage [71].

Most of the quantitative studies did not provide an exact p-value and used 0.05 as a threshold for significance. Furthermore, many did not report the estimated 95% confidence interval (CI), and some did not indicate an exact p-value.

Academic issues

Quantitative findings:

CIS place a great importance on their academic life. On a scale of 5, academic life was marked 4.25 on average, indicating that academic life was extremely important to them [69]. Meanwhile, 91.9% of the participants predominantly agreed that “writing up papers that can earn you good grades” was of “great importance” [69].

Academic stress was deemed to be the main source of depression, anxiety, and stress by more than 40% of CIS [70, 72], and they had a hard time adjusting to a new academic life, which was positively correlated with depression (r = 0.315, p < 0.01) [69]. Longitudinal data also showed that CIS who faced more difficulties in their academic life, became more depressed within three months [73].

On the other hand, student’s average mark was negatively correlated with depression (r=−0.237, p < 0.05) [69]. In other words, a higher mark achieved by students is related to a lower self-rated score for depression.

Qualitative findings:

CIS felt stressed due to the unfamiliarity and difficulties in learning in host countries [74–77], including independent learning styles, heavy study loads, and critical thinking [78]. They found it hard to navigate through academic challenges [78] and the heavy study loads were overwhelming. One described, “… looking down the stairs from the top floor of the library, I felt like I was being suffocated by all the review and preview for my exams and quizzes, desperately trying to catch a breath but to no avail” (p.30) [77], a statement that elaborated how the study loads seemed endless.

Further, some CIS had sleep problems and irregular menstrual cycles as physical signs of feeling stressed when they had to work harder than their local peers to catch up [75]. One student explained that she, driven by an aspiration to achieve marks as good as or better than local students, had to study while her local peers were relaxing [76].

Summary. Both quantitative and qualitative findings report that academic stress are highly related to the mental health problems of CIS. Academic stressors, including pedagogy styles, study loads, and aspirations are identified as the main sources of depression, anxiety, and stress by CIS. Students with better academic performance tend to have better mental health.

Parents and family

Quantitative findings:

Good relationships with families are beneficial for CIS. Parent attachment was found to be negatively associated with depression (β=−0.345, 95%CI: [−0.443, −0.248], p < 0.001) among CIS in South Korea, and a higher score of parent attachment indicates a more stable attachment between children and their parents [79]. Similarly, other scholars also reported that the frequency of conflict (β = 0.35, p < 0.001) and “helicopter” parenting (β = 0.34, p < 0.001) were positively associated with stress among CIS in the USA [80]. These findings suggest that CIS are more likely to develop depression and stress when having a less stable attachment or more conflicts with their parents and being raised by “helicopter” parenting.

In addition, family stressors were identified as the main source of stress or anxiety by 17% of CIS, ranking third place; however, only 3% of local Australian students identified family stress and this ranked last among all six stressors [70].

Qualitative findings:

Family stressors were elaborated on in detail by CIS. For instance, one wrote, “My parents use Skype to watch me study and tell me [to] work harder and late at night” (p.286) [70]. Parents pushed their children to achieve good grades, look for internships, and find job opportunities, and CIS were either willing to make their family proud or were afraid of disappointing them [70, 75]. These comments demonstrate how influential parents are, even if they are thousands of miles away from their children.

During the pandemic, CIS attributed their anxiety to excessive concern from parents. “They were very overacting to the virus in the UK and called me ten times a day, which didn’t help at all but got me overly anxious” (p.9) [78].

Summary. Both quantitative and qualitative evidence suggest that parents and family are related to the mental health of CIS. A good attachment to parents is related to less depression. Overly frequent contact, conflicts and “helicopter” parenting are related to more stress. CIS identify family stressors to be the main sources of anxiety and stress, specifying that parents hold high expectation on them and intrusively supervise their studies overseas.

Language

Quantitative findings:

English competency is reported to be associated with depression among CIS in studies from 1991 to 2015 [72, 73, 81]. Specifically, 12.7% of CIS attributed English language difficulties as a main cause of depression [72], and statistical analysis found that self-assessed English proficiency was negatively associated with depression (β=−0.25, p < 0.001), anxiety (β=−0.27, p < 0.001) and PTSD (β=−0.26, p < 0.001) [81], indicating a higher level of perceived English proficiency is associated with less depression, anxiety, and PTSD symptoms. Longitudinal evidence also showed that students with a higher level of English were less likely to feel more depressed over three months [73].

However, another study in 2021 reported hardly any association between self-assessed English proficiency and mental health problems (β = 0.04, n.s.) [82]. This finding differs from the above findings, and due to limited information was provided regarding the analysis procedure, it is hard to explain the difference. Despite this, the study found that English proficiency was negatively associated with language-related discrimination (β=−0.34, p < 0.001), and such discrimination was positively associated with mental health problems (β = 0.31, p < 0.001). Thus, English proficiency might be indirectly related to the mental health of CIS, and such relation was discussed in the Discrimination section below.

Qualitative findings:

Even though CIS passed standardised English language tests prior to their enrolment, their English competency is still far from enough when dealing with the communication needs in their academic and daily lives [76, 77]. A student used a barren tree as a metaphor to describe what they felt about their limited language competency, “I lost everything I had in China, my social influence, my confidence, my voice, and everything, just like that tree in the picture, barren” (p.31) [77] which underscores the fundamental role that language plays in all aspects of life.

CIS point out that limited English skills increases their study load which negatively impacts their mental health. For example, one said, “the first semester was particularly bad… because of language barriers, it took me about five or six times the time of others to get things done… and I was quite nervous and stressed” (p.6) [74]. This echoes the experiences of CIS in another study, “For a task that took one hour for the Canadian students, I would probably need five hours” (p.30) [77].

Students also used words and phrases like “stressful” and “feel stressed out” when describing the language difficulties encountered in their daily communication with teachers and peer students [74]. As a result, they were unable to make close friends with local people in the host country and felt frustrated [77]. In addition, some were unwilling to communicate with local people because they didn’t want to be seen as stupid [78]. Language insufficiency is a barrier in their social life.

Findings above highlight that English language competency affects the academic and social life of CIS in English-speaking countries, which in turn, impose an influence on their mental health.

Summary. Sufficient evidence has been drawn from quantitative and qualitative studies, suggesting that language ability is related to the mental health of CIS, and it plays a fundamental role in their academic and social life. Although one study did not observe a significant association between language competency and mental health outcomes, the other seven studies have consistent findings, that a poorer level of English is related to worse mental health of CIS. Note that, apart from the studies mentioned above, our review included six studies published in English but conducted in non-English-speaking countries. Among the six studies, some did not consider language competency as a potentially relevant factor to mental health [66, 79, 83, 84], while others [67, 85] only briefly mentioned language competency but did not explicitly investigate its association with mental health.

Social support

Quantitative findings:

Cross-sectional studies consistently report that social support is negatively associated with depression and anxiety [40, 41, 43, 65, 84, 86]. These findings suggest that social support is beneficial for the mental health of CIS. Two studies explored the interaction effects between discrimination and social support on depression and anxiety [44, 86]. Bi et al. [44] found that social support can moderate the positive association between discrimination and depression/anxiety, indicating that social support can be protective. Sun et al. [86] pointed out that the interaction between discrimination and connectedness with American students was negatively associated with the mental health of CIS (β=−3.23, p˂0.01), suggesting that it is good for those CIS in the USA to make connections with local students when discrimination is involved.

Two longitudinal studies also studied the association between social support and mental health, but their findings are different. Ying and Liese [73] reported that CIS appeared to be more depressed over a three-month period if they had less social support from homelands. This is in line with the findings above. However, Ke et al. [65] found that perceived social support did not seem to predict depression and anxiety in 1.5 years. This discrepancy may be attributed to the varying time intervals between the studies (3 months vs. 1.5 years).

Qualitative findings:

Social isolation and lack of social support are identified as major threats to emotional wellbeing, causing depression among CIS [72, 74]. Students reported missing a sense of belonging, for example, they had “some acquaintances, but not friends…whom you could open your heart to” (p.8) [74]. To cope with their feelings of depression, CIS tend to talk to their friends and families back in China [72], and family is their most preferred help resource, followed by friends, church, and professional counselling services [34]. However, the reasons why CIS chose family as their primary source of support were not specified in these studies. When feeling depressed and frustrated, CIS also encourage each other. Students mentioned, “whenever I am with my Chinese friends, I am relaxed and happy” (p.535) [34]. Some CIS in the USA, although not religious, were happy to receive social support from the church [34]. This coping strategy was not mentioned in any other studies included in this review.

Some CIS expressed an inclination to interact with local people [34] and felt stressed if they did not use English in daily communication:

“During the week, other than two classes when I get the chance to listen to American professors lecture in English, I do not talk in or listen to English at all. This makes me very stressed”. (p.536) [34].

Sometimes, however, language insufficiency prevents them from socialising with local people. They viewed themselves as “a person who does not know how to swim, but is forced into a pool” (p.536) [34]. This echoes the findings in another study [69] where nearly half of the participants agreed that making friends with British people was a great difficulty. It is intriguing that CIS enjoy staying in their own groups but feel stressed when they are unable to establish relationships with local people.

*Social isolation was exacerbated during the pandemic:

Apart from the quarantine policies that kept people home, CIS deliberately chose not to socialise with local people to avoid being discriminated against [78]. A student shared:

“Due to growing racism toward Chinese people on public and social media platforms, I feel uncomfortable going outside. I tried to stay alone and felt lonely in my room every day. Sometimes, it made me feel very depressed because my entire daily life was about staring at the computer screen, writing endless assignments, and checking the online class management system”. (p.8) [39].

This quote demonstrates an intertwined relationship between the pandemic, discrimination, social isolation, and online learning. Discrimination and online learning will be discussed in separate sections.

Summary. Both quantitative and qualitative findings suggest that social support is beneficial for the mental health of CIS. Cross-sectional evidence is plenty and consistent, while longitudinal evidence is scarce. It is unclear whether current social support can predict future mental health outcomes. Qualitative data reveals that CIS self-attribute social isolation as an important cause of depression. All the qualitative studies were conducted in English-speaking countries, and CIS frequently reported that they had encountered great difficulties in making friends with local people in those countries.

Discrimination

Quantitative findings:

Discrimination is positively associated with depression, anxiety, and stress [41, 44], suggesting that CIS who experience more discrimination score higher in depression, anxiety, and stress scales. Two types of discrimination are considered - racial and language discrimination [81, 82, 86, 87]. Language discrimination is defined as “the phenomenon of being discriminated against because English is one’s second language or because one has an accent” [81]. Researchers consistently report that perceived language discrimination is significantly positively associated with mental health problems among CIS [81, 82, 86]. Examples of language discrimination can be seen later in the qualitative findings.

Only one study used longitudinal data to explore the association between discrimination and mental health. Sun et al. [86] found that discrimination perceived at the first time point was significantly associated with mental health problems three months later at the second time point (β = 0.31, p < 0.01) whilst mental health problems at the first time point did not predict discrimination at the second point (β=−0.13, ns).

Qualitative findings:

Students reported being mocked by local people because of a poor level of English, and they commented, “过得很不开心的, 感觉很郁闷 (living very unhappily, feeling quite depressed)” [76] (p.8). Other students even felt quite scared after experiencing discriminatory behaviours [74].

*Discrimination was exacerbated during the pandemic:

In the USA, UK, and Australia, CIS more frequently experienced discrimination, such as being harassed, yelled at, and labelled as “coronavirus” by local people [41, 42, 78]. Students claimed, “We Chinese people received cold looks in public. It made us very anxious” (p.8) [39]. Also, Anxiety was positively associated with discrimination from both the local community (β = 0.199, p = 0.01) and the media (β = 0.172, p = 0.025); 56.6% and 77.5% participants were affected, respectively [40]. CIS felt anxious and unsafe when being labelled as “China virus” and “Wuhan virus” on social media [39, 78]. Such experiences placed extra pressure on their mental health.

Summary. Both quantitative and qualitative findings suggest that discrimination harms the mental health of CIS, and the situation worsened during the pandemic. Cross-sectional studies consistently report a positive association between discrimination and mental health problems, and longitudinal data further demonstrate that discrimination could predict future depression and anxiety. Qualitative evidence echoes the quantitative findings above, confirming that discrimination is associated with poorer mental health among CIS.

Acculturation and acculturative stress

Quantitative findings:

Berry’s acculturation theory states that acculturation is the process of cultural change that occurs when two culturally distinct groups come into continuous first-hand contact with each other, a process that is frequently stressful [88]. Researchers have measured both acculturation - the extent of cultural maintenance or cultural change - and acculturative stress.

Adherence to the Chinese culture was negatively associated with depression (β=−0.222, p < 0.001) among CIS in the USA [89], but this association did not apply to CIS in Germany (β=−0.01, n.s.) [85]. With limited evidence, it is hard to determine whether acculturation has any association with depression among CIS.

Acculturative stress has been consistently reported to be positively associated with depression and anxiety among CIS in the USA and Japan [66, 90–92], suggesting that more acculturative stress is related to more symptoms of depression and anxiety.

Qualitative findings:

CIS identify several stressors during acculturation that negatively impact their mental health, including academic pressure, language deficiency, social relationships, and discrimination [34, 76]. These are already discussed previously. Other stressors include length of stay, coping strategies, and acculturation strategies [34]. Here, we provide a further discussion on acculturation strategies. Berry defined four types of acculturation strategies - Assimilation, Separation, Integration, and Marginalisation [88]. Yan and Berliner [34] found that a marginalised acculturation strategy was associated with increased stress while the other three strategies were not related to the stress of CIS in the USA.

Summary. Both quantitative and qualitative studies point out that, acculturative stress, which may be the result of several stressors, is positively associated with depression and anxiety among CIS across countries. The extent to which acculturation is associated with mental health remains unclear.

Factors which emerged during the COVID-19 pandemic

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the prevalence of major depression among CIS nearly doubled from 22.1% in 2019 to 43.8% in 2020, and the prevalence of anxiety increased from 24.7 to 45.7% [65]. As mentioned above, social support and discrimination are the two factors exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic, especially in the host countries with the greatest numbers of CIS. The following sections will synthesise three factors emerged during the pandemic.

Fear of infection

It is consistent in the quantitative findings that the fear of COVID-19 infection was positively associated to anxiety among CIS [40, 45, 93]. The perceived likelihood of survival was negatively associated with the depression of CIS in Korea (β=−1.377, p = 0.007) [83]. Students who believed that they would survive after being infected reported less depression.

Qualitative studies provide more information. CIS worried about their health, “I was so stressed because it was really difficult to locate face masks and other protective gear…” (p.6) [42]. CIS also commented that in their host country, “the lockdown rules were poorly enforced. Just like everybody knew these requirements, only a few abided by them. That’s why I don’t feel safe here…” (p.7) [78]. When feeling unsafe, CIS were unable to book flights back to China at a reasonable price which resulted in additional psychological pressure on their mental health [42].

Both quantitative and qualitative findings suggest that the fear of catching the virus was related to poorer mental health.

Place of study

During the pandemic, some universities offered courses online to allow international students to study remotely from their home countries [94, 95]. Under this condition, some CIS stayed in China and took courses online.

The association between places of study and mental health was only explored in quantitative studies. Before mid-2020, CIS studying in the USA reported better mental health conditions than CIS in China [96]. However, during late 2020 and 2021, CIS in the USA and Australia reported worse mental health than CIS in China [43, 65]. Whether the host or home country was the better option for students’ mental health seems to have depended on different stages of the pandemic. At that time, the development trajectory of the COVID-19 pandemic in China differed from other countries.

Additionally, taking online courses while staying in China might already be a thing of the past. Starting from the first semester of 2023, the China Service Centre for Scholarly Exchange (CSCSE) no longer accredited degrees obtained via online learning if students did not physically study abroad [97]. This phase of education existed only between 2020 and 2022.

Online learning

Only two studies in our review explored the association between online learning and mental health. Both are quantitative. They found that satisfaction with online learning was negatively associated with depression (β=−0.81, p < 0.001) and anxiety (β=−0.11, p < 0.05) [44]. If students agreed that online learning had negative impacts on their personal relationships, their odds of anxiety and depression tend to be higher (OR = 2.645, 95%CI: [1.414, 4.949], p = 0.002; OR = 2.052, 95%CI: [1.155, 3.644], p = 0.014) [43]. On the other hand, if students disagreed that online learning had a negative impact on their academic outcomes and future opportunities, their odds of depression appeared to be lower (OR = 0.549, 95%CI: [0.326, 0.925], p = 0.024) [43]. These findings highlight that if students are satisfied with online learning, they tend to have better mental health. However, if they believe that online learning negatively impacts their social relationships and academic performance, their risk of mental health problems increases.

Other factors

Sleep quality, physical health, and leisure activities are also related to the mental health of CIS. Those with more severe insomnia have a higher risk of depression and anxiety [43], and poorer physical health is related to worse mental health conditions [96]. They also report a poorer mental health when having negative experiences during leisure activities [98]. Moreover, personal characteristics and beliefs are also relevant to their mental health [85]. Students have maladaptive perfectionism are more likely to have higher scores of depression [90, 91], and those who care too much about what other people think tend to have mental health problems [82]. Higher self-esteem is found to be associated with a lower score of depression and anxiety [44, 81]. Visiting green spaces in the natural environment is related to less stress among CIS [71].

Discussion

This review synthesises empirical evidence regarding factors associated with mental health problems of CIS in multiple host countries, most of which are English-speaking. In general, the mental health of CIS is associated with a variety of factors, including academic issues, parents and family, language proficiency, social support, discrimination, acculturative stress, the COVID-19 pandemic, and other factors.

Among these, academic-related issues appear to be a dominant mental health stressor. Although academic issues and family expectations have not been examined in the quantitative studies, qualitative data consistently point out that these are vital, which seems to be culturally specific. Rooted in the Confucian culture, academic achievement is highly valued in the Chinese communities and has been seen as a reflection of moral virtue [99–101]. As a result, Chinese parents hold higher expectations and exhibit a higher level of educational involvement compared to American and European parents [99, 101]. In parallel, Chinese students are more willing to live up to such expectations than their American counterparts, even though these expectations impose academic stress [102, 103]. This demonstrates filial piety and obedience emphasised in Confucianism [104].

Language plays a fundamental role for students studying abroad. CIS identify language difficulties as major barriers to their learning and socialising, leading to negative states such as depression, frustration, and stress. Limited English competency is deemed a direct cause of academic hardships by CIS across the USA, UK, Canada, and Australia [74, 75, 77, 78]. A poor language competence results in embarrassment and insufficiency in daily communications [77, 78] and the experience of language discrimination is positively associated with depression and anxiety [81, 82]. These findings show that proficiency in the language spoken in the host country is fundamental to studying abroad and is related to other factors including academic pressure, social support, and discrimination. However, although qualitative data consistently shows that language is related to mental health of CIS, it was infrequently measured in the quantitative studies included in this review.

Social support and discrimination are the two factors investigated by most studies included in this review. Social support is negatively associated with mental health problems while discrimination is positively associated with mental health problems among CIS. These findings are consistent with the wider literature that better social support is good for students’ mental health [105–107] and that discrimination is detrimental to their mental health [108–110].

Based on our findings, acculturative stress is positively associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms. Qualitative data further suggest that acculturative stress experienced by CIS is frequently related to academic pressure, language difficulties, inadequate social support, and discrimination, as aforementioned. Thus, acculturative stress appears to encompass other factors listed above. As defined by Berry, “acculturative stress is a stress reaction in response to life events that are rooted in the experience of acculturation” (p.294) [88]. During their journey abroad, CIS face multifaceted challenges in their academic, social, and daily lives, affecting their mental health. This aligns with broader literature [111, 112], suggesting that acculturative stress is linked to mental health problems among international students worldwide.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, social isolation and discrimination were exacerbated and three factors - fear of the virus, place of study, and online learning – emerged. These factors should be interpreted within the broader sociopolitical climate during that time when CIS were stigmatised as virus spreaders by some people in host countries [39, 42, 78]. Political discourse and media narratives intensified anti-Asian sentiment and behaviours [113, 114], and CIS perceived wary and hostile attitudes both in real life and online [40, 78]. Such conditions also extended into universities, posing greater mental health challenges [39, 115]. Our findings highlight the importance of considering support measures for both the university community and the wider host society during special periods.

In addition, personal characteristics and natural environments are factors relatively less studied in mental health studies on CIS. Low self-esteem and certain types of personality, such as perfectionism, are related to reduced mental health. Findings in our review are in line with study findings of other groups [116–118]. In terms of natural environments, although only one study reports that visiting green space usage benefits the mental health of CIS, wider literature consistently suggests that individuals have less stress, as well as better physical and mental health, when living closer to green spaces or coastal areas (blue spaces) [119, 120].

Practical implications

Several implications can be drawn from our review. For CIS and their families, it is important to get prepared for the challenges of studying abroad. Students should try their best to improve their language skills before departure, and upon arrival, their families need to provide positive emotional support and try not to impose unrealistic academic expectations and pressure on their children. Universities might offer CIS free academic services to assist their studies, as well as organise social events or information sessions to help them build up new networks and adapt to the host country. If come across mental health problems, CIS should be encouraged to make good use of uni-based psychological resources and pay frequent visits to green spaces. Mental health professionals need to be aware of the Confucian culture context and equip themselves when working with CIS.

Recommendations for future research

First, nearly half of the studies included were conducted in the USA, suggesting a research gap in the mental health of CIS in other main host countries including Australia, the UK, and Canada. Further studies in these different cultural and educational settings are necessary, particularly because acculturative stress is an important consideration. Second, future quantitative research should more systematically consider academic issues and language competency, as their importance has been illustrated in qualitative studies but not frequently considered in existing quantitative studies. For instance, academic transcripts might be collected as evidence of their performance, potentially reflecting whether they encounter academic issues, while language competency can be measured by existing scales and standardised language test results. Third, future studies of CIS should include the cultural context of Confucianism by explicitly considering the influence of child-parent relationships, family expectations, and face concerns on mental health of CIS, as Chinese students are more concerned about academic achievement and family expectations than their American and European counterparts [101–103]. Fourth, there is a need for longitudinal studies to move beyond identification of associations between relevant factors, and to clarify directions of causality so as to inform preventative programs.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. The study followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [51] to ensure transparency and completeness. The protocol for this systematic review was developed a priori and registered on PROSPERO. Both English and Chinese literature were searched to increase comprehensiveness, although only one Chinese paper was eligible. The MMAT was employed to simplify the appraisal process of the included studies while ensuring effectiveness in assessing the methodological quality of multiple study designs. This review synthesised both quantitative and qualitative findings, as well as provided integrated summaries for each factor. Literature published during the COVID-19 pandemic was included, and findings on mental health-related factors that were exacerbated and emerged during the pandemic were synthesised.

A key limitation is that studies written in languages other than English and Chinese are not included. Also excluded is the grey literature which may contain important information on the mental health of CIS. Direct comparison between quantitative studies was also not possible as potentially relevant factors and mental health outcomes of interest have been measured by a wide variety of different scales.

Conclusions

This review synthesises factors associated with the mental health of CIS, especially depression and anxiety. Academic issues, parental and family factors, language competency, social support, discrimination, and other factors are identified. The lack of longitudinal studies prevents causal conclusions to be drawn. During the COVID-19 pandemic, CIS experienced a lack of social support, increased isolation, and an increase in discriminatory behaviours which were associated with poorer mental health, as was dissatisfaction with online learning. Qualitative data show that CIS identify academic-related issues as their main concern, and that these are closely related to family expectations. Proficiency in the language of the host country plays a fundamental role in their study journey and has an impact on mental health. However, these issues are not adequately explored in the quantitative studies included in this review and should be considered in future studies. We also encourage studies on the mental health of CIS in host countries other than the USA and encourage more novel research on nature environments, as well as longitudinal study designs.
