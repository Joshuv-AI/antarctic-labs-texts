Scrape versus imprint cytology in sentinel lymph nodes of breast cancer patients after neoadjuvant therapy: Does combination add value?
Authors: Egemen Akıncıoğlu, Elçin Kadan, Hale Kıvrak, Aysu Sadioğlu, İlke Evrim Seçinti, Gülçin Şimşek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe primary objective of this study is to evaluate the feasibility of cytological methods in intraoperative consultation for patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy (NT), to compare the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of imprint, scrape, and combined cytology, and to assess the reliability of these methods in lymph nodes (LNs) affected by treatment‐related changes. Additionally, this study aims to investigate the impact of metastatic tumor size on the diagnostic accuracy of cytological methods.MethodsNinety‐two patients who underwent sentinel LN sampling during breast surgery between June 2023 and December 2024 were included in this retrospective study. Diagnostic performance of imprint, scrape, and combined cytology was evaluated according to NT status, metastatic tumor size, and treatment‐related changes. Overall accuracy was assessed using Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, with pairwise comparisons performed using DeLong’s test.ResultsA total of 300 LNs were evaluated, of which 161 (53.6%) were from patients receiving NT. In this subgroup, imprint cytology showed high specificity but limited sensitivity, whereas scrape and combined cytology demonstrated higher sensitivity and overall diagnostic accuracy. ROC analysis showed significantly higher diagnostic performance for scrape and combined cytology compared with imprint cytology in patients receiving NT (p = .005). Across all subgroup analyses, including metastasis size and therapy‐related changes, combined cytology consistently showed the highest sensitivity, whereas imprint cytology maintained the highest specificity.ConclusionScrape cytology showed significantly higher diagnostic performance than imprint cytology, particularly after NT. Combined cytology improved performance compared with imprint cytology but did not significantly outperform scrape cytology.Keywords: breast cancer, imprint cytology, neoadjuvant therapy, scrape cytology, sentinel lymph node

INTRODUCTION

Axillary lymph node (LN) involvement is a major prognostic indicator in breast cancer patients and serves as a determinant of disease stage. Although mastectomy and axillary dissection were previously considered the gold standard treatment for breast cancer, breast‐conserving surgery and sentinel lymph node (SLN) sampling have largely replaced them. The SLN represents the first LN to which the tumor drains. In breast cancer, the first SLN was identified in the 1990s by Giuliano and colleagues 1 using blue dye, and by Krag et al. 2 using a radioisotope. Currently, some centers employ both techniques in combination. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 There are different methods for SLN examination: routine paraffin follow‐up, frozen section, touch cytology, and scrape cytology. With recent developments, KRT19 mRNA is also measured in LNs using the intraoperative one‐step nucleic acid amplification method. Routine tissue processing followed by axillary dissection results in a second exposure to anesthesia and surgery, a delay in the initiation of adjuvant treatment, and an economic burden for the patient. For these reasons, SLN assessment with intraoperative consultation (IOC) is more advantageous. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13

Neoadjuvant therapy (NT) is used to reduce the size of the primary tumor in the breast and decrease the number of metastatic LNs in the axilla. This approach facilitates breast‐conserving surgery and reduces the number of cases requiring axillary dissection. In the past, SLN procedures were considered contraindicated in patients receiving NT due to concerns such as chemotherapy delaying lymphatic drainage and impairing wound healing. 6 However, SLN biopsy is now widely performed in many centers for patients undergoing NT. The optimal method for evaluating SLN in NT cases is a topic still under discussion. 14

Imprint cytology is obtained by placing a slide in contact with the cut surface of the LN. In contrast, scrape cytology is obtained by scraping the cut surface of the LN with a scalpel tip and spreading the material on a slide. In IOC, cytological examination represents a faster and less costly method than frozen section analysis, and it does not require additional equipment. Moreover, because no tissue is lost, it offers an advantage for subsequent immunohistochemical and molecular testing. 8 , 15 , 16

In studies evaluating SLN using imprint cytology, specificity rates are consistently high (99.3%–100%), whereas sensitivity rates vary between 33.3% and 90%. 5 , 8 , 9 , 11 , 17 , 18 In the literature, the sensitivity of scrape cytology in SLN assessment ranges from 52.5% to 94%, and it is generally reported to be higher than that of imprint cytology, whereas its specificity ranges from 98% to 100%. 10 , 19 , 20 , 21

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the feasibility of cytological methods in IOC for patients receiving NT, to compare the sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, and negative predictive value of imprint, scrape, and combined (imprint + scrape) cytology, and to assess the reliability of these methods in LNs affected by treatment‐related changes. Additionally, the second objective of this study aims to investigate the impact of metastatic size on the diagnostic accuracy of cytological methods.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Between June 2023 and December 2024, 92 of 111 cases that underwent SLN sampling during breast‐conserving surgery, skin‐sparing mastectomy, or simple mastectomy were retrospectively analyzed in this study. Nineteen cases with diagnoses other than invasive ductal carcinoma, and those lacking imprint and scrape preparations in the pathology archive were excluded.

In our routine practice, both blue‐stained and unstained LNs are evaluated during SLN biopsy. In our institution, axillary LN dissection is performed in patients who have not received NT and present with more than two macro‐metastatic axillary LNs, which is consistent with current American Society of Clinical Oncology guideline recommendations. In cases receiving NT, axillary dissection is performed if any malignant cells are detected, regardless of metastasis size. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25

During IOC, LNs are bisected along their long axis to a maximum thickness of 0.3 cm. An imprint preparation is obtained by touching the slide to the cut surface of the LN, whereas scrape cytology is obtained by scraping the cut surface with a scalpel and spreading the material on a slide (Figure 1A–D) Cytology materials prepared by both methods are stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H & E) (Figure 2A,B).

Clinical information (age and diagnosis) was retrieved from the hospital information system by one pathologist (E.K.). Cytology preparations were independently re‐evaluated by two pathologists (E.A. and H.K.) without access to clinical data. Smear preparations were classified as “positive” if a single atypical cell was present in any LN, and “negative” if no atypical cells were detected. Discrepant cases were resolved by consensus. H & E‐stained paraffin‐embedded LN sections and, in selected cases, KRT19 immunohistochemical staining were reviewed by two pathologists (E.A. and A.S.). Based on tumor size, metastases were categorized into three groups: isolated tumor cells (≤0.2 mm or ≤200 cells), micro‐metastases (>0.2 mm to 2 mm and/or >200 cells), and macro‐metastases (>2 mm), in accordance with the pTNM requirements from the 8th edition, AJCC Staging Manual. 26 , 27 Routine paraffin sectioning results served as the reference standard. 16

The specificity, sensitivity, accuracy, positive predictive value (precision), and negative predictive value of imprint, scrape, and combined (imprint + scrape) cytology in SLN biopsy were calculated separately according to NT status, metastatic tumor size, and treatment‐related LN changes.

Statistical analysis

For each LN, true‐positive (TP), true‐negative (TN), false‐negative (FN), and false‐positive (FP) results were classified, and a 2 × 2 table was constructed. The following formulas were used: sensitivity = true‐positive/(true‐positive + false‐negative); specificity = true‐negative/(true‐negative + false‐positive); accuracy = (true‐positive + true‐negative)/(true‐positive + true‐negative + false‐negative + false‐positive); positive predictive value (precision) = true‐positive/(true‐positive + false‐positive); negative predictive value = true‐negative/(true‐negative + false‐negative). For subgroup analyses, diagnostic performance metrics were recalculated using different histopathological thresholds, redefining positive and negative reference standards accordingly.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 20.0 statistical software; IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. The diagnostic performances of imprint cytology, scrape cytology, and the combined approach were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Diagnostic performance was used with paraffin‐embedded histopathological diagnosis as the reference standard. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated to assess overall diagnostic accuracy. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Pairwise comparisons of correlated ROC curves (imprint, scrape, and combined methods) were performed using DeLong’s test for two correlated AUCs. Comparisons according to metastasis size were performed using the Pearson χ2 test. When the assumption of expected cell counts was violated, the likelihood ratio test was used for 3 × 2 contingency tables. A p value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

General data

The mean age of all patients was 51.7 years (range, 31–78). SLN sampling was performed from one axilla in 89 patients and from both axillae in three patients. A total of 95 IOCs were evaluated, corresponding to 300 LNs. The mean number of LNs per SLN biopsy was 3.15 (range, 1–7). SLN biopsy was performed in 46 patients who received NT (48.4%) and in 49 patients who did not (51.6%). A total of 161 (53.6%) LNs were from patients who received NT and 139 (46.3%) from those who did not. Routine histopathological evaluation revealed metastases in 58 LNs (19.3%), whereas 242 LNs (80.6%) were free of metastasis. Among metastatic LNs, seven contained isolated tumor cells, 13 contained micro‐metastases, and 38 contained macro‐metastases. Treatment‐related changes secondary to NT, including fibrosis, necrosis, multinucleated giant cells, and histiocyte accumulation, were observed in 33 LNs. Results of imprint, scrape, and combined cytology

Imprint cytology identified 30 of 300 LNs (10.0%) as positive, yielding 29 true‐positives, 241 true‐negatives, one false‐positive, and 29 false‐negatives. Overall specificity was high (99.58%), whereas sensitivity was limited (50.00%), resulting in a diagnostic accuracy of 90.00%.

In patients who received NT, imprint cytology maintained perfect specificity (100%) but demonstrated reduced sensitivity (48.48%). In patients without NT, sensitivity was slightly higher (52.00%), whereas specificity remained high (99.12%).

Scrape cytology detected metastatic involvement in 39 of 300 LNs (13.0%) and showed improved sensitivity (60.34%) compared with imprint cytology, while preserving high specificity (98.34%) and an overall diagnostic accuracy of 91.00%. Among patients receiving NT, scrape cytology achieved a marked increase in sensitivity (69.69%) with excellent specificity (99.21%). In contrast, sensitivity was lower in patients without NT (48.00%), although specificity remained high.

Combined cytology identified the highest number of positive LNs (43 of 300, 14.3%), with an overall sensitivity of 65.51% and specificity of 97.93%, resulting in a diagnostic accuracy of 91.66%. In patients receiving NT, combined cytology demonstrated diagnostic performance comparable to scrape cytology, whereas in patients without NT, sensitivity was modestly higher than imprint cytology but did not substantially exceed that of scrape cytology.

Comparison of imprint cytology, scrape cytology, and combined (imprint + scrape) cytology

ROC curve analysis demonstrated an AUC of 0.748 (95% CI, 0.663–0.833; p < .001) for imprint cytology, 0.793 (95% CI, 0.714–0.873; p < .001) for scrape cytology, and 0.817 (95% CI, 0.741–0.894; p < .001) for the combined approach, indicating a gradual improvement in diagnostic performance.

Pairwise comparisons of correlated ROC curves were performed using DeLong’s test. The combined method showed a statistically significant improvement in diagnostic performance compared with imprint cytology (AUC 0.817 vs. 0.748; p = .004), indicating that adding scrape cytology to imprint cytology significantly enhances overall accuracy. Although the AUC of the combined method was higher than that of scraping cytology alone (0.817 vs. 0.793), this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = .108). Similarly, the difference between imprint and scrape cytology (AUC 0.748 vs. 0.793) was not statistically significant (p = .125).

In patients who received NT, ROC curve analysis demonstrated an AUC of 0.741 for the imprint cytology (95% CI, 0.628–0.856; p < .001), 0.845 for the scrape cytology (95% CI, 0.748–0.941; p < .001), and 0.845 for the combined method (95% CI, 0.748–0.941; p < .001). Pairwise comparisons of correlated ROC curves were performed using DeLong’s test. Scrape cytology showed a statistically significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy compared with imprint cytology (p = .005). Similarly, the combined (imprint plus scrape) method was significantly superior to imprint cytology alone (p = .005). In contrast, there was no detectable difference between scrape cytology and the combined method in this subgroup, as both methods had identical AUC values, and the DeLong comparison yielded no evaluable test statistics.

In patients without NT, the AUC values for imprint, scrape, and combined methods were 0.756 (95% CI, 0.627–0.884; p < .001), 0.727 (95% CI, 0.597–0.857; p < .001), and 0.782 (95% CI, 0.661–0.904; p < .001), respectively. Pairwise comparisons of correlated ROC curves using DeLong’s test showed no statistically significant differences in AUC between imprint and scrape (p = .70), imprint and the combined method (p = .62), or scrape and the combined method (p = .24). Thus, although the combined method yielded the numerically highest AUC in the NT = 0 subgroup, its discriminative ability was not statistically superior to that of either imprint or scrape alone.

Results of metastasis size

Metastatic involvement was identified in 58 LN, including seven with isolated tumor cells (ITCs), 13 with micro‐metastases, and 38 with macro‐metastases. Among LNs with ITCs, imprint cytology detected one case, whereas scrape and combined cytology each detected two cases. Two ITC‐positive LNs were from patients who had received NT, both showing treatment‐related changes. In LNs with micro‐metastases, imprint cytology detected six cases, scrape cytology detected eight cases, and combined nine cases. Of these, three LNs were from patients treated with NT, none of which showed treatment‐related changes. Macro‐metastases were detected in 22 LNs by imprint cytology, 25 by scrape cytology, and 27 by combined cytology. Most macro‐metastatic LNs were from patients who had received NT, although treatment‐related changes were infrequent. Diagnostic accuracy increased with metastasis size across all cytological methods, with lower detection rates in isolated tumor cells and higher rates in macro‐metastases. However, this trend did not reach statistical significance for imprint cytology (p = .082), scrape cytology (p = .171), or combined cytology (p = .102).

Excluding ITCs from the reference standard resulted in higher true‐positive rates and fewer false‐negative results, particularly for scrape and combined cytology. Restricting the analysis to macro‐metastases further emphasized this trend, with combined cytology yielding the highest detection rate. Percentages are calculated based on the total number of LNs (n = 300).

Effect of NT‐related changes

Among patients who received NT, treatment‐related changes were observed in 33 of 161 LNs (20.4%). Metastatic involvement was present in four of these LNs, including two with isolated tumor cells and two with macro‐metastases, whereas the remaining 29 LNs were tumor‐free.

Within LNs exhibiting treatment‐related changes (n = 33), imprint cytology detected metastasis in three cases, corresponding to three true‐positives (9.09%), 29 true‐negatives (87.88%), no false‐positives, and one false‐negative (3.03%). In contrast, scrape cytology and combined cytology showed identical diagnostic performance in this subgroup, detecting metastatic involvement in four LNs (12.12%), with 28 true‐negatives (84.85%), one false‐positive (3.03%), and no false‐negatives.

DISCUSSION

In the assessment of the SLN during IOC, a section is taken from the identified LN. If no metastasis is detected, axillary dissection is avoided, reducing the risk of surgical complications such as bleeding, infection, chronic pain, lymphedema, and rare conditions like angiosarcoma. 7

Cytological examination of SLNs can be performed in centers without frozen section capability. 9 A key limitation of diagnosing metastasis solely from cytology is that the size of the metastasis cannot be communicated to the clinician. Today, the decision to proceed with axillary LN dissection can differ according to the patient’s treatment status, the number of metastatic nodes, and metastasis size. Thus, although cytological methods facilitate diagnosis, they may not be fully adequate on their own, particularly in patients who received NT. Because of the structure of the LN, the experience of the person performing the section, and device‐related reasons, the quality of the frozen section may be poor, and a correct evaluation may not be possible based on the frozen section alone. Therefore, in patients receiving NT, frozen section combination with cytology appears to be more beneficial than frozen section alone.

In our department, combined (imprint + scrape) cytology is routinely used simultaneously with frozen sections in SLN examination. During IOC, each dissected LN is assigned a number, and the cytology and frozen section preparations made for that LN are recorded. After imprint and scrape preparations are completed, they are quickly placed in fixative material to preserve cellular morphology.

Several reasons have been proposed for the generally low sensitivity in cytological preparations. One is the increase in FN because cytological methods do not reflect the entire structure of the LN. 11 Another is that tumor cells may not be located on the tissue surface and become visible only when deeper sections are taken. 10 In our study, the overall sensitivity of imprint cytology was 50.00% for all Vs. When isolated tumor cells were excluded from the analysis, sensitivity increased to 54.90%. With the additional exclusion of micro‐metastases, sensitivity further increased to 57.89%. Abe et al. 9 reported a sensitivity of 50% only in LNs containing macro‐metastases. Arlicot and colleagues 11 attributed the low sensitivity of the imprint method (33.3%) in their multicenter study to the generally small primary tumor sizes and differences in pathologists' experience levels. This size‐dependent increase in sensitivity likely reflects the limited ability of imprint cytology to detect small‐volume metastatic disease. In our study, the specificity of the imprint method was 99.6%, like other studies in the literature. We believe the high specificity in our cohort may reflect a conservative diagnostic approach by pathologists, who tend to classify ambiguous findings as negative to avoid false positives. False‐positives in SLN assessment may result in unnecessary axillary LN dissection and increased morbidity. 17 However, if positivity is detected on routine tissue processing, the problem can be corrected with a second operation.

Mannel et al. 21 reported a sensitivity of 50% and a specificity of 100% for scrape cytology in LNs containing only micro‐metastases. Bruzzone et al. 10 likewise reported higher sensitivity for macro‐metastases (94%) than for micro‐metastases (40%). Similarly, in our series, the sensitivity of scrape cytology was 60.3% and the specificity was 98.3% for all LNs. Sensitivity increased to 64.71% after exclusion of isolated tumor cells and further improved to 65.79% when the analysis was restricted to macro‐metastatic disease. As with imprint cytology, the sensitivity of scrape cytology increased with tumor size. Like our study, Avval et al. 16 reported higher sensitivity for scrape cytology (90%) compared to imprint cytology (73%). This difference can be explained by the inherently higher cellularity of scrape cytology. In our study, combined cytology demonstrated an overall sensitivity of 65.51% across all LNs, with sensitivity increasing to 70.59% after exclusion of isolated tumor cells and further to 71.05% when only macro‐metastases were considered, indicating improved performance with increasing metastatic burden.

Detection of micro‐metastases and isolated tumor cells is challenging with both cytological methods and frozen sections, but becomes easier with increasing pathologist experience. Some studies have reported higher sensitivity and specificity when LNs containing micro‐metastases are excluded from analyses. This may be applicable in routine practice, as axillary dissection is no longer performed in patients without NT, even if micro‐metastases are identified on IOC or permanent sections. 6 , 12 , 20 , 28 However, the situation is different in patients who received NT, where even a single atypical cell can warrant axillary LN dissection. Accordingly, this study evaluates the optimal cytological approach for intraoperative assessment in patients receiving NT.

Reported sensitivity rates for imprint cytology in patients who received NT range from 38.2% to 84.6%, with specificity ranging from 94.9% to 100% (Table 6). 4 , 6 , 14 , 15 , 25 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 In two separate studies involving cases known to have pre‐NT metastatic LNs, it was observed that the sensitivity of imprint cytology increased as tumor size increased. They reported that this method could be applied with high accuracy to macro‐metastases. 14 , 32 Ersoy et al. 25 also emphasized that the sensitivity of imprint cytology increased when micro‐metastases were excluded from the calculation in patients with NT. Jain et al. 29 found the sensitivity of imprint cytology to be 100%. Their study differs from others in that they prepared an average of seven (4–12) smears per LN. Furthermore, their cohort included very few patients. 29 Other studies also support the use of imprint cytology alone in cases with NT. 30 There are also studies suggesting that the sensitivity of imprint cytology is quite low in NT‐positive cases and that it should not be performed. 4 In our opinion, because axillary lymph node dissection is also performed in cases with isolated tumor cells and micro‐metastases in NT‐positive cases, a more sensitive cytology method than imprint cytology is required for IOC.

In our study, imprint cytology sensitivity was lower in patients who received NT compared to patients without NT (48.48% vs. 52.00%), whereas scrape cytology and combined cytology sensitivities were higher (both are 69.69%.). Bruzzone et al. 10 similarly found scrape cytology to have higher sensitivity in patients who received NT.

Histopathological changes such as fat necrosis, fibrosis, histiocyte accumulation, and granulation tissue are commonly observed in axillary LNs after NT. Treatment‐related changes in LNs have been reported to increase FN in imprint cytology, thereby lowering sensitivity. 31 , 33 In our series, among LNs with treatment‐related changes, scrape and combined cytology had higher sensitivity than imprint cytology (100%, 100% vs. 75%) We attribute this to the fact that tumor cells, reduced in number and often trapped within stroma or around vascular structures due to treatment effects, may fail to reach the slide surface in imprint cytology.

ROC curve analysis demonstrated a stepwise improvement in diagnostic performance from imprint cytology to scrape cytology and further to the combined approach, highlighting the additive value of scrape cytology in intraoperative sentinel LN evaluation. The significantly higher AUC of the combined method compared with imprint cytology suggests improved discriminative ability, largely driven by a reduction in FN results.

In patients who received NT, scrape and combined cytology showed markedly higher diagnostic performance than imprint cytology, with identical AUC values. This finding indicates that scrape cytology alone may achieve optimal diagnostic yield in LNs with treatment‐related changes, limiting the additional benefit of combination in this subgroup. In contrast, no significant differences were observed among the three methods in patients without NT, although the combined approach yielded numerically higher AUC values.

Overall, these findings support the use of scrape cytology—either alone or in combination—as a more reliable intraoperative approach than imprint cytology, particularly in patients receiving NT.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the diagnostic performance of imprint, scrape, and combined cytology in patients receiving NT and in LNs with treatment‐related changes.

Limitations of our study include that it was conducted at a single center and was not performed during an IOC. In our routine IOC practice, scrape cytology and imprint cytology are evaluated together and interpreted alongside frozen section, resulting in a single integrated intraoperative diagnosis. Therefore, method‐specific intraoperative diagnostic performance could not be assessed retrospectively.

In conclusion, scrape cytology demonstrated a significantly higher diagnostic performance than imprint cytology, especially in patients with NT. The combined use of both methods significantly improved diagnostic accuracy compared with imprint cytology alone; however, it did not provide a statistically significant advantage over scrape cytology. These findings indicate that scrape cytology may be sufficient as a primary diagnostic tool, whereas the combined approach may be advantageous when initial assessment is suboptimal.
