Education vs. service in medical training: a false dichotomy that refuses to die
Authors: Richard J Paulson
Published: 2026

Last week I reviewed the results of our most recent American Council of Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) Fellow survey. As instructed, I reviewed our “grades”—the scores assigned to our program by our fellows as they struggled to understand a series of often ambiguous questions. Among the questions was my least favorite: the one that asks whether there is an appropriate balance between education and service.

This question has been around for many years. The ACGME recently changed the wording slightly. It used to say, “Education (not) compromised by service obligations.” Now it says, “Education compromised by non-physician obligations” (1). This is hardly a meaningful improvement. Does transporting the patient to the radiology suite constitute “service” or is it a “non-physician obligation?” More importantly, why should either be presumed to be incompatible with education?

I am hardly the first to argue against the use of this question in the survey. The title of this editorial is borrowed from Stoff et al. (2), whose 2017 article entitled “Education Versus Service in Residency: A False Dichotomy?” argues eloquently that, “by using this language, the ACGME implies that education and service are mutually exclusive pursuits that must be balanced against one another” (2). I believe that they were exactly right. The premise is fundamentally flawed, and it sends an unfortunate message to trainees. Do we really want to suggest that certain activities essential to patient care are somehow beneath physicians? And where, exactly, do we draw the line? Starting intravenous lines? Drawing blood? Performing ultrasound examinations? Documenting patient encounters?

In our fellowship, I encourage fellows to perform as many ultrasound examinations as possible because I consider ultrasound to be a core skill of our subspecialty. Nevertheless, I have already been cautioned that the large number of ultrasounds performed by our fellows could be viewed as “service.” Do I really need to explain that only repeated exposure allows trainees to encounter the uncommon 1% of cases that ultimately define expertise? Competence in procedural medicine is built through volume and repetition, not through isolated educational exercises.

The question of service versus education presumes that the trainee has a clear idea of what each parameter represents. In 2012, Galvin and Buys performed a structured survey in an effort to identify resident perceptions of “service” vs. “clinical education.” Not surprisingly, they found widely varying interpretations and no consistent definition (3). In the accompanying editorial, Ludmerer suggested that, “we could simply substitute the term exploitation for service” (4). While intentionally provocative, that proposal at least makes the underlying concern explicit. If exploitation is the problem, then let us ask whether education is compromised by exploitation—not by service.

One of the ACGME's six core competencies is Systems-Based Practice, defined as the ability to “understand and respond to the larger context and system of health care to provide optimal patient care and advocacy” (5). Surely, this competency illustrates that education and service are not opposing forces but complementary components of physician training. Learning how to care for patients within a complex health care system necessarily involves providing that care.

Stoff et al. (2) concluded by recommending that the ACGME abandon the false dichotomy between education and service and instead focus on whether the activities of residency meaningfully contribute to a program's educational objectives. I wholeheartedly agree. I would go one step further. The ACGME should also abandon its “one-size-fits-all” approach to accreditation. A subspecialty fellowship consisting of a handful of fellows learning highly specialized procedural skills from a small group of expert faculty bears little resemblance to a large internal medicine residency with dozens of trainees acquiring broad clinical competencies from a large team of faculty with a wide variety of abilities. The educational goals, learning environments, and methods of instruction are fundamentally different. Our accreditation standards should acknowledge those differences rather than forcing all training programs into the same conceptual framework.

Ultimately, the goal of graduate medical education is not to protect trainees from service, but to ensure that service remains inseparable from learning. The best physicians are not trained despite caring for patients; they are trained by caring for them.
