Quantification of pendimethalin residue in green fodder and silage of winter cereals using gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry
Authors: Navjot Singh Brar, Kousik Mandal, Simerjeet Kaur, Amanpal Kaur Sandhu, Makhan Singh Bhullar, Maninder Pal Singh
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Use of agro-chemicals in forage crops is restricted due to the fear of direct toxicity to livestock and risk of pesticide residue accumulation in the food chain. Wheat and barley can be used as green fodder and silage, and herbicide residue estimation in green fodder and silage is important for ensuring the safety of dairy cattle. A field experiment was conducted for two years to study pendimethalin residues in the green fodder and silage of wheat and barley. In both cereal crops, pendimethalin (1.125 kg a.i./ha) was applied as pre-emergence along with an unsprayed control. Pendimethalin residues in fodder, silage, and soil were estimated using gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS). At harvest, pendimethalin residues in fodder and silage of wheat and barley were below the limit of quantification (<0.01 mg/kg) during both crop seasons. Pendimethalin can be safely used for weed control in winter cereals grown for fodder and silage.

Introduction

The availability of high-quality green fodder round the year is one of the critical factors which determine the success of livestock sector.1,2) The major sources of fodder supply are forage crops, crop residues, rangelands, pastures and grazing lands under sub-tropical conditions. At present, India is facing 35.6% deficit of green fodder.3) There is need to explore various crops for fodder production to meet fodder requirement of dairy cattle as well as generating enough returns to sustain livelihood of farmers. In north-west India, farmers realize a higher net income by harvesting small grain cereals as forage rather than as grain. Winter cereals such as wheat and barley are used as green fodder and silage in times of scarcity of forage crops viz., oat (Avena sativa L.), corn (Zea mays L.), egyptian clover (Trifolium alexandrinum L.), etc.

Wheat and barley have the potential to be used as silage for ruminants.4–6) Among the winter crop, wheat is a major cereal crop of Indo-Gangetic plains, and feeding barley occupies the largest share of the domestic forage production.7) Further, winter annual weed, little seed canary grass (Phalaris minor L.) has evolved resistance to conventionally used herbicides (isoproturon, clodinafop, sulfosulfuron), and uncontrolled weed growth can lead to significant reduction in grain yield of winter crops especially wheat.8) In such fields, growers can use wheat for green fodder/silage before shattering of weed seeds in the field. Such use of wheat as fodder meets the fodder demand in case of scarcity and helps in the depletion of weed seed bank.

Weeds compete with forage crops for nutrients, water, light and space and results in yield and quality loss.9) Weeds can be controlled with hand weeding, but non-availability of working force, high labour cost and unfavourable climatic conditions at time of mechanical weeding operations restricted its use in fodder crops.10) Chemical weed control is the easiest, cheaper and simplest method. Pendimethalin, a pre-emergence herbicide is commonly used in cereal crops for control of grass weeds. Pendimethalin is a dinitroaniline herbicide that inhibits cell division through inhibition of microtubule assembly (Herbicide Resistance Action Committee, HRAC 3). It has toxic rating IV (Unlikely to pose any hazards) with oral LD50 of 4050 mg/kg,11) half-life of 15–77 days in soil,12) and is classified as a persistent bio-accumulative toxin by the Environmental Protection Agency.13) In wheat fields, persistence of pendimethalin @ 4 kg a.i./ha was observed up to 200 days in sandy loam soil.14) However, pendimethalin residues in grains at harvest was below detectable level.

There are certain constraints to use of pesticides in forage crops such as risk of pesticide residue accumulation in the food chain through milk and milk products and also chances of direct toxicity to livestock.15) The lack of policies and pesticide recommendations specific to forage-crop protection further restrict the use of any pesticide in the fodder crops in India.16) Fodder crops are generally harvested between 60–120 days after sowing (DAS) and it increases the probability of toxic residue of pendimethalin in green fodder of these crops. Wheat and barley are harvested at milk stage to fed dairy animals as green fodder/silage and there are chances of herbicide residue in green fodder of these crops. It is a matter of concern that if wheat/barley crop in which pendimethalin is used for weed control, is used as forage crops, there can be residues in fodder or silage. To ensure food safety and human health and to ensure the safety of livestock, herbicide residue needs to be examined in green fodder and silage of winter cereals namely wheat and barley. Further, there is need to quantify the pendimethalin in residue in soil at harvest of winter cereals for fodder production.

Materials and methods

1. Experimental site

Field experiment was conducted during 2020–21 and 2021–22 at Student Research Farm (30°54′30″N; 75°46′51″E), Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana in irrigated agro-ecosystem. The meteorological data, including mean temperature, relative humidity and rainfall from the date of sowing to final sampling is presented in Fig. 1. The soil of experiment field was loamy sand with normal pH (7.3) reaction, EC 0.17 dS/m, medium in organic carbon (0.45%), low available nitrogen (235 kg/ha), high in available phosphorus (39 kg/ha) and available potassium (150 kg/ha).

Fig. 1. Meteorological data comprising mean monthly air temperature, rainfall and relative humidity during the experimental period of 2020–21 and 2021–22.

2. Experimental design and treatments

Weed control efficacy and residue of pendimethalin in green fodder and silage of winter cereals (wheat and barley) was studied in rice-wheat and rice-barley cropping systems (Table 1; Fig. 2). The experiment was conducted in randomized complete block design with four replications and the size of each plot was kept 22.68 m2. Rice was grown for grains during summer season whereas wheat and barley were grown for fodder/silage during winter season by following recommended package of practices (Table 2). Pendimethalin 1.125 kg a.i./ha was applied in barley and wheat along with untreated control.

3. Application of herbicide, observations on weeds and harvesting of crop

Pendimethalin at the field use rate (1.125 kg a.i./ha) was sprayed in the field on the same day after sowing. The field was sprayed with the ASPEE Knapsack sprayer fitted with flood jet nozzle using 500 L of water/ha. Weed density and biomass was recorded in each plot (two sub-samples per plot) with the help of 50 cm×50 cm quadrates at 60 DAS and at harvest. Weed control efficiency was calculated based on weed biomass in unsprayed control. Crop was harvested at milk stage as green fodder for silage making (which was achieved after 125 DAS in wheat and 117 DAS in barley). Fresh forages usually contain 70 to 80% water, and forage yield was recorded on dry matter (DM) basis in order to standardize analysis.

4. Sampling procedure for residue estimation

Green fodder samples of wheat and barley (around 1 kg each) were collected randomly from each plot at 60 DAS and harvest. For silage making, wheat and barley green fodder samples were chopped to 2–3 cm particle length and were ensiled individually for 45 days in low density polypropylene bags in triplicate without adding any additive.17) Silage bags were opened after the termination of fermentation process, and representative samples were taken from each bag.

Soil samples (15-cm depth) were randomly collected from herbicide-treated and control plots in each replication using tube auger at harvest. Soil samples were collected from 10 sites from each plot and pooled to draw a representative sample (about 500 g). Samples were sieved with 2 mm sieve and extraneous matter including pebbles was removed.

5. Chemical, reagents and instruments

The certified reference material of pendimethalin (purity 97.34%) was supplied by Dr Ehrenstorfer GmbH, Augsburg, Germany. Pendimethalin (Stomp 30 EC) formulation used for application was obtained from M/s BASF India Limited, Mumbai, India. Solvents like acetonitrile (HPLC grade), sodium sulphate (Na2SO4) anhydrous (AR grade), and sodium chloride (ASC reagent grade ≥99.9%) (NaCl) used were from SRL, Maharashtra, India. Analytical grade activated anhydrous MgSO4 and Primary secondary amine (PSA) sorbent was obtained from Agilent Technologies, USA. For the quantification of pendimethalin residues, we used gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS) of Shimadzu, Model GCMS-TQ8040 NX (M/s Shimadzu, Japan).

6. Preparation of standard solution

A standard stock solution of pendimethalin @ 1 mg/mL was made in acetone and standard solutions (0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04 and 0.05 µg/mL) used in construction of calibration curve were prepared by serial dilutions. Storage of all standard solutions was done at −4°C before use.

7. Extraction and cleanup with QuEChERS method

The green fodder and silage samples were prepared following QuEChERS method to determine pendimethalin residues. For analysis, 10 g sample was taken in a centrifuge tube of 50 mL capacity and it was closed tightly before storing it overnight in deep freezer. Then, put 20 mL of acetonitrile into tube and samples were homogenized for 2–3 min (15,000 rpm) in homogenizer. Then add anhydrous sodium chloride (10±0.1 g) to tube and shake manually followed by 20 min in roto-spin at 50 rpm. Sample was centrifuged in a centrifuge for 3 min @ 2,500 rpm. Top organic solvent layer of 10 mL (supernatant) from tube was transferred into another centrifuge tube containing anhydrous sodium sulphate (10±0.1 g). The sample extract (4 mL) from each tube was transferred into centrifuge tubes (15 mL capacity) containing primary secondary amine (PSA) sorbent (0.10±0.01 g) and activated anhydrous magnesium sulphate (0.6±0.01 g) by dispersive solid phase extraction. The tubes were tightly capped, vortexed for half minute and centrifuged for 2 min (@ 2,500 rpm). Finally, 2 mL aliquot was taken out of this extract for estimation by GC-MS/MS.

The homogeneous representative sample of soil was also processed by QuEChERS method. A soil sample (10 g) was taken in centrifuge tube of 50 mL capacity and stored overnight at −20°C. Next day, put 8 mL of distilled water and 20 mL of acetonitrile in centrifuge tube. Rest of the procedure for processing was same as that for green fodder.

8. Identification and estimation of herbicide residues by GC-MS/MS

Gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS) in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode was used for identification and estimation of pendimethalin residues. The GC-MS/MS was equipped with capillary column (30 m×0.25 mm×0.25 µm) and Helium at flow rate of 0.79 mL/min was used as carrier gas. The temperature of injector was maintained at 285°C and the temperature of programming was 80°C for 2 min hold, then increase @ 20°C/min till 180°C for 2 min hold, increase @ 2°C/min till 190°C for 2 min hold, increase @ 5°C/min till 220°C for 3 min hold. Injection volume was 1 µL in split mode (1 : 10). The m/z ratio of fragments of MRM compared with ions obtained from pendimethalin standards, was used for confirming herbicide in sample. The m/z ratio used for confirmation of pendimethalin was 252, 162.10, 191.10 and 208.10.

The retention time and area of peaks observed in the chromatograms compared to that of standard run was used for quantifying pendimethalin residues in the samples. The peak of pendimethalin was detected at 18.310 min. The residues were quantified by using the formula below: Residues (mg/ kg)= Standard injected (ng)Peak area of the standard×Peak area of the sampleSample injected (µL)× Final volume of sample (mL)Weight of sample (g)

9. Statistical analysis

Weed data was subjected to square root transformation to homogenize the variances however; weed data was discussed using means of original values. Two-year data was analysed for individual years as the experimental errors of the two experiments were not homogeneous tested by Bartlett’s test of homogeneity of variance. The analysis of variance with weed control and replications as fixed effect was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 19. Treatment means of response variables such as weed density, biomass and dry fodder yield were separated using Tukey’s b test at p=0.05.

Results and discussion

1. Weed density and biomass

Five broad leaved weed species included bur clover (Medicago denticulate Willd.), toothed dock (Rumex dentatus L.), scarlet pimpernel (Anagallis arvensis L.), swinecress (Coronopus didymus (L.) Sm.) and common lambsquarters (Chenopodium album L.) were predominant in the field during both years of study.

Total weed density was 63–70% lower in pendimethalin treated plots as compared to untreated control treatment. Pendimethalin significantly reduced density of all weed species except bur clover during both years (Tables 3 and 4). At 60 DAS and harvest, density of toothed dock, corn spurry, scarlet pimpernel, swine cress and common lambsquarters was recorded significantly lower with pendimethalin as compared to unsprayed control in wheat and barley. Application of pendimethalin resulted in significantly lower weed biomass at 60 DAS and at harvest as compared to untreated control treatments in wheat and barley (Table 5). Weed control efficiency with application of pendimethalin was 70.8 and 69.8% in wheat, 75.5 and 72.8% in barley during 2020–21 and 2021–22, respectively at 60 DAS.

In this research work, pendimethalin did not control bur clover but provided effective control of other weed species observed in experimental field. The results are in agreement with earlier findings where standalone application of pendimethalin as pre-emergence resulted in effective control of little seed canary grass, toothed dock and common lambsquarters but provided poor control of bur clover in wheat.18) Lower weed density in herbicide treated plots at 60 DAS and at harvest resulted in significantly lower weed biomass accumulation in these treatments and 66–70% weed control efficiency at harvest. Application of pendimethalin resulted in weed control efficiency of 65.7%19) and 73.5%18) in wheat. Herbicide application also resulted in the suppression of weeds and improved dry biomass production of barley.20)

2. Dry fodder yield

Application of pendimethalin resulted in 32.1–37.5% and 32.1–41.6% higher dry fodder yield in wheat and barley, respectively as compared to unsprayed control during both years of study (Table 5). The differences in dry fodder yield of wheat and barley were non-significant. Weed density and biomass was significantly lower under pendimethalin treated plots of wheat and barley and this lowered crop-weed competition resulted in higher fodder yield as compared to weedy.

3. Efficiency of analytical method

Fortification studies were performed out at different levels to examine the efficacy of the analytical method used. For this study, control samples of soil, green fodder and silage of wheat and barley from untreated plots were fortified with pendimethalin at 0.01 and 0.05 mg kg−1 for validating the method being used. The control samples from untreated plots and reagent blank were also processed to find out any interference due to substrate and reagents. The recovery of pendimethalin in green fodder, silage and soil samples were found to be consistent (Table 6; Fig. 3). The recovery range was observed within the acceptable range of 70–120%.21) The precision of the analytical method is expressed by relative standard deviation (RSD). For different spiking levels, RSD ranged from 1.86–3.93%. The limit of quantification was found to be 0.01 mg/kg and limit of detection being 0.003 mg/kg.

4. Residues of pendimethalin in green fodder and silage

Pendimethalin residues in green fodder was found to the level of 0.03 and 0.02 mg/kg in wheat and 0.02 and 0.02 mg/kg in barley at 60 days after application (DAA) during 2020–21 and 2021–22, respectively. However, pendimethalin residue in green fodder of wheat (125 DAA) and barley (117 DAA) at the time of harvest was detected below limit of quantification (LOQ) during both years (Table 7). Pendimethalin residue in silage of wheat and barley was also below LOQ (<0.01 mg/kg) during both years. Pendimethalin residues to the level of 0.04 µg/g in plant samples were observed at 30 DAS.10) The residues of pendimethalin applied at rate of 1.0–0.5 kg a.i./ha ranged from 0.003 to 0.021 µg/g in bulbs of onion (Allium cepa L.).22) As pendimethalin is soil uptake herbicide and found to be persistent in soil for 3–5 months but its residue was not observed in plant parts.23) However in another study, 0.014 µg/g of pendimethalin residue in radish (Raphanus sativus) was found at harvest.24) Pendimethalin residue in oilseed rape (B. oleracea, B. napus, B. juncea), peas (Pisum sativum) and garlic (Allium sativum) were found to be below the LOQ despite the presence of high level of pendimethalin residue in soil at harvest.25)

Pendimethalin residue in silage was also reported below the LOQ. Although, the safety standards for pendimethalin residue for fodder crops are not quantified for the region under study. Still, it is established that silage formation is a complex bio-chemical process which involves multiple microorganisms and chemical transformations during 45 days of anaerobic fermentation of green fodder.26) Some bacteria can use pesticides as energy sources, such as carbon sources and phosphorus sources to promote the production of organic acids.27) The 100% degradation of residual pesticide in white clover was observed and residues were far below the safety standards after silage fermentation.28)

5. Residue of pendimethalin in soil

At harvest, 0.05 mg/kg and 0.01 mg/kg of pendimethalin residue was found in soils from wheat field (125 DAA), and 0.06 mg/kg and 0.02 mg/kg were found in soil from barley field (117 DAA) during 2020–21 and 2021–22, respectively (Table 7). An earlier study reported 0.05 and 0.06 mg/kg of pendimethalin residue in loamy sand soil after 45 days of its application at 0.56 kg/ha and 0.75 kg/ha, respectively.25) In another study, 0.014 µg/g of pendimethalin residues in soil after 120 days of its application at 0.75 kg a.i/ha was reported in wheat field.29) Under tropical conditions, pendimethalin persisted in soil from 60 to 200 days.11) Persistence of pendimethalin residue up to 30 days in summer season after harvest of groundnut was reported.10) Further, excessive and repeated use of herbicide may cause health hazards and lead to residue accumulation in crop produce, soil and ground water.11)

Further, it was observed that pendimethalin residue in soil at harvest was higher in 2020–21 as compared to 2021–22. It may be due to fact that mean monthly temperature in November (18.8°C) and December (13.7°C) was 1.2°C and 0.4°C more during 2021–22 than temperature during these months in 2020–21 (17.6°C and 13.3°C) (Fig. 1). The dissipation of pendimethalin was faster at higher temperature.29) Further, there were 113.4 and 43.5 mm of rainfall during January and February months during 2021–22 as compared to meagre 2.3 mm and 7.3 mm rainfall in these months during 2020–21 (Fig. 1). This more rainfall during 2021–22 resulted in more soil moisture content and increased soil microbial activity may accelerate the degradation of pendimethalin in soil. The soil microorganisms may utilize pendimethalin as a source of carbon and energy.30) An increase in soil moisture level from 30% to 90% of field capacity resulted in almost four-fold increase of degradation rate of pendimethalin in sandy loam and clay loam soils.31) Degradation of pendimethalin in soil was enhanced after repeated application due to increase in microbial decomposition32) and high residue of pendimethalin in soil was observed, where there is less microbial activity.33) Though soil microbial activity was not quantified in present study but, it could be the reason of faster degradation of pendimethalin in soil, which resulted in detection of lower residue in soil at harvest during 2021–22 than 2020–21.

In summary, it is concluded that pendimethalin resulted in effective weed control in wheat and barley (grown for silage making) and resulted in higher fodder yield. Pendimethalin residue in green fodder of wheat (125 DAA) and barley (117 DAA) was recorded below LOQ (<0.01 mg/kg) at harvest. Similarly, pendimethalin residue in silage of wheat (170 DAA) and barley (162 DAA) was also recorded below LOQ (<0.01 mg/kg). However, pendimethalin residue was 0.02–0.03 mg/kg and 0.02 mg/kg in green fodder of wheat and barley, respectively when harvested at 60 DAA. Therefore, green fodder of wheat and barley is safe for animal consumption when harvested at milk stage. However, 60 days old crop can’t be fed to the dairy animals without fear of toxicity. Silage of wheat and barley can be safely used for animal feed. At harvest pendimethalin residue in soil was 0.05–0.06 mg/kg in first year while residue was 0.01–0.02 mg/kg in second year, indicating the scope of field-scale investigation on role of climatic parameters and microbial populations on herbicide persistence.
