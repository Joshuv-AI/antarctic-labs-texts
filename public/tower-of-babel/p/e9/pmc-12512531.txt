Trends in cancer burden for adolescents and young adults in China from 1990 to 2021
Authors: Xing Xing, Lu Lan, Wenyu Yang, Zhiyong Qu, He Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer among adolescents and young adults (AYAs), defined as ages 15–39 years, is an increasing public health concern globally, characterized by unique epidemiological patterns and significant health and societal burdens. Despite rising evidence of its impact in China, a comprehensive assessment of the cancer burden remains Limited. This study aims to examine trends in the cancer burden among Chinese AYAs from 1990 to 2021.MethodsData were extracted from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study. The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR), and age-standardized disability-adjusted life year (DALY) rate (ASDR) per 100,000 population were calculated and stratified by sex, alongside risk-attributable burdens. Trends were examined using the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) and joinpoint regression, and projections were made using auto-regressive integrated moving average models.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, the incidence of cancer among AYAs increased, with ASIR rising from 36.74 to 51.51 per 100,000 population. However, mortality decreased, with ASMR declining from 22.53 to 14.89 per 100,000 population, and the disability burden also decreased, with ASDR declining from 1,331.48 to 689.68 per 100,000 population. Breast cancer emerged as the leading contributor to incidence burden (ASIR: 6.03), while tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancers, along with leukemia, accounted for the highest mortality (ASMR: 1.73) and DALYs (ASDR: 98.73) burden among AYAs. Tobacco use and dietary risks were the largest contributors to the overall cancer burden, whereas unsafe sex remained the principal risk factor for cervical cancer.ConclusionsOur findings highlight a potentially increasing cancer burden among AYAs in China, primarily due to lifestyle, environmental, and infectious factors. Future prevention efforts should target both traditional risk factors and emerging lifestyle threats, with a focus on cancer prevention and screening for AYAs at high risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15011-0.

Introduction

Cancer among adolescents and young adults (AYAs), aged 15–39 years, has emerged as a significant global health challenge, with distinct epidemiological patterns and substantial lifelong impacts [1–3]. In 2022, this population accounted for approximately 1.3 million new cancer cases and 370,000 deaths worldwide, showing a persistent annual incidence increase of 0.53% since 2010 [3]. AYA cancers present unique clinical challenges, differing from both pediatric and older adult cancers in tumor biology and long-term survival outcomes [4, 5]. Survivors face elevated risks of chronic conditions and secondary malignancies, as well as socioeconomic consequences, including workforce productivity loss, fertility preservation challenges, and significant psychosocial issues [6–9].

Several surveillance studies highlight the growing AYA burden in developed countries. For example, the U.S. data from 2000 to 2019 reveal a concerning 1.00% annual increase in invasive AYA cancers, with 35% of cases associated with obesity [10–12]. Studies from Japan and Europe have identified central nervous system (CNS), breast, gonadal, and hematologic cancers as the most prevalent types; however, survival rates remain suboptimal for certain leukemias (59%) and CNS tumors (62%) [13–15]. These findings have informed targeted screening and prevention programs internationally.

Emerging evidence indicates that AYA cancers are becoming a critical public health burden in China. However, to date, no study has comprehensively characterized the temporal trends, attributable risks, and future trajectories of AYA cancers across all major sites in China. A 2021 global survey study highlighted that China and India bear the highest incidence and disease burden of AYA breast cancer, potentially attributable to developmental risk factors disproportionately affecting young Asian women [16]. Moreover, other global AYA cancer surveillance studies have revealed a substantial disease burden across multiple AYA cancer sites in China, including gastrointestinal [17], gonadal [18], hematologic [19], and thyroid cancers [20]. Most existing research has focused on a single cancer type, which does not provide a comprehensive assessment of the total AYA cancer burden, associated risk factors, or evidence-based projections to inform prevention strategies.

To address this research gap, this study uses updated GBD 2021 data to comprehensively examine the incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of AYA cancers in China from 1990 to 2021. Additionally, we quantify the contribution of risk factors and project future trends through 2040. Our findings are expected to guide targeted prevention strategies and optimize resource allocation for AYA cancer control in China.

Methods

Data sources and study sample

The data were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021; http://www.healthdata.org/gbd). The GBD is a systematic, collaborative research database designed to quantify the health impact of diseases, injuries, and risk factors worldwide, initially launched in 1991 by the World Health Organization (WHO). Its unique strengths in longitudinal consistency, risk factor attribution, and global coverage have made it widely used to analyze disease burden trends, evaluate health disparities, and inform public health policies worldwide [21, 22]. The GBD data are de-identified and aggregated.

In this study, AYA cancers were defined as cancers occurring in individuals aged 15–39 years, consistent with internationally recognized criteria (e.g., National Cancer Institute and Adolescent and Young Adult Oncology Progress Review Group) [1, 2]. We examined all 30 cancer sites classified in the GBD data according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). The analyzed cancer sites primarily included: (1) colon and rectum cancer (CRC), (2) non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC), (3) stomach cancer, (4) leukemia, (5) liver cancer, (6) brain and CNS cancer, (7) nasopharynx cancer, (8) tracheal, bronchus, and lung (TBL) cancer, (9) kidney cancer, (10) thyroid cancer, (11) esophageal cancer, (12) pancreatic cancer, and (13) breast cancer. The complete list of cancer sites with corresponding ICD-10 codes is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The ‘other neoplasms’ category was excluded from primary analyses because it predominantly consists of benign tumors and non-malignant conditions, which differ fundamentally from other invasive cancers.

Measures

We obtained estimates of incidence, mortality, and DALYs, in both absolute numbers and crude rates, as well as risk-attributable burdens (excluding risk factors contributing less than 1%), from the GBD 2021 for AYA cancers in China from 1990 to 2021, stratified by sex. All estimates are reported with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) to account for potential variability in the statistical modeling. For a detailed methodology regarding the GBD estimation process, please refer to previously published studies [23].

Since the GBD study only provides crude rates for specific age groups, we calculated age-standardized incidence rates (ASIRs), age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs), and age-standardized DALY rates (ASDRs) per 100,000 population for AYA cancers in China from 1990 to 2021. These age-standardized rates were computed using the direct standardization method and adjusted to the World Segi’s standard population structure [24]. This methodological approach allows for more accurate comparisons of disease burden by eliminating the confounding effects of variations in age structure.

Statistical analysis

To assess temporal trends in ASIRs, ASMRs, and ASDRs for AYA cancers from 1990 to 2021, we calculated the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) using a linear regression model. The EAPC, a widely adopted metric for quantifying rate trends, was derived using the following functions:

Ln(ASRs) represents the natural logarithm of the age-standardized rates; α denotes the intercept term, reflecting the baseline log-rate; β is the regression coefficient quantifying the annual rate of change; and ε accounts for random error. Trends were classified as significantly increasing when both the EAPC and the lower Limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) were greater than 0, significantly decreasing when both the EAPC and the upper Limit of the 95% CI were less than 0, and stable if the 95% CI included 0.

In addition to the EAPC analysis, we performed joinpoint regression analysis to identify potential inflection points in trends using Joinpoint software (version 5.0.2; National Cancer Institute, USA). Annual percentage changes (APCs) and 95% CIs were calculated for each linear segment, and the optimal number of joinpoints was determined using the minimum Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

To project the disease burden through 2040, we employed the auto-regressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model, a method widely used in GBD-based disease burden studies [25, 26]. The ARIMA (p, d, q) framework includes p autoregressive terms, d differencing orders, and q moving average components. The trend data were first log-transformed to stabilize variance. We then applied differencing as needed to achieve stationarity, which was confirmed via the augmented Dickey-Fuller test. Optimal (p, d, q) parameters were determined through autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation analyses, with model selection guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The final ARIMA model was validated using the Ljung-Box Q test to ensure residual whiteness [27]. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0), and a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Trends in the incidence of AYA cancers in China

From 1990 to 2021, China experienced a clear upward trend in AYA cancer incidence (EAPC: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.61 to 1.03), with the ASIR increasing from 36.74 to 51.51 per 100,000 population (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S2 and Table S8). Besides NMSC, breast cancer exhibited the highest ASIR at 6.03 per 100,000 population, followed by CRC (ASIR: 5.18), cervical cancer (ASIR: 3.74), and stomach cancer (ASIR: 3.64). Most major cancer types showed increasing incidence trends, particularly breast cancer (EAPC: 2.12), CRC (EAPC: 1.41), and cervical cancer (EAPC: 1.16), while stomach cancer (EAPC: −1.56) was the only one to show a decline. Male AYAs accounted for 135,688 new cases (ASIR: 49.21), primarily driven by CRC and stomach cancer. In contrast, female AYAs had 140,251 new cases (ASIR: 53.93), which were dominated by breast and cervical cancers. Both genders experienced upward trends in ASIR (male EAPC: 0.84; female EAPC: 0.78) (Supplementary Table S2 and Fig. S1).

Joinpoint analysis revealed an overall upward trend in AYA cancer incidence, with a particularly accelerated increase during 2013–2021 (APC = 2.52, P < 0.001). No significant variations in trends were observed across anatomical sites, and the patterns were consistent with the EAPC findings (detailed in Supplementary Table S5).

Trends in mortality and dalys of AYA cancers in China

We observed general declines in mortality (EAPC: −1.60, 95% CI: −1.71 to −1.50) and DALYs (EAPC: −1.60, 95% CI: −1.78 to −1.42), with ASMR decreasing from 22.53 to 14.89 and ASDR from 1331.48 to 893.92 per 100,000 population (Fig. 2, Supplementary Tables S3-4 and Table S8). Leukemia was the leading burden (ASMR: 2.13; ASDR: 135.41), but TBL (ASMR: 1.73; ASDR: 98.73) emerged as the predominant cause of cancer mortality and DALYs, followed sequentially by CRC (ASMR: 1.58; ASDR: 93.93) and liver cancer (ASMR: 1.54; ASDR: 87.98). Most of these cancers exhibited significant declines, including TBL (ASMR EAPC: −0.78, ASDR EAPC: −1.01), CRC (ASMR EAPC: −1.25, ASDR EAPC: −1.14), and liver cancer (ASMR EAPC: −1.64, ASDR EAPC: −1.79). Males experienced disproportionately higher ASMR (male: 18.17 vs. female: 11.35) and ASDR (male: 1082.24 vs. female: 689.68) compared to females, driven primarily by TBL cancer, liver cancer, and CRC, while females’ burden was dominated by breast and cervical cancers. Both genders exhibited declines in ASMR (male EAPC: −1.26; female EAPC: −2.08) and ASDR (male EAPC: −1.30; female EAPC: −2.04) (Supplementary Tables S3-4 and Figs. S2-3). Given that DALYs showed similar trends to mortality, detailed figures on DALYs are provided in Supplementary Fig. S4.

Patterns of risk factors for AYA cancers in China

Figure 3 illustrates the proportional contributions of 11 risk factors to DALYs for major cancer sites among Chinese AYAs in 2021. Tobacco use emerged as the predominant risk factor, accounting for 37.7% of DALYs for TBL cancer, 16.8% for pancreatic cancer, and 11.0% for Liver cancer. Dietary risks were the primary attributable factors for 13.3% of breast cancer, 35.4% of CRC, and 7.9% of stomach cancer DALYs. Among male AYAs, high alcohol consumption contributed substantially to esophageal cancer (25.5%) and nasopharyngeal cancer (39.0%) DALYs. Projections of AYA cancers in China through 2040

Figure 4 projects AYA cancer trends in China through 2040. The overall ASIR is projected to increase significantly, reaching 102.01 per 100,000 in 2040, while ASMR is projected to decline notably (9.67 per 100,000), and ASDR is projected to remain stable (888.56 per 100,000) (Supplementary Table S9). Site-specific analysis reveals rising ASIR for breast, CRC, and CNS cancers but declining rates for stomach cancer. Most cancers displayed a decline in ASMR, while ASDR trends mirrored ASMR except for CRC and liver cancers, which remained stable. Sex-stratified results were consistent with the overall trends (Supplementary Figs. S7-S13). The optimal ARIMA model parameters and corresponding AIC/BIC values are detailed in Supplementary Table S10. All models demonstrated satisfactory goodness-of-fit, as confirmed by residual diagnostic tests (p > 0.05 for all tests).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the cancer burden among AYAs in China from 1990 to 2021. To our knowledge, it is the first study to use the 2021 GBD data to characterize temporal trends in AYA cancers in China. Our findings reveal increasing trends in ASIRs for most cancers but decreasing trends in ASMRs and ASDRs. Breast cancer was the leading incidence, and TBL cancer was the top contributor to mortality and DALYs following leukemia. Additionally, tobacco use, dietary risks, and alcohol consumption were primary drivers of the cancer burden, whereas unsafe sex was the leading risk factor specifically for cervical cancer. These results provide critical insights for AYA cancer prevention and health resource allocation in China.

The observed increasing trends in ASIRs and decreasing trends in ASMRs and ASDRs for AYA cancers in China reflect a complex epidemiological pattern influenced by both global trends and local specificities. Globally, rising AYA cancer incidence has been linked to modern lifestyle risk factors that vary significantly across countries. In developed countries, such as the United States and Europe, studies reported similar upward trends in incidence consistent with our findings in China, where AYA populations exhibit elevated risks related to obesity, insufficient physical activity, and Western dietary patterns. These factors have contributed to an increase in hormone-related cancers, such as CRC, breast cancer, and thyroid cancer [11–13]. In contrast, research from developing countries like India indicates that their AYA populations face a dual burden of infection-related cancers (such as hepatitis B-related liver cancer and HPV-driven cervical cancer) and lifestyle-related cancers (such as oral and tongue cancers linked to betel nut chewing and tobacco use) [28]. China occupies a unique position within this spectrum, shaped by rapid economic growth and lifestyle changes. These factors have introduced substantial environmental carcinogens such as air pollution and occupational exposures, while also driving behavioral changes, including increased consumption of processed foods, initiation of smoking and drinking among young adults, and delayed childbearing, all of which contribute to the rising ASIR of AYA cancers [16–20, 29]. Furthermore, the nationwide expansion of cancer screening programs over the past decade has Likely improved case detection, potentially accounting for the accelerated increase in ASIR observed since 2013 [30, 31]. Notably, China retains distinct characteristics in its AYA cancer pattern, including a relatively high proportion of infection-related cancers, such as nasopharyngeal and liver cancers (which remain in the top 7 and top 10 for incidence), compared to Western populations, while simultaneously experiencing increasing rates of “Western-style” cancers such as breast cancer and CRC. The decline in mortality likely reflects improved cancer control measures, including expanded vaccination programs (HBV), better access to treatment, and the promotion of cancer screening programs [30, 31], although urban-rural disparities persist [32]. These patterns suggest that AYA cancer epidemiology in China represents a hybrid mode, displaying the transition of both developing and developed countries, with prevention strategies needing to address both traditional risk factors and emerging lifestyle threats, while emphasizing cancer prevention and screening for high-risk AYAs.

Breast cancer constitutes the predominant incidence burden among AYAs in China, reflecting complex interactions among reproductive, lifestyle, and diagnostic factors. A potentially major contributor to the rising breast cancer incidence in Chinese AYA women is the transformation in reproductive behaviors, including delayed childbearing and lower parity, which result in prolonged lifetime estrogen exposure [16, 33]. Studies have found that AYA women in China tend to have fewer pregnancies, fewer births, and shorter durations of breastfeeding, all of which are associated with an increased risk of breast and cervical cancers [16, 18, 33]. Other important risk factors are lifestyle modifications and dietary changes, particularly the shift from traditional diets to more Westernized, high-fat diets characterized by increased red meat consumption [34]. These dietary changes, together with reduced physical activity due to sedentary work patterns and urban living, have contributed to rising body mass index (BMI) levels in the population. Elevated BMI is a well-established risk factor for both breast cancer and CRC, further exacerbating the disease burden [17, 34, 35]. Moreover, enhanced public awareness of female cancers (e.g., breast cancer and cervical cancer) symptoms and advanced diagnostic technologies have improved detection rates among AYA women in China. Efforts to promote self-examination and early clinical evaluation have led to earlier-stage diagnoses, which may partly explain the rising incidence [31, 36]. China has made significant progress in addressing female cancers, exemplified by national screening programs of breast and cervical cancers targeting rural women since 2009 [31]. However, these screening initiatives primarily target women aged 35–64, leaving the AYA population largely uncovered. Expanding the scope of these screening programs to include younger women should be considered. Additionally, although China has implemented policies to promote fertility, there has been insufficient emphasis on strengthening support for women of reproductive age [37]. Furthermore, our finding that the incidence of male breast cancer showed the most rapid increase (EAPC = 6.12) among all AYA cancers warrants greater attention and further research.

The substantial burden of TBL cancers represents a critical public health challenge in China, with a particularly disproportionate impact on male AYAs. Male AYAs are more likely to engage in smoking and to be exposed to tobacco-related carcinogens through both active smoking and second-hand smoke. This heightened exposure directly contributes to the high mortality observed in the AYA population, as the accumulation of carcinogenic insults over time significantly increases their risk of developing TBL and liver cancers [38–40]. Moreover, rapid industrialization and urbanization have led to persistently high levels of ambient air pollution. Exposure to pollutants such as fine particulate matter (PM2.5) from vehicle emissions, industrial discharges, and indoor pollutants from the use of solid fuels has markedly increased TBL cancer risk among AYAs [38, 39]. Despite these challenges, mortality rates for TBL, CRC, and liver cancers among Chinese AYAs have declined over the past decade. For TBL cancer, enhanced public health interventions have had a measurable impact on reducing TBL cancer mortality. Tobacco control policies, increased taxation on tobacco products, and public education initiatives, all these measures have contributed to a reduction in smoking prevalence among younger generations [38, 41]. Another factor contributing to reduced mortality is the evolution of treatment modalities. Over the past few decades, significant improvements has been made in both conventional and targeted therapies for TBL cancer. Advances in surgical techniques, radiotherapy, and especially the development and application of targeted therapies and immunotherapies have all contributed to better survival outcomes [42]. For CRC and liver cancer, mortality reductions also reflect treatment progress (e.g., improvements in surgery technique for CRC) and prevention (e.g., HBV vaccination for liver cancer). However, the recent increase in TBL and CRC mortality underscores the need for renewed prevention strategies, particularly targeting younger populations who may face emerging risk factors. Priority interventions should include strengthening tobacco control policies and reducing air pollution to address TBL cancer, while maintaining HBV vaccination coverage and implementing targeted cancer screening programs among Chinese AYAs - particularly with tobacco or alcohol use, poor dietary habits (e.g., low fruit and vegetable intake, high processed meat consumption), and obesity-related metabolic risks. Notably, although one study used different age groups (15–49 years) and data sources, our findings consistently identified liver cancer as a major cause of mortality among Chinese young adults [32].

Several Limitations warrant consideration. First, we observed an unexpected surge in the incidence of non-melanoma skin cancer among Chinese AYAs around 2021 (ASIR 2020: 2.89 vs. ASIR 2021: 6.57), which may reflect abrupt changes in diagnostic coding or data collection methods. However, we cannot identify a clear or plausible explanation for this anomaly based on the data introduction and any other sources. Second, certain cancer sites in the GBD database are classified as including benign tumors (as detailed in the supplementary file), which could potentially lead to an overestimation of the disease burden for these cancer sites. Third, the GBD 2021 estimates rely on modeled data rather than direct registry observations, particularly for the AYA population in China, where registry coverage remains incomplete. The modeling framework makes smoothing assumptions that may obscure rapid epidemiological shifts unique to AYAs. Fourth, our ARIMA-based projections through 2040 are subject to inherent uncertainties. Model accuracy decreases with longer prediction horizons, as unpredictable demographic and risk factor changes may disrupt current trends. Finally, the GBD datasets lack subnational stratification (e.g., by SDI or rural-urban residence) for AYA cancer burden, which unfortunately prevents us from examining potential regional disparities. Therefore, there is a clear need to further investigate the burden and patterns of cancers among AYAs both in China and globally to better inform the health outcomes for AYAs.
