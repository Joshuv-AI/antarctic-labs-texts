The Swiss Cheese Effect: A Biomechanical Study of Fifth Metacarpal Structural Integrity After Multiple Kirschner Wire Passes
Authors: Ryan Bickley, Alayna Vaughan, Gabriel Yohe, Kenneth R Means, Pooyan Abbasi, Aviram M Giladi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePercutaneous pinning is common in hand surgery. There is no defined threshold for how many extra passes create a clinically vulnerable bone at risk for fracture. Our study examined whether extra Kirschner wire (K-wire) passes meaningfully weaken the fifth metacarpal.MethodsPaired cadaveric hands modeling a fifth carpometacarpal dislocation were used. Specimens were prepared with a control group of one extra pass and experimental groups consisting of the contralateral paired fifth metacarpals with either three parallel or eight crossing extra passes. The ability to withstand simulated grip cycles and load-to-failure was measured. Comparative statistics were performed between groups.ResultsTwelve pairs were tested. Six experimental limbs had eight crossing extra passes, and six had three parallel extra passes. Specimen age ranged from 35 to 88 (mean 68) years. Five of 12 pairs were nonosteopenic/osteoporotic based on the second metacarpal cortical percentage. All specimens that underwent cyclic opening and closing survived without fracture regardless of extra pass count. Load-to-failure testing showed no statistically or clinically significant differences between paired specimens with one versus three (97 vs 105 N) or paired specimens with one versus eight (212 vs 196 N) extra passes (all P > .05).ConclusionsWe performed a biomechanical study to identify how many extra K-wire passes in a fifth metacarpal can occur before the bone is weakened to the point of potential fracture. Load-to-failure was high with eight extra passes also withstanding 2000 cycles of simulated gripping. A construct with three extra passes similarly had high load-to-failure. Finally, both were not meaningfully different from their paired control group with one extra pass. These results suggest both situations are likely safe for early motion and light gripping.Clinical relevanceThis study shows evidence that multiple K-wire passes during the reduction of a carpometacarpal joint dislocation may not lead to increased hardware failure or future fracture.Key words: Carpometacarpal joint, Fifth metacarpal, Kirschner wire, Percutaneous pinning, Second metacarpal cortical percentage

The use of smooth Kirschner wires (K-wires) is ubiquitous within musculoskeletal surgery. Hand and wrist surgeons use K-wires for percutaneous pinning after open and closed reductions when appropriate. K-wire insertion, although useful for fixation, is not innocuous.

Complications following the use of K-wires include infection at the pin site, malunion, nonunion, and fractures through the pin track itself.1 Fractures through the pin track, although rare, can be a devastating consequence leading to prolonged recovery, functional setbacks, and the potential for additional surgery. Empty iatrogenic cortical holes can act as a stress riser leading to a fracture.2 Animal models of larger long bone fractures suggest that defects 20% and higher can cause a reduction in torsional strength.3

Previous research has found that K-wire tips reach an average of 190 °C after repetitive passes through cadaveric phalanges, which can in turn cause osteonecrosis.4,5 However, less is known about the clinical meaningfulness of how or to what extent the K-wire passes themselves weaken the integrity of the bone.

We do not currently understand the extent of damage caused to a bone with multiple passes of a K-wire during a closed pinning operation. Considering how frequently this technique is used, it is understandable why it has become an important part of surgical training. Although models intended to support the training of this skill are being developed,6 many surgeons hone their K-wire abilities in the operating room under mentorship and supervision. Attending supervision has been shown to decrease complication rates during K-wire fixation of fractures and dislocations in the hand and wrist.7 This is another reason why understanding how a bone tolerates inaccurate passes or adjustments to wire placement is important.

There is no defined threshold for how many extra passes of a smooth K-wire create a hand that is clinically vulnerable and at risk for fracture. However, fractures caused by K-wire tracks have been reported, although cases are limited.1 Notably, we are unsure of how to best avoid this complication while working to achieve excellent percutaneous fixation that may require several attempts at wire placement. Percutaneous K-wire fixation is commonly used in fractures and dislocations of the hand. Fifth carpometacarpal (CMC) dislocations can be stabilized via several different methods. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the biomechanical viability of multiple K-wire passes in a simulated closed pinning of a fifth CMC joint dislocation. We hypothesized that more K-wire passes would lead to a lower load-to-failure and grip tolerance.

Materials and Methods

Twelve fresh-frozen cadavers were used in this study providing a total of 24 paired hands. None of the specimens had previous trauma, surgeries, or deformities reported in their histories, and signs of the three were not visible upon examination or in the radiographs. Each specimen was thawed prior to utilization. A hand radiograph of each specimen was obtained to measure the second metacarpal cortical index (2MCI) as a marker for bone density, with a measurement of 41.5 or lower indicating osteopenia.8 The right hand was used as a control group, whereas the contralateral left hand was used in one of two experimental groups. A fifth CMC dislocation model was chosen as this is a relatively common injury with a reproducible anatomical and radiographic deformity that is frequently treated with K-wires alone. In the control group, a 1.143 mm (0.045 in) K-wire was drilled across the fifth metacarpal in an ulnar-to-radial direction at the level just proximal to the distal metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction. The metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction is a weak point in a metacarpal and is prone to load failure. Six experimental hands were subjected to three extra parallel passes of a K-wire across the metacarpal. For these passes, a 3-dimensional printed jig was used to maintain consistent placement, spacing, and alignment of the passes. Six other experimental hands were subjected to eight extra crossing passes of a K-wire across the metacarpal. Extra passes were shot in the same trajectory as the initial pass but with a proximal or distal starting point in the three-extra pass group, and in various trajectories around a central point in the eight extra pass group. This was performed to control for variables that may accompany altered trajectories for subsequent passes. All K-wire passes were bicortical and just proximal to the distal metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction. An example of how the passes were performed radiographically can be seen in Figure 1A–D.

Biomechanical testing was then performed on each hand. To simulate stress caused by repetitive gripping, the hand was placed on a jig to allow for the opening and closing of the hand 2000 times. Force was applied to both flexor and extensor tendons to simulate a full-grip range of motion. This force was variable, dependent upon the amount needed to ensure the fingertips came within 1 cm of the palm (Fig. 2). Following the cyclic loading of the first four pairs of hands (eight specimens), no failures were observed, and thus, the cyclic loading was not performed for the remaining specimens. Subsequently, the metacarpals were subjected to load-to-failure testing to recreate light gripping forces as would be experienced in a recovery protocol. Each metacarpal was removed from the hand, potted in resin, and subjected to a piston moving from dorsal to volar pushing at the center of the metacarpal head (Fig. 3). Failure was defined as a fracture of the metacarpal.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed to compare biomechanical outcomes between paired specimens. Two-tailed paired t tests were used to compare cantilever load-to-failure between the control group (1 extra K-wire pass) and each experimental group (three parallel passes and eight cruciate passes). Significance was defined as P < .05.

To assess whether bone quality was comparable between specimens, we measured the second metacarpal cortical index (2MCI) and confirmed that all paired hands were within 15% of each other. Additionally, equivalence testing was performed on bone mineral density (BMD)/2MCI values using the two one-sided test procedure. Equivalence margins were incrementally tested from 1% to 50% of the mean BMD/2MCI. The smallest margin at which statistical equivalence was achieved (P < .05) was identified. All statistical calculations and plots were generated using Python (v3.11) with standard scientific libraries.

Results

Twenty-four total cadaveric hands were used in this study with a mean cadaver age of 68 (35–88) years old. 2MCI was measured for each specimen with an average of 55; all paired hands had a 2MCI well within 15% of each other.

Grip tolerance

Each specimen was subjected to repetitive grip simulation. The number of grip cycles for each specimen was recorded, up to 2000 cycles. All specimens included in grip testing survived 2000 grip cycles regardless of the number of passes.

Cantilever load-to-failure

The cantilever load-to-failure was measured for each specimen in Newtons (N). Mean load-to-failure in the first group was 122N for one extra pass and 132N for three extra passes. Mean load-to-failure in the second group was 206N for one extra pass and 194 for eight extra passes. There was no statistically significant difference between one and three extra passes (P = .74) and one versus eight extra passes (P = .63) (Tables 1 and 2 and Fig. 4). Interquartile ranges and medians were reported because of the small sample size and presence of a nonnormal distribution in several variables.

Post hoc analysis

Post hoc power calculations were performed to contextualize the observed findings. For load-to-failure testing, effect sizes between one and three extra passes (Cohen’s d = –0.18) and between one and eight extra passes (Cohen’s d = –0.22) were both small, requiring estimated sample sizes of ∼248 and ∼159 pairs, respectively, to achieve 80% power. Given our actual sample size of 12 pairs, the study was not powered to detect such small differences, supporting the conclusion that any observed differences are unlikely to be clinically meaningful.

For the 2MCI equivalence analysis, statistical equivalence was demonstrated when margins reached approximately 9% to 10% of the mean value, where both 1-sided tests were significant (P < .05). At this threshold, only 6–7 pairs would be required to reach 80% power, confirming that our observed equivalence was adequately powered.

Discussion

In cases where reduction is difficult or an operation is performed at a teaching hospital, several passes with a K-wire through the bone may occur. Our study aimed to assess bone integrity after several K-wire passes in a simulated CMC joint dislocation. Extra passes were made in an ulnar-to-radial direction simulating a fifth–fourth pinning. This was chosen to recreate a clinical scenario in which K-wire fixation is necessary for the stability of a CMC joint dislocation. We found that in terms of simulated light grip testing, regardless of the number of K-wire passes, early gentle range of motion does not create a fracture at the sites where K-wires were drilled.

Multiple passes of a K-wire are often needed for optimal positioning for reduction for use in a pinning technique. There is currently a lack of research addressing repeated passes in small hand bones, but biomechanical work has demonstrated changes to pull-out strength. K-wire pull-out force has been evaluated after multiple redirection attempts in hand-bone fixation showing that after only one redirection, the pull-out force significantly decreased (P < .05).9 One particular case report highlighted an iatrogenic fracture from repeated K-wire passes in a CMC joint fracture dislocation, calling attention to the importance of minimizing fixation attempts.10

Average dominant hand grip strength based on previous dynamometer testing for men is quoted at 53 kg (520 N) and 34 kg (333 N) for women.11,12 Our cantilever load-to-failure testing was meant to simulate light grip forces. Although the forces reached prior to fracture did not meet that of peak grip strength measurements found in the literature, the cadaver withheld other substantial forces seen in light motion similar to what a patient experiences after surgery during hand therapy.

There were several limitations to this study. First, these cadaver models were previously frozen specimens, which may have changed the integrity of the bones. Second, the number of cadavers was limited by availability, which effectively altered our age and sex distribution. Group allocation was solely based on thawed specimens readily available for testing, a fact that may have skewed our results because of only male specimens being used in the one versus three extra passes and an equal distribution of female and male specimens in the one versus eight extra pass specimens. Because of the nature of a cadaver study, we could not assess any detrimental effects of multiple K-wire passes in a clinical setting in terms of heat generated from additional K-wire passes or the time it would take for the errant hole in the bone to fill in during a patient’s healing. Our K-wire passes were performed in dense, more tolerant cortical bone, and not in areas known to be more vulnerable such as the base of the metaphysis, which could have skewed results. Selection of the fifth CMC dislocation model and passing pins at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction were both based on clinical frameworks but came without strong data to indicate that these findings would be consistent with other injury models or even with K-wire passes at other locations in the fifth metacarpal/fifth CMC. Our findings may not be transferable to these other scenarios.

Additionally, according to our BMD analysis, none of our specimens had osteoporosis or osteopenia; therefore, this is not a generalizable data set for many older patients. However, we do believe this aspect of our study would be generalizable to younger patients, who tend to more frequently sustain the types of injuries requiring pinning. Future studies should aim to evaluate the number of errant passes with a K-wire during fixation attempts in the operating room with patient-reported outcomes.

Although the study was underpowered to detect small differences in load-to-failure between groups, the observed effect sizes were minimal and far below what would be considered clinically meaningful. Conversely, the 2MCI analysis demonstrated that our specimens were well-matched for bone quality, with sufficient power to conclude equivalence at clinically relevant margins. Together, these analyses support that the lack of statistical differences in load-to-failure is consistent with a true absence of clinically relevant weakening from additional K-wire passes, rather than a limitation of the sample size.

Because of the small surface area of the metacarpal and carpals, it is logical to assume that violating the cortex with multiple K-wire passes would lead to construct failure and possible fracture. Our study highlights the notion that multiple passes with a K-wire may not be as detrimental as previously thought. Some studies have swayed practitioners against several pinning attempts. This information may ease the mind of an attending surgeon allowing learners to attempt pin placement several times.
