Exploring ‘MoyaMoya’: a multidisciplinary case study workshop on emotional conflicts in clinical practice
Authors: Seiichiro Nagai, Tatsuki Ikejiri, Mitsuru Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: This study describes a multidisciplinary, multi-generational case conference focusing on healthcare professionals’ emotional conflicts and moral distress—what we call “moyamoya” in Japanese—in clinical practice.Materials and Methods: Five hybrid conferences were co-organized by Kyoto-Min-Iren and an independent study group. In each session, one anonymized case was presented. Discussions emphasized sharing emotional conflicts without criticism or seeking conclusions and encouraged dialogue from multiple professional perspectives. Participants included physicians, nurses, medical social workers, administrative staff, and medical students.Results: The conferences appeared to generate diverse discussions and serendipitous insights. The dialogue may have promoted reflection similar to Significant Event Analysis and may foster mutual understanding by making interprofessional perspectives visible. It also appeared to offer opportunities to reconsider the problem-solving orientation common in daily practice and to share an approach attentive to patients’ life backgrounds. For young healthcare professionals and students, it may provide practical learning about the social determinants of health through socially vulnerable cases. However, the absence of thematic limits may pose challenges in facilitating discussions and aligning participants’ interests.Conclusion: A case conference centered on emotional conflicts may foster reflective practice, interprofessional understanding, and learning beyond technical knowledge, while requiring structural refinement to enhance practical value.Keywords: moral distress, multidisciplinary case conference, social determinants of health

To the Editor

Multidisciplinary case conferences in daily clinical practice are often conducted in many medical institutions with the aim of solving clinical problems and providing training. However, many clinical cases are accompanied by deep emotional conflicts and moral distress—what we call “moyamoya” in Japanese—among healthcare professionals1, 2), which may be insufficiently addressed within such problem-solving-oriented frameworks. In the United States and the United Kingdom, initiatives such as Schwartz Rounds provide forums in which healthcare professionals share the emotional impact of patient care3). In contrast, reports of similar practices appear to be limited in Japan. In community and rural settings, where medical decisions are often shaped by patients’ social vulnerability, family circumstances, and resource constraints4), emotional conflicts arising from these complex contexts may be particularly salient, whereas opportunities for dialogue addressing such experiences appear to be limited. Therefore, we organized a multidisciplinary, multi-generational case conference focusing on this “moyamoya”, and this article reports on its activities.

The conferences were co-organized by Kyoto-Min-Iren and an independent study group, “Laboratory for Human Nature, Cultures and Medicine”, and participants were recruited through these networks. The conferences were held in a hybrid format combining face-to-face meetings and online participation (Zoom®, Zoom Video Communications, San Jose, CA, USA). In each session, one emotionally challenging case was presented, and patient information was anonymized, with fictionalization added as necessary to ensure protection of personal information. In the first half, the presenter introduced the case, sharing the “moyamoya” while paying attention to the patient’s life background and values. In the second half, not only medical issues but also questions regarding the case as a whole and its contextual factors were raised, and possible approaches and ideas were freely exchanged from the perspectives of different professions. The guiding principle of the discussion was to avoid criticism and not seek conclusions, with emphasis placed on deliberately leaving the “moyamoya” unresolved to be taken home. As shown below, five conferences have been conducted to date, with approximately ten participants in each session, including physicians (ranging from senior doctors with more than 30 years of postgraduate experience to junior residents), nurses, administrative staff, medical social workers (MSWs), and medical students (Table 1).

Although exploratory in nature, this initiative may have several potential implications. Conceptually, it appears to produce reflective effects similar to Significant Event Analysis conducted in the field of family medicine5), as both approaches encourage healthcare professionals to articulate emotions evoked by clinical encounters and reflect on the gap between those emotions and their cognitive interpretations of the situation. Such reflective dialogue may foster a sense of coherence6), enhance coping skills and psychological resilience, and potentially contribute to burnout prevention. Moreover, “moyamoya” often arises from differences in perspectives among multiple professions7), and dialogue in this context may promote mutual understanding and facilitate learning in communication. Being acknowledged for struggling with unanswerable questions may help alleviate feelings of regret and may generate renewed energy for subsequent clinical practice.

In addition, potential implications may differ according to professional roles and positions. For welfare professionals, this initiative may offer an opportunity to reconsider the problem-solving orientation that often dominates daily practice and to share an orientation toward “accompanying support” that seeks to remain close to patients’ life backgrounds. For young healthcare professionals and students, it may provide practical learning about the social determinants of health8) through cases frequently rooted in social vulnerability, extending beyond textbook knowledge. Furthermore, by initiating discussions from human emotional conflicts rather than medical knowledge, the conference may create an environment in which participants can express their views regardless of their level of clinical experience.

Because the conference allowed free choice of themes and involved participants with diverse backgrounds, it appeared to generate a wide variety of discussions and the possibility of serendipitous insights. On the other hand, not limiting the themes also posed challenges, such as difficulty in guiding discussions and aligning the interests of all participants. In the future, while building on the strengths of multidisciplinary and multi-generational participation, we aim to enhance its practical value by introducing more structured approaches.
