The association between heat waves and pregnancy outcome among women undergoing assisted reproductive technology in Sichuan, China
Authors: Tianyi Ma, Yuanyuan Hu, Die Tang, Yiting Lin, Hanyi Liu, Ying Long, Sophia Zuoqiu, Tian Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the increasing frequency and intensity of extreme weather events due to climate change, heat waves have emerged as a significant public health threat. To date, the potential effect of extreme heat wave events, particularly when combined with air pollution, remains poorly understood for pregnancy outcomes among women undergoing assisted reproductive technology (ART).MethodsA retrospective study included 15,198 women receiving ART and 7519 fresh embryo transfer cycles between 2020 to 2022 at the Reproductive Center of West China Second University Hospital in Chengdu, China. Heat wave, a climate change indicator for extreme temperature events, was calculated based on daily temperature during the period of 85 days prior to oocyte retrieval. All environmental exposure variables, including weather and pollution, were matched geospatially to day 0 to day 85 before oocyte retrieval. Generalized linear model (GLMM) were used to assess the association between environmental exposures and ART outcomes, with secondary analysis using interaction terms between heat waves and individual pollutants.ResultsExposure to one heat wave event was positively correlated with the likelihood of becoming pregnant (+ 34.9% in univariate model and + 34.5% in multivariate model for heat wave events + 1 time) and this association was more pronounced in women under 35 years of age (+ 53.7% heat wave events + 1 time), while no statistical correlation was observed between exposure to two heat wave events and ART outcomes. Additionally, CO exhibited a significant negative association with biochemical pregnancy for women under 35 years old (-66.2% for CO + 1 mg/m3), and SO2 exhibited a significant negative association on biochemical pregnancy rate for women older than 35 years old (-6.5% for SO2 + 1 μg/m3). Results from the interaction model indicated that concurrent exposure to O3 and two heat wave events was statistical associated with clinical pregnancy (OR = 3.77).ConclusionsFindings from this study suggest that heat waves could be an important climatic indicator that reflects the impact of extreme weather on pregnancy outcomes among women receiving ART treatment. The synergy between exposure to extreme temperatures and air pollution could be further analyzed to provide deeper insight into the environmental impact on reproductive health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28343-8.

Background

Over the past several decades, the global prevalence of infertility has shown an upward trend, currently affecting 10% to 25% of reproductive age couples [8, 31, 56]. The growing infertile population has driven an increasing demand for assisted reproductive technology (ART). The high cost and potential health risks associated with ART have imposed considerable economic and psychological burdens on infertile couples [31, 56]. Therefore, understanding and improving these treatment outcomes is crucial for alleviating the burden on patients and optimizing the allocation of healthcare resources.

Follicular development is a complex, multi-stage process in humans, with preantral follicles requiring approximately 85 days to mature into preovulatory follicles. Systematic reviews have indicated that external exposure within the 85-day period prior to oocyte retrieval is associated with a negative impact on pregnancy and live birth, particularly external exposure to ambient air pollution, which may impair the success rate of ART by affecting oocyte development and quality [28, 32]. Ambient air pollution is widely recognized as a major global public health concern and has been repeatedly linked to various adverse health outcomes, including reproductive health outcomes [39, 50]. Studies have confirmed that air pollutants exert a significant impact on the conception rate during ART cycles, potentially leading to infertility, miscarriage, stillbirth, and birth defects [9, 41, 54].

In addition to ambient air pollution, there has been growing interest in the effects of climate change on reproductive health in recent years. Prior research has provided scientific evidence for the climate’s effect on public health, including the induction of cardiovascular diseases, respiratory diseases, and other conditions [19, 24, 27, 55]. However, research on the effects of ambient temperature, an indicator of climate, on ART pregnancy outcomes remains limited and contradictory. For example, past studies in the United States and China have shown that frozen embryo transfer cycles performed in summer yield higher clinical pregnancy rates, while research in Brazil has found that fertilization rates are higher in spring [4, 7, 61]. A cohort study conducted in Shanghai, China, suggested that higher temperatures during cold weather were significantly associated with increased, while another study from Hubei, China, suggested that high temperatures after embryo transfer were linked to reduced clinical pregnancy rates [6, 15]. Nevertheless, all these studies focused exclusively on the analysis of meteorological factors without further investigation of the co-exposure of ambient temperature and air pollutants.

Heat waves are typically defined as prolonged episodes of abnormally high temperatures, and this abnormal increase in atmospheric-related thermal stress can adversely affect human health, particularly among vulnerable groups such as pregnant women [3, 34]. As a comprehensive indicator that emphasizes temperature persistence and abnormality, heat waves can more effectively reflect the cumulative effect of heat load and the body’s additional adaptation than the daily average temperature [30]. In previous studies, heat waves have been more commonly used as a typical extreme weather event in the field of disaster risk assessment, including heat-related deaths, acute changes in cardiovascular and respiratory disease risk, and health impact assessment in the context of combined extreme events [16, 21, 46, 51]. However, heat waves are currently rarely incorporated into existing research on reproductive health, which still mainly focuses on general exposure to ambient temperature. Therefore, in this study, heat waves are used as an indicator of extreme heat exposure to assess their association with ART outcomes and further explore potential interaction effects with air pollution exposure.

Biological plausibility supports a potential relationship between heat exposure and ART outcomes [7, 12, 15]. Follicular development and oocyte maturation are sensitive biological processes, and experimental and epidemiological evidence suggests that thermal stress may affect ovarian function, folliculogenesis, granulosa cell activity and oocyte developmental competence [20, 22]. Potential mechanisms include oxidative stress, altered antioxidant capacity, impaired steroidogenesis, and cellular stress response pathways [20]. In addition, short term thermal stress may activate adaptive responses, including heat shock protein expression pathways, suggesting that the reproductive effects of heat exposure may depend on exposure intensity, duration, and timing rather than following a simple linear pattern [23, 29]. Evaluating heat wave frequency, lagged effects, and cumulative heat wave days during the pre-retrieval window is biologically plausible and may help capture different dimensions of heat exposure burden.

This retrospective cohort study aims to investigate the relationship between heat waves, an established climate change indicator, and ART pregnancy outcomes, while also assessing the combined effects of heat waves and air pollutants on ART pregnancy outcomes. This study also explores potential differences in susceptibility to such combined exposure across age groups. The novelty of this study is twofold: 1) We evaluated heat wave events as sustained extreme temperature exposures instead of focusing only on the average ambient temperature. 2) We incorporated co-exposures into the multivariable framework to explore the association between heat waves and ART outcomes. To our knowledge, this is the first study that utilizes climatic indicators, in combination with ambient air pollution, in assessing ART pregnancy outcomes. By integrating these aspects, the present study seeks to guide future research priorities and support the development of effective preventive measures to protect the reproductive health of current and future generations.

Method

Study population

This was a retrospective cohort study. The subjects of this study were women who received transfers of their fresh embryos at the Reproductive Center of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University, from 2020 to 2022. Only patients living in Sichuan were included and they were assumed not to have relocated during the treatment period. Participants were excluded from our study if they had (1) an incomplete medical history, (2) no qualified embryos available after oocyte retrieval, or (3) undergone preimplantation genetic testing. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committees of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University (KS402).

ART procedures

Briefly, the ART process is generally divided into four steps: the beginning of controlled ovarian stimulation (COS), oocyte retrieval, embryo transfer, and pregnancy test. The center followed a standard operating procedure (SOP) to administer COS treatment to all participants. COS treatment included long gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist (-a) protocols, GnRH antagonist (-ant) regimens, and other regimens, including short gonadotropin agonist protocols, mini-stimulation protocols, or progesterone-prompted ovarian stimulation (PPOS) protocols, which were decided by the physician based on the patient’s ovarian reserve. The dose of gonadotropins was adjusted accordingly based on age, BMI and ovarian response. After two or more follicles reached a diameter of ≥ 18 mm, hCG was injected subcutaneously on the trigger day with or without Triptorelin depending on ovarian response. The oocytes were retrieved after 34–36 h. The selection of subsequent fertilization techniques was contingent upon an evaluation of sperm quality and clinical factors, encompassing the utilization of IVF or ICSI. The fresh cycle protocol involves selecting top-quality embryos for transfer 3 days after oocytes have been successfully retrieved and luteal support has begun on the day of oocyte retrieval.

The primary outcomes were biochemical pregnancy and clinical pregnancy. Biochemical pregnancy is defined as when serum β-HCG is greater than 30 mIU/ml at 14 days after embryo transfer. Clinical pregnancy is defined as the identification of an intrauterine gestational sac by ultrasound after five weeks of embryo transfer.

Environmental pollution and meteorology data

Environmental pollution and meteorological data with 0.01° resolution were matched with participants addresses and based on the date of oocyte retrieval [48, 57–59]. Detailed approaches were explained in earlier publications. In brief, hourly raw concentrations of PM2.5 (μg/m3), PM10 (μg/m3), O3 (μg/m3), NO2 (μg/m3), SO2 (μg/m3), and CO (mg/m3) were obtained from China National Environmental Monitoring Centre (CNEMC, http://www.cnemc.cn/). Hourly raw meteorological data with a spatial resolution of 0.25° were obtained from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA, https://nomads.ncep.noaa.gov/). We resampled the temperature and relative humidity into 0.01° grid cells by using bilinear interpolation. Additionally, we performed additional estimation of PM2.5 and PM10 based on aerosol optical depth (AOD) data from the Himawari-8 and the Multi-Angle Implementation of Atmospheric Correction (MAIAC) [10, 45]. We conducted additional estimation of gaseous pollutants (NO2, SO2, O3, and CO) based on tropospheric NO2 columns and the total SO2 (and O3, CO) columns from the Sentinel-5P TROPOspheric Monitoring Instrument (TROPOMI) [10, 44, 45, 47]. Subsequently, six separate machine learning models were used to estimate the hourly gridded concentration for each of the six criteria air pollutants. Then 24-h average concentrations were calculated for PM2.5 (μg/m3), PM10 (μg/m3), NO2 (μg/m3), SO2 (μg/m3) and CO (mg/m3) and 8-h average was calculated for O3 (μg/m3) [47, 48, 57–59].

Definition of heat wave and exposure window

We selected the follicular development stage during the follicular development window, which is the 86-day period starting from the day of oocyte retrieval to 85 days before oocyte retrieval, as the exposure window. This stage covers the key pre-retrieval period of follicular development and oocyte maturation, during which environmental exposures may be biologically relevant to oocyte quality and subsequent ART outcomes.

In this study, we used daily mean temperature to identify heat wave events. Heat wave was defined as ≥ 3 consecutive days with daily mean temperature above 35℃ during the 86-day window from the day of oocyte retrieval to 85 days before retrieval. If there were periods of non-heat wave days between two qualifying heat wave periods, which were counted as separate heat wave events.

For each ART cycle, we counted the number of heat wave events within this window and categorized exposure as no event, one event, and two events, with no event as the reference category in the regression models.

Statistical analysis

In this study, we used generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with a logit link to assess the association between environmental data and ART outcomes. To account for repeated cycles within the same woman, we included a subject-specific random intercept to account for multiple cycles per woman and unobserved between woman heterogeneity, which minimizes the effect of individual differences. Models were fit with glmmTMB (Template Model Builder, Laplace approximation) to provide efficient and stable likelihood-based estimation for mixed logistic regression.

We estimated the effects of exposures using two models. Single-exposure adjusted models (univariate models) included one environmental exposure at a time and the prespecified covariates, providing an overall association estimate that is less sensitive to collinearity among exposures. Mutually adjusted multi-exposure models (multivariate models) simultaneously included all environmental exposures to estimate the independent association of each exposure while controlling co-exposures.

Results

Summary of study population and ART outcomes

From 2020 to 2022, 19,075 patients underwent IVF/ICSI cycles at Reproductive Center of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University. Among them, 15,198 patients resided in Sichuan province, with 18,342 ART cycles, were enrolled in our study (Fig. 1), and 7519 cycles received fresh embryo transfer. Figure 1 presents the distribution of ART patients classified by age groups in Sichuan Province. Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of a total of 15,198 participants living in Sichuan Province. In general, the average age and the average BMI of the female participants in the study were 31.69 years old (SD = 4.58) and 21.93 (SD = 2.98). As people get older, the BMI tends to increase. The majority of the study population were of Han ethnicity (14,151, 93.13%), while among the ethnic minorities, Yi (3.15%), Tibetan (1.68%), and Qiang (0.46%) comprised a significant proportion. Almost all individuals were non-smokers (99.19%), smokers account for a larger proportion in the older age group (1.22%). Over half had a bachelor’s degree or higher (63.14%), indicating a high level of education. Half of the study population worked indoors (49.26%), with a similarly high proportion of individuals in the undefined groups (freelancer, unemployed) (45.31%). Outdoor workers account for a larger proportion in the older age group (12.74%), compared with the younger group (4.42% and 7.35%). The majority of research individuals were due to female factors (70.72%). As for the remaining exposures (relative humidity, PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, O3 and CO), the 95% CI intervals mostly spanned 1, indicating weak overall associations with ART outcomes. However, for CO in the group < 35 years of multivariate models, OR of biochemical pregnancy was less than 1, which presented a negative effect on biochemical pregnancy (−66.2% for CO + 1 mg/m3) (Table S1). Furthermore, biochemical pregnancy presented a negative association for SO2, OR was similarly less than 1, indicating a negative effect on biochemical pregnancy (−6.5% for SO2 + 1 μg/m3). Overall, except for CO and SO2 in certain pregnancy outcomes, for the whole research exposures under mutual adjustment for co-exposures, the odds ratio (OR) for all other environmental exposures were close to 1, indicating a weak overall association between average exposure levels and ART outcomes, with no consistent age gradient observed.

In this study, we defined a heat wave event as one record of a research individual’s exposure to a heat wave event. In both modeling strategies, experiencing one heat wave event was associated with increased odds of clinical pregnancy (+ 34.9% per event for the univariate model), and this association remained positive after mutual adjustment (+ 34.5% per event for the multivariate model, Table 5). This result indicated that the impact of one heat wave event on clinical pregnancy was not influenced by other exposures. This effect was more obvious in females under 35 years old in the multivariate model (+ 53.7% for heat wave events + 1 time), which has the same level of significance (Table S1). For two heat wave events, the estimated odds ratios (OR) for both ART outcomes were with 95% CI spanning 1, which was primarily reflected in the fact that no association was observed between two heat wave events and pregnancy outcomes. In a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for male age, the multivariate associations between environmental exposures, heat waves, and ART outcomes remained highly consistent with the main results. Specifically, there was still a positive association between one heat wave event and clinical pregnancy after male age adjustment, with OR = 1.347 (95%CI: 1.002—1.809, Table S2).

We further supplemented the distributed lag sensitivity analysis to study the impact of heat wave events on the ART pregnancy outcomes (Figure S1). The result showed that cumulative heat wave exposure in the short period before oocyte retrieval was positively associated with pregnancy outcomes, with stronger evidence for clinical pregnancy than for biochemical pregnancy. The cumulative association was most apparent within the lag 0–14 days window (OR = 1.88, 95%CI:1.03–3.43), whereas estimates for longer cumulative windows were imprecise due to the wide confidence intervals. The lag response curves suggested a short-term window of elevated odds for clinical pregnancy approximately 6–17 days before oocyte retrieval, while no lag association was observed for biochemical pregnancy. This finding indicated a modest elevation in odds during the first two weeks before oocyte retrieval.

To evaluate potential effect modification of heat waves by ambient co-exposures, we extended the main multivariate model by adding an interaction term between heat wave events (one heat wave event compared with no heat wave event, and two heat wave events compared with no heat wave event) and exposures (relative humidity, PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, CO, O3) at a time (Table S3). In this part, we used Wald tests for assessing the coefficients and likelihood ratio test (LRT) for comparing interaction models and the corresponding main model to evaluate the evidence for effect modification.

Across environmental exposures, the LRT revealed no improvement in model fit when adding interaction terms between heat waves and environmental exposures (p for LRT > 0.05, Table S3), and the Wald tests similarly showed no significant interaction coefficients among most environmental exposures. These results indicated that the association between heat waves and ART outcome is independent of most environmental exposures. However, Wald test results showed a significant interaction signal between O3 and two heat wave events in relation to clinical pregnancy, with an interaction OR of 3.77. This indicates that, for each 1 IQR increase in O3 concentration, the odds of clinical pregnancy outcomes associated with two heat wave events were 3.77 times higher compared to the reference group with no heat wave event.

Additional sensitivity analyses using alternative exposure metrics and definitions. 1) We evaluated cumulative hot days as an alternative exposure metric to capture the duration and cumulative effect of extreme high temperature (Figure S2 and Table S4). Cumulative hot days were defined as the total number of days with daily mean temperature above 35℃ during the 0–85 days before oocyte retrieval. The results suggested that each additional hot day was associated with higher odds of clinical pregnancy (+ 2.7% for each additional hot day). 2) Categorized the 0–85 days mean temperature before oocyte retrieval into quintiles and using the lowest quintile as the reference. Compared with the lowest quintile, the highest quintile was associated with higher odds of both biochemical and clinical pregnancy (separately OR = 1.408 and OR = 1.383), while intermediate quintiles showed positive but no significant associations (Table S5). 3) Conducted the sensitivity analysis using alternative heat wave definitions based on the 95th and 99th percentiles, results showed broadly similar but no significant associations (Table S6).

Discussion

Main findings

To our knowledge, this study was the first investigation to examine the impact of exposure to heat waves and ambient air before oocyte retrieval on females receiving ART treatment. Exposure to one heat wave event was positively correlated with clinical pregnancy, and this association was more pronounced in females younger than 35 years. At the same time, exposure to two heat wave events was non-significantly associated with clinical pregnancy.

Effects of heat waves on pregnancy outcomes

Our study results suggest that exposure to extreme climate events, as indicated by heat waves, is associated with the probability of ART pregnancy. Heat waves represent extreme temperature conditions and are more informative than averaging temperature over a period of time, an exposure variable used by many previous studies. Previous studies demonstrated the influence of ambient temperature on ART outcomes exhibited variations across different stages, temperature regimes, and transfer modalities [4, 6, 15, 18, 61]. Recently, studies in Shanghai and Hubei, China, have provided support for the relationship between temperature anomalies and pregnancy outcomes by showing that higher temperatures in cold weather were associated with elevated clinical pregnancy and live birth rates, whereas elevated temperatures in hot weather showed opposite effects [6, 15, 18]. Further subgroup analysis revealed that women aged 35 years are more susceptible to this effect, suggesting a possible age-related biological response to thermal stimulation [6]. In the current study, the exposure period covered up to 85 days prior to oocyte retrieval. Clinically, the follicular development stage is a critical window highly sensitive to temperature fluctuations, and the impact of temperature on folliculogenesis presents contradictory patterns across studies. During this period, higher ambient temperatures were shown to reduce antral follicle count (AFC) and the number of retrieved oocytes in oocyte donation recipients and autologous oocyte patients, indicating potential adverse effects on ovarian reserve [14, 25, 43]. Yet, higher temperatures during ovarian stimulation were associated with increased mature oocyte count, fertilization rate, and post-thaw oocyte survival rate, suggesting that moderate heat exposure may positively regulate oocyte maturation and select heat-resistant oocytes with high developmental potential [6, 7, 26, 35]. Our study results confirm that the period prior to oocyte retrieval is a sensitive window to meteorological conditions.

The association between heat wave exposure and ART pregnancy outcome also differed between exposure to one heat wave event and exposure to two heat wave events prior to oocyte retrieval. Optimal temperature may be beneficial to follicular development, while extreme temperatures may affect the ovarian microenvironment by causing vitamin D deficiency and hormone disorders, influencing vitamin D levels, estrous cycle, vasoconstriction and altering ovarian blood supply, thereby affecting the oocyte meiosis process, hormone regulation, and ART outcomes [11, 17, 43, 49, 60]. Yet, our results suggest that exposure to excessive heat, as represented by two heat wave events, prior to oocyte retrieval would hinder the success of ART pregnancy outcome, suggesting that there could be a possible optimal exposure to heat.

Co-effects of heat wave and air pollution on pregnancy outcomes

We found a significant positive interactive effect between O3 and two heat wave events on clinical pregnancy. Heat waves and O3 share common underlying meteorological drivers or conditions, including abundant solar insolation, high air temperatures, low wind speeds, and precipitation, resulting in a synergistic enhancing effect on the mortality rate of cardiovascular-related accidents [38, 52, 53]. However, the co-effects of O3 and temperature exposure on pregnancy are limited and may not be additive. A study in Australia on preterm birth and low birthweight reported that the effect of exposure to O3 during the entire pregnancy was enhanced at low temperatures [5]. However, a case-crossover study in California found that the effect of O3 exposure on fetal death was attenuated from significant to non-significant after adjustment for apparent temperature [36]. Another study in the United States suggested that the day of pregnancy loss occurrence and the preceding 3 days of O3 exposure and high temperature during the warm season increased the risk for pregnancy loss, but temperature could modify the relationship between O3 and pregnancy loss [42]. The effects of O3 and temperature exposures on pregnancy may share biological pathways. They could induce oxidative stress, systemic inflammation and change endometrial blood flow [36, 37, 40]. Therefore, the joint effect of co-exposure could reach a saturation point, resulting in a synergistic enhancement or antagonism. Besides, O3 has been shown to be effective in treating endometritis and decreasing blood pressure and preventing the progression of hypertensive disease, thus reducing both inflammation and uterine infection and improving endometrial receptivity [1, 2].

Strengths and limitations

The current study has several strengths. First, this study employs a relatively large sample size compared to previous studies. A larger sample size could provide better statistical power in assessing associations and making inferences. Second, this study utilized a new exposure indicator that builds on previous understanding of temperature’s relationship with pregnancy outcomes. This new indicator is more relevant in the context of climate change and could provide better evidence for making public health inferences.

However, this study is limited by the following: 1) data from a single Chinese province restricts generalizability to regions with distinct climates, such as warmer areas may have higher heat tolerance, potentially underestimating effects; 2) exposure assessment based on residential addresses ignored nonresidential activities, which could have resulted in attenuation in the relationship between exposure and health outcome; 3) the exposure period was 85 days prior to oocyte retrieval, which could not provide distinction between shorter time frames for analysis within that time period. 4) Although we additionally adjusted for male age in sensitivity analyses, other detailed paternal factors, such as semen quality parameters and male factor infertility diagnosis, were not consistently available in the current datasets. Residual confounding from unmeasured paternal factors therefore cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusions

This study examines the relationship between heat exposure prior to oocyte retrieval and pregnancy outcome among women receiving ART treatment in southwest China. The results suggest that mild heat exposure (as represented by one heat event) is positively associated with clinical pregnancy. Additionally, concurrent exposure to heat and air pollution also exhibited significant association with pregnancy outcome, though the direction of association differed depending on the type of pollutants (i.e. O3, SO2, and CO). Findings from this study could provide valuable insight for understanding the potential reproductive health risks among women seeking ART in light of rapid global climate change.
