Hemoglobin nanocatalyst with tunable autoxidation activity for tumor apoptosis-ferroptosis combination therapy
Authors: Jing Li, Hongbo Wu, Hanyue Li, Yue Zhang, Hanjie Zhang, Ling Wang, Weilang Zhang, Tingxuan Li, Weiwei Zeng, Daquan Chen, Lin Mei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The clinical translation of catalytic therapy has been hindered in recent decades due to immunological risks, delayed degradation, and poorly understood metabolism associated with catalysts, despite promising results in antitumor therapy. Developing catalysts with nonmetallic active centers may provide an alternative to those with metallic active centers. In this study, a natural protein nanocatalyst, Hb@PtPP, is developed through the moderate polymerization of pyrrole and hemoglobin for tumor catalytic therapy. In comparison with BSA@PtPP prepared from bovine serum albumin, Hb@PtPP exhibits distinctive autoxidation activity in tumor cells in response to elevated hydrogen peroxide levels while maintaining relatively low activity in normal cells. This feature enables Hb@PtPP to effectively combat tumors by initiating a cascade of catalytic reactions, including the production of reactive oxygen species, the release of heme and iron ions, and the accumulation of lipid peroxidation, all of which contribute to apoptosis and ferroptosis in tumor cells. RNA-sequencing results further demonstrate that tumor cells undergo ferroptosis. Additionally, these catalytic processes are augmented by the photothermal effect, resulting in efficient tumor killing and growth inhibition both in vitro and in vivo. This study underscores the crucial role of hemoglobin in advancing catalytic therapy for tumors.

Introduction

Catalytic medicines that leverage external physical energy or internal energy conversion to selectively catalyze natural substances in the diseased microenvironment into pharmacologically active forms like reactive oxygen species (ROS), are gaining popularity in antitumor therapy1, 2, 3. Among these, nanocatalysts with Fe active centers have been extensively studied for their ability to produce lethal hydroxyl radicals (·OH) from hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which is prevalent in malignant tumors4,5. The accumulation of ·OH and Fe ions subsequently causes polyunsaturated fatty acid peroxidation and Fe-dependent lipid peroxidation (LPO), leading to apoptosis and ferroptosis in tumor cells6, 7, 8. Moreover, numerous studies have shown that elevated local temperature positively promotes these catalytic activities, resulting in more effective tumor eradication and regression9, 10, 11, 12. However, most nanocatalysts with Fe active centers have so far focused on 2D nanomaterials, metallic nanomaterials, or single-atom enzymes, which exhibit heterogeneity in vivo and face challenges such as high immunogenicity, delayed degradation, and unidentified metabolism after injection13, 14, 15. Meanwhile, the uncontrolled separation and release of Fe ions from nanocatalysts in circulation represent a significant safety hazard that needs to be urgently addressed, despite its importance in tumors.

Natural proteins, known for their good biocompatibility, biodegradability, and low immunogenicity, have been widely explored as building blocks for nanomedicines, including the commercially available nab-paclitaxel16, 17, 18. The combination of hydrophobic drugs with proteins prolongs their in vivo half-life and increases tumor enrichment19. Regrettably, most proteins exhibit restricted pharmacological efficacy against tumors and fail to recognize tumor cells or the tumor microenvironment. Hemoglobin (Hb), composed of globin chains and heme prosthetic groups containing Fe ions, is responsible for oxygen and carbon dioxide transport throughout the body20, 21, 22. Encouragingly, Hb-based oxygen carriers have shown promise as blood substitute in preclinical trials, nevertheless, they are also plagued by concomitant oxidative stress damage resulting from Hb autoxidation23. When Hb is exposed to high concentrations of peroxides, it undergoes drastic oxidation to ferrylHb (Hb(Fe4+) = O) via a two-electron process, accompanied by the production of ROS, such as superoxide ions (·O2−) and ·OH, as well as the release of heme and Fe ions24, 25, 26, 27. Despite the critical role of Hb in ROS generation and Fe ions import, its autoxidation activity has been largely overlooked in tumor catalytic therapy, with Hb being used primarily as an oxygen donor to alleviate tumor hypoxia.

Polypyrrole (PPy), a conjugated polymer consisting of a five-membered pyrrole ring with repeating units connected at the 2- and 5-positions, has found widespread use in the biomedical field, due to its simple preparation, good biocompatibility, and excellent photothermal properties28, 29, 30, 31. Previous studies have demonstrated that during polymerization, pyrrole units readily bond with protons, and the ensuing positive charges adsorb physiologically active molecules, including natural proteins, via electrostatic interactions32, 33, 34. So far, bovine serum albumin (BSA), ovalbumin, and phycocyanin have been successfully incorporated as stabilizers into PPy nanostructures, with the protein structures and active centers remaining intact35, 36, 37. Inspired by these findings, we strategically employed Hb as a novel protein stabilizer in the oxidative polymerization of pyrrole initiated by the hypervalent oxidant H2PtCl6 (Scheme 1). The PEGylated Hb-stabilized PPy (Hb@PtPP) retained its autoxidation activity, catalyzing the generation of ·O2− and ·OH from intratumoral H2O2 and inducing oxidative damage to tumor cells. This process was accompanied by the degradation of Hb and the co-release of Fe ions and heme, which further exacerbated oxidative stress and LPO due to Fe overload. Conversely, PEGylated BSA-stabilized PPy (BSA@PtPP), which lacked pharmacophore activity, exhibited negligible toxicity both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, when tumor tissues were exposed to an 808 nm laser, the pyrrole-conjugated repeating units efficiently converted light energy into thermal energy, accelerating the autoxidation cascade reactions and resulting in more severe tumor apoptosis-ferroptosis. Materials and methods

Materials

Bovine hemoglobin (Hb), pyrrole (99%), platinic chloride hexahydrate (H2PtCl6·6H2O), methylene blue (MB), and dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR123) were purchased from Aladdin. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) were purchased from Solarbio. 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[amino-(polyethylene glycol) 2000] (DSPE-PEG2000) was purchased from Xi'an Ruixi Biological Technology Co., Ltd. The mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit, lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit, and Calcein-AM/PI assay kit were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology. FerroOrange was purchased from Dojindo Laboratories.

Synthesis of Hb@PtP and BSA@PtP

A solution was prepared by dissolving 100 mg of either Hb or BSA in 5 mL of deionized (Di) water under stirring. Afterward, 5 mL of H2PtCl6·6H2O (7.7 mmol/L) was added to the mixture. After stirring for 20 min, 200 μL of pyrrole was added and stirred for 5 h. Finally, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation (5000 rpm, 5 min, H5-21 KR, Hunan Kecheng Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd.) and dialyzed for 48 h to extract Hb@PtP or BSA@PtP.

Synthesis of Hb@PtPP and BSA@PtPP

A solution was prepared by dissolving 6 mg of Hb@PtP or BSA@PtP in 10 mL of Di water under stirring. Then, 6 mg of DSPE-PEG2000 was added to the mixture and stirred for 2.5 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 15 min to collect precipitation, which was washed three times with Di water to obtain Hb@PtPP or BSA@PtPP.

In vitro ·O2− and ·OH generation

The generation of ·O2− and ·OH was detected using DHR123 probe and MB probe, respectively. DHR123 (0.33 μmol/L) or MB (15 μg/mL) was mixed with PBS solutions containing BSA@PtPP or Hb@PtPP in the presence or absence of 0.5 mmol/L H2O2. The fluorescence intensity and optical absorption of the mixtures were measured at various periods using a fluorescence spectrophotometer and a UV–vis spectrometer, respectively. For laser requirement groups, the mixture was exposed to an 808 nm laser. Furthermore, the trapping agent 5,5-dimethyl-1-oxopyrrolidine (DMPO) was employed, and electron spin resonance (ESR) measurements were performed to examine the generation of ·O2− and ·OH.

In vitro and in vivo photothermal properties

An 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2) was used to irradiate 100 μL of BSA@PtPP or Hb@PtPP solutions at different concentrations (0, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL) for 5 min. During the irradiation, a thermal imaging camera recorded the corresponding temperature changes in those solutions. After that, the photothermal stability of BSA@PtPP or Hb@PtPP solutions was tested by subjecting them to four cycles of 6 min of irradiation followed by 9 min of cooling. To assess their photothermal conversion efficiency, a heating-cooling experiment consisting of 10 min of irradiation followed by 15 min of cooling was performed.

To further evaluate photothermal properties in vivo, 1 × 107 4T1 cells were injected into the upper back of the right thigh of BALB/c mice to establish 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. When the tumor volume reached ∼180 mm3, Hb@PtPP (15 mg/kg) and PBS were administered intravenously to 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. 12 h after injection, the tumor regions of mice were exposed to an 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W/cm2) for 5 min. Thermal imaging cameras captured thermal pictures and monitored temperature variations in the tumors.

Cell cytotoxicity

3T3 and 4T1 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C under 5% CO2. The cells were then treated with fresh DMEM containing various concentrations of BSA@PtPP or Hb@PtPP (0, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL). After 24 h of co-incubation, cell viability was determined using the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8). Next, laser-mediated cytotoxicity in 4T1 cells was investigated. After a 4 h incubation with BSA@PtPP or Hb@PtPP, 4T1 cells were exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation at different densities (0.5, 1, and 1.5 W/cm2) for 5 min, followed by a 24 h incubation. Finally, cell viability was assessed using CCK-8.

To further assess cytotoxicity of Hb@PtPP, calcein acetoxymethyl ester (Calcein-AM) and propyl iodide (PI) dyes were used. In detail, 4T1 cells were planted in a 6-well plate with 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. Then, the cells were incubated with DMEM containing Hb@PtPP (150 μg/mL) or BSA@PtPP (150 μg/mL) for 4 h. For the laser-required groups, 4T1 cells were exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W/cm2) for 5 min. After 12 h of laser exposure, the cells were incubated with Calcein-AM (10 nmol/L) and PI (10 nmol/L) for 30 min. Finally, the cells were observed using inverted fluorescence microscopy (OLYMPUS CKX53, Japan). For cell apoptosis assays, 4T1 cells were treated as described above, with or without co-incubation with the ferroptosis inhibitor Liproxstatin-1 (50 nmol/L). The cells were examined using a flow cytometer (Celesta, BD, USA) following a 30 min incubation with Annexin V-FITC and PI.

Intracellular Fe2+, ROS, and LPO generation

FerroOrange, DCFH-DA, and C11-BODIPY581/591 were used to detect intracellular Fe2+, ROS, and LPO, respectively. 4T1 cells (1 × 105 cells per dish) were seeded in confocal culture dishes or 6-well plates. Following overnight inoculation, the cells were treated with Hb@PtPP (150 μg/mL) and BSA@PtPP (150 μg/mL), respectively. After 4 h, the cells in the laser group were irradiated with 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2) for 5 min and subsequently incubated for an additional 12 h. After washing, the cells were stained with FerroOrange (1 μmol/L), DCFH-DA (10 μmol/L), or C11-BODIPY581/591 (5 μmol/L) for 30 min. Finally, the cells were analyzed using flow cytometer and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, LSM710, Carl ZEISS, Germany). In addition, the intracellular malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured after the aforementioned treatments, according to the instructions of the lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit.

Animal model

Female BALB/c mice (4−6 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing SiPeiFu Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Committee for Experimental Animals Welfare and Ethics of Institute of Radiation Medicine, Chinese Academy of Medical Science & Peking Union Medical College (Tianjin, China). A subcutaneously transplanted cancer model was established by injecting 100 μL of 4T1 cell solution (1 × 107 cells/mL) into the upper back of the right thigh of mice.

In vivo fluorescence imaging and biological distribution

4T1 tumor-bearing mice were injected intravenously with free Cy5 and Cy5-labeled Hb@PtPP at an equivalent Cy5 dosage of 1.5 mg/kg, and the mice were imaged using the In-Vivo Imaging System at various time periods (3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 h). Meanwhile, tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected and imaged.

Antitumor therapy

When the tumor in 4T1-bearing mice reached ∼80 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 5): (a) Control, (b) BSA@PtPP, (c) Hb@PtPP, (d) BSA@PtPP + L, and (e) Hb@PtPP + L. The administered doses of BSA@PtPP and Hb@PtPP were both 20 mg/kg. For the laser required groups, an 808 nm laser with a power density of 1 W/cm2 was applied to tumor tissues for 5 min, 12 h after injection. The body weight and tumor volume of each mouse were measured every two days over a 20-day period, and tumor volume was calculated using Eq. (1):(1)Tumor volume = 1/2 × (Tumor width)2 × (Tumor length)

On Day 20, tumors were harvested for H&E staining, TUNEL, Ki67, and DCFH-DA analysis, while major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected for H&E staining.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t-test. For multiple-group analysis, one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was applied. Statistical significance was defined as follows: ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗P < 0.05.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of BSA@PtPP and Hb@PtPP

BSA-stabilized PPy (BSA@PtP) and Hb-stabilized PPy (Hb@PtP) were synthesized through the polymerization and crosslinking of pyrrole and proteins (BSA or Hb) utilizing H2PtCl6 as a catalyst. This synthesis procedure was performed under gentle conditions, avoiding harsh chemicals and thus minimizing potential negative effects on the biological activities of proteins. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that both PEG-modified BSA@PtP (BSA@PtPP) and Hb@PtP (Hb@PtPP) had nearly spherical morphology (Fig. 1A and Supporting Information Fig. S1). The dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated that the hydrodynamic diameter of both preparations was approximately 95 nm (Supporting Information Fig. S2). The zeta potential of Hb@PtPP significantly decreased from 10.87 ± 0.11 to −19.3 ± 1.60 mV, whereas the zeta potential of BSA@PtPP only slightly decreased from −14.77 ± 1.86 to −19.4 ± 1.15 mV (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, the structures and components of these samples were analyzed using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), UV‒Visible absorption spectra, and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Fig. 1C illustrates that Hb@PtP had a characteristic protein fingerprint pattern akin to that of free Hb, confirming the successful hybridization of Hb into the Hb@PtP nanostructure. UV‒Visible absorption spectra further corroborated the hybridization of Hb, evidenced by a distinct absorption peak at approximately 405 nm in the Hb@PtPP group, which was absent in the BSA@PtP group (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the Hb@PtPP group showed a concentration-dependent increase in the typical Hb absorption peak (Supporting Information Fig. S3). Proteins often include substantial quantities of amide I and amide II fractions, which can serve as indicators of their secondary structure38. As demonstrated by FTIR spectra (Fig. 1E), the absorption bands corresponding to amide I and amide II in Hb@PtP and Hb@PtPP were detected at 1653 and 1524 cm−1, respectively, which were consistent with those observed in native Hb. The results confirmed that Hb incorporated into the nanostructures remained intact. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis indicated that the predominant platinum species in Hb@PtP nanocomposites was platinate, as seen in Supporting Information Fig. S4. Subsequently, the photothermal conversion and autoxidation properties of BSA@PtPP and Hb@PtPP were investigated. The temperature change (ΔT) of Hb@PtPP suspensions at concentrations of 100 and 200 μg/mL rose to 27.9 and 43.7 °C, respectively, after 5 min of 808 nm laser exposure (1 W/cm2) (Fig. 1F). Under identical laser irradiation conditions, the ΔT of BSA@PtPP increased by 21.2 and 29.5 °C at the respective concentrations (Supporting Information Fig. S6A). The marginally elevated ΔT observed in Hb@PtPP may be ascribed to the mechanism of heme-mediated photothermal conversion39. Moreover, the good photothermal stability of BSA@PtPP and Hb@PtPP was assessed over four heating-cooling cycles (Fig. 1G and Fig. S6B). The photothermal conversion efficiencies of BSA@PtPP and Hb@PtPP were calculated to be 21.27% and 24.45%, respectively, based on a single photothermal heating and cooling curve (Fig. 1H and Fig. S6C).

Autoxidation and underlying catalytic mechanism

Driven by the favorable photothermal conversion performance of Hb@PtPP, we next investigated the variations in autoxidation catalytic activity of Hb@PtPP as a function of temperature variations. As previously noted, the initial product of the autoxidation cascade reactions was ·O2−, which can be identified using DHR 123 probe. Compared to BSA@PtPP, Hb@PtPP showed a time-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity under identical H2O2 (500 μmol/L) conditions, indicating a rapid occurrence of Hb@PtPP autoxidation in response to H2O2 (Fig. 2A‒C). In addition, the rate of ·O2− generation increased with the concentration of Hb@PtPP (Supporting Information Fig. S7). The generation of ·O2− from the interaction between Hb@PtPP and H2O2 was reconfirmed by ESR analysis using DMPO as a trapping agent (Fig. 2D). And more importantly, the laser-induced temperature increase accelerated the autoxidation activity, leading to enhanced ·O2− production. Following that, we investigated another byproduct (·OH) produced during the autoxidation cascade reaction, using MB as an indicator. In the presence of H2O2, a distinct generation of ·OH was observed in the Hb@PtPP group, though at a much slower rate compared to ·O2− (Fig. 2E). This slower rate was attributed to the Fenton catalytic process following the release of Fe ions during autoxidation. Notably, Hb@PtPP + H2O2 exhibited a higher potential to produce ·OH than BSA@PtPP + H2O2, consistent with the ·O2− results. Moreover, a rise in local temperature accelerated the reaction rate and promoted ·OH generation (Fig. 2F‒H). Based on these results, an underlying autoxidation catalytic mechanism of Hb@PtPP was proposed and illustrated in Fig. 2J. Upon exposure to H2O2 concentrations that mimic tumors, the Hb component within the Hb@PtPP nanostructure swiftly converted from its original ferrous heme state (Hb(Fe2+) = O2) to ferric heme (HbFe3+), resulting in the production of ·O2− and the degradation of the nanostructure. Subjecting the aforementioned components to a continuous and adequate supply of H2O2 resulted in oxidation, forming ferrylHb (Hb(Fe4+) = O), which subsequently dissociated into heme and iron ions. The liberation of iron ions facilitated the generation of ·OH through the Fenton reaction. More importantly, upon laser irradiation, the pyrrole-conjugated repeating units within the Hb@PtPP nanostructure converted light energy into thermal energy, promoting nanostructure degradation and Hb release, while also increasing the autoxidation rate. In addition, H2O2 gained increased accessibility to Hb, triggering a more pronounced autoxidation cascade reaction and resulting in enhanced oxidative stress.

In vitro biocompatibility and antitumor strategy

The excellent hemocompatibility and biosafety of Hb@PtPP were verified by the significantly reduced hemolysis rate relative to the H2O group, as shown in Supporting Information Fig. S8. Subsequently, the colocalization of 4T1 cells with Hb@PtPP was evaluated using CLSM following different incubation periods, and the results revealed that intracellular accumulation of Hb@PtPP increased with prolonged co-incubation time (Supporting Information Fig. S9). To investigate the in vitro cytotoxicity of BSA@PtPP and Hb@PtPP on normal (3T3) and tumor (4T1) cells, CCK-8 assays were conducted. Following a 24 h treatment with BSA@PtPP, both cell types exhibited a survival rate exceeding 94%, even at the maximum concentration of 200 μg/mL (Fig. 3A). For Hb@PtPP-treated cells, 3T3 cells maintained more than 93% viability across the dosage range, while 4T1 cells revealed concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, with only 73% survival at a concentration of 200 μg/mL (Fig. 3B). This was due in part to the difference in H2O2 concentration between normal and tumor cells, wherein the autoxidation toxicity of Hb@PtPP was catalyzed by the elevated levels of H2O2 in tumor cells, which were several hundred times greater than in normal cells40. Afterward, we investigated the influence of laser-induced hyperthermia on the cytotoxicity of autoxidation catalysis. 4T1 cells subjected to Hb@PtPP showed a concentration- and laser power-dependent decrease in viability when exposed to an 808 nm laser, indicating that increased local temperature enhanced tumor cytotoxicity of Hb@PtPP through hyperthermia and autoxidation toxicity (Fig. 3C). To assess the survival status of 4T1 cells undergoing different treatments, live/dead cell staining assays were employed, where red fluorescence indicated dead cells and green fluorescence indicated viable cells. Supporting Information Fig. S11 illustrates that Hb@PtPP + L induced the most significant cellular damage and mortality, in comparison with Hb@PtPP (autoxidation toxicity) or BSA@PtPP + L (hyperthermia toxicity). The mitochondrial membrane potential assay further confirmed these findings (Supporting Information Fig. S12). Subsequently, the relative contributions of apoptosis, necrosis, and ferroptosis to cytotoxicity were assessed using standard Annexin V-FITC/PI staining assays and the ferroptosis inhibitor Liproxstatin-1. As shown in Fig. 3H, Liproxstatin-1 diminished the lethal toxicity of Hb@PtPP on 4T1 cells, both with or without laser irradiation, implying that ferroptosis was implicated in Hb@PtPP- or Hb@PtPP + L-mediated cytotoxicity, with relative proportions of 4.3% in the Hb@PtPP group and 10.6% in the Hb@PtPP + L group. In addition, the relative proportions of apoptosis and necrosis in the Hb@PtPP + L group were 49.6% and 2.73%, respectively. This finding suggested that laser-mediated hyperthermia augmented the destruction of tumor cells by boosting the autoxidation catalytic activity of Hb@PtPP.

Subsequently, FerroOrange and DCFH-DA probes were employed to evaluate alterations in intracellular Fe2+ and ROS levels under Hb@PtPP-mediated autoxidation. As shown in Fig. 3E, intracellular Fe2+ levels were significantly increased in the Hb@PtPP group compared to the BSA@PtPP group, confirming the role of Hb@PtPP as an iron reservoir. In parallel, intracellular ROS levels were elevated in the Hb@PtPP group, while remaining extremely low in the BSA@PtPP and BSA@PtPP + L groups (Fig. 3F and Supporting Information Fig. S13A). After laser irradiation, higher Fe2+ and ROS levels were observed in Hb@PtPP-treated cells, indicating that hyperthermia accelerated autoxidation, Hb degradation, and Fe2+ release. As mentioned previously, increased intracellular ROS and Fe2+ concentrations facilitated the formation and accumulation of LPO generation. As a result, LPO accumulation was measured using the lipid peroxidation probe C11-BODIPY581/591, which exhibited a fluorescence shift from red to green after oxidation. Notably, 4T1 cells treated with Hb@PtPP + L displayed the highest green fluorescence intensity and the lowest red fluorescence intensity, indicating the accumulation of substantial quantities of LPO, as re-confirmed by the flow cytometry results (Fig. 3D and G). Furthermore, as an aldehyde secondary product of LPO, MDA was considerably elevated in the Hb@PtPP + L-treated group (Fig. S13B). These observations collectively provide strong evidence that Hb@PtPP efficiently catalyzed autoxidation, which was further enhanced by H2O2 and laser irradiation, inducing tumor cells apoptosis and ferroptosis.

RNA-sequencing

To elucidate the antitumor molecular mechanisms of Hb@PtPP, transcriptome analysis was utilized to identify the specific genes that are differentially expressed in 4T1 cells treated with Hb@PtPP + L. A total of 613 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the Hb@PtPP + L group, comprising 363 upregulated genes and 250 downregulated genes (Fig. 4A). To clarify the biological processes (BP), cellular composition (CC), and molecular functions (MF) of the altered genes, as well as related critical signaling pathways, gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were carried out based on the screening of DEGs (Fig. 4B and Supporting Information Fig. S14). The KEGG signaling pathway enrichment map indicated alterations in the expression of genes related to the IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, glutathione metabolism, ferroptosis, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway in the Hb@PtPP + L group (Fig. 4C). The analysis results indicated that the ferroptosis signaling pathway was activated in the Hb@PtPP + L group. In comparison to the control group, tumor cells treated with Hb@PtPP + L demonstrated a substantial increase in the expression of ferroptosis-related DEGs, such as Gclm, Cp, Slc7a11, Hmox1, Fth1, and Ftl1. In vivo biodistribution and antitumor strategy

In order to study the biodistribution and tumor-accumulation of Hb@PtPP in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, Hb@PtPP was first labeled with the near-infrared dye Cy5 (Hb@PtPP-Cy5). A living imaging system was then employed to monitor the temporal variations of the fluorescence signal following intravenous (i.v.) administration. As shown in Fig. 5A and C, Hb@PtPP-Cy5 demonstrated significant tumor retention capability at 12 h post-injection and sustained a strong fluorescence signal until 36 h, indicating its potential for tumor enrichment and retention. This finding was also validated by ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs and tumors at various intervals post-injection (Supporting Information Fig. S15). In contrast, mice injected with free Cy5 showed significantly diminished fluorescence signals in the tumor region. In addition, the organs of mice were collected for imaging 12 h post-injection, revealing that the fluorescent signals of Hb@PtPP-Cy5-treated mice were primarily localized in tumor tissues, consistent with in vivo imaging results (Fig. 5B and D). Subsequently, photothermal imaging of Hb@PtPP was conducted on tumor-bearing mice using a thermal camera. The temperature of the tumor region in the Hb@PtPP-treated group increased progressively, reaching 54.8 °C after 5 min of laser irradiation, as shown in Fig. 5F. In contrast, mice administrated with PBS showed no significant temperature change throughout the exposure. When the tumor volumes of mice reached approximately 80 mm3, all mice were randomly divided into five experimental groups: Control, BSA@PtPP, Hb@PtPP, BSA@PtPP + L, and Hb@PtP-P + L. Mice in laser-required groups received 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W/cm2, 5 min) after i.v. administration of PBS, BSA@PtPP, or Hb@PtPP for 12 h (Fig. 6A). During the 20-day monitoring period, malignant tumors in the Hb@PtPP + L group were almost completely suppressed, in contrast to the Hb@PtPP or BSA@PtPP + L groups (Fig. 6B and C). The body weight of mice in all treatment groups did not significantly differ from that of the control group, suggesting the excellent biosafety of those preparations (Fig. 6D). At the end of the experiment, tumor tissues were collected for the comparison of weight, morphology, and pathological immunohistochemistry. As shown in Fig. 6E and F, the photographs and tumor weights of isolated tumor tissues demonstrated the potent tumor suppression of Hb@PtPP + L. Furthermore, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assays revealed appreciable apoptosis and necrosis in the tumor cells treated with Hb@PtPP and BSA@PtPP + L, although these effects were less pronounced than the pathologically significant cell death observed in the Hb@PtPP + L group (Fig. 6G). Meanwhile, the Hb@PtPP + L group exhibited the strongest inhibition of tumor cell proliferation, consistent with the tumor suppression curves. ROS generation was only detected in the Hb-containing groups as a result of autoxidation catalytic treatment, and laser irradiation significantly enhanced both ROS production and catalytic efficiency. Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a biodegradable Hb nanocatalyst with tunable autoxidation activity for photothermal-enhanced apoptosis-ferroptosis combination therapy. Without altering the function of Hb, monomeric pyrrole was polymerized into Hb@PtP using natural Hb as a stabilizer and H2PtCl6 as an oxidant. PEGylated Hb@PtP exhibited H2O2-sensitive autoxidation activity that not only specifically catalyzed the generation of ·O2− and ·OH from excessive H2O2 within tumors but also triggered the concurrent release of Fe ions and heme through Hb degradation, resulting in Fe overload, oxidative stress, and LPO accumulation. These attributes collectively induced apoptosis and ferroptosis in tumor cells or tissues, which were intensified by exposure to laser radiation. In addition, it was noteworthy that normal cells exhibiting reduced levels of H2O2 and well-established defense mechanisms were minimally affected by Hb@PtPP. The Hb@PtPP developed in this study offers a viable alternative to current material-based nanocatalysts by diminishing immunogenicity and facilitating tumor-selective degradation, thereby advancing the creation of hemoglobin nanomedicines for tumour treatment.
