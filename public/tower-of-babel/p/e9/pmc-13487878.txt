Orb2 RNA-binding activity promotes neural stem cell development and brain growth in Drosophila larvae
Authors: Taylor Hailstock, Joseph Buehler, Beverly V. Robinson, Timothy C.H. Low, Yuqing Hua, Temitope H. Adebambo, Junhyung Ahn, Jack Govaerts, Todd A. Schoborg, Howard D. Lipshitz, Dorothy A. Lerit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeurodevelopment requires precise translational control, the disruption of which is implicated in neurological disorders, developmental delays, and microcephaly. We identify a role for the Drosophila CPEB-family protein Orb2, a translational regulator, in controlling brain size in a dose-dependent manner. Loss of orb2 results in larval brain hypotrophy, whereas orb2 overexpression causes brain overgrowth. We demonstrate that orb2 is required for neural stem cell (NSC) development from embryonic through larval neurogenesis. Structure-function analysis reveals that Orb2 RNA-binding activity promotes brain growth, while its poly-Q and ZZ domains restrain overgrowth. We identify a genetic and biochemical association between Orb2 and Brain tumor (Brat), a repressor of NSC differentiation and brain hypertrophy. Loss of orb2 diminishes Brat levels, while restoring Brat activity is sufficient to rescue orb2-dependent microcephaly. We propose that Orb2 promotes Brat translation directly and through the co-regulation of shared RNA targets, including Myc mRNA, to support Drosophila neurodevelopment.

INTRODUCTION

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) regulate neurodevelopment by controlling neural stem cell (NSC) proliferation, differentiation, and maintenance through post-transcriptional gene regulation.1–3 RBPs are critical for regulating various aspects of stem cell function and homeostasis by directly modulating RNA metabolism, localization, splicing, translation, and transcript turnover, which are essential for proper neuronal development and function.2,4 Consequently, mutations in RBPs are associated with intellectual disability, developmental delays, seizures, microcephaly, other neurodevelopmental disorders, and cancer.5–7

The conserved cytoplasmic polyadenylation element-binding (CPEB) proteins are RBPs that orchestrate the translation of target transcripts through canonical CPE sites in the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR).8 While CPEB proteins such as CPEB4 are implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders, such as autism spectrum disorder and Fragile X Syndrome,9,10 the complete repertoire of CPEB protein interactions and RNA targets, particularly across different tissues and developmental stages, remains incompletely understood.

Drosophila encodes two CPEB proteins from unique genes, Oo18 RNA-binding protein (Orb) and Orb2.11,12 Drosophila orb2 is orthologous to mammalian CPEB2–4 and has known roles in mRNA localization and translation.12–15 Orb2 undergoes conformational changes to switch between a translational repressor and an activator dependent upon prion-like oligomerization, during which the N-terminal glutamine-rich (poly-Q) domain promotes self-association.8,14,16–18 CPEB proteins such as Orb2 bind CPE motifs in target mRNAs through RNA recognition motifs (RRMs).17,19–22 While the C-terminal ZZ-type zinc-finger domain (ZZ-domain) was previously implicated in RNA binding by anchoring CPEB to target transcripts,19 evidence also supports an alternative function in protein-protein binding.23–25 Consistently, recent data identify a role for the ZZ domain in the recruitment of translational repressors to target transcripts.26

CPEB proteins such as Orb2 influence translation levels by regulating the polyadenylation status of target RNAs. Monomeric Orb2 binds to the deadenylase CG13928 to repress translation, while synaptic activity triggers Orb2 oligomerization and binding to CG4612, a predicted poly(A)-binding protein, to promote polyadenylation and translational activation.16,17,21,27,28 For example, recent work indicates that Orb2 promotes rare-codon mRNA translation in the brain.29

Orb2 also autoregulates its own translation by binding CPE motifs in its 3′UTR.21,22 Despite dynamic changes in orb2 expression and localization throughout neurogenesis,12 Orb2 is best known for its roles in learning and memory. Orb2 is extensively studied as a key regulator of long-term memory, wherein activity-induced aggregation facilitates sustained neuronal responses to learning. This molecular process is essential for stabilizing memory traces and supporting lasting behavioral adaptations.14,17,18,20,21,24,27,30–32 By comparison, relatively little is known about Orb2 functions during neurodevelopment.

Drosophila neurodevelopment commences during embryogenesis, when NSCs are specified by spontaneous delamination from the neuroepithelium.33,34 After delamination, NSCs undergo repeated rounds of asymmetric cell division to generate a self-renewing stem cell and a ganglion mother cell fated for differentiation into neuronal or glial subtypes.35,36 The balance between self-renewal versus differentiation is governed by precise segregation of cell fate determinants and is vital to maintain homeostasis in neurogenesis, where deregulation can lead to tumorigenesis, neurodegeneration, or disorders such as microcephaly.37 NSCs enter quiescence in late embryonic stages and resume proliferation during the first larval instar, termed NSC reactivation.38,39 While about 10% of the neurons in the adult brain are born during embryonic stages, the remaining 90% of neurons arise throughout larval development.40 Defects in NSC specification, asymmetric division, or maintenance during early stages are associated with neurodevelopmental deficits.41–47 For example, loss of the translational repressor and tumor suppressor brain tumor (brat) leads to overexpansion of Type II NSCs, resulting in massive brain overgrowth through a mechanism involving deregulated Myc expression.48–51

In this study, we examined the role of Orb2 in neurodevelopment and identified an unexpected requirement for Orb2 in the regulation of larval brain size. We find that Orb2 governs brain size in a dose-dependent manner reliant on its RNA-binding activity, with mutants showing smaller brains consistent with microcephaly. In contrast, orb2 overexpression results in enlarged brains. We identify cell cell-autonomousautonomous requirements for Orb2 to regulate brain size through the specification and maintenance of NSCs during embryonic and larval development. We further uncover that orb2 genetically and biochemically interacts with brat, demonstrating that the opposing activities of these RBPs support balanced brain growth. We propose Orb2 regulates brain size through direct regulation of Brat levels and through the co-regulation of shared translation targets, including Myc mRNA.

RESULTS

Orb2 is a homeostatic regulator of brain size

To investigate Orb2 contributions to neurodevelopment, we first quantified brain volumes from age-matched third-instar larvae.52,53 Strikingly, orb2 mutant brains were ~70% smaller than wild-type (WT) (Figures 1A, 1B, and 1D; p < 0.0001 by one-way ANOVA). These data indicate Orb2 is required for brain growth. To test if orb2 overexpression was sufficient to enlarge larval brains, we drove the expression of a UAS-orb2B-GFP transgene20 using actin-GAL4 (act-GAL4; hereafter, orb2OE) and confirmed overexpression via western blot (Figure S1A and S1B; p < 0.01 by two-tailed t test). Indeed, orb2OE brains showed 2-fold increased brain volumes relative to WT (Figures 1C and 1D; p < 0.0001 by one-way ANOVA). We conclude that Orb2 regulates larval brain size in a dose-dependent manner.

Orb2 is required in neuronal lineages to promote brain growth

The larval brain comprises distinct cellular identities, including NSCs, marked by Miranda (Mira)54 or Inscuteable (Insc)55; glia, marked by the reversed polarity (repo) gene56; and neurons, marked by embryonic lethal abnormal vision (elav).57 To characterize the cellular etiology of orb2-dependent microcephaly, we selectively depleted orb2 in defined cellular lineages within the larval brain, including NSCs, neurons, glia, and the neuroepithelium, using shRNA (orb2RNAi) and a panel of GAL4 drivers.58 We then compared brain volumes from age-matched larval brains to controls that carry the RNAi transgene, but not a GAL4 driver; hereafter, “no GAL4” controls.

We first confirmed that the uniform expression of orb2RNAi with act-GAL4 was sufficient to reduce brain volume (Figures 1E, 1F, and 1L). Depletion of orb2 by RNAi reduced brain volume to a lesser degree than the orb2 null mutation, likely due to the partial depletion of orb2 activity, as shown by immunoblot (~50% reduced; Figures S1C and S1D). Similar reductions in brain volume were also detected when orb2 was depleted in neurogenic lineages: neuroepithelia cells, which give rise to medulla neuroblasts of the optic lobe (c855a-GAL459–61 p < 0.0001), NSCs (wor-GAL462; p < 0.05 or insc-GAL4; p < 0.01), or neurons (elav-GAL4; p < 0.01), underscoring requirements for orb2 in these lineages to promote brain growth (Figures 1G–1J and 1L; by one-way ANOVA; Figure S1E). In contrast, no difference in brain volume was noted upon the depletion of orb2 in glia via repo-GAL4 (Figure 1K and 1L; n.s. by one-way ANOVA). These data indicate that Orb2 facilitates the establishment of normal brain size in neural progenitors and their neuronal progeny.

Orb2 expression becomes progressively restricted to mature neurons

We next examined where Orb2 is expressed during larval neurodevelopment. To assess Orb2 distributions in live larval brains, we imaged third instar larval brains expressing endogenously tagged Orb2-GFP, previously shown to functionally restore orb2 activity,20 relative to an mCherry nuclear reporter driven by cell type-specific drivers. Through this approach, we found that Orb2 is not detectable within glia, marked by NP2222-GAL4 or repo-GAL4, nor in the optic lobe neuroepithelium or lamina precursor cells, marked by c855a-GAL4 (Figure 2A). In contrast, Orb2-GFP was coincident within a subset of smaller insc-GAL4 expressing cells (arrows; Figure 2A). However, at this stage, Orb2 was not detected within third instar larval NSCs, characterized by their distinctive, large nuclei (dashed circles; Figure 2A). Taken together, these data show that Orb2-GFP-positive cells are non-glial descendants of the NSC lineage (i.e., neurons).

Prior work indicates that Orb2 is primarily localized to the cytoplasm of Drosophila neurons, including mature glutamatergic neurons.14,27,29,30 Consistently, Orb2-GFP expression was detected within a subgroup of elavc155-positive cells determined to be mature larval neurons, given strong co-expression with bruchpilot (brp)-GAL463 (arrows; Figure 2A). Unlike brp, elav is expressed not only in mature neurons, but also in NSCs and their immediate progeny.64 Immunostaining confirmed that endogenous Orb2, as detected with anti-Orb2 antibodies, labels a subset of Elav+ neurons, but not Mira+ NSCs, within third instar larval brains (box; Figure 2B).

Because loss of orb2 activity in NSCs results in impaired brain growth (Figure 1), we next assayed whether Orb2 is within NSCs at earlier stages of development. Low levels of Orb2 were detected within embryonic Mira+ dorsoanterior neural progenitors, consistent with a possible early function in neural lineages (Figure S1F). Upon examining first, second, and third instar stages, we noted that the distribution of Orb2 becomes progressively more restricted over time (Figure 2C). At the first larval instar (1 h post larval hatching (PLH)), Orb2-GFP was detected within subsets of insc- or elav-expressing cells. However, the exclusion of Orb2-GFP from NSCs became apparent in the second (24h PLH) and third (72 h PLH) instar larval brains (dashed circles; Figure 2C). While we cannot exclude the possibility that low levels of Orb2 beneath our detection limit persist in late-stage NSCs, our data reveal that Orb2 distributions become progressively restricted within mature neurons over development.

To further examine orb2 expression, we analyzed published single-cell transcriptomics (scRNA-seq) data of the Drosophila larval central nervous system across the first, second, and third instar stages.65 In accordance with our protein reporter assay, orb2 mRNA was coincident with brp within mature larval neurons (MLNs) and Kenyon cells (KCs) of the mushroom body. In contrast, levels of orb2 mRNA were not enriched within mira+ or insc+ NSCs (Figures 2D and S1G). Together, these data suggest that Orb2 likely functions within early neural progenitors and neurons to promote brain growth. These findings underscore a previously undefined role for Orb2 in neurodevelopment.

Multiple Orb2 domains instruct brain size

orb2 encodes two protein isoforms, Orb2A (predicted molecular weight, ~60 kDa) and Orb2B (~75 kDa), which differ only in the length of an unstructured N-terminal region with unknown function (Figure 3A).12 As previously noted, Orb2B is the predominant isoform in third instar larval brains12,21,66 (Figure S1A WT). Consistent with those findings, loss of Orb2B (orb2ΔB) is sufficient to reduce brain size (Figures 3B–3D). However, we cannot formally rule out a contribution from Orb2A.

Because multiple domains within Orb2 support its function as an RBP implicated in translational regulation, we performed structure-function analysis to identify which domain(s) regulate larval brain size (Figure 3A).17,20–22 The glutamine-rich (poly-Q) domain enables Orb2 to form stable oligomers through prion-like conformational changes that are required for synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory.20,21,27 Deletion of the poly-Q domain via the orb2ΔQ allele14 resulted in brains ~40% bigger than WT (Figures 3E, 3F, and 3J; **p < 0.01 by one-way ANOVA). We used CRISPR-Cas9 editing to mutate both RNA recognition motifs (RRMs), targeting the same amino acids previously identified as critical for RNA-binding activity.19,20 Mutation of RRM1 (Y492A, F494A) or both RRMs (Y492A, F494A, R601A, and F604A; see Methods) was sufficient to reduce brain size (Figures 3G, 3H, and 3J; *p < 0.05 or **p < 0.01, respectively, by one-way ANOVA). Within CPEB proteins, the conserved C-terminal cysteine and histidine-rich ZZ domain contributes to protein-protein interactions.22–26 We analyzed mutants lacking the ZZ domain (AA634–704), orb2ΔZZ, generated by CRISPR-Cas9. As in orb2ΔQ, loss of the ZZ domain resulted in brain overgrowth (Figures 3I and 3J; **p < 0.01 by one-way ANOVA). The brain growth responses observed in our mutant panel are unlikely due to changes in Orb2 protein expression, as detected by immunoblotting or previously reported (Figures S1H–S1K).15,17 Taken together, these results indicate that the Orb2 RRM1 is necessary to promote brain growth, while the poly-Q and ZZ domains restrict brain overgrowth.

Orb2-mediated translational activation regulates brain size

Orb2 functions as either a translational repressor or activator depending on its oligomerization status and association with specific cofactors (Figure 4A).16,17,21,27,28 To determine whether Orb2 regulates brain size through its role in translational control, we depleted its translation cofactors by shRNA.17,29 Loss of the repressive cofactor, CG13928, did not alter brain volume or Orb2 protein levels (Figures 4B–4E; n.s. not significant by two-tailed t test). In contrast, the depletion of the activating cofactor, CG4612, increased brain size relative to controls (Figures 4F and 4G; ***p < 0.001 by two-tailed t test). Since loss of the translation activation cofactor CG4612 and orb2OE both resulted in enlarged brains, we predicted that CG4612 depletion would increase Orb2 protein levels. Unexpectedly, CG4612RNAi reduced Orb2 protein levels by ~90% (Figures 4H and 4I; ****p < 0.0001 by two-tailed t test). These data suggest that CG4612 (or one of its targets) normally promotes the translation of Orb2, consistent with Orb2 autoregulation.21,22,67 Alternatively, CG4612 or one of its targets may stabilize the Orb2 protein. Nevertheless, our findings that both the Orb2 poly-Q domain and CG4612 restrict brain growth are consistent with the hypothesis that Orb2 translational activity limits brain size.

Orb2 is necessary to maintain the number and size of central brain NSCs

How does Orb2 promote brain growth? To begin to address this question, we tabulated total NSCs in orb2 versus WT larval brains. Loss of orb2 or NSC-specific depletion of orb2 via wor-GAL4 resulted in ~60% fewer Mira+ NSCs per lobe relative to WT controls (Figures 5A and 5B; p < 0.0001 by one-way ANOVA). Moreover, as compared to WT, NSC volume was also significantly reduced in orb2 mutants and following NSC-specific depletion (Figure 5C; p < 0.0001 and p < 0.001, respectively, by one-way ANOVA). While Orb2 is not detectably enriched in late-stage NSCs, these findings collectively demonstrate its necessity in NSC specification, maintenance, and size, likely acting during earlier neurodevelopmental stages or at sub-detectable levels.

NSC lineages can be identified based on the expression of defined markers. While all NSCs express the transcription factor deadpan (Dpn), only Type I NSCs also express Asense (Ase).68 The Dpn+ Ase− Type II NSCs produce neurons and glia via intermediate neural progenitors, which divide multiple times to form diverse neural niches.68–70 While ~20% fewer Type II NSCs were observed in orb2 mutants (p < 0.01 by one-way ANOVA), no change was detected upon NSC-specific knockdown of orb2 via wor-GAL4, which drives expression in both Type I and Type II NSCs (Figures S2A and S2B n.s. by one-way ANOVA;62,71). It is feasible that sufficient orb2 activity may persist following the incomplete depletion of orb2 via RNAi to protect Type II NSCs from loss.

Consistent with reduced orb2 activity, depletion of CG4612 also decreased total and Type II NSCs (Figures S2C–S2E). Intriguingly, the NSCs within CG4612RNAi brains were enlarged (Figures S2F and S2G; ***p < 0.001 by two-tailed t test). These results argue that brain size may be uncoupled from NSC number and more closely align with cell size. Taken together, these data imply reduced orb2 activity may disrupt NSC growth processes, perhaps through the misexpression of cell growth-related proteins.

Orb2 is required for neurogenesis

Our data show that orb2 loss or depletion results in fewer total NSCs. Because NSCs give rise to the neurons and glia, we also tabulated the number of these cell types within orb2 mutant larvae relative to controls. Loss of orb2 resulted in ~50% more glia, as compared to WT (Figures S3A and S3B; **p < 0.01 by two-tailed t test). In addition, significantly more orb2 glia were proliferating relative to controls, which may contribute to their expansion72 (Figures S3A and S3C; **p < 0.01 by two-tailed t test). In contrast, neurons were ~30% reduced in mutants (Figures S3D and S3E; **p < 0.01 by two-tailed t test). Together, these data suggest that orb2 normally functions to promote neuronal versus glial cell fate.

Orb2 enables NSC specification

To elucidate mechanisms underlying NSC loss in orb2 mutants, we first tested the hypothesis that NSCs are eliminated by cell death. We quantified the coincidence of pro-apoptotic cleaved caspase 3 (CC3)73 in NSCs marked with the Dpn antibody.74,75 As an independent metric, we similarly quantified the coincidence of death caspase protein-1 (Dcp-1) within NSCs. We failed to uncover elevated rates of NSC death in orb2 mutants, indicating NSC loss occurs by other mechanisms (Figures S4A–S4F; n.s. by t test). Another mechanism whereby NSCs may be depleted is via premature differentiation.36,76,77 Normally, the pro-differentiation marker Prospero (Pros) is confined to the nucleus of the differentiating ganglion mother cell and absent from NSCs,78,79 while retention of Pros in the NSC nucleus promotes premature differentiation.76 We quantified the coincidence of Pros with Dpn and detected no significant difference relative to WT, indicating premature differentiation does not direct NSC loss in orb2 mutants (Figures S4G–S4I; n.s. by t test). Finally, we examined whether orb2 affects mitotic progression, reasoning that impaired NSC self-renewal might contribute to NSC loss. However, the mitotic indices in WT and orb2 brains were not significantly different (33.6 ± 4.9% per lobe in WT vs. 36.3 ± 10.1% in orb2 brains; Figures S4J–S4L; n.s. by t test). Taken together, these data suggest that NSC loss is not due to excess apoptosis, premature differentiation, or impaired proliferation.

Both Type I and Type II NSCs arise from proneural clusters during embryogenesis and serve as the primary source of larval NSCs.80,81 We therefore examined whether orb2 is required during embryonic neurogenesis for NSC development. Indeed, orb2 mutant embryos had ~40% fewer Dpn+ neural progenitors compared to age-matched WT embryos (Figures 5D and 5E; p < 0.001 by two-tailed t test). These data underscore an early requirement for Orb2 in the specification and/or maintenance of embryonic neural progenitors and indicate that the reduced number of NSCs in orb2 third-instar larvae may result, at least in part, from the reduced number of NSCs specified in embryos.

orb2 activity is required throughout neurogenesis to regulate brain size

Having identified a requirement for Orb2 in embryos, we next assessed the developmental requirement of orb2 to promote brain growth. We employed a temperature-sensitive GAL80 mutant (GAL80TS) to selectively deplete orb2 in embryos versus larvae. For this strategy, raising progeny at permissive temperatures (18°C) blocks the expression of orb2RNAi, while growth at the restrictive temperature (29°C) inactivates GAL80TS, leading to orb2RNAi expression and depletion of orb282,83 (Figure 5F). To specifically deplete orb2 in early embryos, embryos were reared at the restrictive temperature and then shifted to the permissive temperature. In contrast, the role of orb2 in larval development was examined by rearing embryos at the permissive temperature until larval hatching, at which point larvae were shifted to the restrictive temperature (Figures 5F and 5G). Depleting orb2 during embryogenesis or larval development resulted in significantly reduced larval brain volumes relative to controls (Figures 5H and 5I; p < 0.01 and p < 0.0001, respectively, by one-way ANOVA). Thus, loss of orb2 activity at either early or later stages is sufficient to impair neurodevelopment.

To further examine Orb2 requirements during embryogenesis, we conducted a hatch rate analysis. While most WT embryos hatch into first instar larvae within 24-h, about half of orb2 (M−/Z−) null embryos failed to hatch after 72-h, indicating Orb2 is required for embryonic viability. To distinguish whether orb2 is maternal-effect lethal or required zygotically, after the maternal-to-zygotic transition, we mated orb2 virgin females to WT males.84,85 The resulting orb2/+ (M−/Z+) embryos hatched at WT levels, indicating that maternal orb2 is not required for viability (Figure 5J). We conclude that orb2 is necessary zygotically for embryonic survival, and that escaper larvae require orb2 for proper neurodevelopment.

Orb2 interacts with brat mRNA

Brat is a translational repressor and tumor suppressor also implicated in brain size determination. Brat regulates NSC homeostasis by limiting self-renewal and promoting differentiation. Thus, brat loss results in the massive expansion of Type II NSCs and brain overgrowth.48–51 Considering their opposing phenotypes, we reasoned that Orb2 might instruct brain size through translational control of Brat. In support of this model, prior work indicates that Orb2 binds brat mRNA directly via CPE motifs within the brat 3′UTR. Moreover, dual luciferase reporter assays in Drosophila S2 cells confirm that Orb2 regulates Brat translation in a CPE-dependent manner.22,66

We aligned a region within the brat 3′UTR conserved across multiple brat isoforms to multiple Drosophila species using the conservation insect track on the UCSC genome browser and identified CPE motifs conserved across millions of years of evolutionary distance, hinting they are functionally important86 (Figure 6A; Figures S5A–S5C). To promote translation, CPEB proteins bind CPE sites within 100 nt of polyadenylation signal (PAS) motifs (i.e., the hexanucleotide motif AAUAAA), which recruit the cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor (CPSF) to cleave pre-mRNA and add the poly(A) tail, aiding mRNA stability, export, and translation.87 We identified PAS motifs (blue boxes) within the brat 3′UTR proximal to multiple CPEs (red boxes), consistent with a role for Orb2 in translational activation (Figures 6A and S5A–S5C). We further identified multiple AU-rich elements (AREs; green boxes), which modulate mRNA decay or translation, in this same region (Figures 6A and S5C). This arrangement of CPE, PAS, and ARE motifs suggests precise regulation of brat expression to enable normal neurodevelopment.19,88 Recently, several Orb2 response elements (OREs) enriched within Orb2 translational targets were also identified in the brat 3′UTR.26 Together, these findings position Brat as a strong candidate for Orb2-mediated translational regulation during neurodevelopment.

To uncover the relationship between Orb2 and Brat within larval brains, we first examined their relative distributions. As previously reported, Brat is ubiquitously distributed throughout the central nervous system. In mitotic NSCs, however, Brat colocalizes with the adapter protein Mira as a crescent at the basal cortex (arrowheads, Figure S5D;48–50). In contrast, Orb2 was undetectable in NSCs or Mira+ basal crescents (inset boxes 1 and 2, Figure S5D). Intriguingly, while Orb2 and Brat did partially overlap within differentiated neurons, regions of high Orb2 enrichment contain markedly less Brat, consistent with a potential role for Orb2 in modulating Brat levels or vice versa (inset box 3, Figure S5D).

To assess if Orb2 regulates Brat expression in larval brains, we examined steady-state Brat levels in WT versus orb2 mutants by western blot. Loss of orb2 significantly diminished Brat protein levels from larval brain extracts (Figure 6B and 6C). Importantly, steady-state brat mRNA levels remained unchanged in orb2 mutants relative to controls, indicating changes to Brat protein levels are due to post-transcriptional mechanisms (Figures 6D and S5E). By comparison, Brat seems dispensable for steady-state levels of Orb2 protein, as assessed by immunoblotting (Figures 6E and 6F). These data support a role for Orb2 in regulating Brat protein levels.

While Orb2 may promote Brat translation, an alternative model is that Orb2 is required to stabilize the Brat protein. To distinguish between these possibilities, we examined whether Orb2 associates with brat mRNA. Using Orb2-GFP brain extracts, we confirmed by RNA immunoprecipitation assay that Orb2 associates with its cognate mRNA, but not the negative control, Gapdh1 mRNA (Figure 6G). Further, and consistent with a role in translational regulation, we also found that Orb2 associates with brat mRNA within larval brain extracts (Figure 6G). These data, together with prior work demonstrating direct binding as well as Orb2-and CPE-dependent translation of a brat mRNA reporter discussed above, argue that Orb2 promotes Brat translation during Drosophila neurodevelopment.

orb2 genetically modifies the loss of the tumor suppressor brat

We next assayed whether orb2 and brat genetically interact to regulate brain size. Consistent with a strong synthetic lethal interaction, we were unable to recover brat; orb2 homozygous double-mutant larvae. Nevertheless, we examined whether hemizygosity of orb2, previously shown to reduce orb2 mRNA and protein levels by ~75% and ~60%, respectively,89 was sufficient to modify the brat brain overgrowth phenotype. orb2/+ hemizygotes showed reduced brain volumes relative to WT, consistent with a dose-dependent effect of orb2 on brain size (Figures 6H, 6I, and 6L; **p < 0.01 by ANOVA). In contrast, brat mutant brains showed the expected increase in brain size (Figures 6J and 6L; ***p < 0.001 by ANOVA). Critically, hemizygosity of orb2 was sufficient to dominantly suppress the brain overgrowth of brat mutants (brat; orb2/+; Figures 6K and 6L; *p < 0.05 by ANOVA). Thus, relative Orb2 levels influence the brat phenotype, thereby underscoring the tight genetic coupling of orb2 and brat in the regulation of larval brain size.

The Orb2-Brat axis converges on Myc

The fact that orb2 dominantly suppresses the brat brain overgrowth phenotype is inconsistent with simple genetic epistasis. A similar genetic interdependence among RBPs was previously described for Nab2 and FMRP, where nab2 phenotypes in axon pathfinding were dependent upon the Fmr genotype.90 In that study, the authors suggested Nab2 and FMRP may co-regulate shared transcripts. In light of these findings, we examined recently published embryonic RNA-seq data and noted that Orb2 and Brat are predicted to co-regulate >60 RNAs.26

Among the targets predicted to be shared by Orb2 and Brat is Myc mRNA.26 Myc is among the best characterized Brat substrates, where Brat-mediated inhibition of Myc translation downregulates proliferation and promotes differentiation.49,91–94 Brat translational control of Myc is critical for the regulation of NSC self-renewal vs. differentiation, a mechanism conserved from Drosophila to humans. To test whether the Orb2–Brat axis converges on Myc, we first examined whether Orb2 associated with Myc mRNA. Indeed, Myc mRNA was robustly enriched within Orb2 complexes relative to input (Figure 6G). These data confirm that Orb2 forms a biochemical complex with Myc mRNA.

We next quantified Myc levels in brat, orb2, and brat; orb2/+ mutants. As expected, loss of brat elevated Myc protein levels (Figures 6M and 6N).49 Consistent with a potential co-regulatory mechanism, levels of Myc are suppressed by orb2 hemizygosity in the brat mutant background (Figures 6M and 6N; **p < 0.01 by t test). Importantly, orb2 does not impair Myc mRNA levels, demonstrating that Orb2-dependent regulation occurs post-transcriptionally (Figure 6O). Together, these results indicate that the Orb2–Brat axis converges on Myc, with Orb2 likely promoting Myc expression in opposition to Brat-mediated repression. We propose that the dependence of Brat function on Orb2 is consistent with two models: (1) Orb2 promotion of Brat translation and (2) Coupled or sequential roles for Orb2 and Brat on common transcripts, such as Myc. These models are not mutually exclusive, and the precise nature by which Orb2 and Brat co-regulate shared targets remains to be determined.

Genetic coupling of orb2 and brat

RBPs such as Orb2 and Brat often have hundreds of RNA targets. Given that Brat was prioritized as a potential Orb2 target through a candidate-based approach, we sought to test whether restoring Brat levels in orb2 mutants would be sufficient to rescue orb2-dependent microcephaly. To test this idea, a brat transgene comprising the brat cDNA with a small C-terminal Myc epitope tag was overexpressed in an orb2-depleted background (actGAL4>UAS-brat-Myc; hereafter, bratOE). Remarkably, this brat transgene was sufficient to restore brain size in orb2 mutant larval brains (orb2, bratOE; Figures 7A–7D; ****p < 0.0001). Similar results were also observed following depletion of orb2 by RNAi (Figures S5F–S5I). Moreover, bratOE was also sufficient to rescue NSC number and volume within orb2 larvae (orb2, bratOE; Figures 7E–7I; ****p < 0.0001). Additionally, NSC number was partially rescued within neurogenic embryos (Figures 7J and 7M; **p < 0.01 by ANOVA), while embryonic viability was fully restored (Figures 7J and 7N; ***p < 0.001 by ANOVA). Taken together, these data highlight the significance of the Orb2-Brat interaction for the regulation of neurodevelopment.

DISCUSSION

CPEB proteins such as Orb2 are important for learning and memory.14,17,18,20,21,24,27,30–32 Here, we define a novel and dose-dependent requirement for Orb2 in regulating brain size. The dynamic regulation of orb2 expression throughout neurogenesis likely ensures proper orb2 dosage required for neurodevelopment. Notably, although the Orb2 protein is not detectably enriched in late-stage NSCs, our data support a functional requirement during embryonic and larval stages for NSC specification, maintenance, and size. These findings are consistent with a requirement at earlier stages of neurodevelopment or at levels below detection. We find that Orb2 functions in early neural progenitors and elav+ neurons to promote brain growth through a mechanism also requiring its RNA-binding activity. We further uncover that Orb2 translational activity regulates brain size, as shown by loss of the Orb2 poly-Q domain or the translation co-activator, CG4612.

Given the varying phenotypes resulting from our structure-function analysis, we speculated that Orb2 may promote the translation of a repressor of brain growth. Such a factor would act in opposition to Orb2, which normally promotes brain growth. This model would establish a feedback loop to normalize brain growth, consistent with a role for Orb2 in the homeostatic regulation of brain size (Figure 7O).

Our data suggest that Orb2 normally promotes brain growth through two mechanisms. First, our data are consistent with a role for Orb2 in activating Brat translation (Figure 7Oi). While RBPs such as Orb2 often have many RNA targets, our rescue experiments identify brat as a key functional target contributing to NSC number and brain growth. Prior studies show that Orb2 directly binds brat mRNA via CPEs in its 3′UTR to regulate its translation22 and similarly demonstrated Orb2-dependent control of Brat protein levels.66 Further, dual luciferase reporter assays demonstrate that the brat 3′UTR is sufficient, and the CPEs are necessary for Orb2-dependent translational control.22 In this study, we reveal that Orb2 associates with brat mRNA within larval brains and uncover a requirement for Orb2 to positively regulate Brat protein levels. These data are consistent with a model in which Orb2 promotes Brat translation. However, this linear regulatory relationship may be an oversimplification. An alternative model is that restoring brat activity rebalances homeostasis through the collective regulation of many targets.

Indeed, our work supports a second mechanism whereby Orb2 and Brat co-regulate common RNAs.26 Specifically, we find Orb2 associates with a master regulator of NSC homeostasis and a critical downstream target of Brat, Myc mRNA. Our finding that orb2 attenuates Myc accumulation in brat mutants implies that Orb2 and Brat co-regulate Myc translation to balance Myc levels during neurodevelopment (Figure 7Oii). Collectively, Orb2 contributes to proper brain growth through the regulation of Brat expression and the co-regulation of shared RNA targets within a broader translational network.

In the early embryo, Brat represses hunchback translation, critical for axial patterning.94 In neurogenic embryos, Brat segregates asymmetrically with Mira in dividing NSCs to promote neuronal differentiation.49 In larvae, Brat limits NSC proliferation by regulating various transcription factors, including Myc, and works alongside Pros to repress NSC renewal and promote differentiation.49 Although initially discovered in Drosophila, the inhibition of Myc by Brat and its orthologs (e.g., Trim32 and TRIM3) is conserved within mammals, including humans.49,92,95 Whether orb2 converges on brat functions in these additional contexts warrants further investigation.

Limitations of the study

This study has important limitations that should be considered. Localization of Orb2 within cellular lineages was descriptive and not quantified. Although we demonstrated that Orb2B is necessary and sufficient for brain growth, the relative contribution of Orb2A was not assessed, nor did we evaluate sex-specific differences. This study did not demonstrate direct binding of Orb2 to brat mRNA within larval brains, nor did we specifically test the importance of the CPE motifs within the brat 3′UTR. We used a candidate-driven rationale when selecting Brat for further study, and it was not identified through unbiased screening. Orb2 may regulate Brat translation directly; alternatively, Orb2 and Brat may co-regulate a network of genes, whereby their dosage alters the collective synergy of many targets. Further, the mechanism(s) by which Orb2 and Brat co-regulate common targets (e.g., cooperative, competitive, or sequential binding) remain to be determined.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

All experiments were performed using Drosophila melanogaster. Flies were maintained on standard cornmeal–agar medium at 25°C under a 12 h light/dark cycle unless otherwise specified. Experimental animals were generated using standard genetic crosses as described in the Key Resources Table. Developmental stages used in this study included embryos, larvae, and/or adults as indicated in the relevant figure legends. A mix of male and female flies were randomly selected for use in experiments, and sex was not considered as a biological variable. Because Drosophila melanogaster is an invertebrate, Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approval was not required for this study. No human participants, human-derived samples, or vertebrate animal subjects were used in this study.

Hatch rate analysis

Embryos from y1w1118 control, orb2Δ36, and heterozygotes were collected on yeasted grape juice agar plates and ~200 embryos were transferred at 0–2h AEL to fresh plates and aged for 72 h at 25°C. Unhatched embryos were counted from each plate and compared to controls. orb2Δ36 virgins and males were mated to generate M-Z− embryos, while orb2 heterozygotes (M-Z+) were derived by crossing null virgin females to control males, to assess maternal versus zygotic contributions. Data presented are mean ± SD from three biological replicates.

Larval age-matching

Larval samples were synchronously staged as described.53 Briefly, Drosophila adults were allowed to seed grape juice agar plates supplemented with yeast paste for 48 h at 25°C. Plates were changed every 24 h, after which flies were allowed 30 min to clear previously fertilized eggs. Embryos were collected for 4 h on a fresh plate and incubated at 25°C until first instar larvae hatched. First instar larvae were transferred into vials containing Drosophila culturing medium supplemented with yeast paste dyed with bromophenol blue, then incubated at 25°C until crawling third-instar larvae emerged. Age-matched larvae that both displayed foraging behavior and partial gut clearance of the blue yeast paste were then selected for dissection.38

Temperature-shift assays

For temperature-shift experiments, we used GAL80TS to modulate expression of orb2RNAi. Animals were reared at 18°C to inhibit orb2RNAi expression or at 29°C allow orb2RNAi expression. act-GAL4 virgins were mated to GAL80TS, orb2RNAi recombinant animals in cages for 48 h at 18°C. At 24–48h AEL, orb2 expression was inhibited in first instar larvae by shifting animals to 29°C. To inhibit orb2 activity in embryos, the same parental genotypes were mated at 29°C for 48 h prior to harvesting 4–7 h embryos. Embryos were maintained at 29°C for 24–48h, and first instar larvae were shifted to 18°C to allow orb2RNAi expression. Age-matched third-instar larvae were selected 48–72 h after temperature-shift as described in.53 Experimental groups were compared to GAL80TS, orb2RNAi controls lacking GAL4 raised at 18°C.

Immunofluorescence

Age-matched crawling third instar larval brains were used for dissections and prepared for immunofluorescence as described.109 Brains were dissected in Schneider’s Drosophila Medium (ThermoFisher Scientific, #21720024), fixed in 9% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, blocked in PBT buffer [Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.1% Tween 20] for one hour at room temperature prior to overnight incubation in primary antibodies in PBT with nutation at 4°C. Samples were further blocked with modified PBT (2% BSA, 0.1% Tween 20, and 4% normal goat serum (NGS) in PBS) for one hour before incubation for two hours at room temperature with secondary antibody and DAPI.

Embryos were fixed in a 1:4 solution of 4% paraformaldehyde:heptane for 20 min and devitellinized in methanol.110 Fixed embryos were rehydrated, blocked in PBTx buffer (PBS supplemented with 0.3% Triton X-100 and 1% BSA), and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in PBTx supplemented with 2% NGS. After washing, embryos were further blocked in PBTx supplemented with 2% NGS and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies and DAPI (10 ng/mL, ThermoFisher Scientific, #D1306). All samples were mounted in Aqua-Poly/Mount (Polysciences Inc., #18606–20) prior to imaging.

The following primary antibodies were used: guinea pig anti-Asense (1:5000, gift from Y.N. Jan, University of California San Francisco;97), guinea pig anti-Orb2 (1:1000, gift from K. Si, Stowers institute for Medical Research66), mouse anti-elav 9F8A9 (1:500, DSHB), mouse anti-Orb2 2D11 and 4GS (1:25 each, DSHB), mouse anti-repo 8D12 (1:2000, DSHB), mouse anti-Prospero MR1A (1:500, DSHB), rabbit anti-DCP-1 (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology #9578), rabbit anti-Dpn (1:500, gift from Y.N. Jan, University of California San Francisco;74), rabbit anti-GFP (1:700–1000, Synaptic Systems, #132003), rabbit anti-pH3 (1:1000, Millipore #05570), and rat anti-Mira (1:500, Abcam, #ab197788). Microscopy and live imaging

Images were acquired on a Nikon Ti-E system fitted with a Yokagawa CSU-X1 (Yokogawa Corp. of America) spinning disk head, Hamamatsu Orca Flash 4.0 v2 digital complementary metal oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) camera (Hamamatsu Corp.), Perfect Focus system (Nikon), and Nikon LU-N4 solid state laser launch (15 mW; 405, 488, 561, and 647 nm) The following Nikon objectives were used: 100× 1.49-NA Apo Total Internal Reflection Fluorescence oil immersion, 40 × 1.3-NA Plan Fluor oil immersion, and 20 × 0.75-NA Plan Apo. Images were acquired at 25°C through Nikon Elements AR software on a 64-bit HP Z440 workstation (Hewlett-Packard).

Age-matched larval brains were dissected as described109 and prepared for live imaging.111 Briefly, explanted brains were transferred to a drop of fibrinogen-supplemented media (10mg/mL fibrinogen (Sigma Aldrich #341573) added to Schneider’s Drosophila Medium (ThermoFisher Scientific #21720024)) in a glass bottom imaging dish (MatTek #P35G-1.5–14-C). The brain was oriented with the dorso-anterior central brain contacting the glass, and the clot was polymerized by the addition of thrombin at 100 μg/mL (Sigma Aldrich #T7513). After polymerization, 500 μL of medium was added on top of the clot for the duration of imaging.

Image processing and quantitative analysis

Images were resolved using Fiji (National Institutes of Health112), to separate or merge channels, crop regions of interest, generate maximum-intensity projections, and adjust brightness and contrast. Adobe Photoshop and Adobe Illustrator software (Adobe, Inc.) were used to assemble images into panels.

Volume measurements were obtained in Imaris 9.9, 10.1.0, and 10.1.1 software (Oxford Instruments) using the 3D surface tool to define the optic lobe region of interest on DAPI-visualized brains and statistics function to calculate surface volume. Brain volume is represented by measurement from one lobe per brain and normalized to the control mean.52 NSC and neuroepithelium volume measurements were obtained using the 3D surface tool to detect objects with machine learning function to subtract background. Cell volume was calculated using the statistics function. The experimenter was blinded to genotype prior to analysis.

For embryos, 4–7 h WT and orb2 embryos were collected and stained with anti-Dpn antibodies. Maximum projected images of Dpn+ cells were counted in Fiji using the Cell Counter plugin.113 The experimenter was blinded to genotype prior to analysis.

For larvae, NSCs were identified as large, Mira+ cells located in the central brain. NSC counts are reported from a single optic lobe per sample. Mitotic index was calculated as the number of Mira+, pH3+ NSCs per total Mira+ NSCs. Mitotic events were identified by the presence of pH3 signal. Single-channel.tif raw images for mitotic event, glia, and neurons were segmented in three dimensions using Python scripts adapted from the Allen Institute for Cell Science Cell Segmenter114 as described.103 To score cell death, events were identified by the presence of Dcp-1 in Mira+ cells using the Cell Counter plugin in Fiji.113 The experimenter was blinded to genotype prior to analysis. To quantify neurons per brain lobe, age matched WT and orb2 third-instar larvae expressing elavC155-GAL4 and UAS-mCherryNLS were live imaged. Images were processed with Imaris 9.9 using the spot tool to detect objects, and the number of objects were counted with the statistics function. The experimenter was blinded to genotype prior to analysis.

Count matrices were imported into R, filtered, and quality controlled as described in the Github repository companion to the original publication65 using SeuratV5.102 Cells were grouped into 67 clusters and then assigned a cell type by comparing the positively differentially expressed genes to known biomarkers as defined by Corrales et al. Supplementary Spreadsheet 19. Visualization was completed in R using Seurat visualization functions.

Immunoblotting

Larval brain extracts were prepared from 10 to 20 crawling third instar larva dissected in Schneider’s Drosophila Medium, removed of imaginal discs, transferred to fresh media, then rinsed once in cold PBST (PBS with 0.1% Tween 20). Samples were homogenized on ice in 30 μL of fresh PBST using a cordless motor and plastic disposable homogenizer, supplemented with 5X SDS loading buffer to 2X final concentration, boiled for 10 min at 95°C, then stored at −20°C or immediately resolved on a commercial 10% polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, #4568023). Protein concentration was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, #23227) as per manufacturer’s instructions and measured on a Synergy H1 Multi-Mode plate reader. Proteins were either transferred to a 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membrane (Amersham Protran, #10600001) or PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad, #1620177) by wet transfer in a buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 192 mM glycine, 10% methanol, and 0.02% SDS at 4°C. Membranes were blocked in 3% BSA or 5% dry milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline, 0.05% Tween 20), washed with TBST, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated for 2 h in secondary antibodies diluted 1:2500 in TBST. Bands were visualized with Clarity ECL substrate (Bio-Rad, #1705061) on a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad). Densitometry was measured in Fiji and protein levels were normalized to the loading control as described.115

The following primary antibodies were used: mouse anti-Actin JLA20 (1:1000, DHSB), mouse anti-Brat 3A9 (1:1000, gift from W. Shi, Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology, China;99), mouse anti-Orb2 2D11 and 4G8 (1:25 dilution each; DSHB), and mouse anti-Myc P4C4-B10 (1:10 dilution, DSHB). Secondary antibodies: goat anti-mouse HRP (1:2500 or 5000, ThermoFisher #31430) and goat anti-rabbit HRP (1:5000, ThermoFisher # 656120).

RT-qPCR

RNA was extracted from 20 third instar larval brains per biological replicate using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific #15596026) and treated with 1μL DNase (2 U/μL, Thermo Fisher Scientific #AM1907) prior to reverse transcription. 500 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA with Superscript IV Reverse Transcriptase following the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen #11–756-050). qPCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-time system with iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad #1725121). ROX passive reference dye (50X, Bio-Rad, #172–5858) was added at 0.2 μL per 10 μL reaction for fluorescence normalization. Values were normalized to Ribosomal protein L32 (rp49) expression levels. Cq values from the qPCR results were analyzed, and the expression levels for each gene relative to the control were calculated using the ΔΔCq method. Three biological replicates and three technical replicates were performed on a single 96-well plate. Primers used in this study are listed in the STAR RESOURCE TABLE and produced the following amplicons: brat, 72 bp; rp49, 75 bp; orb2, 83 bp; and Myc, 131 bp.

RNA immunoprecipitation

RNA immunoprecipitation was performed as described.116 Briefly, 50 third instar larval brains were dissected per biological replicate and homogenized on ice in 200 μL lysis buffer (10 mM Tris/Cl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 0.5% Triton X-100) supplemented with 1× EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche #11836170001, 0.4M Pefabloc (Sigma-Aldrich #11429868001), and 1 U/μL SUPERase⋅In RNase Inhibitor (New England Biolabs #AM2696). Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm, and the supernatant incubated with GFP-Trap beads (ChromoTek, #gtma-10) under gentle nutation for 1-h at 4°C. Beads were subsequently washed and resuspended in 120 μL lysis buffer. Fifty microliters of bead slurry was reserved and analyzed for protein content by western blotting. RNA was extracted from the remaining beads using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific #15596026) and treated with 1μL ezDNase (2 U/μL, Thermo Fisher Scientific #11766050) prior to PCR. 75 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA with Superscript IV VILO following the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen #11–756-050). PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad thermocycler with Q5 DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs #M0491). Primers were as described for qPCR. Gapdh1 primers generated a 200 bp product.
