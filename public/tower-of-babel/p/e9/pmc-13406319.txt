Sex-divergent metabolic pathways in the association between vitamin D status and metabolic-associated fatty liver disease
Authors: Kwang Min Kim, Mi Hyeon Jin, Jeong-Hyun Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vitamin D insufficiency has been linked to metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), but whether biological sex shapes this relationship remains poorly characterized. Building on a primary report that established this association in the pooled cohort, we tested whether the association and its candidate metabolic pathways differ by sex and, using age as a proxy for estrogen exposure, whether the association in women varies across the adult life course to clarify pathophysiological differences and therapeutic implications between men and women. In this cross-sectional secondary analysis, we analyzed 22,476 Korean adults with metabolic dysfunction (16,320 men and 6156 women). Sex-stratified multivariable logistic regression estimated the association between vitamin D insufficiency (<20 ng/mL) and MAFLD, and mediation analyses explored the contribution of individual metabolic pathways in each sex. Age was used as a proxy for estrogen status to test effect modification of the association in women. Vitamin D insufficiency was associated with higher MAFLD risk in both sexes, with a stronger association in women (odds ratio [OR]: 1.53, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.18–1.98, P = .001) than in men (OR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.22–1.43, P < .001). Insulin resistance was the predominant statistical mediator in both sexes but contributed more in women (58.6% vs 42.3% in men), whereas systemic inflammation was a significant mediator only in women (19.3%), and lipid metabolism and adiposity were more prominent in men. When age was used as an estrogen proxy, the association in women was numerically strongest in the youngest reproductive-age stratum and attenuated at older ages, although the vitamin D-by-age interaction was not statistically significant. Among women with high-risk alcohol consumption, the point estimate was higher (OR: 4.37, 95% CI: 1.79–10.67, P = .001), although this stratum was small (n = 308) and the estimate imprecise. Vitamin D insufficiency is associated with MAFLD in both sexes, but more strongly in women, and the candidate metabolic pathways differ between the sexes. These observations suggest that sex-specific strategies – targeting insulin sensitivity and inflammation in women and lipid metabolism and weight in men – may merit evaluation for MAFLD prevention and management. Because the design is cross-sectional, these associations are hypothesis-generating and require longitudinal confirmation.

1. Introduction

Metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) is a major and growing global health burden, characterized by hepatic fat accumulation in the setting of metabolic dysfunction. It is now the leading cause of chronic liver disease worldwide, with a prevalence of 20% to 30% in Western populations and 15% to 20% in Asian populations.[1] The shift in the diagnostic paradigm from nonalcoholic fatty liver disease to MAFLD reflects a growing recognition that metabolic dysfunction, rather than the mere absence of alcohol, is central to disease pathogenesis and progression.

Vitamin D, long recognized for its role in calcium homeostasis and skeletal health, is increasingly implicated in metabolic and immune regulation. The vitamin D receptor (VDR) is widely expressed across tissues, including the liver, and vitamin D insufficiency has been linked to several processes central to MAFLD pathogenesis, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and heightened inflammation.[2,3] Epidemiological studies have consistently reported an inverse association between vitamin D status and MAFLD, and meta-analytic estimates suggest that vitamin D deficiency increases the risk of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease by approximately 26%.[4]

In a recent large cross-sectional study, Lee et al examined the association between vitamin D insufficiency and MAFLD in 22,476 Korean adults with metabolic dysfunction.[5] That study provided robust evidence that vitamin D insufficiency is associated with MAFLD; in the multivariable analysis, the odds of MAFLD were 32% higher in men and 53% higher in women, with sex entered as a covariate rather than as an explicit effect modifier.

Both MAFLD and vitamin D metabolism show pronounced sex differences. Men have a higher prevalence of MAFLD than women during the reproductive years, whereas women are more susceptible to vitamin D insufficiency across diverse populations.[6,7] These patterns likely reflect sex-specific differences in body composition, hormonal milieu, and metabolic regulation. Sex steroids directly influence vitamin D biology: estrogen may enhance VDR expression and downstream signaling in women, whereas testosterone may modulate vitamin D bioavailability and metabolic function in men.[8,9] Because circulating estrogen declines sharply across the menopausal transition, adult age can serve as an indirect, population-level proxy for cumulative estrogen exposure; if estrogen amplifies hepatic vitamin D signaling, the vitamin D–MAFLD association in women might therefore be expected to vary with age.

Nonetheless, studies of sex-specific associations between vitamin D insufficiency and MAFLD remain limited and inconsistent. Some report stronger associations in men,[10] others in women,[11] and most either do not stratify by sex or treat sex merely as a covariate, potentially obscuring clinically important sex-specific mechanisms. Korea is a particularly informative setting for such work, given its high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency and rising incidence of MAFLD.[12,13] A population-based study in the United States reported that lower vitamin D concentrations were associated with greater severity of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis and with higher disease-specific mortality in nonalcoholic fatty liver disease,[14] yet how these effects differ by sex is unknown.

This secondary analysis was designed to extend, rather than restate, the primary report. Beyond reproducing a sex-stratified point estimate, it makes 4 contributions: it formally tests the sex difference with a vitamin D-by-sex interaction rather than through covariate adjustment; it decomposes the association into candidate metabolic pathways separately in each sex to determine whether the dominant pathway is itself sex-divergent; it uses age as a proxy for estrogen exposure to probe whether the female association tracks the menopausal transition; and it identifies candidate high-risk female subgroups to generate, rather than establish, precision-prevention hypotheses. Throughout, we frame these findings as associations observed within a cross-sectional design.

Accordingly, using the same dataset as the primary report, this analysis aimed to quantify sex differences in the vitamin D–MAFLD association; identify sex-specific metabolic correlates of vitamin D status; characterize the sex-specific metabolic pathways statistically mediating this association; explore the potential for sex-tailored risk assessment; and examine, using age as a proxy for cumulative estrogen exposure and menopausal status, whether the vitamin D–MAFLD association in women varies across the adult age range. By delineating these sex-specific patterns, we aim to inform tailored, testable prevention and management hypotheses and to support the development of precision medicine approaches to MAFLD.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and population

This cross-sectional secondary analysis is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement for cross-sectional studies and drew on the dataset of the cross-sectional study by Lee et al, which comprised 22,476 participants (16,320 men and 6156 women) who underwent comprehensive health examinations.[5] All participants had baseline metabolic dysfunction and a measured serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 concentration. The identical dataset was used without any modification to the inclusion or exclusion criteria. Restriction to adults with metabolic dysfunction is intrinsic to the MAFLD construct, which by definition requires hepatic steatosis together with metabolic dysfunction; the source population for any MAFLD study is therefore necessarily metabolically characterized. Accordingly, the present estimates apply to this clinically relevant population of metabolically at-risk adults rather than to the general population, a point that we revisit in the limitations. Minor differences in reported values relative to the primary report reflect our sex-stratified analytic approach (with statistics computed separately by sex from the outset) and rounding during processing, rather than any difference in the study sample. The protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board (IRB No. 2022-03-015), and all participants provided written informed consent.

To ensure diagnostic specificity, individuals with viral hepatitis, autoimmune liver disease, drug-induced liver injury, or excessive alcohol consumption (>210 g/week for men and >140 g/week for women) were excluded, as were participants with incomplete data on vitamin D status, anthropometry, or metabolic parameters.

2.2. Assessment of vitamin D status

Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 was measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (25-hydroxyvitamin D kit; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) on samples drawn after an overnight fast of at least 8 hours. To account for seasonal variation, the timing of examination (calendar year and quarter) was recorded and included as a covariate in all models. Vitamin D status was classified as sufficient (≥20 ng/mL) or insufficient (<20 ng/mL) according to Endocrine Society guidance,[15] and, for dose-response analyses, as severely deficient (<10 ng/mL), deficient (10–19.9 ng/mL), or sufficient (≥20 ng/mL).

2.3. MAFLD diagnosis

MAFLD was diagnosed using the international consensus criteria,[1] which require hepatic steatosis together with overweight/obesity, type 2 diabetes, or metabolic dysregulation. Hepatic steatosis was identified using the Fatty Liver Index (FLI), with a value of ≥60 indicating fatty liver. The FLI was calculated from triglycerides (TG), body mass index (BMI), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and waist circumference (WC) using the published equation and is a validated noninvasive marker of hepatic steatosis (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.84, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.81–0.87).[16] An alternative threshold (FLI ≥ 30) was used in sensitivity analyses to assess robustness.

2.4. Measurement of metabolic parameters

Height, weight, and WC were measured using standardized protocols, and BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Blood pressure was measured with an automated sphygmomanometer after at least 5 minutes of rest. Laboratory measurements included fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin, a lipid profile (total cholesterol, TG, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol), and liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and GGT), all assayed by standardized methods on automated analyzers. Insulin resistance was estimated by homeostasis model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), calculated as fasting insulin (microIU/mL) multiplied by fasting glucose (mg/dL) divided by 405.[17] Smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity (assessed with the International Physical Activity Questionnaire) were obtained from standardized questionnaires.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All analyses were stratified by sex. Continuous variables were compared with the Student’s t test or analysis of variance, and categorical variables with the chi-square test or the Fisher exact test, as appropriate. The association between vitamin D insufficiency and MAFLD was estimated using binary logistic regression in 4 sequential models: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (age and timing of examination), Model 3 (additionally smoking, alcohol, and physical activity), and Model 4 (additionally hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia). Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Subgroup analyses assessed the consistency of the association across clinically relevant strata, with interaction terms used to test for effect modification, and sensitivity analyses applied alternative definitions of vitamin D insufficiency and MAFLD. Analyses were performed in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and two-sided P values < .05 were considered significant.

To explore the estrogen hypothesis in the absence of direct hormone measurement, age was used as a proxy for cumulative estrogen exposure and menopausal status. In women, the vitamin D insufficiency–MAFLD association was examined across age categories (<40 years [reproductive, estrogen-replete], 40 to 49 years [menopausal transition], and ≥50 years [approximating postmenopausal status]) and with a formal vitamin D insufficiency-by-age interaction term, with age modeled both continuously (per 10 years) and dichotomously (≥50 years). Identical models were fitted in men as a specificity check. All effect-modification models retained the Model 4 covariate set.

2.5.1. Mediation analysis

Mediation analyses were used to estimate the contribution of individual metabolic pathways to the vitamin D–MAFLD association. The pathways were operationally defined as insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), systemic inflammation (a composite z-score of standardized alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and GGT), lipid metabolism (the TG/HDL-C ratio), and adiposity (WC, chosen over BMI given their high correlation, r > 0.85, to avoid multicollinearity). Indirect effects and 95% CIs were estimated with the Baron and Kenny approach using 5000 bootstrap iterations.

These mediation estimates rest on assumptions that cannot be verified in cross-sectional data, including correct temporal ordering (vitamin D status preceding the mediator, and the mediator preceding MAFLD) and the absence of unmeasured exposure-mediator, exposure-outcome, and mediator-outcome confounding. Because the candidate mediators are biologically intercorrelated (Supplementary Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1) and were each modeled in a separate single-mediator framework, the resulting proportions are overlapping rather than additive, may share variance, and should be interpreted as exploratory effect-decomposition signals rather than identified causal quantities.

3. Results

3.1. Sex differences in demographic and metabolic characteristics

Among the 22,476 participants (16,320 men and 6156 women), MAFLD prevalence and vitamin D status differed markedly by sex (Table 1). Men had a substantially higher prevalence of MAFLD than women (53.27% vs 12.96%, P < .001) but a more favorable vitamin D status. Women had lower mean serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 concentrations (16.67 ± 9.06 vs 18.23 ± 7.39 ng/mL in men, P < .001) and a higher prevalence of insufficiency (73.05% vs 65.07%, P < .001). Men nonetheless displayed a more adverse metabolic profile overall, with higher rates of hypertension and diabetes, higher TG, liver enzymes, and anthropometric measures, higher HOMA-IR, and lower HDL-C.

3.3. Association between vitamin D insufficiency and MAFLD

Vitamin D insufficiency was associated with a higher MAFLD risk in both sexes, more strongly in women (Table 3). After full adjustment, the odds of MAFLD were 53% higher in vitamin D-insufficient women (OR: 1.53, 95% CI: 1.18–1.98, P = .001) and 32% higher in men (OR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.22–1.43, P < .001). A dose-response relationship was evident in both sexes and was steeper in women: severe deficiency (<10 ng/mL) was associated with nearly double the odds of MAFLD in women (OR: 1.97, 95% CI: 1.42–2.73) versus an approximately 50% increase in men (OR: 1.49, 95% CI: 1.33–1.67).

3.4. Sex-specific mediation pathways

Mediation analysis using the predefined pathway variables revealed distinct sex-specific patterns of statistical mediation between vitamin D insufficiency and MAFLD (Fig. 1). In women, insulin resistance was the dominant mediator, accounting for 58.6% of the total effect, followed by systemic inflammation (19.3%) and lipid metabolism (16.2%). In men, the contributions were more evenly distributed – insulin resistance 42.3%, lipid metabolism 27.8%, and adiposity 14.4% – and systemic inflammation was not a significant mediator. As noted in Section 2.5.1, these proportions are exploratory and should not be read as additive or as established causal fractions.

3.5. Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses identified several effect modifications (Fig. 2). The association between vitamin D insufficiency and MAFLD was numerically largest among women with high-risk alcohol consumption (OR: 4.37, 95% CI: 1.79–10.67, P = .001); however, this stratum comprised only 308 women, and the wide CI indicates substantial imprecision. Therefore, the estimate should be regarded as exploratory. The association was also observed among nondiabetic women (OR: 1.55, 95% CI: 1.18–2.04, P = .002). In men, the association was strongest in those with viral hepatitis (OR: 1.97, 95% CI: 1.25–3.10) and in overweight or obese individuals (OR: 1.56, 95% CI: 1.42–1.71).

3.6. Age as a proxy for estrogen status: effect modification in women

Using age as a population-level proxy for cumulative estrogen exposure and menopausal status, the vitamin D insufficiency–MAFLD association in women was numerically strongest in the youngest reproductive-age stratum (age <40 years: OR: 2.65, 95% CI: 0.90–7.80) and was attenuated across the menopausal transition (age 40–49 years: OR: 1.03, 95% CI: 0.53–2.00); the ≥50-year (postmenopausal-proxy) stratum contained few women and few events in this relatively young cohort, yielding an imprecise estimate (OR: 1.89, 95% CI: 0.38–9.34) (Supplementary Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 2; Supplementary Figure 1, Supplemental Digital Content 3). The formal vitamin D insufficiency-by-age interaction was directionally consistent with estrogen amplification, with the association weakening as age increased (ratio of ORs, 0.67 per 10-year increment), but it did not reach statistical significance (P for interaction = .19 for continuous age; P = .78 for the ≥50-year contrast). No comparable age gradient was observed in men (P for interaction = .44). Together with the steeper dose-response in women shown in Table 3, these exploratory analyses are directionally compatible with an estrogen-amplified vitamin D–MAFLD pathway in women but are not confirmatory, a limitation chiefly attributable to the small number of older, postmenopausal women in this cohort.

4. Discussion

This cross-sectional secondary analysis was designed to extend, rather than restate, the primary report by Lee et al.[5] Its incremental contribution is mechanistic and sex-focused: it tests the sex difference formally as an interaction rather than adjusting for sex; it shows that the candidate metabolic pathways linking vitamin D insufficiency to MAFLD are themselves sex-divergent, with insulin resistance and inflammation more prominent in women and lipid metabolism and adiposity more prominent in men; and, following the life course of estrogen, it uses age as a proxy for estrogen exposure to probe whether the female association tracks the menopausal transition. We emphasize that these are associations observed within a cross-sectional design and that the value of the work lies in generating sharper, testable hypotheses for confirmatory studies rather than in establishing a causal mechanism.

In this cohort of 22,476 Korean adults with metabolic dysfunction, vitamin D insufficiency was associated with MAFLD in both sexes, more strongly in women, and the candidate metabolic pathways differed by sex.

The stronger association in women (OR: 1.53 vs 1.32 in men) is consistent with reports of greater vitamin D sensitivity in women,[18,19] which may reflect estrogen-mediated enhancement of VDR expression and downstream signaling.[8] Estrogen can upregulate VDR expression across tissues[20] and may interact with vitamin D in regulating inflammatory and insulin-signaling pathways,[21] potentially accounting for the prominence of these pathways in women. Our exploratory age analysis is consistent with this framing: the association was numerically strongest in the youngest estrogen-replete women and attenuated across the menopausal transition, the direction expected if estrogen amplifies hepatic vitamin D action. We caution, however, that the vitamin D-by-age interaction was not statistically significant and that the postmenopausal stratum was small; age is at best an indirect surrogate for hormonal status, so these patterns motivate, but cannot confirm, an estrogen-dependent mechanism. Direct measurement of sex steroids and menopausal status in adequately powered, age-balanced samples is needed.

Insulin resistance was the principal mediator in both sexes but contributed more in women, a pattern of potential clinical relevance given that vitamin D may improve insulin sensitivity through effects on pancreatic beta-cell function, insulin-receptor expression, and systemic inflammation,[22] possibly in synergy with estrogen in the regulation of glucose homeostasis and mitochondrial function.[23] We interpret the mediation proportions cautiously: because the design is cross-sectional and the candidate mediators are intercorrelated and modeled separately, these values are exploratory decompositions rather than established causal fractions, and the qualitative sex contrast – which pathway dominates in each sex – is the more dependable result.

That systemic inflammation mediated the association only in women is notable and may reflect sex differences in immune reactivity, as women generally mount more robust inflammatory and immune responses.[24] Given the well-described anti-inflammatory actions of vitamin D,[25] these properties may be especially relevant where inflammation contributes more to disease, as appears to be the case in women. Conversely, the greater contribution of lipid metabolism and adiposity in men is consistent with known sex differences in body composition and lipid handling,[26] and with the reported roles of vitamin D in adipogenesis, lipolysis, and fatty acid oxidation[27]; these remain associations rather than demonstrated mechanisms.

The larger point estimate for the association among women with high-risk alcohol consumption (OR: 4.37) may indicate an interaction between alcohol and vitamin D deficiency, but it rests on only 308 women with a very wide CI (1.79–10.67) and is therefore unstable and hypothesis-generating. Plausible contributors include impaired vitamin D metabolism and greater hepatic inflammation with alcohol,[28] together with the lower gastric alcohol dehydrogenase activity and higher body fat percentage of women.[29] This signal should be confirmed in larger samples before any clinical inference is drawn.

Clinically, these findings tentatively suggest that vitamin D screening and supplementation strategies may ultimately need to be sex aware and that interventions targeting insulin sensitivity and inflammation in women versus lipid metabolism and weight in men are reasonable hypotheses for future trials. We stress that these are directions for evaluation rather than current recommendations.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and cannot establish temporal ordering; the mediation results, in particular, should be read as statistical decomposition under unverifiable assumptions rather than as evidence of a mechanism. Second, the cohort was restricted, by the MAFLD definition itself, to adults with metabolic dysfunction, so the findings generalize to metabolically at-risk adults rather than to the general population, and conditioning on a metabolic-dysfunction-defined population could, in principle, bias associations in either direction (for example, through collider effects). Third, several relevant confounders were unavailable and could not be adjusted for, including sunlight or ultraviolet exposure, dietary vitamin D and total energy intake, vitamin D and calcium supplement use, menopausal status, exogenous hormone use, and circulating sex-steroid concentrations. Although examination timing (year and quarter) was modeled to address seasonal variation in 25-hydroxyvitamin D, residual seasonal and behavioral confounding may remain, and these gaps are especially pertinent to the sex-specific and estrogen-proxy analyses. Fourth, age is an imperfect proxy for estrogen status, and the older, postmenopausal female stratum was small, limiting power for the interaction tests. Fifth, MAFLD was defined using the FLI rather than imaging or biopsy, although the FLI is a validated surrogate.[16] Finally, our findings derive from a Korean population and may not generalize fully to other ethnic groups. Prospective studies with repeated measures of vitamin D, sex steroids, and the candidate mediators are needed to test temporality and the estrogen hypothesis directly.

5. Conclusion

This study describes distinct sex differences in the association between vitamin D insufficiency and MAFLD, with different metabolic pathways predominating in each sex. Vitamin D insufficiency was associated with a higher MAFLD risk in both sexes but more strongly in women. Using age as a proxy for estrogen exposure, the association in women was strongest in the youngest reproductive-age stratum and attenuated at older ages – a pattern directionally consistent with, but not confirming, an estrogen-amplified mechanism. These observations suggest that sex-specific approaches may benefit MAFLD prevention and management. Because the analysis is cross-sectional, the associations are hypothesis-generating; future research should employ longitudinal designs with direct hormonal measurement to test these mechanisms and to evaluate the efficacy of sex-specific interventions in MAFLD prevention and treatment.
