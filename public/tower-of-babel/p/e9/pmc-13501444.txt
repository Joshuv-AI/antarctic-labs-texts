Epidemiology of asbestosis in the Netherlands: a 10-year cohort analysis
Authors: Illaa Smesseim, Hugo Schouwink, Jan C. Grutters, Hans Kromhout, Dick Heederik, Jacobus A. Burgers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsbestosis is a progressive interstitial lung disease caused by inhalation of asbestos fibres. Although asbestos use was banned in the Netherlands in 1993, new cases continue to be diagnosed. Since 2014, a national compensation system has enabled systematic assessment of asbestosis incidence and outcomes. This population-based study examined national trends in asbestosis incidence from 2014–2024 and evaluated survival and prognostic factors.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective national cohort study including all adults (≥18 years) diagnosed with asbestosis between 2014–2024 according to Dutch Health Council criteria used for compensation. Diagnoses were established by consensus of three independent pulmonologists based on radiological evidence of diffuse pulmonary fibrosis, ≥5 fibre-years of exposure, and lung function impairment according to American Medical Association (AMA) criteria. Demographic data, lung function, work history and overall survival were collected. Incidence per 100 000 persons per year was calculated.ResultsOf 1004 applicants, 476 (47.4%) met criteria for asbestosis. Median age at diagnosis was 77 years, and 98.9% were male. Annual incidence ranged from 0.115 to 0.347 per 100 000, with no significant temporal trend (Spearman rho 0.41; p=0.214). AMA class 4 was most common (44.5%) and strongly associated with mortality (hazard ratio 2.52, 95% CI 1.81–3.49). Older age also predicted poorer survival. Median survival ranged from 25.5 months (AMA 4) to 83.4 months (AMA 0–2). Time-dependent modelling showed increasing hazard over time for AMA class 4.ConclusionsMore than two decades after the asbestos ban, asbestosis incidence remains stable. AMA class and age are key predictors of survival.

Background

Asbestosis is a rare pneumoconiosis caused by the inhalation of asbestos fibres [1]. In 2017, an incidence of 1400 asbestosis cases (confidence interval (CI) 1220–1600) was reported in western Europe [2]. This accounted for approximately 16% of all pneumoconiosis cases worldwide [2–6]. The overall age-adjusted asbestosis mortality rate in the United States is 4.1 per million persons per year [7].

The diagnosis of asbestosis is based on the Helsinki criteria, which integrate clinical signs, radiological findings, occupational asbestos exposure, latency and lung function, while excluding alternative causes [8, 9]. An invasive procedure to achieve a histological procedure can be avoided because in most cases the diagnosis is established by the combination of radiological findings, clinical signs, occupational history and exclusion of alternative diagnoses (Helsinki criteria) [1]. When these criteria do not result in a clinical diagnosis the following histological criteria are essential for a confident diagnosis: identification of diffuse interstitial fibrosis plus the presence of either two or more asbestos bodies in tissue with a section area of 1 cm2. The severity of asbestosis-related lung function impairment can be classified using the American Medical Association (AMA) system [10]. This system integrates spirometric measures (forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and FEV1/FVC ratio), diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and exercise capacity (VO2max) to categorise impairment from class 1 (minimal or no impairment) to class 4 (severe impairment) [10].

Understanding the diagnosis of asbestosis is important not only for clinical management but also for determining eligibility in compensation programs, especially in countries with strict asbestos regulations like the Netherlands. The use of asbestos has increasingly been restricted worldwide since the 1980s [11]. As of 1 July 1993, the Netherlands, like many other European countries, has completely prohibited the use, storage, sale and import of asbestos [12–17].

Since 2014, a compensation system has been in place in the Netherlands to provide asbestosis victims with financial support. One of the criteria applied is a threshold of 5 fibre-years of asbestos exposure according to the risk matrix [18]. The present study is based on data obtained through this compensation procedure.

The primary aim of this population-based study is to describe asbestosis incidence in the Netherlands over time in the general population 30 years after the nationwide asbestos ban. The secondary objective is to describe survival of asbestosis patients.

Methods

Study population

This is a retrospective national cohort study of adult patients (≥18 years) who were diagnosed with asbestosis from 1 January 2014 to 1 January 2025 according to the Dutch Health Council advice on criteria for asbestosis for compensation purposes [19].

The study population was drawn from a nationwide cohort of individuals who applied to the Institute for Asbestos Victims for compensation due to asbestosis. We included all confirmed adult patients who have been diagnosed with asbestosis according to the asbestosis panel and received financial compensation. Incidence was defined by the date of compensation application. Three independent Dutch pulmonologists with expertise in asbestos-related diseases were pseudo-randomly selected from a pool of experts who regularly perform these reviews to evaluate each application. Reviewers were rotated between groups every 3 months using a computer system (ALEA Clinical), ensuring random assignment and reducing potential selection bias [20]. They assessed applications based on the following criteria: 1) diffuse lung fibrosis detected on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) or lung biopsy with fibrotic abnormalities; 2) history of at least 5 fibre-years of asbestos exposure, occurring at least 15 years before the onset of typical symptoms; and 3) loss of lung function, classified according to the AMA criteria (supplementary material) [10]. The asbestosis diagnosis was considered positive if at least two out of the three pulmonologists confirmed that all criteria were met. Compared with the Helsinki criteria, our study used a strict threshold of fibre-years and AMA-classified lung function, providing a structured approach for the Dutch compensation program. In contrast, the Helsinki criteria are more flexible, integrating clinical signs, radiology, occupational exposure and latency, with histology required only if these features are inconclusive [1]. Lung function impairment was classified according to the AMA system [10]. The following parameters were used: FVC, FEV1, FEV1/FVC, DLCO and VO2max [10]. Each parameter was compared with its respective AMA threshold, and the highest impairment class across all parameters was assigned to the patient. Class 1 indicates minimal or no impairment and class 4 indicates severe impairment [10]. Detailed thresholds are provided in supplementary material.

Data collection and statistical analysis

The following characteristics were retrieved: age at diagnosis confirmed by the asbestosis panel, sex, smoking status, AMA criteria for loss of lung function (AMA class) [10], biopsy taken and presence of asbestos bodies in the biopsies, lung function tests (FVC, DLCO, carbon monoxide transfer coefficient (KCO), total lung capacity (TLC) and Tiffeneau index), total work-years and overall survival. Overall survival was defined as time from date of diagnosis according to the asbestosis panel until death. Lung function was collected as close to the date of diagnosis as possible (within 3 months). AMA class was derived from either the FVC or the DLCO; the lowest value determined class of lung function loss.

Incidence rates were calculated per 100 000 persons per year of diagnosis and by age at diagnosis. The trend in the overall incidence of asbestosis in the Netherlands between 2014 and 2024 was assessed using a Spearman rank correlation. Descriptive statistics were used to describe incidence data and patient characteristics. Overall survival was analysed using the Cox proportional hazards model to evaluate the association of covariates with overall survival. Covariates included AMA class (grouped 0–2, 3 and 4), age (in months), sex and smoking status. Median survival times and interquartile ranges (IQRs, 25th–75th percentiles) were estimated for each AMA class group. Smoking status was included as a covariate in the Cox proportional hazards model. For patients with missing smoking information (30.2% of the cohort), a separate “unknown” category was created. Patients in this category were included in the analysis to preserve sample size and avoid selection bias. No imputation was performed for missing smoking data. The proportional hazards assumption was formally assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. In cases where the proportional hazards assumption was violated, a time-dependent Cox model was applied, incorporating interactions between the relevant covariate and a function of time (√time). Hazard ratios were derived from the Cox models. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated for descriptive purposes to illustrate median survival and IQR by AMA class, but statistical inference was based on the Cox models.

All analyses were conducted using R (v.4.2.1). Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the Netherlands Cancer Institute (IRB no. IRBd19-136).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between 1 January 2014 and 1 January 2025, a total of 1004 individuals applied for compensation because of presumed asbestosis. Among these, 476 (47.4%) were identified as having asbestosis according to the Dutch Health Council criteria for asbestosis (figure 1). The median age of all asbestosis patients was 77.0 years (IQR 71.0–82.0). The population consisted almost entirely of men (98.9%), and the majority were former smokers (56.3%). A total of 44.5% of patients presented with AMA class 4 lung function loss. Only 54 out of 476 patients (11.3%) had undergone a lung biopsy. Annual incidence

The overall incidence of asbestosis in the Netherlands between 2014 and 2024 was 0.241 cases per 100 000 persons per year of diagnosis. The lowest incidence was observed in 2020 (0.155 per 100 000), followed by a peak in 2022 (0.347 per 100 000; figure 2). A Spearman rank correlation showed no significant change in the incidence of asbestosis (Spearman's rho=0.41; p=0.214)

Overall survival

In the standard Cox proportional hazards model, AMA class 4 was associated with a significantly higher hazard of death compared with AMA class 0–2 (hazard ratio (HR) 2.52, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.81–3.49; p<0.001; figure 3). Age was also significantly associated with increased hazard, with a HR of 2.35 per year (95% CI 1.90–2.91; p<0.001), while sex and smoking status were not significant (p=0.499 and p=0.324, respectively). Median overall survival was 83.4 months (IQR 39.1–163.5) for AMA class 0–2, 54.8 months (25.4–94.7) for AMA class 3, and 25.5 months (9.8–57.3) for AMA class 4. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using the Schoenfeld residual test. The global Schoenfeld test had a p-value of 0.0878, suggesting that, overall, the model was borderline for proportional hazards. The observed violation was primarily driven by AMA class (p=0.0077), indicating a violation of the proportional hazards assumption for this covariate. In contrast, the proportional hazards assumption was not violated for sex (p=0.6424), age (p=0.3600) or smoking status (p=0.8669).

Therefore, a time-dependent Cox model was applied, showing that the hazard for AMA class 4 increased over time (time-dependent HR 1.067 per √time unit; p=0.005), while AMA class 3 did not reach significance (HR 1.035; p=0.197; figure 4). These results highlight that both age and AMA class are important determinants of survival, with AMA class 4 demonstrating an increasing risk over time.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that the annual incidence of asbestosis ranged from 0.115 to 0.347 per 100.000 persons between 2014–2024, with the highest rates observed in 2022 and the lowest in 2020. Patients with AMA class 4 at diagnosis had the poorest survival (median 25.5 months, IQR 9.8–57.3) compared with AMA class 3 (median 54.8 months, IQR 25.4–94.7) and 0–2 (median 83.4 months, IQR 39.1–163.5). In Cox regression, AMA class 4 and age were independently associated with higher hazard of death, whereas sex and smoking status were not. The proportional hazards assumption was violated for AMA class, so a time-dependent Cox model was applied, showing that the hazard for AMA class 4 increased over time, whereas AMA class 3 was not significant.

The annual incidence between 2014 and 2024 in the Netherlands differs from international data. According to the Global Burden of Disease study, the age-standardised incidence of asbestosis in western Europe decreased from 0.28 cases per 100 000 persons in 1990 to 0.14 cases per 100 000 persons in 2017 [2]. In high-income North America, the rate in 2017 was 0.62 cases per 100 000 persons. In Australasia, it was 0.48 cases per 100 000 persons. The Dutch rates fall between the western Europe average and the rate in Australasia. They are higher than the western Europe rate in 2017 [2]. This difference could reflect the surge of applications to the national compensation program introduced in 2014. That is why the annual incidence results should be interpreted in the context of the Dutch asbestosis compensation scheme, which was introduced in 2014. The higher incidence observed in the early years may reflect applications from patients with pre-existing pulmonary fibrosis seeking compensation, rather than new cases. Our study only captures individuals who applied for compensation and met the eligibility criteria, such as the 5 fibre-year exposure threshold. Patients who are unaware of the program, have milder disease or do not meet the exposure criteria are systematically excluded. This may have introduced selection bias in both incidence and survival estimates. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as the incidence of compensated asbestosis, rather than the true population-based incidence. The decrease in the number of diagnoses in 2020 and the subsequent peak in the following years can be explained by the COVID-19 pandemic and the associated restrictions in 2020. As observed in pleural mesothelioma, fewer patients presented to the hospital during this period, resulting in a temporary reduction in diagnosed cases in 2020 [21].

Following the 1993 legislation, which had been preceded by an earlier ban on blue asbestos (crocidolite) in 1977, asbestos was no longer used in production processes, although it remained present in various consumer goods and products. Accordingly, we anticipated a decline in the incidence of asbestosis during our study period.

Although the asbestosis incidence reached a low point in 2020 during the pandemic, it rose again in the following years, peaking in 2022 before beginning to decline. However, the Spearman rank correlation showed no significant change in the incidence of asbestosis (Spearman's rho=0.41; p=0.214) between 2014 and 2024.

A limitation of this study is its retrospective design, which led to incomplete clinical data for some patients. Furthermore, the diagnosis of asbestosis, based on the Dutch Health Council criteria, is susceptible to interobserver variability. In this study, diagnoses were made by consensus among three pulmonologists. Involving additional clinicians in the consensus diagnosis might further improve diagnostic reliability.

Also, our findings have a limited generalisability to healthcare systems outside the Netherlands, as the study is based on a Dutch compensation program for asbestosis with a dedicated panel of pulmonologists. For instance, the minimum number of fibre-years required for a diagnosis of asbestosis may differ internationally, which could affect the incidence numbers. Another limitation of our study is that smoking status was unknown for 30.2% of patients. These patients were included in the survival analysis as a separate “unknown” category. However, residual confounding by smoking cannot be excluded. Missing information may have affected the estimated associations between smoking and survival. Lastly, information on comorbidities was not available in the national database. Therefore, survival analyses could not be adjusted for potential confounding by coexisting comorbidities.

This study has several strengths. It included a large national cohort over 10 years. The long follow-up allowed us to perform reliable survival analyses and identify important prognostic factors, such as AMA class at diagnosis according to the asbestosis panel and age. Future research should aim to improve early detection, refine diagnostic criteria and test interventions that could slow disease progression or improve outcomes in high-risk patients. Future research should focus on improving early detection and refining diagnostic criteria to reduce variability between observers. Internationally standardised criteria will allow better comparisons between countries. Studies should assess whether cumulative asbestos exposure for example also adds prognostic value. This could help identify high-risk patients and guide interventions to slow disease progression and improve outcomes.
