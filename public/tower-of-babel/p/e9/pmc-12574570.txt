Mechanisms of Talaromyces marneffei induced CNS injury: Synergistic roles of tauopathy, pyroptosis, and microglial inflammation
Authors: Zongxiang Yuan, Fengyi Wang, Hailun Wei, Xuanrong Li, Wudi Wei, Shengrui Luo, Yukai Zhang, Xueqin Wei, Gang Wang, Hao Liang, Junjun Jiang, Li Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTalaromyces marneffei (T. marneffei) is an opportunistic, dimorphic fungus that has been clinically reported to involve the central nervous system (CNS). However, its neuroinvasive capacity and the underlying pathogenic mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, T. marneffei infection was found to impair learning, memory, and motor balance in mice, as demonstrated by Morris water maze and accelerating rotarod assays. Viable T. marneffei was subsequently isolated from hippocampus and cortex through fungal cultures, confirming its ability to invade the CNS. Histopathological analysis revealed CNS damage following infection, characterized by neuronal pyknosis, neurofibrillary tangles, and an increased presence of glial cells in the cortex. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, transcriptomic profiling showed significant enrichment of neurodegeneration pathways, with elevated Tau and GSDMD expression in hippocampal and cortical regions as well as N2a cells. Additionally, neuronal damage was exacerbated by indirect neurotoxicity mediated by microglial M1 polarization, accompanied by increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β). In conclusion, our findings uncover for the first time a dual-pathogenic mechanism by which T. marneffei induces CNS injury: direct neuronal damage via Tau pathology and GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis and indirect inflammation-mediated injury via M1-polarized microglia. These insights not only advance our understanding of fungal neuroinvasion but also provide a foundation for the development of targeted therapies.Keyword: Talaromyces marneffei, central nervous system, neurodegeneration, pyroptosis, microglia inflammation

Introduction

Talaromyces Marneffei (T. marneffei), previously identified as Penicillium Marneffei [1], is a dimorphic fungus that induces systemic mycosis [2], predominantly infecting immunocompromised individuals, particularly those with HIV-1/AIDS [3]. Compared to the majority of AIDS-related complications, co-infection with T. marneffei is associated with a significantly higher mortality rate [4], and represents the top five causes of HIV-related death in Southeast Asia [5]. Furthermore, T. marneffei can also affect non-HIV-infected individuals with compromised immune systems, including organ transplant recipients receiving immunosuppressive agents, cancer patients undergoing targeted therapy etc. [6]. Recently, some reports have documented instances of T. marneffei infection in individuals without compromised immune systems [7,8]. It is worth noting that among immunocompetent patients, the proportion of T. marneffei infection in the pathogens of pulmonary fungal infection increased from 0.65% in 2013 to 4.63% in 2019 [7]. Therefore, T. marneffei has emerged as an increasingly important public health concern, posing a threat to both immunocompromised and non-immunocompromised individuals.

T. marneffei is a highly invasive pathogen capable of causing disseminated systemic infections [9], affecting multiple organs including the skin, liver, spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow, and gastrointestinal tract [10]. The lungs are the most commonly involved, with patients frequently experiencing respiratory symptoms such as dyspnea following infection [7]. Additionally, individuals infected with T. marneffei frequently exhibit clinical manifestations such as fever, anemia, fungal sepsis, general discomfort, and weight loss [11]. Recent case reports suggest that T. marneffei infection may also result in neurological symptoms including headache, dizziness, vomiting, and altered consciousness, indicating potential involvement of the central nervous system (CNS) of the host [12]. However, there remains a significant lack of studies – both domestically and internationally – regarding the effects of T. marneffei on the CNS. Moreover, current evidence is insufficient to determine whether the pathogen can cross the blood-brain barrier and directly cause CNS damage.

The innate immune response serves as the first line of defense against exogenous pathogenic microorganisms. Previous studies have demonstrated that T. marneffei infection predominantly occurs in immunocompromised individuals, highlighting the critical role of innate immune defense mechanisms in controlling infection [13]. T. marneffei primarily targets monocytes and macrophages, where it proliferates and disseminates via the endoplasmic reticulum system [14]. Macrophages, as the “frontline sentinels” of the innate immune system, rapidly recognize T. marneffei upon entry into the host and initiate a phagocytic response [15]. Previous studies have demonstrated that several fungal pathogens, such as Cryptococcus neoformans, possess the capacity to disseminate to the CNS either as free organisms or within macrophages [16]. Macrophages occupy a pivotal role in this process: on the one hand, they may serve as cellular vehicles that enable pathogens to traverse the blood brain barrier (BBB) via a so-called “Trojan horse” strategy [17]; on the other hand, upon fungal activation, macrophages can secrete pro-inflammatory mediators that drive neuroinflammatory cascades, thereby contributing to inflammatory injury [18]. These dual and context-dependent functions underscore the importance of macrophages in shaping CNS pathology during fungal infections. However, the precise mechanisms by which T. marneffei causes CNS injury remain unclear.

Therefore, we establish both a mouse model and an in vitro cell model of T. marneffei infection to investigate whether the pathogen can invade the brain parenchyma and to elucidate the pathogenic molecular mechanisms underlying CNS damage. Our findings demonstrate that T. marneffei can infiltrate the CNS, inducing GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis and Tau pathology in neurons and brain parenchyma, triggering microglia M1 polarization to release a large amount of cytotoxic factors, and subsequently causing neuronal damage. The findings offer potential therapeutic targets and strategies for clinical intervention in CNS injury caused by T. marneffei infection.

Materials and methods

Ethic statement

Animal care and experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of Guangxi Medical University (approval number: 202,110,004). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility. All procedures, including euthanasia, complied with the 2020 American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. The study was conducted between October 2021 and October 2024.

Fungal strain

T. marneffei strain (ATCC18224) was obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and used in all infection experiments [19]. The strain was grown on Sabouraud’s dextrose agar (SDA) and Potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium, and subsequently stored at 4°C. To obtain T. marneffei conidia, the strain was cultured on SDA and PDA at 25 °C for 10–14 days. Conidia were collected by flooding the culture surface with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the concentration was quantified using a hemocytometer. Lactophenol cotton blue staining was employed to visualize both T. marneffei conidia and fungal morphology.

Animal experiments

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology. Mice were raised in sterile facilities at the Guangxi Medical University Laboratory Animal Center. Mice (8-week-old, weighing approximately 18 g) were randomly divided into two groups, with 5 mice in each group. The mice in the infection group were intravenously injected with 200 microliters of T. marneffei conidia at a dose of 2 × 107 colony-forming units per microliter, while the control group received an equivalent volume of saline injection. Immediately, mice were intraperitoneally injected with the immunosuppressant cyclophosphamide (CTX, 200 mg/kg) every three days for a total of four injections. The two groups of mice were housed in separate cages and provided with unrestricted access to food, and exposed to the alternating light between day and night for each 12 hours. The ambient temperature ranged from 21°C to 23°C, while the relative humidity varied between 24% and 28% and the infection model was successfully established after 14 days (Patent Number: CN202210338790.9).

Morris water maze (MWM)

The Morris water maze (MWM) experiments were conducted in a circular white pool measuring 100 cm in diameter and 40 cm in depth. Four distinct markers were strategically placed at the tangent points of the pool, dividing it into four quadrants. A cylindrical escape platform (15 cm in diameter) was positioned in one quadrant, 15 cm from the pool’s edge and submerged approximately 1 cm below the water surface. The platform location remained constant throughout the experiment.

During the localization navigation phase, each mouse underwent continuous training for five days. Each training session began by placing a mouse facing one of the markers within any quadrant. If the mouse located and remained on the platform for more than 2 seconds within 60 seconds, the trial was concluded. After each trial, mice were guided back to their platforms and allowed to rest for 20 seconds before the next trials. Daily training sessions were conducted in all four quadrants, with escape latencies and swimming trajectories recorded for each trial. All tests were performed at approximately consistent times each day.

On day six, the spatial exploration experiments commenced by removing the escape platform. Mice were individually placed in a quadrant facing the corresponding marker as their starting point. The number of times they crossed the original platform location within 60 seconds was recorded to calculate an average representative value across all four quadrants.

Rotarod test

Five mice were placed on separate runways of the Rota-rod training instrument for each trial. The rod initially rotated at a speed of 5rpm/min and uniformly accelerated to 40rpm/min after a 10-second adaptation period, with the total trial duration set at 300 seconds. The trial was terminated if a mouse fell off the rod or gripped the rotating rod for more than two consecutive revolutions within testing period, and both the latency to fall and the rotational speed at the time of falling were recorded. If a mouse remained on the rod after 300 seconds, its duration was recorded as such while its speed remained at 40rpm/min. Between trials, the rod was thoroughly cleaned with 75% alcohol and deodorant spray to eliminate residual odors, and a 5-minute interval was maintained before initiating the next trial.

Mice brain tissue preparation and staining

Mice were infected with T. marneffei via tail vein injection for 14 days, while the control group was injected with an equal volume of normal saline. For euthanasia, mice were first anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of urethane (1.2 g/kg) [20,21]. After the complete loss of reflexes and the absence of corneal reflex were confirmed, euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia, in accordance with the 2020 American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Following confirmation of death, thoracotomy was performed and transcardial perfusion was initiated. A perfusion needle was inserted into the apex of the heart and secured with a hemostat, the right atrium was cut, and pre-cooled saline was perfused at a constant flow rate of ~1 mL every 5 seconds (~12 mL/min), with a total volume of 50 mL over 4 minutes. Successful perfusion was validated by liver blanching and the appearance of clear effluent from the right atrium, ensuring the exclusion of vascular contamination. The entire brain was then rapidly removed from ice, and the hippocampal and cortical tissues were carefully dissected.

Brain tissues were homogenized and then inoculated onto PDA medium and incubated at 25°C and 37°C, respectively. Once colonies appeared, clean tape was used to collect them, which were subsequently stained with lactophenol cotton blue (LPCB) staining reagent according to the established protocol. Paraffin sections were prepared from the brain tissues of both groups of mice, followed by hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining as per the standard procedure.

RNA-Seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the cerebral cortex of control (n = 5) and T. marneffei-infected (n = 5) mice. RNA samples were pooled into two biological replicates (n = 2) per group for RNA-Seq analysis. Trimmomatic software was utilized for quality control analysis of the original data, while HTSeq was employed to quantify gene expression levels. Subsequently, the gene expression matrix underwent normalization using the “DESeq2” package in R (4.4.1), followed by differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with |fold change| ≥ 1.5 and the adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05. DEG analysis, cluster analysis, KEGG enrichment analysis, and immune infiltration analysis were conducted using R.

Cells culture and treatments

BV2 microglia cells and neuroblastoma N2a cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences. The cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (GIBCO, USA) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were cultured in a medium at 37 °C, under a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

The primers were designed and optimized for quantitative RT-qPCR experiments. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, China), using the manufacturers’ instructions. The concentration and quality of RNA samples were assessed using a NanoDrop® ND-2000 spectrophotometer. TaKaRa 5X PrimeScript TM RT Master Mix-RR036A reverse transcription kit (TaKaRa, Japan) was used as manufacturers’ instructions. RT-qPCR analysis was conducted using SYBR® Premix ExTaqTM (TAKARA, Japan) on the StepOnePlus Real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All qRT-PCR plate amplifications followed the thermal cycler steps: 95°C for 30 seconds followed by 95°C for 15 seconds; followed by 60°C annealing temperature for 1 minute, repeating this final step for 40 cycles.

Protein extraction and western blot

Total protein was extracted from the tissues from mice or cells with RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor mixture (Solarbio, China). And the protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Concentration Assay kit (Beyotime, China). Subsequently, proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After blocking with 5% nonfat milk, specific primary antibodies against Caspase-1, Caspase-3, Gasdermin D (GSDMD), Tau46, and β-actin were incubated with the cells overnight at 4°C. This was followed by incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 hour. Images were acquired and analyzed using an Odyssey CLX two-color infrared laser imaging system (LI-COR, USA). β-actin served as the internal control.

Cells viability

Logarithmic phase cells were inoculated into cell culture plates and randomly divided into the infected group and control group. The infected group was infected with T. marneffei (cells: T. marneffei conidia = 1:10), while the control group was supplemented with an equal volume of cell culture medium. Subsequently, the total cell viability was assessed using the CCK8 kit, AnnexinV-APC/PI flow cytometry was employed to determine the rate of cell apoptosis, and JC-1 kit was utilized to measure early-stage cellular apoptosis.

Cells co-culture experiments

BV2 microglia cells were inoculated into 12-well plates and allowed to adhere completely, and the bacteria were stained as described above based on a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. N2a cells in optimal condition were seeded in nested cell chambers accompanying the well plates. Following a 6-hour incubation period, the BV2 cell plates were removed, supernatant was discarded, and cells were washed with PBS before fresh complete medium was added. The N2a cells were also washed with PBS and then co-cultured with BV2 cells by mounting them in the wells for a duration of 48 hours.

Statistical analysis

The mean ± SD was used to present all data. Statistical analysis was performed using the t-test, and GraphPad Prism 8.0 was utilized for data visualization. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Impact of T. marneffei infection on spatial learning, memory, and motor coordination in mice

To investigate the potential impact of T. marneffei infection on the CNS, we established a mouse model using 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice infected with T. marneffei. Next, MWM and Rotarod test were subsequently employed to evaluate the spatial learning and memory, as well as motor coordination, respectively. In the MWM task, both groups exhibited decreased escape latency over successive training days; however, the infected group showed significantly prolonged escape latency compared to the control group (Figure 1(A), p < 0.05). Moreover, during the probe trial, the frequency with which infected crossed the former platform location was significantly lower than that of control mice (Figure 1(B), p < 0.01). The results from Rotarod test showed that T. marneffei-infected mice exhibited a significant reduction in residence time on the rod, distance traveled, and rod speed prior to falling (Figure 1(C), p < 0.05).

Representative movement trajectories of two groups of mice in space exploration experiment (right). B: T. marneffei infection reduces the number of times mice swim across the escape platform. C: Effect of T. marneffei infection on motor balance in mice was compared based on the duration of time on the rod, distance rotated, and speed of rotation during falling. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01.

T. marneffei is capable of invading mice brain tissue and causing hippocampal and cortical damage

We then performed hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining of the hippocampal and cortical tissues from both T. marneffei infected and control groups. The results revealed that, unlike the control group whose CA1 hippocampal neurons were well-organized, compact, and structurally intact without nuclear pyknosis, the infected group exhibited fewer normal neurons in the CA1 region, with some showing nuclear pyknosis and disorganized, entangled nerve fibers; and similar neuronal conditions were also observed in the cortex of the infected group (Figure 2(A)).

Given that T. marneffei is a temperature-sensitive biphasic fungus that elicits profound systemic infection, the Potato Dextrose Agar culture medium was inoculated with brain tissues from two groups of mice and incubated at 25°C and 37°C, respectively. After one week of cultivation, the cerebral cortex and hippocampus of mice infected with T. marneffei displayed rose-red villous colonies in the 25°C incubator; and gray-white cheese-like colonies were observed in the culture medium after five days of cultivation at a temperature of 37°C (Figure 2(B)). Lactophenol cotton blue (LPCB) stained smears were next performed separately on the growing colonies, and the results found that the smears of colonies cultivated at 25°C exhibited a substantial abundance of elongated mycelium featuring broom-shaped branches, as well as round or oval conidia located at their termini; while the smears of colonies cultivated at 37°C demonstrated yeast-like conidia, which varied in shape from round to elongated, with some resembling sausages (Figure 2(C)). These results suggest that T. marneffei is capable of directly invading brain tissue from the peripheral system.

T. marneffei causes N2a neuronal injury through direct invasion and indirect microglial-mediated mechanisms

To investigate whether T. marneffei induces neuronal damage through direct interaction or indirectly via activation of immune cells such as microglia, we generated GFP-labeled T. marneffei and co-cultured the neuronal cell line N2a either directly with T. marneffei or indirectly with T. marneffei-infected BV2 microglial cells using Transwell inserts for 48 hours in vitro.

Initially, N2a cells were directly co-cultured with GFP-labeled T. marneffei, and the fluorescence microscopy demonstrated the ability of T. marneffei to directly invade N2a cells (Figure 3(A)). And CCK8 assay results demonstrated a significant decrease in cell viability by 32% in the N2a cells directly co-cultured with T. marneffei group compared to the control group (Figure 3(B), p < 0.001). Flow cytometry analysis also revealed a high apoptosis rate of N2a cells in the infected group (Figure 3(C), p < 0.001), while JC-1 staining indicated a statistically significant reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential within the T. marneffei co-cultured group (Figure 3(D), p < 0.01). Additionally, co-culture experiments involving N2a and T. marneffei-infected BV2 cells also showed a notable decrease in N2a cell viability as determined by flow cytometry analysis and CCK8 assay (Figure 3(E,F), p < 0.05).

Transcriptomic analysis reveals neurodegeneration-related pathways in T. marneffei-infected cortical tissue of mice

We then initially applied RNA sequencing analysis to cortical tissue of mice for exploring the possible mechanisms of neuronal damage induced by T. marneffei. A total of 844 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on predefined criteria, including 435 upregulated and 409 downregulated genes (Figure 4(A), Table S2). Clustering analysis revealed distinct gene expression profiles between infected and control groups, and the gene name of Top 30 DEGs were listed in Figure 4(B). And KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the DEGs were potentially associated with neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer disease, Pathways of neurodegeneration-multiple diseases, Huntington disease, Prion disease, and Parkinson disease (Figure 4(C)).

T. marneffei infection induces Tau pathology and pyroptosis in mice hippocampus and cortex

To validate T. marneffei-induced neurodegeneration and identify potential forms of neuronal damage, we examined the expression of neuronal microtubule-associated protein Tau46, the pyroptosis-related proteins Caspase-1 and GSDMD, and the apoptosis-related protein Caspase-3 in the Hippocampus and Cerebral cortex of mice with the T. marneffei infection. The results demonstrated a significant increase in the expression of Tau46, Caspase-1 and GSDMD both in the hippocampus tissues (Figure 5(A), p < 0.05) and cerebral cortex (Figure 5(B), p < 0.05) of T. marneffei-infected mice compared to controls. However, no difference was observed in the expression of Caspase-3 (p > 0.05).

T. marneffei infection induces Tau pathology, pyroptosis and apoptosis in N2a cells

Subsequently, N2a cells were directly co-cultured with T. marneffei for 48 hours, and the protein expression levels of Tau46, Caspase-1, GSDMD, and Caspase-3 were analyzed by Western blotting. The results revealed that all examined proteins were significantly upregulated following T. marneffei infection (Figure 6(A)). Notably, in contrast to the in vivo findings, the pyroptosis-related protein Caspase-1 exhibited the highest level of upregulation, with an approximate 2.2-fold increase (Figure 6(B)).

T. marneffei infection increase the levels of inflammatory factors, leading to M1 polarization of microglia

To investigate the mechanisms underlying indirect neuronal death induced by T. marneffei infection, we conducted immune cell infiltration analysis based on the RNA sequencing data of cortical tissues described above. The results demonstrated an overall increase in immune cell populations following T. marneffei infection, including macrophages, B cells, T cells. Notably, there was a marked elevation in proinflammatory M1 macrophages, accompanied by a reduction in both inactive M0 and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages (Figure 7(A)). To further validate this proinflammatory shift, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was employed to assess the expression of M1 macrophage markers. The results revealed a significant upregulation of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the cortical tissue of T. marneffei-infected mice (Figure 7(B), p < 0.05).

Subsequently, we investigated whether BV2 microglial cells could be directly invaded by T. marneffei. Co-culture with GFP-labeled T. marneffei confirmed that the fungus were capable of invading BV2 cells (Figure 7C). Furthermore, a significant increase in both mRNA expression and protein levels of the inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 was observed in T. marneffei-infected BV2 cells (Figure 7(D,E), p < 0.05).

Discussion

An increasing number of clinical reports have suggested that T. marneffei infection may be associated with CNS-related symptoms [10][,12]; however, the underlying pathogenic mechanisms remain poorly understood. In the present study, we performed in vivo and in vitro experiments to provide experimental evidence that T. marneffei infection is associated with neuronal injury in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, leading to impaired cognitive functions, spatial exploration, and motor coordination. And two mechanisms underlie T. marneffei-induced neuronal injury: (1) direct induction of neuronal damage through GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis and Tau pathology, and (2) indirect neuronal injury mediated by the activation of pro-inflammatory microglial polarization.

In this study, to the best of our knowledge, we provide the first histopathological and microbiological evidence that T. marneffei can invade the cerebral cortex and hippocampal tissue. These findings offer direct experimental support for the neuroinvasive potential of T. marneffei. Clinical reports have also documented the presence of T. marneffei in the cerebrospinal fluid of AIDS patients, as well as in resected brain abscess tissue, further supporting its ability to affect the CNS under immunocompromised conditions [12,22]. In the field of neuro-fungal infections, several other opportunistic fungi (such as Candida albicans, Aspergillus fumigatus) have been shown to penetrate the CNS by crossing the BBB through mechanisms including transcytosis and paracellular migration strategies [23,24]. It is worth noting that in transcytosis, a critical mechanism by which fungi invade brain tissue is the “Trojan horse” mechanism, where the fungus exploits host phagocytes, such as macrophages, to cross the BBB, facilitating immune evasion and fungal dissemination [25,26]. This strategy not only enables fungal persistence but also increases the potential for chronic infection, especially in immunocompromised patients, such as those with HIV/AIDS, organ transplant recipients, or individuals undergoing chemotherapy. Our previous work demonstrated that T. marneffei can survive within THP-1-derived macrophages [19], and in the current study, we showed that the fungus remains viable within microglial cells, suggesting that the “Trojan horse” mechanism is also a likely pathway for T. marneffei to invade the brain. Although our GFP-T. marneffei strain allowed us to visualize interactions with neurons and microglia in vitro, this approach does not establish the ability of the fungus to traverse the BBB. Future studies employing in vivo or in vitro BBB models will be required to rigorously evaluate its neuroinvasive capacity.

Neuronal damage is driven by both the direct pathogenic effects of the fungus and the host immune response’s magnitude and quality. Fungal invasion into the brain triggers neuroinflammatory cascades that are protective in nature but can result in pathological outcomes when excessive [27]. Furthermore, fungi may also contribute to chronic or autoimmune neurodegeneration through prolonged neuroinflammation [28], which may exacerbate neurodegeneration.

In terms of the form and mechanism of neuronal death following T. marneffei invasion into brain tissues, RNA sequencing analysis of cortical samples from infected mice revealed the activation of neurodegeneration-related pathways. Furthermore, both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that T. marneffei significantly upregulated the expression of Tau46, indicating potential involvement of Tau pathology in T. marneffei-induced neuronal injury. Tau is a microtubule-associated protein predominantly expressed in neurons, where it maintains microtubule stability and axonal transport; abnormal hyperphosphorylation of Tau is a hallmark of several neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease [29], Parkinson’s Disease [30] and etc. Under pathological conditions, the Tau protein forms amyloid aggregates centered around microtubule binding regions, which destroys microtubule structure and function, ultimately leading to neuronal degeneration and death [31]. A previous study on Malassezia has found that the fungal infection promotes Tau hyperphosphorylation by releasing inflammatory factors [32]. Meanwhile, Benzaldehyde 2 secreted by Aspergillus terreus C23-3 exerts neuroprotective effects through targeted suppression of the Tau signaling pathway, effectively reversing neuroinflammation and neuronal damage [33]. Thus, Tau protein levels may not only serve as a potential biomarker but also serve as a promising therapeutic target for T. marneffei-induced neuropathology.

The reports have showed that neuronal pyroptosis and apoptosis are also the significant contributors to dysfunction in the CNS [34,35]. Previous study reported that mice infected with Rhizopus arrhizus exhibit a significant increase in apoptotic rate in brain tissue, directly inducing neuronal death [36]. In our study, however, there was no significant alteration in the apoptotic marker Caspase-3 protein. Notably, we observed that the expression of GSDMD protein was significantly elevated in the T. marneffei-infected group both in vivo and in vitro experiments. GSDMD protein functions as an executor of pyroptosis, facilitating the cleavage and activation of Caspase-1 proteins [37]. Activation of specific caspase family members triggers cleavage of GSDMD, resulting in plasma membrane pore formation, cell swelling and rupture, intracellular content release, robust inflammatory responses, and pyroptotic cell death [38]. Emerging evidence indicates that T. marneffei induces hepatocyte pyroptosis by activating the AIM2 inflammasome/Caspase-1/4- GSDMD axis, which may serve as a critical contributor to T. marneffei -associated hepatic injury [39]. Although the evidence from this study suggests that pyroptosis may play a potential role in T. marneffei associated neuronal injury, these findings remain somewhat inconclusive. Future research should include additional pyroptosis-associated markers, while genetic or pharmacological inhibition of key molecules will be essential to confirm their functional significance.

In addition to the direct mechanisms of neuronal damage caused by T. marneffei, we further observed that the proportion of M1-polarized microglia (the resident macrophages of the CNS [40]) increased in the brain tissue of infected mice. Moreover, in vitro co-culture experiments demonstrated that microglia exposed to T. marneffei underwent M1 inflammatory polarization, subsequently contributing to neuronal death. This observation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that extracellular vesicles (EVs) can promote classical M1 polarization in macrophages [41,42]. Notably, T. marneffei-derived EVs have been reported to be internalized by THP-1 macrophages, leading to increased secretion of IL-1β and IL-6, upregulation of CD86 expression, and enhanced phagocytic and killing capacities, collectively indicative of robust M1 polarization [41]. These findings suggest that EV-mediated signaling may represent a conserved mechanism by which fungal pathogens modulate innate immune cells across different host contexts. The innate immune system, particularly macrophages, plays a central role in host defense against T. marneffei [43]. Macrophages can be differentiated into M1 macrophage (classically activated, pro-inflammatory; secretes IL-12, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α) and M2 macrophage (alternatively activated, anti-inflammatory; secretes IL-10, TGF-β, ARG1) in different microenvironments [44]. These cells transition from a quiescent state to an activated state in response to cerebral tissue damage [45]. Previous study demonstrated that monocytes co-cultured with T. marneffei conidia exhibited significantly upregulated surface expression of CD40 and CD86 molecules, accompanied by increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β [46]. In addition to their antimicrobial functions, the activation of microglia can also lead to the release of cytotoxic factors, which in turn cause neuronal damage [47,48]. For instance, activated microglia generate excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), via the NOX2 enzyme complex, while secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, thereby directly inducing neuronal oxidative damage and apoptosis [47]. Therefore, we speculate that T. marneffei infection activates microglial inflammatory M1 polarization, which is a key mechanism of neuronal injury.

In summary, through both in vivo and in vitro experiments, our study provides the first direct experimental evidence that T. marneffei can invade the brain parenchyma and cause CNS injury. Mechanistically, T. marneffei induces neuronal damage through a novel dual-pathogenetic mechanism:it directly triggers GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis and Tau pathology in neurons, and indirectly promotes M1 polarization of BV2/microglia, resulting in the release of cytotoxic factors that further exacerbate neuronal injury. Collectively, these findings not only expand our understanding of the neuroinvasive potential and pathogenicity of T. marneffei but also offer new insights into the diagnosis and treatment of fungal CNS infections in immunocompromised populations.
