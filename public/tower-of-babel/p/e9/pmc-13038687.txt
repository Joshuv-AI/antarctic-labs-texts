BRAFV595E detection, uroplakin II expression and PAS staining for diagnosing canine urothelial carcinoma
Authors: Monticha Kitnitchee, Sirintra Sirivisoot, Jesada Siripoonsub, Narisara Chimnakboon, Panitnan Punyathi, Somporn Techangamsuwan, Anudep Rungsipipat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gold standard for diagnosing canine urothelial carcinoma (CaUC) is histopathological examination of an incisional/excisional biopsy which is an invasive technique. The risk of tumor seeding by performing invasive techniques in CaUC is known for reason to explore minimally invasive diagnostic tools. Recently, accurate urine-based screening tests, particularly for BRAFV595E detection, have emerged with promising utility. In addition, uroplakin (UPII) is a specific urothelial cell marker for immunohistochemistry (IHC) that is widely used in humans and considered superior to uroplakin III (UPIII) particularly in higher diagnostic sensitivity of poorly differentiated or metastatic carcinoma; however, its expression in CaUC has not been evaluated. This retrospective study aimed to assess UPII and Ki-67 expression in 20 CaUCs (19 urinary bladder and 1 prostate gland) using special stain and IHC. Glycoprotein accumulation in neoplastic urothelial cells was also evaluated using periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining to confirm the location of uroplakin plaques. Tumor proliferation was evaluated using the Ki-67 score by IHC. Additionally, BRAFV595E detection by real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was investigated in three specimen types: urinary cell pellet (UCP), fresh tumor tissue (FT), and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue (FFPE), and the suitability of these for CaUC screening was compared in 23 CaUCs and 34 controls.Overall, BRAFV595E was detected in 47.8% (11/23) of CaUCs and 2.9% (1/34) of controls. UCP and FT showed consistent BRAF genotypes (k = 0.865, p < 0.001), and heterozygous BRAF mutations were confirmed by chromatogram analysis. UPII was expressed in all CaUCs (100%, 20/20). PAS staining was positive in 86.7% of CaUC cases (13/15). Ki-67 score was not related to histological grade. No association was observed between histological grade, BRAF variant, Ki-67, and UPII expression. In conclusion, qPCR is a promising tool for BRAFV595E detection, and combined IHC for UPII with PAS staining may enhance diagnostic accuracy for CaUC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11259-026-11177-5.Keywords: BRAFV595E, Canine, Immunohistochemistry, qPCR, Uroplakin II, Urothelial carcinoma

Introduction

Urinary bladder cancer occurs in approximately 2% of dogs, but its aggressive behavior has prompted the development of novel diagnostic tools and therapeutic strategies (Decker et al. 2015). Canine urothelial carcinoma (CaUC) accounts for most of these cases and is difficult to differentiate from polypoid cystitis using ultrasonography and urine cytology (Fulkerson and Knapp 2015; Gentilini et al. 2022). Histopathological evaluation with uroplakin expression is therefore considered the gold standard for CaUC diagnosis (Norris et al. 1992; Ramos-Vara et al. 2003). However, partial cystectomy or full-thickness cystoscopy is required, both of which are invasive. In addition, CaUC may be misdiagnosed when cystoscopic biopsies yield only small specimens (Butty et al. 2021).

Uroplakins are transmembrane proteins forming the uroplakin plaque in urothelial cells. They play a role in the urothelial differentiation of the basal cells. They are widely used as specific tissue-based markers to identify urothelial carcinoma and distinguish it from prostate carcinoma. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for uroplakin III (UPIII) has been studied in CaUC (Ramos-Vara et al. 2003; Sledge et al. 2015). However, loss of UPIII expression is frequently observed in high-grade CaUC, suggesting poor differentiation (Sledge et al. 2015). Uroplakin II (UPII), a newer IHC antibody, has shown higher sensitivity and stronger expression in human urothelial carcinomas (Hoang et al. 2014; Kristoffersen et al. 2022). However, the diagnostic utility and expression patterns of UPII in CaUC remain underexplored, considered for use as a marker to be evaluated (Knapp et al. 2014).

Glycoprotein accumulation in neoplastic urothelial cells, as highlighted by the periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) stain, can also help identify the localization of uroplakin plaques, as UPIII proteins undergo glycosylation on their extracellular domains (Meuten 2017). Dynamic changes in glycoprotein accumulation in human clear cell urothelial carcinoma have been linked to aggressive behavior and rapid progression (Kątnik-Prastowska et al. 2014). While PAS staining has been widely used to evaluate various carcinomas in humans (Buijs et al. 2004; Lin et al. 2024), its application in canine urothelial carcinoma (CaUC) has not yet been investigated.

The Ki-67 index is used to assess many tumor outcomes, as it may correlate with differentiated tumor subtypes, aggressive behavior, and prognosis. Canine tumors, including lymphoma, mast cell tumors, and mammary tumors, have been extensively studied using the Ki-67 index as a potential marker (Sierra Matiz et al. 2018). However, some studies on CaUC have reported no correlation between Ki-67 and aggressive histological features or clinical outcomes (Govoni et al. 2022; Hanazono et al. 2016).

Determining whether UPII expression and glycoprotein accumulation patterns revealed by PAS, as well as tumor proliferation defined by the Ki-67 score, in neoplastic urothelial cells could serve as diagnostic or prognostic markers for CaUC remains underexplored.

BRAFV595E, the homolog of human BRAFV600E, is a specific variant observed in CaUC and prostate carcinoma (Decker et al. 2015). Its absence in polypoid cystitis (Butty et al. 2021) makes BRAFV600E detection a valuable tool for screening, diagnosis, and eligibility assessment for targeted therapy, particularly for liquid biopsy-based methods (Mochizuki et al. 2015a). In veterinary research, urine-based BRAFV595E detection has been extensively developed using digital droplet polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR), but fewer studies have employed real-time PCR (qPCR) (Gentilini et al. 2022; Grassinger et al. 2019). Urine or urinary cell pellets (UPCs) are suitable for molecular testing, and fresh tumor tissue (FT) retains its potential for mutation detection for PCR-based mutation detection (Gentilini et al. 2022). In contrast, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue (FFPE) is less reliable for genomic nucleic acid analysis in molecular pathology due to DNA fragmentation and crosslinking (Aupperle-Lellbach et al. 2018; Oba et al. 2022). Therefore, further investigation is required to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of qPCR for BRAFV595E across different specimen types.

This study aimed to evaluate UPII expression by IHC and glycoprotein accumulation by PAS in CaUC as well as to assess BRAFV595E detection using qPCR in UCP, FT, and FFPE specimens.

Materials and methods

Study design for BRAF genotyping analysis

The case study group included 23 dogs with urothelial carcinoma, from which UCPs and FT were collected at the Small Animal Teaching Hospital, Faculty of Veterinary Science, Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok. FFPE was also obtained, when available, from the Veterinary Pathology Diagnostic Laboratory, Faculty of Veterinary Science, Chulalongkorn University. The control group consisted of 34 samples: 10 bacterial cystitis (BC), nine polypoid cystitis (PC), five normal urinary bladder (NUB), five benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), and five non-urothelial tumors (NUTs).

UCPs of 23 CaUCs were collected from live dogs, by voiding or catheterization, then kept at 2–4 degrees Celsius (oC), not longer than 24 h before evaluation. Nine FTs of CaUCs were collected from live dogs (6) when the cystotomy was performed and necropsied dogs (3) as a postmortem examination by the owner’s consent. Eleven FFPE blocks from the cases were prepared when available tissue was submitted to the Veterinary Pathology Diagnostic Laboratory. For the NUB group, UCPs were collected from five necropsied dogs that died from causes unrelated to the urinary tract. UB mucosa was gently scraped from the bladder surface postmortem to obtain cellular material for analysis. To minimize bias from post-mortem autolysis and urothelial detachment, necropsies were performed as soon as possible. Carcasses were refrigerated at 4 °C immediately following death, and all mucosal samples were harvested within a maximum window of 8 h. The BPH group included five necropsied male dogs from which prostate FTs were collected during postmortem examination. The FTs of the NUT group consisted of five biopsy-proven cases submitted to the Veterinary Pathology Diagnostic Laboratory, including two mammary adenocarcinomas, two cutaneous mast cell tumors, and one hepatic lymphoma, from which representative FFPE sections were obtained for comparative evaluation.

Briefly, UCPs were collected from 5 ml of urine, centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 5 min, and the resulting pellet was washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) by re-centrifugation under the same conditions. The final UCP was resuspended in 200 µL of PBS for immediate DNA extraction or stored at − 20 °C. FT samples were dissected from the mass and manually homogenized by mechanical mincing. Microdissection of FFPE tissue was performed by core sampling. Specifically, three tumor cores were obtained using an 18-gauge needle. The targeted tumor areas were identified and confirmed by histopathological evaluation. Core samples were deparaffinized with xylene before DNA extraction. All three sample types were digested with proteinase K and incubated at 56 °C, following the protocol for the DNA extraction kit (Qiagen, DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit). FT and FFPE samples were incubated overnight, while UCPs were incubated for 10 min.

DNA quality was evaluated based on purity, with samples exhibiting an A260/A280 ratio outside the range of 1.7–1.9 excluded from further analysis. DNA samples with concentrations below two ng/µL were excluded from subsequent genotyping analysis by qPCR and sequencing.

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Chulalongkorn University (Approval No. 2431053).

Urine cytology

UCPs from both case and control groups were evaluated, comprising 22 CaUC samples and 15 controls (10 BCs, 5 NUBs). Urine was centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 5 min to obtain pellets. The resulting pellets were smeared onto glass slides, fixed in methanol, and stained using the Diff–Quik method. Cytological features were then evaluated descriptively.

Histopathological evaluation

Archived FFPE tissues from 20 CaUC dogs were included in the histological study. FFPE blocks were sectioned at 4 μm and mounted onto glass slides. The slides were processed using an automated stainer for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. H&E-stained sections were evaluated by veterinary pathologists for histological classification and grading according to the grading scheme described by Meuten (2017).

PAS staining

All 20 FFPE sections from CaUC dogs were sectioned at 4 μm and mounted onto glass slides. Following overnight air-drying, the slides were oven-dried at 60 °C for 20 min. The deparaffinized and rehydrated slides were sequentially rinsed with running tap water, then with distilled water, and finally incubated in 0.5% periodic acid for 5 min. After rinsing, the slides were treated with Schiff’s reagent for 30 min, followed by two sequential immersions in 0.5% sodium metabisulfite for 5 min each. Slides were then rinsed with tap water, washed with distilled water, and counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin. Magenta coloration produced by PAS staining was documented and evaluated descriptively for staining intensity.

Uroplakin II expression in CaUC

UPII IHC staining was performed on 20 CaUC tissue sections cut at 4 μm. Following deparaffinization, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in PBS for 30 min. Samples were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. Antigen retrieval was carried out in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min, followed by a 15-minute cool-down. To reduce nonspecific protein binding, 2.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min.

The monoclonal antibody against UPII (ready-to-use, clone BC21, Zytomed) was used as the primary antibody and incubated at 4 °C overnight. CaUC was used as a positive control, and BPH as a negative control. Detection was performed with the Dako REAL EnVision system (Lot 41564345, Dako) for 30 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity for UPII was described by pattern and localization in neoplastic cells. Complete or partial loss of immunoreactivity was defined when fewer than 50% of cells showed a positive signal.

Ki-67 expression in CaUC

Ki-67 immunohistochemistry was carried out on 20 CaUC tissue sections, each 4 μm thick, prepared from FFPE blocks. A monoclonal antibody against Ki-67 (clone MIB-1, Dako) was used at a 1:100 dilution and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Ki-67 expression was evaluated in hot spot areas where neoplastic cells were present. Positive cells were counted in five high-power fields (400X). The Ki-67 score was defined as a proliferation index, calculated as the percentage of positive nuclei among a total count of at least 500 neoplastic cells within tumor hotspots. The results were categorized into three groups: score 1 (< 10% positive cells), score 2 (10–50% positive cells), and score 3 (> 50% positive cells), following the criteria described in a previous study (Hanazono et al. 2016).

BRAF genotyping analysis

BRAF variants were examined using a TaqMan single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) assay adopted from a previous study (Mochizuki et al. 2015a) on a Rotor Gene Q qPCR cycler (Qiagen). A minor modification was made to the wild-type minor groove binder (MGB) probe, substituting the HEX fluorescent dye, a generic dye compatible with many qPCR instruments. Each qPCR reaction contained 20 ng of DNA template, 10 µL of qPCRBIO Probe Mix Lo-ROX (PCR Biosystems), 400 nM each of forward and reverse primers, and 200 nM of TaqMan MGB probes. PCR Cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s.

BRAF genotypes were manually evaluated based on amplification patterns corresponding to heterozygous and/or variants. PCR products with ambiguous BRAF results were further analyzed by Sanger sequencing (Bionics) using the PCR assay proposed in a previous study (Mochizuki et al. 2015b). Each PCR reaction contained 20 ng of DNA template. Chromatograms were visualized with Condo Code Aligner (version 12.0.1). Genotyping by Sanger sequencing was based on the identification of homozygous or heterozygous variants at the target nucleotide position.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 (20) (IBM). To evaluate the agreement between different sample types in detecting BRAF mutation status, Cohen’s kappa statistics were performed for paired comparisons among UCP, FT, and FFPE tissues. The level of agreement was interpreted based on standard guidelines: values ≤ 0 indicate no agreement, 0.01–0.20 as slight, 0.21–0.40 as fair, 0.41–0.60 as moderate, 0.61–0.80 as substantial, and 0.81–1.00 as almost perfect agreement (McHugh 2012). The prevalence of BRAFV595E in the CaUC group was reported descriptively. Associations between BRAF genotypes and histological grade were evaluated with Fisher’s exact test and Cramer’s V statistics with Ki-67 score and UPII expression, respectively. (see Additional File 1). Patterns of PAS staining, Ki-67 expression, and UPII expression were documented descriptively.

Results

Sample collection description for BRAF genotyping analysis

Samples were categorized into two groups: case (CaUC) and control. The case group consisted of 23 dogs diagnosed with CaUC. The control group included 34 dogs, as described above.

Among dogs diagnosed with CaUC, the median age was 9 years. Spayed females (8/23, 34.7%) were the most frequently affected, and beagles (4/23, 17.4%) were the most common breed. In contrast, dogs with a BC diagnosis had a median age of 11 years. Chihuahuas were most affected (3/10, 30%), and intact female dogs represented 40% (4/10). Among the 23 CaUC cases with available clinical information, 5 (5/23, 21.7%) had documented metastasis, and recurrence was reported in 2 Beagle dogs (2/23, 8.6%). BC was caused by a variety of pathogens, including Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas spp., Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Citrobacter freundii, as determined by bacterial culture (VITEK system).

A definitive diagnosis of CaUC was established in 20 dogs based on histopathological examination combined with UPII IHC, including 15 full-thickness biopsies and five cystoscopic biopsies. The remaining three CaUC cases were diagnosed by histopathology alone but lacked sufficient tissue for UPII evaluation.

In total, 22 UCPs, 9 FTs, and 11 FFPE tissues were obtained from CaUC cases. The three-specimen comparison was performed exclusively on the 9 cases in which the corresponding UCPs, FTs, and FFPE samples were available from the same patient. This subset allowed for the most accurate comparison of BRAFV595E detection across the different sample types. The distribution of specimens used across each experimental study is detailed in Table 1.

The control group consisted of 34 dogs, divided into five subgroups: BC, PC, NUB, BPH, and NUT. All 10 dogs with BC provided UCPs, collected from voided urine by centrifugation. In the PC group, nine dogs were diagnosed by histopathology; six FTs were collected during surgery, and three FFPEs were retrieved from archival tissue blocks. In the NUB group, five UCP samples were collected as described in the sample collection section. Additionally, five FT samples each from BPH and NUT were obtained from necropsied dogs and from biopsies submitted to the Veterinary Pathology Diagnostic Laboratory, as described above.

Urinary cytology

Urinary cytology was evaluated under light microscopy for all 22 CaUC cases. Additionally, 10 UCPs from dogs with BC were examined in parallel for comparative analysis.

In CaUC cases, cytology frequently revealed high cellularity with clusters and scattered individual urothelial cells exhibiting marked anisocytosis and anisokaryosis, coarse chromatin, and prominent nucleoli. Hyperplastic urothelial cells often formed sheet-like arrangements. Malamed-Wolinska bodies—eosinophilic, hyaline, cytoplasmic inclusions characteristic of both urothelial neoplasia and urothelial degeneration.

Presence of Malamed-Wolinska bodies was available in cytology of 19 CaUC cases included, 15 full-thickness biopsies and 4 cystoscopic biopsies. It was presented in both high- and low-grade UC, together with the Uroplakin II expressed in all cases. The Malamed-Wolinska bodies were observed in 1 CaUCs expressed Uroplakin II at superficial layers (1/19, 5.2%), 4 cases that were diffused expression (4/19, 21%), and 9 cases of multifocal expression (9/19, 47.3%). While the absence of these intracytoplasmic bodies was observed in 1 case of superficial expression (1/19, 5.2%), 2 cases of diffuse expression, and 2 cases of multifocal expression (2/19, 10.5%).

In BC cases, urothelial cells occasionally displayed mild reactive changes, including mild nuclear enlargement and slight anisocytosis, but did not meet the criteria for malignancy. Some samples showed moderate cellularity, with scattered urothelial clusters exhibiting mild hyperplasia and inflammatory cells on the background, consistent with chronic irritation.

Histopathology

Histological grading was evaluated in 15 full-thickness biopsy specimens from dogs diagnosed with CaUC. Among these, 8 cases (53.3%) were classified as low-grade, characterized by mild to moderate nuclear atypia, low mitotic activity, and preserved epithelial architecture as revealed in Fig. 1A. Six cases (40.0%) were classified as high-grade, exhibiting marked pleomorphism, increased mitotic figures, and loss of cellular polarity as shown in Fig. 1B. Additionally, one case (6.7%) was diagnosed as carcinoma in situ, with neoplastic cells confined to the urothelial lining without evidence of stromal invasion.

In terms of growth pattern, 15 full-thickness CaUC specimens revealed a predominance of non-papillary and infiltrating tumors, which were identified in 8 cases (53.3%) as demonstrated in Fig. 1B. Papillary and infiltrating tumors were observed in 1 case (6.7%). Non-papillary, non-infiltrating tumors accounted for 3 cases (20.0%) as revealed in Fig. 1C. A single case (6.7%) exhibited a papillary, non-infiltrating pattern (Fig. 1A). Prostatic urothelial carcinoma was identified in 1 case (6.7%), originating from the prostatic urethra and extending into the adjacent stroma. Additionally, 1 case (6.7%) of carcinoma in situ, characterized by cystic hyperplasia (Fig. 1D), as demonstrated in five cystoscopic biopsies provided limited but diagnostically useful tissue samples for histopathological evaluation. In all cases, neoplastic urothelial cells replaced the mucosal urothelium, forming disorganized epithelial layers with variable nuclear atypia. The cellular architecture was predominantly non-papillary, with tumor cells arranged in irregular nests or cohesive sheets confined to the mucosal surface. Evidence of stromal invasion was absent due to the limited depth of tissue obtained via endoscopic sampling.

PAS staining

PAS staining was performed on 15 full-thickness CaUC samples and five cystoscopic biopsies. Among the full-thickness samples, 13 out of 15 cases (86.7%) showed positive PAS staining, characterized by intense magenta to pink cytoplasmic droplets within neoplastic urothelial cells. The positive staining was typically localized to the apical region of superficial cells in the mucosal layer and varied from focal to diffuse distribution across tumor sections.

The accumulation of PAS-reactive material was frequently associated with cytoplasmic vacuolation, including prominent intracytoplasmic inclusions consistent with Malamed-Wolinska bodies. In several cases, these inclusions produced a signet ring cell appearance, characterized by large, round, PAS-positive vacuoles displacing the nucleus to the cell periphery (Fig. 2A-C). These morphologic changes were most prominent in areas of epithelial crowding, increased cellular pleomorphism, or disorganized architecture, which were most commonly observed in high-grade tumors.

All five cystoscopic biopsies also demonstrated intense PAS positivity, with staining predominantly in the apex of neoplastic cells. Additionally, the single case of prostatic urothelial carcinoma showed mild PAS positivity, with weak magenta droplets observed in the cytoplasm.

Two cases (13.3%) were PAS-negative: one was a high-grade, non-papillary, infiltrating CaUC, and the other was a low-grade, papillary, non-infiltrating CaUC (Fig. 2D). These cases lacked cytoplasmic glycoprotein accumulation and showed no detectable magenta staining.

UPII immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining using the UPII clone BC21 demonstrated positive expression in all CaUC samples (100%, 20/20), including both full-thickness and cystoscopic biopsies. The immunoreactivity appeared as brown-colored droplets or diffuse homogeneous staining within neoplastic cells, with variable intensity and distribution across tumor sections.

UPII expression patterns were categorized based on their localization within the mucosal layer. Superficial layer expression was detected in 2 out of 20 CaUC cases (10%), both of which were low-grade CaUCs, with staining restricted to the apical urothelial cell layers as demonstrated in Fig. 3A. Diffuse expression across all mucosal layers was observed in 7 out of 20 CaUC cases (35%), including 4 of 6 high-grade tumors (66.7%), indicating a broader distribution in poorly differentiated neoplasms as revealed in Fig. 3B and C. Multifocal (random) expression was identified in 11 out of 20 CaUC cases (55%) (Fig. 3D), present in both low- and high-grade tumors, and was frequently seen in cystoscopic biopsies, although depth and pattern were undetermined in these limited samples.

Regarding subcellular localization, intracytoplasmic staining was the predominant pattern, observed in 18 of 20 cases (90%), while membranous staining was less frequent and typically incomplete. The cytoplasmic labeling varied from fine granular to large homogeneous deposits, often co-localizing with areas of preserved epithelial polarity or differentiation.

Ki-67 immunohistochemistry

Positive immunoreactivity for Ki-67 was observed in the nuclei of tumor cells, appearing as an intense, homogeneous brown coloration. The number of positive cells ranged from 2 to 150 per five high-power fields (HPFs, 400X). To standardize quantitative assessment, a proliferation index was applied. This index was calculated as the percentage of positive nuclei among at least 500 neoplastic cells counted specifically within tumor hotspots.

In low-grade CaUC, the average Ki-67 count was 49.5 ± 53.8, which was higher than that observed in high-grade CaUC, where the average Ki-67 count was 28.33 ± 29.14. Most CaUC cases corresponded to a proliferation score of 2 (89.83% ± 37.08), followed by score 1 (12.21% ± 14.08). Despite these findings, Ki-67 scores did not appear to vary consistently with histological grade.

BRAF genotyping analysis

The BRAFV595E mutation was detected by qPCR, appearing as green fluorescence (HEX) in both homozygous A and heterozygous genotypes. In contrast, the wild-type BRAF allele was detected as homozygous T, indicated by yellow fluorescence (VIC) (Fig. 4). in Among 23 CaUC cases, the mutation was detected in 11/23 (47.8%) samples via UCP, 10/23 (43.5%) via FT, and 9/23 (39.1%) via FFPE. In contrast, all 34 control samples were negative for the BRAFV595E mutation (33/34, 97.1%), except for one control sample from UCP of polypoid cystitis, which was mutant (1/34, 2.9%).

Using histopathologically confirmed CaUC cases as the gold standard, the diagnostic performance of BRAFV595E detection was calculated for the UCP sample type. For UCP samples, the sensitivity was 47.8% and the specificity was 97%, yielding a positive predictive value (PPV) of 92.3% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 73.3%.

Pairwise comparisons were performed, and UCP and FT showed almost perfect agreement (κ = 0.865, p < 0.001). The other pairs demonstrated moderate agreement, including UCP and FFPE (κ = 0.444, p = 0.038) and FT and FFPE (κ = 0.545, p = 0.018).

Among CaUC cases with available clinical information, the BRAFV595E mutation was detected in all five cases with metastatic disease. In addition to the recurrence disease, noted in two Beagle dogs, involving the urinary bladder and cutaneous sites.

Association between BRAFV595E, Malamed-Wolinska bodies, histological grade, Ki-67 expression and UPII expression

The BRAFV595E mutation was detected in 83.3% (5/6) of high-grade CaUC cases, indicating a higher frequency in aggressive tumor phenotypes. In comparison, the mutation was present in 44.4% (4/9) of low-grade CaUC cases, suggesting a lower prevalence in less aggressive forms. The mutation was not detected in any of the five cystoscopic biopsy samples for which histological grade could not be determined. Statistical analysis using Fisher’s exact test showed no significant association (Table 2) between BRAFV595E mutation status and histological grade (p = 0.168), indicating that although the mutation appeared more frequent in high-grade tumors, the difference was not statistically significant. Similarly, BRAFV595E mutation status showed no significant association with Ki-67 score (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.506) or UPII expression (Cramer’s V = 0.247, p = 0.490). Based on Cramer’s V test, no significant association between Malamed-Wolinska bodies and UPII expression was observed (p = 0.617) as shown in Table 3. Other association results were shown in Tables 2 and 3.

Discussion

To the author’s knowledge, this is the first study to examine BRAFV595E mutation status together with UPII immunoreactivity, Ki-67 expression, and PAS staining as complementary techniques in CaUC diagnosis. CaUC is the most common malignant tumor of the canine urinary bladder, and recent studies have focused on identifying tumor markers using newer technologies. Among these, the BRAFV595E mutation has shown strong potential for diagnostic use, but its role as a prognostic factor remains poorly established.

In this study, Beagles were the most diagnosed breed with CaUC, which contrasts with previous reports identifying Terriers as the related breed with a higher associated risk for developing the disease (Knapp et al. 2014). Breed predisposition may be influenced by geographical or institutional bias, as well as overrepresentation of certain breeds within a hospital caseload. Nevertheless, our findings add to existing knowledge by demonstrating CaUC with BRAF mutation in a non-Terrier breed, expanding beyond what has been reported in previous studies Grassinger et al. 2019). In addition, BRAFV595E was detected in all five metastasis cases, including two Beagles with recurrent disease, supporting its aggressive tumor behavior regardless of histological grade. However, a large-cohort study would be necessary to provide more substantial evidence for breed predisposition and to clarify the role of BRAFV595E as a prognostic factor.

Based on the findings in this study, urinary cytology showed limited diagnostic utility for CaUC. However, a direct comparison of diagnostic accuracy between urinary cytology and histopathology was not performed. The crowded clusters of dysplastic urothelial cells with intra-cytoplasmic vacuolation, called Malamed Wolinska bodies, showed overlapping characteristics, cytomorphologic overlap between reactive changes from bacterial cystitis secondary to degenerative urothelial cells and neoplastic transformation. Even though, the Malamed-Wolinska bodies was not a sensitive cytological feature for urothelial carcinoma in human as it was found in benign and degenerated urothelial cells by urinary cytology (Riley et al. 2014). In contrast, Malamed-Wolinska was usually seen by malignant cells of urinary cytology, metastatic UC and primary prostatic UC in dog, rather than prostatic carcinoma (Vasilatis and Ghosh 2024). Therefore, full-thickness biopsies are recommended to allow accurate pathological evaluation and histological grading. (Norris et al. l. 1992; Patrick et al. 2006; Wycislo and Piech 2019). On the other hand, detection of the BRAF mutation by qPCR provided a useful non-invasive diagnostic option, with a positive predictive value of 92.3% and a negative predictive value of 73.3% when using urine samples as a form of liquid biopsy. As the critical concern in CaUC diagnosis is the risk of tumor seeding associated with performing incisional biopsy. Consequently. The use of minimally invasive specimens such as urinary cytology or cell pellets becomes more essential in diagnostic workup. Our study explores this vision by evaluating BRAF marker to enhance the diagnostic accuracy of these less invasive modalities.

Although BRAFV595E detection was precise across all sample types, especially UCPs and FT (κ = 0.865, p < 0.001), the sensitivity was still moderate. The lower prevalence of BRAFV595E in CaUC from UCPs (47.8%) in this study may be due to both pre-analytic and analytic factors. On the pre-analytic side, specimen type and DNA quality are essential. Urea in urine can act as a PCR inhibitor, even though washing steps during sample preparation can reduce it (Shapiro et al. 2015). Another issue is that DNA from UCPs contains both normal and tumor cells, which can interfere with mutation detection by qPCR. This is different from cell-free DNA, which recent studies suggest may be more reliable for mutation analysis (Gentilini et al. 2022). Secondly, qPCR has some limitations for mutation detection, particularly when the somatic mutation is present at low levels, and the signal is masked by wild-type DNA from inflammatory or non-neoplastic epithelial cells (Gentilini et al. 2022; Grassinger et al. 2019). Therefore, ddPCR is often used for mutation detection, especially when only a small number of tumor cells are shed into the urine and the tumor DNA fraction is low. (Gentilini et al. 2022; Mochizuki et al. 2015a). CaUC is known to have a high rate of genetic alterations and mutations, and this molecular heterogeneity, with other factors mentioned above, likely contributed to the moderate negative predictive value (73.3%) seen with qPCR in this study. Our results show that while BRAFV595E detected in qPCR is highly specific and helpful for confirming CaUC when positive, it is not sensitive enough to exclude CaUC when negative, especially in urine samples.

The presence of BRAFV595E in BC has not yet been documented elsewhere, but in this study (Butty et al. 2021). The BRAF genotype was confirmed in both qPCR and Sanger sequencing. Unfortunately, histopathological examination was not available in the BC case; the proliferative lesion could not be undoubtedly classified as CaUC or a hyperplastic lesion due to chronic cystitis. The cohort study of preneoplastic lesions with BRAFV595E could provide information on genetic alterations related to oncogenesis.

In contrast to numerous studies reporting high-grade CaUC as the majority, our result demonstrated low-grade CaUC instead (Meuten 2017; Patrick et al. 2006). However, the predominance of infiltrative pattern (9/15, 60%) observed from this study suggests an aggressive tumor behavior, supported by metastasis (5/20, 25%) regardless of histological grade. In future studies, CaUC outcomes should consider tumor subtypes in conjunction with histological grade and molecular classifications, such as luminal and basal subtypes, as proposed in previous studies (Sommer et al. 2018).

CaUCs were successfully diagnosed through histopathological evaluation in our study, supported by UPII expression and PAS staining in both full-thickness and cystoscopic biopsies. PAS staining served as a supportive tool for detecting glycoprotein accumulation in tumor cells or well-differentiated urothelial cells. These findings may reflect the glycoprotein of uroplakin plaque, which is specific to urothelium, supporting the majority of low-grade CaUC in this study (Meuten 2017). The presence of magenta coloration in Malamed-Wolinska bodies indicated the accumulation of hyaline and protein resulting from the degeneration of urothelial cells (Ayra et al. 2012).

However, the PAS-negative presentation in high-grade and low-grade CaUC in this study could limit its diagnostic utility as a standalone marker for tumor aggressiveness. Although PAS staining is widely used in the diagnosis of various human carcinomas and serves as a prognostic marker in the clear cell variant of urothelial carcinoma, its application in veterinary medicine remains limited and warrants further investigation (Wang et al. 2025).

As the UPII expression has not been previously validated in canine tissue, the UPII immunoreactivity observed in this study provided informative evidence for its utility as a diagnostic marker for CaUC, as shown in the human study. UPII (Hoang et al. 2014). Three distinct expression patterns, similar to UPIII, were identified in previous studies of CaUC (Sledge et al. 2015). The area of expression loss exhibited a random or multifocal expression pattern, which was frequently observed in low-grade and undetermined-grade CaUC. This contrasts with the previous study in both the CaUC and human study, particularly the areas with the most loss of expression were exhibited in high-grade and aggressive urothelial carcinoma (Hoang et al. 2014; Kristoffersen et al. 2022). UPII appeared to be a more effective marker for high-grade CaUC in this study. One possible explanation is that UPII is a relatively small protein (15 kDa) compared to UPIII (47 kDa) and has a less complex structure. This may enable better preservation and accessibility during the antigen retrieval step in IHC, resulting in stronger and more consistent staining in aggressive tumors.

Ki-67 expression in CaUC in our study was similar to the findings of a previous report (Hanazono et al. 2016). Histological grade was not associated with the Ki-67 score as described in this study and other studies. The semi-quantitative Ki-67 score was not associated with recurrence or aggressive behavior, unlike what has been described in human urinary bladder cancer (Peng et al. 2025). Therefore, the value of Ki-67 as a prognostic marker for CaUC remains uncertain, partly due to the limited follow-up and progression data available in this study.

Interestingly, the frequent detection of BRAFV595E in high-grade CaUC (5/6, 83.3%) suggests a possible association between tumor aggressiveness, similar to a previous study (Grassinger et al. 2019). However, a significant association was not observed in either our study or theirs. Likewise, no correlation was found between BRAFV595E and Ki-67 score in our results. The lack of statistical significance may partly be due to the limited sample size. It is also possible that other tumorigenesis pathways, in addition to BRAF, contribute to tumor heterogeneity and clonal variation within CaUC. The observed trend between BRAFV595E and high-grade CaUC warrants further investigation in larger cohorts to clarify its prognostic significance in relation to other malignant features. Our results indicate that UPII expression was not associated with histological grade or BRAFV595E status. In human urothelial carcinoma, UPII has been reported as a marker of the luminal subtype, which is generally linked to less aggressive behavior (Pryma et al. 2022). Although luminal and basal molecular subtypes have also been described in canine CaUC (Sommer et al. 2021), the role of UPII as a subtype marker in dogs has not yet been established. A larger sample size will be needed to clarify its prognostic value and to explore whether other tumorigenesis pathways may influence UPII expression in CaUC.

In conclusion, this study supports the use of qPCR for BRAFV595E mutation detection in both UPC and FT specimens, highlighting its potential for minimally invasive diagnostics in CaUC. While PAS staining has limited discriminatory power, UPII expression in IHC remains a useful tissue-based marker, particularly for identifying urothelial origin in ambiguous or poorly differentiated lesions. The integration of molecular and IHC tools offers a promising path toward minimally invasive diagnosis and improved management of CaUC.
