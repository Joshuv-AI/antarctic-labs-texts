Development of an interpretable machine learning model for predicting prolonged hospital stay in patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jinzhan Chen, Zhisheng Chen, Shuwen Yang, Hongni Jiang, Congyi Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveProlonged hospital stay in patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) significantly impacts patient outcomes and healthcare resource allocation. This study aimed to develop and validate an interpretable machine learning (ML) model for forecasting prolonged hospital stays of the AECOPD population.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed utilizing data from the MIMIC-IV database on patients diagnosed with AECOPD. The dataset was split into a training set (80%) and a validation set (20%). Feature selection was executed through LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm. Logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), neural network (NN), gradient boosting machine (GBM), naive Bayes (NB), as well as K-nearest neighbors (KNN) models were constructed based on the selected features. Model performance was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC), calibration and decision curves. The interpretability of the optimal model was enhanced through SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis.Results7,373 AECOPD patients were encompassed. The RF model demonstrated the best predictive performance, with an area of the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.817 in the training set and 0.715 in the validation set, along with good calibration and clinical utility. SHAP analysis further enhanced the interpretability of the model, providing valuable clinical decision support.ConclusionThe RF model developed in our study exhibited excellent performance in predicting prolonged hospital stays in AECOPD patients. Background

Acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) is a severe acute manifestation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), often resulting in hospitalization and even death [1–3]. AECOPD is typically accompanied by intensified airway inflammation, worsening airway obstruction, and quick worsening of respiratory function, causing considerable discomfort and health impairment to patients [4]. Hospitalization is a critical component of AECOPD management; however, prolonged hospital stays frequently indicate suboptimal therapeutic outcomes, increased complications, or early recurrence [5]. Extended hospital stays not only impose substantial financial burdens on patients but also lead to the overutilization of healthcare resources, particularly in resource-limited settings [6].

Clinicians commonly rely on traditional predictive methods and empirical judgment to assess the prognosis of AECOPD patients. However, these approaches are often constrained by the complexity and variability of patient data, limiting their ability to harness the latent information embedded within large datasets [7]. To enhance predictive accuracy, machine learning (ML) methods have gained traction in medical prediction, particularly in the assessment of the prognosis of disease [8, 9]. ML excels in identifying intricate patterns within large-scale data, enabling precise risk prediction, especially for multifactorial conditions like AECOPD. Nonetheless, despite their robust predictive capabilities, many existing ML models are less interpretable. In clinical practice, both physicians and patients need to understand the basis of model predictions. Poor transparency and interpretability undermine the clinical feasibility and acceptance of these models [10]. Therefore, interpretable ML models have emerged as a prominent research focus, aiming to enhance transparency and provide valuable support for clinical decision-making [11, 12]. In recent years, ML models have been gradually applied to clinical studies of patients with AECOPD, mainly for predicting the risk of acute exacerbation in chronic COPD patients [13–15]. However, interpretable ML prediction models for prolonged hospitalization in AECOPD patients are still lacking.

To address this issue, the present study proposed to construct an interpretable ML model to forecast the likelihood of prolonged hospital stays of AECOPD individuals. Using LASSO regression [16] and the Boruta algorithm [17] for feature selection, multiple classical ML models were constructed, including logistic regression (LR) [18], random forest (RF) [19], neural network (NN) [20], gradient boosting machine (GBM) [21], naive Bayes (NB) [22], and k-nearest neighbors (KNN) [23]. Their performance was assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC), calibration and decision curves. To improve interpretability, Shapley value analysis [24] was applied to the optimal model to elucidate the contribution of individual characteristics to the prediction results, thereby fostering clinicians’ trust in the model’s outputs. This study aims to provide an efficient, accurate, and interpretable ML model that can offer scientific support for predicting prolonged hospital stays in AECOPD patients, facilitating early intervention and personalized treatment.

Materials and methods

Study cohort

The study cohort was sourced from the MIMIC-IV database 3.0, a longitudinal single-center database of the Laboratory for Computational Physiology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). The MIMIC-IV project has gained approval from the institutional review boards of MIT and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC). Patient information has been anonymized, and informed consent was therefore not necessitated.

The inclusion criteria were: patients diagnosed with AECOPD, identified in accordance with the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions (ICD-9 and ICD-10). Exclusion criteria: (1) age ≤ 18; (2) no admission to the intensive care unit (ICU); (3) ICU stay < 1 day. For patients with many admissions, only data from the initial admission were included. The patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Patient characteristics

Demographic information, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, follow-up duration, and scores were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database via Structured Query Language (SQL). Demographic data comprised sex and age. Comorbidities, identified according to ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, included myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, renal disease, liver disease, diabetes, acute kidney injury (AKI), and sepsis. Laboratory parameters included white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin, platelet count, anion gap, bicarbonate, glucose, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (SCr), sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, prothrombin time (PT), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), urine output, body temperature(Tb), heart rate(HR), respiratory rate(RR), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and SpO₂. Treatment information included the use of in-hospital antibiotics, invasive mechanical ventilation, vasopressin, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs). Scoring systems encompassed Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS). For repeated-measures longitudinal data during hospitalization, only measurements taken within the first 24 h after the patient’s admission to the intensive care unit were selected for analysis in this study. This selection provided an early window of observation for the prediction of prolonged hospital stay (Fig. 2). Specific data extraction criteria were as follows: hemoglobin, platelets, glucose, sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, DBP, and SpO2 indicators used the minimum value of the first day’s measurements, anion gap, bicarbonate, bun, creatinine, PT, PTT, temperature, heart rate, and respiratory rate indicators used the maximum value of the first day’s measurements, and urine output indicator was calculated as the sum of all measurements on the first day. To mitigate bias due to sample exclusion, variables with > 30% missing values were ostracized, while the remaining variables were processed with multiple imputations.

Temporal structure of the prediction task. The observation window refers to the first 24 h after ICU admission, during which all clinical variables were collected. The prediction window extends from the end of the observation period to the end of hospitalization

Definition of prolonged hospitalization

Prolonged hospitalization was defined as a patient’s length of stay exceeding the 75% quartile of the study cohort [25].

Feature selection

The study cohort was randomized into a training cohort and a validation cohort in an 8:2 ratio. The former was employed for feature selection and model training, while the latter was utilized for model evaluation. Feature selection was performed using LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm. LASSO regression, a linear regression method, introduces L_1 regularization in the objective function to minimize the sum of squared residuals and a penalty term. This approach maintains predictive accuracy and shrinks certain regression coefficients to zero, thereby automating feature selection. The Boruta algorithm, a feature selection method based on RF, was used to compare each original feature with a randomly generated “shadow” feature, which has negligible predictive ability. RF algorithm was then used to assess feature importance. If a feature’s importance significantly exceeded that of its shadow counterpart, it was classified as “Confirmed” and retained; features with lower importance were classified as “Rejected” and removed. Features of uncertain importance were classified as “Tentative” and require further evaluation.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis/statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean [SD]) and compared between groups through the t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were shown as median and interquartile range (Median [IQR]) and compared via the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages (n [%]) and compared through Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. The outcome was defined as a length of hospital stay exceeding the 75th percentile of the cohort. Based on selected features, models including LR, RF, NN, GBM, NB, and KNN were constructed. A nomogram was generated to visualize the LR model. The hyperparameter tuning for RF, NN, GBM, NB, and KNN models was conducted via the “mlr” package, employing five-fold cross-validation with 100 iterations of random search. Optimal parameters were used to build the final models. Model performance was validated via ROC curves, calibration plots, and clinical decision curves. Every analysis was conducted via R 4.4.2 and Python 4.4.2. A two-tailed p < 0.05 suggested statistical significance.

Results

Study population

7,373 patients with AECOPD were studied, comprising 5,899 patients in the training set and 1,474 patients in the validation set. Among the overall study cohort, 1,842 patients experienced prolonged hospitalization stays. Baseline characteristics were stratified based on prolonged hospitalization. Patients with prolonged hospitalization exhibited significantly higher prevalences of congestive heart failure, renal disease, liver disease, diabetes mellitus, AKI, as well as sepsis. Additionally, these patients had a significantly higher likelihood of receiving antibiotics, invasive mechanical ventilation, vasopressors, and ACEIs. Clinical parameters including WBC, BUN, SCr, PT, PTT, Tb, HR, RR, APS III, SOFA, GCS, OASIS, SAPS II, and SIRS were also significantly elevated in patients with prolonged hospitalization (Table 1). Moreover, descriptive analyses were conducted separately for the training and validation sets. Most characteristics exhibited no marked differences between the cohorts (Supplementary Table 1).

Feature selection

Feature selection was performed in two steps using LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm.

LASSO regression

LASSO regression was utilized to determine the optimal λ value that minimized the binomial deviance using five-fold cross-validation. Figure 3A illustrates the coefficient path from LASSO regression; the x-axis represents the logarithm of λ (log(λ)), indicating increasing regularization strength; the y-axis represents the magnitude of feature coefficients. Each curve corresponds to a single feature, showing how its coefficient changes with increasing λ. As λ increases, the coefficients of certain features gradually decrease and eventually approach zero. Figure 3B presents the cross-validation curve of LASSO regression, presenting the binomial deviance across different λ values. Two vertical dashed lines are depicted: the left line marks the optimal λ value selected through cross-validation (minimum binomial deviance), while the right line is positioned one standard error to the right of the left line, resulting in a more parsimonious selection of features. For this step, our study adopted the λ value corresponding to the minimum binomial deviance (0.01148092) and selected variables with non-zero coefficients (Supplementary Table 2, Figs. 3A–B).

Feature Selection Results. (A) Coefficient Path Diagram in LASSO Regression; (B) Cross-Validation Curve in LASSO Regression; (C) Results based on Boruta feature selection

Boruta algorithm

Subsequently, the variables with non-zero coefficients identified in the LASSO regression step were further refined using the Boruta algorithm. Figure 3C displays the results of Boruta-based feature selection; the x-axis (Attributes) shows the features, and the y-axis (Importance) indicates the importance scores of each feature. Features classified as “Confirmed” by Boruta are represented by green boxes, while those classified as “Tentative” are represented by yellow boxes. At this step, only features categorized as “Confirmed” were selected (Fig. 3C).

The features ultimately included in the model were hemoglobin, platelets, anion gap, BUN, sodium, chloride, antibiotic use, invasive ventilation, vasopressin, SOFA, Tb, HR, AKI, and sepsis. Among these, AKI ranked as the most important feature.

Model development and validation

LR, RF, NN, GBM, NB, and KNN models were established based on the features selected above. The LR model was visualized as a nomogram, where “Points” represent the score for each variable, and “Total points” denotes the cumulative score, which corresponds to the predicted probability (Pr|) of extended hospital stay (Fig. 4). Supplementary Table 3 provides detailed information on the model (Supplementary Table 3). For the RF, NN, GBM, NB, and KNN models, the optimal parameters were selected using a random search method based on 5-fold cross-validation, and models were subsequently developed using these optimal parameters. All models were validated through ROC, calibration, and clinical decision curves. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that in the training cohort, the area of the ROC curve (AUC) for LR, RF, NN, GBM, NB, and KNN in predicting extended hospital stay were 0.710 [0.696, 0.725], 0.817 [0.805, 0.829], 0.699 [0.684, 0.714], 0.743 [0.729, 0.757], 0.689 [0.674, 0.704], and 0.760 [0.746, 0.773], respectively. In the validation cohort, the AUC for LR, RF, NN, GBM, NB, and KNN were 0.710 [0.680, 0.740], 0.715 [0.686, 0.744], 0.678 [0.648, 0.708], 0.718 [0.688, 0.747], 0.710 [0.681, 0.739], and 0.686 [0.655, 0.716], respectively, with RF showing the highest overall AUC (Figs. 5A-B). The sensitivity, specificity, precision, recall, and F1 score were calculated for every model. The RF model showed the best specificity, precision, recall, and F1 score, while NB exhibited the highest sensitivity (Supplementary Table 5). Calibration curve analysis indicated that, except for NB, where the calibration curve deviated from the reference line, the predicted values of all other models demonstrated good agreement with the actual values in both the training and validation groups (Figs. 5C-D). Decision curve analysis revealed that all models exhibited good net benefits in both cohorts (Fig. 6). In the training set, RF exhibited the highest net benefit (Fig. 6A); in the validation cohort, RF and GBM showed the highest net benefit, with no significant difference between the two (Fig. 6B). In conclusion, RF was the optimal model.

SHAP analysis

To increase the interpretability of the model, a SHAP analysis was undertaken on the optimal RF model. Figure 6C displays the SHAP value distribution for each feature, where the SHAP value reflects the influence of every feature on the model output. The horizontal axis represented the SHAP value for each feature, reflecting its contribution to the model’s prediction. Positive SHAP values suggested that a feature increased the probability of the model predicting the positive class, while negative ones meant that the feature reduced the probability of a positive class prediction. AKI and sepsis were identified as having a significant positive influence on the model’s predictions (Fig. 6C). Figure 6D shows the average SHAP values of the features, reflecting their importance. AKI was found to be the most influential feature, followed by sepsis and hemoglobin (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

Data from AECOPD patients collected in the MIMIC-IV database were utilized to construct and validate LR, RF, NN, GBM, NB, and KNN models for predicting the risk of prolonged hospital stay. Feature selection was performed using LASSO regression and the Boruta method. Several clinical features significantly associated with prolonged hospital stay were identified, including hemoglobin, platelet count, anion gap, BUN, sodium ions, chloride ions, antibiotic use, invasive ventilation, vasopressors, SOFA score, Tb, HR, AKI, and sepsis. Among these, AKI was ranked as the most important feature. Ultimately, the RF model was chosen as the optimal model and its interpretability was improved via SHAP analysis.

In the feature selection process, the relationship was explored between various clinical characteristics and prolonged hospital stays in AECOPD patients. Laboratory indicators such as hemoglobin, BUN, chloride ions, Tb, platelet count, and anion gap reflected the patient’s physiological state and disease severity. Low hemoglobin levels, higher BUN, abnormalities in sodium and chloride ions, and elevated Tb were all closely associated with prolonged hospital stay (ORs of 0.904, 1.006, 0.974, and 1.282, respectively) [26, 27]. Moreover, the occurrence of AKI and sepsis significantly increased the risk of prolonged hospitalization, with an OR of as high as 2.772 for AKI, indicating that AKI was a critical prognostic factor [28]. Treatment interventions, such as the use of antibiotics, invasive ventilation, and vasopressors, were also associated with extended hospital stays, particularly the use of vasopressors (OR = 1.440), suggesting an increased need for treatment in critically ill patients, which further extended the hospitalization duration [29]. These characteristics not only reflect the patient’s disease burden and multi-organ dysfunction but also provide crucial insights for clinical interventions. Timely identification of these high-risk features can help improve prognosis and reduce hospital stay duration [30, 31].

To further enhance the model’s interpretability, SHAP analysis was conducted on the optimal RF model. SHAP values, calculated using game theory, measure each feature’s contribution to output, offering transparent explanations for individual samples [32]. The distribution of SHAP values was analyzed for each feature, and the relationships between variables and outcomes aligned with the trends noted in LR. The results of SHAP analysis further indicated that acute kidney injury (AKI) was the most important predictive feature, followed by sepsis and hemoglobin level. This finding can be explained by the following mechanisms: first, AKI affects the prognosis of AECOPD through multiple pathophysiologic pathways, including deterioration of respiratory function triggered by fluid overload, exacerbation of systemic inflammatory response by accumulation of uremic toxins, and increased risk of delirium due to renal-cerebral axis disturbances [33]. Second, sepsis exacerbates endothelial damage and oxidative stress, inducing multiple organ dysfunction through damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as high mobility group protein 1 (HMGB1) [34]. Finally, hemoglobin levels below 90 g/L often reflect chronic hypoxia, which not only affects tissue oxygen supply, but also upregulates erythropoietin through hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), creating a vicious cycle [35]. The synergistic effect of these three significantly prolongs the recovery time of organ function, suggesting that clinical attention should be focused on the monitoring of renal function, infection control, and anemia correction in patients with AECOPD. Through SHAP analysis, the RF model’s rationality and reliability were further validated in feature selection and prediction. SHAP analysis not only helped us identify key features but also provided clinicians with more in-depth insights into the model’s predictions, thereby increasing its transparency and trustworthiness.

Regarding performance metrics, the models were evaluated using AUC values. In the training group, the RF model exhibited significantly outperformed other models, with an AUC of 0.817. GBM and KNN also showed good AUC values of 0.743 and 0.760, both surpassing that of the LR model (0.710). In the validation cohort, RF and GBM similarly exhibited good predictive capability, with AUC values of 0.715 and 0.718, respectively. As a comparison, the LR model in the validation cohort had an AUC of 0.710, similar to that in the training cohort, but its performance was still inferior to RF and GBM. This result is consistent with prior research, which notes that traditional LR models typically perform well on simpler tasks but are less effective in complex nonlinear relationships and feature interactions. ML methods such as RF and GBM often provide better performance [36, 37] and offer greater adaptability and generalization ability when handling complex data [38]. The results of this study showed that the RF model demonstrated optimal predictive efficacy and robustness in predicting prolonged hospitalization in patients with AECOPD, a finding that was different from the results of previous studies addressing the prediction of acute exacerbations in patients with chronic COPD. Existing literature reports show that CatBoost model performs the best among the prediction models for acute exacerbations in patients with chronic COPD (AUC = 0.972) [39], whereas another study demonstrated that gradient boosting machine (GBM) and support vector machine (SVM) have the optimal prediction performance (AUC of 0.833 and 0.836, respectively) [14]. We analyzed that this difference may stem from the following two factors: first, the heterogeneity of the study population, previous studies mainly focused on chronic COPD patients, while the present study focused on AECOPD patients, and there are significant differences between the two groups in terms of the severity of the disease and clinical characteristics; and second, the difference in the study endpoints, and the essential difference in the potential influencing factors of different clinical outcomes. In addition, we observed. The RF model in this study although showing good overall predictive performance, there is still room for improvement in its sensitivity performance. This phenomenon may stem from the following reasons: first, the length of hospitalization in AECOPD patients is influenced by multiple factors, including social factors that are difficult to quantify; second, the heterogeneity of acute exacerbations leads to variable clinical trajectories, and Wedzicha et al. [40] pointed out that such heterogeneity significantly affects prognostic prediction Furthermore, the subjective nature of medical decision-making, such as physicians’ criteria for “safe discharge”, may introduce labeling noise. The sensitivity could be improved in the future by incorporating ambulatory physiologic monitoring data or using transfer learning methods.

Although ML models outperform LR, their interpretability is relatively limited. LR models, due to their linear structure, are more straightforward to understand and interpret. The regression coefficients of features directly reflect the magnitude and direction of the influence of features on the predicted outcome, making them particularly suitable for clinical applications that require clear insights into the impact of variables [18, 41–43]. In contrast, ML models, such as RF, GBM, and NN, are more complex in terms of model interpretation, especially when handling high-dimensional data and intricate interactions. However, interpretability tools, such as SHAP, have significantly elevated the interpretability of ML models. In this study, the RF model, through SHAP analysis, clearly demonstrated the contribution of each feature to the prediction of prolonged hospital stays, thereby aiding in the understanding of which features exert a greater influence on the output. Nonetheless, ML models are still disadvantageous in terms of interpretability, particularly in the medical field where transparency is crucial [32, 44].

In summary, this study developed and validated an ML-based predictive model to assess the risk of prolonged hospital stays in the AECOPD population. SHAP analysis provided clear explanations of the feature contributions for clinicians. However, there are several limitations. Firstly, the data utilized in our study are gathered from the MIMIC-IV database, which introduces selection bias and has single-center limitations. Future research should validate the findings in multicenter, large-scale datasets. Furthermore, owing to the limitations of the MIMIC-IV database in terms of available variables, some potentially relevant indicators, such as lifestyle factors, household income, and imaging data, are not included in the analysis. Finally, the model is only validated in a single-center setting, and external validation in prospective cohorts is necessary to improve its generalizability.

Conclusion

This study constructed and validated an ML-based predictive model to assess the risk of prolonged hospital stays in AECOPD patients. Through LASSO regression and the SCr selection method, several important features were successfully identified. The RF model achieved good predictive performance. SHAP analysis further confirmed the interpretability of the model, providing an in-depth understanding of the model’s output. Our findings offer a valuable reference for the assessment of prolonged hospital stay risks in AECOPD patients.
