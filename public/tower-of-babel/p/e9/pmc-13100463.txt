Advanced Extractables and Leachables Assessment of Microcarriers Used for Adherent Cell Cultures
Authors: Armin Hauk, Ina Pahl, Jonas Austerjost, Julia Hupfeld, Julia Bernfeld, Antonina Lavrentieva, Roberto Menzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe study outlines principles and workflows for a comprehensive extractables and leachables qualification trial of microcarriers for use in a cell therapy application. Styrene‐based MCs were qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed in accordance with USP 〈665〉, followed by a kinetic investigation. Extractables data were fitted to an algorithm to enable reconstructive modeling of dynamic experimental data for both stable and degradable extractables. In a subsequent step, the temporal exposure to process equipment‐related leachables (PERLs) was modeled for MCs used in a hypothetical cell therapy application with partial and continuous medium exchange. The results demonstrated that, in a dynamic environment, the release of PERLs from MCs in a perfused system does not influence product quality at levels that would pose a patient safety risk. Furthermore, process concentrations remain significantly below exposure levels that could have detrimental effects on therapeutic human cells. This demonstrates the benefits of perfused systems, or systems with partial medium exchange, as the washout effect dominates PERL release rates. PERL concentrations in such dynamic systems will always be significantly lower than PERL equilibrium concentrations under static, stagnant conditions.Keywords: adherent cell culture, cell therapy (ATMPs), dynamic exposure modeling, extractables and leachables (E&L), human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs), perfusion bioreactors, styrene‐based microcarrier beads

Introduction

Applications using polymer‐based microcarriers (MCs) are increasingly adopted in the manufacturing of therapeutic cell products and vaccines. MCs are polymer beads of about 100–300 µm in size which provide a surface for attachment in adherent cell culture [1, 2, 3]. Examples include culturing mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) for regenerative medicine and expanding host cell lines, such as Vero cells, for virus production in human and animal vaccine manufacturing. Images of these cells grown on MCs are shown in Figure 1. Transitioning from research to clinical trials and ultimately to GMP manufacturing requires thorough process qualification to ensure patient safety. This includes, among other things, an extractables and leachables (E&L) safety assessment for the MCs [4, 5, 6, 7].

Left fluorescence microscopic picture of SoloHill MC with fluorescence‐labeled adherent mesenchymal stem cells; right scanning electron micrograph of adherent Vero cells covering almost entirely the MC, the diameter of the MCs is approximately 0.1 and 0.2 mm.

Several studies have shown that E&L—and associated process equipment‐related leachables (PERLs)—can interact with cells, and certain compounds have been shown to be detrimental to cell performance, such as viability and growth, for example, in CHO host cells used in classical biopharmaceutical manufacturing, as well as in HEK cells and T cells used in ATMP manufacturing [8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

To date, research on the release of extractables and leachables (E&Ls) from polymer‐based MCs—and, in particular on their impact on cell performance—remains limited. A recent investigation revealed that irradiated MCs influence virus production in human ARPE‐19 cells, though the specific compound responsible for this effect remained unidentified [13].

Cytotoxicity studies to test individual compounds, but also cell media extracts, can be conducted in accordance or similar to standardized test ASTM E3231‐19. This highly sensitive, serum‐free test was primarily developped for mammalian CHO cells, but is also usable to study cell culture growth performance of CAR T cells to reveal possible effects caused by extractables [11]. A recent study used a different approach applying an advanced non‐targeted cell painting assay. The results confirmed that certain PERLs have an impact on human cells but also indicated that a wide range of extractables substances do not negatively influence human cells when manipulated ex vivo [14].

This manuscript presents a comprehensive workflow (see Figure 2 for an overview) for the qualification of polymer‐based MC beads. The approach combines identification of extractables, assessment of their release kinetics, dynamic exposure modeling, and safety evaluation for both patients and products, that is, the therapeutic cells. For the latter, biocompatibility and toxicologically relevant endpoints were, for the first time, evaluated using human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs).

Microcarriers for Cell Expansion

Advanced therapies and medicinal products (ATMPs) are an innovative class of medicinal products based on cells, genes or tissues [15]. Today, single use systems (SUS), already successfully used in biopharmaceutical production, have entered the area of ATMP manufacturing [16, 17, 18, 19]. In cell‐based therapies, cells themselves are used as a drug (“living drug”), contrary to traditional medicines using synthetically derived compounds (small molecules) or compounds such as proteins and peptides produced from living organisms (large molecules/biologics) [20]. Due to the complex nature and large size of cells, manufacturing these therapies presents unique challenges. Moreover, because clinical applications of cell‐based products require large numbers of cells (from 1 up to 8 × 106 cells/kg), scalable manufacturing technologies are essential to provide sufficient cell quantities and doses, particularly in the allogeneic setting [21, 22].

For the production of anchorage‐dependent cells such as MSCs, pluripotent stem cells (PSCs), or PSC‐derived cells, traditional platforms like planar tissue culture plates, flasks, multilayer cell stacks, or roller bottles have been used [23, 24]. However, these static 2D systems are labor‐intensive, costly, and limited in scalability and automation. Consequently, MC‐based expansion in stirred‐tank bioreactors has been explored which offers advantages over traditional planar culture, including higher surface‐to‐volume ratio, improved process monitoring and control, automation, and enhanced process robustness [1, 25]. Polystyrene and related polymers are well‐established, biocompatible MC materials, used successfully in cell culture for decades—from traditional dishes and flasks to modern 3D systems [26].

MC‐based bioreactor expansion typically begins with inoculation using a seed train from either a frozen cell bank (allogeneic therapies) or donor/patient biopsy (autologous therapies). Culture times vary by process and cell type, with MSCs usually expanded for 6 to 10 days at a surface‐to‐volume ratio of 5 to 25 cm2/mL [27, 28, 29]. Medium exchange between 25% and 50% is commonly performed after 2 to 4 days [30], though other feeding strategies, including continuous perfusion, have also been developed [31, 32].

For cell therapy (CT) downstream processing, cells are first harvested from the MC. For this, the cell‐MC suspension is washed with buffer (commonly 1–2 washes using buffer volumes equal to working volume) to remove inhibitors from culture medium, followed by enzymatic cell detachment [32, 33]. Once detached, the enzymatic reaction is quenched to maintain cell viability and quality by adding serum containing medium or other proteolytic inhibitors [29, 32]. Cells and MC are then separated, usually using size exclusion filtration, followed by concentration and washing steps to further remove medium residues, enzymes, or other impurities [34, 35]. Depending on storage requirements and therapeutic application, the cells are finally resuspended in a cryopreservation solution transferred to storage.

Extractables Safety Assessment

A common workflow of an E&L assessment for SUS is illustrated in Figure 3 [36]. It includes extractables studies on SU components, followed by extrapolation to foreseeable exposure (PERLs) for patients, the process, and product, here, specifically the therapeutic cells. Finally, exposures are compared with relevant thresholds to establish safety margins [36]. This publication focuses on exposure estimation and on thresholds for PERLs from MCs in an exemplary CT application.

Workflow of an extractables assessment with the aim to evaluate the potential influence of PERLs on process performance, product quality and patient safety as outlined in the USP 〈665〉 acceptance criteria for the organic extractables profile.

For extractables testing of SUS we rely today on standardized extraction protocols like USP 〈665〉 [37]. Typically, extraction protocols and the associated exposure extrapolation methods are designed to assess static conditions dedicated to only a few defined extraction time points [37, 38, 39, 40]. In contrast, evaluating PERLs from MCs requires dynamic extrapolation. In addition to processing and dwell time, parameters such as medium exchange or, in perfusion systems, flow (volume change over time, Vl/t) are relevant for exposure estimation. Consequently, we first investigated the kinetics of PERL release from MCs and established a model to reconstructively simulate the extraction experiments. When kinetic studies indicated substance transformation during extraction, this was incorporated into the model. The model was then applied predictively to calculate exposure under continuous perfusion and stepwise medium exchange, forming the basis of a safety assessment. For patient safety evaluation, permitted daily exposure (PDE) values were applied, and cell safety was assessed via biocompatibility studies using human MSCs exposed to compound concentrations exceeding the predicted exposure. This methodology has recently been successfully applied to leachables assessment from a medical device [41].

Extractables Data and Dynamic Exposure Calculations

Typically, extraction protocols, such as USP 〈665〉 generate extractables data under static conditions with or without agitation or recirculation. These static extractables data can be scaled to actual use conditions, considering size and process volume. Methods for performing this scaling manually or in silico, while avoiding physical and mathematical pitfalls, are described elsewhere [42, 43, 44, 45]. Few publications address dynamic PERL exposure [46, 47, 48], including studies on SUS bag migration [43], rinsing curves for sterile filters [49]. In process engineering literature, dynamic chemical and biotechnological processes are of high interest and are therefore extensively discussed [18, 47, 50]. Interestingly, dynamic exposure calculation methods are well established and commonly used for food contact notifications in the EU [51, 52]. For certain applications in the medical device area, the US FDA developed similar dynamic exposure models.1 Recent publications indicate that the FDA is increasingly considering dynamic modeling in process safety assessments, in addition to the rather simplistic “worst‐case” assumptions based on static extractables data [53, 54].

Comprehensive mechanistic exposure calculations for PERL release from MCs require complex modeling, including parameters often unavailable for ancillary materials, such as diffusion coefficients in the polymer, partition coefficients between MC, media, and cells, and initial PERL concentrations. To address these challenges, we developed a simplified semi‐empirical model based on linear differential equations, solvable analytically. Extractables data serve as input and estimated rate constants are sufficient to approximate extraction experiments. Where possible, this semi‐empirical approach is compared with an established mechanistic migration model (see end Section 2.3).

Experimental and Methods

Samples and Extraction

The MCs investigated are manufactured from a polystyrene‐based copolymer with a cationic‐modified surface to support attachment of high‐density, fast‐growing Vero cells, primary cells, or stem cells. Test samples were irradiated prior to extraction in accordance with the final marketed product. Extractions were performed following the USP 〈665〉 protocol, using the three extraction solutions: 50% ethanol (1:1 v/v ethanol:water), a low‐pH solution (pH 3), and a high‐pH solution (pH 10). Extractions were conducted at 40°C under static conditions with agitation at 75 rpm in 250 mL glass bottles with glass joints. Blank samples were prepared under identical conditions but without MCs. The 50% ethanol solution mimics the high‐organic environment encountered in CT media, which includes dissolved proteins, nutrients, metabolites, and particulate cell matter. Sampling time points of 1, 3, 7, and 21 days were used for kinetic investigation of PERL concentrations, with small aliquots taken for analysis. Analytical Systems

High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC): Ultraviolet (UV) Detector

An Agilent 1200 system, including a G 1314A VWD detector, and a C18 Nucleosil column (5 µm, 250 mm/4.6 mm) with a Nucleosil C18 100–5 guard column, was utilized. Acetonitrile (A) and purified water (B) were employed as eluents with a gradient: 0 min/A 0%, 20 min/A 60%, 40 min/A 100%, 55 min/A 100%, at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Injection volume was set to 20 µL with a column temperature of 40°C.

Gas Chromatography−Mass Spectrometry (GC‐MS)

Gas chromatography−mass spectrometry (GC‐MS) analyses were performed on a Clarus 600GC and Clarus 600T MS Turbo with a USP G27 column using electron ionization (EI) at 70 eV. Operating parameters for liquid injection were injector 75°C (0.1 min) to 250°C (200°C/min) with 1 µL splitless injection and helium carrier gas at 1 mL/min. The oven temperature ranged from 35°C (0.1 min) to 300°C (15°C/min) with a 30 min hold, and the transfer line and source temperature were set to 250°C. The scan range was from 35 to 700 m/z. TurboMass 5.4.2 was utilized for processing. Response factor was set to one for compounds not quantified via response factors of authentic reference standards. Derivatization of the extracts after solvent exchange to dichloromethane to form the corresponding trimethylsilyl ether(s) was performed using N‐Methyl‐N‐(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA, derivatization grade).

Biocompatibility Tests

Human adipose‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (hAD‐MSC) were isolated from adipose tissue obtained during abdominoplasty, following the protocol of Zhu et al. [59]. The cells were characterized by MSC‐typical surface markers and functional properties, including adipogenic, chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation potential [60].

For cytotoxicity testing, cells were expanded in T‐flasks, harvested, and counted using a Countess automated cell counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at 6000 cells per well in 100 µL growth medium (α‐MEM supplemented with 10% human serum and 0.5% gentamicin) and incubated overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2 to allow attachment. External wells were not seeded with cells but instead filled with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) to minimize edge effects.

On the following day, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing one of the following treatments: (i) pure medium (control; n = 4 wells), (ii) 10% DMSO (positive control; n = 4 wells), (iii) 1% DMSO (solvent control; n = 4 wells), or (iv) test compounds dissolved in DMSO (n = 3 wells per concentration). Stock solutions of each leachable were prepared in DMSO and diluted in growth medium so that the final DMSO concentration was 1% (v/v) in all conditions. The concentration levels for the PERLs were defined based on exposure modeling from the partial media exchange scenarios, which produced the higher predicted exposures and are provided in Table 6. Cells were incubated for 24 h, and cytotoxicity was evaluated using the CellTiter‐Blue (CTB) viability assay (Promega). CTB reagent was diluted to 10% (v/v) in basal medium, and 100 µL was added per well after removal of the culture medium. For background correction, CTB solution was also added to three empty wells. Plates were incubated for 1 h at 37°C and subsequently read on a Fluoroskan microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Background fluorescence was averaged and subtracted from the raw readings. Fluorescence values were then normalized to pure medium control and expressed as percentage of control.

Results

Extractables Profile Microcarrier

The extractables profile of the MCs was fully elucidated and linked to the material of construction. Measured and predicted extractables include styrene‐based monomers and oligomers formed as byproducts of [2 + 2] cycloaddition reactions. Quantification was performed for selected and most prominent extractables using authentic reference materials, including 4‐methylstyrene (CAS 622‐97‐9), 4‐methylbenzaldehyde (CAS 104‐87‐0), 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride (CAS 1592‐20‐7) and 4‐vinylbenzyl alcohol (CAS 1074‐61‐9). These compounds are linked to the polymer base material, that is, are monomers or degradants thereof. They were selected for exposure modeling due to their high concentration levels and reliable quantification. Specifically, 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride is particularly interesting due to its reactivity associated with degradation processes and potential toxicity, making it particularly relevant for cytotoxicity studies. The temporal development of the concentration of these four compounds was monitored in kinetic experiments, and the results are summarized in Table 3.

Other extractables were lower in concentration and are not further investigated in the following study for the sake of simplicity. Additional extractables detected include benzoic acid (CAS 65‐85‐0), identified by GC‐MS after derivatization to its trimethylsilyl ether. This compound is highly likely to be linked to the commonly used radical initiator for styrene polymerization—benzoyl peroxide—and is a degradation product [61]. Notably, none of the reported oligomers for polystyrene, formed as byproducts from [2 + 2] cycloaddition reactions of the styrene monomers, were present in any extract. However, additional compounds related to 4‐methylstyrene were detected, such as 4‐ethyltoluene. This compound is likely a byproduct of 4‐methylstyrene, as it is used as a starting material in its synthesis [61]. Finally, 4‐vinylbenzyl ethyl ether and 3‐vinylbenzyl ethyl ether were detected, likely formed by the reaction of the extraction solution ethanol with vinyl‐benzyl chloride, which is used as commercial mixture of 3‐ and 4‐vinyl isomers (see example GC‐MS chromatogram Supporting Information S4). Generally, all extractables were exclusively detected in the 50% ethanol solution, except for 4‐methylbenzaldehyde and 4‐vinylbenzyl alcohol, which were also present in the pH 3 and pH 10 aqueous extracts.

Kinetic Experiments and Determination of Parameters for Exposure Modelling

A visual inspection of the kinetic extractables data of 4‐methylstyrene and 4‐methylbenzaldehyde (Table 3 and Figure 5) suggests that they can be categorized as class A substances. By contrast, 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride is a class B substance, and 4‐vinylbenzyl alcohol is classified as a class C substance as indicated by their concentration developments (Table 3 and Figure 6). Consequently, it was possible to fit initial flux (F^0,A) and flux decrease rate (k F,A ), as well as diffusion coefficient (Dp ) and partition coefficient (Kpl ) for 4‐methylstyrene and 4‐methylbenz‐aldehyde (Table 4). For 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride and 4‐methylbenzaldehyde it was possible to fit initial fluxes (F^0,B and F^0,C), the respective flux decrease‐rates (k F,B and k F,C ) and, in addition, the kdeg value for 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride (Table 4). Required dimensional information for the MCs tested, like diameter, density, surface, weight, extraction solution volume, and derived parameters were taken from Table 1. Trajectories for 4‐methylstyrene and 4‐methylbenzaldehyde, calculated with Equations (2) and (7), are shown in Figure 5 for comparison. The mechanistic migration model (Equation 7) can perfectly reconstruct the experimental findings for both substances. Therefore, this model can be considered a suitable baseline‐method for migration calculations in stagnant, non‐perfused systems for stable substances. The fitted diffusion coefficients for 4‐methylstyrene and 4‐methylbenzylaldehyde are similar to values for styrene in various polystyrenes reported elsewhere [62]. This is reasonable, as diffusion coefficients in polymers are mainly correlated to the molecular mass of the molecules [52]. The empirical model (Equation 2) can approximate the experimental data for 4‐methylstyrene and 4‐methylbenzylaldehyde only between t = 0 up to 20 days. In this time interval, slope and concentration values differ not significantly from the experimental data and the trajectories of the migration calculation (Figure 5). The most relevant difference between trajectories of Equations (2) and (7) is that they do not converge to the same equilibrium concentration. In contrast to the approximation, the mechanistic model does not reach equilibrium within the 21 days of experiments. This is plausible as it correctly describes the subsequent release of PERLs from the depth of the material with a slowly decreasing diffusion gradient, whereas the empirical model does not include this effect. Consequently, Equation (2) and the fitted parameters can only be used for interpolation but shall not be used for extrapolations outside the tested time points (t > 20 days).

The analytical results of the kinetic experiments for 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride and 4‐vinylbenzyl alcohol were used to fit model parameters to Equation (4) together with Equation (6). The analytical data together with the respective curves for the temporal development of the concentration of both substances calculated reconstructively are shown in Figure 6.

The correlation between measured data and the trajectories for the empirical model for 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride and 4‐vinylbenzyl alcohol in Figure 6 is in good agreement and significantly better compared to 4‐methylstyrene and 4‐methylbenzaldehyde presented in Figure 5. The reason is that the fitting process is using additional parameters that is, the hydrolysis rate kdeg for 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride, and F^0,C and k F,C for 4‐vinylbenzyl alcohol and, therefore, correlation becomes necessarily mathematically “more accurate”. Comparing trajectories, including the transformation reaction (bold lines in Figure 6) versus omitting it (dotted lines in Figure 6), supports the hypothesis that 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride undergoes first order hydrolysis to 4‐vinylbenzyl alcohol. It sufficiently explains the analytical findings and improves the modeling output. The release‐decrease rate for 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride (kF = − 0.85) is different from the other rates (Table 4). This could be a hint to an “overfitting” of the model. A situation one can encounter, with model algorithms too closely related to the model training data (here, the analytical results from the kinetic study). Hence also this model is limited to be used for interpolation. The reconstructed extraction curve for 4‐vinylbenzylchloride reaches the highest concentration after t# = 3.5 days with cl = 7.3 µg/mL (Equations 4 and 5). The highest concentration for 4‐vinylbenzyl alcohol can be expected after contact times longer than those used in the kinetic experiment. A rough estimation of the 4‐vinylbenzyl alcohol equilibrium concentration was made by an extrapolation with Equation (6) for t→∞, resulting in cl ≈ 13 µg/mL. Notably, standardized protocols require only an extraction for 21 days for SUS used for a prolonged time. Such a one‐point analysis after 21 days finds 100% of the maximum amount of all extractables which are not consumed or formed. In contrast, only 40% of the maximum amount of 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride and approximately 75% of the estimated maximum concentration of 4‐vinylbenzyl alcohol were detected in the MC extraction solution, which could justify the use of a correction factor only for unstable compounds.

PERL Exposure Prediction

An advanced safety assessment of PERLs, as outlined in Figure 3, was performed to enable predictive exposure estimation. The model used is depicted in Figure 7 together with the relevant assumptions. This box model accounts for the release of PERLs from MCs and their potential reactions or formation of the four investigated PERLs in the liquid phase, under conditions of either continuous (perfused) or partial media exchange. The model does not include potential adsorption of PERLs onto cells, which is justified by the initially low quantity of cells attached to the MCs and the reversible nature of the process. However, cell adsorption can be incorporated into such models, as demonstrated by Bossong et al. [63]. Incorporating cell adsorption requires knowledge of the adsorption constant (Kd‐values), which can be either generic or determined experimentally [8]. Including the dynamic growth of the biomass would require a numerical model description, which is out of scope for this investigation.

Box model of MC in a bioreactor with a cell layer (Vbio = const); the MC releases PERLs with a flux (F) into the bioreactor (Vl = const), the contacting liquid Vl∗ is considered well‐mixed and is exchanged with a flow (Ql ); between biofilm and liquid phase (Vl∗=Vl−Vbio). A fast, reversible equilibrium is established between the biofilm and liquid phase.

The assessment concludes with a safety margin calculation considering both patient exposure and the maximum exposure of cells during ex vivo manipulation. A basic sensitivity analysis of the model (see Supporting Information S7 and S8), shows that the model is not sensitive to fluctuations in input parameters and their deviations are not progressively propagated into the results. The limitation of the empirically developed model is that it allows only temporal interpolation of the concentration development of the investigated PERLs and biosorption cannot be included.

Partial Media Exchange

Partial media exchange involves frequent, partial replacement of a fraction of the total liquid volume (V l ) with fresh media (V l, dis ) after certain time steps (Δt). In the present example, a constant liquid exchange factor X = V l /V l, dis and constant time intervals are assumed. The dynamics of partial media exchange can be calculated using recursive schemes based on Equation (8) for stable class A substances, Equation (9) for degradable class B substance, and Equation (12) for class C substances (Supporting Information S6). Only 4‐vinylbenzyl chloride reaches its maximum before the first media exchange. Overall, maximum concentrations under partial media exchange are slightly higher than in the continuously perfused system (compare with Figure 8) due to the stagnant periods between exchanges that allow accumulation, particularly during early contact when PERL flux rates are highest. For instance, 4‐methylstyrene reaches a maximum of 20 µg/mL under partial exchange, compared to 14.3 µg/mL in the continuously perfused system. Consequently, the maximum concentrations from the partial media exchange scenario were used as reference values for biocompatibility testing.

Under the conditions studied, continuous perfusion results in lower maximum exposure‐ concentrations compared to partial media exchange. However, alternative partial exchange strategies, such as higher media replacement rates, could lead to lower maximum concentrations.

Safety Assessment

A tiered approach was applied for the safety assessment of the intended use scenario. First, potential systemic PERL exposure to the patient was evaluated in accordance with standard E&L assessment practices. Second, the effect on the therapeutic cells was assessed to address the product quality.

Although different PERLs exhibit varying affinities for cells [8], additional evaluation of PERL adsorption on the cells is not required for MC safety assessment. This approach is justified because the biocompatibility data were obtained across a range of liquid‐phase concentrations, allowing a direct comparison between model predictions and NOAEL values without knowledge of the exact amount of PERLs adsorbed by the cells.

It is possible to adjust flow or media exchange rates. This can be used to establish the minimum perfusion or exchange rate where the cell exposure is kept below the NOAEL (see Supporting Information S8).

Summary

A comprehensive assessment of MCs and their PERLs under dynamic conditions was developed and successfully applied. This included studying the extractables profile and release kinetics of four selected main extractables from styrene‐based MCs and deriving parameters for a dynamic exposure model. The model, based on differential equations, was analytically solved to reconstruct the kinetic extractables experiments and to predict PERL exposure under use conditions.

Notably, complex modeling was not required; simple empirical algorithms describing PERL release from the MCs—including PERL degradation and formation during extraction—were sufficient to establish the model. Incorporating dynamic dilution into the model, to account for continuous or stepwise partial media exchange, allowed prediction of PERL exposure under realistic use conditions. The dynamic modeling revealed that PERLs do not accumulate to equilibrium concentrations. Instead, the exposure trajectories exhibited well‐defined “wash‐out” curves, characterized by a peak exposure reached at a specific time point, followed by declining PERL levels. This outcome is consistent with the fact that release rates of PERLs from plastics are orders of magnitude lower than dilution rates in perfused bioreactors. This “wash‐out” effect is an effect of dilution, a generally accepted physical principle in drug manufacturing, supporting the robustness and inherent validity of the model.

Direct measurement of PERLs in cell suspensions, especially under dynamic conditions, can be extremely challenging, requiring labor‐intensive steps such as extraction, substance enrichment, cleanup, and measurement, which are prone to errors. Therefore, model calculations serve as a reasonable and reliable alternative and can further serve as basis for process robustness investigations. End‐users can integrate these results for their analytical validations. It might be justified to perform test runs (validation) using a simulating solution rather than a complex medium with cells to facilitate the chemical analysis.

The maximum calculated exposures were used as range finders to define relevant concentration levels for subsequent biocompatibility studies. These concentrations were applied in cytotoxicity assays with MSCs, enabling derivation of NOAELs for the selected PERLs. Finally, toxicological thresholds such as PDE and TTC values, together with the experimentally derived NOAELs, were integrated into an exemplary safety assessment to establish margins of safety for both patients and therapeutic cells.

Overall, the data are consistent with the established biocompatibility of polystyrene and support the conclusion that the tested surface‑coated polystyrene MCs do not pose a risk to patient safety or product quality.
