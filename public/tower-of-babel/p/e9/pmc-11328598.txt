Systemic immunoinflammatory indexes in albuminuric adults are negatively associated with α-klotho: evidence from NHANES 2007–2016
Authors: Meng Jia, Shisheng Han, Yi Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) is a novel inflammatory biomarker closely associated with the inflammatory response and chronic kidney disease. Klotho is implicated as a pathogenic factor in the progression of kidney disease, and supplementation of Klotho may delay the progression of chronic kidney disease by inhibiting the inflammatory response. Our aim is to investigate the potential relationship between SII and Klotho in adult patients in the United States and explore the differences in the populations with and without albuminuria.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study recruiting adult participants with complete data on SII, Klotho, and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey from 2007 to 2016. SII was calculated as platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count, with abnormal elevation defined as values exceeding 330 × 10^9/L. Albuminuria was defined as ACR >30 mg/g. Weighted multivariable regression analysis and subgroup analysis were employed to explore the independent relationship between SII and Klotho.ResultsOur study included a total of 10,592 individuals. In all populations, non-albuminuria population, and proteinuria population with ACR ≥ 30, participants with abnormally elevated SII levels, as compared to those with SII less than 330 × 10^9/L, showed a negative correlation between elevated SII levels and increased Klotho, which persisted after adjusting for covariates.ConclusionsThere is a negative correlation between SII and Klotho in adult patients in the United States. This finding complements previous research but requires further analysis through large prospective studies.Keywords: Systemic immune-inflammation index, α-Klotho, albuminuria adults, cross-sectional study, NHANES

Introduction

The Klotho gene, a senescence inhibitory gene encoding α-Klotho, β-Klotho, and γ-Klotho proteins, acts as a humoral factor with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and anti-aging effects [1,2]. Highly expressed in the kidneys, particularly in the renal tubules [3], the Klotho gene serves as an exclusive co-receptor for fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF 23) in adults, existing in transmembrane and soluble circulating forms [4,5]. Animal models demonstrate Klotho’s potential as a longevity-associated gene for gene therapy, reversing premature senescence-associated phenotypes [6].

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is diagnosed with the presence of >3 months of structural or functional kidney abnormalities [7]. By the end of the century, CKD is expected to become a major cause of death, ranking as the second most common cause in countries with long life expectancies [8]. sKlotho levels decline in early CKD stages, and Klotho deficiency may contribute to renal injury progression [9–12]. Patients with CKD and Klotho levels ≤ 396.3 pg/mL face a 2.03 times higher risk of progression and death than those with levels > 396.3 pg/mL [13]. In transgenic animal models, sKlotho protein replacement therapy exhibits benefits such as improved oxidative stress, inflammation inhibition, regulated autophagy, maintained platelet homeostasis, apoptosis inhibition, and reduced renal fibrosis [14–22]. Maintenance or upregulation of Klotho levels is considered a reasonable therapeutic strategy for CKD [23].

Studies have shown that serum sKlotho is decreased in patients with CKD while the expression of various inflammatory mediators is significantly elevated, with a negative correlation between the two [24,25], and this negative relationship has been further confirmed in patients with other diseases, such as coronary artery disease [26]. Renal Klotho expression has also been shown to be down-regulated in folic acid nephropathy or lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammation [27,28]. In three different types of colitis animal models, TNF-α or IFN-gamma (Interferon-gamma) was also found to reduce renal Klotho expression, and TNF-α neutralizing antibodies also reversed this process [29]. The TNF-α neutralizing antibody also reversed this process [29]. Mechanistic studies have shown that incubation of murine RTC with TNF-α or IFN-gamma significantly inhibited the transcriptional activity of the Klotho gene promoter, while Klotho mRNA degradation was not affected [29], suggesting that inflammatory mediators down-regulate the expression of Klotho at the pre-transcriptional level [28]. The Klotho gene is now known to be a major contributor to inflammation. It is now known that the promoter region of Klotho gene has NF-κB binding site, and inflammatory mediators such as TWEAK and TNF-α can promote the binding of RelA, a subunit of NF-κB, to the Klotho promoter binding site, which will lead to the deacetylation of histones in Klotho gene [28], but not Klotho mRNA degradation [29]. This binding will lead to histone deacetylation of Klotho gene and inhibit its transcriptional activity, and this effect can be blocked by histone deacetylation inhibitors [27], suggesting that the deacetylation of Klotho promoter may be the mechanism by which inflammation inhibits the expression of Klotho.

Albuminuria results from renal damage, causing impaired protein filtration and increased urinary albumin excretion (UAE), typically defined as an albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) ≥ 30 mg/g. This detection method is efficient and convenient [30]. Early CKD patients with normal glomerular filtration rates often exhibit albuminuria, associated with an elevated risk of CKD progression or premature death [31]. Even in populations with a normal eGFR, an ACR of 10–29 mg/g is linked to a 50% higher risk of all-cause mortality compared to the general population [32]. Albuminuria serves not only as a marker and risk indicator for renal injury but also as a risk factor and therapeutic target for the progression of chronic kidney disease and cardiovascular disease [33].

The Systemic Immunoinflammatory Index (SII) is an index that integrates three different types of inflammatory cells—platelets, neutrophils, and lymphocytes. Proposed by Hu et al. [34] in 2014 and experimentally calculated as platelet count multiplied by neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count, SII reflects both local immune response and systemic inflammation throughout the body [35–37]. Numerous studies have verified its high prognostic value in various tumors, including lung cancer [38], esophageal cancer [39], and epithelial ovarian cancer [40]. In cardiovascular disease, a study by Yang et al. in 2020 reported that SII demonstrated superior predictive value compared to traditional risk factors for major cardiovascular events in patients after coronary intervention [41]. Additionally, Ran et al. found that SII could function as an independent risk factor for protein energy expenditure in maintenance hemodialysis patients, further emphasizing its relevance [42].

Di et al. [43] observed a parallel association between SII levels above 330 × 10^9/L and the occurrence of kidney stones in adults aged 20–50 years, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.282, a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 1.023–1.608, and a p value of .034. Guo et al. [44] found that elevated SII levels were linked to an increased incidence of Diabetic Kidney Disease (DKD), with an OR of 1.42, a 95% CI of 1.10–1.83, and a p value of .01. Meanwhile, Zhang et al. discovered a negative association between Klotho levels as a dependent variable and the prevalence of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), and albuminuria as a categorical dependent variable was indicative of renal damage [23]. However, no studies have specifically investigated the correlation between SII and Klotho in a population with proteinuria. Our study aimed to determine whether SII could serve as a predictor of renal inflammatory injury in patients with albuminuria, using Klotho as a marker of renal inflammatory injury. We are the first to conduct a large cross-sectional study in an adult population, exploring the relationship between SII and Klotho in a US population of patients with albuminuria. This research provides additional information and evidence to enhance research guidance on SII and Klotho for kidney health and healthy aging.

Subjects and methods

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a continuous survey collected every two years (1999–present) [45] to measure the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized civilian US population, with all protocols approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Committee [46], questionnaire data collected through interviews at mobile examination center (MEC) screening, and study participants gave consent to store blood samples and use the blood for future research testing. For more details about NHANES, see their website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm, accessed on 1 May 2023).

The study was conducted using a cross-sectional design of the NHANES dataset, assessing the population for the five NHANES cycles 2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2013–2014 and 2015–2016 (n = 50,588). It is worth noting that CRP data are only recorded in four years for the two NHANES cycles 2007–2008, 2009–2010,and hs-CRP data are only recorded in four years for the two NHANES cycles 2013–2014, 2015–2016.

Exclusion criteria included: (a) missing Klotho data; (b) incomplete data of urinary albumin: creatinine ratio (ACR); (c) incomplete and extreme SII data (neutrophil, lymphocyte and platelet counts);(d) age <18 years or pregnant women.

Finally, 10,592 participants were included in a complete case analysis according to strict criteria (Figure 1).

Definition of covariates

This study includes covariates that may influence the relationship between SII and Klotho. Demographic parameters included age, gender, marriage, race, education level, household income to poverty ratio, and body mass index (BMI).

Hypertension was validated based on the answer of ‘Ever told you had high blood pressure’ on the BPQ questionnaire, which provides personal interview data on awareness, treatment, and control of high blood pressure and high cholesterol. Diabetes mellitus was validated based on the answer of ‘Doctor told you have diabetes’ on the DIQ questionnaire, which provides personal interview data on diabetes, prediabetes, use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic medications, and diabetic retinopathy. It also provides self-reported information on awareness of risk factors for diabetes, general knowledge of diabetic complications, and medical or personal cares associated with diabetes. Gout, congestive heart failure and coronary heart disease were validated based on the answer of ‘Doctor ever told you that you had gout?’, ‘Ever told had congestive heart failure?’ and ‘Ever told had coronary heart disease?’ on the MCQ questionnaire, which provides self-reported personal interview data on a broad range of health conditions for both children and adults. Biochemical characteristics included Klotho, hemoglobin, total blood protein, blood albumin, blood urea nitrogen, blood creatinine, blood uric acid, glutathione, glutamic oxalacetic transaminase, triglycerides, total cholesterol, urinary albumin/creatinine ratio and sampling was performed in the Mobile Inspection Center(MEC). All samples were stored at −80 °C prior to the experiment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in accordance with Centers for Disease Control and Prevention(CDC) guidelines [46]. Given that NHANES uses a complex probability sampling design and has oversampled some population groups to ensure adequate representation, sample weights were used to combine harvesting survey periods and estimate means and standard errors. comparisons in the SII subgroups were made using survey-weighted logistic regression (mean ± standard deviation (SD)) for continuous variables and survey-weighted chi-square tests for categorical variables (counts, (n)) were performed. Based on previous studies by NHANES, we set the value of abnormally elevated SII at 330 × 109/L [47]. multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to explore the relationship between SII and Klotho. In sensitivity analyses, Klotho was transformed from a continuous variable to a categorical variable (trichotomies) to assess the robustness of the results. Crude model is unadjusted for covariates. Model 2 is a minimally adjusted model for age (overall only), gender, ethnicity, marital status, education level, household income to poverty ratio. Model 3 is the model adjusted for age (overall only), gender, ethnicity, marital status, education level, household income to poverty ratio, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, gout, congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, cholesterol, triglycerides, serum uric acid, serum creatinine, white blood cell count. All analyses were performed using R version 3.4.3 (https://www.r-project.org/, accessed on 1 May 2023, R Foundation). The level of statistical significance was set at a two-sided p value <.05.

Results

A total of 10,592 people were included in the analysis, which were divided into two groups according to whether the ACR ≥ 30 mg/g, and according to whether the SII ≥ 330 × 10^9/L can also be divided into two groups.

Compared with the population with ACR < 30 mg/g, the proteinuria group with ACR ≥ 30 mg/g showed statistically significant differences (p < .05) in segmented neutrophils number, SII (systemic immune-inflammation index), NLR (neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio), CRP (C-reactive protein), hsCRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein), white blood cell count, Klotho, age, race/ethnicity, education level, hypertension, congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, diabetes, BMI (body mass index), hemoglobin, total protein, albumin, aspartate aminotransferase, blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, serum uric acid, cholesterol, and triglycerides (see Table 1). Specifically, segmented neutrophils number, SII, NLR, CRP, hsCRP, white blood cell count, BMI, total protein, aspartate aminotransferase, blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, serum uric acid, and triglycerides were higher, while Klotho, hemoglobin, albumin, and cholesterol were lower. The non-Hispanic White population with some college or an associate degree and hypertension was more prevalent, without accompanying diabetes, chronic heart failure, or coronary heart disease.

For the non-proteinuria population with ACR < 30 mg/g, inter-group differences were compared based on whether SII was greater than 330 × 10^9/L. The population was mainly composed of non-Hispanic White individuals, without diabetes and coronary heart disease.

Discussion

We conducted a rigorous subgroup analysis with a substantial clinical sample size to investigate the correlation between SII and α-Klotho in patients with albuminuria. Our objective was to elucidate the phenotypic profile of α-Klotho expression and the progression of the inflammatory response in humans. Our screening criteria were stringent, and the sample size was relatively adequate. We made several adjustments for different confounding variables. However, three limitations need to be acknowledged: firstly, the cross-sectional study design prevented the establishment of causality. Second, despite adjusting for several relevant confounders, we couldn’t entirely eliminate the effects of other potential confounding variables. Third, common loss of neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts may have led to selection bias.

Given the constraints of the NHANES database, our study falls under the cross-sectional design. In future investigations, we plan to explore other inflammatory factors, such as CRP, calcitonin, and sedimentation rate, in our clinical database, comparing their sensitivity to SII. The advantage of SII lies in its ability to assess the inflammatory status of the population based on existing complete blood count results. Furthermore, it can predict Klotho concentrations in populations with and without proteinuria.

The immune system has long been recognized as a prognostic influence in a variety of diseases. In the present cross-sectional study, we found that higher SII levels were independently associated with Klotho levels and that high Klotho levels were protective factors for elevated SII. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between SII and Klotho in a US population.

Neutrophils and lymphocytes are leukocytes that mediate nonspecific and specific immunity, respectively, and play a key role in the development and progression of inflammatory responses. Neutrophils release neutrophil elastase (NE), which plays a role in chronic inflammation, and the release of reactive oxygen species and NE in patients with elevated neutrophil activity may directly contribute to renal cell injury [48]. Neutrophil counts were confirmed to be the most reliable independent risk factor for CKD in a 2022 cross-sectional cohort study [48]. Lymphocytes are inflammatory mediators with protective and regulatory functions and have an immune recognition function role. Platelets are an atypical first-line inflammatory biomarker that attach to leukocytes and endothelial cells and alter the activity of the inflammatory components of these cells. Calculating SII from three circulating immune cells (neutrophils, lymphocytes and platelets) [34] provides more clinical information than one or two types of peripheral blood. patients with high SII levels are often associated with neutrophilia, thrombocytosis and lymphocytopenia. SII has also been shown to be more sensitive than neutrophil to lymphocyte count ratio (NLR), platelet to lymphocyte count ratio (PLR) better reflects the inflammatory state and has shown better prognostic value in several studies [34–39]. At the same time, SII has promising clinical applications due to its easy availability, noninvasive approach and low cost. Therefore, it has good prospects for clinical application. Several studies have been conducted using different epidemiological methods in patients with different types of kidney injury, confirming the association between SII and kidney disease [49–51]. Qin et al. found a positive association between SII and increased urinary albumin excretion in adults in the United States [52].

Complex pathophysiological mechanisms suggest that CKD is a state of Klotho deficiency in the kidney, plasma and urine and that Klotho downregulation may play a pathogenic role in CKD progression. Relevant human studies have shown a negative correlation between Klotho and ACR [53–55]. Albuminuria directly reduces Klotho expression through the recruitment of an inflammatory response [22]. In animal models, overall overexpression or exogenous supplementation of Klotho leads to a reduction in UAE [56]. The potential mechanism for increased urinary Klotho excretion in mice with a genetic predisposition to albuminuria may involve inhibition of the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, protection of podocytes from injury either directly in situ or through autocrine or paracrine effects [57], inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated apoptosis by Klotho protein through promotion of autophagy [58], induction of apoptosis mediated by Sirt1, PGC-1α ERR-1α and PPARα to reduce expression-mediated oxidative stress [59], and inhibition of TGF-β and TNF signaling to regulate vascular calcification and endothelial dysfunction [60].

The inflammatory response is a factor in the pathogenesis of many renal diseases and is involved in the progression of almost all renal diseases. Cellular senescence is usually accompanied by the generation of a senescence-associated secretory phenotype, which in turn leads to chronic low-grade inflammation and disease in vivo, which in turn accelerates the aging process in senescent cells and their neighbors. The initiation of an immunosenescence-like phenotype in monocytes is accompanied by activation of the CREB34L and TFE3 Golgi stress responses and the ATF6 and IRE1 endoplasmic reticulum stress responses, which upregulate pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, and overexpression of α-KL inhibits these changes [61]. Another study also confirmed that serum inflammatory factor levels in CKD patients were positively correlated with indolephenol sulfate and negatively correlated with α-KL. overexpression of α-Klotho not only inhibited nuclear factor (NF)-κB signaling and the release of factors such as transforming growth factor (TGF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, and IL-1β, but also facilitated the polarization of macrophages to the M2 type, which thereby inhibiting the systemic inflammatory response in CKD mice [62,63]. The findings suggest that Klotho can suppress CKD-associated systemic inflammation by inhibiting IS-induced RIG-1/NF-κB activation and monocyte inflammatory factor release [63].

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study demonstrates a robust negative independent correlation between high SII levels and elevated Klotho in the US adult albuminuric population, and that high Klotho levels are a protective factor against abnormally elevated SII, which may have predictive and diagnostic implications in clinical practice. Further prospective studies will be conducted in the future to validate our results.
