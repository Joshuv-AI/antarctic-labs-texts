Late presentation of an anomalous left coronary artery originating from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA) in a 70-year-old male: a case report
Authors: Seyedeh Zeinab Seyedi, Ali Abedi, Bita Mollashahi, Mohammad Namazinia, Seyed Javad Davari-Sani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA), also known as Bland–White–Garland syndrome, is a rare congenital anomaly with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 300,000 live births. More than 90% of untreated patients die within the first year of life, and survival into late adulthood is exceptionally uncommon, with only a few cases reported beyond the sixth decade. Such late presentations are clinically important, because they demonstrate the role of extensive collateral circulation in survival and pose unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Case presentationWe report the case of a 70-year-old Iranian man who presented with acute chest pain lasting 12 hours. On admission, his vital signs were stable, and electrocardiography revealed sinus rhythm with ST-segment depression in multiple leads. Serial troponin measurements were negative, and transthoracic echocardiography showed no abnormalities. Owing to persistent clinical suspicion, coronary computed tomography angiography was performed, which revealed anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery, with a markedly dilated right coronary artery supplying collaterals to the left coronary system. The patient underwent successful surgical reimplantation of the left main coronary artery into the aorta and was discharged in stable condition.ConclusionsThis case represents one of the oldest reported diagnoses of ALCAPA, underscoring the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for congenital anomalies even in elderly patients with atypical acute coronary presentations. It contributes to the limited literature on late survival without prior intervention and emphasizes the pivotal role of coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) in noninvasive diagnosis and surgical planning.

Introduction

Coronary artery anomalies (CAAs) are relatively uncommon, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1% in the general population [1, 2]. Many CAAs are identified incidentally during coronary angiography, where their prevalence has been reported to range from 1% to 5% among patients undergoing the procedure [3].

Among CAAs, anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA), also known as Bland–White–Garland syndrome, is a rare congenital coronary anomaly that accounts for approximately 0.25–0.5% of congenital heart diseases, with an estimated incidence of about 1 in 300,000 live births [4]. Without surgical correction, nearly 90% of affected infants die within the first year of life [5]. Survival into adulthood usually depends on the development of extensive right-to-left collateral circulation, which can preserve myocardial perfusion but may still result in coronary steal and chronic ischemic injury [4, 6].

Adult presentation of ALCAPA is exceptionally uncommon. Most reported adult patients present in early to middle adulthood with angina, arrhythmia, or heart failure, whereas reports in the elderly are exceedingly rare [4, 6]. Here, we describe the case of a 70-year-old man with ALCAPA who presented with acute chest pain and was diagnosed using coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA). This case underscores the importance of considering coronary anomalies in older patients with unexplained chest pain and nondiagnostic initial testing, while also contributing to the limited literature on late presentation and surgical outcomes.

Case history

A 70-year-old Iranian man presented to the emergency department with acute-onset chest pain lasting 12 hours. He was a retired office employee, a non-smoker, and reported no history of alcohol consumption. His past medical history was notable for hypertension and dyslipidemia, both managed with oral medications (an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and statin therapy). He had no prior history of cardiac evaluation or intervention. Family history was negative for premature coronary artery disease or sudden cardiac death. No relevant psychosocial stressors were identified.

On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable (blood pressure: 120/75 mmHg, heart rate: 80 bpm, respiratory rate: 16/minutes). Cardiovascular examination revealed normal heart sounds without murmurs, gallops, or rubs. Lung auscultation, as well as abdominal and neurological examinations, were unremarkable. The chest pain resolved spontaneously during the initial evaluation in the emergency department.

Electrocardiography (ECG) showed sinus rhythm with normal axis and ST-segment depression in the lateral, inferior, and precordial leads (V2–V6) (Fig. 1). Serial troponin I levels, measured six hours apart, were negative. Despite negative biomarkers and a normal echocardiogram, persistent clinical suspicion for coronary artery disease prompted further evaluation with coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA). CCTA revealed an anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the main pulmonary artery (Fig. 2). The right coronary artery (RCA) was markedly dilated (13 mm) and originated from the right coronary sinus (Fig. 3). The patient subsequently underwent surgical reimplantation of the left main coronary artery into the appropriate aortic sinus. The procedure was uneventful, and his postoperative course included a 48-h stay in the intensive care unit, followed by transfer to the general ward. He was discharged on postoperative day 7 in good condition.

At 1-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic, with no recurrence of chest pain or dyspnea. Echocardiography demonstrated preserved systolic function and no evidence of ischemia. He continued treatment with aspirin, a beta-blocker, and a statin, with further outpatient monitoring planned.

Discussion

This case highlights that even in elderly patients, ALCAPA may present with acute coronary syndrome-like symptoms despite negative biomarkers and unremarkable echocardiographic findings. Survival into adulthood with ALCAPA is uncommon and is typically attributed to robust intercoronary collateralization from the right coronary artery (RCA) to the left coronary system. These collaterals reduce the risk of infarction but predispose to a coronary steal phenomenon from the left coronary artery into the low-pressure pulmonary artery, resulting in ischemia. The markedly dilated RCA (13 mm) in our patient reflects long-standing collateral flow and explains survival into late adulthood, with new ischemic symptoms prompting presentation [7, 8].

Clinical classification and case uniqueness. ALCAPA is classically classified into infantile and adult forms. Some authors propose a “late-adult” subtype for presentations at or beyond 60 years of age. Our patient fits this late-adult category. Comparable reports in the seventh and eighth decades (ages 70, 73, 75, and 76 years) exist but remain exceedingly rare, underscoring the clinical significance of this case [8–12].

Clinical spectrum and relevance to the present case. Adult ALCAPA may manifest with angina, dyspnea, arrhythmias, or, in rare cases, sudden cardiac death. Our patient’s acute coronary syndrome (ACS)-like presentation, with nondiagnostic biomarkers and a normal transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE), lies within this spectrum. This emphasizes that conventional ACS algorithms may miss congenital anomalies if clinical suspicion is not maintained [8].

Diagnostic strategy and the role of CCTA. Transthoracic echocardiography in adults often fails to identify the anomalous coronary origin; indirect clues may include a dilated proximal RCA and retrograde flow into the pulmonary artery. Invasive coronary angiography delineates collateral filling and runoff but is limited by its invasiveness and primarily luminographic nature. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging adds functional and tissue characterization, including ischemia and scar detection. In this case, coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) was chosen for its high spatial resolution and three-dimensional delineation of ostial origin and course, enabling a definitive noninvasive diagnosis in an elderly patient despite negative troponins and a nondiagnostic TTE [13–16].

Prognosis and conservative management. Long-term outcomes after anatomical repair are generally excellent, with improvement in left ventricular function and symptom resolution. Current guidelines recommend surgical correction for adults with ALCAPA (Class I). However, some reports suggest that in asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients older than 50 years, individualized conservative management may be considered, as the estimated risk of sudden death declines with age while operative risks increase. Such decisions should be made in a multidisciplinary Heart Team setting. As our patient was symptomatic, surgical repair was the appropriate strategy [15, 17–19].

Surgical management and alternatives. The patient underwent reimplantation of the left main coronary artery into the appropriate aortic sinus. This is the preferred technique, as it restores a dual coronary system with near-normal physiology and avoids the late complications reported with the Takeuchi baffle. Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) with ligation of the anomalous vessel is reserved for cases, where reimplantation is not anatomically feasible. Our patient’s postoperative course was uneventful (48 hours in the intensive care unit, discharge on postoperative day 7), and he remained asymptomatic at 1-month follow-up while receiving aspirin, a beta-blocker, and a statin [20].

Conclusion

ALCAPA may rarely present in late adulthood through survival on extensive RCA-to-LCA collaterals, with eventual coronary steal leading to ischemic symptoms. In elderly patients with unexplained chest pain and nondiagnostic initial testing, CCTA should be considered early to exclude coronary anomalies. In this symptomatic 70-year-old man, anatomical reimplantation successfully restored a dual coronary system and yielded an excellent short-term outcome. This case expands the limited literature on late-adult ALCAPA and reinforces a critical message for clinicians: coronary anomalies should remain part of the differential diagnosis of chest pain—even in the elderly [8, 13]. Furthermore, this report emphasizes the importance of early diagnosis and individualized treatment planning in adult ALCAPA, as surgical correction can provide excellent long-term outcomes even in older patients.
