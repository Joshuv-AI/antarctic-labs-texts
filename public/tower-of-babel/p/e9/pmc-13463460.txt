Investigating the relationship between energy-adjusted dietary inflammatory index with anthropometric indices, clinical symptoms, inflammatory markers, sleep quality and quality of life in patients with rheumatoid arthritis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Amirabbas Moarefian, Hadi Bazyar, Fatemeh Borazjani, Forough Nokhostin, Ahmad Zare Javid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease causing joint inflammation, pain, and deformity, significantly impacting physical and mental well-being. Sleep disturbances and obesity exacerbate symptoms, increasing disease severity. Inflammation plays a key role in RA progression, and diet may influence inflammation. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) reflects the inflammatory potential of dietary intake, but its link to RA symptoms is unclear. This study explores DII associations with RA outcomes, sleep quality, anthropometric indices, and quality of life (QoL).MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 246 RA patients attending a rheumatology clinic in Ahvaz, Iran. Dietary intake was assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire, and the energy-adjusted Dietary Inflammatory Index (E-DII) was calculated. To analyze the data, we employed various statistical methods, including analysis of variance, chi-square tests, linear and Poisson regression, as well as logistic regression, as appropriate.ResultsIn this study, patients with RA (163 females and 83 males; mean age 50.40 ± 6.33 years) were evaluated. Higher E-DII was significantly associated with higher poor sleep quality across all models (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.14–1.95). Higher E-DII scores were significantly associated with increased odds of both general obesity (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.32–2.30) and central obesity (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.31–2.47). Additionally, our results demonstrated that higher E‑DII scores were significantly and directly associated with disease activity scores, CRP levels, and tender joint counts.ConclusionsOur study suggests that among RA patients, a higher intake of pro-inflammatory diet is linked with a higher likelihood of sleep deprivation, increased general and central obesity, and an increased tender joint counts. Further longitudinal studies are necessary to confirm these findings and elucidate the relationship between the E-DII and these outcomes in RA patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01380-7.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent joint inflammation, pain, swelling, and morning stiffness. Over time, it can lead to joint deformities and physical disability [1]. RA affects approximately 0.5–1% of the global population and around 0.37% of the population in Iran. The disease occurs more frequently in developed countries and is more prevalent among women [2, 3]. Sleep disturbances are highly prevalent among RA patients affecting between 54% and 70% and are linked to pain, fatigue, disease activity, and psychological comorbidities such as depression [4, 5]. Patients often experience non-restorative sleep, difficulty falling or staying asleep, and excessive daytime sleepiness, which further diminish pain tolerance, mood, cognitive functioning, and overall well-being [6–9]. Obesity is another factor intricately linked with both RA and inflammation [10]. It is characterized by a chronic low-grade inflammatory state driven by adipose tissue, which produces pro-inflammatory cytokines like tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [11]. Some studies have shown that obesity is associated with increased RA disease activity, reduced likelihood of achieving remission, and poorer treatment outcomes [12, 13]. Additionally, excess body weight negatively impacts physical functioning and sleep, further worsening quality of life (QoL) in RA patients [14]. The presence of obesity can also mask or complicate clinical assessments of disease activity, and long-term weight gain has been associated with a higher likelihood of RA development [15].

Although the precise etiology of RA remains unknown, approximately half of the risk can be attributed to genetic predisposition, with environmental and lifestyle factors such as smoking, obesity, and poor dietary choices also playing a role in disease onset and progression [16–18]. Inflammatory responses play a central role in RA pathogenesis, with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), TNF-α, and IL-6 being commonly observed in affected individuals [19, 20]. In patients with RA, liver and kidney abnormalities may occur as a consequence of chronic inflammatory burden, the underlying disease process, or, more commonly, long-term use of medications such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, and disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) [21]. The chronic inflammation associated with RA not only causes physical symptoms but also significantly impairs QoL [22, 23]. Numerous studies have shown that anti-inflammatory diets and foods can be effective in reducing inflammation [24–26]. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) is a tool developed by Shivappa et al. [27] to assess the inflammatory potential of dietary intake, evaluating 45 food parameters based on their effects on six inflammatory biomarkers, including IL-6, CRP, interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and TNF-α [28].

Higher DII scores, reflecting a pro-inflammatory dietary pattern, have been associated with an increased risk of metabolic syndrome, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease [29–31], as well as heightened systemic inflammation and poorer sleep quality [32, 33]. Conversely, evidence suggests that anti-inflammatory dietary patterns may attenuate inflammatory burden and reduce disease severity in patients with RA [34]. Although previous studies have investigated the DII in relation to rheumatoid arthritis, the association between the energy-adjusted DII and a comprehensive set of outcomes including anthropometric indices, clinical symptoms, inflammatory markers, sleep quality, and quality of life has not been extensively explored in this population. Therefore, the present study aims to address this gap by providing a more holistic evaluation of the potential role of diet-associated inflammation in RA management.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted on patients with RA attending the outpatient rheumatology clinic in Ahvaz City, Iran, in 2024. Based on an estimated prevalence of insufficient sleep quality of 81% in RA patients [35], a 95% confidence level, and a precision (d) of 5%, a sample size of 237 subjects was required. Accounting for an estimated 10% non-response rate, the target sample size was set at approximately 264 subjects. The diagnosis of RA followed the 2010 criteria established by the American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) [36]. Adults aged 18–65 years who had been diagnosed with RA for at least one year were eligible for inclusion. Individuals who were pregnant or lactating, those with serious comorbidities (such as heart failure, cancer, or renal failure), and patients with other connective tissue or joint diseases (including ankylosing spondylitis, gout, and lupus) were excluded. Additionally, individuals with Cushing’s syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, those who had undergone bone or joint surgery, and those adhering to specific dietary regimens or taking medications (e.g., for depression and anxiety) other than their routine RA medications for less than six months before the study were also not included. Furthermore, participants who reported a dietary intake of less than 800 or more than 4200 kcal per day (10 participants), or who did not provide adequate information regarding quality of life (3 participants) and sleep status (5 participants), were also excluded [34, 37]. In total, 246 RA patients meeting the eligibility criteria were included in this study. Participants were selected using simple random sampling. Prior to enrollment, they received comprehensive information about the study’s goals and potential benefits. The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences (IR.AJUMS.REC.1403.143). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Outcome ascertainment

The 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria for RA include the serologic abnormality (score range 0–3), increased acute-phase response (score from 0 to 1), number and location of involved joints (score from 0 to 5), and symptom duration (score from 0 to 1). To be diagnosed with definite RA, participants must have a total score of at least six points and demonstrate confirmed synovitis in at least one joint, which cannot be better explained by another condition [36].

Assessment of dietary intakes

Dietary intake over the past year was evaluated via face-to-face interviews conducted by trained dieticians, employing a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) comprising 168 items. During the interview, participants indicated how often they consumed each food, selecting from daily, weekly, monthly, or yearly frequencies. The reliability and validity of this specific FFQ had already been confirmed for the Iranian population [38]. Using the Household Measures Guide, standard unit sizes and items reported via household measures were converted to grams [39]. Energy values for the food frequency questionnaire items were derived from the USDA Food Ingredients table in the Nutritionist IV database, following modifications to account for Iranian food composition [40].

Assessment of dietary inflammatory index

The DII score is calculated based on 45 food parameters using the method described by Shivappa et al. [27]. DII score, where lower values indicate an anti-inflammatory diet and higher values a pro-inflammatory diet, was calculated for each participant using 30 specific dietary parameters. These parameters were selected based on the limitations of the dietary assessment tool available for the Iranian population, and included nutrients such as (Vitamin B12, Vitamin B9, Vitamin B6, Vitamin B2, Vitamin B1, Vitamin B3, Beta Carotene, Caffeine, Carbohydrate, Cholesterol, Energy, Total fat, Fiber, Garlic, Fe, Mg, MUFA, n-3 Fatty acids, n-6 Fatty acids, Onions, Proteins, PUFA, Saturated fat, Se, Vitamin D, Vitamin E, Vitamin C, Vitamin A, Zn, and peppers) (Supplementary Table S1). For each parameter, the participant’s intake was standardized by subtracting the standard global mean and dividing by the international standard deviation to create a z-score. This z-score was then converted to a centered percentile score (doubled and subtracted by 1) to minimize right-skewness. Each centered percentile was multiplied by its respective literature-derived inflammatory effect score [27]. Finally, the weighted scores for all 30 parameters were summed to yield the overall DII score for the participant. To decrease the effect of different energy intakes between participants, the energy-adjusted Dietary Inflammatory Index (E-DII) was calculated per 1000 kcal/day consumed [28].

Anthropometric assessments

Participants’ weight and height were recorded while standing in minimal clothing and no shoes. Weight was measured using a body composition analyzer (Type: GLAMOR GBF-830, China), and height was measured with a wall-mounted tape meter to the nearest 0.5 cm. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (in kg) by height (in m) squared. Waist Circumference (WC) was measured at the midpoint between the lower costal border and the iliac crest using a tape measure while Hip circumference (HC) was determined over the broadest region of the buttocks and was recorded to the closest 0.1 cm. The Waist-to-Hip Ratio (WHR) is calculated by dividing waist circumference by hip circumference, providing insight into body fat distribution. Similarly, the Waist-to-Height Ratio (WHtR) is determined by dividing waist circumference by height, offering a simple yet effective measure of obesity status. Based on our data, we classified participants based on general and abdominal obesity. General obesity was defined as having a BMI of 25 or higher. Abdominal obesity was determined using multiple indices: a waist circumference of 88 cm or more for females and 102 cm or more for males; a waist-to-hip ratio of 0.8 or higher for females and 1.0 or higher for males; and a waist-to-height ratio of 0.5 or above for individuals under 40 years old and 0.6 or above for those 40 years old and above. Measurements below these thresholds were considered normal [41].

Measurement of blood biomarkers

After fasting for 12 h overnight, a 10 ml venous blood sample was collected from all participants. The blood samples were then centrifuged, and the resulting serum were stored at -70 °C until further analysis. Using methodology recommended by the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC) [42], we determined serum concentrations of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and aspartate transaminase (AST). All these analyses were performed using commercial kits (Pars Azmon Inc). Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (Cr) concentrations were measured using enzymatic techniques. ESR was evaluated by the Westergren method, and Serum CRP levels and rheumatoid factor (RF) were measured using the quantitative kit (Pars Azmon Inc).

Severity of pain

Patients’ average pain levels during the previous week were measured using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). This scale uses a 0 to 100 range, where higher scores correspond to more intense pain [43].

Disease activity

Disease activity score 28 (DAS-28) is a validated index for monitoring the disease activity of RA [34]. The DAS-28 is a composite index derived from the counts of swollen and tender joints, levels of ESR, and patient self-assessment of global health via VAS. All participants underwent joint assessment by the study rheumatologist to ascertain swollen joints and tender joint counts, facilitating subsequent DAS-28 score calculation [34, 44].

Quality of life evaluation

Quality of life (QoL) was assessed using the validated Short Form-36 Health Status Questionnaire (SF-36) [45]. The SF-36 evaluates eight health-related QoL domains: physical performance, physical limitations, emotional limitations, energy and freshness, mental health, social performance, physical pain, and general health. Two summary measures, total physical health, and total mental health, are derived from distinct component scores. All dimensions score from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health-related QoL [45, 46].

Sleep quality evaluation

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to assess the quality and quantity of sleep that participants experienced over the past month [47]. This index consists of seven components that address various aspects of sleep, including latency, quality, efficiency, disturbances, daytime dysfunction, duration, and medication use. Each components is scored from 0 to 3, resulting in a total score that can range from 0 to 21. According to previously validated criteria, a PSQI score of less than 6 indicates good sleep quality, while a score of 6 or higher suggests poor sleep quality [48, 49].

Assessment of other variables

The individuals’ ages, marital status, duration of disease, levels of education, job status, and smoking status were all recorded by trained interviewers. To determine the levels of physical activity (PA), we used the globally recognized and validated Short Form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [50].

Statistical analysis

We first divided the E-DII scores into quartiles, and then grouped the study participants by these quartiles. Second, an analysis was conducted to compare participants’ characteristics across quartiles of E-DII score. Using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and chi-squared tests, respectively, we examined the values of continuous and categorical variables E-DII scores. Where ANOVA indicated a significant difference, pairwise multiple comparisons were performed by means of the Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc test. The participants’ dietary intakes across E-DII quartiles were assessed using ANOVA. Associations between the E-DII and various continuously measured outcomes, including anthropometric indices, clinical symptoms, inflammatory markers, sleep quality, and quality of life parameters were tested by linear regression analysis and using two models, Model 1: Unadjusted, Model 2: adjustment for potential confounders including age, sex, physical activity, job, marital status, education, body mass index, disease duration, smoking status, and medications. For outcomes representing count data, such as the number of swollen and tender joints, Poisson regression analysis was performed. Rate ratio (RR) along with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated; the first quartile of E-DII is considered for reference. In addition, multivariable logistic regression analysis was utilized to evaluate the associations between the E-DII quartiles and dichotomous outcomes including general obesity, central obesity (based on waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, and waist-to-height ratio), sleep quality, and RF status. Odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% CIs were calculated. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22. P-values were considered significant if they were less than 0.05.

Results

This study was conducted on 246 participants, consisting of 163 females and 83 males, whose average age was 50.40 ± 6.33 years. Table 1 presents the general characteristics of the study population across quartiles of E-DII. Participants in the highest E-DII quartile indicating the most pro-inflammatory diet showed significantly greater weight, BMI, and WC compared to those with lower scores. Other anthropometric indices did not show significant differences across the E-DII quartiles. Regarding disease activity and inflammatory markers, VAS, DAS28, CRP, ALP, and AST significantly increased across E-DII quartiles. No significant differences were observed in RF, BUN, Cr, ALT, and ESR. Additionally, participants in the highest E-DII quartile exhibited significantly higher PSQI scores (6.47 ± 2.35) compared to those in the lowest E-DII quartile (5.37 ± 1.93). No other significant difference was observed in age, sex, marital status, physical activity, duration of disease, smoking status, and total QoL score across the E-DII quartile (P > 0.05).

Table 3 shows that higher E-DII scores were significantly associated with anthropometric measures in RA patients. Specifically, each unit increase in E-DII was linked to higher body weight, BMI, WC, HC, WHR, and WHtR (all P < 0.05) in all models. Additionally, increased E-DII scores were significantly associated with elevated AST, ALT, and ALP in all models (P < 0.05), while BUN and Cr were not significantly related. Clinically, higher E-DII scores were associated with higher VAS and DAS28 scores as well as higher CRP levels, but no significant associations were observed with ESR (P > 0.05).

Table 5 shows the associations of the E-DII score with sleep quality and QoL parameters in RA patients. Higher E-DII scores were consistently associated with poorer sleep quality. In Model 1, a one-unit increase in E-DII was associated with a 0.64-point increase in the PSQI score (SE = 0.16, p < 0.001). This association remained statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (B = 0.60, SE = 0.19, p = 0.003), indicating that greater dietary inflammatory potential correlates with worse sleep quality. Our results did not demonstrate a significant association between E-DII scores and total QOL score. Additionally among the QoL components, only the mental health domain demonstrated a significant association with E-DII scores. In the crude model, higher E-DII scores were associated with a reduction in mental health (B = − 1.89, SE = 0.69, p = 0.007). Although this association became marginal in the fully adjusted model (B = − 1.64, SE = 0.81, p = 0.05).

Table 6 outlines the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals for various outcomes across E-DII quartiles in rheumatoid arthritis patients. General obesity and central obesity defined by WC were significantly associated with higher E-DII scores in all models. In contrast, central obesity based on WHR was not significantly associated with E-DII in any model, and for WHtR the crude model showed non-significant associations (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.94–1.52, p = 0.12) while significance emerged only after full adjustment (OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.03–1.85, p = 0.03). Additionally, higher E-DII scores were consistently associated with poorer sleep quality, with ORs of 1.43 (95% CI: 1.14–1.81, p = 0.002) in the crude model, and (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.14–1.95, p = 0.003) in the fully adjusted model. Finally, no significant associations were observed between E-DII scores and RF status in any model.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively examine the relationship between E-DII and anthropometric indices, clinical symptoms, inflammatory markers, sleep quality, and QoL in Iranian patients with RA. Our result indicate Participants in the lowest E-DII quartile consumed higher amounts of fiber, omega-3 fatty acids, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats, as well as several micronutrients including vitamin A, vitamin D, magnesium, zinc, selenium, and B vitamins. These nutrients are well-recognized for their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, which may contribute to modulating systemic inflammation in RA patients. The higher intake of omega-3 fatty acids and fiber in Q1 is particularly noteworthy, given their established role in reducing inflammatory markers and improving clinical outcomes in inflammatory disorders [51]. Interestingly, although total fat intake was higher in Q4, this appeared to be driven primarily by omega-6 fatty acids, which have been associated with pro-inflammatory pathways when consumed in excess relative to omega-3 fatty acids [52]. The higher intake of caffeine, garlic, and pepper in Q4 may reflect specific dietary habits rather than overall diet quality. Collectively, these findings support the construct validity of the E-DII in this population, as lower scores were associated with a dietary pattern rich in anti-inflammatory nutrients. This may have important implications for dietary recommendations aimed at reducing inflammation and disease burden in RA patients. Our study found that RA patients with higher E-DII scores had significantly increased odds of general obesity and central obesity, defined by WC and WHtR. Additionally, they exhibited poor sleep quality. A one-unit increase in the E-DII score was significantly associated with elevated levels of liver enzymes (AST, ALT, and ALP), CRP, and worse clinical indices of disease activity as indicated by VAS scores and DAS28. However, we did not find a significant association between E-DII scores and QoL, ESR, BUN, or Cr.

Our results indicate a direct and significant association between E-DII quartiles and both general and abdominal obesity. In line with us Ramallal et al. [53]. In the SUN cohort found that higher DII scores correlated with an increased risk of obesity. Additionally, a recent meta-analysis of four studies assessing the relationship between DII and obesity revealed that a pro-inflammatory diet is linked to a higher risk of obesity [54]. Additionally, Saber et al. [55]. demonstrated a significant association between higher E-DII scores and general obesity, though this was not the case for abdominal obesity. In contrast, some studies have reported no statistically significant associations. For example, Bavi et al. [41] observed a tendency toward higher DII values reflective of a more pro-inflammatory diet among individuals with general and central obesity, but these associations did not reach statistical significance. Similarly, a cross-sectional study by Neufcourt et al. [56]. indicated no significant relationship between DII scores and the odds of abdominal obesity. Moreover, our results showed that each one-unit increase in E-DII was associated with higher body weight, BMI, WC, HC, WHR, and WHtR. These findings align with Tavakolli et al. [57], who reported that as DII scores increased, so did key anthropometric measures, particularly weight and waist circumference. Another study conducted on an adolescent population showed a consistent association between DII and anthropometric parameters including BMI, WC, and WHtR in both males and females [58]. In contrast, a pilot study involving 124 obese individuals did not find any statistically significant association between the DII score and weight, BMI, WC, or WHtR [59]. Although most studies reveal a direct and significant relationship between DII and various anthropometric indices, to our knowledge, no study has specifically examined these associations in patients with RA. This highlights a clear need for further research in this particular population.

Our results indicate that RA patients who follow a predominantly pro-inflammatory diet are more likely to experience poor sleep quality. This finding is consistent with previous research on Italian adults, which showed that individuals with higher DII levels were less likely to have adequate sleep quality [60]. In another study among sleep apnea patients in Brazil, elevated DII scores predicted greater apnea severity and heightened daytime sleepiness [61]. Similarly, according to El-Ali et al. [62]. higher E-DII scores (top quartile) were associated with an elevated risk of poor sleep quality relative to the lowest quartile. Furthermore, a systematic review by Golmohammadi et al. [63]. suggested that adhering to an anti-inflammatory diet could potentially improve sleep outcomes. However, a separate study involving 379 college students in the United Arab Emirates reported no significant association between E-DII scores and sleep quality, indicating that the impact of dietary inflammatory potential on sleep may vary across populations [64].

Our results showed that higher E‑DII scores were not significantly associated with either physical or mental quality of life in fully adjusted models. This finding aligns with Song et al. [65], who reported no significant relationship between DII scores and QoL in post-menopausal women. In contrast, other studies have found that a pro-inflammatory diet is inversely and significantly associated with QoL among patients with knee osteoarthritis [66]. Additionally, Golmohammadi et al. [67]. conducted a systematic review of seven studies, which revealed a significant inverse association between DII scores and overall quality of life, as well as its subscales. This relationship was observed across a variety of populations, including individuals undergoing hemodialysis [68], patients with asthma [69], obese women [70], and patients with multiple sclerosis [71]. In contrast, our study did not observe a significant association between E-DII scores and QOL. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the study populations, variations in the adjustment for confounding factors, and inconsistent QoL assessment methods across studies.

We assessed disease activity, inflammatory markers, and joint involvement among RA patients. Our results demonstrated that increased E-DII scores were significantly associated with higher VAS scores, elevated disease activity scores, raised CRP levels, and a greater rate of tender joints. However, no significant associations were observed with ESR or the rate of swollen joints. Consistent with our findings, Tandorsost et al. [72]. reported that patients in the highest DII group exhibited significantly higher DAS28 values, a greater number of tender joints, and elevated levels of serum inflammatory markers such as hs-CRP and tumor necrosis factor-alpha. Moreover, in a cross sectional study, DII scores were significantly related to general health measured by VAS, as well as to the number of swollen joints, tender joints, and ESR levels [34]. In contrast, the trial by Matsumoto et al. [73]. found no significant association between the DII score and RA disease activity.

One possible mechanism connecting DII with RA is that a pro-inflammatory diet may raise the levels of inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, tumor necrosis factor alpha receptor 2 (TNFα-R2), and CRP. This increase may be associated with greater disease activity and joint inflammation, which could contribute to RA development [74]. The underlying inflammatory status in obesity remains not fully elucidated. Although adipose tissue is known to induce inflammation, this relationship appears bidirectional, creating a self-perpetuating cycle; excess energy intake and specific dietary components further contribute to inflammation [75]. Mechanistically, pro-inflammatory diets activate pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) via receptors like NOD-like receptors (NLRs) and Toll-like receptors (TLRs), driving inflammation in adipose tissue [76]. Furthermore, in chronic inflammatory conditions, adiposity-related signals such as insulin and leptin stimulate the peripheral sympathetic nervous system, which can lead to beta-adrenergic desensitization and, subsequently, to increased body fat accumulation [77]. Additionally, a heightened inflammatory diet may reduce the activity of key neurotransmitters, such as noradrenaline, dopamine, and serotonin, leading to alterations in sleep patterns [52, 78]. On the contrary, some nutrients common in anti-inflammatory diets such as tryptophan, magnesium, vitamin B6, and various antioxidants can facilitate the biosynthesis of melatonin and improve circadian regulation [79, 80].

The current study offers several advantages. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first investigation to examine the association between E-DII and sleep quality, anthropometric indices, and quality of life in Iranian patients with RA. Moreover, the use of E-DII minimizes confounding due to differences in overall food intake, allowing for a more refined comparison of dietary quality among individuals with varying caloric intakes. However, this investigation had several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents us from establishing a causal relationship between the E-DII and outcomes such as sleep quality, anthropometric indices, and QoL. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Reverse causality cannot be ruled out; for instance, RA severity, functional limitations, or medication use may influence dietary choices and, consequently, E-DII scores. Additionally, potential bidirectional relationships between inflammation, adiposity, sleep disturbances, and dietary patterns may exist. Second, there is potential for self-report bias, as data on diet, sleep, and quality of life were obtained via questionnaires. Third, residual confounding may exist due to unmeasured or unknown factors, for example, the use of dietary supplements. Additionally, our participants were RA patients from Ahvaz, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Another limitation of the present study is the large number of statistical tests performed across multiple outcomes, including anthropometric measures, inflammatory biomarkers, obesity definitions. As no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons (e.g., false discovery rate correction) was applied, there is an increased risk of type I error. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in future studies. Future research should focus on longitudinal and intervention studies to establish causal links between the E-DII and sleep, anthropometric indices, QoL, and other clinical outcomes in RA patients. Moreover, comparing the E-DII with other dietary patterns could help identify the most effective diet for improving the status of RA patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this cross-sectional study demonstrated that a higher intake of a pro-inflammatory diet is associated with an increased likelihood of sleep deprivation, general obesity, central obesity and a higher rate of tender joints in RA patients. However, no significant association was observed with QoL. Further longitudinal studies research is necessary to confirm these findings and further elucidate the relationship between the E-DII and these outcomes.
