From Care to Justice: Reframing Nursing Education Through Relational Ethics and Epistemic Inclusion
Authors: Eunice K Assem‐Erhaze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCaring forms the philosophical and moral foundation of nursing, yet nursing education often reproduces the epistemic exclusion it professes to resist. Writing as a nursing educator and disability justice advocate, I employ philosophical hermeneutics and critical analysis to interpret nursing education through the intersecting frameworks of Noddings' ethics of care, critical disability theory, and Fricker's account of epistemic injustice. I argue that the faculty‐student relationship functions as a moral microcosm in which nursing's ethical identity is either enacted or fractured, and that for students with learning disabilities, authentic caring requires a fundamental reorientation from benevolent provision to relational reciprocity. Through analysis of the hidden curriculum as a site of moral withdrawal, I develop the distinction between ‘caring for’ and ‘caring with’, proposing co‐agency as the relational and institutional expression of genuine caring pedagogy. I further examine how bureaucratic structures and the feminized distribution of moral labour constrain relational care, and argue that structural reform is a moral necessity rather than a procedural adjustment. The paper concludes by integrating care and justice as mutually constitutive: justice is the structural expression of care, and care is the relational practice through which justice is enacted in everyday educational life. Caring pedagogy is not preparatory to ethical nursing practice; it is one of its earliest expressions.Keywords: co‐agency, disability justice, epistemic justice, ethics of care, learning disabilities, nursing education, nursing philosophy, relational pedagogy

Introduction

Caring forms the philosophical and moral foundation of nursing, anchoring the profession's commitment to human dignity, relational ethics and alleviating suffering (Watson 1988; Swanson 1991). I write this paper as a nursing educator and advocate for disability justice, drawing on sustained work alongside nursing students who identify with learning disabilities and on my commitments to equity and inclusive pedagogy. I write from the standpoint of a nondisabled scholar, and this positionality requires ongoing attentiveness to the epistemic authority of those whose lives are directly shaped by disability.

Within nursing education, a troubling paradox emerges. While care is celebrated as central to professional identity, many students with learning disabilities experience marginalization and differential recognition within professional training environments (Englund and Lancaster 2022). Epistemic injustice, as Fricker (2007) defines it, refers to harm done to a person specifically in their capacity as a knower: when their testimony is disbelieved or when the conceptual frameworks available within their social world are inadequate to make sense of their experience. Interpreted through this lens, such patterns suggest that certain forms of knowledge and experience may be systematically discounted within professional discourse. Recent scholarship calls for relational ethics grounded in structural inclusion (Wolfs et al. 2024), and Lemon (2023) argues that inclusive pedagogy must cultivate belonging as an ethical relation rather than a procedural requirement. I extend this work by integrating disability justice and epistemic care into the moral ontology of nursing education.

Historically, nursing education has often conceptualised caring either as a professional virtue or as a competency to be evaluated (Beck 1993; Bradshaw 1999; van Hooft 1999). When care becomes an observable attribute rather than a relational ethic, an important question becomes obscured: if caring underpins nursing's moral identity, why is it inconsistently enacted within nursing education? The moral commitments guiding patient care do not automatically translate into educational environments, particularly when institutional norms prioritise efficiency, performance and professional gatekeeping. For students with learning disabilities, this contradiction carries particular weight: studies of nursing students with dyslexia document experiences of marginalization and limited recognition within professional training environments (Morris and Turnbull 2006, 2007), suggesting that certain forms of knowledge may be systematically discounted within professional discourse. Drawing on disability justice (Invalid 2019) and epistemic justice (Fricker 2007), I argue that caring pedagogy must be understood as a relational and institutional practice rather than an individual virtue, and that reinterpreting caring through relational reciprocity allows inclusion to be framed as a moral condition for the integrity of nursing education itself.

Philosophical Methodology

This enquiry employs philosophical hermeneutics and critical analysis to interpret nursing education through the intersecting lenses of Noddings' ethics of care, disability justice and epistemic justice. The aim is interpretive rather than empirical: to examine the moral and ontological assumptions shaping educational relationships and to evaluate their ethical adequacy. Two methodological orientations guide this analysis. Hermeneutic interpretation, drawing on Gadamer (2004), seeks to understand how caring and knowing are constituted within lived educational practice. Critical analysis then evaluates whether these practices embody justice, reciprocity and inclusivity. These orientations operate in a mutually corrective relationship (i.e. hermeneutics deepens understanding of meaning and context, while critique evaluates those meanings in relation to ethical responsibility and social justice). This interpretive and critical pairing aligns with recent scholarship in nursing philosophy that situates care ethics within broader discussions of relational autonomy and institutional ethics (Bolton 2004; Held 2005).

Guided by Gadamerian hermeneutics and feminist care ethics, educational practice is treated as a site of moral dialogue rather than as an object of measurement. Following Gadamer's concept of the fore‐structure of understanding, interpretation is always situated. Moral and experiential horizons shape how educational practices are perceived and interpreted, yet these horizons can shift through reflexive engagement with differing perspectives. This interpretive stance resonates with the broader tradition of nursing philosophy in which enquiry moves beyond description toward normative reflection and ethical transformation (Ocean et al. 2022). Within this synthesis, philosophy functions as an ethical enquiry into the moral conditions of educational practice. It becomes a means of examining how the values nursing professes are realised or contradicted within the everyday structures of education.

Scope and Argument

I argue that re‐centring caring relationships in nursing education, particularly within faculty–student interactions that challenge normative expectations, is essential to nursing's moral foundation and professional integrity. The faculty–student relationship functions as a microcosm of nursing's ethical life, in which tensions among authority and empathy, evaluation and empowerment, and accountability and responsiveness are continually negotiated (Chipeta and Hwang 2024). How educators navigate these tensions determines whether caring remains rhetorical or becomes ontological, the lived expression of nursing's moral core. This argument develops in five interconnected parts. Part One establishes the theoretical foundation by integrating Noddings' ethics of care, critical disability theory and Fricker's account of epistemic injustice. Part Two examines how institutional forces, including the hidden curriculum, may reproduce exclusion and ableism. Part Three develops the distinction between ‘caring for’ and ‘caring with’, arguing that authentic caring pedagogy requires the latter orientation of reciprocity and shared moral agency. Part Four considers the ethical tensions educators face when enacting relational care within bureaucratic institutions. Part Five proposes a vision of transformative caring pedagogy that aligns relational ethics, disability justice and structural reform. Although this analysis centres on learning disabilities, its philosophical claims regarding relational care and epistemic justice extend to broader disability contexts. Learning disabilities are emphasised here because of their relative invisibility within nursing discourse and because they illuminate how epistemic and moral exclusion often operate together within professional education. When nursing educators practice relational caring by recognizing students with learning disabilities as moral and epistemic agents, engaging in authentic dialogue, and advocating for structural justice, they enact the profession's core ethical commitments.

Part 1: Theoretical Foundations

Feminist Perspectives on Relational Ethics

Contemporary feminist philosophers have expanded care ethics by connecting relational ethics to the institutional conditions that shape moral life. Gauthier‐Mamaril (2022) reconceptualizes autonomy as relational, a capacity sustained through caring relationships and supported by just institutions. Schuchter and Heller (2018) interpret care as both a critical and restorative practice, linking moral effort to the creation of ethical organizations. Gómez‐Vírseda et al. (2020) describe relational autonomy as the bridge between care and justice, transforming care from an individual virtue into a structural and institutional responsibility. Tronto (2013) argues that democratic caring requires the redistribution of care responsibilities across institutions and communities. Laurin and Martin (2022) develop this insight within nursing contexts, advocating for more collective and democratic forms of institutional care practice capable of alleviating the moral distress that arises when caring labour falls unevenly on individuals.

Wolfs et al. (2024) extend this reasoning by reimagining the professional covenant as a shared social ethic of accountability rather than a one‐directional moral obligation. Together, these perspectives suggest that care, when understood ontologically, cannot remain confined to interpersonal relationships. It requires institutional structures that sustain justice and recognition for learners whose ways of knowing are frequently marginalized within professional education. Kittay (2019) argues that dependency is a fundamental feature of human life and that institutions carry an ethical responsibility to recognise rather than conceal it. Critical disability scholars extend this insight, demonstrating how ableist institutional cultures render vulnerability morally suspect and systematically exclude those whose needs challenge normative assumptions (Campbell 2009; Goodley 2014). Slote (2007) conceptualises care as a reflective moral psychology grounded in emotional responsiveness, empathy and reciprocal recognition. Within this framework, care becomes an epistemic practice, one that allows individuals and institutions to recognise forms of knowledge that might otherwise remain excluded from professional discourse.

Epistemic Justice as Caring Praxis

Fricker (2007) account of epistemic injustice identifies two primary forms. Testimonial injustice occurs when a speaker receives less credibility than they deserve because of identity prejudice, for example, when a student who discloses dyslexia encounters skepticism regarding their competence or academic integrity. Hermeneutical injustice occurs when individuals are disadvantaged because the conceptual frameworks available within a social context are insufficient to interpret their experiences, for instance, when educational institutions lack the language or conceptual tools to recognise non‐normative cognitive styles as legitimate. Together, these forms of injustice undermine both the credibility of individuals and the visibility of their experiences, constituting a direct harm to persons as knowers.

Integrating Noddings' ethics of care with epistemic justice helps avoid paternalistic interpretations of care by foregrounding the epistemic and moral authority of individuals with disabilities. Critical disability theory reframes disability not as an inherent deficit but as an outcome of social, cultural and institutional design (Devlin and Pothier 2006). Applied to learning disabilities, this perspective shifts attention from individual limitation toward the rigidity of educational structures. Within many nursing programmes, learning disabilities are treated as problems to be managed rather than as opportunities to reconsider pedagogical design and relational inclusion (Campbell 2009; Scullion 2010).

Disability justice scholarship further emphasizes that people with disabilities are producers of moral knowledge rather than merely recipients of care (Kafer 2013; Garland‐Thomson 2020). This insight resonates with Noddings (1984) concept of engrossment, which requires attentiveness to others' experiences and perspectives. Genuine care requires epistemic humility, openness to perspectives that challenge dominant assumptions about intelligence, competence and professional identity. Gadamer's hermeneutic philosophy reinforces this orientation: understanding depends on openness to others' perspectives, and listening without rigid preconceptions becomes both an epistemic and an ethical practice.

Philosophical scholarship has also explored how learning disabilities may function as epistemic resources rather than limitations (Chapman and Carel 2022; Garland‐Thomson 2020; Kafer 2013). Chapman and Carel (2022) argue that learning disabilities can enrich moral imagination and challenge dominant epistemic frameworks. In the context of nursing education, this insight suggests that neurodivergent perspectives may also expand prevailing understandings of professional and clinical knowledge. Chapman and Carel (2022) argue that neurodivergent cognitive styles, including forms of creative reasoning, sustained attention and reflective depth, can enrich moral imagination and challenge dominant epistemic frameworks. In nursing education, this suggests that neurodivergent perspectives may also expand prevailing understandings of professional and clinical knowledge. Carel and Kidd (2014) extend Fricker's analysis to clinical healthcare settings, arguing that testimonial injustice shapes patient–clinician relationships organized around expert hierarchies. This dynamic can be extended to educational cultures where students, and particularly students with disabilities, are positioned as credentialing rather than as credible. When faculty recognise the cognitive and moral knowledge that students with learning disabilities bring to educational relationships, they are not lowering standards but deepening their understanding of what it means to know in nursing.

It is important to distinguish care from empathy, as these concepts are often conflated within nursing discourse. Empathy refers to an emotional capacity to resonate with another person's feelings (Wu 2021). Care, as conceptualised here, refers to a deeper ontological commitment, a sustained orientation of being with others that shapes how knowledge itself is formed (Ghanbari‐Afra et al. 2022). Gadamer (2004) hermeneutic philosophy grounds this insight: understanding depends on openness to others' perspectives, and genuine listening becomes both an epistemic and an ethical practice. Reynolds et al. (2000) offer empirical support for this claim within nursing contexts, documenting how relational attentiveness shapes moral perception in clinical practice.

Noddings' Relational Ethics and Its Limits

Noddings (1984, 1999) situates moral knowledge within three interrelated processes. The first is engrossment: attentive engagement with another person. The second is motivational displacement: temporarily setting aside personal priorities to meet another's needs. The third is reciprocity: a relational completion when the cared‐for recognises and acknowledges the care offered. Care, therefore, cannot be understood as a one‐directional act of giving; it is a dynamic relational encounter that depends upon mutual recognition. Within educational contexts, this insight carries important implications: caring remains incomplete unless students experience themselves as genuinely recognised within the learning relationship.

Scholars have raised important critiques of Noddings' framework. Critics argue that care ethics may unintentionally romanticise women's moral labour and overlook the structural conditions that shape inequality and injustice (Crigger 1997; Held 2005). These concerns are particularly relevant within nursing, where the ethic of care can be absorbed into forms of invisible emotional labour that remain unrecognized or undervalued. Such critiques highlight the importance of situating relational care within broader social and political contexts.

Integrating Noddings' work with disability justice (Invalid 2019) and epistemic justice (Fricker 2007) addresses these limitations by framing care as both relational and political. Care involves not only responsiveness within interpersonal relationships but also attention to the institutional structures that enable or constrain recognition and participation. When care is understood simultaneously as relational engagement and structural critique, its ethical scope expands: it becomes both an affective relation and a commitment to social justice (Laurin and Martin 2022; Tronto 2013; Wolfs et al. 2024).

Care Ethics and Relational Ontology

The philosophical tradition of caring in nursing has been profoundly shaped by Jean Watson (1988) Theory of Human Caring, which grounds the profession's moral identity in transpersonal caring within a humanistic‐existential orientation. Watson conceptualises caring as an existential commitment that transcends technical action and sustains human dignity through presence and moral intent. Although her work situates caring primarily within the nurse–patient relationship, the concept can be extended to nursing education by understanding caring as a pedagogical and institutional ontology, a moral way of being that shapes how nurses are educated, recognised, and included within professional communities.

Joan Tronto (2013) political ethics of care broadens this moral vision by linking care to collective responsibility and by emphasizing that justice depends on the social and political conditions that enable care to flourish. Where Watson grounds care in a humanistic orientation, Tronto highlights its political dimensions, showing how institutional arrangements may sustain or obstruct moral practice. Tronto (2013) associates each phase of care with a corresponding moral element, attentiveness, responsibility, competence, responsiveness and caring with, each of which makes normative demands not only on individual practitioners but on the institutions within which they work. Applied to nursing education, this framework suggests that an institution claiming to value care must demonstrate attentiveness to the conditions students face, take responsibility for designing accessible environments, and respond meaningfully when care fails. Nel Noddings (1988) brings care into the immediacy of relational experience, positioning it as a reciprocal and contextually grounded encounter. Drawing on Noddings allows the moral and political dimensions of care to be brought together: caring pedagogy functions as both an ethical relation and an institutional foundation for nursing's professional identity.

This argument is further clarified through a phenomenological understanding of care. Heidegger (1962) described human existence as constituted through Mitsein, being‐with others in the world. Relation becomes the ground of moral knowing; institutional ethics represents the social expression of that relational condition. From this perspective, caring precedes deliberate ethical choice: it forms the relational fabric through which responsibility, reciprocity, and justice become possible. Ontology in this context refers to the moral infrastructure of being‐with others that educational institutions either sustain or undermine through their practices.

Part 2: The Hidden Curriculum as Moral Silence

Epistemic Norms and the Hidden Curriculum

Within nursing education, caring is often described as the profession's moral core. However, the deeper meanings of care are frequently obscured by what educational theorists describe as the hidden curriculum, the implicit values, hierarchies and epistemic assumptions that shape what students actually learn within professional education (Bevis and Watson 1989; Diekelmann 2001; Kelly 2020; Raso et al. 2019). The hidden curriculum operates through everyday practices, expectations and interactions that communicate who is recognised as a legitimate knower and what forms of knowledge are considered credible. These messages are rarely articulated directly, yet they powerfully shape professional identity and participation (Fricker 2007).

Within nursing education, the hidden curriculum often privileges particular cognitive and behavioural norms. Linear reasoning, rapid decision‐making, independence and confident communication are frequently treated as markers of professionalism. Although these traits may appear neutral, they often reflect ableist assumptions about cognitive uniformity and normative performance. Students whose learning processes differ from these expectations encounter implicit barriers to recognition and participation. For students with learning disabilities, these unspoken expectations produce a dual form of marginalization: their ways of knowing may be subtly devalued while simultaneously remaining invisible within institutional discourse. Experiences of difficulty or difference are often interpreted as personal shortcomings rather than as signals that pedagogical structures may require adaptation. Qualitative studies consistently document that students with learning disabilities navigate professional education without adequate acknowledgement of their needs, developing compensatory strategies independently and often concealing their experiences to avoid stigma (Denhart 2008; Ridley 2011). Such reflections illustrate how epistemic invisibility, rather than overt exclusion, becomes part of the silent curriculum of belonging. Over time, professional competence becomes intertwined with conformity to a narrow cognitive style. Faculty members may feel uncertain about how to address differences in learning and respond by maintaining polite distance. Students, aware of the stigma associated with disclosure, may remain silent about their experiences. This dynamic can constitute a form of moral withdrawal in which dialogue diminishes and relational engagement weakens.

Moral Withdrawal and Epistemic Silence

From the perspective of Noddings' ethics of care, such silence represents a significant failure of care. Caring requires attentiveness, responsiveness and a willingness to enter relational vulnerability. The hidden curriculum often produces the opposite dynamic, encouraging caution and self‐protection rather than openness. Educators may fear accusations of bias; students may fear stigma or negative judgement. The resulting atmosphere is one of polite quietness that conceals exclusion. I understand this as moral withdrawal: a relational breakdown that can interrupt dialogue and weaken the foundations of care.

Empirical studies confirm that this withdrawal is not merely theoretical. Across higher education and nursing contexts, students with learning disabilities consistently report concealing their diagnoses and developing compensatory strategies in response to perceived social and institutional pressures, managing others' discomfort rather than receiving acknowledgement of their own needs (Denhart 2008; L'Ecuyer 2019; Ridley 2011). These accounts illustrate that the hidden curriculum operates not only pedagogically but also ethically, shaping who is recognised as a credible knower within nursing education.

Addressing this moral silence requires attention to epistemic repair, a concept developed within the epistemic justice literature to describe the process of acknowledging patterns of past discrediting, developing new interpretive frameworks that make marginalized experiences intelligible, and recognizing alternative forms of knowledge as legitimate (Fricker 2007; Kidd et al. 2017). This process resonates with Noddings (1984) concept of engrossment, which describes a form of moral attentiveness that suspends preconceptions and allows genuine dialogue to emerge. Through such attentiveness, educational relationships can begin to move beyond silence toward recognition, reciprocity and shared understanding.

The Transition From Student to Practitioner

The consequences of moral withdrawal become particularly visible during the transition from student to practitioner. During nursing education, students with learning disabilities may receive formal accommodations such as extended examination time or assistive technologies. However, these supports frequently diminish or disappear after graduation, as individuals move into professional practice. This abrupt shift reveals a deeper philosophical tension: if care is treated as a temporary educational provision, it is not being understood as a continuing ethical commitment. The discontinuity also reflects institutional assumptions about disability, that it is a temporary educational challenge to be managed rather than a dimension of identity that accompanies individuals throughout their professional lives. Care cannot be simply a procedural response to institutional requirements, applied temporarily and then withdrawn; it must be understood as a shared moral foundation grounded in recognition, reciprocity and epistemic humility. The hidden curriculum, by weakening relational engagement and normalizing silence around difference, prepares the ground for this discontinuity, but also reveals the possibility for transformation. Reorienting educational relationships toward dialogue and mutual recognition invites a shift from models of caring that position educators as benevolent providers toward relational practices that emphasise shared participation and responsibility.

Part 3: Reciprocity, Co‐Agency and the Reclamation of Power

Caring for Versus Caring With

Moving beyond moral withdrawal requires nursing education to reconstruct the relational foundations of care. Designing curricula and assessment frameworks according to the principles of universal design allows students to demonstrate competence through multiple pathways, such as oral explanation, written reflection, collaborative reasoning and embodied clinical practice, consistent with capabilities‐based approaches to evaluating student learning (Walker 2008). Caring tends to preserve hierarchical asymmetry: the educator provides care while the student receives it. Although often motivated by good intentions, this arrangement can unintentionally reinforce dependence and limit student agency. Care becomes something granted rather than something shared, and the educator's moral authority remains largely unquestioned.

Caring with represents a different relational orientation. Here, care is understood as a collaborative moral practice that emerges through dialogue and shared participation. Educators and students work together to shape the conditions of learning, recognition and ethical understanding. The concept of co‐agency helps clarify how such relational care can operate within educational settings. Drawing on Anne Winter (2018) account of relational equality in education, caring relationships can be understood as spaces in which participants engage one another as members of a shared moral community rather than as occupants of fixed hierarchical roles. In this sense, co‐agency refers to the mutual enactment of responsibility and recognition within caring relationships, affirming that each participant can act as both a moral subject and a moral partner even within structures that maintain differences in authority. Co‐agency does not eliminate power differences, but it changes how power functions. Authority shifts from control toward cooperation and from benevolence toward reciprocity. Students with learning disabilities, in particular, possess experiential insights that can deepen pedagogical awareness and refine educational practice. When care is enacted through co‐agency, these perspectives are treated not as obstacles to overcome but as valuable contributions to the ethical and epistemic life of nursing education.

Rethinking Competence Through Caring With

Consider a clinical instructor working with a student nurse with dyslexia who is preparing for a high‐stakes medication calculation assessment. Within a caring‐for model, the instructor might grant additional time, fulfilling an accommodation requirement and signalling procedural compliance, but leaving the interaction largely one‐directional. A caring with approach begins differently. The instructor initiates dialogue: ‘Tell me how you usually approach dosage problems and what strategies help you reason through them’. This invitation shifts the interaction from accommodation to collaboration. Together, they might develop a strategy that reflects the student's cognitive strengths, a colour‐coded reasoning method combined with verbal explanation, allowing the student to externalise their analytic process. In such a scenario, the student may demonstrate clear conceptual understanding, even if the reasoning unfolds through a different cognitive pathway. This encounter may also prompt reflection on the assessment structure itself: timed examinations often reward speed and automatic recall, yet safe medication practice depends more on careful reasoning and verification than on rapid calculation. The accommodation, therefore, becomes more than a procedural adjustment; it becomes an epistemic moment that invites expanded understanding of what competence means. Such examples illustrate that caring with is not a gesture of sentimental inclusion; it represents ethical collaboration grounded in dialogue and mutual learning. In this relational process, both educator and student participate in revising their understanding of knowledge, competence and care. Within this praxis, caring evolves from a static professional virtue into a dynamic epistemic practice through which justice, recognition and relational understanding develop together.

Relational Power as Moral Practice

Noddings (1984, 1999) reminds us that care does not eliminate power; it places moral responsibility upon how power is exercised. In educational relationships, power becomes ethical when it supports rather than diminishes others' agency. Co‐agency, therefore, requires reflexive authority: educators must remain aware of their institutional position and deliberately use their influence to foster participation, dialogue and shared responsibility. This perspective resonates with feminist care theorists who extend the moral significance of care beyond interpersonal kindness into institutional and public life. Held (2005) argues that institutions themselves must embody the relational responsibilities of care rather than leaving care solely to individual actors. Similarly, Clegg and Rowland (2010) describe professional education as a space where relational practices such as kindness, attentiveness and ethical presence must continually negotiate with bureaucratic systems and managerial rationality.

When students' lived experiences inform the design of educational practices, care shifts from discretionary kindness to a generative institutional principle. For students with learning disabilities, this transformation can be emancipatory: rather than being positioned as recipients of accommodations, they are recognised as contributors whose perspectives enrich moral and pedagogical understanding. Ogbonna and Harris (2004) observe that when the relational work of teaching is devalued institutionally, educators may experience increasing alienation from the moral dimensions of their practice. This dynamic ultimately harms both faculty and students. Faculty members also experience a transformation within a co‐agency framework: authority is no longer understood primarily as a mechanism for control or evaluation but as an ethical resource that enables collaboration and shared enquiry. Through co‐agency, educator and learner participate together in shaping the conditions of ethical learning, enacting the relational foundations of nursing's moral commitments and renewing the profession's orientation toward justice, relational responsibility and shared human recognition.

Part 4: Boundaries, Bureaucracy and the Feminized Burden

The Bureaucratization of Care

The feminist ethics of care reminds us that moral responsibility does not reside solely in individual virtue; it also depends on the social and institutional arrangements that sustain or undermine caring relationships. Held (2005) emphasizes that ethical care must be supported by the structures within which people live and work. This perspective clarifies a persistent paradox within nursing education: the bureaucratic systems designed to ensure professionalism and fairness can also suppress the relational conditions that make genuine care possible.

Noddings (2013) observes that conflict and moral tension are unavoidable elements of caring relationships. However, within bureaucratised academic environments, these risks intensify. The procedural logic of grading rubrics, standardized assessments and formal fairness policies can transform moral engagement into administrative compliance. Faculty who invest relationally in students may fear accusations of partiality; students who seek connection may be interpreted as overly dependent. In such contexts, care may become performative, appearing as courteous empathy but rarely developing into the deeper relational presence that care ethics envisions. Hochschild (2012) demonstrates how emotional labour within institutional settings is frequently rendered invisible and undercompensated. Bolton (2004) develops this analysis within nursing and workplace contexts, showing how caring within bureaucratic systems intensifies this invisibility. Within care ethics, such dynamics constitute a form of moral labour, the relational and ethical work of sustaining genuine care, whose costs remain largely unrecognized within institutional structures. Educators must continually balance empathy with evaluation and relational attentiveness with administrative responsibility (Zhai et al. 2025). When caring is expected but insufficiently supported, it can gradually shift into emotional labour largely invisible within institutional structures and often falling disproportionately upon educators shaped by gendered expectations of compassion and relational responsibility (Bolton 2004; Hochschild 2012).

The Feminized Burden of Moral Labour

The gendered dimensions of moral labour in education often remain insufficiently acknowledged. Women faculty, particularly those from marginalized communities, frequently encounter a double bind within academic institutions: they may be criticised as uncaring when enforcing institutional standards and judged as unprofessional when demonstrating empathy or relational engagement with students (Ahmed 2020). This tension is not incidental but structural, reflecting the ways in which institutions simultaneously demand and penalise the relational and emotional labour associated with feminist and engaged pedagogy (hooks 1994). Within a profession that publicly celebrates care while simultaneously constraining it through bureaucratic systems, this contradiction reproduces many of the inequities that nursing seeks to challenge.

The distribution of relational and moral labour within academic institutions is rarely neutral. Gender shapes this distribution in ways that feminist care scholars have extensively documented, with caring and mentoring responsibilities falling disproportionately on women faculty (Bolton 2004; Pettersen 2012). Ahmed (2020) extends this analysis to race, demonstrating how educators of colour are frequently conscripted into diversity and inclusion work that remains institutionally unrecognized and unrewarded. Together, these patterns suggest that faculty members who occupy marginalized positions, whether by gender, race, or disability, often become the informal custodians of inclusive practice within their institutions, absorbing its ethical and emotional costs without the structural support they need.

Academic workload models commonly reward quantifiable outputs, publications, credentials and standardized evaluation metrics, while activities such as mentorship, relational teaching, and the cultivation of inclusive learning environments receive less institutional recognition. As a result, the moral labour required to sustain inclusive educational spaces may remain invisible within formal systems of evaluation. Andersson et al. (2022) find in a systematic review that effective ethics education requires attention to both critique and care. This has structural implications: when institutions fail to recognise relational moral labour, they risk undermining the very commitments ethics education is designed to cultivate.

Boundaries as Ethical Contours

Boundaries within caring relationships should not be confused with emotional distance or detachment. Properly understood, boundaries function as the ethical contours that sustain care: they allow educators to remain present without becoming enmeshed, to demonstrate empathy without losing professional clarity, and to maintain accountability without exhausting their moral and emotional resources. Kristoffersen (2019) observes that healthy boundaries preserve moral endurance by defining the conditions under which caring relationships can continue over time. When faculty members become overburdened by institutional pressures, they may withdraw into emotional distance as a means of self‐protection. For students, particularly those with learning disabilities, this withdrawal can be experienced not as neutrality but as absence. One student with dyslexia described this experience, noting that the difficulty was not that educators explicitly questioned their abilities, but that they felt unseen within the educational environment (Ridley 2011). Such invisibility rarely arises from deliberate neglect; more often, it reflects systemic conditions that make sustained relational engagement difficult or risky for educators. Authentic caring pedagogy, therefore, requires both individual and institutional commitment: educators must remain attentive to the relational responsibilities of teaching, while institutions must create conditions that make such attentiveness possible, providing time for mentorship, recognizing emotional and pedagogical labour, and valuing relational care as a legitimate form of professional excellence (Held 2005).

Part 5: Reconciling Care and Justice

The Moral Dimension: Caring as Epistemic Justice

Reimagining caring pedagogy through disability ethics and epistemic justice requires a movement beyond sentiment toward structural transformation. Care must shift from an individual virtue to a collective moral responsibility embedded within educational institutions.

The preceding analysis suggests that disability ethics does not simply add a new perspective to care ethics but substantially extends and strengthens it. By foregrounding epistemic justice, structural inclusion, and the moral authority of disabled knowers, disability ethics reveals dimensions of caring that relational ethics alone cannot fully address: the institutional conditions that make genuine care possible, the epistemic practices through which recognition is granted or withheld, and the structural reforms through which caring commitments must be expressed if they are to carry moral force. Caring begins with moral attentiveness: recognizing students as moral and epistemic agents whose experiences carry ethical significance. Students with learning differences are not simply recipients of support but contributors to the collective process of knowledge formation. This recognition requires epistemic justice, understood as restoring credibility and interpretive authority to individuals whose voices have historically been marginalized or dismissed (Fricker 2007; Chapman and Carel 2022). In educational practice, classroom interactions and assessment practices must function as spaces of recognition rather than solely as instruments of measurement. Expanding the modes through which competence can be demonstrated, oral explanation, reflective dialogue, collaborative reasoning, and embodied clinical understanding, allows knowledge to appear in multiple forms. Within this framework, epistemic humility becomes both an ethical disposition and a pedagogical method: as a virtue, it encourages educators to remain open to perspectives that challenge established assumptions about intelligence and competence; as a method, it creates relational conditions in which justice becomes visible through everyday educational practice.

The Relational Dimension: Caring as Co‐Agency

Noddings (2013) principle of reciprocity gains renewed significance when understood through co‐agency, a concept central to both disability justice and relational equality. Co‐agency provides a practical expression of relational autonomy: agency that develops and is sustained through dependable relationships and equitable institutional structures (Gómez‐Vírseda et al. 2020). Co‐agency reframes caring relationships as partnerships of shared moral responsibility. In educational settings, faculty and students together shape the moral environment of learning. Students with learning disabilities are recognised as participants in the construction of their learning processes rather than as passive recipients of accommodation; their experiences, insights and strategies become part of the educational dialogue.

Gómez‐Vírseda et al. (2020) argue that relational autonomy should not be understood as diminished independence, it represents the capacity to act responsibly within trustworthy relationships and supportive institutional conditions. Agency emerges through connection rather than in isolation. Within nursing education, caring with becomes the pedagogical expression of relational autonomy: when educators and students engage in reciprocal dialogue and shared responsibility, ethical learning emerges through relationship rather than through unilateral instruction. Caring relationships thereby support both professional development and moral formation, grounding educational practice in reciprocity, recognition and mutual accountability.

Operationalizing Care as Justice

The convergence of care and justice must extend beyond conceptual argument and take form within institutional practice. In Tronto (2013) account, a caring institution demonstrates competence by anticipating diversity in its design, responsibility by distributing relational labour fairly, and solidarity by ensuring that those most affected by exclusion participate in shaping policies and practices. Within nursing education, these commitments can be translated into concrete practices. One important step involves designing curricula and assessment frameworks according to the principles of universal design: when course structures anticipate diverse forms of learning and expression, students are provided with multiple avenues to demonstrate competence. Oral explanation, written reflection, collaborative reasoning and embodied clinical practice can all function as legitimate expressions of professional knowledge, operationalizing epistemic justice by recognizing that knowledge appears through varied cognitive and communicative pathways. A second dimension concerns the institutional recognition of relational and pedagogical labour: workload models and promotion criteria should acknowledge mentorship, inclusive teaching and sustained engagement with students as integral to professional excellence rather than as voluntary contributions. A third dimension involves inclusive governance: committees responsible for curriculum design, programme evaluation and policy development should include faculty members and students with disabilities so that institutional standards are shaped by diverse experiences and perspectives, encouraging reciprocal accountability and ensuring that educational norms are informed by collective moral reasoning rather than inherited assumptions.

Professional Standards and the Limits of Inclusion

A persistent concern within inclusive pedagogy is whether expanding care risks weakening professional rigour. This tension deserves careful philosophical attention. Nursing practice requires judgement, precision and accountability because patient safety depends upon them. However, many expectations embedded in professional culture reflect not essential competencies but inherited norms that privilege particular cognitive or communicative styles. Equating speed with intelligence, confidence with competence, or independence with professional worth may reflect ableist assumptions rather than genuine clinical requirements (Campbell 2009; Goodley 2014). Distinguishing legitimate professional standards from such norms requires epistemic humility as a form of moral practice, a willingness to ask whether a given standard is genuinely necessary for safe clinical practice or whether it privileges a narrow expression of competence. In high‐acuity settings, rapid decision‐making can be necessary to protect patients. However, the broader cultural celebration of speed as a moral virtue often reflects managerial expectations more than ethical necessity. Similarly, professional communication is sometimes interpreted as requiring immediate verbal fluency, overlooking reflective, analytical, or methodical reasoning that is equally valuable in clinical settings. Recognizing these distinctions requires ongoing institutional dialogue among educators, students, professional regulators and healthcare institutions, a dialogue that can preserve the rigour necessary for safe practice while ensuring that professional standards remain responsive to diverse forms of knowledge, cognition and clinical insight.

Integrating Care and Justice

This synthesis challenges the longstanding separation between care and justice in moral philosophy. Noddings (1984) critiqued justice‐oriented ethics for their abstraction and distance from lived relational experience, while disability scholars have critiqued care ethics for the risk of paternalism and dependency (Garland‐Thomson 2020). When these traditions are placed in dialogue, a more integrated vision emerges: justice can be understood as the structural expression of care. In contrast, care represents the relational practice through which justice is enacted in everyday life. Relational autonomy provides the bridge between these perspectives by locating moral agency within networks of trust, recognition and institutional support (Gómez‐Vírseda et al. 2020). In nursing philosophy, this integration carries significant implications: caring pedagogy becomes the practical site where the ontological, epistemological and ethical dimensions of nursing converge. When nurses are educated in environments that embody relational care, the profession's ethical commitments become internalized as lived practice rather than abstract ideals. The integration of care and justice is therefore not simply an additive theoretical move; it represents a reconceptualization of what nursing education is for: not only the transmission of clinical competence, but the formation of moral practitioners capable of sustaining ethical relationships with patients, communities and each other.

The Structural Dimension: Caring as Institutional Ethic

Caring must extend beyond interpersonal encounters to the institutional structures that shape educational relationships. When care is understood structurally, inclusion is no longer framed as accommodation for exceptional cases; it becomes an anticipatory design that recognises diversity as a normal condition of learning and professional participation. This perspective aligns with Tronto (2013) political ethics of care, which situates competence, responsiveness and responsibility within collective moral practice rather than within individual goodwill. Educational structures workload models, mentorship recognition, evaluation frameworks should reflect the relational work required to sustain ethical learning environments. Teaching excellence should include the quality of relational engagement with students; mentorship and inclusive pedagogical practice should be recognised as meaningful scholarly contributions rather than as informal or secondary activities. Gómez‐Vírseda et al. (2020) argue that institutional ethics requires both critique and care. When relational practices such as mentoring, dialogue and inclusive teaching are institutionally supported, care becomes sustainable rather than dependent on the personal dedication of individual educators. Without such structural support, caring remains privatised and fragile dependent on personal goodwill rather than on shared institutional responsibility. An institutional ethic of care therefore requires structures that recognise and sustain the relational labour upon which nursing education depends.

Implications for Nursing Philosophy and Professional Practice

The philosophical identity of nursing has long been grounded in caring, both as an ontological claim and as an epistemological orientation. Watson (1988) and Swanson (1991) argue that nursing knowledge emerges through relational attentiveness: knowing and caring are inseparable, because understanding another person requires presence, attentiveness and relational engagement. In recent decades, however, the ethical language of care has been challenged by the expansion of technocratic rationality within health and educational systems. Institutional priorities such as measurable outcomes, bureaucratic efficiency and alignment with biomedical models of expertise have increasingly reshaped nursing education (Clegg and Rowland 2010; Tronto 2013), sometimes reframing caring as a competency to be assessed rather than as a relational orientation that shapes professional identity. In such contexts, the ethical subject of care risks being replaced by the compliant performer who demonstrates competence through standardized metrics.

Reengaging Noddings' relational philosophy offers a way to recover the moral foundations of nursing education. Caring is not simply an activity that nurses perform but a relational way of being that shapes how they teach, learn and evaluate professional practice. When educators approach care as ethical recognition rather than procedural support, they affirm students with learning differences as full participants in nursing's moral and professional community, transforming inclusion from technical accommodation into recognition of dignity.

This perspective also invites a transformation in how teaching is understood. Students become participants in the co‐creation of moral and professional understanding rather than recipients of disciplinary knowledge. Epistemic humility replaces epistemic authority, allowing educators to learn from the experiences students bring to the educational environment, including the insights of students with learning disabilities about learning, care and institutional systems.

This relational understanding reshapes the meaning of evaluation. Professional knowledge cannot be measured solely by technical mastery; it also involves the capacity to listen, respond ethically and participate in relational dialogue. Hickey and Riddle (2024) propose a framework for relational pedagogy organized around informality, exchange and enactment that resists reduction to standardized metrics. Bringing such pedagogies into nursing education requires institutional willingness to value what is difficult to measure: relational presence, moral engagement and the capacity to affirm the dignity of those being educated. This is not a rejection of accountability but a broadening of it.

Conclusion

Caring must be reclaimed as the relational foundation of nursing education. Care functions simultaneously as a moral commitment, a political responsibility, and an epistemic practice grounded in attentiveness, responsiveness and relational recognition. Interpreted through disability ethics, caring becomes a form of epistemic humility that understands knowledge as relational and interdependent, one in which the adaptive strategies and alternative cognitive pathways of students with learning disabilities are recognised as legitimate forms of knowing rather than deviations from a normative standard. This shift transforms inclusion from procedural accommodation into genuine epistemic and moral recognition. When educators embody these commitments, pedagogy aligns with the ethical foundations of nursing: attentiveness to difference, sensitivity to vulnerability, and resistance to equating professional worth with standardized performance become guiding principles rather than aspirational ideals, and teaching emerges as an ethical practice rather than merely preparation for professional action. By linking relational autonomy with institutional responsibility, caring pedagogy promotes epistemic inclusion and strengthens the moral framework of nursing education. Future scholarship should continue examining how caring pedagogies can be sustained within institutions shaped by standardization, while amplifying the voices of nursing students with learning disabilities through participatory research. Because the ways nurses are educated shape the moral character of the profession, nursing education is not simply preparation for ethical practice but one of its earliest expressions.
