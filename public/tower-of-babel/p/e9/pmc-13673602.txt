Mental health-related quality of life is associated with sustained lipid-lowering therapy adherence after acute coronary syndrome
Authors: Asem H Ali, Katherine A Tak, Elisa M Taylor-Yeremeeva, Cassie R Shao, Carrie R Shao, Nico Fandino-Rubio, Maritza Jerome, Darleen Lessard, Catarina I Kiefe, David D McManus, Khanh-Van Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute coronary syndrome (ACS) is a critical manifestation of cardiovascular disease, which necessitates rigorous secondary prevention to reduce recurrent events and mortality. While lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) is integral to post-ACS secondary prevention, long-term adherence remains a significant clinical challenge. This study evaluated psychological, socioeconomic, and clinical factors associated with LLT adherence in patients after ACS, with a specific focus on mental health components and chronic comorbidities.MethodsACS survivors were included from the Transitions, Risks, and Actions in Coronary Events-Center for Outcomes Research and Education cohort (TRACE-CORE). Baseline demographic, medical history and mental health data - including the 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) Mental Component (MCS) and Physical Component Scores (PCS) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)- were collected during hospitalization. Adherence to LLT was assessed up to twelve-months post-discharge. We performed a comparative analysis between patients with complete versus incomplete follow-up and utilized binary logistic regression to identify factors associated with adherence to LLT.ResultsOf the cohort, 51% completed the 12-month follow-up. Completers were significantly older, had higher education, better mental health metrics (higher MCS, lower PHQ-9), and higher prevalence of chronic conditions including diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. Participants in the adherent group were more likely to be males and White and had higher MCS and PCS scores. Lower income (<$35,000) was associated with adherence in unadjusted analyses but did not remain independently associated after multivariable adjustment. In the regression, each 10-point increase in MCS (OR 1.24; 95% CI 1.07–1.44), hyperlipidemia (OR 1.42;1.05-1.92), male sex (OR 1.36;1.02-1.81) and white race (1.47;1.02-2.13) were associated with increased odds of adherence. ConclusionsMental health-related quality of life, as measured by MCS, along with hyperlipidemia, male sex, and White race, was associated with LLT adherence after ACS. These findings suggest that post-ACS secondary prevention may benefit from incorporating patient-reported mental health assessment to identify individuals at increased risk for nonadherence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12944-026-03017-x.Keywords: ACS, Lipid-lowering therapy, Psychological factors, Socioeconomic status, Medication adherence, Mental component score

Introduction

Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) remains a major global health concern with significant morbidity and mortality rates, resulting in over 1 million hospitalizations annually in the United States [1]. In addition to its high mortality burden, with an annual age-adjusted mortality rate typically reported in the range of 300 per 10,000, ACS is also associated with considerable morbidity from complications such as heart failure, arrhythmias, and other hemodynamic disturbances requiring intensive care management [2]. Importantly, outcomes after ACS vary significantly based on demographic factors such as sex, race, geographic location, and socioeconomic status [3, 4].

Management of hyperlipidemia with lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) is a cornerstone of both primary and secondary prevention against ACS. LLT consists of two broad categories of medications, statins and non-statins (e.g. ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, inclisiran, bempedoic acid) with statins being the first-line LLT in most cases. Multiple clinical trials have demonstrated that statins significantly reduce the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events, and as such, they are recommended by the guidelines of all pertinent organizations and associations as part of secondary prevention [5–7]. Newest guidelines continue to recommend the use of both statin and non-statin LLT for secondary prevention of ACS [7]. Yet, despite the robust evidence for use of LLT for both primary and secondary prevention of ACS and other forms of ASCVD, there remains a significant gap in prescription rates, treatment targets, and long-term adherence, for both statins as well as non-statin LLT such as ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors [8–10].

Previous studies have identified several patient-level factors influencing statin adherence, including education, engagement, beliefs, and motivations [11, 12]. Comorbid conditions may also influence adherence behavior. Recent evidence from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) suggests that patients managing multiple chronic conditions may develop more robust medication-taking routines or benefit from more intensive medical surveillance, thereby increasing statin persistence [13]. In post-ACS populations, medication nonadherence has also been shown to emerge early after discharge and to be associated with potentially actionable demographic, behavioral, and psychosocial factors [14]. A systematic review and meta-analysis further identified depression and other psychosocial factors as important predictors of medication nonadherence following ACS [15]. Mental health is another key determinant of cardiovascular outcomes, and conditions such as depression have been linked to poor medication adherence [14–18]. However, depression represents only one dimension of psychological well-being. Broader measures of mental health-related quality of life, such as the Mental Component Score (MCS) of the 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36), may capture additional psychosocial factors that influence long-term adherence behavior. Although SF-36/SF-12 health status measures have been used to evaluate prognosis and health-related quality of life in ACS populations [19], and adherence has been associated with health-related quality of life in patients with ischemic heart disease [20], limited data exist regarding whether MCS independently predicts long-term adherence to lipid-lowering therapy following ACS or provides information beyond depressive symptom burden measured by the PHQ-9. In the context of contemporary ACS management guidelines, we sought to examine demographic, clinical, socioeconomic, and psychological factors associated with adherence to LLT among patients with ACS [7].

The Transitions, Risks, and Actions in Coronary Events Center for Outcomes Research and Education (TRACE-CORE) cohort was a longitudinal, multiracial study designed to enhance the understanding of the management of patients after ACS and identify modifiable risk factors associated with rehospitalizations, mortality, and quality of life in this population [21]. Leveraging data from TRACE-CORE, we sought to examine whether mental health-related quality of life, as measured by SF-36 MCS, was associated with sustained LLT adherence after ACS, while accounting for demographic, socioeconomic, clinical, and depressive symptom measures.

Methods

We assessed patients from the TRACE-CORE cohort for this study. Details of the design, patient recruitment practices, and data collection activities have been previously described [21]. In brief, TRACE-CORE was a multisite prospective cohort study designed to recruit and follow a cohort of adult patients, age 21 years or older, who had been hospitalized with ACS at six hospitals, three in Worcester, Massachusetts, two in Atlanta, Georgia, and a large teaching hospital in Macon, Georgia. All eligible and consenting adult patients who had been discharged from the hospital after an ACS event were enrolled between April 2011 and May 2013. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained for the parent TRACECORE study from the Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects in Research at the University of Massachusetts Medical School (UMMS) and the other study sites (see declarations section). All participants provided written informed consent.

Patient-reported health status and medication adherence were assessed using validated instruments, as previously reported [21]. Briefly, mental and physical health–related quality of life were measured using the Mental Component Summary (MCS) and Physical Component Summary (PCS) scores of the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) [22], which are norm-based composite scores standardized to a mean of 50 and standard deviation of 10 in the general U.S. population, with higher scores indicating better health status. Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [23], with scores ranging from 0 to 27 and higher scores indicating greater symptom severity.

Baseline data collected within the initial hospitalization included demographic characteristics, as well as the Mental Component Score (MCS) and Physical Component Scores (PCS) of the SF-36, and the PHQ-9. Lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) included medications available and captured during the study period, including statins, statin-combination therapies, bile acid sequestrants, cholesterol absorption inhibitors, fibrate derivatives, nicotinic acid derivatives, and omega-3 fatty acids. A detailed list of medications included under LLT is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Baseline data were collected during hospitalization or within 72 h of discharge. Follow-up telephone interviews were conducted at 3, 6, and 12 months after discharge to assess reported medication adherence. Patients were called up to ten times at each time point to ensure follow-up and collect information on adherence. After 10 call attempts at any given time point, patients were considered lost to follow-up and were thus classified as having incomplete data. Those with follow-up at the 12-month time point were considered to have complete data. Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, race, education level, household income, and self-reported medical comorbidities. Baseline comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and coronary heart disease, were obtained from participant questionnaires administered at enrollment and were analyzed as binary (present/absent) variables. Hyperlipidemia and coronary heart disease reflected pre-existing self-reported medical history rather than diagnoses assigned during the index ACS hospitalization. Of the participants with complete 12-month follow-up, LLT adherence was assessed based on self-reported use of prescribed LLT at each follow-up assessment. Participants were classified as adherent if they reported taking LLT as prescribed at all follow-up time points. Participants reporting nonuse or nonadherence at any follow-up time point were classified as partially adherent. This stringent definition was selected to identify sustained LLT adherence over the follow-up period. Participants with adherence data available at all follow-up time points were considered to have complete follow-up data and were included in the primary complete-case analysis. Discharge LLT documentation was not used as an exclusion criterion for the primary adherence analysis to account for post-discharge initiation, incomplete discharge medication capture, or subsequent medication changes.

We compared baseline characteristics of participants who had complete data (complete group), compared to those with incomplete data (incomplete group) (Table 1), as well as the baseline characteristics of those who were adherent versus those who were partially-adherent within the complete group (Table 2). We used chi-squared and independent samples t-tests to compare differences in the distributions of categorical and continuous variables amongst these groups, respectively. We performed a comparative analysis between patients with complete versus incomplete follow-up. To evaluate adherence over time without violating the assumption of independent observations, we constructed a single, conservative composite longitudinal outcome (Fully Adherent vs. Partially Adherent) across all check-in points. We then utilized a multivariable binary logistic regression model to determine the baseline factors independently associated with this composite adherence status. The independent variables included in this adjusted model were sex, age, race (White versus Other), income, education, clinical comorbidities (including medical history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and coronary heart disease), as well as baseline MCS of the SF-36 and PHQ-9. For interpretability, MCS was modeled per 10-point increase, corresponding approximately to one standard deviation of the SF-36 Mental Component Score. As a sensitivity analysis, multiple imputation analysis by chained equations was performed to address missing adherence outcomes and covariate data under a missing-at-random assumption. Twenty imputed datasets were generated and analyzed using the same multivariable logistic regression model. Estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules. Variance inflation factors were calculated to assess multicollinearity among model covariates.

Primary descriptive and regression analyses were conducted using JMP Statistical Software, Student Edition, Version 19.1.0. Multiple imputation and variance inflation factor analyses were conducted in R using the mice and car packages. The forest plot was generated using Python v3.10.

Results

There was a total of 2,187 participants, of whom 93 were deceased by the end of one year and thus were excluded from the analysis. Of the remaining patients, 976 patients had incomplete data, and thus 1,118 participants (51.1%) were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Study flow diagram illustrating participant screening, exclusion, and final analysis sample size. Overview of patient selection and classification from the TRACE-CORE cohort. We compared baseline characteristics of participants who had complete data, the “complete” group, compared to those with incomplete data, the “incomplete” group, as well as the baseline characteristics of those who were “adherent” versus those who were “partially-adherent”

Those with complete follow-up data were older (mean age 63.4 ± 10.7 vs. 58.4 ± 11.3 years, p < 0.001) and had a higher level of education (p < 0.001, Table 1) than those in the incomplete data group. Participants in the complete follow-up group also had a significantly higher prevalence of diagnosed cardiovascular risk factors, including hyperlipidemia (72.9% vs. 62.4%, p < 0.001), hypertension (78.3% vs. 70.7%, p < 0.001), and diabetes mellitus (32.8% vs. 28.2%, p = 0.021). There was no significant difference in the history of coronary heart disease between those with complete and incomplete follow-up (41.4% vs. 38.1%, p = 0.124).

They also had a higher MCS (mean 48.6 ± 12.0 vs. 46.1 ± 13.3, p < 0.001) and PCS (mean 41.8 ± 10.9 vs. 40.8 ± 10.7, p < 0.001). Median PHQ-9 scores were higher in the incomplete compared to the complete group (5 [2, 10] vs. 4 [2, 8], p = 0.001), suggesting a greater burden of depressive symptoms in those with incomplete follow-up data. Additionally, mental health status as assessed by the MCS category differed substantially. A lower proportion of the incomplete group had high MCS scores compared to the complete group (46.3% vs. 57.3%). Conversely, participants with incomplete follow-up were more likely to fall into the low MCS category compared with those with complete follow-up (30.5% vs. 22.5%) and, to a lesser extent, into the moderate category (23.2% vs. 20.2%; p < 0.001 overall). There was no difference in follow-up status between male and female participants (p = 0.22).

Among the 1,118 participants with complete follow-up data at 12 months, 765 (68.4%) were classified as fully adherent and 353 (31.6%) as partially adherent (Table 2). The two groups were similar in age (mean 63.3 ± 10.7 vs. 63.5 ± 10.7 years respectively, p = 0.48). However, sex distribution differed significantly between groups, with a higher proportion of males in the adherent group compared to the partially adherent group (68.4% vs. 58.9%, p = 0.003). Racial composition also varied, with white participants representing a larger share of the fully adherent group than the partially adherent group (83.8% vs. 74.5%), and black participants representing a smaller share of the adherent group (10.5% vs. 16.7%, p = 0.007 for all).

Income distribution was significantly different, with partially adherent participants more likely to have incomes below $35,000 (35.3% vs. 27.6%) and less likely to fall in the $35,000–$74,999 range (19.8% vs. 27.1%, p = 0.01 for all). Educational level showed a similar trend, with a higher proportion of those with less than a high school education being partially adherent than adherent (14.2% vs. 9.0%), and those with a college degree being more likely to be in the adherent group compared to the partially adherent group (17.9% vs. 13.0%, p = 0.08 for all).

Regarding medical history, the prevalence of coronary heart disease (40.7% vs. 43.1%, p = 0.45), diabetes mellitus (33.9% vs. 30.6%, p = 0.28), and hypertension (78.3% vs. 78.2%, p = 0.96) was similar between groups. However, those in the adherent group had a significantly higher prevalence of hyperlipidemia compared to the partially adherent group (75.2% vs. 68.0%, p = 0.012).

Following a similar pattern to those with complete data, those in the adherent group had a higher MCS (49.5 ± 11.7 vs. 46.7 ± 12.4, p < 0.001) and PCS (42.4 ± 10.7 vs. 40.9 ± 11.2, p = 0.04). MCS classification differed substantially, with more adherent than partially adherent participants falling in the “High” MCS category (61.4% vs. 48.4%, overall p < 0.001). Conversely, partially adherent individuals had more frequent “Low” and “Moderate” MCS classifications. However, PHQ-9 depression scores did not differ significantly between the adherent and partially adherent groups (p = 0.16).

To determine factors associated with adherence, a multivariable binary logistic regression model was used. The final complete-case model included 1,118 participants, including 765 adherent and 353 partially adherent participants. The model included 15 covariate degrees of freedom, corresponding to approximately 23.5 events per variable using the smaller outcome category; the model AIC was 1381.1. In this model, each 10-point increase in MCS was associated with higher odds of LLT adherence after adjustment for age, sex, race, income, education, history of CHD, DM, HLD, HTN, and PHQ-9 (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.07–1.44, p = 0.004; Fig. 2).

Among clinical comorbidities, only a history of hyperlipidemia was independently associated with increased odds of adherence (OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.05–1.92, p = 0.025). Male sex was associated with higher odds of adherence compared to female sex (OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.02–1.81, p = 0.027). White participants had a higher likelihood of falling in the adherent group (OR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.02–2.1, p = 0.040). Participants with less than a high school education were more likely to fall in the partially adherent group (OR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.33–0.95, p = 0.042). History of coronary heart disease, diabetes, and hypertension, as well as PHQ-9 and income, did not significantly predict adherence in the adjusted model (p > 0.05 for all). In a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation by chained equations to account for missing data, the association between baseline MCS and adherence remained significant. Each 10-point increase in MCS was associated with a 21% higher odds of adherence (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.05–1.40; p = 0.012), consistent with the complete-case analysis (Supplementary Table 2). All adjusted GVIF values were < 1.5, indicating no evidence of meaningful multicollinearity among model covariates (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort of patients hospitalized with ACS, adherence to LLT over 12 months remained suboptimal despite structured follow-up. Nearly one-third of participants with complete follow-up were classified as partially adherent, underscoring the persistence of adherence gaps after ACS even in a research cohort with longitudinal follow-up. Among the variables examined, higher SF-36 MCS was independently associated with greater odds of LLT adherence after adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, clinical, and depressive symptom measures. This association remained significant in multiple imputation sensitivity analyses, suggesting that the observed relationship between mental health-related quality of life and adherence was not solely attributable to missing follow-up data.

These findings are clinically relevant in the context of contemporary ACS management, where early initiation and sustained adherence to intensive LLT are central components of secondary prevention [7]. Although guideline-directed therapy provides a clear pharmacologic pathway for reducing recurrent ASCVD risk, its effectiveness in practice depends on patients’ ability to sustain long-term medication-taking behavior. Our results suggest that patient-reported mental health-related quality of life may help identify individuals at higher risk for LLT nonadherence after ACS and may complement traditional clinical risk assessment. In practice, brief patient-reported mental health assessment during post-discharge follow-up, cardiac rehabilitation intake, or early outpatient cardiology visits could help identify patients who may benefit from additional adherence support, medication counseling, or care coordination.

Prior studies have demonstrated that psychosocial factors are associated with medication nonadherence after ACS. A systematic review and meta-analysis identified depression and other psychosocial constructs as important predictors of medication nonadherence following ACS [15], and post-ACS studies have further shown that psychosocial context, depressive symptoms, and emotional distress are associated with adherence to secondary prevention therapies [17, 18, 24, 25]. Our study extends this literature by focusing specifically on SF-36 MCS, a broader measure of mental health-related quality of life, rather than depressive symptoms alone. Although PHQ-9 was not independently associated with LLT adherence in the final adjusted model, MCS remained significant. This suggests that MCS may capture dimensions of psychological functioning, perceived well-being, emotional role functioning, social engagement, or self-management capacity that are not fully reflected by depressive symptom severity alone.

The distinction between MCS and PHQ-9 is important. PHQ-9 is designed to assess depressive symptom burden, whereas MCS reflects a broader mental health-related quality-of-life construct. Prior studies evaluating depression and adherence in cardiovascular populations have reported mixed findings, with some showing associations between depressive symptoms and nonadherence and others suggesting that broader psychosocial or behavioral factors may be more informative [24, 26–28]. In our cohort, PHQ-9 differed between participants with complete and incomplete follow-up but was not independently associated with adherence among completers after adjustment. This pattern suggests that depressive symptoms may be more closely related to overall engagement with follow-up, whereas broader mental health-related quality of life may be more strongly associated with sustained medication adherence.

We also observed that clinical comorbidities were differentially associated with follow-up completion and medication adherence. Participants with diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia were more likely to complete follow-up, suggesting that patients with established chronic disease may be more engaged with the healthcare system. This finding is consistent with prior work suggesting that individuals with multiple chronic conditions may develop more stable medication routines or receive more frequent medical surveillance [13]. However, in the adjusted adherence model, only self-reported history of hyperlipidemia remained independently associated with adherence, while diabetes, hypertension, and coronary heart disease were not. These findings suggest that healthcare engagement and medication adherence are related but distinct behaviors, and that clinical comorbidity burden alone may not fully explain sustained LLT adherence after ACS.

Demographic and socioeconomic differences were also observed. Male sex and White race were independently associated with higher odds of adherence in the complete-case model, consistent with prior literature demonstrating sex and racial disparities in cardiovascular medication use and adherence [29–38]. Income differed between adherent and partially adherent participants in unadjusted analyses, but income was not independently associated with adherence after multivariable adjustment. Therefore, the observed socioeconomic gradient should be interpreted cautiously. Socioeconomic barriers may still contribute to adherence through mechanisms not fully captured in the model, including medication cost, health literacy, access to follow-up, competing life demands, and trust in healthcare systems.

Loss to follow-up represents an important limitation of this study. Nearly half of eligible participants had incomplete follow-up data, and those with incomplete data had lower MCS, higher PHQ-9 scores, and lower educational attainment. These differences suggest that loss to follow-up was unlikely to be completely random and may reflect psychosocial and socioeconomic barriers to study engagement. Because only participants with complete follow-up data were included in the primary analysis, selection bias cannot be excluded. However, a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation by chained equations yielded materially similar results, with MCS remaining independently associated with adherence. These findings support the robustness of the observed association between mental health-related quality of life and LLT adherence despite the presence of missing data.

Several additional limitations should be considered. Adherence was self-reported and dichotomized into adherent versus partially adherent categories, which may not capture the full complexity of medication-taking behavior, including intermittent use, delayed initiation, temporary discontinuation, or medication switching. The partially adherent group may therefore include heterogeneous adherence patterns. The study also did not capture detailed reasons for nonadherence, such as medication cost, side effects, patient beliefs, pharmacy access, or clinician prescribing decisions. In addition, the TRACE-CORE cohort was enrolled between 2011 and 2013, before widespread use of newer LLT agents such as PCSK9 inhibitors, inclisiran, and bempedoic acid. Although medication options and guideline recommendations have evolved, adherence to LLT remains a persistent challenge in contemporary secondary prevention populations [8–10]. Contemporary real-world data also suggest that adherence and persistence remain important challenges with newer LLT agents, including PCSK9 inhibitors [39]. Finally, because this was an observational analysis, the associations identified should not be interpreted as causal. Unmeasured factors related to health literacy, healthcare access, social support, or care coordination may have contributed to the observed relationships.

With respect to future implementation, mental health-related quality-of-life assessment could be incorporated into existing post-ACS care pathways with minimal added burden. The SF-36 MCS, or the shorter SF-12 equivalent, could be administered at hospital discharge, cardiac rehabilitation intake, or the first post-discharge cardiology visit, with scores documented alongside established secondary prevention measures. Patients with lower MCS scores could then be prioritized for adherence-support strategies, such as pharmacist-led medication counseling, simplified dosing regimens, or closer early follow-up. Prospective studies are needed to define optimal MCS thresholds for risk stratification and to evaluate whether MCS-guided adherence support improves LLT persistence and lipid goal attainment after ACS.

In conclusion, mental health-related quality of life, as measured by SF-36 MCS, was independently associated with sustained LLT adherence following ACS. This association persisted after adjustment for depressive symptoms and other demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors, and remained robust in multiple imputation sensitivity analyses. These findings suggest that patient-reported mental health-related quality of life may help identify individuals at increased risk for LLT nonadherence after ACS. Future studies should evaluate whether incorporating mental health-related quality-of-life assessment into post-ACS care pathways can improve risk stratification and inform targeted adherence-support interventions.
