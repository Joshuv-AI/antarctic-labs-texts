Aging increases susceptibility to liver fibrosis through enhanced NAT10-mediated ac4C modification of TGFβ1 mRNA
Authors: Xuyun Peng, Panlong Li, Ying Zhang, Qi Zhang, Weicheng Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe epidemiological observational studies unveiled that aging is one of the risk factors for liver fibrosis, and the hepatic tissues in the elderly harbor more fibrotic lesions when compared to those in young people. Background

Aging is one of the major risk factors for several chronic liver diseases [1]. Age-related liver diseases include metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcoholic liver disease, fibrosis, and cirrhosis [2]. According to the ultrasound observation, the aging liver exhibits decreased liver volume and blood flow [3]. Histological investigations find that more fibrotic lesions are displayed in the hepatic tissues of elderly people when compared to those of young people [3]. The epidemiological observational studies indicate that aging enhances the susceptibility to chronic liver injury, leading to an increased rate of liver fibrosis in the elderly [3, 4]. Furthermore, it is reported that there is a positive correlation between liver stiffness values and age [5].

Interestingly, an increased number of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) is found in the aged liver [6]. Furthermore, in the elderly mice, the HSCs display a higher proliferation rate and a moderately activated status, with elevated levels of fibrosis marker genes, such as α-SMA and COL1A1 [6]. HSCs play a vital role in liver fibrosis [7]. Under normal conditions, HSCs are usually in a quiescent state. When the liver undergoes chronic liver injury, HSCs proliferate rapidly and become activated. The activated HSCs produce excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) and the deposition of ECM subsequently accelerates the progression of liver fibrosis. TGFβ-Smad2/3 pathway plays an essential role in mediating the activation of HSCs [8]. By binding to its receptor, TGFβ enhances the phosphorylation of Smad2/3 proteins, leading to the enhanced Smad2/3 nuclear translocation and activation of downstream gene transcription.

Intriguingly, when the cellular senescence occurs, the senescent cells exhibit the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) and secrete multiple cytokines like TGFβ1 [9–11]. As a pleiotropic cytokine, TGFβ1 drives cellular senescence by increasing ROS production and ECM deposition [12], which is very similar as the progression of liver fibrosis. However, in spite of the recent advances, the underlying molecular mechanisms of how aging accelerates liver fibrosis remain elusive.

Epigenetic alteration constitutes one of the most important underlying mechanisms of aging [13]. A vast variety of epigenetic alterations tightly orchestrate the progression of age-related diseases, including DNA methylation, histone methylation, histone acetylation, abnormal chromatin structure, and the aberrant expression of non-coding RNAs. The alteration of epigenetic landscape is usually reversible, leading to dynamic changes in gene expression patterns. Tri-methylation on histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9me3) is one of the evolutionarily conserved histone modification forms across different species. H3K9me3 is widely deposited in the human genome and serves as a repressive epigenetic mark, usually leading to reduced gene expression. ChIP-seq data unveils that H3K9me3 modification is significantly reduced in elderly hepatocytes, resulting in global gene activation and the functional alteration of aged liver [13].

Nevertheless, in the past decades, the field of epigenetics mainly focused on DNA or histone levels, with limited studies on the RNA level. Recently, RNA acetylation has also been discovered in RNA. N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification is the earliest identified RNA acetylation modification form, and mass spectrometry revealed that this modification is widely present in bacterial tRNA [14]. Subsequent studies found that ac4C modification is widespread in eukaryotic RNA, mainly on tRNA and 18S rRNA, and this modification is catalyzed by NAT10 [15]. In 2018, by using the techniques like acRIP-seq and ribosome profiling, one research group discovered that ac4C modification is extensively present in human cells. This type of modification is enriched in the 5′ UTR and CDS regions, which may enhance mRNA stability or promote protein translation [16]. NAT10 is currently known as the only acetyltransferase that catalyzes ac4C acetylation on RNA [17], and its function in liver disease is still being explored.

In this study, we tried to define how TGFβ/SMAD signaling pathway drives cellular senescence and liver fibrosis by triggering the alteration of epigenetic landscape. We found that the middle-aged mice were more vulnerable to CCl4 insult when compared to the young mice. TGFβ1 and NAT10 were elevated in the mice with liver fibrosis. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that TGFβ/SMAD directly potentiated the transcription of NAT10 and NAT10 increased the ac4C RNA modification of TGFβ1 mRNA, which forms a positive feedback loop. Then, RNA-binding protein PTBP1 can recognize the acetylated mRNA, leading to the stabilization of TGFβ1 mRNA and activation of TGFβ/SMAD signaling pathway. Animal studies demonstrated that Remodelin, a commercially available NAT10 inhibitor, significantly alleviated liver fibrosis and cellular senescence. Taken together, these findings highlight a novel mechanism of how TGFβ1 drives cellular senescence and liver fibrosis through ac4C RNA modification, providing a novel therapeutic strategy for liver fibrosis.

Methods

Animals and liver fibrosis models

Animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility with a 12-h light–dark cycle, under controlled temperature and humidity conditions. Animals had free access to sterile food and water. The middle-aged model was 12-month-old male C57BL/6 mice; the young model was 2-month-old male C57BL/6 mice. They were purchased from GemPharmatech. Then given carbon tetrachloride (CCl4; Macklin, C805325) for 4 weeks. Four hours after the last CCl4 injection, mice were collected under isoflurane anesthesia for further analysis.

The young model was purchased from GemPharmatech. Intraperitoneal injection of 20% CCl4 was dissolved in corn oil (Macklin, C805618). Intraperitoneal injection of 20% CCl4 at 5 mL/kg body weight was conducted twice a week. The diet containing 0.1% 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC; Sigma, 137030) for 4 weeks was used to induce DDC-mediated liver fibrosis model. In the Remodelin (Selleck, S7641) treatment of liver fibrosis model, CCl4 was injected intraperitoneally into mice for 2 weeks. Then, Remodelin was dissolved in 10% DMSO and intraperitoneal injection (5 mg/kg) was carried out three times a week. Injection of CCl4 was conducted simultaneously with Remodelin treatment for 4 weeks. Twenty-four hours after the last Remodelin treatment, the hepatic tissues were collected for subsequent experiments under isoflurane anesthesia. Mice were euthanized by CO2 in a humane manner. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use committee of Sun Yat-sen University.

Cell culture

JS1 cell line was kindly gifted by Professor Hui Chen (Sun Yat-sen University). LX2 cell line was purchased from the Cellcook and HEK293T cells were purchased from the MEISEN Cell. All the cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCell BIO, FSP500).

Construction of stable cell line

The shRNA vector pLKO, which contains puromycin resistance gene, and the shRNA knockdown sequence was provided in Supplementary Table 1. HEK293T cells were cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. When the cells reached 60%–70% confluency, transfection was performed using Opti-MEM (Gibco, 11058021). To generate the viral particles, the shRNA plasmid was co-transfected with the lentiviral packing plasmid (pMD2.G and psPAX2) into the HEK293T cells. After 48 h, the cultured medium was collected from HEK293T cells and then used to infect the LX2 cells, along with 10 μg/mL Polybrene to enhance infection efficiency. The medium was replaced with a complete culture medium after 48 h. When the LX2 cells reaches 90% confluence, puromycin (Thermo Fisher, A1113803) was added for selection.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection

LX2 cells were placed in a six-well plate in advance, and transfection was conducted when the cell density reached 50%–70%. The siRNA was synthesized by Tsingke Company. According to the formula in the manufacturer’s instruction, the final concentration was 50 nM and the cells were added to the complete medium after 8 h. The cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection. The siRNA sequence was detailed in Additional file 1: Table S1.

RT-PCR

The tissues were extracted with TRIzol reagent. The cells were extracted by RNA extraction kit (ES Science, RN001). After the RNA concentration was measured, reverse transcription was performed with the reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, R323), and PCR reaction system was prepared which was added to the quantitative PCR 96-well plate, then placed in the fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument, and PCR reaction procedure was set for the reaction according to the instructions (Vazyme, Q311). The primer sequence was detailed in Additional file 1: Tables S2 and S3.

Western blot

RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) containing protease inhibitor (Beyotime, P1010) and phosphatase inhibitor (Beyotime, P1081 and P1086) was used to lyse tissues or cells. The BCA assay kit (Beyotime, P0012) was utilized for protein quantification. Electrophoresis was carried out by concentrating gel (80 V, 40 min) and separating glue (120 V, 80 min). PVDF membrane was blocked in 5% milk, and then incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. After washed 3 times in 1 × TBST for 10 min, the PVDF membrane (Merck Millipore, ISEQ00010) was incubated with secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, membranes were washed 3 times in 1 × TBST for 10 min each and visualized by an imaging system (Tanon). The antibody information was provided in Additional file 1: Table S4.

Immunohistochemistry

Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. The sections were subjected to Sirius Red, Masson, and H&E staining at the Servicebio Company. The IHC staining was conducted using the following method. Paraffin sections were dewaxed by xylene, then hydrated with gradient ethanol, and washed with double distilled water. The sections were immersed in sodium citrate pH = 6 (Zsbio, ZLI-9065) or EDTA buffer pH = 9 (Servicebio, G1203) for thermal repair. Endogenous peroxidase was eliminated with a 3% H2O2 solution (ZhengJunshengwu, GYHQ-500), followed by PBS flushing and blockage with 10% goat serum (Gibco, 16210064) at room temperature for 30 min. The indicated primary antibodies were incubated with the sections at 4 °C for 16 h. After three washes with PBS, the secondary antibody was added, and the sections were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After washes with PBS, DAB color developing solution was added, and color development was observed under the microscope. Color development was stopped by three washes with distilled water, followed by hematoxylin staining and three more washes with distilled water. After dehydration and clearing treatments, the sections were sealed with gum, and the results of histochemical staining were observed under the microscope.

Determination of TGFβ1 and hydroxyproline activity levels

TGFβ1 and hydroxyproline activity levels were measured with a TGFβ1 ELISA kit (Proteintech, KE10005) and hydroxyproline ELISA kit (KeyGEN, KGT562) respectively. Sera were collected from mice and then centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, the standard curves were generated for TGFβ1 and hydroxyproline activity levels. The experimental procedure was carried out according to the instructions. The optical density was measured by using a spectrophotometer.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

We analyzed the online available ChIP-seq data Cistrome (http://cistrome.org/db/#/) [18]. SMAD2, SMAD3, and NAT10 antibodies and normal rabbit IgG (1 μg) were used for chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays. The experiment was conducted as previously described [19]. DNA recovered and purified from the immunoprecipitation was subjected to quantitative PCR. The primer sequence was listed in Additional file 1: Table S5.

Dot blot for RNA modification

After RNA extraction, the concentration of RNA was determined and the serially diluted RNA was heated. The samples were chilled on ice after denaturation. The RNA was directly loaded onto the NC membrane, which was then cross-linked under ultraviolet irradiation. The membrane was washed with 10 mL of wash buffer (PBS buffer with 0.02% Tween 20). It was subsequently incubated in 10 mL of blocking buffer (milk in PBS with 0.02% Tween 20) for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was then incubated with the antibody in antibody dilution buffer (milk in PBS with 0.02% Tween 20) overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking. Afterward, the membrane was washed three times for 5 min each. It was then incubated with secondary antibodies in 10 mL of antibody dilution buffer for 1 h at room temperature with gentle shaking. Following this, the membrane was washed three more times for 5 min each. An ECL luminescent solution was prepared. Finally, the membrane was exposed to a gel imaging analyzer to create an image.

Methylene blue staining was used for nucleic acid quantification. The RNA was directly loaded onto the NC membrane. After UV cross-linking for 20 min, the membrane was stained with methylene blue by incubating it in the staining solution for 10 min, followed by washing with PBST for 10 min (with multiple PBST changes during the process). The membrane was then photographed. The image was decolorized, and grayscale analysis was performed using ImageJ for statistical quantification.

β-Gal staining

We performed β-Gal staining using a commercial kit (Beyotime, C0602) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, frozen sections were first thawed at room temperature for 30 min, followed by washing with PBS. The sections were then fixed with fixative solution for 30 min. After PBS rinsing, staining solution was added, and samples were incubated overnight in a 37 °C CO2-free incubator. On the following day, sections were washed with PBS and photographed under the microscope.

Flow cytometry

LX2 or JS1 cells were washed twice by PBS softly on the second day and then stained with PI cell cycle Detection Kit (KeyGEN, KGA512) for 40 min at room temperature. After staining, cells were detected by flow cytometry, and the fluorescence signals were obtained at 633 and 488 nm respectively. For the detection of cellular apoptosis by flow cytometry, an apoptosis kit (KeyGEN, KGA108) was used in this study. All procedures were carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions provided in the product manuals. Flow cytometry analysis was performed on BD Influx Cell Sorter and data were analyzed using FlowJo software and Modift. The mean fluorescence intensity values, as calculated using FlowJo software, were utilized for statistical analysis.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

LX2 cells were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde (final concentration) for 10 min, followed by treatment with 125 mM glycine (final concentration) at room temperature for 5 min. After two washes with ice-cold PBS, RIP lysis buffer containing protease (Beyotime, P1010) and RNase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, N8080119) was added. The lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 30 min. The supernatant was collected and incubated with anti-ac4C/NAT10 antibody-conjugated Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 20399/20333). After overnight incubation, the beads were washed with IP buffer. Coprecipitated RNAs were isolated by resuspending the beads in TRIzol RNA extraction reagent. The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA for analysis. The primer sequence was detailed in Supplementary Table 5.

Isolation of primary hepatic stellate cells

The experiment was conducted based on a previously published protocol [20]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized and the EGTA buffer was injected into the liver at a rate of 5 mL/min. Then, the Enzyme buffer containing collagenase (Roche, 11088882001) was used for the infusion. Then, liver was carefully removed, and the cells were filtered through a 100-μm cell sieve and collected. The mixture was centrifuged at 580 g for 10 min (4 °C). The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in 32 mL of GBSS/B buffer. Then, 16 mL of Nycodenz solution was added and mixed gently by pipetting or inversion. Finally, the suspension was centrifuged at 1380 g for 17 min (4 °C) without brake. After centrifugation, hepatic stellate cells appeared as a thin white layer at the GBSS/B-Nycodenz interface.

Immunofluorescent staining assay

Primary Mouse Hepatic Stellate Cells/LX2 was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (JETWAY, JTW003) and permeabilized with 0.02% Triton X-100 (Sigma, X100). The permeabilized cells were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. DAPI (Invitrogen, P36935) was used for nuclei staining.

Co-immunoprecipitation

We analyzed the protein–protein interaction network of NAT10 by analyzing the data of BioGRID (https://thebiogrid.org/) [21]. Cells were lysed with 1 mL IP buffer (Beyotime, P0013) supplemented with proteinase inhibitors. Cell lysates were used for immunoprecipitation with 1 μg of antibody or the corresponding IgG. Proteins were conjugated to Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by incubation at 4 °C overnight. Beads were then washed 6 times with IP washing buffer (50 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100). Proteins were dissolved in SDS buffer and analyzed by western blotting.

Luciferase reporter assay

Briefly, the day before transfection, HEK293T, was seeded into 48-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. Ten nanograms of pRL-TK plasmid and 1 μg pGL4 reporter constructs were co-transfected with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000-015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Five hundred nanograms of pmirGLO reporter constructs transfected with Lipofectamine 3000. The medium was changed 8 h later. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were treated with TGFβ1 (Proteintech, HZ1011), SB525334 (MCE, HY-12043), LY364947 (MCE, HY-13462), or vehicle for 48 h. Cells were then lysed and luciferase activity was measured with the Dual Luciferase Assay Kit (Vazyme, DL101). The luciferase values were referred to Renilla luciferase activity and normalized to pGL4-basic or vehicle control as indicated.

RNA stability assay

Cells were treated with Actinomycin D (MCE, HY-17559) at a final concentration of 5 mg/mL and collected at indicated time points. Total RNA was extracted by Total RNA Extraction Kit and analyzed by RT-PCR assays.

acRIP-seq

At least 1 × 107 LX2 cells were collected for this experiment. Following the same preliminary steps as in the RIP assay (2.13), target RNA was enriched using the ac4C antibody. The RNA was extracted by TRIzol and sent to Guangzhou Epigenetic Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for subsequent processing, namely library construction and sequencing analysis.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 was used to analyze the experimental data. The results were shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between two groups was determined by Student t-test, while significance among three groups was determined by one-way ANOVA. When there are two factors of intervention, we use two-way ANOVA. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.005; ***P < 0.0005.

Results

Middle-aged mice were more susceptible to CCl4-mediated liver fibrosis

Emerging evidence indicates that the incidence of liver fibrosis gradually increases with aging. To further explore this question, we established CCl4-mediated liver fibrosis in young and middle-aged mice. According to the H&E staining results (Fig. 1A), we observed that all four groups of mice exhibited minor fatty vacuoles. In the young mice treated with CCl4, necrotic foci were present, along with hepatocytes showing a reticular structural pattern. In contrast, the middle-aged CCl4-treated group displayed more severe pathological changes, including ballooning degeneration of hepatocytes, significant hepatocyte enlargement, and a pronounced reticular cytoplasmic structure. Histologically, Masson’s Trichrome staining and Sirius Red indicated that more collagens were deposited in the hepatic tissues of middle-aged mice (Fig. 1B). In spite of similar baseline AST and ALT levels, in the CCl4-mediated liver fibrosis mouse model, elevated AST levels were found in the middle-aged mice when compared to those in the young mice (Fig. 1C). The qRT-PCR results found that fibrotic marker Col1a1 were also elevated in the middle-aged mice when compared to those in the young mice (Fig. 1D). Then, we examined the cellular senescence markers p16 and found p16 were slightly increased in the normal middle-aged mice with insignificant statistic values when compared to the normal young mice, but p16 was significantly elevated in the CCl4-treated middle-aged mice (Fig. 1E). The β-galactosidase activity assay further confirmed that more senescent cells were deposited in the middle-aged mice when compared to those in the young mice (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, we also isolated the primary HSCs from the mice and found that more senescent HSCs were displayed in the hepatic tissues in the CCl4-treated young mice than those in the normal young mice (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). These results were consistent with a previous report that more senescent cells accumulated in murine hepatic tissues with fibrosis and these senescent cells are predominantly derived from activated HSCs [22].

Increased expression of TGFβ1 and NAT10 were found in middle-aged mice

As altered epigenetic landscape is one of the hallmarks of aging, we then evaluated the expression of known RNA-modifying enzymes. METTL3, FTO, and ALKBH5 are m6A RNA-modifying enzymes. TRMT6 is an enzyme that catalyzes the addition of an m1A group to tRNA and mRNA. According to the results of western blots, only NAT10 was significantly elevated in the hepatic tissues of middle-aged mice (Fig. 2A). Given that senescent cells produced TGFβ1 to drive cellular senescence, we found that TGFβ1 was increased in the serum and liver of the middle-aged mice (Fig. 2B). Then, we demonstrated that treatment of TGFβ1 increased the protein and RNA levels of NAT10 (Fig. 2C and D). As NAT10 is an ac4C RNA-modifying enzyme, we monitored the ac4C levels by dot blot. The dot blot showed that ac4C RNA modification was increased after exposure to TGFβ1 (Fig. 2E).

Then, we treated the LX2 cells with etoposide to induce cellular senescence. We found NAT10 was increased after etoposide treatment (Additional file 2: Fig. S2A and B). Accordingly, suppression of NAT10 by inhibitor or shRNA impaired the expression of senescent marker genes p16 and p21 (Additional file 2: Fig. S2C and D), indicating that NAT10 might modulates cellular senescence.

NAT10 and ac4C RNA modifications were elevated in the mice with liver fibrosis

To characterize the expression profiles of NAT10 in liver fibrosis, we examined the expression patterns of three RNA modifications, namely m6A, m1A, and ac4C. In the CCl4- and DDC-mediated liver fibrosis mouse model, we found that ac4C was the only RNA modification that displayed enhanced expression in the fibrotic tissues (Fig. 3A). Consistently, the protein and RNA levels of NAT10 were increased in the mice with liver fibrosis (Fig. 3B–D). Moreover, the histological studies also confirmed the elevation of NAT10 and α-SMA in the mice with liver fibrosis (Fig. 3E).

SMAD2 and SMAD3 directly potentiated RNA transcription of NAT10

As TGFβ1 exerted its function by triggering the phosphorylation of SMAD2 and SMAD3, we then analyzed the online available ChIP-seq data Cistrome (http://cistrome.org/db/#/) [18]. Based on the ChIP-seq data of SMAD2 and SMAD3, there were strong SMAD2 and SMAD3 binding peaks within the promoter of the NAT10 gene locus (Fig. 4A). We then conducted a ChIP assay and verified that both SMAD2 and SMAD3 directly interacted with the promoter region of the NAT10 gene (Fig. 4B). Reinforced expression of SMAD2 and SMAD3 enhanced the expression of NAT10 (Fig. 4C). Then, we cloned the promoter region of NAT10 gene into the pGL4 enhancer vector and found that overexpression SMAD2/3 significantly enhanced the luciferase activity of the pGL4-NAT10-promoter (Fig. 4D). To further validate the regulatory roles of SMAD2/3, we used SB525334 and LY364947, two antagonists targeting TGFβ1 receptors. The treatment of SB525334 and LY364947 attenuated the enhanced effect of TGFβ1 on NAT10 (Fig. 4E). Consistently, SB525334 and LY364947 also impaired the luciferase activity of pGL4-NAT10-promoter (Fig. 4F).

TGFβ1 enhances NAT10 transcriptional activity through SMAD2 and SMAD3

To gain the mechanistic insights, we conducted acetylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (acRIP-seq) to illustrate the genome-wide landscape of ac4C modification. According to the acRIP-seq, we found that the acetylated RNA transcripts were enriched in multiple signaling pathways, such as TGFβ signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, and insulin signaling pathway (Fig. 5A). The ac4C RNA modification was mainly enriched in the CDS and 3′UTR regions of mRNA (Fig. 5B). The visual genome result displayed the location of ac4C modification in different acetylated RNA transcripts of the TGFβ signaling pathway (Fig. 5C, Additional file 2: Fig. S3A and B). As ac4C may affect mRNA stability, we silenced NAT10 by viral shRNA vector (Fig. 5D) and examined the expression profiles of acetylated RNA transcripts after the knock down of NAT10. Interestingly, we found that the RNA levels of most of the acetylated RNA transcripts were decreased after inhibition of NAT10 (Fig. 5E, Additional file 2: Fig. S3C and D), suggesting that NAT10 may enhance the mRNA stability of acetylated RNA. Among the candidate genes, TGFβ1 was selected for further investigations because of its biological importance. Overexpression of NAT10 enhanced the expression of TGFβ1, while inhibition of NAT10 suppressed its expression (Fig. 5F). To validate the ac4C modification on TGFβ1 mRNA, we performed an RNA immunoprecipitation assay (RIP assay). The RIP assay verified that ac4C antibody and NAT10 antibody did pull down TGFβ1 mRNA transcript (Fig. 5G and H), suggesting that ac4C RNA modification occurred in the TGFβ1 mRNA transcript.

NAT10 increased RNA ac4C modification and stability of TGFβ1 mRNA

To examine the impact of acetylation on TGFβ1 mRNA, we treated LX2 cells with Actinomycin D (Act-D), which is frequently used in mRNA stability assays to suppress the synthesis of new mRNA. Actinomycin D allows the evaluation of mRNA degradation by measuring mRNA expression following transcription inhibition. According to the mRNA stability assays, the half-life of TGFβ1 mRNA was longer in NAT10-overexpressing cells, while it is shorter after knockdown of NAT10 (Fig. 5I). As the ac4C modification existed in the 5′ UTR of TGFβ1 mRNA, we cloned the 5′-UTR of TGFβ1 mRNA into pmirGLO vector. Overexpression of NAT10 enhanced the luciferase activity of the pmirGLO-TGFβ1-5′UTR vector, while inhibition of NAT10 suppressed its luciferase activity (Fig. 5J). To confirm the exact location of the ac4C sites in TGFβ1 mRNA, an orthogonal method with nucleotide resolution was performed. The ac4C nucleotide can be converted towards tetrahydro-ac4C by using sodium borohydride (NaBH4), resulting in C:T mismatches during first strand cDNA synthesis. Sanger sequencing of cDNA product exhibited an average of C:T mismatches in TGFβ1 mRNAs after treatment with NaBH4, indicating that TGFβ1 mRNA was indeed acetylated (Fig. 5K). Moreover, suppression of NAT10 by shRNA or Remodelin impaired the luciferase activity of TGFβ luciferase reporter harboring nine SMAD2/3 binding sites (Fig. 5L).

PTBP1 is a reader of acetylated TGFβ1 mRNA

In order to characterize how ac4C modification enhances the mRNA stability of TGFβ1, we analyzed the protein–protein interaction network of NAT10 by analyzing the data of BioGRID (https://thebiogrid.org/) [21]. Among the protein-binding partners of NAT10, we chose PTBP1 for further study because of its known function in modulating RNA stability (Fig. 6A) [23]. Based on the co-IP and confocal imaging results, we demonstrated that NAT10 physically interacted with RNA-binding protein PTBP1 in LX2 and JS1 cells (Fig. 6B–D). To validate whether PTBP1 can read the ac4C signals on RNA transcripts, we utilized PTBP1 antibody to pull down the RNA transcripts associated with PTBP1 and then performed the ac4C dot blot (Fig. 6E). The dot blot confirmed that PTBP1 significantly enriched the acetylated RNA transcripts (Fig. 6F), suggesting that PTBP1 might be a novel reader protein of ac4C-modifying RNA transcripts. Subsequently, the RIP assay validated that PTBP1 directly interacted with TGFβ1 mRNA (Fig. 6G).

Then, we found that TGFβ1 mRNA was reduced after the knock down of PTBP1 (Fig. 6H) and was increased after overexpression of PTBP1 (Additional file 2: Fig. S3E). The half-life of TGFβ1 mRNA was found to be shorter after the silencing of PTBP1 (Fig. 6I). As the ac4C modification existed in the 5′ UTR of TGFβ1 mRNA, we cloned the 5′-UTR of TGFβ1 mRNA into the pmirGLO vector. Overexpression of PTBP1 enhanced the luciferase activity of the pmirGLO-TGFβ1-5′UTR vector, while inhibition of PTBP1 suppressed its luciferase activity (Fig. 6J). In order to validate whether TGFβ1 mRNA expression was regulated by NAT10 in a PTBP1-dependent manner, we established a stable NAT10-overexpressing cell line. It was found that NAT10 enhanced the expression of TGFβ1. However, silencing of PTBP1 eliminated the NAT10-mediated enhanced expression of TGFβ1 (Fig. 6K), suggesting that NAT10 enhanced TGFβ1 mRNA stability in a PTBP1-dependent manner.

NAT10 inhibitor Remodelin alleviated CCl4-mediated liver fibrosis and HSC activation

To examine the effect of NAT10 on the liver fibrosis, we established CCl4-mediated liver fibrosis and used NAT10 inhibitor Remodelin to interfere with disease progression as indicated (Fig. 7A). The histological staining displayed that Remodelin impaired collagen deposition in the hepatic tissues of mice with liver fibrosis (Fig. 7B). Remodelin also reduced hepatic p21 expression and serum TGFβ1 levels (Fig. 7C and D), indicating that Remodelin may slow down the aging progression within the liver. Remodelin could slightly decrease hydroxyproline levels and significantly impair the expression of fibrosis-related genes α-Sma and Col1a1 (Fig. 7E and F).

Subsequently, we tried to evaluate the effect of Remodelin in modulating the proliferation and activation of HSCs. We found that Remodelin suppressed the cell proliferation and induced G0/G1 phase cell cycle arrest of LX2 cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S4A–C). Furthermore, treatment of Remodelin also inhibited the expression of TGFβ1-induced fibrotic genes (Additional file 2: Fig. S4D) in LX2 cells. Consistently, Remodelin also triggered G0/G1 phase cell cycle arrest without increasing apoptosis in JS1 cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S5A and B). The RNA levels of TGFβ1-induced fibrotic genes were also suppressed by Remodelin in JS1 cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S5C).

To sum up, our current study depicted a self-reinforcing feedback loop between the TGFβ1 signaling pathway and NAT10 (Fig. 8). TGFβ1, secreted by senescent cells, potentiated the gene transcription of NAT10 by SMAD2/3. NAT10 catalyzed the ac4C RNA modification on TGFβ1 mRNA. The acetylated TGFβ1 mRNA was recognized by RNA-binding protein PTBP1, leading to increased mRNA stability of TGFβ1. This positive feedback loop drives cellular senescence and activation of hepatic stellate cells, resulting in enhanced liver fibrosis.

Discussion

Liver fibrosis is an abnormal tissue repair in response to chronic liver injury, such as hepatitis virus infection, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcohol abuse, and bile cholestasis [24]. If left untreated, liver fibrosis may progress to liver cirrhosis, along with severe complications like portal hypertension [25]. Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) play a vital role during the disease progression. In normal liver, HSCs are usually in a resting state. However, when the liver is under attack by chronic stimulus, HSCs undergo rapid proliferation and activation, transforming into their activated form called myofibroblasts (MFBs). The activation of HSCs is characterized by enhanced cell proliferation, increased expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) level, and reduced cytoplasmic retinol-containing lipid droplets, leading to excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins in the liver. Thus, blockage of HSC activation may serve as a novel therapeutic approach for liver fibrosis. Nevertheless, in spite of that more than 100 drugs are being evaluated for treating liver fibrosis, and none has yet been approved by the FDA.

In this study, we found that inhibition of NAT10 significantly attenuated HSC activation and liver fibrosis. We reported that NAT10 increased the ac4C RNA modification of TGFβ1 mRNA and TGFβ/SMAD pathway directly activated the gene transcription of NAT10, which forms a self-reinforcing feedback loop. This positive feedback loop drives HSC activation and liver fibrosis, paving a path for the identification of a novel anti-fibrosis drug target based on RNA modification.

In 2010, the concept of RNA epigenetics was proposed [26]. The birth of this concept largely extends the scope of epigenetics. The hypothesis of RNA epigenetics proposes that there are numerous reversible chemical modifications (such as methylation and acetylation) on RNA, which are dynamically balanced by RNA writer enzymes and eraser enzymes. The reader protein can recognize these RNA modifications, and thus tightly modulates the RNA stability, cellular localization, splicing, and translation of modified mRNA, ultimately leading to changes in gene networks and cell fate decisions. Currently, scientists have discovered over a hundred types of RNA modifications. The most common mRNA modifications include m6A, m5C, A-to-I, m7G, and ac4C [27–29]. These RNA modifications not only occur on mRNA but also on non-coding RNAs such as tRNA, rRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA [30]. m6A methylation is the most abundant RNA modification in eukaryotic cells, which is mainly mediated by various RNA methyltransferases such as METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, and HAKAI [31]. The RNA demethylases such as FTO and ALKBH5 can remove the m6A modification accordingly [28]. METTL3 can promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition and tumor metastasis in gastric cancer and liver cancer cells. Mechanistic studies have found that METTL3 catalyzed the stability of ZMYM1 mRNA and thus promoted the translation of EMT-related transcription factor Snail [31, 32]. Recently, STM2457, a highly potent and selective first-in-class catalytic inhibitor of METTL3, was identified and characterized [33]. It was found that treatment of tumors by STM2457 leads to reduced tumor growth and increased differentiation of acute myeloid leukemia [33]. STM2457 selectively decreased m6A levels on known leukemogenic mRNAs. These findings indicate the biological and pathological importance of RNA modification in human diseases.

A recent study reported that suppression of NAT10 significantly extended health span in a mouse model mimicking Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS) [34]. HGPS is a devastating genetic disease characterized by accelerated aging. The young patients usually display hair loss, osteoporosis, low body weight, lipodystrophy, and scleroderma. Interestingly, oral administration of Remodelin ameliorates cardiac pathologies and weight loss in progeria mice. Furthermore, the genetic depletion of NAT10 improved the health of HGPS mice [34]. Thus, this observation indicates a potent role of NAT10 in aging [35]. Meanwhile, consistent with our current results, recent studies demonstrate that NAT10 not only promotes cardiac fibrosis, but also accelerates the process of pulmonary fibrosis through TGFβ1 [36, 37]. However, the underlying mechanisms remain largely unknown. According to the current knowledge, NAT10 may enhance mRNA stability and translation. For instance, recent studies have reported that NAT10 promotes gastric cancer cell migration by inducing ac4C modification in the 3′-UTR region of COL5A1 mRNA [38, 39]. Additionally, research groups have reported that NAT10 protein modulated cancer cell growth and bipolar spindle assembly by mediating protein acetylation [40, 41].

In the current study, we found that inhibition of RNA ac4C-modifying enzyme accelerated liver fibrosis by triggering the acetylation of TGFβ1 mRNA. This raised a question about whether the eradication of the acetylation of TGFβ1 mRNA may ameliorate liver fibrosis. Currently, only one potential RNA ac4C deacetylase named SIRT7 has been reported. It was found that SIRT7 may serve as a potent deacetylase of the ac4C of rRNA. Interestingly, SIRT7 was initially identified as a protein deacetylase and plays an essential role in aging [42]. SIRT7 belongs to the sirtuin family. So far, seven sirtuins have been identified in mammals. Since the first report on a yeast sirtuin named Sir2 indicates that it significantly extends budding yeast lifespan [43], numerous efforts have been made to illustrate the biological functions of sirtuins in aging and cellular senescence. Significant reduction of SIRT7 has been reported in most organs of mammals as a consequence of aging, including the skin, liver, and colon. Meanwhile, SIRT7-deficient mice exhibited specific features of aging, including kyphosis, osteopenia, and loss of subcutaneous fat. Mechanistic studies found that SIRT7 slowed down stem cell aging by activating of the LINE1 retrotransposon (LINE1) [44], resulting in the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and subsequent cellular senescence. Furthermore, SIRT7 was found to be decreased in the activated HSCs and knockdown of SIRT7 impaired the activation of HSCs [45]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the deacetylase SIRT7 may antagonize aging and liver fibrosis, which is consistent with our findings that inhibition of acetylase NAT10 alleviated aging and liver fibrosis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study identified a previously unknown role of RNA ac4C-modifying enzyme NAT10 in driving cellular senescence and liver fibrosis by altering the epigenetic landscape. We confirmed that the middle-aged mice were more susceptible to CCl4 insult, which was consistent with previous clinical observations. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the inhibition of NAT10 by Remodelin could inhibit liver fibrosis and cellular senescence simultaneously. Mechanistic studies characterized a novel self-reinforcing feedback loop between TGFβ1 and NAT10. In addition, a novel reader protein PTBP1 which recognized ac4C modification was identified in our study. Taken together, our study tried to build up a missing link between RNA modification, cellular senescence, and liver fibrosis, providing a novel therapeutic strategy for liver fibrosis.
