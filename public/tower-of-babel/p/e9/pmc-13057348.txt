Preliminary study on the distribution and risk assessments of microplastic pollution in surface water in Chengdu, China
Authors: Juan Chen, Yong Chen, Xin Peng, Ping Wang, Pengjie Li, Jing Huang, Xian Yong, Yinger Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastics have emerged as a significant global environmental issue that has garnered considerable attention from both the public and governmental bodies. In this study, microplastics (0.02–5.0 mm) in the surface water of the Minjiang River and Tuojiang River valleys in Chengdu were quantitatively characterized and analyzed using micro-infrared spectroscopy. Key metrics such as the pollution load index, polymer risk, and potential ecological risk index were calculated to evaluate and analyze the risks associated with microplastics. The results indicated that the concentrations of microplastics in the study area’s surface water ranged from 5.0 to 18.2 items/L. Additionally, the microplastic content in the Tuojiang River valley was higher than that in the Minjiang River valley. Microplastics were predominantly transparent or yellow, primarily occurring as granules, fragments, and fibers, with sizes mainly below 1000 μm. In 2021, the predominant types were polyethylene terephthalate (34.2%) and polyethylene (20.7%). In 2023, polyamide (32.8%) and polyethylene (19.5%) became the predominant types, indicating a significant shift in the composition. The contamination risk of microplastics was significantly influenced by the type of polymer. These findings provided a theoretical foundation for the detection and risk assessment of microplastics in surface waters within the Chengdu region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41638-5.

Introduction

Mass production of plastics commenced in the 1950 s, with an initial annual output of approximately 1.5 million metric tons. By 2018, global production had surged to 360 million tons1, and this figure continues to rise. To date, cumulative human production of plastics has surpassed 10 billion tons. However, nearly half of this volume constitutes plastic waste due to inadequate disposal and recycling practices, thereby contributing significantly to environmental pollution in the form of harmful solid waste2–4. Large plastic debris eventually decomposes into smaller particles, with fragments measuring less than 5 mm classified as “microplastics"56. Microplastics have been extensively detected in diverse environmental media, including oceans, rivers, soils and air7–11. These particles exhibit a wide range of morphologies; some possess sharp edges that can cause physical injury upon ingestion by organisms12. Moreover, microplastics inherently contain toxic plasticizers introduced during the production process. Due to their strong hydrophobicity and large specific surface area, microplastics can also adsorb and accumulate other environmental pollutants. These compounds associated with microplastics may cause chemical damage to organisms exposed to or ingesting them13,14. In aquatic environments, the surfaces of microplastics provide ideal substrates for bacterial colonization. Harmful algae or pathogens attached to these surfaces may generate biotoxic effects during waterborne transport15. Consequently, these contaminants pose significant threats to both environmental and biological health. For instance, upon ingestion of microplastics in water, blue mussels accumulated these particles within their lysosomal systems, resulting in substantial tissue alterations and inflammatory reactions. This accumulation may ultimately lead to the formation of cellular tumors or induce lysosomal membrane instability16. The ingestion of microplastics adsorbed with triclosan by zebrafish exacerbated liver damage and enhanced toxic effects on the brain14. Consequently, assessing the pollution levels of microplastics in water bodies and conducting comprehensive risk assessments are essential for implementing ecological environmental protection measures and advancing microplastic pollution control.

Risk assessment is a crucial methodology for evaluating the potential threats that pollutants may pose to both the ecological environment and human health. In the context of microplastics pollution, the evaluation primarily depends on three key factors: the concentration of microplastics in water, their chemical toxicity, and their biological impacts. Qu et al. (2018)17 investigated microplastic pollution in coastal seawater and mussels in China, revealing a significant positive correlation between the concentration of microplastics in mussels and the microplastic content in the surrounding water. This finding has been interpreted to indicate that mussels could serve as reliable bioindicators for monitoring microplastic pollution levels in aquatic environments. Xu et al. (2018)18 performed a detailed analysis of the risks associated with microplastic pollution in the Yangtze River estuary using a model constructed from polymer toxicity data. Wan et al. (2024)19 evaluated the risk of microplastic contamination in both surface water and sediments within China’s Wuding River Basin by integrating microplastic concentration and chemical hazard data. Nevertheless, there is paucity of reports concerning the risk assessment of microplastics in the Minjiang and Tuojiang River valleys. Prior research had demonstrated that the concentration of microplastics in urban rivers in Chengdu is significantly influenced by population growth and economic development in coastal areas20,21. In contrast to previous studies, this study expanded the research area, increased the frequency of microplastic detection, and quantitatively assessed the pollution risk of microplastics. These improvements provide a deeper understanding of microplastic pollution in surface water in this region.

Study area

Situated in western China, Chengdu, the capital of Sichuan Province, functions as the primary distribution hub for daily-use plastic products within the region. The city hosts a substantial number of industries related to plastics, including sectors such as plastic films, foam plastics, daily-use plastic items, automotive manufacturing, electronics, biomedical applications, pipelines, and wire and cable production. With a population exceeding 20 million, Chengdu experiences significant consumption of plastic products. During their usage, these products release microplastics22. Chengdu annually discharges over 1 billion tons of wastewater, which includes both domestic sewage from urban residents and industrial wastewater. This effluent potentially contains large quantities of microplastics that may subsequently enter natural water bodies23,24.

The primary rivers in Chengdu are the Minjiang River and the Tuojiang River (as illustrated in Fig. 1), which together provide water resources totaling approximately 9 billion cubic meters. The Dujiangyan Water Conservancy Project divides the Minjiang River into the inner river and the outer river. The outer river, also known as the Jinmahe River, is the main channel of the Minjiang River within Chengdu, spanning approximately 81 km. In Xinjin, the Jinmahe River receives tributaries from the Xihe River and the Laonanhe River on its right bank. The Fuhe River and the Nanhe River are two major rivers flowing through Chengdu, originating from the inner river. The Shahe River branches off from the left bank of the Fuhe River, encircling the main urban area before rejoining the Nanhe River southeast of Chengdu City and eventually merging into the Shuangliu Huanglongxi River. The Fuhe River originates from Dujiangyan and extends for about 150 km. Additionally, the inner river features a main canal, the Jianganhe River, which stretches approximately 90 km and flows southeast along the Jinmahe River.

The upper reaches of the Tuojiang River are formed by the confluence of three rivers: the Mianyuanhe River, the Shitingjiang River, and the Jianjiang River. These rivers converge near Zhaozhen in Jintang County to form the main stem of the Tuojiang River. Additionally, the Tuojiang River receives inflows from the Pihe River and the Qinbaijiang River, both tributaries of the Minjiang River. The Chengdu section of the Tuojiang River extends diagonally across the city from northeast to southeast, spanning approximately 125 km east-west and 133 km north-south, covering a total area of nearly 6,500 square kilometers.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Centered on the hydrological and land use characteristics of the valley, this study systematically evaluated the selection of point source and non-point source pollution. Samples were collected from the upper, middle, and lower reaches of the river. In the upstream region, non-point source pollution, particularly from agricultural lands, was predominant. Midstream sampling sites were influenced by both point sources, including densely populated urban areas, residential zones, and wastewater treatment plants, as well as non-point sources from agricultural regions. Downstream, due to high population density and mixed land use comprising agricultural, urban, and residential areas, the cumulative impact of both point and non-point sources pollution was more pronounced. The Minjiang River valley comprised 7 rivers (Shahe, Nanhe, Fuhe, Jianganhe, Jinmahe, Xihe and Laonanhe) with a total of 20 sampling points, while the Tuojiang River valley included 4 rivers (Jianjiang, Qingbaijiang, Pihe and Tuojiang) with 10 sampling points. The selection of sampling locations avoided stagnant water areas, backwater zones, and sewage outlets, prioritizing straight river sections to ensure bed stability, smooth water flow, wide water surfaces, and the absence of rapids and shallows. Surface water samples were collected using a glass water sampler at a depth of 0.5 m below the surface. The samples were then transferred to a common glass beaker, thoroughly mixed, and equally divided into three 1 L brown glass bottles (including two parallel samples) for analysis25. Immediately following collection, the samples were transported back to the laboratory and stored in a refrigerator maintained at 4 °C prior to analysis. Surface water samples were collected in June of both 2021 and 2023. During these sampling periods, the water levels and flow rates of the rivers in the study area were relatively stable. The second sampling was delayed until 2023 due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic following the initial sampling in 2021.

Laboratory analysis

We referred to the methods of Nuelle et al.26 and Shim et al.27 to isolate and quantify microplastics in the samples. Microplastics were digested using 35% H2O2 (digestion time approximately 48 h) to remove surface organic matter and microorganisms. The digested samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm glass fiber filter membrane to concentrate microplastics, after which the membrane was dried at 40–50 °C. The suspected plastic particles on the filter membrane were transferred onto gold-coated glass slides using a fine steel needle (minimum diameter: approximately 0.16 mm) and a micro-blade under a stereomicroscope (SC-III; Shanghai Yixiang Instrument Co., Ltd., China.), followed by morphological observation of the microplastics. The stereomicroscope was configured to operate in a standalone mode: images were captured directly via a dedicated hardware button and saved to an external USB drive, with no computer software involved in the acquisition process. The main functional groups in the qualitative samples were characterized using the reflectance mode of a micro-Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR) (Nicolet iN10 MX; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The obtained infrared spectra were compared with the reference database (OMNIC library), and particles were identified as microplastics only when the spectral match exceeded 70%. Subsequently, the polymer type, size, and particle count were recorded. The size of the microplastics analyzed in our study is primarily distributed within the range of 20 μm to 5000 µm28. Currently, there is no universally standardized international unit for the concentration of microplastics. In this article, the unit used was “items/L”, which was commonly used in the reported literature29,30.

Pollution load index, polymeric hazard and potential ecological risk index

Tomlinson et al. (1980)31 introduced the Pollution Load Index (PLI) as a tool to evaluate the extent of pollution in natural ecosystems based on pollutant concentrations. The PLI serves as a standardized method for monitoring and assessing pollution levels, facilitating comparisons across different monitoring sites. By quantifying pollutant concentrations, the PLI value can be calculated. A PLI value exceeding 1 indicates the presence of significant pollution at the site. Rakib et al. (2022)32 subsequently applied this methodology to assess the risk posed by microplastics in estuarine and coastal ecosystems in Bangladesh. Different types of polymers pose varying levels of harm to the environment and health; therefore, evaluating the risks of microplastics based solely on concentration is insufficient. Lithner et al. (2011)33 developed a polymer hazard ranking model that assesses the toxicity levels of polymers in ecosystems and quantifies the risks associated with specific polymers. Table 1 provided the risk values for several polymers. The Potential Ecological Risk Index (PRI), originally proposed by Hakanson (1980)34, has been widely applied in studies of compound pollution in aquatic environments. This method integrates both the concentration and toxicity of individual microplastics, thereby offering a more comprehensive evaluation of the risks posed by microplastics.

Quality control

During sampling and experimental analysis, glassware was used exclusively to avoid any plastic materials. All glass containers were soaked in a nitric acid solution for at least 12 h prior to use and subsequently rinsed thoroughly with deionized water at least three times to ensure thorough cleanliness. Glassware was air-dried in a clean environment prior to use to minimize the risk of airborne microplastic contamination. Before sample analysis, the test bench was wiped with alcohol to prevent potential microplastic contamination. Researchers wore cotton clothing throughout the sampling and experimental procedures to reduce the likelihood of fiber shedding.

To ensure data accuracy, two parallel samples were collected at each sampling point. Both field blanks and laboratory blanks were implemented. The whole-process blank was prepared by sealing a bottle of ultrapure water in the laboratory before sampling. This bottle was transported to the field, opened and resealed concurrently with sample collection, and then returned to the laboratory with the actual samples for identical analytical processing. This procedure monitored potential contamination during collection, transport, and analysis. Two whole-process blanks were included per batch. For the laboratory blank, deionized water was processed instead of a real sample. These blank samples were analyzed to correct for any background contamination introduced during sample treatment. The results showed that no microplastics (> 20 μm) were detected in either the whole-process or laboratory blanks, indicating that contamination was effectively controlled throughout the experimental procedure.

Results and discussion

Abundance of microplastics in surface water

Microplastics were detected in river surface water samples from the study area in both 2021 and 2023. The distribution of microplastics is influenced by a variety of factors, including the intrinsic properties of plastics, hydrological conditions, meteorological factors37, and the population density and industrial development levels of surrounding areas38,39. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the average abundance of microplastics in the surface waters of the Minjiang and Tuojiang River valleys was presented. Upon examination of the data, it was evident that the concentration of microplastics in these rivers exhibited minimal variation between 2021 and 2023. In the study area, the concentration of microplastics in the surface water of the Shahe River was the lowest among all rivers. The Shahe River, which flows through Chengdu for approximately 22 km, serves as the primary river channel in the city and belongs to the Minjiang River system. Since 2001, the local government has been implementing an extensive and uninterrupted comprehensive renovation project on the Shahe River. As a result of long-term treatment, the ecological environment along both sides of the river has significantly improved, leading to the establishment of an urban river park. It is speculated that the primary reason for the low concentration of microplastics in the Shahe River may be attributed to its continuous and effective comprehensive improvement measures, as well as the well-preserved ecological environment.

The Jianjiang River, located within the Tuojiang River valley, exhibits a high average concentration of microplastics in its surface water. The Jianjiang River primarily flows through the Pengzhou area of Chengdu, a region recognized as one of Chinese five major vegetable production bases and the largest vegetable planting base in Chengdu. In recent years, the local government has actively promoted green ecological tourism integrated with agricultural production. Consequently, numerous farmhouses have emerged along the Jianjiang River, and extensive greenhouse farming areas have been established. We hypothesized that the discharge of domestic waste, domestic sewage, and agricultural irrigation activities are likely to be the primary sources of microplastics in the regional water environment.

In 2021 and 2023, the average concentrations of microplastics in surface waters of the Minjiang River valley were 8.1 items/L and 8.9 items/L, respectively, whereas those in the Tuojiang River valley were 14.7 items/L and 14.2 items/L, respectively, indicating a higher overall level of microplastic pollution in the Tuojiang River valley compared to the Minjiang River valley. This difference may be attributed to variations in hydrological conditions and the intensity of human activities between the two valleys. As a region with relatively severe water pollution in Sichuan Province, the Tuojiang River valley suffers from limited water resource availability. Furthermore, the rapid population growth and industrialization in recent years have intensified the pollutant load discharged into the river. A horizontal comparison (Table 3) reveals that microplastic concentrations in the Minjiang and Tuojiang River valleys are significantly higher than those in other water bodies, such as the Pearl River (0.379–7.924 items/L)40 and the Melbourne City River (0.06–2.50 items/L)41. The above results not only reveal the severity of microplastic pollution in the Minjiang and Tuojiang River valleys, but also highlight the profound impact of the underlying regional development model on the aquatic environment. Future efforts must focus on integrated river basin management and industrial structure adjustment to systematically control microplastic emissions and ensure water ecological security.

Shape, color, size, and polymer types of microplastics

In this study, the morphologies of microplastics in the samples were classified into two categories: fibers and fragments. Fibrous microplastics predominantly exhibit filamentous or linear structures, whereas fragmented microplastics are characterized by irregularly shaped particles or broken fragments. Figure 3(a) and 3(b) illustrate the typical morphological features of microplastics observed under a stereo microscope. To examine their microstructural characteristics, higher-magnification images were obtained as shown in Fig. 3(c) and 3(d), using a ProgRes CT3 precision microscope camera (JENOPTIK, Germany). Image acquisition was controlled by the manufacturer-provided CapturePro software, with key parameters including exposure, gain, and white balance adjusted for optimal clarity. All images were saved in a lossless format for subsequent analysis. As shown in Fig. 3(c), fibrous microplastics display distinct fracture features, while the surface of fragmented microplastics exhibits multiple microporous structures, as depicted in Fig. 3(d). The results presented in Fig. 4 (detailed data provide in supplementary Table S1 and S2) indicate pronounced spatial and temporal variations in microplastic morphology within riverine environments. Fragmented microplastics predominate as the primary morphological type in the study area, which is likely attributable to their diverse and widespread sources. These fragments typically originate from the physical breakdown and environmental degradation of larger plastic items, suggesting that nearly all categories of plastic waste may serve as potential precursors to fragmented microplastics. Among the surface water samples collected in the study area, transparent microplastics were the most prevalent, accounting for over 40% of all observed particles, followed by colored microplastics such as red, yellow, blue, and green (Fig. 5, data see supplementary Table S3 and S4). Significant variations in microplastic color composition were observed across different sampling locations, a finding consistent with previous studies by Su et al. (2018)40 and Abayomi et al. (2017)7. Specifically, Su et al. (2018)40 reported that microplastics in the Pearl River surface waters were predominantly white, while Abayomi et al. (2017)7 found that blue microplastics were the most common in the Arabian Gulf. In the Minjiang and Tuojiang river valleys, microplastics in surface waters are predominantly small in size, with the majority concentrated in the range below 500 μm (Fig. 6, detailed data provide in supplementary Table S5 and S6). The particle size distribution observed in these basins is similar to that reported for microplastics in the surface waters of the Wuding River19 and Pearl River40. However, the optical microscopy-based identification method employed in this study has an inherent detection limit for particle size. This implies that sub-micron and even nano-sized plastic particles may be present in the environment at higher abundances than currently reported. The obtained results primarily reflect the detectable particle size range of the methodology, suggesting that the actual extent of microplastic pollution could be more severe and complex. Future research should integrate more sensitive analytical techniques, such as FTIR imaging with an FPA detector43, to better characterize the occurrence and ecological risks of smaller microplastics. This represents a critical direction for methodological advancement in this field. The shape, color, and particle size of microplastics exhibit pronounced spatial and temporal variations in the riverine environment of the study area. This heterogeneity is likely attributable to the combined influence of multiple factors, including the physicochemical properties of microplastics, river hydrodynamic conditions, environmental variables, and anthropogenic activities across spatial and temporal scales. There remains a knowledge gap regarding the underlying mechanisms through which these factors regulate microplastic distribution in river systems, calling for comprehensive and mechanistic studies.

In this study, micro-infrared spectroscopy was employed to conduct qualitative analysis of microplastics in surface water samples collected from the Minjiang and Tuojiang River basins. The characteristic infrared spectra of representative polymers are presented in Fig. 7. The results reveal distinct spatial and temporal variations in the distribution of microplastic types (Fig. 8, data see supplementary Table S7 and S8). In the year 2021, PET (34.2%) and PE (20.7%) were the dominant types of microplastics in the samples (Fig. 9(a)), while in the year 2023, PA (32.8%) and PE (19.5%) were the predominant types (Fig. 9(b)). In addition, EVA, PVC, PP, and small amounts of PS, polyacrylamide (PAM), and polyphenylene sulfide (PPS) were also found. PET, PA, PE, and PS were relatively common types of microplastics discovered in rivers39–40. For instance, PET accounted for 40.9% of microplastics in surface waters of urban rivers in Wuhan28, while PE accounts for approximately 23% in Dutch surface waters44. In this study, PLA, PBT, and PTF were also detected in the samples, and their corresponding plastic products are commonly used in daily human life. However, the detected quantities of these microplastics are relatively low, and their potential toxicity risks remain insufficiently assessed32 (Table 1). Accordingly, this study grouped the proportions of PLA, PBT, and PTFE into the “others” category in Figs. 7 and 8. The classification of plastics is primarily determined by their origin and specific polymer composition. Commonly used plastics, including PET, PA, and EVA are extensively utilized in daily life (refer to Table 1). Consequently, it is expected that these materials constitute the predominant types of microplastics found in the environment. However, the types of microplastics in the water environment are highly diverse. The sources of each type and the dynamic changes in their proportions within samples over time and space constitute an extremely complex issue. Currently, our understanding remains limited, and there is an urgent need for more systematic and in-depth research to conduct comprehensive and detailed investigations.

Microplastics risk assessments

Based on Eq. (1) to (6), the PLIriver, Hriver, and PRIriver for the rivers in the study area were calculated. The results are presented in Tables 4, 5 and 6. As shown in Figure Table 4, the PLIriver values of all rivers in the study area except the Shahe River were greater than 1 in 2021 and 2023, indicating the presence of microplastic pollution in these rivers. Among them, the Jianjiang River and the Tuojiang River exhibited higher PLIriver values, primarily due to their higher average microplastic concentrations. Generally, a higher microplastic concentration corresponds to a greater PLIriver value. Overall, the microplastic load index of the rivers in the study area showed no significant change between 2021 and 2023.

In this study, risk assessment was conducted for the following polymer types: PET, PE, PA, EVA, PP, and PVC. As shown in Table 5, the highest polymer type risk level in rivers within the study area in 2021 and 2022 was Grade II. Notably, the Hriver values in 2023 were generally higher than those in 2021. Specifically, in 2021, seven rivers—Nanhe, Fuhe, Xihe, Laonanhe, Jianjiang, Qingbaijiang, and Tuojiang—exhibited a polymer type risk level of Grade I, whereas by 2023, only Nanhe remained at Grade I. This trend indicates an increasing risk associated with microplastic polymer types in the rivers of the study area, warranting increased attention. The observed increase is primarily attributed to the rising proportion of PA in surface water (see Figs. 8 and 9). According to Table 2, PA has a polymer risk score of 47, which is among the highest for the dominant microplastic types in surface water, second only to PVC.

According to Table 6, except for Fuhe, Pihe, and Jianganhe, the PRIriver values of the remaining rivers in 2023 are higher than those in 2021, which is generally consistent with the trend in polymer-type-related risk. This finding indicates that the potential ecological risk index not only reflects the pollution levels of microplastics but also incorporates the toxic effects associated with different polymer types. Compared to assessments based solely on microplastic concentrations, variations in polymer toxicity may exert a more significant influence on the evaluation of potential ecological risks. This finding is consistent with the study by Xu et al. (2018)18, which indicates that high-risk areas of microplastic pollution in the Yangtze River estuary water environment are primarily attributable to the substantial accumulation of highly toxic polymers. It should be noted that this study considered only polymer types with high detection frequency, excluding those detected infrequently. Consequently, the actual ecological risk of microplastic pollution may be underestimated relative to the current assessment.

Comprehensive analysis revealed that although the microplastic load index remained relatively stable during the observation period, the increased relative abundance of highly hazardous polymers (e.g., PA and PVC) significantly heightened their potential ecological risks. Therefore, future assessment and management of microplastic pollution should urgently integrate polymer composition and associated toxicity characteristics into environmental evaluation systems and establish a toxicity-weighted risk management strategy to enable more accurate ecological risk control and provide robust scientific support for safeguarding water environment safety.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated microplastic pollution in the surface waters of the Minjiang and Tuojiang River valleys within Chengdu. Our findings revealed no significant temporal variations in microplastic concentrations, spatial variations in concentrations only between river valleys but not within river valleys, and significant temporal changes in polymer type composition. The pollution load index indicated that microplastic contamination was present in all examined rivers except for the Shahe River. The toxicity of polymers exerted a more significant influence on the risk of microplastic pollution compared to the pollution load index. In natural aquatic environments, microplastic contaminants not only impact the aquatic ecosystem but also potentially pose threats to human health. Therefore, enhancing the risk assessment metrics for microplastics to gain a more comprehensive understanding of their adverse effects is a critical focus for future research. It is imperative to initiate long-term microplastics monitoring projects at the earliest opportunity. Such initiatives will facilitate a comprehensive assessment of the cumulative effects and temporal trends of microplastics in the environment, thereby enhancing our understanding of their persistence and potential risks to both environmental and human health.
