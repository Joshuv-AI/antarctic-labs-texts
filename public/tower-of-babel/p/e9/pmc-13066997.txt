Prognostic modeling and validation of middle ear malignancy outcomes on the basis of SEER population data
Authors: Yi Ren, Li-Ping Peng, Tao Hou, Qiao Huang, Dun-Chang Mo, Long Chen, Shi-Hua Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalignant neoplasms of the middle ear represent a rare yet aggressive entity, with limited evidence regarding prognostic determinants and therapeutic outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the association between radiotherapy (RT) and survival outcomes, and to develop a prognostic nomogram for middle ear malignancies using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was performed on 206 patients diagnosed with middle ear malignancies from the SEER database [1975–2021]. Demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, race, tumor stage, surgical intervention, RT, and chemotherapy, were analyzed. Survival outcomes were assessed using the Kaplan-Meier (K-M) method and multivariate Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression models. A nomogram predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (OS) was constructed and internally validated.ResultsRT was administered to 56% of patients (n=112) and was associated with a 72% increased risk of mortality compared to patients not receiving RT [hazard ratio (HR) =1.72, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.16–2.55; P=0.007]. Patients aged ≥65 years exhibited a 2.6-fold higher mortality risk (HR =2.61; 95% CI: 1.75–3.88; P<0.001). Compared with localized disease, unknown tumor stage was linked to a 75% lower mortality risk (HR =0.25; 95% CI: 0.09–0.71; P=0.009), likely reflecting data limitations rather than a true protective effect. Advanced tumor stage significantly impacted OS (P=0.007). The nomogram incorporating age, race, laterality, tumor stage, surgical intervention, chemotherapy, and RT demonstrated good discriminative ability, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.707, 0.740, and 0.752 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS prediction, respectively.ConclusionsAn association between RT and increased mortality risk was observed in patients with middle ear malignancies; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to potential confounding and treatment selection bias inherent in retrospective database analyses. Age and tumor stage were identified as important prognostic factors. The developed nomogram offers a practical tool for individualized survival prediction to aid clinical decision-making. Prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify the role of RT in this patient population.Keywords: Radiotherapy (RT); middle ear malignancy; Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER); survival; nomogram

Introduction

Malignant tumors of the middle ear constitute a rare yet aggressive subset of head and neck malignancies that predominantly arise from the temporal bone and adjacent anatomical structures (1,2). Their low incidence has resulted in a limited understanding of their natural history, prognostic determinants, and optimal therapeutic approaches, presenting significant challenges in clinical management (3). Population-based epidemiological studies have consistently reported poor overall survival (OS) rates for these malignancies, with squamous cell carcinoma representing the most prevalent histological subtype and exhibiting the worst prognosis. In contrast, patients with adenocarcinomas and other nonsquamous histologies, particularly those diagnosed at early stages, have relatively favorable survival outcomes (4,5).

The management of early-stage middle ear malignancies primarily emphasizes surgical intervention, which effectively removes localized tumor tissue, thereby improving survival rates and quality of life. However, for locally advanced or metastatic disease, surgery alone often proves insufficient, necessitating multimodal approaches combining radiotherapy (RT) and chemotherapy. A study has shown that the efficacy of RT in middle ear malignancies is closely associated with tumor stage, with early-stage patients demonstrating favorable responses to RT, whereas postoperative RT offers greater benefits for advanced-stage patients (6). Furthermore, superselective intra-arterial chemotherapy combined with RT has emerged as a promising therapeutic option for locally advanced disease and has the potential to increase both overall and progression-free survival rates (7). Recent advancements in RT techniques, including intensity-modulated RT (IMRT) and stereotactic body RT (SBRT), have significantly improved local control and survival outcomes in head and neck cancers (8-10). However, the scarcity of specific data evaluating these modalities in middle ear malignancies, attributable to the rarity of the disease and the absence of large-scale prospective studies, remains a critical limitation (11,12).

To address these gaps, this study utilized the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database to systematically evaluate prognostic factors influencing survival in patients with malignant middle ear tumors and to develop a nomogram for individualized survival prediction. The proposed model aims to provide clinicians with a robust risk stratification tool to guide personalized treatment decisions. By expanding the clinical data pool for this rare malignancy, our findings offer valuable insights that may inform the design of future clinical trials and the development of optimized therapeutic strategies. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-aw-2518/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

For this retrospective study, we downloaded data from the Incidence-SEER Research Data, 8 Registries, Nov 2023 Sub [1975–2021] database. Using SEER*Stat software version 8.4.4, we filtered the data of patients diagnosed with middle ear malignancies from 1975 to 2021. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Patients diagnosed with middle ear malignancy were identified via the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd edition (ICD-O-3) classification, which was updated to incorporate the hematopoietic codes from the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumors of Hematopoietic and Lymphoid Tissues [2008]. We filtered data for patients with ‘nose, nasal cavity and middle ear’ as the site and morphology, using the site recode ICD-O-3/WHO 2008, and further selected data labeled C30.1-middle ear through the primary site-labeled filter. The study exclusively included patients who received beam RT, either alone or in conjunction with other forms of RT. Cases lacking data on the survival time and administration status of RT (recommended but unknown if administered) were excluded from the analysis.

Procedures and outcomes

The variables included in the data analysis were age, sex, race, year of diagnosis, laterality, clinical cancer stage, surgical intervention, RT, chemotherapy, survival duration in months, and survival status. The dataset consists of patients aged 15 years and above who were diagnosed with middle ear malignancies. Since age is considered a categorical variable in the SEER database and considering that the age distribution is linked to a higher incidence of middle ear malignancy, we divided age into two categories: individuals younger than 65 years and those aged 65 years and older. The year of diagnosis is categorized into two periods, 1975–1998 and 1999–2021, on the basis of the median year. In terms of stage information, we selected multiple variables, including the derived American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage group 6 [2004–2015], the derived AJCC stage group 7 [2010–2015], the 7th edition stage group recoding [2016–2017], and the derived extent of disease (EOD) 2018 stage group (2018 onward). However, owing to incomplete data, we ultimately opted for combined summary stage (2004+) information, classifying the stage labels into “localized”, “regional”, “distant”, and “unknown”. Surgery is dichotomized as “no” or “yes”, whereas RT is categorized as “no or unknown” or “yes”. The SEER database divides chemotherapy into “no” and “yes”. Importantly, no data were gathered on pathological diagnostic subtypes.

This study focused primarily on survival analysis of middle ear malignancies in the database. Survival time was measured from the end of diagnosis until death or the conclusion of follow-up. Additionally, survival status is categorized into deceased and censored, with censoring defined as being alive at the end of follow-up or lost to follow-up.

Statistical analysis

For categorical variables, baseline demographic characteristics were compared between the RT and non-RT groups via Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Survival analysis was conducted using complete-case data, with univariate analyses performed through the Kaplan-Meier (K-M) method complemented by log-rank tests. Multivariate analysis was implemented via Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression, incorporating covariates such as age, sex, race, year of diagnosis, surgical status, RT status, and chemotherapy status. Variable selection was optimized through the stepwise selection method on the basis of the Akaike information criterion (AIC).

The PH assumption was evaluated through Schoenfeld residual analysis, where covariates demonstrating nonhorizontal residual plots or P values <0.05 were considered to violate the PH assumption, necessitating stratification or time-dependent covariate adjustments. Following AIC-based screening and PH assumption testing, the sex variable was excluded, and the year of diagnosis was removed as a potential confounding factor. Although surgery and chemotherapy were not statistically significant in the multivariate Cox regression, these variables were retained in the nomogram due to their established clinical importance as key prognostic factors in middle ear malignancies. This approach balances statistical findings with clinical relevance to enhance the nomogram’s practical applicability and interpretability. Finally, the model incorporated seven variables for nomogram construction.

Model validation was performed via internal data, with model performance assessed through calibration curves and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05 (two-tailed). All the statistical computations were executed via R statistical software (version 4.4.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Given the retrospective nature of this study and reliance on registry data, potential confounding by indication and selection bias are important considerations. Treatment allocation, including receipt of RT, was not randomized and may have been influenced by unmeasured clinical factors such as tumor burden, patient comorbidities, or physician preference. Additionally, missing or incomplete data on tumor staging and treatment details may have introduced information bias. These limitations restrict the ability to establish causal relationships and necessitate cautious interpretation of associations observed in this analysis.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 206 patients diagnosed with middle ear malignancies were identified from the SEER database. Among these patients, 112 (54%) received RT, whereas 94 (46%) did not (Figure 1). The baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. The median age distribution revealed that 56.31% of patients were younger than 65 years, and 43.69% were aged 65 years or older. The majority of patients were White (79.13%), followed by other races (14.08%) and Black (6.80%). The sex distribution was approximately balanced, with 48.54% female and 51.46% male patients. The diagnosis years were split nearly evenly, with 44.66% diagnosed between 1975 and 1998 and 55.34% diagnosed between 1999 and 2021. Laterality was also balanced between left (53.88%) and right (46.12%) ear involvement.

Significant differences were observed between the RT and non-RT groups in terms of year of diagnosis (P<0.001), stage distribution (P<0.001), surgery (P<0.001), and chemotherapy administration (P<0.001). Specifically, patients who received RT were more likely to be diagnosed in recent years, have localized or distant disease, undergo surgery, and receive chemotherapy than those who did not receive RT. No significant differences were found in age, race, sex, or laterality between the two groups.

These differences suggest that treatment allocation may have been influenced by diagnosis year, disease stage, and other treatment factors, indicating potential selection bias that should be considered in subsequent analyses.

Survival analyses

The median survival duration was determined to be 25 months, with a range spanning from 0 to 535 months. The K-M survival curves (Figure 2) revealed a significant survival benefit for patients who received RT compared with those who did not receive RT (log-rank P<0.0001). The survival probability over time was consistently greater in the RT group than in the non-RT group, indicating improved OS. However, it should be noted that this univariate analysis did not adjust for potential confounders, and baseline differences between RT and non-RT groups may affect the interpretation of survival outcomes.

K-M survival curves. K-M, Kaplan-Meier.

The multivariate Cox PH regression analysis yielded a concordance index (C-index) of 0.7, indicating robust predictive performance. This model identified RT, age, and tumor stage as statistically significant independent prognostic factors for OS (Figure 3). Specifically, RT was associated with a 72% increased mortality risk compared with non-RT patients [hazard ratio (HR) =1.72; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.16–2.55; P=0.007]. Patients aged ≥65 years presented a 2.61-fold greater mortality risk than their younger counterparts did (HR =2.61; 95% CI: 1.75–3.88; P<0.001). Tumor stage analysis revealed that patients with unknown disease stage had a 75% reduction in mortality risk compared with those with localized disease (HR =0.25; 95% CI: 0.09–0.71; P=0.009), whereas regional (HR =1.27; 95% CI: 0.76–2.15; P=0.36) and distant disease stages (HR =0.88; 95% CI: 0.62–1.25; P=0.47) showed no statistically significant associations. The global P value for tumor stage was significant (P=0.007), indicating an overall prognostic influence of this variable. Other covariates, including sex, race, laterality, surgical intervention, and chemotherapy, were not significantly associated with survival outcomes. It is important to highlight that the association between RT and increased mortality risk may reflect treatment selection bias, where patients with more advanced disease or poorer prognosis were more likely to receive RT. Additionally, the significantly lower mortality risk observed in patients with unknown tumor stage likely results from missing or misclassified data, warranting cautious interpretation of this finding.

Nomogram construction and validation

A nomogram was constructed that incorporates age, race, laterality, stage, surgery, chemotherapy, and RT to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities (Figure 5). Each variable was assigned a weighted score reflecting its impact on survival.

Nomogram prediction of 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates on the basis of prognostic factors. OS, overall survival.

Internal validation via bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) demonstrated good calibration of the nomogram predictions at 1, 3, and 5 years (Figure 6A-6C). The calibration curves showed close agreement between the predicted and observed survival probabilities.

Calibration curves: calibration of the nomogram predictions at (A) 1-year, (B) 3-year, and (C) 5-year.

The discriminative ability of the nomogram was evaluated via time-dependent ROC curve analysis (Figure 7). The area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.707, 0.740, and 0.752 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival prediction, respectively, indicating satisfactory predictive accuracy. Despite the favorable model performance, given the limitations of the data source and potential biases, clinical application of the nomogram should be undertaken with caution and further validated in independent cohorts.

Discussion

Using the SEER database, our study conducted a comprehensive analysis of prognostic factors affecting OS in patients with middle ear malignancies and developed a nomogram for individualized survival prediction. In contrast to previous studies indicating a survival benefit from RT in middle ear malignancies (11,13), our findings demonstrated a significant association between RT and increased mortality risk, as evidenced by both unadjusted K-M analysis and multivariate Cox regression models. Age and tumor stage were identified as critical independent prognostic factors, with advanced age (≥65 years) and higher tumor stage correlating with elevated mortality risk. This unexpected observation may be attributed to treatment selection bias, whereby patients with more advanced or aggressive disease were preferentially treated with RT. Additionally, unmeasured confounders such as comorbidities, performance status, and treatment intent, which are not captured in the SEER database, may have influenced survival outcomes (14,15). Therefore, the observed association between RT and increased mortality should be interpreted with caution and does not imply a causal detrimental effect of RT.

Notably, the study period encompassed nearly five decades [1975–2021], during which significant advancements in diagnostic criteria, staging systems, and treatment modalities occurred. These temporal variations may have influenced patient management and survival outcomes, potentially introducing heterogeneity into the analysis and limiting the generalizability of the findings. Although the year of diagnosis was initially considered as a variable in our analysis, it was ultimately excluded from the final model due to a lack of statistical significance and potential collinearity with other covariates. We acknowledge that this temporal heterogeneity represents an important limitation of our study, as evolving treatment paradigms over such a long period may confound treatment effect estimates. Future studies should consider more detailed temporal stratification or standardized staging systems to better account for these changes and improve the robustness of prognostic modeling. While the protective effect of RT in head and neck cancers, including temporal bone malignancies, has been well documented (13,16-18), our study underscores the complexity of treatment effects in this rare malignancy and the challenges inherent in retrospective registry-based analyses. Prospective studies with detailed clinical and treatment data are warranted to clarify the true impact of RT on survival in patients with middle ear malignancies. Conversely, neither surgery nor chemotherapy demonstrated statistically significant survival benefits in this cohort, likely due to selection bias, treatment heterogeneity, and incomplete treatment details within the SEER database. Nevertheless, considering their established clinical importance, we retained these variables in the nomogram to improve its practical utility. We acknowledge that including variables without statistical significance may affect the model’s predictive accuracy, and thus, external validation is warranted to confirm the nomogram’s robustness. Additionally, the lack of detailed histological subtypes and molecular profiles precluded the exploration of subtype-specific responses to RT, which have been shown to vary in prior studies (11,19,20). These factors collectively highlight the complexity of treatment effects in this rare malignancy and the challenges inherent in retrospective registry-based analyses.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size and absence of detailed clinicopathological data, such as TNM staging, molecular markers, and specific surgical techniques, may compromise the accuracy and generalizability of the results. Additionally, the lack of detailed histologic subtype information, RT dose and technique, and surgical extent in the SEER database limits the granularity of our analysis. These missing clinical details restrict the exploration of subtype-specific treatment responses and prognostic differences, which have been demonstrated in prior studies to vary significantly among middle ear malignancies. The heterogeneity in tumor biology and treatment approaches likely impacts survival outcomes and complicates interpretation. Second, in constructing the nomogram, strict adherence to variable selection and exclusion criteria would have limited the model to only three variables. However, considering their clinical relevance, several variables excluded by the statistical criteria were retained because they are recognized as key prognostic factors (21). Guided by Schoenfeld residual analysis, only the variable “sex” was removed from the model, despite its clinical importance. These necessary compromises inevitably affect the accuracy and robustness of the prognostic model. Furthermore, the presence of missing or unknown data, particularly regarding tumor stage, may have introduced bias and affected the reliability of the survival estimates. Notably, the “unknown” stage group exhibited a paradoxically lower risk of death compared to the Localized group (HR =0.25), likely reflecting SEER database data quality issues such as insufficient data entry, delays in confirming survival status, and incomplete staging information. These factors may cause misclassification and bias, potentially reducing the nomogram’s reliability. Moreover, missing or misclassified stage data can distort HR estimates and compromise the robustness of multivariate analyses, especially in rare disease cohorts with limited sample sizes like middle ear malignancies. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting predictions involving the “unknown” stage, and efforts to improve data completeness are essential for future studies to enhance the accuracy and reliability of prognostic models. Finally, external validation of the nomogram in independent cohorts is warranted to confirm its predictive utility before clinical application.

In summary, our study provides valuable insights into prognostic factors and survival prediction in middle ear malignancies using a large population-based dataset. However, the findings should be interpreted with caution due to inherent limitations and potential biases. Future prospective studies with comprehensive clinical and molecular data are needed to refine prognostic models and optimize individualized treatment strategies for this rare and challenging disease.

Conclusions

This population-based study identified RT, age, and tumor stage as key independent prognostic factors for OS in middle ear malignancies. The observed association between RT and increased mortality likely reflects treatment selection bias. The developed nomogram offers a practical tool for individualized survival prediction, though its clinical utility requires further validation. Limitations such as retrospective design, limited clinical details, and lack of external validation highlight the need for larger prospective studies to better define prognostic factors and optimize treatment strategies in this rare malignancy.
