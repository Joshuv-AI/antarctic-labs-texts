Recycling of Mo and V from Spent Hydrogenation Catalysts via a One-Step Soda-Roasting Process
Authors: Weizhen Wang, Zhisheng Shi, Chaoxin Yuan, Baozhong Ma, Guanyong Sun, Guiqing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spent hydrogenation catalysts (SHC) are solid wastes from petroleum refining but contain high levels of MoS2 and V2S3, offering a valuable source for recycling Mo, V, and S. This study presented a one-step soda-roasting process designed to achieve simultaneous desulfurization and sodium conversion of SHC. During roasting, MoS2 and V2S3 were oxidized and reacted with Na2CO3 to form water-soluble Na2MoO4 and NaVO3, thereby enhancing the leaching efficiency of Mo and V. Increasing temperature favored spontaneous desulfurization and prevents its fixation in the calcine. Ni was immobilized as stable NiAl2O4, while alumina transformed into α-Al2O3, thereby suppressing the leaching of Ni and Al. Response surface methodology was employed to optimize soda-roasting parameters. About 99.55% of S was removed, and 98.26% of V and 99.68% of Mo were leached out, after roasting at 1150 °C for 60 min with Na2CO3 dosage molar ratio of 1.21. Compared with conventional multistep roasting, the one-step process generates a net incremental benefit of USD 3.405 million/a at 15 000 t/a, with the extra investment recovered in only 0.12 years. This process provides a direct soda-roasting approach for recovery of Mo, V and removal of S from SHC.

Introduction

Hydrogenation catalysts play a critical role in petroleum refining processes such as hydrodesulfurization, hydrodemetallization, and heavy oil upgrading. Their use significantly enhances fuel quality and facilitates compliance with increasingly stringent environmental regulations. However, these catalysts gradually deactivate during operation due to coking, sintering, and the deposition of heavy metals. , Globally, an estimated 150,000 to 170,000 t of spent hydrogenation catalysts (SHC) are generated annually. SHC pose substantial environmental hazards owing to their content of heavy metals, toxic organic compounds, and residual flammable hydrocarbons. Improper storage can lead to contamination of groundwater, soil, and air. , At the same time, SHC contain strategically valuable elements, including sulfur, molybdenum, vanadium, nickel, cobalt, and tungsten. Thereby they are qualified as a form of urban mine. , Consequently, recycling of the SHC has attracted the attention for the considerable environmental and economic benefits.

Current approaches to resource recovery from SHC fall into three primary categories: hydrometallurgical leaching, − roasting–leaching hybrid methods, and pyrometallurgical enrichment. Hydrometallurgical leaching typically employs acidic or alkaline solutions to selectively extract target metals with high efficiency. , Nevertheless, this method often consumes massive reagents, causes severe equipment corrosion, and entails costly effluent treatment. Moreover, most hydrometallurgical processes necessitate prior removal of organic residues, increasing process complexity and operational costs.

Pyrometallurgical enrichment offers shorter process chains and higher throughput. However, it is constrained by high energy consumption, substantial capital costs, and limited selectivity in metal separation, which collectively impede its industrial scalability. In contrast, the soda roasting–leaching process has emerged as the dominant industrial method due to its technological maturity and high metal recovery efficiency. , During roasting, Mo, V, and W are converted into water-soluble sodium salts, which are subsequently recovered via aqueous leaching. This approach enhances the selective extraction of valuable metals and reduces downstream separation complexity.

Despite these advantages, conventional soda roasting is subject to several critical limitations that hinder sustainable development. These include the necessity of complex pretreatment steps, low desulfurization efficiency, and inefficient sulfur utilization. The roasting and leaching processes typically require preremoval of organic matter before roasting, , commonly achieved through solvent washing, , mechanical degreasing, or oxidative roasting. Such pretreatment is intended to prevent localized overheating, the formation of insoluble carbonitrides, and the release of hazardous gases. Nevertheless, these additional steps increase process complexity, elevate energy demand, and pose risks of secondary pollution.

In addition, SHC contain sulfur at concentrations exceeding 8%, and the efficient removal and recovery of sulfur represent a challenge. The sulfur-fixing effect of Na2CO3 in the roasting system is the key factor limiting sulfur recovery. During roasting, Na2CO3 reacts with SO2 generated from sulfide oxidation to form stable sulfates. This reaction directly suppresses sulfur volatilization and impedes sulfide decomposition, resulting in a sulfur removal efficiency of only approximately 60%. ,

The low desulfurization efficiency leads to several downstream complications. For example, the SO2 concentration in the flue gas is typically around 1 vol %, which is insufficient to meet the minimum threshold required for conventional sulfuric acid production. Consequently, the flue gas must be treated as waste, precluding the resource recovery of sulfur. Moreover, residual sulfur reacts with Na2CO3 to form extra sulfate species. This side reaction increases reagent consumption, elevates costs, introduces sulfate impurities, and complicates downstream processes.

Recent studies have explored in situ sulfur fixation strategies to enable cleaner roasting processes. Specifically, microwave-assisted soda roasting has demonstrated potential for converting sulfur into Na2SO4, thereby suppressing SO x emissions and facilitating sulfur immobilization. , However, nearly all existing strategies require degreasing pretreatment prior to roasting. Consequently, few investigations have focused on the direct treatment of SHC with high organic content.

However, conventional roasting methods still result in high aluminum leaching during the subsequent leaching process. Liu et al. proposed a two-stage roasting process: organic matter was first removed by roasting at 450 °C for 2 h, after which the residue was mixed with Na2CO3 at a 1:1 mass ratio and roasted again at 1000 °C for 2 h. Although this approach achieved an aluminum leaching rate of 99.21%, it introduces significant challenges. Specifically, the high aluminum concentration complicates the downstream separation of Mo, V, and Al, raising costs and operational complexity.

Furthermore, addressing the pervasive issue of excessive aluminum dissolution, recent advances in waste valorization offer new perspectives. Yuan et al. converted Salvia miltiorrhiza dregs into a phosphate-modified multifunctional biochar (3K-BC) that demonstrated high adsorption capacities for Pb and Cd, effective soil heavy metal immobilization, and plant growth promotion. This suggests that Al-rich leaching residues, through appropriate thermochemical conversion or surface functionalization, could be developed into high-performance adsorbents or soil remediation agents rather than being treated as mere waste. Hence, controlled suppression of Al dissolution not only simplifies Mo/V separation, but also enables high-value utilization of the Al-enriched residuefully aligned with the circular economy principle.

Overall, most reported recycling strategies require mandatory degreasing and organic removal pretreatment before roasting. Although conventional soda roasting and leaching have been widely applied for sulfide waste treatment, such processes are unsuitable for oil-rich SHC. Residual organics and complex sulfide components interfere with phase transformation. Additionally, excessive Al leaching in existing multistep procedures restricts separation of Mo and V. Therefore, developing a strategy for the direct treatment of oily SHC with controllable impurity leaching is highly essential.

The direct roasting–leaching strategy proposed herein also resonates with recent trends in green metallurgy. Li et al. employed waste coffee shells as a renewable reductant to convert inert hematite into reactive magnetite and zerovalent iron at 700 °C, achieving >97% iron extraction under mild oxalic acid leaching. , That work highlighted a synergistic mechanism between biomass-driven reductive pore-creation and organic acid complexation leaching. Analogously, our direct Na2CO3 roasting of oily SHC exploits the thermal energy and reducing microenvironment from organic combustion to simultaneously achieve deep desulfurization and reactive phase formationan in situ coprocessing strategy. Furthermore, Abdalla et al. developed an anionic Schiff base adsorbent (HYM/QA) with an ultrahigh V­(V) capacity of 392.25 mg g–1, enabling direct production of high-purity V2O5 from complex leachate. Although the present study ends at the neutral V/Mo leachate stage, the above advances confirm its downstream value: the efficient selective leaching of V and Mo with deep Al suppression provides an ideal feed solution for future selective separation, forming the logical starting point for our subsequent short-flow production of high-purity V and Mo products.

This study proposes a direct roasting and leaching route that eliminates independent oil-removal pretreatment. The effects of roasting temperature, roasting duration, and Na2CO3 dosage on removal of S and extraction of Mo and V were investigated. Mineral phase transformations and reaction mechanisms during roasting were elucidated via thermodynamic analysis and microscopic characterization. This work offers an optimized route for efficient SHC utilization.

Experimental Methods

Materials and Reagents

The experimental raw material was an alumina-based molybdenum–nickel SHC provided by a refinery in Sichuan Province, China. The SHC consisted of black cylindrical particles, 2 to 5 mm in length, with visible oil residues on the surface, as illustrated in Figure (a). Analytical-grade Na2CO3, obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., was employed as the roasting agent for desulfurization.

(a) Photo of SHC (Photograph courtesy of “Zhisheng Shi”. Copyright 2025.); (b) XRD patterns of SHC after washing. Valence states of elements in SHC after washing: (c) S; (d) Ni; (e) Mo; (f) V.

Experimental Procedures

During roasting procedure, the SHC was mixed with Na2CO3 at concentrations ranging from 13.2 wt % to 16.8 wt %. The mixture was ground in a vibratory mill and sieved through a 20-mesh screen. Then it was transferred to a crucible and heated in a box furnace (NBD-M1500, Nobody Materials Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China). Following the isothermal hold, the roasted product was removed and cooled. Roasting temperature experiments were conducted with a fixed Na2CO3 molar ratio (n(Na2CO3) = 1/2n(V) + n(Mo)), a roasting duration of 120 min, and heating at 10 °C/min to target temperatures of 850, 900, 1000, 1100, and 1200 °C. Roasting time experiments employed a fixed Na2CO3 molar ratio of 1.2 and a roasting temperature of 1100 °C, with isothermal durations of 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. Na2CO3 dosage experiments were performed at a fixed roasting temperature of 1100 °C and duration of 90 min, with Na2CO3 molar ratios of 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, and 1.5.

For leaching, a predetermined mass of the roasted calcine was placed into a reactor with deionized water at a liquid-to-solid ratio of 5:1. The suspension was stirred at 400 rpm in a water bath at 50 °C for 2 h. After leaching, the mixture was filtered for solid–liquid separation, yielding a V- and Mo-rich leachate and a solid residue enriched in Ni and Al.

Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed to achieve multifactor synergistic optimization of roasting process parameters, including roasting temperature, roasting duration, and Na2CO3 dosage.

Thermodynamic equilibrium simulations were conducted using the Equilib module of FactSage software. The model system comprised untreated SHC and Na2CO3 under an oxidizing atmosphere. Input parameters included stoichiometric amounts of Na2CO3 and O2, with phases defined as ideal gases, pure solids, or pure liquids. Stable condensed phases were selected automatically, and calculations were performed from 900 to 1200 °C in 50 °C increments at 1 atm.

Characterization

Elemental concentrations of C, S, Mo, V, Ni, and Al in the SHC, calcine, leach solution, and residue were quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 5110, USA). Recovery efficiencies were calculated using both elemental concentrations and signal-to-noise ratios to minimize systematic uncertainties. Leaching efficiencies of Mo and V were determined based on residual metal contents in the solid residue, while Al leaching efficiency was calculated from its concentration in the leachate. Phase compositions of the SHC, calcine, and residue were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku Ultima IV, Japan). Thermal behaviors of the SHC and the mixture of SHC with Na2CO3 were analyzed by thermogravimetry and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC, NETZSCH STA 449 F3, Germany).

Surface organic contaminants were removed via triple washing with anhydrous ethanol to avoid interference in subsequent phase and oxidation state analyses. XRD analysis of the SHC is shown in Figure (b). Although washing removed surface oils, residual amorphous carbon remained. This phase produces no distinct diffraction peaks but increases X-ray scattering, lowering peak intensities and signal-to-noise ratio.

Comparison with reference patterns identified the primary phases as alumina, NiS, and V2S3. Al2O3, serving as the catalyst support, exhibited strong peaks at 2θ ≈ 37.6°, 45.9°, and 67.0°. NiS, representing sulfided active components, showed weaker peaks at 2θ ≈ 26.1°, 36.2°, and 54.9°. Vanadium sulfide formed via cleavage and sulfidation of vanadyl pyrroles and vanadates in the feedstock, depositing on or within the support and contributing to deactivation. Its characteristic peaks appeared at 2θ ≈ 17.5°, 35.1°, and 45.8°.

XPS was employed to investigate the chemical states of S, Ni, Mo, and V in the washed SHC, as presented in Figure (c–f). Residual carbon was detected and used for charge referencing during spectral calibration.

Sulfur existed mainly as S2– and SO4 2–. The S2– was ascribed to lattice sulfur in nickel–molybdenum sulfides (the active sites for hydrogenation), and its presence indicates partial preservation of the active sulfide structure after deactivation. The SO4 2– originated from oxidation during catalyst service or air exposure after use, where trace oxygen in feedstock and subsequent atmospheric contact caused gradual sulfide oxidation to sulfate.

The XPS signal of Mo appeared at 223 to 236 eV. Peak deconvolution confirmed MoS2 as the dominant phase, with minor interference from the S 2s peak, consistent with the active sulfided structure of the catalyst. Ni existed mainly as Ni2+, with no metallic Ni0 or highly oxidized Ni3+ observed, indicating no excessive reduction or deep oxidation during catalyst operation. Under sulfidation, nickel usually forms NiS or Mo–Ni–S bimetallic phases as a promoter. V was mainly observed as V3+, corresponding to vanadium sulfide deposition on the catalyst surface. The absence of V5+ implied negligible oxidation during vanadium accumulation.

TG-DSC was employed to examine the thermal reaction characteristics and temperature ranges of organics and sulfides in the SHC. The results provide a basis for optimizing soda roasting parameters and clarifying reaction mechanisms. Figure presents the TG-DSC curves of the SHC from room temperature to 1200 °C. The thermal behavior profile is characterized by three distinct stages.

Thermal reaction characteristics and temperature ranges of organics and sulfides in the SHC.

Initially, in the first stage (30 to 232 °C), the sample mass decreased gradually to 94.51% of the initial weight. The DSC signal remained nearly thermally neutral during this period. This stage corresponds to the volatilization of adsorbed water and trace light oil fractions. The results indicate that the bulk material remained stable.

Subsequently, the second stage (232 to 820 °C) exhibited a significant mass drop to 57.46% of the initial weight. This loss accounted for 86.24% of the total weight loss. The maximum weight loss rate occurred at 446 °C and reached −0.0176%/°C at the derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) peak. This stage represents the major combustion of oil and coke deposits. During this process, organics were converted into CO2 and H2O­(g) and released. Concurrently, oxidation reactions of MoS2, V2S3, and NiS took place. This stage involves coupled combustion of organic components and sulfide oxidation. Thermodynamic equilibrium calculations in Section clarify the reaction characteristics and critical temperature range.

Finally, in the third stage (820 to 1200 °C), the mass decreased slowly to 57.04% of the initial weight. The DTG curve approached zero, and DSC thermal effects diminished. These observations suggest that the reactions were complete. The system then achieves complete reaction and structural stabilization, with no obvious gas release or mass change. These thermal intervals provide a basis for analyzing the roasting mechanism.

Figure illustrates the variation in Gibbs free energy (ΔG) with temperature for the key roasting reactions. All reactions exhibit negative ΔG values across the temperature range from 600 to 1200 °C, indicating that they proceed spontaneously under these conditions. Among them, carbon combustion and sulfide oxidation display the most negative ΔG values, signifying the highest thermodynamic favorability. To investigate mechanisms and reaction feasibility during soda roasting, thermodynamic equilibrium simulations were conducted based on components of SHC. The simulated SHC was composed of C20H42, C, S, V2S3, MoS2, NiS, and Al2O3, based on the composition presented in Section . All calculations were performed using 100 g of SHC, with detailed composition given in Table S1. The theoretical stoichiometric requirement of Na2CO3 was determined from balanced reaction equations, with an initial dosage of 12.00 g applied in the baseline simulation. Additionally, 3.750 mol of O2 was introduced to represent the oxidizing atmosphere. Simulations were performed across a temperature range from 900 to 1200 °C under the assumption of thermodynamic equilibrium.

The major product phases identified included gaseous species (CO2, H2O, SO2, O2, SO3, CO, etc.), oxide phases (Al2O3, VO x , NiO), slag phases (Na2O, VO x ), spinel (NiAl2O4), Na2MoO4, and Na2SO4. The gas-phase components are reported as volume fractions, whereas condensed phases are expressed as mass fractions.

The effects of reaction temperature, Na2CO3 dosage, and oxygen input on phase distribution were systematically evaluated. At a fixed O2 supply of 3.750 mol and roasting temperature of 1100 °C, the effect of Na2CO3 dosage on sulfur speciation was examined (Figure (a)). As the Na2CO3 dosage increased from 9.6 to 18.0 g, the SO2 volume fraction in the off-gas decreased from 9.46 vol % to 7.63 vol %, while the mass of Na2SO4 rose from 2.39 to 13.10 g. This trend indicates that excess Na2CO3 enhances sulfur fixation in the form of Na2SO4, thereby reducing the amount of sulfur available for volatilization as SO2. Consequently, excessive Na2CO3 addition is detrimental to SO2 recovery and may adversely affect process economics. To optimize SO2 yield for sulfuric acid production, Na2CO3 dosage must be carefully controlled to balance efficient V and Mo extraction with sufficient sulfur release as SO2.

(a) Effect of Na 2 CO 3 dosage on the mass of each phase; (b) effect of oxygen dosage on the mass of each phase; (c) effect of oxygen content on CO and SO 2 ; (d) effect of roasting temperature on sulfur content and desulfurization efficiency.

The masses of the oxide and spinel phases remained nearly constant across increasingNa2CO3 dosages, while the slag phase varied by less than ±0.2%. These results indicate that variations in Na2CO3 dosage have a negligible effect on the stability of the oxide and spinel phases.

Moreover, as the Na2CO3 dosage was increased from 9.6 to 18.0 g, the mass fraction of volatile molybdenum in the flue gas decreased from 8.46% to 4.85%, while the Na2MoO4 content increased correspondingly. This trend confirms that a higher Na2CO3 dosage promotes the conversion of molybdenum into Na2MoO4, thereby enhancing molybdenum recovery efficiency.

Thermodynamic simulations were performed at a Na2CO3 dosage of 12.0 g and roasting temperature of 1100 °C (Figure (b,c)). At O2 inputs of 3.000 and 3.375 mol, CO concentrations in the flue gas were 9.67 vol% and 3.81 vol%, respectively, indicating incomplete oxidation of carbonaceous species due to insufficient oxygen. When the O2 input reached or exceeded 3.750 mol, CO was no longer detected, confirming complete oxidation to CO2 under oxygen-sufficient conditions. These findings underscore the critical role of oxygen stoichiometry in determining carbon oxidation pathways.

Under oxygen-deficient conditions, the oxidation of V2S3, MoS2 and Ni3S2 was limited. As oxygen availability increased, the concentration of SO2 in the gas phase gradually decreased, while the formation of Na2SO4 increased, indicating enhanced sulfur fixation in the solid phase. Further oxygen enrichment triggered a sequential oxidation of transition metal sulfides: molybdenum sulfides oxidized first, followed by vanadium sulfides, and finally nickel sulfides. This sequence reveals a distinct oxidation hierarchy among these sulfides.

At low oxygen partial pressures, nickel remained predominantly in the sulfide form, which inhibited the formation of NiAl2O4 due to insufficient oxidant. Under oxygen-rich conditions, nickel sulfide oxidizes only after the sulfides of Mo and V, allowing subsequent reaction with Al2O3 to form the NiAl2O4 spinel. Similarly, vanadium sulfide was readily oxidized under mild conditions and partially partitioned into the slag. With increased oxygen, vanadium was further oxidized and reacted with Na2CO3 to form NaVO3 in the slag.

Molybdenum was distributed between Na2MoO4 and volatile species. As oxygen concentration rose, the proportion of volatile molybdenum increased, whereas Na2MoO4 formation decreased. Excess oxygen promotes the generation of volatile MoO3, which escapes from the reaction zone before it can react with Na2CO3. Consequently, the yield of Na2MoO4 is suppressed under highly oxidizing conditions. These findings indicate that precise control of oxygen partial pressure is essential to maximize Na2MoO4 recovery.

Simulations were conducted over a temperature range from 900 to 1200 °C, with Na2CO3 fixed at 12.0 g and O2 at 3.75 mol, as illustrated in Figure (d). Increasing the roasting temperature led to a marked rise in SO2 concentration in the flue gas and a corresponding decline in Na2SO4 content in the solid phase. This trend can be attributed to enhanced reaction kinetics at elevated temperatures, which favor the direct decomposition and oxidation of sulfur-containing compounds to SO2. Furthermore, because Na2SO4 formation is exothermic, higher temperatures shift the equilibrium toward SO2 release. Between 900 and 1150 °C, formation was progressively suppressed; above 1200 °C, it was no longer detectable, with all sulfur emitted exclusively as gaseous SO2.

Molybdenum exhibited distinct temperature-dependent behavior. Significant volatilization occurred between 900 and 1000 °C, likely due to the formation and evaporation of MoO3 following sulfide decomposition. In contrast, from 1050 to 1200 °C, molybdenum was predominantly retained in the solid phase as Na2MoO4. This transition reflects the thermodynamic favorability of the reaction between MoO3 and Na2CO3 at higher temperatures. Consequently, the concentration of volatile molybdenum species decreased with increasing temperature, while Na2MoO4 content increased, indicating that roasting temperature can be strategically optimized to enhance molybdenum fixation.

Concurrently, the contents of NiAl2O4 and Na2MoO4 increased steadily from 900 to 1150 °C, whereas Na2SO4 declined. At 1200 °C, Al2O3 became more actively involved in spinel formation, and a new crystalline phase, NaAl9O14, emerged. The formation of this phase consumed both Na2O and Al2O3, thereby altering the solid-phase equilibrium and influencing the distribution of other sodium-bearing compounds.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that sulfur can be volatilized from SHC through careful optimization of Na2CO3 dosage, oxygen supply, and roasting temperature, enabling its recovery as SO2. V and Mo are efficiently converted into water-soluble sodium salts, which facilitates their subsequent leaching and recovery. Meanwhile, Ni and Al are immobilized in stable, leach-resistant spinel phases such as NiAl2O4. These findings confirm the thermodynamic feasibility of the proposed roasting process and provide a robust theoretical basis for its scale-up and industrial implementation.

Simultaneous Desulfurization and Sodium Conversion for SHC

A one-step soda-roasting treatment was applied to enhance oxidization of S, V2S3 and MoS2 into NaVO3, Na2MoO4 and SO2, which achieved the simultaneous desulfurization and sodium conversion for SHC. The effect of roasting temperature, roasting time and Na2CO3 dosage was investigated.

Roasting time was fixed at 120 min, and the Na2CO3 dosage was maintained at 1.2 times the stoichiometric requirement. The influence of roasting temperature (850 to 1200 °C) on desulfurization efficiency and the leaching behavior of V and Mo is presented in Figure (a,b). As the temperature increased from 850 to 1200 °C, the residual sulfur content decreased from 2.45 wt % to 0.13 wt %, corresponding to an increase in desulfurization efficiency from 87.95% to 99.48%.

Effects of roasting temperature (a), roasting time (c), and Na 2 CO 3 molar ratio (e) on desulfurization efficiency; effects of roasting temperature (b), roasting time (d), and Na2CO3 molar ratio (f) on leaching efficiencies of V, Mo and Al.

At lower temperatures (850 to 900 °C), carbon and residual oil in the SHC underwent partial combustion. However, oxidation rates of metal sulfides, such as MoS2, Ni3S2, and vanadium sulfides, remained low. Moreover, the generated SO2 reacted with Na2CO3, leading to the formation of sulfur-containing species such as sulfides, sulfites, and sulfates, which retained sulfur in the solid phase. Consequently, desulfurization efficiency improved only gradually.

In contrast, when the temperature exceeded 1000 °C, sufficient activation energy was provided to drive rapid oxidation of sulfide phases. S2– was efficiently converted to SO2, which can be captured for sulfuric acid production. This resulted in a marked reduction in residual sulfur in the roasted residue. At 1200 °C, a desulfurization efficiency of 99.48% was achieved, indicating near-complete conversion of oxidizable sulfur species. Further increases in temperature yielded negligible additional improvements in desulfurization performance.

The effect of roasting temperature (900 to 1200 °C) on the leaching efficiencies of V and Mo is shown in Figure (b). Both elements exhibited a steady increase in leaching efficiency with rising temperature. In contrast, Al leaching remained consistently low (below 0.05%) across all temperatures tested. At 900 °C, the reactions between V2O5 and MoO3 with Na2CO3 proceeded incompletely, yielding NaVO3 and Na2MoO4. Under these conditions, V and Mo leaching efficiencies were 94.51% and 95.96%, respectively. As the roasting temperature increased to 1200 °C, conversion to the corresponding sodium salts became more complete, resulting in leaching efficiencies of 98.23% for vanadium and 99.67% for molybdenum.

XRD patterns of the roasted residues (Figure ) confirm a phase transformation in alumina. All samples roasted between 900 and 1200 °C contained α-Al2O3 (peaks at 2θ ≈ 35.1°, 43.4°, 57.5°; PDF#00–005–0712). This contrasts with the γ-Al2O3 present in the raw catalyst (peaks at 2θ ≈ 37.5°, 45.8°, 67.2°; PDF#00–010–0425). This transition from γ-Al2O3 to α-Al2O3, occurs via an intermediate θ-Al2O3 phase above 900 °C and is driven by thermodynamic stabilization at elevated temperatures. The resulting α-Al2O3 possesses a hexagonal close-packed structure, reduced defect density, and enhanced chemical stability. Consequently, the formation of soluble sodium aluminate is suppressed, which is consistent with the consistently low aluminum leaching efficiency (<0.05%).

XRD patterns of roasted SHC at different roasting temperatures.

At 900 °C, NaVO3 (PDF#04–007–7019) was fully formed, as evidenced by characteristic XRD peaks at 2θ ≈ 24.6°, 27.3°, and 28.4°. In contrast, no diffraction peaks corresponding to Na2MoO4 were detected, indicating that vanadium sulfides had been completely oxidized to V2O5 and subsequently reacted with Na2CO3, whereas molybdenum oxidation remained incomplete under these conditions.

Between 1000 and 1200 °C, distinct peaks attributable to Na2MoO4 (PDF#97–000–4156; 2θ ≈ 12.8°, 24.6°, 28.2°) emerged and increased in intensity with rising temperature, while peak broadening decreased. This trend reflects enhanced reaction completeness and improved crystallinity, likely due to greater atomic mobility and higher activation energy at elevated temperatures.

Na2SO4 (PDF#97–008–1506) was observed at 900 °C (peaks at 2θ ≈ 19.0°, 28.1°, 32.1°) but disappeared entirely above 1000 °C. This observation is consistent with increasing desulfurization efficiency. At lower temperatures, SO2 generated from sulfide oxidation partially reacted with Na2CO3 to form residual Na2SO4. However, at higher temperatures, accelerated SO2 volatilization coupled with thermal decomposition of Na2SO4 facilitated more complete sulfur removal.

Additionally, diffraction peaks corresponding to NiAl2O4 (PDF#97–024–7072; 2θ ≈ 37.1°, 45.1°, 65.7°) appeared between 1000 and 1200 °C and intensified with increasing temperature. This phase forms via solid-state reaction between NiO and α-Al2O3 and is insoluble in water, thereby causing nickel to report to the leaching residue.

Collectively, the XRD results demonstrate that roasting temperature critically governs phase evolution during thermal treatment. Elevated temperatures promote the transformation of γ-Al2O3 to α-Al2O3, enhance desulfurization, facilitate the formation of sodium vanadate and molybdate, and stabilize the NiAl2O4 spinel. Based on optimal desulfurization efficiency (99.07%) and high leaching yields (V: 98.16%; Mo: 99.28%), a roasting temperature of 1100 °C was selected as the benchmark condition for subsequent experiments.

The roasting temperature was fixed at 1100 °C, and the Na2CO3 dosage was set to 1.2 times the stoichiometric requirement. The effect of roasting time on desulfurization efficiency and the leaching behavior of V and Mo was evaluated, with results presented in Figure (c,d). As the roasting time increased from 15 to 120 min, the residual sulfur content decreased continuously from 1.16 wt % to 0.20 wt %, corresponding to an increase in desulfurization efficiency from 94.49% to 99.10%.

Extending the roasting duration to 90 min allowed sufficient time for oxygen diffusion, interfacial contact, and oxidation of sulfides. Under these conditions, unreacted sulfides were progressively oxidized and decomposed, releasing SO2. As a result, the residual sulfur content declined rapidly to 0.22 wt %, and desulfurization efficiency rose to 98.99%. A further increase in roasting time to 120 min reduced the sulfur content only marginally to 0.20 wt %, with a corresponding efficiency of 99.07%. The diminished rate of sulfur removal beyond 90 min suggests that the remaining sulfur is associated with encapsulated or inert sulfide phases. Continued reaction under these conditions requires oxygen or sodium species to diffuse through a product layer, which increases mass transfer resistance and slows reaction kinetics.

The effect of roasting time on the leaching efficiencies of V and Mo is presented in Figure (d). Leaching efficiencies for both metals increased steadily with extended roasting duration. At 30 min, V and Mo leaching efficiencies reached 97.70% and 99.21%, respectively. By 120 min, these values increased marginally to 98.16% and 99.28%, indicating that near-complete extraction had been achieved.

Soda roasting is employed to convert water-insoluble V and Mo compounds present in the SHC into water-soluble sodium salts. The high leaching efficiencies observed at 30 min suggest that the principal conversion reactions were largely complete by this point. Further roasting from 30 to 120 min enabled additional reaction between residual V and Mo species and O2/Na2CO3, thereby promoting their transformation into leachable sodium salts. Notably, aluminum leaching efficiency remained below 0.02% throughout the roasting time study. This indicates that Na2CO3 preferentially reacted with sulfur, vanadium, and molybdenum, while Al2O3 remained essentially unreactive under the applied conditions. Consequently, the resulting leach liquor contained negligible aluminum impuritiesa feature that facilitates downstream separation of valuable metals. Based on a balance between desulfurization efficiency and metal leaching performance, a roasting time of 90 min was selected as the benchmark for subsequent experiments. At this duration, desulfurization efficiency reached 99.07%, while V and Mo leaching efficiencies were 98.16% and 99.28%, respectively.

Time-dependent XRD patterns obtained at 1100 °C are shown in Figure . At 15 min holding time, phases included Na2SO4, γ-Al2O3, and NaVO3; Na2MoO4 nor α-Al2O3 was detected. These results suggest incomplete oxidation of sulfide, partial capture SO2 as Na2SO4, and insufficient time for support phase transition or Ni–Al interaction.

XRD patterns of SHC calcines at different roasting durations. Note: Roasting time refers to the isothermal holding duration after the sample reaches the target temperature.

After 30 min of roasting, the peaks corresponding to Na2SO4 diminished markedly. Concurrently, the intensity of γ-Al2O3 decreased, while peaks characteristic of α-Al2O3 began to emerge. The intensities of NaVO3 peaks increased, and new peaks attributable to Na2MoO4 appeared. The reduction in Na2SO4 is consistent with SO2 volatilization and thermal decomposition under prolonged heating conditions.

The progressive increase in NaVO3 and Na2MoO4 peak intensities indicates ongoing soda roasting reactions. Furthermore, the onset of the γ-Al2O3 to α-Al2O3 phase transformation suggests the formation of a chemically inert alumina support, which accounts for the low aluminum leaching observed in subsequent leaching tests.

With extended roasting time, the peaks of NaVO3 and Na2MoO4 further intensified, and their full width at half-maximum (FWHM) narrowed, indicating enhanced crystallinity and improved leachability. Simultaneously, NiAl2O4 began to form. Given its aqueous insolubility, this spinel phase retains nickel in the solid residue.

By 90 min, the phase composition stabilized. Prolonged roasting beyond this point resulted in sharper diffraction peaks and reduced FWHM values, reflecting continued improvement in the crystallinity of sodium vanadate and molybdate salts. Under these conditions, desulfurization efficiency exceeded 99%, the γ-Al2O3 to α-Al2O3 conversion was initiated, the crystallinity and solubility of NaVO3 and Na2MoO4 were optimized, NiAl2O4 formation was stabilized, and nickel retention in the residue surpassed 99%. This combination of effects maximizes the separation efficiency between nickel and the target metalsV and Mo.

The effect of Na2CO3 dosage on desulfurization efficiency and the leaching behavior of V and Mo was investigated under fixed roasting conditions (1100 °C for 90 min). The molar ratio of Na2CO3 to the stoichiometric requirement was varied from 1.1 to 1.5. Excess Na2CO3 was introduced to enhance reaction kinetics.

As shown in Figure (e,f), a molar ratio of 1.1 resulted in a residual sulfur content of 0.065 wt % and a desulfurization efficiency of 99.70%. At this dosage, Na2CO3 was predominantly consumed in the conversion of V and Mo sulfides and oxides, leaving minimal excess available for sulfur fixation. Consequently, sulfur retention in the slag was minimized, and desulfurization efficiency was maximized.

When the Na2CO3 dosage exceeded the stoichiometric requirement, an increasing proportion participated in sulfur fixation. Excess Na2CO3 reacted with sulfur-bearing species that would otherwise volatilize as SO2, forming stable Na2SO4 that remained in the oxidized slag. This led to higher sulfur content in the solid residue and a corresponding decrease in desulfurization efficiency with increasing Na2CO3 dosage.

Increasing the Na2CO3 molar ratio from 1.1 to 1.5 improved vanadium leaching efficiency from 96.12% to 98.35% and molybdenum leaching efficiency from 95.88% to 99.67%. This trend reflects the dependence of the soda roasting reaction on reactant concentration. At a molar ratio of 1.1, insufficient Na2CO3 limited the complete conversion of certain vanadium- and molybdenum-bearing phases, resulting in their retention in the slag and reduced leaching efficiency. In contrast, higher Na2CO3 dosages enhanced reactant availability, thereby driving the roasting reactions toward completion and promoting the formation of water-soluble sodium vanadate and molybdate species. Consequently, dissolution during subsequent leaching was facilitated, leading to increased metal recovery. However, further increases in the molar ratio from 1.4 to 1.5 yielded only negligible improvements in leaching efficiency for both metals.

Aluminum leaching remained consistently low across all tested ratios, with efficiencies below 0.02%. This behavior can be attributed to the thermodynamic stability of aluminum oxide, which exhibits lower reactivity toward Na2CO3 compared with V and Mo oxides. Moreover, during roasting, γ-Al2O3 undergoes phase transformation to the denser and more inert α-Al2O3 polymorph. This structural change impedes diffusion reaction with Na2CO3, suppressing the formation of soluble aluminum species.

A Na2CO3 molar ratio of 1.2 was found to offer the best balance between process performance and operational considerations. At this ratio, desulfurization efficiency reached 99.07%, while V and Mo leaching efficiencies were 98.16% and 99.28%, respectively. Although a ratio of 1.3 marginally increased leaching efficiencies to 98.27% (V) and 99.60% (Mo), it was accompanied by a marked decline in desulfurization efficiency (97.85%). Furthermore, the slight gain in metal recovery would result in elevated sodium ion concentrations in the leachate, thereby increasing downstream wastewater treatment costs. Therefore, a molar ratio of 1.2 is identified as optimal, achieving high desulfurization and metal extraction efficiencies while maintaining process economy and minimizing environmental impact.

Optimization of Soda Roasting for Desulfurization and Metal Leaching by RSM

Following single-factor experiments that independently evaluated the effects of roasting temperature, duration, and Na2CO3 dosage, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed to achieve multifactor synergistic optimization of these parameters. This approach enabled the precise identification of optimal process conditions while accounting for potential interactions among the variables. For example, the extent to which elevated temperature enhances reaction kinetics may depend on the Na2CO3 dosage applied. A detailed description of the experimental design, model fitting procedures, and interaction analysis is provided in the Supporting Information.

A significant interactive effect between roasting temperature and Na2CO3 dosage was observed during desulfurization and the conversion of V and Mo, arising from competing reaction pathways. As an alkaline flux, Na2CO3 promotes the formation of soluble metal salts and improves metal recovery. However, SO2 released reacts with excess Na2CO3 to form stable Na2SO4, which retains sulfur in the calcine and lowers desulfurization efficiency.

Elevated temperatures promoted full oxidation of sulfides to SO2, suppressed sulfate formation, and enhanced sulfur removal. Meanwhile, increased thermal energy accelerated atomic diffusion and thermodynamic driving forces, facilitating conversion of target metals.

Excessive Na2CO3 addition inhibited desulfurization, whereas high temperatures mitigated competing sulfate formation. Desulfurization thus depended on the interplay of these parameters. Increasing temperature improved V extraction at insufficient reagent dosages, indicating that higher thermal energy could partially compensate for inadequate alkali by overcoming solid-phase mass transfer limitations. Roasting temperature and Na2CO3 dosage therefore synergistically governed desulfurization kinetics and metal conversion, collectively determining sulfur removal efficiency and metal recovery.

The RSM-based optimization yielded favorable recovery outcomes. RSM was employed to optimize roasting parameters. About 99.55% of S was removed, and 98.26% of V and 99.68% of Mo were leached out, with the leaching efficiency of Al being less than 0.5%, after roasting at 1148 °C for 63 min (practical rounded parameters: 1150 °C and 60 min) with Na2CO3 dosage molar ratio of 1.21.

Reaction Mechanism of One-Step Oxidation and Soda Roasting

Figure presents the TG-DSC curves of the mixture comprising SHC and optimally proportioned Na2CO3 from room temperature to 1200 °C. These curves clarify the temperature range, mass loss characteristics, and thermal effect variation of each reaction stage. The thermal behavior exhibits four distinct stages as a function of temperature.

TG-DSC curves of the mixed material of SHC and Na 2 CO 3 from room temperature to 1200 °C

In the temperature range from 30 to 232 °C, the TG value decreases slowly to 94.30%, while the DSC signal remains nearly thermally neutral. This stage corresponds to the desorption of surface-adsorbed water and trace light oil components from the raw material. This observation is consistent with the roasting of the raw material alone (Figure ), indicating that sodium carbonate does not interfere with the initial volatilization process.

Between 232 and 500 °C, the TG value drops rapidly to 74.92%. The DTG curve reaches its maximum mass loss rate at 446 °C, accompanied by a strong negative heat flow in the DSC curve. This stage corresponds to the organic components reaching their ignition threshold, leading to intense combustion of heavy fractions and coke deposits. The prominent DTG peak confirms that the combustion reaction is most vigorous in this region. Furthermore, the proximity of this peak temperature to that of the raw material combustion peak suggests that sodium carbonate does not alter the combustion activity of the organic matter. Concurrently, the TG mass loss rate is slightly higher than that of the raw material, indirectly indicating that sulfide oxidation initiates earlier, thereby paving the way for subsequent soda roasting reactions.

Between 500 to 1050 °C, the TG value gradually decreases from 74.92% to 59.36%. The DTG signal decays slowly but remains negative, indicating continuous mass loss. This suggests that sulfide oxidation and reactions with sodium salts proceed continuously. This stage represents the characteristic phase of the soda roasting reaction. It corresponds to the oxidation of MoS2, V2S3, and NiS to form MoO3, V2O5, and NiO, respectively, followed by their reaction with Na2CO3 to produce Na2MoO4 and NaVO3. The continuous mass loss arises from the evolution of dual gaseous products: SO2 released from sulfide oxidation and CO2 released from the soda roasting reaction, indicating that the soda roasting reaction approaches completion. Thermodynamic Gibbs free energy calculations confirmed that all oxidation and soda-roasting reactions proceeded spontaneously above 600 °C, consistent with the thermodynamic characteristics of the continuous reactions in this stage.

Between 1050 and 1200 °C, the TG value gradually decreases to 57.50%, and the DTG approaches zero. A weak negative heat flow appears in the DSC curve at 1200 °C. The limited mass loss indicates that the system enters a stable stage. Meanwhile, the weak endothermic peak corresponds to solid-state reactions, specifically the formation of NiAl2O4 from NiO and residual γ-Al2O3, as well as the further transformation of γ-Al2O3 into structurally dense α-Al2O3.

XPS characterization (Figure ) of the calcine under optimal process conditions further confirms the final phase composition. The characteristic peaks at 232.2 and 235.1 eV in the Mo 3d spectrum are assigned to Mo6+, verifying the complete conversion of molybdenum to Na2MoO4. The peaks at 516.9 and 523.8 eV in the V 2p spectrum correspond to V5+, indicating the directional formation of NaVO3 from vanadium. The characteristic peaks at 855.8 and 873.6 eV in the Ni 2p spectrum are attributed to Ni2+, with no characteristic peaks of NiO detected.

XPS spectra of surface element valences in sodium roasted calcine of SHC: (a) S; (b) Ni; (c) Mo; (d) V.

XRD analysis of the residue (Figure S4) confirmed that nickel was immobilized as stable NiAl2O4. The S 2p spectrum showed that sulfur existed mainly as SO4 2– with only trace S2– detected. The residual sulfur content in the calcine was 0.20 wt %, indicating that part of the sulfur-containing gaseous products reacted with sodium carbonate and was oxidized to sulfate during roasting. XPS depth profiling (see Figure S5 and Table S6) further reveals that sulfate and sulfide signals coexist with stable intensities from the original particle surface down to a depth of 100 nm, confirming that the residual sulfur is not surface-enriched but uniformly distributed within the product at an extremely low concentration. Given that the desulfurization rate has reached 99.55%, the total amount of residual sulfur is very low. During the leaching process, sulfate enters the solution in ionic form while sulfides remain in the leaching residue; consequently, neither enters the subsequent vanadium or molybdenum products and thus product purity is unaffected.

TG-DSC results validated the full process: low-temperature removal, medium-temperature combustion and oxidation, high-temperature soda roasting, and final stabilization. These results supported the spontaneous reaction tendency, temperature window, and phase transformation rules derived from thermodynamic calculations. Together, they verified a continuous reaction mechanism involving sulfide oxidation, sodium conversion, and high-temperature phase stabilization.

Figure summarizes the overall reaction mechanism of the one-step soda-roasting treatment. Under high-temperature and oxygen-rich conditions, MoS2 and V2S3 in the SHC underwent synergistic oxidation-soda roasting reactions with Na2CO3. Drawing inspiration from the concept of a stepwise removal mechanism, this system also follows a thermodynamically driven and well-defined sequential reaction order.

Mechanism of a one-step soda roasting process.

First, the reaction is initiated through an electron transfer process analogous to electrochemical activation: the sulfur sites of MoS2 and V2S3 preferentially donate electrons to O2 molecules, leading to their prior activation and oxidation. This generates high-melting-point MoO3 and V2O5, which potentially form a dense surface layer. At this stage, the introduction of Na2CO3 transforms the process into a highly efficient chemical paired reaction: the sulfide oxidation acts as the oxidative half, while the simultaneous neutralization and conversion of the acidic oxide layer by Na2CO3 serves as the coupling half. These two half-reactions occur synchronously and promote each other, driving the overall process toward completion from both chemical equilibrium and mass transfer perspectives. This coupling not only instantly converts the dense oxide layer into low-melting-point Na2MoO4 and NaVO3, but more importantly, it creates new reaction interfaces and channels that facilitate the continuous inward migration of active oxygen species (O*). Therefore, it can ensure the sustained and deep oxidation of the remaining sulfide components.

This process transforms the sulfides into water-soluble Na2MoO4 and NaVO3 while releasing a mixed flue gas composed primarily of SO2 and CO2. The role of the high-temperature condition (1100 °C) extends beyond merely reducing the activation energy. The high-temperature environment in this study is equally crucial. It greatly promotes the escape and mass transfer rate of the gaseous product (SO2) from the solid calcine matrix to the gas phase, thereby achieving an ultrahigh desulfurization efficiency of >99.5% and effectively inhibiting the refixation of sulfur in the form of sulfate in the roasted product. This allows for convenient downstream treatment and resource utilization of the sulfur in the tail gas.

Furthermore, in accordance with the aforementioned sequential oxidation order, nickel sulfide is oxidized to NiO only after the efficient conversion of the primary target elements Mo and V. The NiO subsequently reacts with γ-Al2O3 to form the thermally stable spinel phase NiAl2O4. This reaction achieved selective immobilization of nickel and minimized leaching during downstream processing. Additionally, residual γ-Al2O3 underwent a phase transformation to α-Al2O3, a chemically inert and structurally stable polymorph. This transformation suppressed aluminum dissolution during subsequent hydrometallurgical steps, thereby reducing impurity load and simplifying V and Mo separation. Ultimately, through such a sequential conversion strategy, we have achieved the efficient extraction of valuable metals and the targeted immobilization of impurity elements.

Conclusion

A one-step soda-roasting process was developed for recovery of Mo and V from SHC. Thermodynamic analysis confirmed that MoS2 and V2S3 readily oxidize to water-soluble Na2MoO4 and NaVO3 under soda-roasting conditions. Increasing temperature favors spontaneous desulfurization and prevents its fixation in the calcine. Nickel is immobilized as stable NiAl2O4, and alumina converts to α-Al2O3, thus inhibiting leaching of Ni and Al. Response surface methodology optimization yielded the following conditions: 1150 °C, 60 min, Na2CO3 molar ratio of 1.21. Under these conditions, sulfur removal reached 99.55%, with Mo and V leaching efficiencies of 99.68% and 98.26%, respectively, while Al leaching was suppressed below 0.5%. Compared with conventional roasting, the proposed process saves USD 0.439 million/a in operating costs and generates an additional USD 2.966 million/a from enhanced V/Mo recovery, leading to an incremental payback period of 0.12 years at 15,000 t/a. This study clarifies the soda-roasting mechanism of SHC and provides a technical basis for its one-step treatment.
