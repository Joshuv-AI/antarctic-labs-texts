Complete mitochondrial genome of an abyssal cusk-eel Barathrites iris (Ophidiiformes: Ophidiidae)
Authors: Tengda Luo, Yanhong Zhang, Boqiong Wu, Bo Lu, Qiang Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBarathrites iris Zugmayer, 1911 is an abyssal fish species inhabiting depths exceeding 3,000 m. We successfully sequenced and characterized the complete mitochondrial genome of B. iris, which represents a closed circular molecule of 16,646 bp with a GC content of 48.98%. Phylogenetic analysis of Ophidiiformes based on whole mitochondrial genome sequences demonstrated that B. iris forms a sister group with Penopus microphthalmus while supporting the polyphyly of Bythitidae as well as the paraphyletic status of Ophidioidei. As the first report of a complete mitochondrial genome for B. iris, this study provides valuable genomic resources for investigating deep-sea adaptation mechanisms in Ophidiiformes and contributes to our understanding of evolutionary patterns in abyssal teleost fishes.

Introduction

Barathrites iris Zugmayer, 1911 is a rare abyssal cusk-eel with a cosmopolitan distribution across the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian Oceans, inhabiting the abyssal zone at depths of 3465–5285 m. This small, slender fish reaches a maximum length of 62.5 cm, and was first described by Zugmayer in 1911 from the southwestern Azores in the Atlantic Ocean. As a carnivorous fish, this species plays an important role in the deep-sea ecosystem (Ohashi et al. 2012).

B. iris is taxonomically classified under the order Ophidiiformes and the cusk-eel family Ophidiidae. Ophidiidae make up the majority of fish fauna of lower abyssal and upper hadal zones together with Macrouridae and Liparidae (Jamieson et al. 2021), and include one of the deepest-known fishes (Abyssobrotula galatheae). The taxonomy of Ophidiiformes has undergone several revisions in the past decade. Nelson (2016) divided the order into two suborders: the oviparous Ophidioidei (Carapidae and Ophidiidae), and the viviparous Bythitoidei (Bythitidae, Aphyonidae, and Parabrotulidae). Subsequently, Møller et al. (2016) merged Aphyonidae into Bythitidae and Carapidae into Ophidiidae based on molecular, morphological, and fossil evidence, and also established a new family, Dinematichthyidae, separated from Bythitidae. More recently, based on maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference analyses of both purine-pyrimidine (RY) and non-RY coding matrices as well as multispecies coalescent analysis of RY-coded data, Wong and Chen (2024) reinstated Carapidae, regarded Parabrotulidae as a senior synonym of Bythitidae, and established two new families: Acanthonidae and Brotulidae. In contrast, Thomas and Christine (2024) placed all ophidiid species within a more inclusive Ophidiidae. Collectively, these studies support the monophyly of both Ophidioidei and Bythitoidei.

Although complete mitochondrial genomes have been sequenced for various ophidiiform species from freshwater, coastal, and deep-sea environments, no such data have been available for B. iris until now. This study presents the first complete mitochondrial genome of B. iris, which represents the deepest-dwelling ophidiiform species with complete mitogenome data. These findings provide valuable insights into the phylogeny of Ophidiiformes, the molecular adaptations of cusk-eels and other deep-sea fishes to extreme environments, comparative genomics of abyssal fishes, and evolutionary patterns along depth gradients in marine teleosts.

Materials and methods

A specimen of B. iris (Figure 1) was collected from a polymetallic nodule mining area in the eastern Pacific Ocean (156°36.5575′ E, 9°20.3681′ N) at a depth of 5,258 m. The sample was deposited in the Repository of the Second Institute of Oceanography, Ministry of Natural Resources, China (contact: Bo Lu, lub@sio.org.cn; voucher no. RSIOOST79003). For genome sequencing, muscle tissue was dissected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. The mitochondrial genome was de novo assembled from next-generation sequencing data using NOVOPlasty, followed by annotation with MITOS (http://mitos.bioinf.uni-leipzig.de/). A circular map of the mitochondrial genome was generated using Proksee (https://proksee.ca). The sequencing depth and coverage of the B. iris mitochondrial genome are shown in the (supplementary materials Figure S1).

Barathrites iris. The photo was taken by Bo Lu. This species is a typical deep-sea cuskeel, characterized by a dark body, small eyes, a protruding snout, and continuous median fins.

For phylogenetic analysis, mitochondrial genome sequences of representative species from all available genera of Ophidiiformes in GenBank were retrieved. For genera with multiple species, one representative species was selected. The Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua) was used as the outgroup due to its close phylogenetic relationship with Ophidiiformes, thereby improving the robustness of the tree topology.

Totally 27 sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.526 and trimmed with trimAl v1.4. The best-fit substitution model (GTR+F + R5) was determined with IQ-TREE multicore v2.1.4-beta. Phylogenetic reconstruction was then performed in RAxML-HPC v8.2.12 under the GTRGAMMAX model with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Results

The assembly results showed that the mitochondrial genome of B. iris is a closed circular molecule comprising 16,646 bp and contains 22 tRNA genes, 2 rRNA genes, and 13 protein-coding sequences (CDSs) (atp6, atp8, cox1–3, cob, nad1–6 and nad4l) (Figure 2). A non-coding region was identified between position 1237 and 2171. Among 22 tRNA genes, trnT (TGT), trnF (GAA), trnV (TAC), trnL2 (TAA), trnI (GAT), trnM (CAT), trnW (TCA), trnD (GTC), trnK (TTT), trnG (TCC), trnR (TCG), trnH (GTG), trnL1 (TAG), and trnS1 (GCT) locate on the heavy strand, while trnE (TTC), trnP (TGG), trnQ (TTG), trnA (TGC), trnN (GTT), trnC (GCA), trnV (GTA), and trnS2 (TGA) are found on the light strand. With the exception of the nad6 gene, all protein-coding genes were located on the heavy strand. The G + C content of the mitochondrial genome was 48.98%. The predicted secondary structures of the tRNAs of B. iris are shown in the (supplementary materials Figure S2). Twenty-one tRNAs exhibited a typical cloverleaf structure, while trnS1 (GCT) had a truncated DHU arm, forming a loop without a stem.

The phylogenetic tree of Ophidiiformes was reconstructed based on complete mitochondrial genome sequences using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method (Figure 3). The phylogenetic analysis revealed that B. iris formed a sister group with Penopus microphthalmus, another abyssal cusk-eel distributed at depths of 960–3535 m. The analysis also showed that Thalassobathia pelagica, traditionally classified as a bythitid, was placed within the Ophidiidae lineage; and that two ophidioid species, Xyelacyba myersi and Brotula multibarbata, were placed as the sister group to Bythitoidei.

Discussion and conclusions

The mitochondrial genome of B. iris shows a gene arrangement similar to that of other fishes, including the zebrafish and medaka (Miya et al. 2003; Sabharwal et al. 2022). Its GC content (48.98%) is consistent with results from other fishes (Huang et al. 2025; Liao et al. 2024). While a reduced tRNA-Ser(GCT) is common in fishes (Satoh et al. 2016), B. iris retains an incomplete DHU arm that forms an atypical cloverleaf structure.

This study provided the first mitochondrial genomic phylogenetic analysis of Ophidiiformes. Although the analysis encompassed only 26 of the more than 560 recognized ophidiiform species, it covered most major lineages of the order. These findings nevertheless offer significant phylogenetic insights into this diverse group. The analysis showed that B. iris formed a monophyletic group with several other deep-sea cusk-eels, supported the paraphyly of Ophidioidei, confirmed the validity of Brotulidae and Acanthonidae, and implied that Carapidae should be synonymous with Ophidiidae. Additionally, we found that the previously assumed bythitid Thalassobathia pelagica actually belonged to Ophidiidae, which is consistent with previous morphological hypotheses (Nielsen et al. 2010).

The analysis also supported a shallow-water origin for Ophidiiformes, consistent with the hypothesis proposed by Wong and Chen (2024), and implied parallel invasions into the deep sea by Ophidioidei, Bythitoidei and Acanthonidae. Similar evolutionary patterns have been observed in several other deep-sea clades, including one of the most dominant deep-sea fish lineages, the lanternfish family Myctophidae, which arose in Eocene shallow seas but did not colonize the deep sea until the Oligocene (Schwarzhans and Carnevale 2021).

This study presents the first mitochondrial genome of B. iris, extending the known depth distribution of Ophidiiformes fishes with available mitochondrial genomes. Ophidiiformes constitute a long-lived lineage dating back to the mid-Cretaceous (120–90 Ma) and may have thrived in deep-water environments since the Late Cretaceous (Møller et al. 2016; Wong and Chen 2024). Occupying a wide variety of habitats ranging from freshwater to the abyssal zone, Ophidiiformes can be regarded as a key for exploring the molecular mechanism of the deep-sea adaption of fish as well as their transition between shallow water and the deep, cold abyssal zone with eternal darkness, and the B. iris mitochondrial genome we provide offers valuable data for resolving these issues.
