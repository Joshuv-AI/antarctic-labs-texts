Optic disc melanocytoma with disc oedema and juxtapapillary haemorrhage: a case report

Background

Optic disc melanocytoma (ODM) is a deeply pigmented benign tumour arising from melanocytes within the optic nerve head. These tumours were initially regarded as malignant melanoma until Zimmerman and Garron clarified its benign nature in 1962 [1]. Since then, unnecessary enucleation of these tumours has become rare. Optic disc melanocytomas are most often discovered incidentally, and usually exhibit minimal growth throughout life [2]. Nevertheless, visual disturbances such as blurred vision, visual field defects, and relative afferent pupillary defects (RAPD) have been reported in a subset of patients [2].

Various complications associated with optic disc melanocytoma have been described, including optic disc oedema, peripapillary or vitreous haemorrhage, macular exudation, retinal vascular occlusion, and glaucomatous visual field loss [2–6]. These manifestations may confound the clinical picture, leading to diagnostic uncertainty, particularly when there is a concern about malignant transformation or coexisting choroidal melanoma [4, 6]. In particular, the combination of pronounced optic disc oedema and juxtapapillary haemorrhage surrounding a pigmented disc lesion can simulate a more aggressive disease, and invasive diagnostic or therapeutic procedures may be considered unnecessarily.

Herein, we report a case of optic disc melanocytoma of the right eye complicated by optic disc oedema and juxtapapillary haemorrhage in a patient who was initially advised to undergo a tumour biopsy. Herein, we describe the clinical course, angiographic characteristics, and favourable outcomes with conservative management, and discuss the differential diagnosis and practical considerations in avoiding unnecessary interventions. This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines for case reports.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old Chinese woman was referred to the Department of Ophthalmology of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University for a second opinion regarding a pigmented lesion on her right optic disc. At the referral hospital, a tumour biopsy was suggested due to concerns regarding a malignant optic disc lesion. The patient reported a 2-week history of blurred vision and floaters in the right eye. Her medical history was unremarkable, except for a 20-year history of mild myopia. She had no history of ocular trauma, intraocular inflammation, or systemic malignancy.

At presentation, the patient’s best-corrected visual acuity was 20/25 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye. A relatively afferent pupillary defect was observed in the right eye. The intraocular pressure was within normal limits in both eyes. Examination of the anterior segment was unremarkable bilaterally. Dilated fundus examination of the right eye revealed a dark brown, densely pigmented lesion involving the optic disc (Fig. 1A). The mass occupied most of the disc surface, extended over the superior disc margin, and slightly involved the adjacent peripapillary retina. The lesion had a granular surface and feathery margins along the nerve fibre layer, which are typical features of optic disc melanocytoma. Marked optic disc oedema and wedge-shaped juxtapapillary haemorrhage were observed in the inferior peripapillary region. Retinal vessels traversing the lesion appeared mildly tortuous. The macula was spared. The left fundus was normal.

Fundoscopy and fluorescein angiography of the lesion. (A) Fundus photograph demonstrating a dark-brown lesion located in the optic disc. Optic disc oedema and juxtapapillary haemorrhage are observed in the inferior peripapillary region. (B) Fluorescein angiography showing peripapillary telangiectasia and mild tortuosity of the medium-sized retinal vessels at 21 s. (C) After 6 months of observation, optic disc haemorrhage and oedema had resolved entirely in the right eye. (D) Fluorescein angiography showing improvement in the peripapillary telangiectasia and vessel tortuosity

Fluorescein angiography (FA) of the right eye revealed hypofluorescence of the pigmented mass throughout the early phases, consistent with blockage by dense melanin (Fig. 1B). In the late phase, mild staining was observed on the surface of the lesion, without any significant leakage. Peripapillary telangiectasia and mild tortuosity of the medium-sized retinal vessels were also observed, corresponding to clinically visible vascular changes. No choroidal neovascularization or extensive subretinal fluid were observed. Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) was not performed because the patient declined further imaging after completion of the initial diagnostic workup.

Based on the characteristic clinical and angiographic features, a diagnosis of right-eye optic disc melanocytoma complicated by optic disc oedema and juxtapapillary haemorrhage was made. There were no signs suggestive of overt malignant transformation, such as rapid enlargement of the mass, extensive subretinal exudation, or marked visual loss. In light of the benign appearance and potential risks of invasive biopsy near the optic nerve head, we recommended close observation with short-term follow-up, rather than immediate surgical intervention. The patient agreed to undergo this approach after detailed counselling.

Follow-up examinations were performed at one, three, and six months. During this period, the patient remained asymptomatic except for transient subjective floaters, which gradually diminished. At the 6-month visit, the best-corrected visual acuity improved to 20/20 in both eyes. Fundus examination revealed complete resolution of the optic disc oedema and juxtapapillary haemorrhage, with a stable-appearing, dark-brown optic disc melanocytoma and mild vitreous seeding overlying the lesion (Fig. 1C). Repeat FA of the right eye demonstrated attenuation of peripapillary telangiectasia and decreased vessel tortuosity compared with baseline (Fig. 1D). No interval growth of the mass or new complications were observed.

Discussion

Optic disc melanocytoma is generally considered a benign lesion, with a favourable long-term prognosis. In large clinical series, most tumours remain stable or show only minimal enlargement throughout years of follow-up [2, 3]. However, visual morbidity due to optic nerve dysfunction, vascular compromise, or secondary glaucoma may occur in some cases [5, 6]. The present case underscores that optic disc melanocytoma can be associated with optic disc oedema and juxtapapillary haemorrhage, which may raise concerns regarding malignant disease.

Peripapillary haemorrhage associated with optic disc melanocytoma has been attributed to the mechanical compression of small vessels at the disc margin or secondary vascular changes such as telangiectasia and venous congestion [3, 5, 7]. Optic disc oedema may similarly reflect local axoplasmic stasis from tumour-related compression of the optic nerve fibres. In our patient, fundus and angiographic examinations revealed peripapillary telangiectasia and mild tortuosity of the medium-sized retinal vessels, supporting the notion of local hemodynamic disturbance. This is in line with the circulation impairment reported by Tsai et al., who found a lower mean blur rate in the disc and macula of ODM eyes than in the fellow eye [7]. The spontaneous resolution of both disc oedema and haemorrhage over several months without any active intervention further supports a benign, self-limited mechanism, rather than an infiltrative malignant process.

In this context, recent advances in multimodal imaging provide further insight into the vascular and hemodynamic mechanisms underlying these transient fundus changes. For example, it has been suggested that optic disc melanocytoma may be associated with local microvascular and hemodynamic disturbances [7], potentially related to mechanical compression at the disc margin and secondary venous congestion. Compression of the small peripapillary vessels and impairment of axoplasmic flow may plausibly account for the development of optic disc oedema, while fragile telangiectatic capillaries and congested venules may predispose patients to juxtapapillary haemorrhage. Recent multimodal imaging studies support this concept, as OCTA has demonstrated abnormal and relatively disorganized peripapillary and optic nerve head microvasculature, together with reduced vessel density and localized nonperfusion areas, in eyes with optic disc melanocytoma [8]. These findings are consistent with localized perfusion deficits and vascular compromise [7, 9]. Moreover, a recent OCT/OCTA case series reported reduced optic nerve head vessel density, predominantly at the radial peripapillary capillary layer, along with patchy nonperfusion areas [9]. These changes tended to occur in eyes with larger tumours and worse visual function, suggesting an association between perfusion abnormalities and optic nerve dysfunction in selected cases [9].

From a prognostic perspective, current evidence has failed to establish telangiectasia or vessel tortuosity as reliable predictors of recurrent haemorrhage or tumour progression. Nevertheless, the reported association between OCTA-detected perfusion deficits and functional impairment supports the practical value of close follow-up and repeated multimodal imaging (such as fundus photography, OCT, and OCTA when available) to document lesion stability and monitor for recurrent vascular events or evolving optic nerve changes [7, 9].

Importantly, the spontaneous resolution of optic disc oedema and juxtapapillary haemorrhage observed in the present case is consistent with the transient nature of these secondary vascular and hemodynamic disturbances, rather than any change in the melanocytoma itself. Optic disc melanocytoma is generally a stable, benign lesion, and spontaneous regression of the tumour is not expected. In contrast, peripapillary venous congestion, axoplasmic flow stasis, and microvascular dysregulation may fluctuate over time; as local compression or vascular disturbance improves, the restoration of axoplasmic transport and capillary integrity may lead to gradual resolution of disc oedema and haemorrhage. Recent OCTA-based studies and multimodal circulation assessments support a localized perfusion and hemodynamic component in optic disc melanocytoma, including altered optic nerve head and peripapillary microvasculature with reduced perfusion signals, consistent with a reversible ischemic–congestive mechanism in selected cases [7, 9, 10]. Taken together, these observations further support the utility of conservative management with close follow-up and multimodal imaging in patients with typical melanocytoma features and no evidence of malignant transformation [9].

The differential diagnosis of pigmented optic disc lesions accompanied by disc oedema and haemorrhage is broad. Benign entities include choroidal nevi extending to the disc margin, hyperplasia or hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium, combined hamartoma of the retina and retinal pigment epithelium, and cavernous haemangioma [2]. Malignant lesions, such as juxtapapillary choroidal melanoma and primary malignant melanoma of the optic nerve, although rare, should also be considered [4, 5]. Choroidal melanoma is generally less deeply pigmented than melanocytoma; commonly has a more elevated, dome-shaped configuration; and typically does not demonstrate the characteristic feathery extension along the nerve fibre layer. Moreover, uveal melanoma is relatively uncommon in Asian populations, whereas optic disc melanocytoma occurs at a similar frequency across races [2, 5]. Primary malignant melanoma of the optic nerve is extremely rare and difficult to differentiate clinically; however, it is more likely to present with rapidly progressive visual loss, marked disc elevation, and extensive subretinal exudation [4].

The malignant transformation of optic disc melanocytoma is considered rare, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1–2% in a previous published series [3]. The features that should prompt heightened suspicion include rapid growth of the lesion, extensive subretinal fluid, exudative retinal detachment, severe visual deterioration, and radiographic evidence of extrascleral extension. In such cases, further diagnostic evaluation, including fine-needle aspiration biopsy, or even enucleation in highly suspicious cases, may be warranted [11]. In contrast, our patient exhibited a stable, densely pigmented lesion with a typical melanocytoma morphology, mild and reversible visual dysfunction, and no evidence of progressive growth or aggressive behaviour during follow-up. These features support conservative observation rather than immediate biopsy.

Our case highlights an important practical issue: the risk of overtreatment when an optic disc melanocytoma presents with atypically associated findings. The patient was initially advised to undergo a tumour biopsy at another institution because of concerns about a malignant optic disc lesion. Given the proximity of the lesion to the optic nerve head, invasive biopsy carries not only the risk of sight-threatening complications, but also the potential for misinterpretation of limited tissue samples. Careful clinical evaluation, multimodal imaging, and awareness of the spectrum of benign complications of optic disc melanocytoma are therefore crucial in guiding appropriate management [12, 13]. Our case further supports recent multimodal imaging reports showing that typical ODM features can be confirmed without an invasive biopsy. In many cases, as illustrated here, short-interval follow-up with repeat imaging can provide sufficient reassurance of lesion stability and allow time for the associated oedema and haemorrhage to resolve. Tsai et al. used OCTA combined with laser speckle flowgraphy to demonstrate reduced blood flow velocity in the optic disc and macula of an eye with melanocytoma, despite preserved visual function, suggesting that ODM may cause early hemodynamic impairment [7]. Similar OCTA studies have reported decreased peripapillary and macular vessel densities in complicated cases [8, 13].

Limitations

As this is a single case report, the observations and conclusions drawn from this case may not be generalizable to all eyes with optic disc melanocytoma. Another limitation is the lack of OCTA imaging, which could have provided more detailed information on tumour configuration and peripapillary perfusion and allowed a more direct comparison with recent multimodal imaging studies. Nevertheless, the combination of typical funduscopic appearance, fluorescein angiographic features, and short-interval clinical follow-up was sufficient to establish the diagnosis and document a benign clinical course in this case.

Conclusions

Optic disc melanocytoma is a benign pigmented tumour that occasionally presents with optic disc oedema and juxtapapillary haemorrhage. These uncommon features can mimic malignant transformation or other aggressive optic disc lesions, and may lead to recommendations for invasive diagnostic procedures. Our case demonstrates that in the presence of typical clinical and angiographic characteristics of melanocytoma, and the absence of clear signs of malignancy, close observation with serial imaging can be a safe and effective strategy. Awareness of this presentation may help clinicians to avoid any unnecessary biopsies or surgeries in similar cases. This case highlights the importance of recognizing the benign causes of optic disc oedema and juxtapapillary haemorrhage to prevent unnecessary invasive procedures such as optic disc biopsy.
