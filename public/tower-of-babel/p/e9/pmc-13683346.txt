Flavonoid-rich foods, genetic risk, and female breast cancer risk: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Rui Yang, Yi-Lin Xu, Ying Wang, Wei-Yi Xing, Wen-Rui Zheng, Jia-Xin Liu, Bang-Quan Liu, Jia-Cheng Liu, Chao Ni, Ting-Ting Gong, Shan-Yan Gao, Qi-Jun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Flavodiet Score (FDS) represents flavonoid-rich food intake, but its association with breast cancer risk and whether genetic risk modifies this association remain unclear. We analyzed data from 93,271 females in the UK Biobank with a median follow-up of 11.8 years. Associations were examined using delayed-entry Cox proportional hazards regression models. The polygenic risk score (PRS) for breast cancer was constructed based on 168 single nucleotide polymorphisms. Results showed that females in the highest FDS quintile had a 15% reduced risk compared with those in the lowest quintile. Higher intakes of specific flavonoid subclasses (such as flavanones and proanthocyanidins) and of flavonoid-rich foods (including oranges and apples) were also associated with reduced breast cancer risk. Furthermore, the association between FDS and breast cancer risk did not vary significantly according to menopausal status. Notably, among females with high PRS, those in the highest quintile of FDS showed a significantly reduced breast cancer risk. In conclusion, adherence to a flavonoid-rich diet may reduce breast cancer risk, providing valuable guidance for future intervention studies and public health strategies.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most prevalent malignancies and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths1,2. The vast majority of breast cancer cases occur among females, with incidence rates approximately 100 times higher than among males3. According to the GLOBOCAN 2020 report, 2,261,419 new breast cancer cases (47.9%) and 684,996 breast cancer deaths (13.6%) among females occurred globally in 2020, surpassing lung cancer to become the most commonly diagnosed type of cancer4. Although breast cancer was once regarded as a disease predominantly affecting developed countries, both incidence and mortality rates have risen significantly in less developed regions of the globe4,5. Given its status as a major global health challenge, identifying modifiable risk factors is essential for the prevention of breast cancer6,7.

Diet, as one of the most important modifiable risk factors, plays a crucial role in breast cancer prevention8,9. Emerging evidence suggests that plant-based diets are associated with lower cancer incidence10–13. These findings highlight the importance of the quality of plant foods for cancer prevention. Flavonoids are polyphenolic compounds with antioxidant effects and various biological activities, commonly found in many plant-based foods and beverages14–16. Previous epidemiological studies have explored the association between flavonoids, flavonoid-rich foods, and breast cancer risk17–19. For example, certain flavonoid compounds, including flavonols, flavones, flavan-3-ols, and lignans, have been inversely associated with breast cancer among females20. Flavonoid-rich foods have been shown to promote better health, providing valuable dietary guidance for intervention studies and public health initiatives21,22. However, given that the dietary acquisition of flavonoids is relatively consistent across regions23,24, it is crucial to develop a composite score of flavonoid-rich foods in disease prevention. Researchers developed the Flavodiet Score (FDS), which consists of tea (black and green), red wine, apples, berries, grapes, oranges, grapefruits, sweet peppers, onions, and dark chocolate25. Studies have shown that higher FDS is associated with several health outcomes, such as a reduced dementia risk, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease risks, and type 2 diabetes, as well as improved lung function25–28. These findings highlight the importance of utilizing FDS to explore the associations between flavonoid-rich foods and breast cancer risk among females.

In addition, it is well-established that genetic factors play a significant role in cancer development29. Previous research has demonstrated that genetic predisposition may interact with dietary patterns in the development of breast cancer30. Polygenic risk scores (PRSs) have emerged as a promising tool for predicting cancer risk based on genome-wide association studies (GWAS)31,32. However, the interaction between FDS and the genetic risk of breast cancer remains unexplored.

In this study, we utilized data from the UK Biobank to explore the associations of FDS, flavonoid subclass intakes, and intakes of individual flavonoid-rich foods with breast cancer risk among females, and further assessed whether genetic risk can modify these associations.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

This study included 93,271 female participants (median age: 56.00 years). During a median follow-up of 11.80 years (interquartile range: 11.39–12.60 years), 3110 incident cases of breast cancer were recorded. Baseline characteristics according to the quintiles of FDS are presented in Table 1. Compared with those in the lowest FDS (quintile 1), participants in the highest FDS (quintile 5) were more likely to be older, had lower BMI, higher educational attainment, higher income, to be more physically active, lower rates of current smoking, and lower index of multiple deprivation. They also had lower alcohol intake not from red wine, higher hPDI index, and a higher energy intake. Baseline characteristics stratified by breast cancer incidence are presented in Supplementary Table 4. Participants reported a median 4.00 (interquartile range: 2.25–5.12) servings per day of flavonoid-rich foods. Association of FDS, flavonoid subclass intakes, and flavonoid-rich foods with breast cancer risk

Among females, a higher FDS was associated with a lower risk of breast cancer. Specifically, participants in the highest quintile (Q5) had a 15% lower risk compared to those in the lowest quintile (Q1) (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.85 [0.75, 0.95]). Moreover, a significant linear trend was observed across increasing quintiles of FDS (P for trend = 0.031) (Table 2). The crude cumulative incidence of breast cancer was highest in participants with the lowest FDS, followed by those in the highest FDS (Fig. 1). When examining associations with the flavonoid subclasses, the highest quintile of flavanones intake was associated with a lower breast cancer risk compared with the lowest quintile (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.87 [0.78, 0.98]), although the test for linear trend was not statistically significant (P for trend = 0.109). In contrast, proanthocyanidins intake showed both a significant pairwise association (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.81 [0.72, 0.92]) and a significant linear trend (P for trend = 0.002) (Table 2). The RCS curves showed linear dose-response associations of proanthocyanidins with the risk of breast cancer (P for overall < 0.05 and P for non-linear >0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 2). In Model 2, no statistically significant associations were identified between the intake of total flavonoids or any other flavonoid subclasses and risk of breast cancer. Associations for flavonoid-rich foods are presented in Supplementary Table 6. High apple intake was associated with a reduced risk of breast cancer compared to low intake (HR High vs Low [95% CI]: 0.90 [0.82, 0.99], P for trend = 0.021). Additionally, oranges intake showed a linear inverse association with the risk of breast cancer (P for overall < 0.05 and P for non-linear > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 3), with a significantly lower risk observed in participants with high orange intake (HR High vs Low [95% CI]: 0.83 [0.75, 0.92], P for trend < 0.001).Fig. 1Crude cumulative incidence of breast cancer by flavodiet score.Cumulative incidence was estimated as 1 minus the Kaplan-Meier survival estimate. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

In subgroup analyses (Supplementary Tables 10–14), participants in the highest quintile of the FDS had a lower risk of breast cancer in several strata, including females with younger participants (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.80, [0.68, 0.93]), BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.83, [0.71, 0.96]), never smokers, (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.85, [0.73, 0.98]) and those with higher alcohol intake not from red wine (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.85, [0.73, 0.98]). Notably, when examining specific flavonoid subclasses, the highest quintile of proanthocyanidins intake was consistently associated with a lower risk of breast cancer across subgroups defined by age, alcohol intake not from red wine, and BMI. In additional stratified analyses, this inverse association remained statistically significant among never smokers (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.84, [0.71, 0.98]), previous smokers (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.77, [0.62, 0.95]), and postmenopausal females (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.82, [0.70, 0.96]). Similarly, higher flavanones intake was associated with reduced risk among never smokers (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.78, [0.67, 0.91]), while higher flavonols intake was inversely associated with risk among current smokers (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.65, [0.43, 0.98]). In sensitivity analyses, the associations remained robust (Supplementary Tables 15–17). After excluding participants diagnosed with breast cancer within the first two years of follow-up to address potential reverse causality, the inverse association between the highest FDS quintile and breast cancer risk remained consistent (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.84 [0.74, 0.95]) (Supplementary Table 15). Similarly, the highest quintile of the red wine-excluded FDS continued to show an inverse association with breast cancer risk (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.83 [0.74, 0.94]) (Supplementary Table 16). Results were not altered in analyses using MICE to address missing covariate data (HRQ5vsQ1 [95% CI]: 0.85 [0.76, 0.95]) (Supplementary Table 17). Furthermore, the associations for flavonoid subclasses also remained materially unchanged in these sensitivity analyses.

Discussion

Among 93,271 females in the UK Biobank, we found that a higher FDS was significantly related to a reduced risk of breast cancer. This association was also consistent across individuals with high genetic susceptibilities to breast cancer. Additionally, higher intake of oranges, flavanones, and proanthocyanidins was inversely associated with risk of breast cancer. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating flavonoid-rich foods into dietary interventions as a public health strategy for breast cancer prevention.

Although females in the highest quintile showed a statistically significant reduction in risk in some analyses, the corresponding linear trend tests were not always statistically significant. This pattern likely reflects the absence of a strictly monotonic dose-response relationship across quintiles, with the inverse association primarily driven by the highest exposure category while intermediate categories showed hazard ratios closer to 1.0. Because trend tests assume a linear relationship across ordered categories, a threshold-like pattern such as this may attenuate the statistical significance of the overall trend.

Given the limited evidence regarding the association between FDS and cancer risk, we compared our findings with previous studies examining the relationship between flavonoid subclasses, specific flavonoid-rich foods and risk of breast cancer. However, although some studies have evaluated the association between total flavonoid intake and breast cancer risk alongside specific subclasses, the definition and composition of total flavonoids varied considerably across studies33,34. Therefore, direct comparisons based on total flavonoid intake are not feasible, and we focused our comparisons on specific flavonoid subclasses and food sources. Notably, a recent study using the same prospective cohort indicated that flavonol, flavone, and anthocyanidin were not significantly associated with breast cancer risk, which is consistent with our results35. Additionally, a case-control study in Nassau and Suffolk counties, Long Island, NewYork, found that no association of total flavonoid intake, flavonols, flavones, flavanones, flavan-3-ols, and anthocyanidins with risk of breast cancer among premenopausal females, which also consistent with our analysis stratified by menopausal status20. In contrast, the results for postmenopausal females are inconsistent with ours, possibly due to use of different methods for estimating flavonoid subclasses, their study was based on a database developed for the Long Island Breast Cancer Study, whereas we matched the USDA database to the UK Biobank database. Findings from a hospital-based study conducted in four major hospitals in the Greater Athens area indicated that flavone intake was inversely associated with the risk of breast cancer19. This discrepancy may be due to variations in dietary patterns across countries, while the Greek diet is characterized by a high consumption of wild vegetables rich in flavones36. However, given the differences in the flavonoid subclasses included across studies, our findings should be interpreted with caution, and further studies are warranted to explore these associations more comprehensively.

Our results are generally consistent with findings reported in existing literature. Studies found no association between green or black tea consumption and breast cancer risk37,38. A study indicated citrus (including oranges) was associated with a 37% reduction in breast cancer risk. In comparison, our results showed that oranges can reduce 17% breast cancer risk, which is consistent with previous study39. Similar to the finding from the Nurses Health Study II about the association between flavonoid-rich food and risk of breast cancer, we also reported no significant association of tea and onions with breast cancer risk18. Additionally, a case-control study among Greenlandic Inuit females found that combined intake of fruits and vegetables was significantly associated with a reduced breast cancer risk, consistent with findings from a meta-analysis40,41. However, among these studies, specific foods were treated as a whole, whereas we analyzed specific flavonoid-rich foods separately, making direct comparison with our findings difficult. This difference in analytical approach may partially account for the observed inconsistencies in results. In sensitivity analyses, we excluded red wine from the FDS and separately removed it as a contributing food source in the calculation of flavonoid subclasses to evaluate the potential influence of alcohol-specific reporting bias. Reassuringly, excluding red wine did not materially alter the association between the FDS and breast cancer risk, indicating that the observed inverse relationship was not driven by red wine consumption. Similarly, associations between several flavonoid subclasses (including proanthocyanidins and flavanones) and breast cancer risk remained largely unchanged after excluding red wine from their contributing food sources. These consistent findings across multiple approaches strengthen the robustness of our results and highlight the potential importance of flavonoid-rich foods within an overall healthy dietary pattern, independent of alcohol consumption.

Numerous lines of evidence indicate that gene-environment interactions contribute substantially to the etiology of human cancers42–44. In our study, PRS was strongly associated with breast cancer risk, consistent with prior research demonstrating that PRS captures a substantial proportion of inherited susceptibility to breast cancer and supports the robustness of genetic risk stratification45,46. Moreover, previous studies suggest that the magnitude of genetic risk may be modified by lifestyle factors47–49. In our stratified analyses, the protective association of the FDS was most pronounced and statistically significant among females with high genetic risk. These findings do not support the hypothesis that high genetic predisposition masks the influence of modifiable factors. Instead, they suggest that individuals at elevated baseline risk may derive greater absolute benefit from favorable dietary exposures. This notion is supported by prior research, including a UK Biobank study found that lifestyle interventions had the greatest impact on premenopausal females at high genetic risk46, and a similar pattern was observed for flavonoid-rich diets and dementia risk27. These findings underscore the potential of flavonoid-rich dietary patterns to partially mitigate genetic predisposition to breast cancer and highlight the importance of integrating both genetic and dietary factors in risk reduction strategies.

FDS as an indicator of flavonoid-rich food intake, may be associated with breast cancer risk through multiple biological mechanisms. For example, flavanones which are abundant in citrus fruits, may confer protective effects by mitigating oxidative stress and modulating inflammatory responses50,51. Moreover, flavonoids have been shown to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, and to inhibit key signaling pathways such as nuclear factor-κB, cyclooxygenase-2, and toll-like receptors, which are involved in tumor development52–54. Additionally, some flavonoids interact with estrogen receptor-α and may increase levels of sex hormone-binding globulin, thereby limiting the binding of circulating estrogens to their receptors55–57. In conclusion, these mechanisms may contribute to the observed inverse association between FDS and breast cancer risk.

This study has several strengths. Firstly, it is a large prospective cohort study, which has a large sample size and long follow-up period. Secondly, we explore associations with breast cancer using a new index developed to represent the consumption of flavonoid-rich foods, which is easier to measure and may inform the development of dietary guidelines for breast cancer prevention. Thirdly, this study is the first to explore the association between FDS and the risk of breast cancer in the UK Biobank, and created PRS for breast cancer in order to explore substantial interaction between FDS and genetic risk. Finally, we have attempted to improve the accuracy of dietary data by including only participants with at least one 24-h dietary assessments, supported by the reproducibility of the flavodiet score over time26.

The study also has several limitations. Firstly, this study, an observational in nature, cannot establish causation between FDS and risk of breast cancer. However, in order to reduce reverse causality in part, we excluded the participants who were followed up for more than 2 years to conduct a sensitivity analyse. Secondly, the Oxford WebQ is an online 24-hour dietary questionnaire that is appropriate for repeated administration in large-scale prospective studies58, the over- or under- reporting and recall bias cannot be ruled out although the Oxford WebQ dietary questionnaire exhibits reasonable validity59. Thirdly, we matched the Oxford WebQ food item with the USDA flavonoid code, but the flavonoid subclass intake may still be misreported due to the inability to directly acquire flavonoid subclass intake data in the UK Biobank. Further, we adjusted the major confounders, but there is still a possibility of additional unknown or unmeasured factors existing. Fourth, the lack of tumor hormone receptor data precluded analyses stratified by estrogen and progesterone receptor subtypes, limiting our ability to assess potential heterogeneity across breast cancer molecular subtypes. Fifth, the use of mean substitution for missing continuous variables may have introduced some bias or underestimated uncertainty. However, to address this concern, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation, and the results were consistent with the primary findings. Finally, according to the participants from the UK Biobank, generally have a healthier lifestyle, healthier dietary patterns, lower rates of obesity and smoking, experience less socioeconomic deprivation than the general population, and consume higher amounts of tea, and only individuals of white European ancestry were included in the final participants of study. Therefore, we may not generalize the results to other population.

In conclusion, this study revealed a higher FDS was associated with a lower risk of breast cancer among females. The findings further suggest that adherence to a flavonoid-rich dietary pattern may be particularly relevant for those with elevated genetic risk. Together, these results underscore the potential role of flavonoid-rich diets in breast cancer prevention strategies. Future research should seek to replicate these findings in diverse populations, elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms, and evaluate the impact of flavonoid-rich dietary interventions in prospective and experimental studies.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large-scale population-based prospective cohort study60. From 2006 to 2010, over 500,000 participants aged between 38 and 73 were recruited from 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales61. Participants completed a touchscreen questionnaire and a comprehensive baseline assessment, which included socio-demographic data, health status, and dietary intake (assessed through a food frequency questionnaire). Additional details about the research protocol have been previously published61. The study received ethical approval from the National Health Service Northwest Multicentre Research Ethics Committee (Reference 11/NW/0382) and all participants provided written informed consent at the time of recruitment. A more detailed description of the research protocol is available at https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk.

In the present study (project number: 211772), 502,132 participant data were available. Participants who had diagnosed cancer (except non-melanoma skin cancer) (n = 50,487) and those not of white British ethnicity background (n = 28,182) were excluded. Participants were also excluded if they had completed less than one dietary assessment (n = 243,052), missing genotyping data for constructing PRS at baseline (n = 3606), had implausible total energy intakes ( < 800 or >4200 kcal/d for males and <600 or >3500 kcal/d for females) (n = 1947)27, or had lost follow-up before completing the dietary assessment (n = 408). In addition, we excluded male participants (n = 81,179). Consequently, a total of 93,271 females were included in the present study (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Dietary assessment

The Oxford WebQ is a validated dietary questionnaire used to estimate the consumption frequency of over 200 commonly consumed foods and beverages within the past 24 hours59. The US Department of Agriculture database, the largest existing flavonoid database, was used to estimate the flavonoid content of foods and beverages reported in the Oxford WebQ. The details of estimating flavonoid intakes have been described in previous studies25,27. The following seven flavonoid subcategories were ultimately identified: anthocyanins, proanthocyanidins, theaflavins and thearubigins, flavanols, flavones, flavan-3-ols, and flavanones26,28. Total flavonoid intake was estimated as the sum of all subclasses. The top three foods that contributed the highest percentage to the total flavonoid and flavonoid subclass intakes were considered eligible for inclusion in the FDS (excluding fruit juices). In addition, dark chocolate was also considered eligible for inclusion within the score as it typically contains high concentrations of flavonoids. Consequently, the primary exposure variable was FDS, which was composed of 10 foods from the Oxford WebQ: tea (including black and green), red wine, apples, berries, grapes, oranges (including satsumas), grapefruits, sweet peppers, onions, and dark chocolate. The final score was calculated by summing the total consumption amounts (number of servings per day) of the 10 selected foods, details of which had been published previously25. Tea (including black and green) intakes were capped at a maximum of 4 servings/day due to the high intakes reported in this British population. For participants who completed multiple WebQs (2–5 assessments), the FDS was calculated at each dietary assessment. The cumulative mean FDS was derived by averaging all available FDS values for each individual and used as the primary exposure variable, thereby reducing within-person variation and better reflecting long-term habitual intake. In analyses on single flavonoid-rich foods, foods were categorized into 3 groups (0 recorded consumption [low intake], > 0 and ≤ median [moderate intake], > median [high intake]). Due to the nature of the 24-hour dietary assessment, individuals reporting zero consumption likely include both true non-consumers and those with consumption frequencies too low to be detected by the assessment. Therefore, throughout the text we refer to the 0 recorded consumption and low intake group as “low intake”. Detailed information for dietary assessment can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Flavonoid subclasses intake

The primary data preparation steps for estimating flavonoid intake in the UK Biobank involved matching each Oxford WebQ food item to a corresponding USDA flavonoid code. Final flavonoid intake values (mg/d) were calculated by multiplying the daily intake of each food item (g/d), by the corresponding flavonoid subclass content (mg/g). Isoflavone intake was not estimated due to the low consumption of isoflavone-rich foods in the UK population62. Oxford WebQ food items that could not be matched with a USDA flavonoid code were assigned a flavonoid value of zero. And the details of estimating flavonoid intakes have been described in previous studies25,27.

Intakes were derived for the main subclasses of flavonoids habitually consumed. Subclasses were treated as mutually exclusive categories according to the USDA flavonoid database classification. Proanthocyanidins and flavan-3-ols are defined as distinct subclasses in the database and were not double counted in the calculation of total flavonoid intake. Total flavonoid intakes were calculated by summing the 7 component subclasses (Anthocyanins, Proanthocyanidins [excluding monomers], Theaflavins and Thearubigins, Flavanols, Flavones, Flavan-3-ols, and Flavanones).

Polygenic risk score

Genotyping was assayed using the UK Biobank Axiom Array and UK BiLEVE Axiom Array, which were then imputed the genotyping results into the combined haplotype reference panel of the Haplotype Reference Consortium and UK10K63. In this study, 168 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) showing significant genome-wide association with breast cancer were selected from a previous GWAS64. Details on the selection of SNPs are provided in Supplementary Table 3. The PRS for each individual was calculated by summing the number of risk alleles (0, 1, or 2) weighted by their effect sizes using PLINK 2.0. PRS were classified into three genetic risk groups: low (quintile 1), intermediate (quintiles 2–4), and high (quintile 5)65.

Outcomes

The cancer registry in the UK Biobank was obtained through record electronic linkage with the National Health Service Digital and National Health Service Central Register in England, Wales, and Scotland. Types of incident breast cancer cases were coded according to the 9th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9) and ICD-10, as well as self-reported data fields. Detailed codes are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of the first completed Oxford WebQ dietary assessment to the date of breast cancer diagnosis, death, or censoring, whichever came first. Censoring was completed on the following dates: October 31, 2022, for England; August 31, 2022, for Scotland; and May 31, 2022, for Wales.

Covariates

Potential confounders were identified by reviewing prior research25–28,66. The following characteristics were considered as potential covariates in this study: age (continuous, years), education levels (high, middle, no above), annual household income (less than £18,000, £18,000 to £30,999, £31,000 to £51,999, £52,000 to £100,000, > £100,000, or unknown/missing), index of multiple deprivation (continuous, a numerical deprivation score generated based on employment, car ownership, home ownership, and household overcrowding according to postcode of participants’ home address; or a lower value represents higher socioeconomic status), body mass index (BMI) (continuous, kg/m2), alcohol intake not from red wine (continuous, g/d), smoking status (current, never, previous, or unknown/missing), healthful plant-based diet index (hPDI) (score, in quintiles), energy intake (continuous, kcal/day), physical activity (continuous, minutes/week), number of dietary assessments completed with plausible energy intakes (1, 2, 3, 4, or 5), family history of breast cancer (no, yes, or unknown/missing), comorbidities (including hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes) (0, 1, 2, 3, or unknown/missing), PRS (quintile 1, quintile 2–4, or quintile 5), menopausal status (no, yes, not sure, or unknown/missing), parity (continuous, children), hormone replacement therapy (no, yes, or unknown/missing), history of oral contraceptive use (no, yes, or unknown/missing), and age at menarche (continuous, years). Detailed information is provided in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2.

For missing data, the categorical variable was assigned to an “unknown/missing” category and the average value was entered for the continuous variable.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics at baseline were presented as mean with standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables, median with interquartile range for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and as counts (percentages) for categorical variables, stratified by quintiles of FDS and disease status. Differences were compared using the Chi-square test for categorical variables and Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables.

Delayed-entry Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate the associations of the FDS, flavonoid subclass intakes, and flavonoid-rich foods with the risk of breast cancer, producing hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Tests for linear trends were performed by treating the median value of each quintile as a continuous variable in the delayed-entry Cox proportional hazards regression model. For the primary exposure-outcome associations, the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and corresponding q-values are reported67. The proportional hazards assumption was not violated in the primary analyses, as assessed using Schoenfeld residuals (P > 0.05). Two models were used in all analyses: model 1 was not adjusted for any covariates; model 2 was adjusted for age, education levels, annual household income, index of multiple deprivation, BMI, alcohol intake not from red wine, smoking status, hPDI, energy intake, physical activity, number of dietary assessments completed with plausible energy intakes, family history of breast cancer, comorbidities, PRS, menopausal status, parity, hormone replacement therapy, history of oral contraceptive use, and age at menarche.

Moreover, we performed a stratified analysis across three genetic risk groups to examine the association between FDS and risk of breast cancer. Furthermore, we evaluated the joint effect of FDS and genetic risk on breast cancer risk, using a reference group with low FDS and high genetic risk. Additive interaction was evaluated by calculating the relative excess risk due to interaction, the attributable proportion, and the synergy index68,69. To examine the significance of multiplicative interactions, the likelihood ratio test was used by comparing models that include and exclude the cross-product term between the FDS and the groups of genetic predispositions (high, intermediate, and low).

Additionally, Kaplan-Meier curves of time to cumulative incidence of breast cancer were plotted. To evaluate the exposure-response relationships between FDS and breast cancer risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models adjusted for covariates in model 2 were performed (with three nodes at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles).

We conducted stratified analyses of the associations between FDS and risk of breast cancer according to genetic risk. Stratified analyses were performed to evaluate the associations of FDS with breast cancer risk by age ( < 60 and ≥ 60 years), BMI ( < 25 and ≥ 25 kg/m2), smoking status (current, never, and previous), alcohol intake not from red wine ( < 1 g/d and ≥ 1 g/d), menopausal status (no and yes). Additionally, we conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess potential sources of bias and to further clarify whether flavonoids may underlie the observed associations. Firstly, to minimize the potential for reverse causality, we excluded participants who were diagnosed with breast cancer within the first two years of follow-up. Secondly, we constructed a modified score excluding red wine to evaluate the potential impact of alcohol-related reporting bias on our findings. Self-reported alcohol intake is susceptible to measurement error, including recall bias and social desirability bias70, which may distort risk estimates and potentially favor inverse associations with breast cancer risk. To further disentangle the role of flavonoids from that of alcohol, we additionally examined associations for flavonoid subclasses after excluding red wine, given that red wine is a major contributor to several flavonoid subclasses71. Importantly, alcohol consumption is not recommended as a strategy for the prevention of breast cancer72. Finally, we performed a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE) to address potential bias from missing covariate data.

All statistical analyses were performed by R software version 4.3.1. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
