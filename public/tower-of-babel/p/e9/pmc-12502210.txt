Development of an indirect ELISA for Duck circovirus detection based on prokaryotically expressed antigen
Authors: Mian Wu, Yudong Zhu, Mingtian Mao, Bing Li, Suyun Liang, Huihui Li, Chengguang Lu, Meixi Lu, Hongyu Ma, Xuezhi Cui, Qinghai Han, Dongping Wang, Huiling Zhang, Guodong Luo, Youxiang Diao, Junxun Li, Yi Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuck circovirus (DuCV) is an immunosuppressive virus that primarily causes growth retardation and immunosuppression in infected ducks, often leading to secondary infections, thereby posing a serious threat to the global duck industry. Currently, most indirect ELISA assays for DuCV rely on antibodies targeting the Cap structural protein; however, these methods cannot distinguish between antibodies generated by natural infection and those induced by vaccination. In this study, the Rep non-structural protein and truncated Cap structural protein lacking the nuclear localization signal (ΔCap) were successfully expressed using a prokaryotic Escherichia coli expression system. Two indirect ELISA methods were developed using the two proteins as coating antigens, and the method conditions were further optimized. The results demonstrated that both the Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA exhibited high specificity and sensitivity. When applied to duck serum samples from parts of Shandong Province, the correlation rates between the ELISA and conventional PCR methods were 95.5% and 93.3%, respectively. Furthermore, the positive detection rates in sera from vaccinated ducks were 10% for Rep-ELISA and 91% for ΔCap-ELISA, indicating that the ELISA method established in this study can differentiate between natural infection and vaccine immunization. Introduction

Duck circovirus (DuCV) is a small, non-enveloped, single-stranded circular DNA virus that belongs to the genus Circovirus within the family Circoviridae [1]. This genus also includes other members such as porcine circovirus types 1 and 2 (PCV1 and PCV2), beak and feather disease virus (BFDV), and goose circovirus (GoCV) [2]. DuCV was first identified in Germany in 2003 [1], and since then, cases of DuCV infection have been reported in Hungary [3], the United States [4], South Korea [5], Taiwan [6], and mainland China [7]. Epidemiological investigations from 2006 to 2022 revealed that DuCV is widely prevalent in several Chinese provinces, including Shandong, Fujian, Zhejiang, Sichuan, and Jiangsu, with infection rates increasing annually [2].Clinically, DuCV-infected ducks often exhibit growth retardation, scab formation on the back, and disheveled feathers [8], with higher morbidity rates observed in flocks between 3 and 5 weeks of age [2]. Studies have shown that DuCV infection is frequently associated with lymphoid tissue damage, leading to immunosuppression and increased risk of secondary infections [9]. Common co-infecting pathogens include Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, duck hepatitis virus (DHV), and novel goose parvovirus (NGPV) [4, 10, 11].

The DuCV is approximately 1.9 kb in length [1], making it the smallest known duck virus genome to date. Based on comprehensive genetic and phylogenetic analyses, DuCV can be classified into two genotypes, DuCV-1 and DuCV-2 [12], both of which are widely circulating in China. In 2022, a novel genotype, DuCV-3, was identified in Hunan Province, China, showing only 62.3–63.7% sequence homology with DuCV-1 and DuCV-2 genomes [2].The DuCV genome contains three major open reading frames (ORFs). ORF1 encodes the replication protein (Rep), which is highly conserved at the amino acid level across different DuCV strains [13]. Studies have shown that the Rep protein also plays a role in promoting cell apoptosis [14]. ORF2 encodes the capsid protein (Cap), the only structural protein of DuCV and its major immunogenic component [15], playing a key role in studies of viral antigenicity. The amino acid sequence of the Cap protein is relatively conserved between DuCV-1 and DuCV-2 but shows significant divergence in DuCV-3 [6].Notably, the N-terminal region of the Cap gene (1–108 bp) contains two nuclear localization signals (NLSs) rich in basic amino acids [16, 17], which limit its expression in Escherichia coli prokaryotic systems. Previous studies suggested that the NLS region is not essential for antigenic epitope formation [18, 19], and its removal can improve the expression efficiency of the Cap protein.

Currently, no efficient cell culture system is available for the propagation of DuCV. The virus is still isolated primarily from duck embryos; however, its detection becomes difficult after four passages [2]. Studies have shown that peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) can support low-level replication of DuCV in vitro, and an inactivated vaccine developed using this system can induce a high-level antibody response in Muscovy ducks [20]. However, due to the complex procedure of PBMC isolation, low viral replication efficiency, and high production cost, this type of vaccine has not been widely applied in practical production settings [21]. In addition, as a novel immunization strategy, DNA vaccines can express target antigens in antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby activating B and T lymphocytes to stimulate a systemic immune response in the host [22]. Previous studies have constructed DNA vaccine vectors based on the DuCV Cap protein and conducted immunization experiments in ducklings, demonstrating that such vaccines can effectively induce high levels of specific antibodies and confer a certain degree of viral protection [15]. Nevertheless, these DNA vaccines have not yet been commercially developed, and their protective duration still requires further validation and optimization. Currently, neither inactivated vaccines nor DNA vaccines for DuCV have achieved industrial-scale production or widespread application, making it difficult to establish effective herd immunity and thus increasing the overall challenge of DuCV prevention and control. In terms of diagnostics, there is still a lack of commercialized and standardized diagnostic reagents for DuCV. Currently, serological detection of DuCV mainly relies on indirect ELISA assays based on the Cap protein [17], which are used to detect specific antibodies in duck sera. However, structural protein antibodies are also detected in ducks immunized with inactivated vaccines [23], making it difficult to differentiate between natural infection and vaccine-induced immunity using Cap-based ELISA alone.

In this study, the Rep protein and truncated Cap protein (ΔCap) were successfully expressed using a prokaryotic Escherichia coli system. Based on these recombinant proteins, two indirect ELISA, Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA, were developed to enhance the accuracy and specificity of DuCV antibody detection. Moreover, Rep-ELISA enables differentiation between natural infection and inactivated vaccine-induced immunity, offering new technical support for DuCV-related research and prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Virus and serum samples

The DuCV strain used in this study was provided by Shandong New Hope Liuhe Co., Ltd. and was subsequently isolated, identified, and preserved in our laboratory (GenBank ID: OR567883). The positive and negative sera of DuCV, NGPV, duck Tembusu virus (TMUV), duck hepatitis A virus type 1 (DHAV-1) and type 3 (DHAV-3), and duck reovirus (DRV) used in this study were collected and preserved in our laboratory. Between 2024 and 2025, 292 serum samples were collected from Cherry Valley duck flocks in Shandong Province.

Construction of recombinant plasmids

The NLS (1–108 bp) of the Cap protein in the DuCV strain was deleted, and homology-directed primers for the Rep and ΔCap genes were designed using SnapGene (GSL Biotech, Chicago, USA). Primers were designed to target the BamHI/XhoI (Takara, Dalian, China) double restriction sites of the pET-32a(+) vector. The primer sequences used are listed in Table 1. Viral DNA was extracted from DuCV-infected allantoic fluid using the phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol method (25:24:1) and was used as a template for PCR amplification. The amplified products were ligated into a linearized pET-32a(+) vector using Gibson Assembly (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) to construct recombinant plasmids. The resulting plasmids were validated using BamHI/XhoI double digestion and confirmed by DNA sequencing (BGI Tech, China).

Prokaryotic expression, purification, and Western blot analysis of recombinant proteins

The recombinant plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells (Stratagene Corporation, La Jolla, CA, USA). Positive clones were selected and cultured overnight at 37 °C in LB broth supplemented with ampicillin (AMP; 100 µg/mL). An appropriate amount of bacterial liquid was transferred to a fresh culture medium and cultured until the bacterial liquid reached an OD600 of 0.6–0.8. IPTG was then added to a final concentration of 0.8 mM, and the culture was induced at 37 °C for 5 h. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed by sonication, and the expression of Rep and ΔCap proteins was analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Target proteins were purified using the urea gradient method, and their concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (NCM, China), followed by western blot analysis.

To assess the reactivity of the recombinant proteins with DuCV-positive serum, both the purified proteins and the pET-32a vector control were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose (NC) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST at room temperature for 2 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with DuCV-positive serum (1:50 dilution). The following day, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-duck IgG (KPL, USA; 1:1000 dilution) at room temperature for 1 h. The results were observed after chemiluminescence detection using the ECL reagent kit (NCM, China).

Optimization of indirect Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA conditions

Checkerboard titration was performed to determine the optimal dilutions of the coating antigens and serum samples. Purified Rep and ΔCap proteins were diluted in 0.05 mol/L carbonate buffer (pH 9.6) to final concentrations of 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, and 3.2 µg per well, and 100 µL was added to each well and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the ELISA plates were washed three times with PBST, followed by blocking with 200 µL of 5% skim milk (dissolved in PBST) at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing, DuCV-positive and-negative sera were serially diluted at 1:50, 1:100, 1:200, 1:400, and 1:800, and 100 µL of each dilution was added to the corresponding antigen-coated wells and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The plates were then washed, and 100 µL of HRP-conjugated goat anti-duck IgG (1:1000 dilution) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After the final wash, 100 µL of TMB substrate solution (NCM, China) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 15 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 µL of 3 M H₂SO₄ per well, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The optimal conditions were determined based on the highest positive-to-negative (P/N) ratio and a positive serum OD₄₅₀ value closest to 1.0. Further optimization was performed for antigen coating conditions (37 °C for 2 h; 4 °C for 12 h; and 4 °C for 24 h), blocking time with 5% skim milk (37 °C for 1 h, 2 h, and 3 h), and dilution ratios of the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000, 1:2000, 1:4000, and 1:8000).

Determination of the cut-off value for indirect ELISA

To establish the cut-off values for the two indirect ELISA assays described above, optical density at 450 nm (OD₄₅₀) was measured using 24 duck serum samples that were confirmed to be DuCV-negative by PCR. The mean OD₄₅₀ value () and standard deviation (SD) of these negative samples were calculated, and the cut-off value was defined as + 3SD.

Specificity analysis of the indirect ELISA

The specificity of the two established indirect ELISA was evaluated using serum samples positive for several duck-origin pathogens, including NGPV, TMUV, DHAV-1, DHAV-3, and DRV. DuCV-positive and-negative serum samples were used as controls. This analysis aimed to assess the specificity and potential cross-reactivity of the assay with antigens from other pathogens.

Reproducibility and sensitivity analysis of the indirect ELISA

To evaluate the reproducibility of indirect ELISA, four DuCV-positive serum samples and two SPF duck-negative serum samples were selected. Intra-assay reproducibility was assessed by testing all six samples on the same ELISA plate batch with four replicates per sample. Inter-assay reproducibility was evaluated using four independently prepared batches of ELISA plates, with each sample tested in quadruplicate. The mean OD₄₅₀ value (), standard deviation (SD), and coefficient of variation (CV = SD/ × 100%) were calculated for each group.

To assess assay sensitivity, one DuCV-positive serum sample was serially diluted two-fold (from 1:50 to 1:12,800) and tested using an indirect ELISA. The detection limit was defined as the highest dilution at which the OD₄₅₀ value remained greater than or equal to the established cut-off.

Detection of clinical samples

To evaluate the performance of the Rep-based and ΔCap-based indirect ELISA assays in detecting DuCV-specific antibodies and to distinguish between natural infection and vaccine-induced immunity, serum samples were collected from Cherry Valley duck flocks in Shandong Province. A total of 100 samples were obtained from ducks immunized with an inactivated DuCV vaccine, and 192 samples were collected from unvaccinated ducks. The unvaccinated samples (n = 192) were tested in parallel using both ELISA assays and conventional PCR, and the overall correlation between ELISA and PCR results was calculated. Vaccinated samples (n = 100) were analyzed independently using the two ELISA methods, and their clinical utility was assessed by comparing the corresponding positive detection rates.

Results

Construction of recombinant plasmids

The Rep and ΔCap genes of DuCV were successfully amplified by PCR and the resulting products matched the expected sizes (Fig. 1A and B). Double digestion of recombinant plasmids pET-32a-Rep and pET-32a-ΔCap with BamHI and XhoI yielded the expected fragment sizes (Fig. 1C and D). The integrity and correctness of the constructs were further confirmed by DNA sequencing, indicating successful construction of the recombinant plasmids.

Expression, purification, and Western blot analysis of recombinant proteins

The recombinant plasmids, pET-32a-Rep and pET-32a-ΔCap, were expressed in an Escherichia coli system. SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed the successful expression of recombinant Rep (51 kDa) and ΔCap (43 kDa) (Fig. 2A). After purification using a stepwise urea gradient, the protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay to be 0.6 µg/µL for Rep and 0.5 µg/µL for ΔCap. The standard curve used for quantification followed the equation: Y = 4.5698x – 0.4917 (R² = 0.9908), where Y represents the corrected absorbance and x represents the protein concentration.

Western blotting revealed distinct bands at the expected molecular weights for both proteins, which were specifically recognized by anti-His antibodies (Fig. 2B). Further validation using DuCV-positive serum as the primary antibody and HRP-conjugated goat anti-duck IgG as the secondary antibody demonstrated that both Rep and ΔCap proteins reacted specifically with positive serum (Fig. 2C), indicating good antigenic reactivity.

Establishment of the indirect ELISA method

The reaction conditions for the indirect Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA were optimized using a checkerboard titration assay. The results showed that the optimal condition for the Rep-ELISA was a coating concentration of 0.4 µg/well for the Rep protein and a serum dilution of 1:200, under which the P/N ratio was the highest and the P value was closest to 1 (Fig. 3A). For the ΔCap-ELISA, the best results were obtained with a coating concentration of 0.4 µg/well for the ΔCap protein and a serum dilution of 1:100, resulting in the highest P/N ratio and optimal P value (Fig. 4A). Further optimization was performed for coating conditions, blocking time, and HRP-conjugated secondary antibody dilution. The results indicated that both ELISA methods achieved optimal performance under the following conditions: coating at 4 °C for 24 h (Figs. 3B and 4B), blocking at 37 °C for 1 h (Figs. 3C and 4C), and using a 1:1000 dilution of HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Figs. 3D and 4D).

Determination of the cut-off values for indirect ELISA

A total of 24 duck serum samples confirmed to be negative for DuCV infection were tested using established indirect ELISA methods. The mean OD₄₅₀ values were calculated as 0.171 for the Rep protein and 0.140 for the ΔCap protein, with standard deviations (SD) of 0.045 and 0.052, respectively. Based on these data, the cut-off values were determined to be 0.308 for Rep-ELISA (Fig. 5A) and 0.259 for ΔCap-ELISA (Fig. 5B). Samples with OD₄₅₀ values higher than the corresponding cut-off value were considered positive.

Specificity analysis of the indirect ELISA

The established Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA were used to test positive sera against NGPV, TMUV, DHAV-1, DHAV-3, DRV, and DuCV, and negative duck sera. The results showed that, except for the DuCV-positive sera, the OD₄₅₀ values of negative sera and sera positive for other viral antibodies were significantly lower than the cut-off values (Fig. 6A and B). These findings indicate that both indirect ELISA methods exhibit good specificity for DuCV antibodies and are suitable for serological differential diagnosis of other common viral infections in ducks.

Reproducibility and sensitivity analysis of the indirect ELISA

To evaluate the reproducibility of Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA, four DuCV-positive serum samples and two SPF duck-negative serum samples were tested. The results showed that the intra-assay CVs of the Rep-ELISA ranged from 5.52 to 7.40% and the inter-assay CVs ranged from 4.198 to 8.952% (Table 2). For the ΔCap-ELISA, the intra-assay CVs ranged from 2.029 to 7.807% and the inter-assay CVs ranged from 3.468 to 8.968% (Table 3). All CV values were < 10%, indicating that both assays demonstrated acceptable stability.

Clinical sample testing

The established ELISA methods were used to test 192 clinical duck serum samples, and the results were compared with PCR testing. A total of 84 positive serum samples were detected by PCR (43.75%), 91 positive serum samples were identified by Rep-ELISA (47.40%), and 93 positive serum samples were detected by ΔCap-ELISA (48.44%). The correlation rates between Rep-ELISA, ΔCap-ELISA, and PCR results were 92.3% and 90.3%, respectively (Tables 4 and 5), the correlation rate between Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA was 91.67% (Table 6). In addition, testing 100 serum samples from immunized ducks showed that the positive rates for Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA were 10% and 91%, respectively (Table 7). These results demonstrate that the established indirect ELISA methods exhibit high consistency with PCR testing for clinical samples while also effectively distinguishing between DuCV infection antibodies and inactivated vaccine-induced antibodies, highlighting their promising potential for practical applications.

Discussion

Duck circovirus (DuCV), an immunosuppressive virus, has spread globally since it was first reported in 2003 [24]. The DuCV genome is approximately 1.9 kb in length and contains a stem-loop structure. Six putative open reading frames (ORFs) longer than 200 bp have been predicted, which are distributed on both the positive and complementary strands of the genome [7, 25]. ORF1 encodes Rep protein, a key regulatory factor in viral replication [1], which is expressed during the early stage of DuCV infection, making it a potential target for early diagnosis. In this study, Rep protein was found to react with DuCV-positive sera, indicating strong antigenic reactivity. In studies on porcine circovirus (PCV), the Rep protein has been shown to induce the production of specific antibodies in the host [26]. ORF2 encodes the Cap protein, the sole structural protein of the virus, which possesses the ability to self-assemble into virus-like particles (VLPs) with conformational features closely resembling those of native virions [17, 27], making it the development ovaccine kit development. Additionally, a unique quadruplicate tandem repeat (QTR) sequence was identified in the non-coding region between Rep and Cap genes. Acting as a downstream sequence element (DSE), QTR enhances mRNA stability, thereby regulating viral gene expression [25]. QTR exhibits notable differences, yet relative conservation between DuCV-1 and DuCV-2, making it a potential molecular marker for genetic typing.

Currently, the diagnostic techniques for DuCV infection primarily include molecular detection methods and serological assays. Among them, molecular methods mainly consist of conventional PCR, real-time quantitative PCR, and nucleic acid probe hybridization techniques [17, 28–30]. Serological detection methods mainly include indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [21]. A previous study successfully detected intracellular expression of the Cap protein by infecting DuCV into RAW264.7 mouse macrophage cell lines and performing IFA analysis [31]. IFA offers advantages such as high sensitivity, specificity, and direct visual observation, but its relatively complex procedures and high technical requirements limit its widespread use in basic-level laboratories. In contrast, indirect ELISA offers significant advantages in clinical diagnosis, owing to its simplicity, high sensitivity, and capacity for high-throughput sample testing [17, 32]. However, current ELISA methods are difficult to distinguish between antibodies generated by natural infections and those induced by vaccination, and they exhibit limited sensitivity during the early stages of infection. To address these limitations, this study utilized a prokaryotic expression system to produce Rep and ΔCap proteins and established two ELISA assays, Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA, which can function synergistically to enhance diagnostic accuracy.

Although the Escherichia coli expression system offers advantages such as low cost and ease of operation, overexpressed heterologous proteins are prone to forming inclusion bodies [33, 34]. In this study, Rep and ΔCap proteins were purified from inclusion bodies using a stepwise urea gradient refolding method. Western blot analysis confirmed that both proteins were specifically recognized by the corresponding antibodies, indicating their antigenicity and suitability for ELISA detection. The accuracy of ELISA is influenced by various parameters; for instance, excessively high antigen coating concentrations may cause nonspecific adsorption and elevated background signals, whereas overly low concentrations may compromise the detection sensitivity [35]. In this study, checkerboard titration was employed to optimize assay conditions, ultimately determining the optimal coating concentration for both Rep-ELISA and ΔCap -ELISA to be 0.4 µg/well, with serum dilutions of 1:200 and 1:100, respectively. Additionally, nonspecific reactions in duck serum samples were effectively minimized by optimizing antigen coating conditions, blocking time with 5% skim milk, and dilution of the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody to characterize the assay performance. Cross-reactivity tests demonstrated that both Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA showed no significant cross-reactivity with positive sera against common duck pathogens, such as NGPV, TMUV, and DHAV, indicating high specificity. Reproducibility testing showed that both assays exhibited intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) below 10%, reflecting good stability. Sensitivity analysis revealed that both ELISA methods were capable of detecting positive sera diluted 1:800, indicating high sensitivity.

In the validation study using non-immunized serum samples, the positive detection rates of PCR, Rep-ELISA, and ΔCap-ELISA were 43.75%, 47.40%, and 48.44%, respectively, indicating a high level of consistency between the two ELISA methods in clinical samples. Furthermore, the correlation rates of Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA with the PCR results were 92.3% and 90.3%, respectively. The observed differences in the detection results may be attributed to the shorter duration of viremia compared to the persistence of DuCV-specific antibodies following infection. Additionally, when 100 serum samples from ducks immunized with inactivated vaccines were tested using the two indirect ELISA assays, the positive rate for Rep-ELISA was 10%, whereas that for ΔCap-ELISA was 91%. The 10 positive samples detected by Rep-ELISA may have resulted from natural infection in individuals with insufficient immune response following vaccination, leading to the production of antibodies against the Rep protein. These findings indicate that the established Rep-ELISA is capable of distinguishing antibodies induced by natural infection from those generated by inactivated vaccination, whereas the ΔCap-ELISA can be used to evaluate antibody levels induced by immunization.

In conclusion, the Rep-ELISA and ΔCap-ELISA assays established in this study enable the simultaneous detection of antibodies against DuCV non-structural and structural proteins, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy. Moreover, they offer a novel approach for distinguishing between natural infections and inactivated vaccine-induced immunity. Given their low cost, simplicity, and rapid detection capabilities, these assays are suitable for epidemiological surveillance and prevention and control of DuCV, with promising practical application prospects. To further enhance the stability and clinical applicability of the assay system, future studies will expand the sample size and diversity, conduct large-scale validation, and promote the translational application of this method in disease prevention and control.
