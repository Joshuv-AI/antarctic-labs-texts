Stress coping theory-based nursing program enhances psychosocial adaptation in acute leukemia
Authors: Hong Cheng, Chengli Zhong, Sishi Fang, Huafen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCaregivers of acute leukemia (AL) patients face heavy physical and psychological burdens, yet few stress coping theory-based interventions address their positive experiences and coping needs. We aimed to explore the effects of a nursing intervention based on stress coping theory on primary caregivers of AL patients.MethodsThis is a non-randomized controlled trial. Participants were selected via convenience sampling. Eighty primary caregivers of AL patients hospitalized between October 2023 and December 2024 were enrolled and assigned to an intervention group (n = 40) and control group (n = 40). The intervention group received a 5-week stress coping theory-based nursing program in addition to routine care, while the control group received routine care only. Caregiver outcomes were assessed at baseline and one week post-intervention using the Positive Aspects of Caregiving Scale, Social Support Rating Scale, Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire, and Zarit Burden Inventory. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Within-group comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests or chi-square tests. A significance level of 5% (p < 0.05) was set for all analyses.ResultsAt baseline, there were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups in Positive Aspects of Caregiving Scale, Social Support Rating Scale, or Zarit Burden Inventory scores (all P > 0.05). There was a significant difference in Passive Coping scores (P = 0.011) between intervention and control groups at baseline in Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire scores. After the 5-week intervention, the intervention group showed significantly higher Positive Aspects of Caregiving Scale and Social Support Rating Scale scores and lower Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire negative coping and Zarit Burden Inventory scores compared with the control group (P < 0.05). Within the intervention group, Positive Aspects of Caregiving Scale, Social Support Rating Scale scores increased, and Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire negative coping and Zarit Burden Inventory scores decreased significantly from baseline (P < 0.05).ConclusionNursing interventions based on stress coping theory effectively enhanced positive caregiving experiences and perceived social support while reducing caregiving burden and negative coping among primary caregivers of AL patients.Trial registrationChiCTR2500110154, date: October 9, 2025.Keywords: Acute leukemia, Stress coping, Caregivers, Positive aspects of caregiving, Caregiver burden, Social support

Background

Acute leukemia (AL) is a malignant hematologic disease characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of immature hematopoietic cells [1]. Despite advances in diagnostic and prognostic methods, targeted therapies, and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, patients often undergo prolonged chemotherapy and experience recurrent complications, which impose substantial physical, psychological, and financial burdens [2]. These challenges highlight the critical role of caregivers in supporting patients, making the study of caregivers’ experiences and well-being particularly important. Positive aspects of caregiving refer to the beneficial outcomes from caregiver’s experiences such as personal growth, role satisfaction, self-affirmation, and improved coping skills [3]. These positive feelings are important for caregivers to manage negative emotions, enhance the quality of life and support the patients’ recovery [4]. Such positive aspects of caregiving are particularly valuable in AL, where extended chemotherapy regimens, recurrent complications, and disease-related suffering impose considerable physical, psychological, and financial stress on both patients and their caregivers [5]. Previous study indicated that in caregivers of AL, positive aspects of caregiving tend to be limited and are negatively associated with caregiving burden [6]. Social support and coping styles are important correlates of caregiver outcomes in AL, with lower social support and less adaptive coping linked to worse caregiver adjustment [7]. These findings support the need for psychological support interventions.

A growing body of research supported the efficacy of psychosocial interventions in ameliorating caregiver distress and promoting well-being. For instance, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) based programs have been shown to reduce anxiety and depression symptoms among caregivers of cancer patients by modifying maladaptive thought patterns and enhancing coping skills [8]. Similarly, mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) have demonstrated benefits in lowering perceived stress and improving emotional regulation, thereby fostering sense of present-moment awareness and acceptance in caregivers [9]. Moreover, interventions integrating psycho-education with skill-building components (e.g., problem solving training, communication skills, and stress management techniques) have consistently yielded improvements in caregivers’ psychological adjustment and quality of life [10]. Recent research conducted among caregivers of children with rare diseases further illuminates the complex interplay between stress, coping, and positive aspects of caregiving (PAC) [11]. Despite the importance of fostering positive aspects of caregiving, few studies have targeted psychological interventions for caregivers of patients with AL, and existing programs remain immature due to the lack of a solid theoretical framework. Existing interventions largely aim to fill gaps in caregivers’ knowledge, skills, or social support, such as skills training, cognitive therapy, or support groups, by focusing on reducing negative emotions [12, 13]. Nevertheless, current interventions and research have paid little attention to how caregivers might foster positive feelings through meaningful coping strategies. This gap highlights the need for theoretically grounded interventions tailored to the unique challenges faced by caregivers of patients with AL.

According to stress coping theory, a psycho-cognitive framework proposed by Lazarus and Folkman [14], caregivers can manage stressors by utilizing coping strategies or resources, thereby supporting their mental health throughout the caregiving process [15]. Coping includes behavioral and cognitive strategies such as problem-focused actions, avoidance, acceptance, information-seeking, and defense mechanisms. The effectiveness of these strategies depends on functional status, social support, and personal abilities, directly affecting caregivers’ physical and mental well-being. Meaning-focused coping, which fosters positive emotions, facilitates cognitive reappraisal, and encourages seeking social support, provides a theoretical basis for interventions aimed at enhancing caregivers’ well-being and support networks [16, 17]. Stress coping theory provides a theoretical foundation for developing targeted interventions. The present intervention was designed to enhance positive feelings among caregivers by identifying key stressors and coping resources. We hypothesized that for caregivers of patients with AL, the primary stressors include the prolonged, demanding nature of care responsibilities and the associated financial strain. Once these stressors arise, the individual undergoes a primary appraisal, perceiving the situation as stressful, which in turn gives rise to caregiver burden as a psychological response. Meanwhile, whether caregivers can adapt to and cope with the stress generated by caregiving burden is closely related to factors such as individual coping strategies and social support. Social support is a coping resource. It directly influences the outcome of secondary appraisal, which in turn affects the individual’s stress response. When caregivers are able to alleviate or mitigate these stressors, they are more likely to exhibit a positive and healthy state, with an increase in positive experiences. Conversely, when caregivers are unable to resolve or even amplify these stressors, a maladaptive response is triggered. This leads to physiological, psychological, and social instability, ultimately compromising their positive feelings and mental health.

The importance of the present research lies in its innovative integration of the well-established Stress Coping Theory into a structured nursing program tailored for AL caregivers [17]. Unlike prior interventions that predominantly target negative feelings [12, 13], this study focused on enhancing PAC, perceived social support, and adaptive coping—key factors that can foster resilience and sustain caregiving capacity over the long and arduous treatment trajectory [3, 18]. By translating theoretical principles into a manualized, multi-session intervention delivered by a multidisciplinary team, this study provided a novel perspective for clinical practice. If proven effective, such a program could offer a shift in supportive care—from merely alleviating suffering to actively promoting growth and well-being among caregivers, which may ultimately contribute to more stable and supportive care environments for patients with AL.

Based on this theoretical foundation, this study applied stress coping theory to develop a primary caregiving intervention program for the main caregivers of patients with AL. We aim to enhance caregivers’ positive aspects of caregiving, strengthen their perceived social support, and reduce caregiver burden and maladaptive coping strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a non-randomized controlled trial that only implemented investigator blinding. To minimize confounding factors, this study was conducted simultaneously in two wards. Participants were assigned based on ward allocation: two non-contiguous wards within the department were designated for the intervention group and the control group, respectively. Both wards were affiliated with the same Hematology Department of the hospital, with no significant differences in staffing composition or medical coordination. The treatment approach in both wards followed a personalized chemotherapy-based regimen. This study was registered at the Chinese clinical trial registry (ChiCTR2500110154) on October 9, 2025. Ethical approval

Each caregiver provided written informed consent. This study was designed in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval No.: 20230050B). Written informed consent for was obtained from all participants.

Study setting

Participants and their caregivers were recruited from AL patients hospitalized in the hematology department of The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Ninety-two primary caregivers of consecutive AL patients admitted to the Hematology Department between October 2023 and December 2024 were enrolled.

Sampling methods

Questionnaires were distributed and individuals who expressed interest underwent eligibility screening conducted by two trained assessors. Subsequently, a hematologist performed a diagnostic evaluation to confirm AL. The inclusion criteria were: caring for a patient newly diagnosed with AL according to morphology, immunology, cytogenetics, and molecular biology (MICM) standard [1]; serving as the main caregiver, providing the longest daily care for at least one month; and aged ≥ 18 years and willing to participate in the study. Caregivers were excluded if they had mental illness or cognitive impairment, faced language barriers that impeded communication, had experienced bereavement, divorce, or other mood-affecting events within the past two months, or were paid caregivers.

The sample size was calculated for a two-group comparison using the formula for the difference between two independent means: n1 = n2=2[(tα/2+tβ)s/δ]2. Based on pre-experiment results, an effect size of 0.8, a two-sided alpha of 0.05, statistical power of 0.80, a 1:1 allocation ratio, and an anticipated attrition rate of 10–20%, we aimed to recruit at least 39 caregivers per group.

Grouping

The control group received standard care based on the AL clinical nursing routine. This included: (a) disease-related education (e.g., explanation of chemotherapy regimens and targeted therapies, monitoring for potential adverse events); (b) instruction on daily caregiving tasks (e.g., preventing infections, managing bleeding, maintaining hygiene and nutrition); and (c) general psychological support, which comprised unstructured emotional support provided by nurses, such as listening to concerns, offering encouragement, and addressing immediate emotional distress. No structured psychological intervention or coping skills training was provided. To prevent contamination, the control group was managed in a separate ward. The intervention group received a combination of standard care and additional intervention program. The intervention program, designed based on caregiver needs identified through interviews, a previous survey, and expert consultation, targeted primary caregivers of AL patients. It lasted 5 weeks, with one 50-minute session per week, using one-on-one sessions in the first two weeks and group sessions in weeks three to five. The weekly sessions were conducted by a nurse-led multidisciplinary team. Sessions were scheduled in advance, conducted according to the program, and caregivers’ understanding and feedback were checked to ensure effectiveness (Table 1). To ensure intervention fidelity, the following measures were implemented: (1) All intervention providers received standardized training based on stress-coping theory before delivering the sessions; (2) A structured intervention manual was used to guide each session; (3) Attendance rates and session completion were recorded to monitor adherence; (participation and completion rates were 100%) and (4) After each session, participants completed a brief face-to-face interview to assess their understanding and engagement with the intervention (Fig. 1).

A multidisciplinary team implemented the program, including a researcher, psychology professor, hematologist, four nurses, and a postgraduate student. The researcher was responsible for the design and data analysis; the professor trained the team and provided psychological support; the hematologist guided disease knowledge; the nurses delivered the intervention and follow-up; and the student assisted with data collection. The nurses involved in the intervention met the following requirements: a bachelor’s degree or higher, at least five years of work experience in the hematology department, the professional title of charge nurse or above, and a position level of N3 or higher. The team received four 30–40 min unified training sessions based on stress coping theory to ensure consistency. Nurses underwent a structured training program before the trial began. The training curriculum was as follows: (1) Theoretical foundation: the stress coping theory and its application in caregiver interventions; (2) Practical skills: the content, structure, and delivery of each session, techniques for facilitating group discussions, and supportive guidance skills; (3) Administration of assessment tools and evaluation procedures. Training methods included didactic lectures, group discussions, and role-playing exercises. Trainers assessed the interventionists’ proficiency through direct observation during simulated practice demonstrations of the intervention sessions.

Data collection

All measures were conducted in the ward by two trained assessors who were blinded to the group allocation. The assessors received standardized training program prior to the trial on the use of the assessment tools and operational procedures. Two trained investigators collected data from the caregivers through face-to-face interviews. Demographic characteristics including age, gender, income, self- management were collected at baseline from each group. The positive aspects of caregiving (PAC) scale, social support rating scale (SSRS), simplified coping style questionnaire (SCSQ), and Zarit Burden Inventory (ZBI) were utilized to evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention on the caregivers before the intervention and within one week after the intervention. Measures were collected at two time points: before the intervention (T0) and within one week after the intervention (T1).

The PAC scale includes 9 items in two dimensions: self-affirmation and life outlook [19]. It uses a 5-point Likert scale with a total score ranging from 9 to 45. Higher scores indicate more positive caregiving experiences [20, 21]. SSRS measures an individual’s level of social support across three dimensions: objective support, subjective support, and social support utilization. It includes 10 items, with higher scores indicating better social support [22]. SCSQ assesses positive and negative coping across 20 items, each scored from 0 (not adopted) to 3 (often adopted). Higher average scores in a dimension indicate a greater tendency to use that coping style [23]. The ZBI measures personal and responsibility burden across 18 items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 88 [24, 25]. All scales have demonstrated good reliability in Chinese populations, with Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.78 to 0.90.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data conforming to a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, and within-group comparisons using paired t-test. Categorical variables were compared with chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and analyses were conducted with SPSS 26.0.

Results

Eligible participants and characteristics

Initially, 92 primary caregivers of AL patients were enrolled, with 45 assigned to the intervention group and 47 to the control group. In the intervention group, 3 participants were dropped out at their willing as the patients were discharged and 2 caregivers were terminated the study as the patients died due to complications. In the control group, 1 participant was lost to follow-up, 3 caregivers terminated the study at their willing and 3 caregivers terminated the study due to patients’ death. Ultimately, 40 participants in each group were included in the final analysis.

Adherence to the intervention program

Among the 40 caregivers who completed the study in the intervention group, the overall attendance rate for the five weekly sessions was high. On average, participants attended 4.6 out of 5 sessions (92% attendance rate). Thirty-five caregivers (87.5%) attended all five sessions, while the remaining five attended at least three sessions. No participant attended fewer than three sessions.

No significant differences were observed between the two groups of caregivers in terms of demographic characteristics, including gender, age, marital status, and education level. Other characteristics, such as occupation, family income, payment method, relationship with the patient, and self-care ability, were also similar between the groups (P > 0.05, Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess a Stress and Coping Theory-based intervention designed to elevate aspects of caregiving, social support, and positive experiences in AL caregivers. The results confirmed its superiority over routine care, nursing interventions based on stress coping theory effectively enhanced positive caregiving experiences and perceived social support while reducing caregiving burden and negative coping among primary caregivers of AL patients. To our knowledge, this is the first study that applies the theoretical framework to improve positive aspects in caregivers of newly diagnosed acute leukemia patients, thereby offering a novel perspective for evaluating intervention outcomes for caregivers.

Caregivers of AL patients face substantial physical, psychological, and financial burdens due to prolonged chemotherapy, recurrent complications, and disease-related suffering, yet few interventions have targeted their positive experiences and coping strategies. Existing programs mainly focus on alleviating negative emotions or improving knowledge and skills, often neglecting strategies that foster positive aspects of caregiving and enhance social support [26, 27]. Our results showed that the intervention significantly increased caregivers’ PAC, SSRS, and SCSQ scores, while reducing ZBI scores, indicating improvements in self-affirmation, overall positive caregiving experiences, adaptive coping, social support, and a reduction in caregiver burden.

Caregivers of patients with AL often face high levels of stress due to the severity of the disease and the long-term caregiving burden. According to stress coping theory [28], such stressors can negatively impact caregivers’ well-being but can also provide opportunities for personal growth if managed with positive coping strategies. In this study, after receiving standard care combined with the additional intervention, caregivers in the intervention group showed significantly higher total PAC scores and dimension scores than those in the control group (P < 0.001). These results align with previous findings [29, 30], supporting the effectiveness of nursing interventions grounded in stress coping theory for enhancing caregivers’ positive experiences. In our intervention, caregivers were guided to enhance self-awareness, explore their potential, and adopt positive coping strategies. This not only improved their ability to care for patients but also helped them recognize the personal growth gained through caregiving, such as increased knowledge, skills, and confidence, thereby reinforcing their sense of self-affirmation and overall PAC. In addition, the intervention helped caregivers reframe setbacks in the caregiving process as opportunities for personal growth. Through sessions such as “How to Explore Meaningful Events and Self-Assessments,” they were guided to shift their attention away from negative thoughts, identify positive aspects of caregiving, and engage in patient care with a more constructive mindset. This process of cognitive reappraisal not only stimulated positive emotions but also enhanced caregivers’ confidence and hope, thereby further improving their PAC levels. Interestingly, we found that the Life Prospect scores in PAC scale were decreased following the intervention. The decline in Life Prospect scores may reflect the caregivers’ increasing awareness of the disease prognosis, despite their improved ability to cope with daily challenges.

Caregivers of patients with AL often experience insufficient social support, which can exacerbate their psychological stress and caregiving burden. According to stress coping theory, adequate social support not only helps caregivers share emotional and practical burdens but also promotes positive coping and resilience [11]. In this study, the SSRS scores of caregivers in the intervention group were significantly higher than those in the control group after the intervention (P < 0.001), indicating that nursing interventions based on stress coping theory can effectively enhance caregivers’ perceived social support. These findings are consistent with the previous study, in which there was a significant improvement in social support among family caregivers of dementia patients following an intervention using the Focused Solution Model [31].

Several factors may explain this improvement. First, the intervention established a mutual-assistance group for family members, where caregivers were encouraged to share caregiving challenges and psychological stress with others. This collective interaction not only strengthened family cohesion but also alleviated the burden on the primary caregiver. For example, in the “How to Use Available Support Systems and Respond Proactively” sessions, caregivers and family members discussed caregiving challenges together, fostering a stronger sense of unity. Second, the intervention guided caregivers to explore and better utilize their existing support systems, including community and social resources. During the “How to Discover Support Resources” sessions, caregivers were introduced to community-based support groups and social service agencies, which expanded their available support networks. Previous studies have suggested that social support mediates the relationship between caregiving burden and positive caregiving experiences [32]. By increasing caregivers’ perceived social support and sense of hope, nursing interventions may help reduce caregiving burden while enhancing positive caregiving experiences.

Caregivers of patients with AL often experience a heavy burden due to the intensive treatment process, long-term chemotherapy, and uncertainty regarding prognosis and recurrence. This burden is particularly significant for middle-aged and elderly caregivers, who are often spouses or adult children. Previous studies have noted that while caregiving burden can be substantial, it can coexist with PAC and is not necessarily inversely correlated [33]. Influenced by familism, family-centered cultural values may heighten the sense of burden while simultaneously fostering positive caregiving experiences that help caregivers find meaning in their roles [34]. Furthermore, research suggests that caregiver burden is shaped not only by patient-related factors but also by family culture and the availability of social support [32].

The results of this study showed that nursing interventions based on stress coping theory were effective in reducing caregiver burden, increasing coping resources, and enhancing PAC levels. These findings indicate that structured support and guided coping strategies can help caregivers reframe their experiences and better manage the challenges of caregiving, even under heavy stress. One possible explanation for these results is that the intervention emphasized joint family coping and social support. At the early stage of diagnosis and treatment, caregivers’ stressors were assessed, and coping strategies were developed in line with their prior experiences and personal beliefs. Subsequently, caregivers were guided to apply action-oriented and cognitive regulation techniques to better manage stress. For example, during the “How to Adapt the Caregiving Role and Face Stress” sessions, caregivers learned to identify their primary stressors and formulate effective coping strategies, which in turn reduced their perceived burden and promoted more positive caregiving experiences.

There are some limitations in this study. This study was conducted at a single hospital, which may limit the representativeness of the sample. Additionally, the follow-up period was relatively short due to time constraints, restricting the observation of long-term effects. Future research should include larger, more diverse samples and extend follow-up periods to evaluate the sustained impact of such interventions, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of their effectiveness.

The findings of this study carry several implications for integrating stress coping theory-based interventions into routine clinical nursing practice. First, the intervention was delivered by trained nurses within existing ward settings, and each component of the program was aligned with the patients’ treatment phases and length of hospital stay. This means it can be feasibly incorporated into current leukemia care workflows without requiring additional staffing. Second, regarding implementation, hospitals should consider incorporating structured training on stress coping theory into the continuing education curriculum for oncology and hematology nurses. This would ensure that the necessary skills and resources are readily available. Finally, sustainability should be considered. Embedding the intervention into standardized care pathways and securing institutional support may facilitate its long-term adoption. Future strategies should also explore expanding this model to other hospitals and regions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. (1) Participants were all recruited from a single hospital in an eastern Chinese city, resulting in a small and homogeneous sample. Future research should conduct multicenter, large-sample randomized controlled trials to provide higher-level evidence. (2) The use of self-report scales (PAC, ZBI) may have introduced response bias, which is particularly notable in studies focusing on positive experiences. Future studies should incorporate more objective measures to confirm the findings. (3) The one-week follow-up period was relatively short, and the long-term effects on caregivers and patients remain unclear. Further studies with extended follow-up durations (e.g., 3 months, 6 months) are needed to evaluate the long-term effectiveness of the nursing intervention. (4) Limitations of the non-randomized, non-blind trial design: First, due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and care providers was not feasible, which may introduce performance bias. Second, although we employed a controlled design, random allocation of participants was not implemented for practical and ethical reasons related to clinical workflow and ward management. Instead, we used a quasi-experimental approach by assigning two separate wards to the intervention and control groups. This may limit the internal validity of the findings, as baseline differences between groups, despite being statistically controlled, could still affect outcomes.

Conclusion

In this study, a nursing intervention grounded in stress coping theory was implemented for the primary caregivers of AL patients. The findings demonstrated that the intervention significantly enhanced caregivers’ PAC, increased their social support, reduced caregiving burden, and decreased negative coping styles. These results suggest that stress coping–based nursing interventions can effectively support caregivers and improve their overall caregiving experience.
