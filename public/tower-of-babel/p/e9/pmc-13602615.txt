Cross-sectional bidirectional associations between executive function and emotion regulation in adolescents: the moderating role of digital engagement intensity
Authors: Guochun Shi, Zimo Shi, Qianqian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pervasive integration of digital technologies into adolescent daily life raises critical questions about the interplay between core neurocognitive capacities and emotional development. Although both executive function (EF) and emotion regulation (ER) have been independently linked to digital media exposure, few studies have examined how these two constructs are associated within digitally mediated contexts, and fewer still have modeled digital engagement as a contextual moderator rather than a simple predictor.MethodsA sample of 368 adolescents aged 12–17 completed behavioral assessments of inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility alongside self-report and task-based measures of emotion regulation strategies. Objective screen time data, including application-category breakdowns, were collected over a 14-day monitoring period using device-level tracking applications. Structural equation modeling was employed to test cross-sectional associations between EF and ER, and to examine whether objectively measured digital use intensity moderated these associations in both directions. Exploratory analyses additionally examined whether moderation patterns differed across digital activity categories.ResultsThe structural model revealed significant cross-sectional associations in both directions: stronger EF was associated with greater use of cognitive reappraisal, while effective regulation was positively linked to executive performance; conversely, habitual expressive suppression was associated with diminished EF. Among EF components, inhibitory control showed the strongest association with adaptive regulation. Digital use intensity moderated both directional associations, though the attenuating effect was somewhat more pronounced for the EF–reappraisal link than for the reverse pathway. A threshold pattern emerged, with high-intensity users showing marked declines relative to moderate and low users. Exploratory, unadjusted analyses tentatively suggested that the moderation was concentrated in social media and short-form video use, whereas gaming and educational activities showed weaker or non-significant patterns; these category-specific results are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.ConclusionsThese cross-sectional findings are consistent with a context-sensitive view in which EF and ER show reciprocal concurrent associations—what we describe here as cross-sectional bidirectional associations rather than temporal feedback—and digital engagement functions as an environmental moderator shaping that coupling. The results suggest that integrated intervention approaches combining executive function training, social-emotional learning, and constructive digital literacy may hold promise. However, given the cross-sectional design, the present findings should be regarded as preliminary evidence for future longitudinal research that can directly evaluate temporal ordering.

Introduction

The rapid proliferation of digital technologies has fundamentally reshaped the developmental landscape for adolescents, introducing both unprecedented opportunities and significant cognitive-emotional challenges. Smartphones, social media platforms, and immersive digital content now constitute a pervasive ecology in which young people spend a substantial portion of their waking hours [1]. This saturation raises pressing questions about how core neurocognitive capacities—particularly executive function (EF) and emotion regulation (ER)—are associated, and how they may co-vary under sustained digital exposure. Unlike previous generations, today’s adolescents must navigate a constant stream of notifications, algorithmically curated content, and socially comparative feedback loops, all during a critical window of prefrontal cortical maturation [2]. The convergence of these environmental pressures with ongoing neural development creates a context that warrants careful empirical scrutiny.

Executive function, broadly encompassing inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility, serves as a foundational scaffold for goal-directed behavior and adaptive decision-making throughout adolescence [3]. A growing body of evidence suggests that excessive screen time and multitasking across digital platforms may be associated with weakened attentional control and reduced efficiency of task-switching processes [4]. Yet the picture is not uniformly negative; certain structured digital activities, such as cognitive training games, appear to relate positively to specific EF components under controlled conditions [5]. This apparent paradox underscores the need for nuanced investigation that moves beyond simplistic screen-time metrics toward a deeper understanding of how different digital experiences selectively relate to executive processes.

Emotion regulation—the ability to monitor, evaluate, and modulate emotional responses—follows a parallel but distinct developmental trajectory during adolescence, one heavily influenced by social context and environmental feedback [6]. Digital environments introduce novel regulatory demands: adolescents must manage emotional reactions to cyberbullying, social exclusion signaled through platform metrics, and the affective volatility inherent in rapid content consumption [7]. Recent cross-sectional studies have documented associations between heavy social media use and diminished ER capacity, manifesting as heightened rumination, difficulty disengaging from negative content, and increased emotional reactivity [8]. Recent work further indicates that emotion regulation difficulties may co-occur with specific patterns of social media use, suggesting that the relationship between digital engagement and affective functioning is not a simple one-way street [9]. The mechanisms driving these associations, however, remain poorly understood.

What makes the link between EF and ER particularly compelling in digital contexts is the possibility of a bidirectional association. Theoretical frameworks rooted in cognitive control models posit that executive processes provide the top-down regulatory infrastructure necessary for effective emotion modulation [10]. Conversely, intense or poorly regulated emotional states can draw on shared neurocognitive resources and relate to weaker concurrent cognitive performance—a pattern sometimes described as affective interference [11]. A recent meta-analysis by Toh and colleagues synthesized 179 effect sizes across 59 independent samples and reported small-to-moderate positive associations between all three core EF components (inhibition, updating, and shifting) and cognitive reappraisal, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.13 to 0.19 [12]. Importantly, that meta-analysis found that the relationship between inhibition and reappraisal was stronger when EF was assessed using affectively valenced stimuli, suggesting that context-dependent measurement approaches—such as those employed here—may capture aspects of the EF–ER link that standard neutral paradigms miss. In digitally saturated environments, the bidirectional EF–ER association may be especially pronounced: weaker inhibitory control could relate to greater susceptibility to emotionally provocative online content, which in turn co-occurs with elevated regulatory demands. Despite the theoretical plausibility of such concurrent linkages, few studies have empirically examined the bidirectional EF–ER association specifically within digital contexts [13].

Several gaps in the existing literature motivate the present research. First, most prior investigations have examined EF and ER as parallel but separate outcomes of digital technology use [4, 8, 14], without systematically integrating the two constructs within a unified analytical framework. Second, the specificity of digital environmental influences—how particular features of online platforms differentially relate to cognitive versus emotional processes—has received insufficient attention [15, 16], a gap underscored by recent reviews questioning whether aggregated screen-time metrics can capture meaningful differences across activity types [17]. Third, while cross-sectional designs predominate in this area [13, 14], the field lacks studies that integrate EF, ER, and the digital ecology of adolescent daily life within a single associative model. We acknowledge from the outset that the present cross-sectional design itself cannot address temporal ordering and is best suited to providing concurrent associative evidence rather than process-level inference [18]. These shortcomings collectively hinder both theoretical advancement and the design of evidence-based interventions.

This study aims to address these limitations through three primary contributions. First, we examine how objectively measured digital engagement intensity is associated with EF and ER in an adolescent sample. Second, we propose and test a cross-sectional model that captures concurrent associations between executive and emotional processes, examining evidence for bidirectional links between EF and ER within the context of digitally mediated daily life. Third, drawing on the empirical findings, we articulate targeted intervention principles that account for the specific cognitive-emotional dynamics of digital environments rather than relying on broad screen-time reduction recommendations alone [19]. Our central hypothesis posits that EF and ER are bidirectionally associated and that digital use intensity moderates this association, such that high-intensity digital consumption is linked to a weaker positive coupling between EF and adaptive ER. Because the data are cross-sectional, all hypotheses are framed as concurrent associations, and the findings are intended to provide preliminary evidence to inform future longitudinal research [18].

The originality of this work lies in its integrative approach—bridging cognitive neuroscience perspectives on executive control with affective science models of emotion regulation under ecologically valid digital conditions. By moving beyond isolated variable associations toward an examination of their joint pattern of associations, the present investigation offers a more nuanced portrait of adolescent cognitive-emotional functioning in the contemporary technological milieu.

Conceptual definitions and theoretical frameworks for executive function and emotion regulation

Executive function is most commonly operationalized through three distinguishable yet interrelated core components: inhibitory control, working memory updating, and cognitive flexibility [20]. During adolescence, these capacities undergo protracted maturation tied to the structural and functional refinement of prefrontal-parietal circuitry, with inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility showing particularly steep developmental gains between ages 12 and 17 [21]. This extended maturational window renders EF especially susceptible to environmental perturbation—a point of direct relevance to the digital exposure questions motivating this study.

Emotion regulation, as conceptualized within Gross’s process model, unfolds across a temporal sequence spanning situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, and response modulation [22]. Two strategies have attracted the most empirical attention: cognitive reappraisal, which targets the meaning assigned to an emotional stimulus before a full affective response crystallizes, and expressive suppression, which operates downstream by inhibiting outward emotional expression after the response has already been generated [23]. Adolescents progressively shift toward greater reliance on reappraisal as prefrontal regulatory networks mature, though this developmental shift is neither uniform nor inevitable [24].

How, then, are EF and ER associated at a mechanistic level? Integrative control theory offers one compelling account, proposing that the same prefrontal control architecture subserving EF—particularly inhibitory control and set-shifting—provides the computational resources necessary for deliberate emotion regulation strategies like reappraisal [25]. The dual-systems model adds developmental specificity by positing an imbalance between a rapidly maturing limbic-affective system and a slower-developing prefrontal-cognitive system during adolescence [26]. This maturational asymmetry means that strong emotional impulses can readily overwhelm still-consolidating executive resources, while deficiencies in EF simultaneously constrain the repertoire of regulatory strategies available to the adolescent. At a finer-grained level, each EF facet may contribute to reappraisal through a distinct mechanism: inhibition likely supports the suppression of initial automatic appraisals, working memory updating assists in maintaining and manipulating reappraised interpretations in mind, and cognitive shifting facilitates the transition from one appraisal frame to another [12]. Taken together, these frameworks suggest that EF and ER are not merely correlated outcomes of shared neural substrates but are associated through bidirectional links—executive processes appear to support regulation, and dysregulated affect appears to relate to lower executive efficiency [27]. This bidirectional associative framework forms the theoretical basis upon which our subsequent hypotheses regarding digital environmental moderation are constructed. We emphasize that, given the cross-sectional design of the present study, the term “bidirectional” is used here to describe concurrent associations in both directions rather than to imply temporal precedence or causal feedback [18].

Pathways of digital environmental influence on adolescent cognition and emotion

Different digital activities impose distinct cognitive and affective demands, and collapsing them into a single “screen time” variable obscures these differential effects [17]. Social media platforms, for instance, engage adolescents in continuous social comparison and feedback-monitoring loops that fragment sustained attention and elevate cognitive load even during ostensibly passive browsing [15]. Short-form video consumption presents a related but distinguishable challenge: the rapid succession of high-arousal, algorithmically optimized clips conditions reward circuitry toward novelty-seeking while progressively eroding tolerance for cognitively effortful, slow-paced tasks [16]. Media multitasking—simultaneously toggling between messaging, streaming, and academic work—compounds these pressures by demanding frequent attentional reallocation, a process that taxes inhibitory control and degrades working memory performance over time [28].

At a neurobiological level, these behavioral patterns appear to be associated with the protracted functional maturation of prefrontal cortex. Neuroimaging evidence indicates that heavy digital multitaskers exhibit reduced gray matter density in anterior cingulate regions associated with conflict monitoring and top-down attentional control [29]. Chronic exposure to emotionally charged digital content—cyberbullying incidents, sensationalized news feeds, or socially evaluative metrics such as “likes”—simultaneously co-occurs with heightened amygdala reactivity, shifting the baseline emotional arousal state upward and rendering regulatory demands more frequent and more intense [30]. The prefrontal-amygdala connectivity that ordinarily strengthens across adolescence may, under conditions of persistent digital overstimulation, be linked to consolidation of maladaptive patterns favoring reactive rather than deliberate emotional responding [31].

What deserves particular emphasis is that content type and interaction modality likely matter more than sheer duration of exposure, and recent reviews suggest that aggregated screen-time indicators may obscure meaningful between-activity differences [17]. Active, purposeful digital engagement—such as structured educational activities or creative content production—places qualitatively different demands on prefrontal networks compared with passive, algorithm-driven scrolling [32]. This distinction carries important implications for the present study. Our primary digital engagement measure captures total usage intensity through objective screen time tracking, and we adopt this aggregated measure as the principal moderator in our main models because it provided the most reliable continuous indicator with minimal missing data. However, because device-level tracking also yielded application-category data (social media, short-form video, gaming, entertainment streaming, educational), we additionally conducted exploratory category-specific analyses to probe whether the observed moderation patterns differed by activity type. These exploratory results are intended as hypothesis-generating evidence for future research rather than as confirmatory tests.

Research hypotheses on the association between executive function and emotion regulation

Drawing on the integrative control and dual-systems frameworks outlined above, we advance three testable hypotheses that collectively define the conceptual model guiding this investigation. Each hypothesis is framed in terms of cross-sectional associations rather than temporal or causal processes [18].

Hypothesis 1 (H1)

Higher levels of executive function—particularly inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility—are associated with greater concurrent adoption of adaptive emotion regulation strategies such as cognitive reappraisal, while weaker EF is associated with increased concurrent reliance on maladaptive strategies like expressive suppression. This prediction follows from evidence that reappraisal demands substantial prefrontal computational resources for reinterpreting affective meaning, resources that are directly supplied by intact executive processes [33].

Hypothesis 2 (H2)

Emotion regulation is reciprocally and concurrently associated with executive performance. Specifically, adolescents who habitually employ ineffective regulatory strategies experience prolonged affective arousal that may co-occur with reduced inhibitory control and working memory performance [34]. The reverse association has received preliminary support in adult samples, yet remains largely unexamined in adolescent populations where the prefrontal-limbic balance is inherently more variable [35]. Importantly, this hypothesis is tested as a concurrent association; the cross-sectional data cannot distinguish whether EF and ER influence each other over time or merely co-vary because of shared antecedent factors [18].

Hypothesis 3 (H3)

The intensity of digital engagement moderates the association between EF and ER in both directions. We anticipate that heavy digital consumption is associated with a weaker positive coupling between EF and adaptive ER, and—symmetrically—with a weaker positive coupling between adaptive ER and EF [36]. In contrast, lower levels of digital use are expected to be associated with stronger positive coupling in both directions. We model the interaction on both directional pathways rather than assuming asymmetry, because the cross-sectional design provides no principled basis for restricting moderation to one direction.

Together, these three hypotheses form an integrated cross-sectional model in which EF and ER are bidirectionally associated, and the strength of their association is conditional on digital engagement intensity. This framework moves beyond predominantly unidirectional models that have dominated the field and positions digital engagement not merely as an antecedent variable but as a contextual moderator shaping the concurrent link between cognition and affect during a sensitive developmental period.

Participant selection and experimental procedures

Participants were recruited from middle and high schools across three urban districts using a stratified random sampling strategy, with strata defined by school type (public vs. private), grade level (7th through 11th), and socioeconomic catchment area. Inclusion criteria required participants to be aged 12–17, possess normal or corrected-to-normal vision, report no diagnosed neurological or psychiatric conditions, and have regular access to at least one digital device. Individuals currently receiving psychotropic medication or those with color vision deficiency—relevant to the emotion induction stimuli—were excluded. An a priori power analysis conducted in G*Power, targeting a medium effect size (Cohen’s f² = 0.15) with 80% statistical power at α = 0.05 for the planned structural equation models, indicated a minimum required sample of 320 participants [37]. To accommodate anticipated attrition of approximately 15%, we enrolled 386 adolescents. Table 1 summarizes the demographic composition of the final analytic sample after exclusions.

As presented in Table 1, the sample achieved a roughly balanced distribution across gender, age group, and school type, supporting the generalizability of subsequent analyses.

The experimental protocol unfolded across two sessions separated by one week. This two-session design served two purposes: first, it reduced participant fatigue by distributing the assessment battery across separate occasions, thereby minimizing carry-over effects that could compromise data quality; second, the one-week interval enabled estimation of test-retest reliability for the behavioral EF tasks. During Session 1, participants completed a battery of self-report questionnaires assessing digital media habits, trait emotion regulation tendencies, and demographic background variables [38]. Session 2 comprised the behavioral task battery administered in a counterbalanced order across participants to control for sequence effects. Inhibitory control was assessed via a Go/No-Go paradigm with a 75/25 go-to-no-go trial ratio; working memory updating was measured through a visual 2-back task; and cognitive flexibility was indexed by a cued task-switching paradigm with predictable alternation runs [39]. Following the EF battery, an emotion induction procedure using validated film clips of negative, positive, and neutral valence was administered, after which participants completed a real-time emotion regulation task requiring either reappraisal or suppression of their affective responses [40]. Physiological indices (heart rate variability, skin conductance) were recorded continuously throughout the emotion regulation phase. Measurement instruments and variable operationalization

Executive function was assessed through a multi-method approach combining behavioral paradigms with self-report measures. The three behavioral tasks described in Section Participant Selection and Experimental Procedures—Go/No-Go, 2-back, and task-switching—provided performance-based indices of inhibitory control (commission error rate), working memory updating (discriminability index d′), and cognitive flexibility (switch cost in milliseconds), respectively. To complement these laboratory measures, participants completed the self-report version of the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function (BRIEF-SR), which captures ecologically valid EF difficulties across daily contexts through eight clinical subscales aggregated into two broad indices: the Behavioral Regulation Index and the Metacognition Index [41]. The rationale for this dual-assessment strategy was straightforward: behavioral tasks isolate component processes under controlled conditions, whereas the BRIEF-SR captures how those processes manifest in real-world functioning—and the two do not always converge.

Emotion regulation was similarly operationalized at both trait and task levels. The Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) yielded two subscale scores—cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression—reflecting habitual strategy preferences [23]. At the behavioral level, an emotional Stroop task presented emotionally valenced words (positive, negative, neutral) in colored fonts, with the interference score (response time difference between emotional and neutral trials) serving as an index of implicit emotional reactivity and attentional bias toward affective material [42].

Digital engagement was quantified through two convergent methods. Objective screen time data were extracted from device-level tracking applications (e.g., Apple Screen Time, Android Digital Wellbeing) installed on participants’ smartphones with parental consent, providing daily logs of total usage duration, application-specific duration by category (social media, short-form video, gaming, entertainment streaming, educational), and pickup frequency over a 14-day monitoring window. The primary moderator variable used in subsequent main analyses—digital use intensity—was operationalized as the average daily total screen time (in hours) across the 14-day period. We chose total screen time as the primary metric because it provided the most reliable, continuously measured indicator with minimal missing data across participants. In addition, we retained the application-category breakdowns for the exploratory analyses described in Section Data analysis strategy, allowing us to probe whether the moderation patterns documented for total screen time were broadly distributed or driven primarily by particular activity types [17]. Subjective digital behavior patterns were assessed via the Adolescent Digital Behavior Questionnaire (ADBQ), a 24-item instrument distinguishing passive consumption, active social interaction, and multitasking frequency on a five-point Likert scale [43]. It is worth being explicit about how these instruments fed into the structural model, since the assessment battery is deliberately multi-method. We modeled EF and ER as reflective latent variables, each indicated jointly by task-based and self-report measures rather than by a single modality. The latent EF factor loaded on four indicators: the Go/No-Go commission-error rate, the 2-back discriminability index, the task-switching switch cost, and the BRIEF-SR (entered through its Behavioral Regulation and Metacognition indices). The latent ER factor loaded on three indicators: the ERQ reappraisal and suppression subscale scores together with the emotional Stroop interference score. We chose to combine task and questionnaire indicators within each factor on the reasoning that performance measures and ecological self-reports tap overlapping but non-identical facets of the same underlying capacity; loading them on a common factor lets the latent variable capture the shared variance while the unique, modality-specific variance is absorbed into the residuals. Standardized loadings were all significant and acceptable in magnitude, and we report them alongside the structural estimates so that readers can judge the contribution of each indicator directly.

The results in Table 2 indicate that all self-report instruments exceeded acceptable thresholds for internal consistency (α > 0.85) and temporal stability (r > .80), while behavioral tasks demonstrated adequate test-retest reliability across the one-week interval. Average variance extracted values for latent constructs surpassed the 0.50 benchmark, confirming satisfactory convergent validity for subsequent structural equation modeling analyses.

Data analysis strategy

Prior to hypothesis testing, raw data underwent a systematic preprocessing pipeline. Missing values—accounting for less than 5% of total data points—were handled via full information maximum likelihood estimation to preserve sample size and minimize bias [45]. Outliers were identified using Mahalanobis distance with a chi-square threshold at p < .001 and winsorized to three standard deviations rather than deleted, preserving distributional information. Normality was evaluated through Shapiro-Wilk tests supplemented by skewness and kurtosis inspection (absolute values < 2.0 and < 7.0, respectively).

Analytic procedures proceeded in stages. Descriptive statistics and Pearson bivariate correlations established preliminary associations among all study variables. Hierarchical regression models then tested the incremental concurrent contribution of EF components on ER strategy use after controlling for age, gender, and socioeconomic covariates. The core aim was to model the direct cross-sectional associations between EF and ER, and to test whether digital use intensity moderated these associations in both directions. To test H3 regarding digital engagement as a moderator, we specified the interaction terms within the structural equation modeling framework. We should be candid about why we estimated reciprocal paths in a single cross-sectional model rather than committing to one direction. Our theoretical starting point—integrative control and dual-systems accounts—treats the EF–ER relationship as genuinely two-way, so a model that forced a single arrow would build in an assumption the theory does not license. Estimating both directions at once lets the data speak to the relative magnitude of each association without our pre-judging it. That said, cross-sectional reciprocal specifications are known to admit equivalent or near-equivalent alternatives that fit comparably well [47], and we did not want to leave that possibility unexamined. We therefore fitted three competing specifications for comparison: a forward-only model (EF → ER), a reverse-only model (ER → EF), and a non-directional model in which EF and ER were simply allowed to covary. Their fit was weighed against the reciprocal model using the same benchmarks plus the information criteria (AIC, BIC); the comparison is reported in Section Moderation effects of digital use intensity and interactive model testing. We flag from the outset that any preference among these models rests on theoretical plausibility as much as on fit, and we revisit this as a limitation.

In addition to these confirmatory models, we conducted a set of exploratory category-specific moderation analyses. For each of the five application categories captured by the device-level tracking (social media, short-form video, gaming, entertainment streaming, educational), we re-estimated the SEM in Eq. 3 with that category-specific average daily duration substituted for total screen time as the moderator. These analyses are exploratory in nature—motivated by recent reviews calling for activity-level rather than aggregated screen-time analyses [17]—and the resulting category-specific coefficients are intended to generate hypotheses for future work rather than to provide confirmatory tests.

The statistical significance of conditional effects was evaluated via bias-corrected bootstrap resampling with 5,000 iterations [48]. Structural equation models were evaluated against established fit benchmarks: χ²/df < 3.0, CFI > 0.95, TLI > 0.95, RMSEA < 0.06, and SRMR < 0.08. Exact p-values for χ² tests are reported for all models. Throughout the manuscript, we interpret the SEM path estimates as cross-sectional associations rather than as evidence of causal direction or temporal precedence, in line with established methodological cautions for cross-sectional structural analyses [18]. All analyses were conducted in Mplus 8.3 with robust maximum likelihood estimation.

The pattern is telling: the EF component most relevant to suppressing prepotent responses aligns most strongly with the regulation strategy that demands active reinterpretation, a correspondence that resonates with integrative control predictions [50].

Independent-samples t-tests revealed no significant gender differences in EF composite scores (t(366) = 1.42, p = .156), though females scored marginally higher on cognitive reappraisal (t(366) = 2.18, p = .030, Cohen’s d = 0.23). Age group comparisons yielded more robust effects: the older cohort (15–17 years) outperformed the younger group (12–14 years) on all three EF indices (ps < 0.01), confirming the expected developmental trajectory.

The most striking group differences emerged when participants were stratified by digital use intensity into low, moderate, and high tertiles based on objective screen time data. One-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects for both the EF composite (F(2, 365) = 14.87, p < .001, ηp² = 0.08) and cognitive reappraisal (F(2, 365) = 11.23, p < .001, ηp² = 0.06) [14]. Post-hoc Bonferroni comparisons indicated that the high-use group scored significantly lower than both moderate and low-use groups on EF and reappraisal, whereas moderate and low groups did not differ from each other—suggesting a possible threshold rather than linear dose-response pattern. Concurrent associations between executive function and emotion regulation

To test H1—that EF components are concurrently associated with ER strategy use—we conducted hierarchical regression analyses with cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression as separate dependent variables. The results in Table 4 reveal a clear asymmetry. EF components collectively explained 23% of unique variance in cognitive reappraisal beyond covariates—a substantial increment—but only 11% in expressive suppression. This disparity is not entirely surprising; reappraisal is a cognitively demanding, prefrontally mediated strategy, whereas suppression operates more reflexively at the response output stage and may relate less to the deliberate control processes captured by our EF battery [51].

Among the three components, inhibitory control emerged as the strongest concurrent correlate of reappraisal (β = 0.31, p < .001), followed by working memory updating (β = 0.19, p = .001) and cognitive flexibility (β = 0.14, p = .008). The ordering shifted for suppression: inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility contributed comparably (β = −0.18 and − 0.16, respectively), while working memory played a weaker role (β = −0.11, p = .038). What stands out here is the sign reversal—better EF was associated with less concurrent reliance on suppression, consistent with the theoretical expectation that adolescents with stronger executive resources tend to gravitate toward more adaptive regulatory approaches [52].

As Table 5 shows, the cross-sectional association between EF and cognitive reappraisal was robust (β = 0.42, p < .001), and—consistent with H2—the reverse association from reappraisal to EF was also significant (β = 0.19, p = .002), supporting the bidirectional pattern between EF and ER as concurrent variables. The reverse association from suppression to EF was negative and significant (β = −0.15, p = .012), indicating that habitual suppression use co-occurs with diminished executive performance, a pattern consistent with the resource-depletion account outlined in our theoretical framework [53]. We emphasize that these cross-sectional associations, while consistent with bidirectional theoretical models, do not permit causal inference regarding temporal ordering or directional priority [18].

The interaction terms provided support for H3, with some nuance that the symmetric model specification makes visible. Digital use intensity significantly moderated the EF-to-reappraisal association (β = −0.14, p = .005), meaning that heavy digital engagement co-occurred with a weaker positive association between strong EF and adaptive regulation. The corresponding moderation on the reverse pathway (Reappraisal × Digital use → EF) reached a smaller magnitude and only marginal significance (β = −0.09, p = .050), and the parallel suppression × digital use → EF term was not significant (β = 0.06, p = .197). Thus, the moderation pattern is not strictly limited to one direction, but the attenuating effect of digital use intensity does appear somewhat more pronounced for the EF → reappraisal association than for the reverse pathway. We avoid stronger claims of pathway specificity, given that both directional interactions were estimated in a single model with comparable statistical power [18]. Simple slope analysis clarified the EF → reappraisal moderation pattern: the relationship between EF and cognitive reappraisal was strong at low digital use levels (β = 0.56, p < .001), moderate at average levels (β = 0.42, p < .001), but attenuated—though still significant—at high levels (β = 0.28, p = .003) [54]. Discussion

The findings of this study converge on three principal insights that, taken together, advance our understanding of how executive function and emotion regulation are associated during adolescence—and how digital environments shape that association. Each warrants careful consideration against existing theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence, with the consistent caveat that all results reflect cross-sectional associations and are intended to inform, not pre-empt, future longitudinal work [18].

The first and perhaps most foundational finding is the observed bidirectional association between EF and ER. The structural model revealed not only that stronger executive function was associated with greater concurrent reliance on cognitive reappraisal (the forward path), but also that effective emotion regulation was positively linked to executive performance (the reverse path). This pattern is consistent with what is known about prefrontal-limbic circuit development during adolescence [21, 26]. The dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal regions that support inhibitory control and working memory are anatomically and functionally intertwined with the ventromedial prefrontal and anterior cingulate areas implicated in affect regulation [27, 33]. During adolescence, these circuits are still consolidating—white matter tracts connecting prefrontal cortex to amygdala continue myelinating well into the early twenties [31]. The bidirectional associations we observed are consistent with this shared neural infrastructure: when one system functions well, it may co-vary positively with the other. What our data add is evidence that the reverse association—from ER back to EF—is empirically detectable in an adolescent sample, not merely a theoretical postulate. Habitual reliance on expressive suppression, a strategy that demands ongoing cognitive effort to maintain behavioral masking without resolving the underlying emotional activation [53], was associated with measurably weaker concurrent executive performance. We interpret this finding cautiously: the cross-sectional design does not allow us to determine whether suppression use degrades EF over time, whether adolescents with pre-existing executive weaknesses are more likely to default to suppression, or whether both processes co-occur because of a shared third factor [18].

The second key finding—that inhibitory control emerged as the dominant concurrent correlate of cognitive reappraisal, outpacing both working memory and cognitive flexibility—warrants further examination. On one level, this result aligns with the integrative control account: reappraisal requires suppressing an initial, automatic appraisal of an emotional stimulus before generating an alternative interpretation, and inhibitory control is precisely the capacity that enables such suppression [10, 25]. Interestingly, the Toh et al. [12] meta-analysis found that the association between inhibition and reappraisal was particularly strong when EF was measured using affectively valenced stimuli. Our study incorporated an emotional Stroop task alongside standard EF measures, and the observed strength of the inhibitory control–reappraisal link may partly reflect this measurement approach. We also consider the digital context as adding a layer of specificity that purely laboratory-based studies may miss. In environments saturated with high-arousal, rapid-fire content—short videos engineered for maximum emotional impact, social media feeds designed to capture and hold attention [16]—the concurrent demands placed on inhibitory resources are relentless. Every notification resisted, every impulse to scroll suppressed, every emotional reaction to provocative content dampened co-occurs with a draw on a finite pool of inhibitory capacity [28]. This resource-depletion interpretation aligns with the threshold pattern observed in our group comparisons: moderate digital users showed EF and ER profiles comparable to low users, but high users displayed a sharp performance decline [14], suggesting that inhibitory reserves can absorb moderate demand but appear to buckle under excessive load.

The third finding, and arguably the most consequential for intervention, concerns the moderating role of digital use intensity. The simple slope analysis painted a clear picture: as digital consumption increased, the positive concurrent link between EF and adaptive emotion regulation progressively weakened. Among low-frequency digital users, strong inhibitory control was robustly associated with effective reappraisal. Among the heaviest users, that association was significantly attenuated. Importantly, in the symmetric SEM, the corresponding moderation on the reverse pathway from reappraisal to EF was smaller in magnitude and only marginally significant, with the parallel suppression-side moderation non-significant. We therefore avoid stronger claims that digital engagement selectively affects a single pathway; instead, the evidence is most readily summarized as a broad attenuation of the EF–ER coupling that is somewhat more pronounced for the EF → ER direction than for the reverse. This nuance is a direct consequence of the symmetric model specification, and we explicitly acknowledge it as a feature of the present analytic strategy. The moderation effect challenges overly optimistic accounts suggesting that cognitive strengths can simply buffer against digital correlates [5, 55]; our data suggest instead that the apparent buffering capacity of executive function has limits, and high-intensity digital engagement co-occurs with a smaller buffering margin. The mechanism likely involves both acute attentional fragmentation—interrupting the sustained cognitive processing that reappraisal demands—and more gradual habituation effects that may relate to shallower, more reactive processing styles [4, 29]. Brown and colleagues recently demonstrated that emotion regulation difficulties can predict specific patterns of social media engagement, including image management, social comparison, and negative engagement behaviors [9]. Together with our findings, this suggests a potentially self-reinforcing concurrent pattern, though longitudinal data are needed to confirm any cyclical structure [18]. Prior media-effects research has documented similar associative patterns in attention and academic performance [36]; extending these patterns to the EF–ER interface, as the present study does, is consequential primarily as a foundation for future longitudinal research.

The exploratory category-specific analyses sharpen this picture further. The attenuating moderation of the EF–reappraisal coupling was visible primarily for social media and short-form video use, smaller and one-sided for entertainment streaming, and absent for gaming and educational apps. This pattern echoes recent reviews questioning the assumption that aggregated screen time captures the relevant variance and arguing for activity-specific approaches [17]. We interpret these findings cautiously: the analyses were exploratory rather than preregistered, the categorization was based on application-store classifications rather than fine-grained content coding, and the category-specific durations are themselves correlated. Still, the qualitative pattern—algorithmically curated, high-arousal content streams associated with stronger moderation than goal-directed activities like gaming or educational use—is consistent with theoretical accounts emphasizing content type over duration [15, 16, 32] and provides a concrete direction for future confirmatory research.

These findings carry direct implications for school-based mental health programming and digital literacy education. Traditional approaches to adolescent emotional wellbeing often target EF and ER as separate intervention domains—executive function training on one hand, social-emotional learning curricula on the other [3, 51]. Our results suggest these should be integrated. If the two capacities are bidirectionally associated, strengthening one without attending to the other may yield limited returns. Programs that simultaneously train inhibitory control through structured cognitive exercises and teach adaptive regulation strategies like reappraisal could plausibly capitalize on this concurrent association, though the directional and temporal benefits will need to be confirmed with intervention or longitudinal designs [5, 18, 32]. Equally important, our moderation findings argue against purely restrictive approaches to adolescent digital use—simply reducing screen time—in favor of more nuanced digital literacy interventions that help young people shift from passive, high-frequency consumption patterns toward active, purposeful engagement [17, 19]. The goal should not be digital abstinence but digital competence: teaching adolescents to interact with technology in ways that preserve rather than deplete the executive resources they need for emotional self-regulation.

Several limitations temper these conclusions and should be considered when interpreting the results. Most critically, the cross-sectional design precludes firm causal inferences, and methodological work has shown that cross-sectional estimates of bidirectional or mediational structures can substantially diverge from longitudinal estimates of the same processes [18]. We therefore frame all SEM paths—forward, reverse, and moderated—as concurrent associations rather than as evidence of causal direction, and we view this study as providing preliminary associative evidence to motivate future longitudinal investigations of EF, ER, and digital engagement [13, 18]. A related implication is that our pathway-level comparisons (e.g., the apparently stronger moderation on the EF → ER direction) should not be over-interpreted; the symmetric model specification adopted here allows for direct comparison across pathways, but identifying genuine asymmetry would require longitudinal cross-lagged designs. We are equally mindful that the reciprocal model has equivalent or near-equivalent alternatives: as Table 7 makes plain, a forward-only specification fits the present data essentially as well, so our preference for the reciprocal model is grounded in theory rather than in a decisive statistical margin [47]. Readers should therefore treat the reverse and reciprocal paths as one defensible reading of the covariance structure among several. Objective screen time data, while superior to self-report alone, captured total usage duration as the primary metric in the confirmatory model, and although our exploratory category-specific analyses begin to address activity-type heterogeneity [17], they cannot distinguish qualitative aspects of digital experience with full granularity. The self-report measures of ER (the ERQ) are vulnerable to social desirability bias and limited introspective accuracy; we supplemented these with task-based indices, but the behavioral measures capture somewhat different constructs than habitual strategy use. Finally, our urban-focused sampling strategy limits generalizability to rural populations where digital access patterns and developmental contexts may differ substantially.

Conclusion

This study set out to examine how executive function and emotion regulation are associated in adolescents growing up in digitally saturated environments, and whether the intensity of digital engagement shapes that association. Through a multi-method design combining behavioral paradigms, self-report instruments, and objective screen time tracking in a sample of 368 adolescents, we arrived at three core conclusions that collectively inform how researchers and practitioners should think about cognitive-emotional functioning in the digital age. Because the design is cross-sectional, we frame these conclusions as preliminary associative evidence to be tested in future longitudinal research [18].

First, executive function and emotion regulation are not merely correlated but appear bidirectionally associated in this cross-sectional sample. The structural equation model confirmed significant paths in both directions: stronger EF was associated with greater concurrent adoption of cognitive reappraisal, and effective regulation in turn was positively linked to executive performance. Conversely, habitual suppression use was associated with weaker EF, pointing to a concurrent pattern that may, in longitudinal designs, reveal a vicious cycle. This bidirectional pattern—long theorized on the basis of shared prefrontal-limbic circuitry [25, 27]—had not, to our knowledge, been empirically demonstrated within a digitally contextualized adolescent sample. The practical takeaway is straightforward: interventions targeting only one side of this association will likely underperform those that address both simultaneously, although the directional ordering and causal status of this benefit await longitudinal verification [18].

Second, among the three EF components, inhibitory control stood out as the most potent concurrent correlate of adaptive emotion regulation, consistent with recent meta-analytic evidence indicating a robust inhibition–reappraisal link [12]. Working memory and cognitive flexibility contributed meaningfully, but neither approached the predictive strength of inhibitory control for reappraisal use. We interpret this dominance as reflecting the particular demands digital environments impose on adolescent self-regulation [16, 28]. The constant stream of attention-capturing, emotionally provocative content places disproportionate strain on the capacity to withhold prepotent responses—precisely the capacity indexed by inhibitory control. This finding carries clear implications for intervention design: cognitive training programs aimed at bolstering adolescent emotional health should prioritize inhibitory control exercises, particularly for young people embedded in high-stimulation digital ecologies [5].

Third, digital use intensity functioned as a significant moderator, progressively weakening the concurrent link between EF and adaptive regulation as consumption increased. The simple slope analysis was particularly revealing: at low and moderate levels of digital engagement, strong executive function was reliably associated with effective reappraisal; at high levels, this association was substantially diminished. The threshold-like pattern observed in both the ANOVA and moderation analyses suggests that moderate digital engagement may be relatively benign in associative terms, but beyond a certain point, the cumulative cognitive and emotional correlates of digital saturation appear to overwhelm the apparent buffering associated with well-developed executive resources [14]. The symmetric model specification further indicates that the moderation, while detectable on both directional pathways, is somewhat more pronounced for the EF → reappraisal direction—a nuance that should be revisited in longitudinal data before being claimed as a genuine pathway-specific effect [18]. Exploratory category-specific analyses additionally hinted that social media and short-form video use, rather than gaming or educational apps, were where the moderation mostly resided [17]. We word this loosely on purpose: these tests were unadjusted for multiplicity and the category durations are themselves intercorrelated, so they speak to plausible directions for future work rather than to the unique contribution of any one activity.

From a theoretical standpoint, this work contributes a context-sensitive associative model that positions digital engagement not as a simple antecedent of cognitive or emotional outcomes but as an environmental moderator conditioning the concurrent relationship between the two [17, 43]. This represents an advance over unidirectional frameworks that treat EF and ER as parallel consequences of screen exposure. For educational practice, our findings argue for integrated programming that weaves executive function training into social-emotional learning curricula while simultaneously cultivating digital literacy—not in the restrictive sense of reducing screen time, but in the constructive sense of helping adolescents shift toward intentional, cognitively engaged digital behavior [19, 32].

Future research should pursue at least three directions. Longitudinal panel designs with multiple assessment waves would enable cross-lagged analysis of developmental trajectories and stronger causal inference [13, 18]. Integration of functional neuroimaging—particularly resting-state connectivity and task-based fMRI during concurrent EF and ER challenges—could illuminate the neural substrates underlying the bidirectional associations we identified behaviorally [27, 33]. Finally, cross-cultural comparisons are needed to determine whether the moderating role of digital engagement holds across societies with different technological ecosystems, parenting norms around digital access, and cultural orientations toward emotional expression and cognitive control. These extensions would transform the snapshot provided here into a more complete developmental and mechanistic account of how the digital world may relate to the adolescent mind.
