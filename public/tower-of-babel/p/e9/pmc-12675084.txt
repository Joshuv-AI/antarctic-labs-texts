The lifetime risk of developing atrial fibrillation (AF) among middle-aged adults is about 30%. AF may cause adverse symptoms that impair quality of life. AF increases risks of stroke, systemic embolism, heart failure, dementia, and cardiovascular mortality. Episodes of AF may self-terminate or persist in the absence of clinical intervention. AF has a propensity to recur and progress.

Early clinical practice guidelines stated that the “goal of maintenance [of sinus rhythm] therapy is suppression of symptoms and sometimes prevention of tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy” (p. 2126)1 while expressing uncertainty regarding outcomes such as thromboembolism, heart failure, and death. Safety concerns of antiarrhythmic therapy, particularly regarding proarrhythmia, were emphasized. Trials such as the Atrial Fibrillation Follow-up Investigation of Rhythm Management study appeared to reinforce concerns about the efficacy and safety of antiarrhythmic therapy.2

Emerging evidence supports a modern paradigm in which safe and effective rhythm control reduces adverse cardiovascular outcomes.3 Pulmonary vein isolation has become central to rhythm control, with trials showing benefits in select patients with heart failure. Antiarrhythmic drug therapy also demonstrates reductions in hospitalization, morbidity, and mortality among patients with early or nonpermanent AF, without recent heart failure. Indeed, current guidelines now endorse rhythm control in specific clinical scenarios.4

Existing antiarrhythmic drugs have been plagued by notable safety hazards and contraindications for large segments of the population with AF, yet they are still used because of a substantial unmet need. Despite technological advances, catheter ablation efficacy remains imperfect, with recurrences in roughly one third of patients within a year. Many patients continue to receive treatment with antiarrhythmics even after ablation.5 The substantial public health burden of AF, coupled with its clinical heterogeneity, indicates that a single therapeutic approach is unlikely to address the full scope of the disease.

It continues to be the case that antiarrhythmic drug development is focused on ion channel inhibition or modulation, although some novel approaches are emerging. Molecules under active clinical development for maintenance of sinus rhythm are summarized in Table 1. Some compounds target established ion channels in new ways (eg, inhaled flecainide), whereas others target novel channels (eg, small-conductance calcium-activated potassium channels). Non–ion channel targets include serum glucocorticoid-inducible kinase 1 and calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II. It seems likely that future drug development will focus less on channelopathy and more on underlying pathophysiologic processes (eg, inflammation). New insights gleaned from human genetics have highlighted the importance of ion channels, sarcomeric targets, and developmental programs in AF susceptibility. As the genetic contribution to AF risk is increasingly understood, it seems likely that other pathways and targets will be identified to treat, and possibly even modify, disease.

Recent advances have created a favorable environment for novel antiarrhythmic development. Improved rhythm monitoring has emerged, including 12-lead electrocardiograms, ambulatory monitors, mobile handheld electrocardiograms, and implanted monitors. These monitoring approaches enable more convenient, patient-centric, and denser monitoring than previous approaches, allowing a finer assessment of disease in response to therapy. Increasing recognition of the link between AF burden—the amount of time spent in AF—and adverse clinical outcomes supports the use of a more clinically relevant, quantitative, and statistically efficient disease end point than the traditional measure of time to recurrent AF.

The recent recognition that rhythm control not only improves patient symptoms but also reduces major cardiovascular events has reinvigorated efforts to identify safe and effective rhythm control strategies. This renewed momentum is bolstered by a deeper understanding of AF mechanisms and significant advances in cardiac rhythm monitoring and disease quantification. Together, these developments create an unprecedented opportunity to accelerate the discovery and development of novel antiarrhythmic therapies—ushering in a new era of rhythm control management.
