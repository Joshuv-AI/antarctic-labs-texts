Bibliometric analysis of atrial fibrillation and frailty research based on CiteSpace and VOSviewer
Authors: Wei Pan, Yuanfeng Wu, Linqi Liu, Min Yu, Zeliang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Research on atrial fibrillation (AF) and frailty has increasingly attracted scholarly and clinical attention in recent years, driven by the need to understand their complex interactions and improve care for vulnerable populations. This study aims to systematically analyze the evolving landscape of AF (frailty research).Methods:We extracted data from the Web of Science Core Collection, identifying 687 relevant articles published between 2001 and 2024. Employing bibliometric tools (VOSviewer and CiteSpace), we mapped knowledge networks, analyzing publication trends, contributor geography (46 countries/regions), institutional involvement (1540 institutions), and researcher collaboration (4096 researchers).Results:Publication Trends: Growth Trajectory: Consistent growth in AF-frailty (related publications over the 2001 to 2024 period. Geographic & Institutional Contributions: The United States led in research output, with global participation across 46 countries/regions and 1540 institutions).Conclusions:AF (frailty research has matured into a multidisciplinary field with expanding global contributions). Future advancements require intensified multidisciplinary collaboration and integration of precision medicine frameworks. Prioritizing these will enable optimized, personalized care for frail AF patients, addressing unmet clinical needs in this high-risk population.

1. Introduction

Research on atrial fibrillation (AF) and frailty has gained significant attention in recent years. This study offers a comprehensive analysis of the current state, key research hotspots, and future trends in this field.[1] Between 2001 and 2024, a total of 687 relevant articles were identified from the Web of Science Core Collection.[2] Using VOSviewer and CiteSpace as bibliometric tools, we analyzed and visualized knowledge networks within the literature. Over this period, the volume of publications on AF and frailty has shown consistent growth, with contributions from 46 countries/regions, 1540 institutions, and 4096 researchers. Among these, the United States emerged as the most prolific contributor. Our bibliometric and visual analysis highlights several major research hotspots, including the relationship between AF and frailty,[3] the risk-benefit balance of anticoagulation therapy, the impact of frailty on clinical outcomes, and investigations into potential underlying mechanisms.[4] Emerging areas of research include personalized anticoagulation strategies, frailty assessment and intervention, inflammation-targeted therapies for AF, and multi-omics approaches. Looking ahead, future studies should prioritize multidisciplinary collaboration and the integration of precision medicine principles to develop optimized treatment strategies for frail patients with AF.

Since Sir Thomas Lewis first described AF in 1906, advancements in diagnosis and treatment (including anticoagulation, catheter ablation, and mechanistic insights) have transformed AF management.[5] As the most common cardiac arrhythmia, AF is strongly linked to stroke, heart failure, and mortality.[6] Concurrently, frailty, an aging-related syndrome, has emerged as a critical focus in AF research: it elevates stroke/bleeding risks, impairs quality of life, and worsens clinical outcomes in AF patients.[7] The Global Burden of Disease study notes ~60 million people worldwide had AF in 2021, with a large subset being frail,[8] highlighting the clinical challenge of their coexistence.[9]

Frailty and AF share a bidirectional relationship: frailty may accelerate AF onset via chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation,[10] while AF exacerbates frailty through cardiac dysfunction and systemic inflammation.[11–13] This interplay complicates treatment (e.g., frail patients are less likely to receive anticoagulation due to bleeding concerns, despite its proven net benefit in reducing stroke risk).[10,11]

A substantial literature exists on AF and frailty, but bibliometric analyses (quantitative tools to map research trends) are lacking.[14] Here, we retrieved 687 articles (2001–2024) from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) and used VOSviewer and CiteSpace to visualize knowledge networks. This study aims to identify evolutionary trends, hotspots, and frontiers in AF-frailty research, guiding future clinical and basic research to optimize management for frail AF patients.[15]

2. Methods

2.1. Data collection

This study conducted a search in the WoSCC database on December 31, 2024. The search strategy was as follows[2]: (TS=(“Atrial Fibrillation” OR “Atrial Fibrillations” OR “Fibrillation, Atrial” OR “Fibrillations, Atrial” OR “Auricular Fibrillation” OR “Auricular Fibrillations” OR “Fibrillation, Auricular” OR “Fibrillations, Auricular” OR “Persistent Atrial Fibrillation” OR “Atrial Fibrillation, Persistent” OR “Atrial Fibrillations, Persistent” OR “Fibrillation, Persistent Atrial” OR “Fibrillations, Persistent Atrial” OR “Persistent Atrial Fibrillations” OR “Familial Atrial Fibrillation” OR “Atrial Fibrillation, Familial” OR “Atrial Fibrillations, Familial” OR “Familial Atrial Fibrillations” OR “Fibrillation, Familial Atrial” OR “Fibrillations, Familial Atrial” OR “Paroxysmal Atrial Fibrillation” OR “Atrial Fibrillation, Paroxysmal” OR “Atrial Fibrillations, Paroxysmal” OR “Fibrillation, Paroxysmal Atrial” OR “Fibrillations, Paroxysmal Atrial” OR “Paroxysmal Atrial Fibrillations”)) AND (TS=(“Frailties” OR “Frailness” OR “Frailty Syndrome” OR “Debility” OR “Debilities” OR “Frailty”)). The search was limited to the period from 2001 to 2024, yielding a total of 687 articles. Only English-language articles and reviews were included,[16] while 138 other types of publications were excluded, including Retracted Publications (n = 1), Corrections (n = 1), Proceedings Papers (n = 2), Book Chapters (n = 2), Early Access articles (n = 11), Letters (n = 13), Editorial Materials (n = 23), Meeting Abstracts (n = 64), and Non-English publications (n = 21). After exclusions, 549 articles remained, comprising 444 research articles and 105 review articles.[4] Figure 1 illustrates the detailed workflow of data collection and processing.

2.2. Data analysis

The processed data were analyzed and visualized using bibliometric tools, including Microsoft Excel 2021, VOSviewer, CiteSpace, Charticulator, and Scimago Graphica. The distribution of publications by country was mapped using ArcGIS, while Scimago Graphica was used to illustrate international collaboration on a world map. Microsoft Excel 2021 was employed to analyze publication trends from 2000 to 2024, showcasing the publication trajectory in this field over the past 25 years. VOSviewer 1.6.20 (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, Leiden, Netherlands) was utilized to analyze and visualize country collaborations, author and institutional collaborations, and publication and citation networks. VOSviewer, short for “Visualization of Similarities Viewer,” is a software tool for constructing and visualizing bibliometric networks, developed by Nees Jan van Eck and Ludo Waltman.[14] Scimago Graphica was used to display the annual publication output of countries with high publication volumes. Additionally, Charticulator was employed to create a chord diagram illustrating international collaborations. Charticulator, a project by Microsoft Research, allows researchers to customize visualizations without coding through an online platform (https://donghaoren.org/charticulator/index.html). CiteSpace 6.4.R1 (Chaomei Chen, College of Computing and Informatics, Drexel University, Philadelphia) was used for dual-map overlay analysis of journals, keyword co-occurrence analysis, co-citation analysis of references, and burst detection of keywords and references, with all results visualized. CiteSpace is a bibliometric and visualization software developed by Professor Chaomei Chen and his team.[17]

Figure 2 illustrates the annual publication trends in this field from 2001 to 2024.[18] Although there were some fluctuations, the overall trend shows a steady increase in the number of publications. The red dashed line in the figure represents the fitted curve, which further demonstrates the continuous growth in publication output. Based on this trend, it can be predicted that the number of publications in this field will continue to rise. These results indicate that this research area is developing rapidly and gaining increasing attention.

The work of McManus et al highlights the potential of wearable devices and remote monitoring technologies in managing frail AF patients.[23] Future studies should further explore the use of these technologies for early diagnosis and long-term monitoring, which could significantly improve patient outcomes.

3.4. Analysis of keywords

This study used the g-index with k = 15 to extract keywords from the 549 included articles, resulting in 294 keywords. The keywords were then processed by merging and deleting.[29] Merged keywords refer to synonyms, such as merging “elderly” into “older adults.” Deleted keywords are those that do not convey important information or are irrelevant to this study, such as “outcm.” A total of 39 keywords were merged, and 3 keywords were deleted. After creating the merge and deletion files, the data was reanalyzed. To prevent the emergence of new keywords, k was adjusted to 5 before the analysis, identifying 116 keywords in total. The results were then visualized. Figure 7A is a keyword co-occurrence network diagram. In this diagram, nodes represent keywords, and the size of the nodes represents the frequency of keyword occurrences (the larger the node, the more frequently the keyword appears).[14] The color of the nodes transitions from blue at the center to red at the periphery, indicating that the keywords appeared more recently as the color becomes redder. Each color corresponds to a specific year, as shown in the legend in the bottom left corner of the figure. Each year is also represented by a corresponding colored ring on the nodes. The wider the ring, the more frequently the keyword appeared in that year. Some nodes have a purple ring around their periphery, indicating that the centrality of the node is >0.1, meaning the node plays an important bridging role in the network. The lines between nodes represent co-occurrences of 2 keywords in the same article. The thicker the line, the more frequently the 2 keywords co-occurred. The center of each node displays the node label, which represents the keyword. The font size of the label is proportional to the frequency of the keyword (the more frequent the keyword, the larger the font size). From the diagram, it can be seen that frequently occurring keywords include “atrial fibrillation,” “frailty,” “older adults,” “risk,” and “mortality,” which represent the key research focuses in the field.

(A) Keyword co-occurrence network diagram. (B) Keyword clustering diagram. (C) Keyword timeline view.

Figure 7B presents a keyword clustering analysis based on Figure 7A. Using the log-likelihood ratio algorithm in CiteSpace, cluster labels were extracted from the keyword fields. A total of 9 clusters were generated.[17] The modularity score (Q = 0.7525 > 0.3) indicates that the clustering structure is significant, and the silhouette score (S = 0.9255 > 0.7) suggests that the clustering is convincing. The 9 clusters are as follows: #0 anticoagulants, #1 mortality, #2 bleeding, #3 transcatheter aortic valve implantation, #4 heart failure, #5 therapy, #6 prevention, #7 cardiovascular diseases, and #9 efficacy. These clusters represent different research directions within the field. Figure 7C shows the keyword timeline view. The horizontal axis represents the time when a keyword first appeared. From the Figure 7, it can be seen that some keywords, such as “atrial fibrillation,” “older adults,” “risk,” “mortality,” and “stroke,” appeared earlier and have persisted for a long time. These are also among the most frequently occurring keywords overall. Some keywords, although they appeared early, did not receive much attention later, such as “case fatality rates,” “cerebral infarction,” “mini mental state,” “recurrence,” and “survival.” Recently emerging keywords include “thromboembolism” and “index,” which may become future research hotspots. The analysis of keyword bursts helps explore the shifts in research hotspots in a given field, understand the current research focuses, and predict future research trends. Table 6 lists the top 25 keywords by burst strength. Among them, “replacement” has the highest burst strength of 5.81, with a burst period starting in 2013 and lasting until 2018. 4. Discussion

In this study, we conducted a systematic literature search in the WoSCC database to identify articles on AF and frailty published between 2001 and 2024.[33] Bibliometric visualization was performed on 549 papers from 54 research categories across 46 countries/regions, offering a comprehensive overview of the field’s development.

Since Sir Thomas Lewis first described the electrophysiological characteristics of AF in 1906, the field has evolved through over a century of challenges and advancements. For much of this period, progress in AF management was hindered by limited mechanistic understanding and sparse therapeutic options, a trend mirrored in our bibliometric findings. However, since 2000, breakthroughs (including the introduction of anticoagulants [warfarin and novel oral anticoagulants], the refinement of catheter ablation techniques, and deeper insights into AF pathogenesis) have driven significant improvements in clinical practice.[28] These advancements are reflected in the sharp rise in publication volumes and citation frequencies observed in our analysis, alongside growing international collaboration, which has accelerated knowledge dissemination and innovation.

In recent years, frailty (an aging-related syndrome characterized by declines in physical function, cognitive impairment,[30] and reduced physiological reserve) has emerged as a pivotal focus in AF research. Our analysis highlights the recognition of a complex,[32] bidirectional interplay between frailty and AF: frailty may accelerate AF onset and progression via chronic inflammation (e.g., elevated interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein), oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation, which promote atrial fibrosis and electrical remodeling.[34,35] Conversely, AF exacerbates frailty through worsening cardiac dysfunction and systemic inflammatory responses, creating a vicious cycle.[36] This interplay underscores the critical need to integrate frailty assessment into AF management, a theme echoed in the sustained citation of studies exploring these mechanisms.[37,38]

Early debates in the field centered on whether frailty should inform treatment decisions, particularly regarding anticoagulation. Initial concerns about bleeding risks led to underuse of anticoagulants in frail patients, but accumulating evidence (consistent with our keyword analysis) demonstrated that anticoagulation (especially with novel oral anticoagulants) significantly reduces stroke risk in this population, with benefits outweighing risks.[39,40] This shift established frailty as a key determinant of AF management, driving the development of specialized assessment tools. Fried’s frailty phenotype and Rockwood’s Frailty Index (FI) have become foundational, enabling better identification of high-risk patients and optimization of treatment strategies.[41] Integrating these tools with traditional risk scores (e.g., CHA2DS2-VASc, Hypertension, Abnormal Renal/Liver Function, Stroke, Bleeding History, Labile INR, Elderly, Drugs/Alcohol) has further refined stroke and bleeding risk prediction, as exemplified by Yao et al’s FI-integrated stratification model, which improved anticoagulation adherence and clinical outcomes.[42]

Identified trends like personalized anticoagulation could translate to clinical practice via: Guideline integration: Embed frailty assessment (e.g., Fried phenotype, Frailty Index[36,37]) into AF management guidelines (e.g., 2024 European Society of Cardiology updates), using tools like the modified congestive heart failure, hypertension, age ≥75 years, diabetes mellitus, stroke/transient ischemic attack, vascular disease, age 65 to 74 years, sex category + FI score to stratify stroke/bleeding risk. Interventions: Test low-dose novel oral anticoagulants in frail cohorts (e.g., age ≥ 80 years) to balance efficacy and bleeding risk, as suggested by Lefebvre et al.[31] Multidisciplinary care: develop AF-frailty clinics combining cardiology, geriatrics, and pharmacy to implement tailored strategies (e.g., exercise rehabilitation + nutritional support[43]).

Our bibliometric analysis identifies current research hotspots, including individualized management, frailty interventions, and mechanistic exploration. Individualized strategies (tailored to frailty status, inflammatory profiles, and metabolic characteristics) are increasingly prioritized. For instance, dynamic monitoring of inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) via liquid biopsy has shown promise in predicting stroke risk, while multi-omics studies reveal that energy metabolism disorders in frail patients may accelerate atrial fibrosis, offering novel therapeutic targets.[43] These trends align with the growing focus on precision medicine in cardiovascular research.

Frailty interventions represent a burgeoning frontier, with studies emphasizing multidimensional approaches. Kojima et al’s prospective trial demonstrated that combining exercise rehabilitation and nutritional support improved functional status and anticoagulation adherence in frail AF patients, reducing 1-year and 2-year stroke risks by 30% and 45%, respectively.[44] Multidisciplinary team management, targeting high frailty scores, has also reduced hospitalization rates and complications.[45] Technological advancements, such as AI-based remote monitoring platforms and wearable devices, now enable real-time assessment of frailty status, facilitating timely intervention adjustments.[46]

Pharmacological interventions are also evolving, with anti-inflammatory therapies (e.g., IL-6 inhibitors) and metabolic regulators (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors) showing potential in breaking the AF-frailty cycle.[47,48] A randomized trial highlighted that IL-6 inhibitors reduced inflammation and improved physical function in frail AF patients with high C-reactive protein levels,[49–51] while combination therapies targeting both inflammation and metabolism exhibit synergistic effects.[52–54] However, long-term safety and efficacy require validation in large-scale trials, and personalized approaches (accounting for genetic polymorphisms and metabolic traits) are needed to address variability in treatment response.[54,55]

Future research, as indicated by citation bursts and keyword trends, should focus on 3 key areas: unraveling the molecular mechanisms linking frailty and AF, particularly via multi-omics to identify novel pathways (e.g., energy metabolism, inflammatory signaling); refining dynamic assessment tools that integrate frailty status, biomarkers, and imaging data to enable real-time risk stratification; and developing targeted interventions, including precision nutrition, exercise regimens, and novel pharmacotherapies, to disrupt the AF-frailty cycle. These efforts will not only enhance our understanding of this complex comorbidity but also improve long-term outcomes for frail AF patients, ultimately advancing toward more personalized, effective clinical care.[54]

5. Conclusion and perspective

Frailty has become a critical consideration in the management of AF. This global overview, based on bibliometric methods and visualization tools, highlights the evolution and development of research in the field of frailty and AF over the past decades. The study of frailty and AF represents a novel interdisciplinary field formed through the integration of geriatrics, cardiology, inflammation science, and metabolism research.[34] In recent years, researchers have proposed the “four pillars” of frailty management: assessment, intervention, monitoring, and individualized treatment.[56] This framework outlines the key directions for the diagnosis, treatment, and long-term management of frail AF patients. Future research in this field should focus on individualized anticoagulation strategies for frail patients, balancing stroke and bleeding risks, and understanding the impact of frailty on AF complications.[57] By adopting a multidisciplinary approach and leveraging the integration of biomarkers, imaging techniques, and multi-omics analysis, researchers can gain deeper insights into the interaction mechanisms between frailty and AF.[41] Furthermore, collaboration between research groups across different disciplines should be strengthened to optimize frailty intervention strategies and develop novel therapeutic approaches. Bridging discoveries from laboratory research and clinical practice will be key to providing more precise management strategies for frail AF patients, ultimately improving their long-term outcomes.[42] Progress in this field will not only enhance the quality of life for frail patients but also advance the application of personalized medicine in cardiovascular disease management.
