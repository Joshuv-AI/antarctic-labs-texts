Real-world outcomes of left atrial appendage closure in very elderly compared with younger patients
Authors: Adam Mohmand-Borkowski, Nora Glass, Tendoh Timoh, Peter L. Friedman, Tomasz Rozmyslowicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) has become an attractive alternative to chronic anticoagulation in the very elderly with atrial fibrillation (AF). Data on the outcomes and usefulness of this procedure in this population are limited.ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to analyze the real-world outcomes of LAAO in the very elderly.MethodsThe outcomes of LAAO implantation in very elderly patients aged ≥85 years (average 88.1 years) compared with those in younger patients aged <85 years (average 76.7 years), from a single hospital center serving one of the oldest communities in the United States, were retrospectively analyzed. Successful procedures (at implant), procedural complications, 45-day device-related readmission, device-related thrombus (DRT), stroke, and death within 1 year were examined.ResultsA total of 342 LAAO device implantations were performed during the analysis period, with a high success rate of 98.5% and a very low major complication rate of 0.3%. There was no difference in the in-hospital outcomes or complication rates between the 2 groups. The ischemic stroke rate was 2.6% at 1 year and was not significantly different between the groups. The 1-year all-cause mortality was 14.6% in those aged ≥85 years as compared with 7.9% in those aged <85 years (P = .09).ConclusionLAAO is as safe and effective in the very elderly as in the younger population and may be performed with a very low complication rate. There is a trend toward increased 1-year mortality in the very elderly.

Key Findings ▪Implantation of left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) is as safe and effective in the very elderly (≥85-year-old) as in the younger population.▪There is a trend toward increased 1-year mortality in the very elderly.▪The utility of LAAO implantation should be considered in the very elderly patients.

Introduction

With the current epidemic of atrial fibrillation (AF) and its growing prevalence, it has been estimated that there are currently at least 10.55 million patients diagnosed with AF in the United States (US), representing 4.45% of the general population.1 With the rapid growth in the elderly population in the US, the group of patients that are aged ≥85 years is projected to increase by 111%, that is, from 6.5 million to 13.7 million, from 2022 to 2040.2 This is in line with the increase in the prevalence of AF, with recent analysis estimating the prevalence of AF to be 34.2% in this patient age group.1 Furthermore, the most worrisome complications of AF, that is, AF-associated stroke and stroke-related mortality, are disproportionally higher in the elderly population aged >85 years, with 40% of patients with acute ischemic stroke in this age group being diagnosed with AF.3 The administration of oral anticoagulation therapy to prevent thromboembolic stroke in AF in this population is frequently challenging, as they have more comorbidity-related contraindications for chronic anticoagulation therapy, and a higher incidence of hemorrhagic events than younger patients.4 Furthermore, patients aged ≥85 years who have started chronic anticoagulation therapy are at a higher risk of thrombotic and bleeding events in the first year of therapy compared to patients that are aged 75–84 years, thus frequently leaving the very elderly patients without any stroke protection.5,6

An left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) procedure with a Watchman device (Boston Scientific), as an alternative to chronic anticoagulation therapy for stroke prevention, was non-inferior to warfarin in preventing stroke in patients with non-valvular AF and a high risk of bleeding in the PROTECT AF and PREVAIL trials7,8 and was non-inferior to the direct oral anticoagulants in the PRAGUE-17 trial.9 Further improvements in patient outcomes with the growing experience and introduction of newer-generation Watchman FLX device have been reported.10, 11, 12 As the very elderly patients have a higher risk of stroke as well as bleeding, the LAAO procedure appears to be an attractive alternative to chronic anticoagulation therapy in this population. Indeed, in the National Cardiovascular Data Registry (NCDR) LAAO Registry, 14.1% of patients enrolled in the registry from 2016 to 2018 were aged >85 years old.13 Data on the outcomes of the LAAO procedure in the very elderly population are limited, with this group of patients being underrepresented in the landmark left atrial appendage closure device trials. 2 analyses of outcomes in the very elderly patients aged >85 years in Europe and Japan suggested the feasibility and safety of LAAO procedure in this population group.14,15 However, a population-based analysis from the Nationwide Readmission Database in the US showed similar in-hospital mortality, but an increased risk for peri-procedural complications and 30-day readmission in octogenarians when compared with younger patients.16 With the increase in popularity and indications of the LAAO procedure and in the aging population in the US, the clinical utility, safety, and cost effectiveness of LAAO implantation in the very elderly population remains underexplored. In our retrospective analysis, we evaluated the outcomes of LAAO therapy in very elderly patients depending on the indications and patient pre-procedural risks using real-world data from a single hospital center, serving Cape Cod, MA, which has one of the oldest patient populations in the US.

Methods

Data source

We included consecutive patients with non-valvular AF who underwent the percutaneous LAAO therapy using the Watchman device in a single, high-volume Watchman implant center from January 2018 to July 2023. We conducted this study retrospectively using the information submitted by our institution to the NCDR LAAO Registry. This study was exempted by the Institutional Review Board at our institution because only de-identified patient data, previously released to the NCDR Registry, were used for our retrospective analysis. The research reported in this paper adhered to Declaration of Helsinki guidelines on human studies.

Study population

All patients included in the analysis had the LAAO device implanted based on the US Food and Drug Administration approval of Watchman device in the US and Council for Medical Schemes guidelines. The patients underwent the implantation of Watchman 2.5 device until February 2021, and then that of the Watchman FLX device until July 2023. All consecutive patients from January 2018 to July 2023 were included in the analysis. The baseline patient clinical characteristics used in the analysis included gender, weight <60 kg vs ≥60 kg, CHADSVASc score, history of heart failure, history of transient ischemic attack (TIA)/stroke, history of falls, and bleeding history (including gastrointestinal, intracranial, or other bleeding).

Procedure and post-procedure management

All the LAAO implantation procedures were performed during the analysis period by 3 certified operators with the support of an imaging cardiologist and an anesthesiologist. Imaging with transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and atrial angiography was performed during the implant procedures. TEE was performed by an imaging cardiologist with special training in imaging with Watchman implantations. In cases with contraindications for TEE, procedures were conducted with the guidance of intracardiac echocardiography performed by one of the implanting operators. Intravenous heparin with an activated clotting time of 250–350 seconds was administered during the procedure. All patients were monitored for at least 24 hours post-discharge. Post-procedural antithrombotic therapy was initially administered according to the US Food and Drug Administration approval, including the anticoagulation therapy and aspirin for 45 days, followed by dual-antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel for 6 months, and then aspirin only. With the evolution of post-procedural protocols, subsequently, a less stringent protocol was adopted with anticoagulation therapy (direct oral anticoagulant or warfarin) and aspirin for 45 days and a shortened period of dual-antiplatelet therapy for up to 3 months post-procedure and then single antiplatelet therapy. All patients had pre-procedural imaging with either cardiac computed tomography (CT) or TEE. At least one TEE (or cardiac CT) was performed 45 days post-implant in all patients prior to the decision to discontinue anticoagulation. If clinically indicated (eg, a major bleeding event or the presence of thrombus on the device), the length of anticoagulation (and/or dual-antiplatelet therapy) post-implant was adjusted based on the discretion of the implantation team.

Measured outcomes

The patients involved in the analysis were divided into 2 cohorts based on age: ≥85 years and <85 years. Clinically relevant outcomes were compared between the 2 study groups, including successful procedures at implant, in-hospital peri-procedural events, 45-day device-related readmission, stroke (ischemic vs hemorrhagic), and death regardless of cause within 1 year. Peri-procedural events included procedure period until patient’s discharge. Procedure-related events were divided into major peri-procedural events (including death, cerebrovascular events, systemic embolism, major bleeding, pericardial effusion, device embolization, and vascular complications requiring interventional therapy) and other peri-procedural events (such a presence of thrombus on the sheath noted during the procedure), which did not result in clinical events. The clinical course of patients diagnosed with device-related thrombus (DRT) in the imaging studies during the period of the study was also analyzed.

Statistical analysis and modeling

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. The baseline patient characteristics were stratified based on age groups (<85 years and ≥85 years) and further categorized by key demographic and clinical features. Counts and percentages were calculated for categorical variables, the mean of continuous variables. The χ2 test was used to compare the differences in categorical variables, whereas the Student t test was applied to compare the means of numerical variables. To account for multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate correction was applied to adjust the P-values. Clinical and demographic characteristics were summarized and compared between the outcome variables to examine statistically significant results within 1 year (individuals who survived 1 year after the procedure and those who did not, and individuals who had stroke and those who did not). Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher exact test, whereas continuous variables were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

A statistical model was developed for the combined clinical outcomes. To assess the adverse events following the procedure, the logistic regression15 model was implemented. The outcome was defined as a binary variable, where 0 indicates that no adverse events occurred and the patient survived the first year after the procedure, and 1 represents the occurrence of at least one of the following events: peri-procedural complications, 45-day device-related readmission, death within the first year, or stroke. All available clinical and procedural variables were tested in the modeling process to ensure a comprehensive assessment.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 342 patients who underwent an LAAO device implantation with the Watchman device (144 Watchman 2.5 and 199 Watchman FLX devices) between January 2018 and July 2023 were included in our study. All patients had CHA2DS2-VASc scores of ≥3 as per the inclusion criteria. In total, 253 patients (74%) were aged <85 years and 89 patients (26%) were aged ≥85 years. The average age in the group aged <85 years was 76.7 years (57.6–84.9), and the average age in the group aged ≥85 years was 88.1 years (85.0–93.4). The study population included 109 women (32%) and 233 men (68%), with similar gender distributions in the groups aged <85 years and ≥ 85 years. The average weight in the <85-year-old group was 90.1 kg vs 79.9 kg in the 85-year-old group, with this weight difference being statistically significant (P = .006). Patients aged ≥85 years had non-statistically significant higher stroke risk (CHA2DS2-VASc 5.2 vs 4.8, P = .29) and bleeding risk (HAS-BLED 3.7 vs 3.5, P = .42) compared with younger patients. There was no significant difference in bleeding history and history of prior strokes between the 2 groups, with a trend toward an increased prevalence of heart failure and risk of falls in the group aged ≥85 years (P = .11). In-hospital outcomes

The procedure success rate was 98.5%, with 337 patients having a device placed successfully and 5 failed implantations. There was no significant difference in the success rate between the 2 groups of patients (<85 years old and ≥85 years old). All patients with a successfully implanted device met the implantation PASS criteria and had no significant leaks of ≥3 mm. There was only 1 major peri-procedural event which occurred in the group of patients aged <85 years (pseudoaneurysm requiring thrombin injection by interventional radiology with subsequent resolution). Remarkably, there were no other in-hospital major complications, such as procedural death, cerebrovascular events, systemic embolism, pericardial effusion, and device embolization. There were 4 cases of thrombus attached to the sheath during the device implantations, which subsequently resolved with no clinical events.

Device implant-related readmission within 45 days

There were 2 cases of device-related readmission within 45 days post-procedure, 1 related to device dislodgment (requiring subsequent device extraction and reimplantation), and the other to device thrombus. Non-implantation procedure-related admissions were excluded from the analysis.

Presence of DRT within 1 year

A total of 14 patients (4.1%) who underwent a successful Watchman implantation were diagnosed with DRT in post-procedure imaging with TEE or cardiac CT within 1 year post-implant. All 14 patients with subsequent DRT were on chronic anticoagulation at the time of the implant with either direct oral anticoagulants: apixaban or rivaroxaban (13 patients) or warfarin (1 patient). None of the DRTs occurred during post-procedural anticoagulation therapy, with 2 DRTs diagnosed on dual-antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel and 12 DRTs detected once patients had been transitioned to monotherapy with aspirin. A total of 10 of 14 DRTs occurred in patients with no leak or other abnormalities associated with the Watchman device, a small leak of 1–2 mm was present in 3 of 14 patients with DRT, and 1 of the patients with DRT was noted to have device malposition (the Watchman moved deeper in left atrial appendage as compared with the implant). There was no statistically significant difference in the risk of DRT in <85-year-old patients (3.5%) and ≥85-year-old patients (5.6%).

Stroke and all-cause mortality within 1 year post-procedure

Of the 342 patients who underwent a Watchman procedure implantation, 21 patients (6.1%) had a cerebrovascular accident of any etiology within a year, which included ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage of any cause. The ischemic stroke rate was 2.6%, with no statistically significant difference between the <85-year-old and ≥85-year-old patient groups. The annualized ischemic stroke rate was 3.4/100 patient-years in ≥85 years vs 2.4/100 patient-years in <85 years; P = .701. The all-cause mortality within 1 year post-procedure was 9.6% (33 patients), with a trend toward statistical significance (P = .093) between both groups, with a mortality of 7.9% (20 patients) in the <85-year-old patient cohort and 14.6% (13 patients) in the ≥85-year-old cohort. Statistical analysis of study outcomes

To explore the relationships between patient characteristics and postoperative adverse events, we developed a logistic regression model.17 The model was designed to identify significant associations between pre-procedural characteristics and procedure outcomes. The dependent variable was a binary outcome indicating the presence or absence of procedural adverse events. Owing to a high variance inflation factor for some of the variables, the set of independent variables was reduced to age, sex, weight, history of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, history of TIA, pre-procedure clinical bleeding events, and increased fall risk (Table 4). Among the covariates, only weight was significantly associated with the outcome at the .05 significance level (β = -0.02; P = .05), indicating that a higher weight is linked to a reduced risk of procedural adverse events. Discussion

LAAO has become an appealing stroke prevention strategy in the very elderly population, with two-thirds of the patients with AF aged ≥85 years not receiving anticoagulation therapy because of frequent contraindications and the complications of therapy.18 However, there are non-conclusive data regarding the efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness of LAAO in this patient group. In a multicenter international registry, 15.5% of the patients died within the first year of left atrial appendage closure, with the overall increasing age being associated with an increased mortality risk.19 In a large nationwide database, octogenarians undergoing percutaneous LAAO were found to have similar in-hospital mortality but an increased risk of peri-procedural complications and 30-day readmission when compared with younger patients with an overall low rate of complications.16 The analysis of data for patients enrolled in randomized trials and non-randomized registries of the Watchman 2.5 device showed higher 5-year event rates, with octogenarians deriving similar benefits from LAAO as their younger counterparts.20 Despite a significant reduction in intracranial bleeding at the 5-year follow-up, the incidence of major bleeding events was similarly high in LAAO (20.7%) and non-LAAO (21.2%) in octogenarian patients, reflecting the complexity of older patients and the risk of both ischemic and bleeding events.

Data focusing on the very elderly >85-year-old patients are limited and involve a small group of patients. The analysis of the outcomes of >85-year-old patients in the European EVOLUTION registry suggested that LAAO is safe and effective even though these patients are at a high risk of embolic and hemorrhagic events.14 In a smaller study from Japan, including 34 patients who were 85 years old, the efficacy and complication rates of percutaneous LAAO therapy using the Watchman device in elderly patients aged ≥85 years were comparable to those in a younger cohort.15 With the individualized approach to LAAO therapy using risk profiles in the very elderly population, we analyzed data on the unique, very elderly population undergoing LAAO Watchman implantation in our institution.

The average age of patients undergoing the Watchman implantation in our hospital during the study period was high, that is, 80.3 years, with patients aged >85 years representing 26% of the study group; a significant age difference was present in the <85-year-old and ≥85-year-old (76.7 vs 88.1) patient groups, but they had similar gender distributions, representing a well-balanced analysis cohort. The mean CHADSVASc score in our overall elderly population with multiple comorbidities was 4.9, with no statistically significant difference between the 2 age groups. The in-hospital success rate was high (98.5%), with no difference between the ≥85-year-old patients and younger patients. The procedure was performed with a remarkably low major peri-procedural complication rate (0.3%), supporting the in-hospital safety of Watchman device implantation in the very elderly population as recently reported in a large retrospective analysis.21 Importantly, there were no major procedural complications, such as pericardial effusion, stroke, device embolization, or peri-procedural death. 4 unusual cases of thrombus on the sheath occurred during the procedure in clusters, and they were resolved with subsequent anticoagulation therapy and were suspected to be related to inaccurate activated clotting time results and heparin dosing. Watchman FLX appears to have significant advantages over Watchman 2.5; however, with relatively small sample size, very high implantation success, and low complication rates overall, there was no difference in outcomes between these devices in our study.

There was a trend toward an increase in non–procedure-related 1-year mortality in patients aged ≥85 years as compared with patients aged <85 years (14.6% vs 7.9%, P = .093), which underscores the need for the detailed consideration of the benefits and risks as well as cost-effectiveness of LAAO in the very elderly, especially in the setting of multiple comorbidities that can affect short- and mid-term mortality. The size of the groups was too small to perform statistical comparison of the cause of death, but notably, the causes of death in 13 patients aged ≥85 years included 4 cases of death with decompensation of heart failure, 1 cardiac arrest, 1 recurrent gastrointestinal bleed while on anticoagulation, 1 recurrent cerebral hemorrhage while off anticoagulation, and 5 other non-cardiac deaths.

There was no statistically significant difference in the total stroke rates in both age cohorts. The higher rates of cerebrovascular accident of any cause at 1 year were partially driven, in our analysis, by higher rates of intracerebral hemorrhage (2.3%), with only 2 of 9 patients with intracerbral bleeding having clear hemorrhagic stroke, with other hemorrhagic events primary related to injuries and falls, which underscores the fragility of our elderly population. The ischemic stroke rates (2.4% and 3.4%, accordingly) at 1 year were not significantly different between the age groups. Although higher rates of ischemic strokes in our study could be related to increased risk of other than cardioembolic etiology in our elderly population, the difficulty in post-procedural management, requiring adjusting post-procedural anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy in the elderly population with multiple comorbidities, could also contribute to the rates of ischemic stroke in this population. This should be considered when extrapolating the results of rigorous clinical trials to the expected outcomes of LAAO therapy in real-world clinical practice. The 1-year risk of DRT in our study was 4.1%, with no evidence of an increased risk of DRT in ≥85-year-old patients, and was overall consistent with that reported by previous studies. None of the patients with DRT had a leak of >2 mm, and only 1 patient had other device-related risks for DRT (ie, device malposition). Death within 1 year occurred in 2 of 14 patients diagnosed with DRT, with a cause of death unrelated to the procedure.

A prior history of major bleeding, frequent falls, or prior stroke did not influence the 1-year mortality or 1-year stroke rate in our study. Logistic regression modeling was performed to correlate indications and patient baseline characteristics with combined outcomes, and its results showed that increased age, patient gender, and prior stroke or bleeding or fall risk were not associated with the occurrence of a combined risk of adverse events within 1 year, with only a low pre-procedural weight being associated with worse outcomes, which was likely an indicator of patient frailty.

Combined analyses of clinical trials and registries, based on outcomes in octogenarians, suggested that age should not be a contraindication for LAAO,20 and a large analysis of the in-hospital outcomes of LAAO implantation in octogenarians and non-agenarians concluded that age should not be a contraindication for the procedure in otherwise suitable candidates.21 As elderly patients participating in large clinical trials are usually healthier and are more likely to adhere to peri-procedural management, our data from a single community hospital serving the very elderly population provide unique insights into the trend toward higher non-procedure-related mortality risks in patients aged >85 years after the Watchman’s implantation. Although age alone should not be a contraindication for LAAO implantation, observed trend toward increased mortality in the very elderly as well as less favorable stroke rates after the LAAO procedure in our real-word cohort, despite conducting the procedure with high success and very low complication rate, are thought provoking. Indeed, conducting a study comparing anticoagulation with LAAO implantation or no therapy in very elderly patients with AF with multiple comorbidities could be considered, to help guide future therapy in this patient population. With the increasing peri-procedural safety and expanding indications of LAAO implantation, a further analysis with a larger cohort of very elderly patients in a community-based setting is needed to validate our study findings.

Limitations

This study analyzed the outcomes of Watchman device implantation and does not provide insight into the outcomes of using other LAAO devices in the very elderly. Comparison of LAAO outcomes with patients who had contraindications to chronic anticoagulation for stroke prevention but did not undergo LAAO closure (no therapy group) was not conducted in our study. Major bleeding events, other than peri-procedural bleeding events and intracerebral hemorrhage, during the 1-year follow-up period are not reported. The relatively small sample size and collection of data from a single hospital center represent possible limitations of this study. A small dataset, imbalanced response data, and a limited number of variables describing the patient conditions limit the predictive power of statistical modeling.

Conclusion

The LAAO is as safe and effective in the very elderly as in the younger population, and may be performed with a very low complication rate. There is a trend toward increased 1-year all-cause mortality in the very elderly. Detailed assessments of a patient’s comorbidities and the utility of LAAO implantation should be considered in very elderly patients.
