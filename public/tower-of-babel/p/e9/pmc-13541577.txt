Anticoagulation discontinuation after atrial fibrillation ablation: reassuring data, but for whom?
Authors: Aakash Somappa, Camilo A Estrada-Vargas, Komandoor Srivathsan
Published: 2026

Whether oral anticoagulation (OAC) can be safely withdrawn after apparently successful catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) is among the more consequential unresolved questions in clinical electrophysiology. In this issue of the Journal, Mantoo and colleagues address it directly, with a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing continuation with discontinuation of OAC beyond the post-ablation blanking period [1]. Their analysis arrives at a moment of real uncertainty. Current guidelines continue to anchor long-term anticoagulation to baseline thromboembolic risk rather than to apparent post-ablation rhythm success [2]. Yet many clinicians have begun to ask whether a patient rendered durably free of AF still carries enough thromboembolic risk to justify indefinite anticoagulation.

The synthesis is substantial: ten studies — three randomized and seven observational — comprising more than 240,000 patients. Discontinuation of OAC was not associated with a detectable increase in thromboembolic events, while major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and any bleeding were all lower [1]. The bleeding signal is the most persuasive contribution of the work and deserves emphasis. A 62% relative reduction in major bleeding and a 77% reduction in intracranial hemorrhage are large, directionally consistent across study designs, and biologically coherent: withdrawing a therapy with a well-characterized hemorrhagic cost predictably lowers that cost. For a patient in sustained sinus rhythm after ablation, sparing that hemorrhagic burden is precisely what makes discontinuation attractive.

The efficacy findings warrant greater caution, and here the authors are commendably candid. The pooled analysis also reports lower ischemic stroke and all-cause mortality among patients who stopped OAC — associations that should not be read as causal. The more plausible explanation is confounding by indication: in practice, clinicians preferentially withdraw OAC from younger patients with fewer comorbidities and lower CHA2DS2-VASc scores while continuing it in those judged to be at higher risk. The primary thromboembolic estimate is, moreover, heavily influenced by a single very large observational registry, whereas the randomized trials contribute too few events to anchor it [1]. A reassuring pooled estimate therefore does not, by itself, establish thromboembolic safety.

This brings us to the question that matters most at the bedside: in whom? The evidence base was not designed to resolve outcomes within higher-risk thromboembolic strata, and the pooled analysis was not stratified across the CHA2DS2-VASc spectrum. In a patient with low baseline risk and durable rhythm control, withdrawal is easier to contemplate; as age, heart failure, vascular disease, or prior stroke accumulate, extrapolation becomes progressively less secure. Our recent network meta-analysis of randomized trials offers complementary evidence: among patients who discontinued antithrombotic therapy after apparently successful ablation, pooled stroke incidence was 0.23 events per 100 person-years overall and remained low, at 0.22 per 100 person-years, among those with CHA2DS2-VASc ≥3. That higher-risk subgroup, however, comprised only 379 patients with few events, precluding firm conclusions about safety [3].

There is also a mechanistic reason for this asymmetry. AF may contribute to thromboembolism, but it is also a marker of underlying atrial and vascular disease, and successful rhythm control does not necessarily eliminate that substrate. In the higher-risk patient, restoring sinus rhythm may therefore be insufficient, on its own, to justify withdrawing OAC.

Compounding this is the difficulty of defining a successful ablation. Freedom from AF was ascertained inconsistently across studies, and asymptomatic recurrence is common; continuous-monitoring data such as those from CIRCA-DOSE show that recurrence and true AF burden are readily underestimated by symptom-driven follow-up [1,4]. A patient labeled AF-free on the basis of short-term Holter monitoring is not equivalent to one with continuously documented minimal AF burden, and the safety of discontinuation may differ substantially between them.

Where does this leave the clinician? The randomized evidence is contemporary but remains underpowered for thromboembolic endpoints; the pooled estimate in Mantoo et al.'s analysis was neutral but strikingly imprecise (RR 1.06; 95% CI 0.23–4.86), and the absence of a detected increase is not proof of safety [1]. On present evidence, the current risk-based guidelines [2] should not change. What this analysis provides is not a mandate to stop OAC but a strong case that the question now deserves adequately powered trials — ones that enrich for higher-risk patients and rely on continuous rhythm monitoring.

Until such data arrive, the decision remains individualized, weighing a demonstrated reduction in bleeding against an incompletely characterized thromboembolic risk. For the patient at low baseline risk with durable, well-documented sinus rhythm, this analysis offers reasonable reassurance. For the higher-risk patient — particularly one with prior stroke, advanced age, or heart failure — it offers a hypothesis, not yet an answer.
