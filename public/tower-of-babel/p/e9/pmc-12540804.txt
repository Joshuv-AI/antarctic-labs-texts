The sperm quality change in 6 months recovery from COVID-19: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Xiaoli Zhao, Yu Zhao, Nan Jiang, Kaixi Li, Jiaqi Xu, Ruihong Ma, Zuqian Luan, Tian Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 pandemic has yielded substantial evidence indicating that SARS-CoV-2 may impair reproductive function, particularly by adversely affecting sperm quality. Given the potential implications for male reproductive health, it is crucial to assess the reversibility of such damage, determine the precise recovery timeline, and evaluate post-infection health parameters. Male patients who had undergone sperm analysis prior to SARS-CoV-2 infection were enrolled to evaluate longitudinal changes in sperm quality during a 6-month recovery period. Sperm parameters were collected at five consecutive time points: before infection and at 1, 2, 3, 6 months post-infection. Additionally, quality of life (QoL), psychological status, and sexual function were evaluated via standardized questionnaires within the first month after infection. The primary outcomes were sperm concentration and progressive motility. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were applied for statistical analysis of repeated measures, while Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to assess temporal trends in sperm recovery. 1009 subjects underwent sperm analysis during widespread COVID-19 transmission, from April 2022 to January 2023. Among them, 139 eligible patients completed sperm analysis in the first month post-infection, with 92 (66%) patients were tested in the second month, 72 (52%) patients in the third month, and 64 (46%) patients completed the sperm analysis in the sixth month. The final analytical cohort comprised 74 participants with complete pre-infection baselines and ≥ 3 post-COVID-19 semen analyses at specified intervals (1, 2, 3, 6 months). Sperm concentration [39.22 (95% CI 32.02–46.41)] and progressive motility [36.41 (95% CI 32.29–40.53)] showed a significant decline at 1-month post-infection compared to pre-infection levels. Partial recovery occurred during the second and third months, though values remained significantly lower than baseline pre-infection levels at 6-month follow-up (p < 0.05). Kaplan–Meier analysis estimated median recovery times of 5 months (95% CI 4.57–5.43) for sperm concentration and 3 months (95% CI 2.08–3.82) for progressive motility, with no age-dependent differences observed (log-rank p > 0.05). Comprehensive assessments showed preserved psychological status, quality of life, and urogenital function at 1-month post-infection. Sperm quality was negatively impacted by COVID-19 infection, but temporary, with a recovery period of 3–5 months. Introduction

Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has evolved into a global pandemic characterized by respiratory manifestations and multi-organ dysfunction, attending approximately 15 million fatalities worldwide as of current reports1. SARS-CoV-2 infection has been shown to affect multiple physiological systems, including respiratory, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, urinary, and nervous systems2,3. Epidemiological studies also revealed significant sex-based disparities in infection patterns, with male individuals exhibiting both higher susceptibility to infection and worse clinical outcomes4. Previous studies had demonstrated deleterious effects of COVID-19 on semen parameters5,6, suggesting a potential reproductive toxicity of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

SARS-CoV-2 exerts reproductive toxicity through interconnected mechanisms mediated by viral entry into ACE2-expressing testicular cells, including Leydig, Sertoli, and germ cells. Histopathological evidence from COVID-19 fatalities confirms viral orchitis with apoptotic germ cells and lymphocytic infiltration7, which clinically correlates with elevated seminal inflammatory markers and diminished sperm quality8. The infection triggers immune dysregulation through excessive proinflammatory cytokine release, potentially inducing cytokine storm-mediated tissue damage9. Concurrently, SARS-CoV-2 disrupts redox homeostasis by both increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production10,11 and suppressing antioxidant enzyme activity12. Furthermore, SARS-CoV-2 infection mediates hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular (HPT) axis disruption, leading to elevated serum luteinizing hormone (LH) and decreased testosterone levels13,14. This endocrine dysfunction results from testicular invasion by SARS-CoV-2, as evidenced by clinical observations of hypogonadism in COVID-19 patients15,16. The synergistic effects of direct viral damage, immune-mediated injury, oxidative stress, and hormonal dysregulation collectively disrupt the testicular microenvironment and compromises reproductive function8,17,18.

Research from various countries has yielded inconsistent findings regarding the impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection on sperm quality and male fertility19,20. However, these studies most used uninfected controls rather than tracking individual patients before and after infection. Additionally, emerging evidence highlights the need to examine long-term consequences of COVID-19 on male reproductive health8,21,22. This underscores the necessity for longitudinal studies that monitor the same cohort through pre- and post-infection periods to establish definitive conclusions. Emerging evidence suggests that the SARS-CoV-2 infection transiently impairs sperm quality, with a retrospective study indicating potential recovery within 3–6 months21. Based on current understanding of viral tropism and immune-mediated mechanisms, we hypothesize that COVID-19 infection induces temporary declines in semen parameters, with recovery to pre-infection levels occurring within six months post-infection. This longitudinal study aimed to quantify infection-associated changes in semen quality by comparing pre- and post-infection parameters within the same cohort, while precisely mapping the recovery trajectory through serial evaluations over a six-month follow-up period.

Materials and methods

Study design

This observational study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Teaching Hospital of Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) (Approval number: TYLL2023[Z]022). This research protocol has been registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number ChiCTR2400089593) on 14/09/2024. Participants were recruited from the Reproductive Medicine Department of the First Teaching Hospital of Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, all of whom had undergone sperm analysis prior to their COVID-19 infection. The participants’ psychological status, sexual function, and prostate-related symptoms were assessed using standardized questionnaires during routine diagnosis. Health status data were collected within the first month following COVID-19 infection, and sperm parameters were measured at 1, 2, 3 and 6 months post-infection. Given the retrospective nature of this study, approval has been obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Teaching Hospital of Tianjin University of TCM to waive the requirement for obtaining informed consent from the participants.

Participants

This study involved 1009 male patients seeking care in the reproductive department from April 2022 to January 2023, all of whom maintained regular sexual activity (≥ 1 ejaculation per week) and were actively planning for pregnancy. The inclusion criteria specified that individuals be aged between 20 and 45 years, an interval selected to encompass the most representative reproductive age range for men, have contracted COVID-19 during the period from December 2022 to February 2023, with subsequent negative conversion. Additionally, participants underwent semen analysis prior to COVID-19 infection and completed at least three post-infection semen analyses during 6-month follow-up period (months 1, 2, 3 and 6). Exclusion criteria were established in cases before SARS-CoV-2 infection, and included individuals without previous COVID-19 infection history; those exhibiting infertility factors such as severe oligozoospermia, asthenozoospermia, teratozoospermia, azoospermia, cryptorchidism, genital injury or malformation, and chromosomal abnormalities; patients who had undergone vasectomy or prostate surgery; and men with ejaculatory dysfunction.

All enrolled individuals had mild COVID-19 and were managed as outpatients. Treatment focused on symptomatic relief, with most participants using over-the-counter antipyretics and analgesics such as ibuprofen, as well as and Chinese patent medicines like Lianhua Qingwen capsules. A small number of participants received Oseltamivir for influenza-like symptoms, but no participants received specific anti-SARS-CoV-2 agents or systemic corticosteroids. The short-term use of these medications is not known to affect male reproductive function.

COVID-19 diagnosis and semen analysis

All participants documented clinical symptoms and underwent COVID-19 antigen NP-swab testing for confirmation of COVID-19 infection and recovery. Semen collection followed standardized protocols: after 2–7 days of sexual abstinence, samples were obtained by masturbation into sterile containers and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to complete liquefaction. Semen analysis was conducted in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) laboratory manual for the examination of human semen (6th edition, 2021)23,24. The macroscopic characteristics of the samples, including appearance, volume, pH, and liquefaction, were assessed. Volume was measured utilizing a weighing method, and quantitative pH values were determined with pH test strips. Semen viscosity was evaluated by pipette-derived thread length, with a thread length of less than 2 cm classified as normal viscosity. Computer-Assisted Semen Analysis (CASA) was utilized to analyze sperm motility parameters by placing a 10 µl sample on a Makler Counting Chamber and examined it under ×200 magnification. Sperm concentration was calculated by counting the number of spermatozoa in 10 squares of the grid and expressing the result in millions per milliliter. Sperm motility was classified using a three-category scheme: progressive motility (rapid progressive and slow progressive motility), non-progressive motility, and immotility.

Data collection and outcome measures

Longitudinal data collection occurred at baseline (pre-infection) and at 1, 2, 3 and 6 months post-COVID-19 infection. The demographic information included age, height, weight, Body Mass Index (BMI), comorbidities, history of orchitis, genital infections, mumps, andrological surgeries, as well as records of alcohol consumption, and smoking history. Furthermore, details regarding the initial COVID-19 infection were also documented.

The primary outcomes were sperm concentration and progressive motility. Secondary outcomes comprised multiple semen metrics, specifically: (1) volume; (2) non-progressive motility (NP); (3) rapid progressive motility; (4) immobile sperm percentage (IM); (5) curvilinear velocity (VCL); (6) straight-line velocity (VSL); (7) average path velocity (VAP); (8) migration angle deviation (MAD); (9) amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH); (10) beat cross frequency; (11) linearity; (12) wobble; (13) straightness. Additionally, we assessed patient-reported outcomes related to quality of life; psychological status, and urogenital function, such as erectile function, prostate symptoms, and ejaculation function.

Quality of life was measured with the validated SF-36 Questionnaire, a comprehensive instrument assessing eight health domains: (1) physical functioning, (2) role-physical, (3) bodily pain, (4) general health, (5) vitality, (6) social functioning, (7) role-emotional, (8) mental health. The scale’s standardized scores demonstrate positive correlation with overall health status, where higher values reflect a superior health status.

Depressive and anxiety states were evaluated utilizing the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) and the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), respectively. According to established cutoffs, SDS scores ≥ 53 indicate clinically significant depressive symptoms, graded as mild (53–62), moderate (63–72), or severe (≥ 73). Similarly, SAS ≥ 50 suggest clinically relevant anxiety, categorized as mild (50–59), moderate (60–69), or severe (≥ 70).

Erectile function was assessed utilizing the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF-5). Scores were classified as follows: ≤ 7 indicate severe erectile dysfunction (ED), 8–11 indicate moderate ED, 12–16 indicate mild to moderate ED, 17–21 indicate mild ED, and ≥ 22 indicate the absence of ED. Lower urinary symptoms were assessed via the International Prostate Symptom Score (I-PSS), categorized as mild (0–7), moderate (8–19), and severe (20–35). Additionally, the IPSS quality of Life (QoL) subscore, which ranges from 0 to 6, was utilized to quantify symptom-related life impact. Ejaculatory function was measured using the Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool (PEDT), with the following diagnostic thresholds: ≥ 11 indicate premature ejaculation, scores in the range of 9–10 suggest a potential for premature ejaculation, and scores ≤ 8 effectively rule out premature ejaculation. All questionnaire scores were collected at the time of enrollment, specifically within the first month following COVID-19 infection.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as percentages, whereas the primary and secondary outcome measures were reported as means along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed utilizing the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient.

To address missing at random (MAR) data at a singular post-COVID-19 observation timepoint, we utilized the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method for data imputation, with a maximum of 50 iterations. Five imputed datasets were generated and aggregated using the pooling function for statistical analyses of both primary and secondary outcome measures. To evaluate the impact of missing data handling methods on the primary outcomes, sensitivity analyses were conducted using two approaches: To evaluate the impact of missing data handling methods on the primary outcomes, sensitivity analyses were conducted using two approaches: non-parametric bootstrapping with 2000 resamples to assess sampling variability and the MICE method under the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption to evaluate sensitivity to missing data patterns.

We analyzed longitudinal changes in sperm parameters across five time points (pre-COVID-19 infection, and at 1, 2, 3 and 6 months post-infection) using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) with an exchangeable working correlation structure selected through Quasi-likelihood under the Independence Model Criterion (QIC) optimization, employing robust standard errors to analyze the trajectories. The model specified timepoints as the primary fixed effect with sperm kinetic parameters as continuous dependent variables, where the intercept term represented baseline (pre infection) values, enabling quantification of temporal fluctuations in sperm quality parameters while controlling for repeated measurements.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to assess time-to-recovery for concentration and progressive motility. Recovery was defined as achieving ≥ 80% of pre-infection baseline values, with the recovery interval calculated from the first observed abnormality post-infection to this predefined threshold. The “recovery time” is defined as the duration from the first observed decline post-infection to the return to 80% of the pre-infection level. Survival curves were constructed at four standardized follow-up intervals, with the he cumulative probability of non-recovery estimated through product-limit methods. Only cases presenting ages in the interval 20–45 years were included in the evaluation. Age-stratified analyses (<35 years and ≥35 years) were conducted to evaluate potential effect modification on recovery kinetics.

Questionnaire reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, with validity evaluated through Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Continuous scores were presented as means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

Demographic characteristics

This observational study was conducted between January and July 2023. From an initial pool of 1,009 male patients who underwent semen analysis at our reproductive center from April 2022 to January 2023, we excluded 870 participants based on predefined criteria. The primary exclusion reason was indeterminate COVID-19 infection status (n = 356), followed by refusal to participate (n = 231), non-local residence (n = 23), age ineligibility (n = 16), pre-existing oligoasthenozoospermia (n = 34), and ejaculatory dysfunction (n = 8). An additional 202 participants were excluded due to missing 1-month post-infection testing (n = 75) and incomplete the questionnaire (n = 127). In the end, 139 patients were enrolled in our study in the first month post-infection. Among those, 92 subjects (66.2%) completed follow-up sperm testing in the second month, 72 subjects (51.8%) in the third month, and 64 subjects (46.0%) in the sixth month. Participant attrition was primarily attributed to spousal pregnancy, loss of contact, inconvenience for clinical visits, and missed visits during protocol-defined follow-up periods (Fig. 1). A total of 74 participants completed ≥ 3 post-infection sperm analyses: 59 completed all four scheduled post-infection assessments (months 1, 2, 3, 6), 11 assessed at months 1, 2 and 3, three evaluated at months 1, 2 and 6, and one tested at months 1, 3 and 6.

Flow of the observational study.

The average age of the participants in the study was 34.42 ± 4.56 years (range 22–42 years), with a mean BMI of 25.8. Among the participants, three were diagnosed with mumps, two had a prior history of varicocele, and another had undergone hernia repair surgery. Additionally, 4.05% of the participants presented with pre-existing medical conditions, including hypertension, as detailed in Table 1.

Primary outcomes

Comparative analysis revealed significant acute deterioration in semen parameters following COVID-19 infection. Sperm concentration declined from a pre-infection level of 78.91 (95% CI 65.56–92.26) to 39.22 (95% CI 32.02–46.41) during the first month post-infection (p < 0.01). A similar trend was evident in the progressive motility, which fell from 57.58 (95% CI 47.16–56.00) to 36.41 (95% CI 32.29–40.53). While partial recovery occurred during the second and third months, both sperm concentration and the progressive motility remained significantly lower than the pre-infection levels at 6-month follow up (p < 0.01) (Table 2). In addition, sensitivity analysis was conducted to compare the sperm parameters at different time points (Supplementary Data Table S1).

To obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the recovery trajectory of sperm quality following COVID-19 infection, a survival analysis on 74 participants was performed. The results are illustrated in Fig. 2. Survival curves of sperm concentration revealed an initial risk population of 64 participants, among whom 10 were censored (8 maintained normal levels throughout follow-up, and 2 exhibited initial concentration decline at the 6-month mark). The cumulative non-recovery probability decreased from 81.3% (52/64) at 1 month to 59.4% (38/64) at 2 months, with median recovery time reaching 5 months (95% CI 4.57–5.43). For sperm progressive motility, the survival curve showed a faster recovery pattern, with initial risk population of 59 participants, and 15 censored cases (14 demonstrated no abnormal progressive motility, and 1 showed initial progressive motility decline at 6 months post-infection). Non-recovery probabilities declined from 78.0% (46/59) at 1 month to 30.5% (18/59) by 5 months. The median recovery time was 3 months (95% CI 2.08–3.92). Both parameters exhibited biphasic recovery kinetics, characterized by rapid initial improvement (months 1–2) followed by slower progression (months 3–5). Notably, 53.1% (34/64) of participants with abnormal concentration and 30.5% (18/59) with impaired progressive motility remained unrecovered at 5 months, suggesting potential persistent effects requiring extended follow-up. We stratified the cohort by age (< 35 years and ≥ 35 years) and conducted Kaplan–Meier survival analyses to evaluate potential differences in recovery timelines for sperm concentration and progressive motility (Table 3; Fig. 3). For sperm concentration, the survival curves almost completely overlapped between the two groups, with a median recovery time of 5 months in both groups (< 35 years: 95% CI 3.45–6.56; ≥ 35 years: 95% CI 3.12–6.88; log-rank p = 0.576). Progressive motility recovery showed a non-significant trend toward age-dependent differences, with younger men (< 35 years) exhibiting numerically faster recovery (3 months, 95% CI 1.98–4.02) compared to older counterparts (4 months, 95% CI 2.08–3.92; log-rank p = 0.427). While these findings suggest potential age-related effects on motility recovery, the differences did not reach statistical significance in our cohort.

Sperm kinematic parameters demonstrated significant temporal changes following COVID-19 infection. Motility indicators, including curvilinear velocity (VCL), straight-line velocity (VSL), and average path velocity (VAP), as well as parameters related to the angles of sperm movement, such as migration angle deviation (MAD), linearity, and straightness, exhibited measurable declines during the first month post-infection (p < 0.05). These parameters showed progressive recovery starting from the second month, with complete return to pre-infection levels between the third and sixth month. The amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH), beat cross frequency (BCF), and wobble remained significantly depressed versus baseline (p < 0.05) throughout the six-month observation period.

The instruments demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity (Supplementary Data Table S2). As detailed in Table 5, the post-COVID-19 quality of life scores (mean = 124.76, 95% CI 121.24–127.59) comparable to normative data for the general Chinese population25, indicating minimal infection impact on health-related quality of life. Psychological assessment revealed subclinical scores for both depression (SDS = 39.90, 95% CI 38.26–43.18) and anxiety (SAS = 33.39, 95% CI 31.84–37.09), remaining below established pathological thresholds (SAS ≥ 50; SDS ≥ 53). Urological evaluations revealed minimal prostate symptoms and high subjective satisfaction (I-PSS = 5.40, 95% CI 4.32–6.92; QoL = 2.07, 95% CI 1.74–2.41). Sex function evaluations suggested normal erectile function and ejaculatory function (IIEF = 21.27, 95% CI 20.94–22.41; PEDT = 4.75, 95% CI 3.86–6.00). These collective findings demonstrate preserved psychological well-being, urological health, and quality of life post-COVID-19 infection in our cohort, providing no substantive evidence of impairment in these domains that would warrant further analysis of sperm quality correlates.

Discussion

The global decline in male fertility represents a significant public health challenge26, potentially accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic through both acute infectious and post-recovery multiorgan dysfunction27. While emerging evidence suggests SARS-CoV-2 virus may impair testicular function and spermatogenesis7,8, comprehensive longitudinal data remain scarce regarding the infection’s impact on sperm parameters and their recovery trajectory. Future studies must incorporate extended follow-up periods to evaluate long-term reproductive sequelae, while employing multivariable models to control for established risk confounders including smoking, alcohol consumption, urogenital comorbidities, varicocele, comorbidities and relative medicines, and psychological factors28–30.

This longitudinal study demonstrates transient impairment of sperm concentration and progressive motility following SARS-CoV-2 infection, with maximal reduction at one-month post-infection and partial recovery to baseline within 3–5 months. Our longitudinal within-subject analysis, unlike previous cross-sectional approaches vulnerable to inter-individual variability19, confirms the transient spermatogenic impairment reported in convalescent males21. However, the observed proportion of unrecovered cases at 6-month follow-up in our cohort, though limited by sample size, indicates that current recovery estimates may not fully capture population-level heterogeneity, necessitating extended monitoring in larger studies. Notably, age-stratified analyses revealed no differential recovery trajectories. Compared to other spermatogenic insults, COVID-19’s median recovery time demonstrates intermediate severity, exceeding rebound times for both acute febrile episodes (20 days)31 and prolonged hyperpyrexia (2–3 months)32 while remaining substantially slower than anabolic steroid-induced suppression requiring ≥ 12 months for full restoration33,34. This moderate recovery window aligns with COVID-19’s classification as a moderate-grade spermatogenic insult. The recovery of sperm parameters to within normal ranges is an encouraging sign of restored reproductive potential. However, the observed partial recovery in some cases suggests that functional deficits may persist, necessitating clinical semen analysis for fertility planning.

Sperm kinematic analysis provides critical insights into fertilization competence, asthenozoospermia diagnosis, and functional-metabolic integrity of spermatozoa35,36. Longitudinal assessment revealed distinct recovery patterns in post-COVID-19 sperm kinematic parameters, with VCL, VSL, VAP, MAD, Linearity and straightness exhibiting transient reductions during first month followed by recovery to baseline levels within 3–6 months. In contrast, ALH, BCF, and wobble remained persistently depressed throughout the 6-month observation, suggesting unresolved ultrastructural or functional impairments. The initial decline in VCL, VSL, VAP and BCF aligns with reversible oxidative stress mechanisms observed in acute viral infections and toxicant exposure37,38.

Contrasting with reports of persistent psychological and sexual dysfunction post-COVID-1939–41, our cohort showed no clinically significant impairments in prostate symptoms, psychological status or quality of life at one-month post-infection. This aligns with emerging evidence of comparable anxiety and quality-of-life metrics between infected and uninfected groups, though direct comparability requires scrutiny due to potential cohort difference. This discrepancy may primarily stem from our cohort’s younger, healthier demographic profile (mean age 33.5 years, minimal comorbidities), which potentially confers greater psychological resilience. Compounding this factor, the single-timepoint questionnaire methodology may lack sensitivity to detect transient dysfunction. Most critically, the recognized temporal trajectory of long COVID manifestations, particularly the delayed emergence of urogenital complications typically occurring ≥ 3 months post-infection42–46, aligns with our negative findings at the early assessment window. However, our capacity to definitively attribute such sequelae to SARS-CoV-2 infection remains methodologically constrained by the absence of both pre-infection baselines and longitudinal tracking across the recovery continuum in this cross-sectional single-timepoint assessment.

Spermatogenesis, as a multifaceted process demanding coordinated germ cell function, reproductive organ integrity, and hormonal regulation47, can be disrupted by SARS-CoV-2 through dual mechanistic pathways. Potential direct testicular invasion via ACE2/TMPRSS2-expressing cells remains biologically plausible48. Given the ubiquitous testicular expression of viral entry receptors, infection cannot be definitively ruled out despite the absence of direct viral detection in testicular tissues. Concomitant immune cell infiltration (notably CD3 + T lymphocytes and CD68 + macrophages) and associated inflammation may locally compromise the testicular microenvironment5,49,50. Indirect pathways include fever-induced vascular congestion and edema, systemic inflammation elevating oxidative stress that consequently mediates mitochondrial damage in spermatozoa via ROS51,52, and transient hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular (HPT) axis suppression manifested by reduced testosterone, LH, and FSH levels53,54. Moreover, testosterone binding to androgen receptors (AR) on Sertoli cells is essential for initiating spermatogenic programming and maintaining sperm production efficiency. Critically, AR activity modulates testicular immune homeostasis, where reduced testosterone levels combined with decreased AR sensitivity (e.g., CAG repeat lengths > 23) may predispose to exaggerated inflammatory responses. This AR-dysregulated state potentially exacerbates SARS-CoV-2-induced immune cell infiltration and systemic oxidative stress, creating a pathogenic loop that compromises testicular microenvironment integrity while contributing to multi-organ damage in severe COVID-1955. Crucially, these perturbations exhibit reversibility, as endocrine and spermatogenic parameters generally resolve following clinical recovery.

The strengthen and limitation

Our longitudinal repeated-measures design tracking the same cohort across five timepoints (pre-infection to 6-month recovery) provides unique insights into COVID-19’s impact on sperm quality. Using pre-infection baselines as internal controls mitigated inter-individual heterogeneity confounding cross-sectional studies, while the 6-month duration captured critical recovery phases for defining fertility restoration benchmarks. The study’s primary limitation stems from restricted generalizability due to single-center sampling and small sample size. Further constrained by the absence of pre-infection psychosocial baselines and longitudinal mental health assessments beyond the 1-month timepoint, our capacity to evaluate psychological sequelae and their temporal interplay with semen parameter recovery remains incomplete. Additionally, the lack of mechanistic biomarkers, particularly sperm morphology, endocrine profiles, and DNA fragmentation index, precludes pathophysiological interpretation of recovery patterns. Finally, undetermined long-term effects (> 1 year) in high-risk subgroups with subclinical dysfunction warrant future investigation.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that SARS-CoV-2 infection causes reversible impairment of sperm quality, characterized by significant reductions in concentration and motility with median recovery within 3–5 months post-infection. No clinically meaningful deterioration in health, sexual function, or psychological well-being was observed at one-month assessment though larger validation studies are warranted given methodological constraints.
