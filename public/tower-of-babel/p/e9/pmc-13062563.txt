Factors associated with vegetarian orientation among Seoul citizens: focusing on food literacy and sociodemographic characteristics
Authors: Minjeong Jeong, Eunjin Jang, Youngmi Lee, Sohyun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESThis study examined factors associated with vegetarian orientation among adults in Seoul, with a focus on food literacy (FL) and sociodemographic characteristics.SUBJECTS/METHODSA cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data from the 2023 Seoul Food Survey in Seoul, South Korea. The study included 3,592 adults aged ≥ 18 yrs, who were categorized into vegan, lacto-ovo vegetarian, pollo-pesco vegetarian, flexitarian, and omnivore groups. FL was assessed using a 33-item scale covering 3 domains: nutritional and safety, cultural and relational, and socioecological. Descriptive statistics, general linear models, and logistic regression analyses were performed.RESULTSAmong the participants, 16.0% followed a vegetarian-oriented diet, with flexitarians comprising the largest subgroup (10.9%). Vegetarian orientation was positively associated with being female (odds ratio [OR], 1.41; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.05–1.90), age of ≥ 65 yrs (OR, 2.51; 95% CI, 1.50–4.21) and having a college education (OR, 2.51; 95% CI, 1.78–3.53). Negative associations were found for higher household income (≥ 5 million KRW/month) (OR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.47–0.85) and low food security (OR, 0.33; 95% CI, 0.24–0.44). Vegetarian-oriented individuals had higher FL scores, particularly in the socioecological domain. In the multivariable models, higher FL scores remained significantly associated with vegetarian orientation. Health management (63.8%) was the most commonly reported motivation.CONCLUSIONGender, age, educational level and FL in the socioecological domain were significantly associated with vegetarian orientation. Our study findings underscore the need for policies and programs that promote sustainable diets tailored to specific sociodemographic groups with varying FL levels.

INTRODUCTION

With the recent increasing interest in health and the environment worldwide, sustainable diets that reduce meat intake have gained considerable attention [12]. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations defines sustainable diets as eating patterns that protect the ecosystems and respect biodiversity [3], which can positively affect various areas, including cardiovascular disease prevention, weight management and environmental protection [4]. Among these sustainable diets, vegetarian diets may offer potential health benefits when appropriately planned, while also contributing to environmental protection and resource conservation [5]. However, without adequate intake of alternative protein sources or fortified foods, vegetarian diets may increase the risk of deficiencies in key nutrients commonly obtained from animal-based foods, such as protein, vitamin B12, iron, and zinc [14]. The United Nations Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change reported that transitioning from a meat-based diet to a vegetarian-based diet plays a key role in achieving greenhouse gas reduction targets [6], underscoring the importance of vegetarianism as a choice extending beyond healthy eating to environmental conservation.

In South Korea, the growing interest in sustainable diets has led to an annual increase in the number of people adopting vegetarian practices. As of 2022, the Korea Vegetarian Union estimated that approximately 1.5–2 million people (approximately 3–4% of the population) were vegetarians [7]. Notably, among those aged > 60 yrs, approximately one in 3 individuals identified themselves as vegetarians or aspired to adopt vegetarianism, indicating variations in vegetarian practice by age group [8]. Given age-related changes in dietary intake and nutrient absorption, maintaining adequate intake of essential nutrients may be particularly relevant for older adults adopting vegetarian diets [14].

The increasing number of vegetarians may be closely linked to the individuals’ awareness of health and environmental issues. In both international and South Korean studies, the key motivators for vegetarianism include environmental protection, animal welfare and health concerns [9101112]. Whilst international research often emphasises ethical and environmental values, Korean studies have more frequently highlighted health-related motivations, including disease prevention and weight control [101112]. Additionally, the findings from studies conducted in middle income countries, such as India and Brazil, underscore the role of cultural and religious traditions in shaping the practices of vegetarians [1314]. These findings provide broad insights into the motivations observed across different geographical regions and populations. Vegetarianism can also take the following forms based on the types of food that are restricted: vegan, lacto-ovo vegetarian, pollo-pesco vegetarian and flexitarian [715161718]. Different populations may choose different forms of vegetarianism and these differences may reflect cultural and societal contexts in shaping dietary behaviours.

As the importance of healthy eating habits has come to the forefront, food literacy (FL) has emerged as an important concept related to forming eating habits. FL is derived from health literacy, encompassing an individual’s knowledge, skills and attitudes related to food choices and further influencing one’s health status [1920]. Based on the 2023 Seoul Food Survey, FL is defined as ‘the competencies related to food that involve not only selecting, managing and cooking food for sustaining life and maintaining a healthy lifestyle but also understanding the cultural value of food and recognising the communal, agricultural, and ecological value it carries.’ FL is divided into the following 3 domains: nutritional and safety, cultural and relational, and socioecological [21].

Across countries, FL has consistently emerged as a key determinant of healthy eating behaviours, although its specific impacts vary by context. In Australia, higher FL was associated with healthier choices among socioeconomically vulnerable groups, and in South Korea, it was associated with an increased intake of fruits and vegetables and lower odds of developing obesity [2223]. In Norway, a study among youths found a positive association between FL and vegetarianism [24]. Separately, a comparison of vegetarians and non-vegetarians in another study found that omnivores had the lowest nutrition knowledge scores, suggesting a possible link between dietary orientation and nutrition knowledge [25]. These findings suggest that FL not only supports nutrition-related behaviours but also may guide environmentally conscious dietary patterns across diverse populations.

Although prior studies have examined FL and its influence on various dietary behaviours, only a few have explored its relationship with vegetarian orientation, particularly in conjunction with sociodemographic characteristics. Most existing literature has focused on individual factors, including gender, age, or income, without considering how they intersect with FL to shape dietary choices. Furthermore, most of these studies originated from Western countries, with limited empirical evidence from Asian populations, including South Korea. Understanding this relationship is critical because the broader definition of FL encompasses not only nutritional knowledge and cooking skills, but also environmental awareness that may influence motivations for vegetarian practices [242627].

Seoul provides a unique context to explore these associations due to its rapidly evolving food environment, rising meat consumption among certain age groups, and increasing cultural diversity [28]. Dietary patterns in Seoul include both vegetarian and omnivorous diets, and either pattern may support health depending on overall dietary composition and varying nutrient requirements. Ensuring nutritional adequacy remains important, as animal-based foods are major sources of certain essential nutrients [14]. In South Korea, traditional diets emphasise the consumption of rice and vegetables, but recent dietary shifts towards higher meat intake have made vegetarian practices less common [1125]. Studying Seoul’s diverse population can reveal how FL and sociodemographic factors interact to shape vegetarian orientation [2129]. The present study aimed to examine the association between vegetarian orientation, FL and sociodemographic characteristics among adults in Seoul, whilst also exploring the motivations for vegetarian practice among the participants.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study design and data collection procedure

We utilised data from the 2023 Seoul Food Survey, which is an annual survey conducted since 2020 to examine the dietary habits of Seoul residents to support the formulation of tailored policies and programs. The 2023 Seoul Food Survey was conducted from September 8 to October 13, 2023, and collected 3,594 responses from adults aged ≥ 18 yrs who were residing in Seoul. A stratified multistage cluster sampling method was used to ensure representativeness of the Seoul adult population, with stratification based on administrative district, gender and age, reflecting the distribution of the registered resident population. Within each stratum, enumeration areas were selected as clusters using proportional-probability-to-size sampling, and households within each cluster were randomly selected to minimise selection bias. Trained interviewers from K-stat Research conducted face-to-face household interviews using a structured questionnaire developed by the Food Policy Team of the Seoul Metropolitan Government. Participating households received small non-monetary incentives (e.g., a travel toiletry kit, masks, and an insulated bag) as part of the survey protocol. The household response rate for the 2023 survey was 71%. Direct comparisons between responding and non-responding households were not available; however, when a selected household could not be surveyed, a replacement household was selected using the same sampling framework, matched by administrative district, housing type, and neighborhood unit. In the present analysis, sampling weights provided by the survey were applied to account for the complex survey design and to reduce potential selection bias. Data were collected across all 25 administrative districts over a 5-week period. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adults aged ≥ 18 yrs, (2) currently residing in one of the 25 administrative districts and (3) survey completion through in-person interviews. Of the 3,594 respondents who completed the survey, 2 participants were excluded from the analysis due to incomplete responses for key variables (e.g., vegetarian orientation and food security), resulting in a final analytic sample of 3,592 individuals. All participants provided written informed consent, and the Public Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol (P01-202309-01-004) [21]. This study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study content and methods

Sociodemographic characteristics

We examined the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, including gender, age, educational level, household type, occupation, monthly household income, subjective health status and food security. To ensure appropriate statistical analysis, the continuous variables were reclassified into categorical variables based on the frequency distribution of the responses. The participants were categorised into the following age groups: 18–34, 35–64, and ≥ 65 yrs. These cut-offs were selected a priori based on policy-relevant age thresholds commonly used in South Korea. Individuals aged 19–34 yrs are commonly defined as “youth” under the Framework Act on Youth, while those aged ≥ 65 yrs are widely used as the statutory definition of older adults in social welfare policies, such as the Welfare of Older Persons Act [3132]. Participants aged 18 yrs were included in the youngest age group to reflect the eligibility criteria of the Seoul Food Survey, which defines adulthood as age ≥ 18 yrs. Based on educational attainment, participants were classified as high school or college graduates. Household type was categorised as single-person (n = 617), couple-only (n = 1,435), 2-generation (n = 1,511), and other households (n = 31); however, due to the small number of respondents in the other household category, the respondents were merged with those with a single-person household for analysis. Occupation was categorised as professional, service/sales, manual workers, or others (including students, homemakers and miscellaneous). Monthly household income was classified as < 5 and ≥ 5 million Korean Won (KRW), based on its proximity to the median household income of 5.7 million KRW in Seoul as of the third quarter of 2022 [19]. Food security was classified as high if the respondent indicated that the quantity and quality of food that they consumed were both sufficient and low if one or both were insufficient.

Vegetarian orientation and motivation

The participants were first asked to identify their dietary pattern, and their responses were categorised into the following 5 types: (1) vegan (no consumption of any animal products), (2) lacto-ovo vegetarian (consumes dairy and eggs but excludes meat and fish), (3) pollo-pesco vegetarian (includes poultry and/or fish but excludes red meat), (4) flexitarian (primarily plant-based diet with occasional meat consumption) and (5) omnivorous (regular consumption of both plant- and animal-based foods). For the purpose of analysis, categories (1)–(4) were grouped together as ‘vegetarian-oriented,’ as they reflect a reduced or restricted intake of animal products. The omnivorous group included participants who reported no intentional restriction of animal-based foods. This classification was consistently applied across all analyses to examine the factors influencing vegetarian orientation. The major motivations for following a vegetarian-oriented diet were assessed by asking respondents to select one primary reason among the following 5 items: health management, weight control, environmental protection, animal welfare, or others. Owing to the small number of respondents in the environmental protection (n = 74) and animal welfare (n = 116) categories, these 2 categories were merged into a single category labelled environmental protection. Meanwhile, the participants selecting ‘others’ as their primary motivation (n = 2) were excluded from the analysis, because their response could not be clearly classified into a specific category [21].

FL assessment

FL is a multidimensional construct that reflects individuals’ abilities to make informed food-related decisions in everyday life, encompassing not only nutrition-related knowledge but also social and environmental considerations. In the present study, FL was assessed using a validated instrument consisting of 33 items across 3 domains: nutritional and safety, cultural and relational, and socioecological. The nutritional and safety domain (14 items) assesses competencies related to understanding and applying basic nutrition and food safety information. This domain includes items that measure individuals’ ability to read and interpret nutrition labels, select foods based on nutritional value, plan balanced meals, and apply safe food handling and cooking practices. It also captures confidence in preparing meals and managing food choices in ways that support personal health. The cultural and relational domain (8 items) focuses on the social and cultural aspects of food. Items in this domain assess interest in food culture, enjoyment of eating, and recognition of food as a medium for social interaction and relationship-building. This domain also reflects attitudes toward shared meals, appreciation of traditional food practices, and the perceived role of food in enhancing family and community well-being. The socioecological domain (11 items) captures awareness of the broader social, environmental, and ethical implications of food choices. This domain includes items related to consideration of environmental sustainability, food waste reduction, support for local food systems, and the social responsibility associated with food consumption. It reflects the extent to which individuals take into account ecological impact and societal values when selecting and consuming food. This 3-domain framework is consistent with previous studies conducted in South Korea, which conceptualize FL as an integrated set of competencies encompassing nutritional, social, and socioecological dimensions of food-related decision-making [30]. By adopting this framework, the present study captures FL as a comprehensive construct that extends beyond individual dietary knowledge to include cultural engagement and sustainability-oriented perspectives.

All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’. The FL tool, previously developed and validated, was used in the Seoul Food Survey. It showed construct validity through a factor analysis, and its reliability was confirmed as appropriate, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.88 [28]. The tool assesses perceived competencies in the following 3 domains: nutritional and safety (e.g., ‘I check nutrition labels when purchasing food’), cultural and relational (e.g., ‘I share meals with family or friends regularly’) and socioecological domains (e.g., ‘I consider environmental impacts when choosing foods’). The FL scores were calculated based on the responses in the 3 main domains. Each item was scored on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4 points), and the score for each domain was converted to a 100-point scale by multiplying the mean of its items by 5. The total FL score was computed by summing the 3 domain scores and dividing by 3. The FL scores were used either as continuous variables or grouped into quartiles for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Cross-tabulations and χ2 tests were first performed to examine the associations between sociodemographic characteristics and vegetarian orientation. Differences in motivations for practicing vegetarianism across sociodemographic groups were also analysed. Univariable logistic regression analyses were then conducted to assess the association between vegetarian orientation and each covariate. Three multivariable logistic regression models were constructed sequentially. Model 1 included sociodemographic variables (Gender, age, educational level, household type, occupation, and monthly household income). Model 2 additionally adjusted for food security and subjective health status. Model 3 further included the overall FL score to examine its independent association with vegetarian orientation. Additionally, general linear models were used to compare FL scores across vegetarian groups. Gender-stratified analyses were conducted based on known gender differences in FL and dietary behaviour [21]. All models were checked for multicollinearity using variance inflation factors, and the analyses were weighted to account for sampling design. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and all analyses were performed using STATA 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

Sociodemographic characteristics by gender

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic characteristics by gender. Among the 3,592 participants, 48.0% and 52.0% were male and female, respectively. Educational level, household type, and occupation differed significantly between genders. Among men, 33.8% were high school graduates, whereas this proportion was higher among women (47.6%). In contrast, a higher proportion of men had a college education or higher (66.2%) compared to women (52.4%) (P < 0.001). Regarding household type, women were more likely to live in single-person or other households (24.6%) than men (9.1%), whereas men were more likely to live in couple-only households (46.4%) compared with women (35.2%) (P < 0.001). Professional (47.7%) and manual labour (15.4%) occupations were more common among men, while service/sales (38.3%) and other occupations (30.2%) were more prevalent among women (P < 0.001). Vegetarian orientation differed marginally by gender, with flexitarian diets being more common among women (14.1%) than men (8.7%), whereas omnivory was more prevalent among men (84.4%) than women (78.9%) (P = 0.050).

Sociodemographic characteristics by vegetarian orientation

Table 2 shows the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics according to vegetarian orientation, examined using univariate analysis. Among the participants, 84.1% were omnivores, followed by flexitarians (10.9%), pollo-pesco vegetarians (2.4%), lacto-ovo vegetarians (1.4%), and vegans (1.2%). Women comprised a higher proportion of flexitarians (68.4%), whereas men accounted for a higher proportion of omnivores (44.4%) (P = 0.039). Participants aged ≥ 65 yrs constituted relatively higher proportions among vegans (23.7%), flexitarians (22.1%), and pollo-pesco vegetarians (19.8%), whereas omnivory was most prevalent among participants aged 18–34 yrs (16.8%) (P < 0.001). With respect to education, flexitarian diets were more common among high school graduates (45.1%), whereas omnivory was more prevalent among participants with a college education or higher (58.1%) (P = 0.002). Couple-only households accounted for the largest proportion of flexitarians (53.3%), whereas 2-generation households showed the highest proportion of omnivores (44.0%) (P < 0.001). Participants with a monthly household income of < 5 million KRW accounted for a higher proportion of flexitarians (61.6%), whereas those with an income ≥ 5 million KRW were more likely to be omnivores (48.4%) (P = 0.004). In terms of food security, participants with sufficient food security comprised higher proportions of flexitarians (57.1%) and pollo-pesco vegetarians (71.6%), whereas those with insufficient food security were more likely to be omnivores (68.3%) (P < 0.001). Participants reporting poorer subjective health status accounted for a relatively higher proportion of flexitarians (4.7%) compared with those reporting good health status (3.8%) (P = 0.011).

Motivation for vegetarian orientation

Table 3 presents the sociodemographic characteristics associated with the primary motivation for vegetarian orientation. Among the 672 participants following a vegetarian-oriented diet, health management was the most common motivation (63.8%), followed by weight control (18.9%) and environmental protection (16.5%). Among participants motivated by health management, 51.1% were high school graduates, whereas participants motivated by weight control (65.8%) and environmental protection (73.7%) were more likely to have a college education or higher (P = 0.001). In terms of occupation, service/sales workers accounted for the largest proportion of participants motivated by environmental protection (52.1%), whereas health management motivation was more common among professionals (32.3%) and those in other occupations (22.7%) (P = 0.007). No statistically significant differences were observed in motivation by household income, food security, or subjective health status. A supplementary analysis using more detailed income categories (< 3.5, 3.5–5, and ≥ 5 million KRW) showed similar patterns, with health management being more common in the lowest income group and weight control and environmental protection being more frequent in higher income groups (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

Using the 2023 Seoul Food Survey data, we examined the association between vegetarian orientation and FL among Seoul residents. Our study results indicated that 16.0% of the 3,592 respondents practiced a vegetarian-oriented diet, with 11.0%, 2.4%, 1.4% and 1.2% of them being flexitarians, pollo-pesco vegetarians, lacto-ovo vegetarians, and vegans, respectively. The pollo-pesco vegetarian and flexitarian groups had significantly higher overall FL and FL scores in the socioecological domain than the omnivorous group, confirming a link between vegetarian orientation and FL.

The major factors influencing vegetarian orientation were gender, age, educational level, household type, occupation, monthly household income, food security, subjective health status and FL scores, indicating that a vegetarian orientation was associated with higher FL levels, particularly in the socioecological domain. This finding is consistent with the results from international studies conducted in Australia, Norway and Saudi Arabia, which reported similar associations between FL and plant-based or vegetarian dietary practices [202224]. However, in some subgroups, the differences were not significant, which is likely due to the small sample sizes, cultural dietary norms, or limited variability in FL within those groups, warranting cautious interpretation of the results [2533].

Female respondents were more likely to follow a vegetarian-oriented diet than male respondents (P = 0.039), aligning with the findings from previous studies indicating that women place greater importance on health and environmental factors, leading to more active engagement in vegetarian practices [33]. Additionally, women tend to be more conscious of their weight and prefer plant-based diets that are lower in caloric density and higher in fibre content, which helps promote satiety [3435]. Contrarily, men are less likely to associate vegetarianism with masculinity and often perceive meat consumption as a cultural symbol of masculinity [3637]. These findings suggest that women generally show greater interest in health and food selection, resulting in a higher likelihood of adopting vegetarian-oriented diets. However, although gender showed a significant association with vegetarian orientation in the univariable analysis, this association was not significant in the final multivariable model, suggesting that gender differences may be partly explained by other covariates, such as FL and educational level.

Participants aged ≥ 65 yrs were more likely to follow a vegetarian diet (29.8%) compared to other age groups (P < 0.001), which aligns with the findings from previous research showing that older adults are more likely to reduce their meat consumption and prefer fibre-rich vegetables and grains, primarily for health management, including the prevention of chronic diseases. Additionally, age-related declines in gastrointestinal function and dental health may further contribute to these dietary choices. Such dietary modifications can contribute to improved digestive health and cardiovascular disease prevention [38]. Economic factors may also play an important role in these dietary choices among older adults. Financially disadvantaged older adults are at a higher risk of experiencing food insecurity, which can limit their food choices [3940]. Given that the present study was conducted in Seoul, where the income of the participants are relatively higher than those of individuals from other parts of the country, the proportion of participants with a household income of < 5 million KRW was low. Multivariable analysis (Table 5) showed no significant difference in the vegetarian practice rates between the respondents earning < 5 million KRW a month and those earning ≥ 5 million KRW a month. However, when the monthly household income was further categorised into < 3.5, 3.5–5 and ≥ 5 million KRW based on the 2023 Seoul Food Survey, the differences in vegetarian practice rates and motivations among the participants stratified by monthly household income became evident (data not shown in the tables). Participants earning < 3.5 million KRW primarily adopted vegetarian diets for health management, whereas those earning 3.5–5 million KRW exhibited relatively higher vegetarian practice rates, often driven by environmental concerns. Contrarily, individuals with a monthly household income of ≥ 5 million KRW cited weight management as their main motivation for vegetarianism [21]. These findings suggest that the socioeconomic status may influence both the motivation for and the prevalence of vegetarian practice. Therefore, policymakers should consider the economic and health characteristics of older adults and develop targeted strategies that support access to both healthy plant-based diets and adequate protein intake. Additionally, income-specific vegetarian policies may help simultaneously promote public health and environmental sustainability.

In the univariable analysis (Table 2), the vegetarian practice rate was higher among the high school graduates (19.9%) than among the college graduates (13.4%) (P = 0.002), and the highest rate was observed among the individuals aged ≥ 65 yrs (29.7%). However, in the multivariable analysis (Table 5), which controlled for demographic factors such as age and gender, the college graduates were approximately 2.51 times more likely to follow a vegetarian-oriented diet compared to the high school graduates (P < 0.001), reaffirming the positive association between educational level and vegetarian orientation [3841]. This may indicate that individuals with higher educational levels have greater awareness of the health and environmental benefits of vegetarianism, as well as have better access to relevant information and resources, increasing their likelihood of adopting such dietary practices [41]. Meanwhile, highly educated individuals are more likely to perceive constraints related to cooking and meal preparation time, which can serve as a practical barrier to adopting a vegetarian diet [4243]. Therefore, when designing policies and programs to promote plant-based healthy diets, considering not only the educational level but also other demographic factors, including age, health status and socioeconomic status, is essential to ensure a more tailored approach.

Even after adjusting for age, income and educational level, couple-only households had a significantly higher vegetarian practice rate than single-person households (Tables 5), indicating that couple-only households were more likely to have stable access to a variety of foods. Conversely, single-person households may face financial constraints in terms of purchasing fresh fruits and vegetables and may rely more on dining out or ready-made meals, which can reduce their overall fruit and vegetable intake [4445]. Single-person households also tend to buy in smaller quantities, making fresh food purchases less cost-effective and increasing the time burden associated with meal preparation [46]. This indicates that fostering an environment where single-person households can easily access vegetables and fruits, coupled with policy support to mitigate the cost and effort of maintaining a plant-based diet, could help improve their dietary choices.

The FL scores varied by vegetarian orientation, with the pollo-pesco vegetarian and flexitarian groups exhibiting significantly higher scores in the socioecological domain, highlighting that awareness of environmental sustainability and broader impacts of food choices can significantly shape an individual’s dietary behaviour. Compared with vegans, pollo-pesco vegetarians and flexitarians do not strictly exclude all animal-based foods but rather limit their meat or fish intake as needed. This flexibility may lead to a broader range of food choices and more consistent engagement with environmental considerations, potentially explaining their higher socioecological competency. However, this study did not assess dietary variety or fresh produce intake, and following a vegetarian diet does not necessarily indicate a diverse or fresh produce-rich eating pattern [4748]. Moreover, although vegetarian diets have been associated with various health and environmental benefits, inadequate intake of animal-based foods or appropriate plant-based alternatives may increase the risk of deficiencies in certain nutrients, such as vitamin B12, iron, calcium, zinc, and omega-3 fatty acids. Therefore, adopting a vegetarian-oriented diet should be accompanied by careful food selection, nutritional education, and, when necessary, appropriate supplementation to ensure nutritional adequacy. In the present study, the primary motivation for vegetarianism among participants was health management, indicating that factors such as health, weight control and environmental protection interact in shaping dietary choices [21]. Therefore, programs for improving FL should consider individual motivations and demographic characteristics, offering tailored approaches that address health, environmental and ethical dimensions to promote sustainable dietary practices.

In conclusion, the present study examined the relationship between vegetarian orientation and FL among adults in Seoul, confirming that vegetarian practices are closely linked to health and environmental considerations but differ by various sociodemographic factors. Notably, vegetarian-oriented individuals had significantly higher FL scores, particularly in the socioecological domain, indicating an association between dietary orientation and sustainability-related FL; however, the cross-sectional nature of the present study limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Considering the limited research addressing vegetarianism and FL, our findings offer a valuable foundation for understanding people’s diet choices and for developing educational and policy measures that foster sustainable eating habits by improving the ecological aspects of FL.

The present study has several limitations. First, the data were derived from the 2023 Seoul Food Survey, which may limit the generalisability of the study findings to other regions or rural populations. Second, due to the cross-sectional nature of the present study, the findings are limited to observed associations and cannot determine temporal relationships. Third, although the FL tool has shown acceptable reliability and validity, it only assesses perceived, rather than objective, competencies particularly in the nutritional and safety domain, which may limit its use as a direct indicator of factual knowledge. Fourth, as the data used in the present were self-reported, they may be subject to recall bias or social desirability effects. Lastly, potentially relevant variables, including religious beliefs, cultural influences or neighbourhood food environment, which could influence vegetarian orientation, were not included in the analysis.

Future research should expand to diverse regions and age groups by conducting larger-scale analyses of the associations among vegetarian orientation, sociodemographic characteristics and FL. Researchers should also investigate how specific FL subdomains influence vegetarian practices across different demographic groups, laying the groundwork for tailored interventions promoting sustainable diets. Longitudinal studies should also be conducted in the future to further clarify the reciprocal interactions between vegetarian practices, FL and health outcomes, ultimately informing effective policy and educational strategies to support sustainable diet practices. By incorporating these approaches, policymakers and educators can design comprehensive programs that foster sustainable eating habits across various populations.
