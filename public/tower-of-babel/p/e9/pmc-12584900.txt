Current diagnostic approach to fungal infection in the critically ill patient
Authors: Alfredo Maldonado-Barrueco, Inmaculada Quiles-Melero, Julio García-Rodríguez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Invasive fungal infections are a major cause of morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients, with an increasing global incidence and species diversity, especially after the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. Diagnosis relies on a combination of classical methods (microscopy, culture) and non-classical tools including biomarkers (1,3-β-D-glucan, galactomannan, mannan) and molecular assays. Fungal infections (candidiasis, aspergillosis, pneumocystosis, and mucormycosis) requires tailored diagnostic strategies based on host risk factors, epidemiology and specimen type. Combining diagnostic tests improves sensitivity and negative predictive value, guiding timely antifungal treatment. An integrated, pathogen-specific approach is essential to improve outcomes in the critical ill patient.

Introduction

Invasive fungal infections (IFIs) represent a significant global health concern, particularly among critically ill patients and individuals with compromised immune systems. Recent estimates, based on data from over 120 countries and literature spanning 2010–2023, indicate an annual incidence of approximately 6.5 million IFIs, resulting in 3.8 million deaths of which about 2.5 million are directly attributable. Invasive aspergillosis leads with over 2.1 million cases and 1.8 million deaths (85.2% mortality), followed by chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (1.8 million cases, 340,000 deaths), candidemia and invasive candidiasis (1.6 million cases, 995,000 deaths), Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) (505,000 cases, 214,000 deaths), and cryptococcal meningitis (194,000 cases, 147,000 deaths). Additional serious fungal infections affect 300,000 people annually, causing 161,000 deaths. Fungal asthma impacts 11.5 million individuals, contributing to an estimated 46,000 deaths per year [1].

IFIs are increasingly recognized as common complications in critically ill patients, particularly those with immune dysregulation. Epidemiological data show a rising incidence of these infections, along with a notable shift in fungal species. Infections caused by non-Candida albicans and cryptic Aspergillus species (often exhibiting intrinsic resistance to azoles and other antifungal agents) are becoming more prevalent. The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic has further exacerbated this trend, contributing to a marked increase in IFIs among hospitalized patients. These developments underscore the urgent need for improved global surveillance, diagnostic capacity, antifungal stewardship, and equitable access to effective therapies [2].

Data from the ENVIN-HELICS (National Surveillance Study of Nosocomial Infection in ICU - Hospitals in Europe Link for Infection Control through Surveillance) reported an increase in the incidence of ICU-associated infections since 2005 [3]. In relation to fungal infection, the trend is an increase in aspergillosis (primarily because of COVID-19-Associated Pulmonary Aspergillosis) while candidemia has decreased, probably due to greater control in the use and replacement of medical devices.

Microbiological diagnostic tests for fungal infections

Microbiological fungal diagnosis typically involves classical culture and direct microscopy. The sensitivity of these methods varies depending on the sample type and the diagnostic technique used (Table 1). In relation to invasive aspergillosis (IA), the isolation of Aspergillus spp. from lower respiratory tract is the first indication of IA. However, the probability of a non-selected risk patient from whom Aspergillus spp. has been isolated having IA was calculated to be 12%. However, this diagnostic yield is influenced by underlying comorbidities: 72% in patients with neutropenia, 58% in solid organ transplant recipients, and 28% in critically ill patients without those risk factors [4].

Currently, several non-classical diagnostic techniques are available for the detection of invasive fungal infections (IFIs), primarily based on the identification of specific fungal biomarkers. These include: 1,3-®-D-glucan panfungal antigen (BDG), Aspergillus spp. antigen (galactomannan, GAL), lateral flow for antigen/antibodies detection, immunochromatography, Candida albicans, germ tube antibodies (CAGTA), manano antigen/antibodies (Mn/Ab Mn) and nuclear acid amplification tests (NAAT) The selection of a specific test depends largely on the suspected fungal pathogen, such as Candida spp., Aspergillus spp., Pneumocystis jirovecii, or organisms from the order Mucorales. In all cases, the pre-test probability is a critical factor in guiding both the choice of diagnostic method and the interpretation of results. According to Fagan’s application of Bayes’ theorem to diagnostic reasoning, the positive likelihood ratio indicates how much a positive test result increases the probability that the patient truly has the disease, by comparing the probability of a true positive to that of a false positive [7].

Invasive Candidiasis (IC). Blood cultures remain the gold standard for the diagnosis of candidemia. According to the 2025 guidelines [8], biomarkers and nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) are not yet routinely recommended as standalone tools for diagnosing invasive candidiasis (IC). However, they provide valuable support in both diagnosis and prognosis.

Available biomarkers include (1→3)-β-D-glucan (BDG), a panfungal marker; the detection of mannan antigen and anti-mannan antibodies (Mn/Ab Mn); and the Candida albicans germ tube antibody (CAGTA), which targets cell wall components of Candida spp. The sensitivity and specificity of these techniques vary between 60–87% and 58–90%, respectively (Table 2).

Several commercial NAAT platforms are available for Candida spp. detection, with reported sensitivities ranging from 60% to 95% and specificities between 92% and 99%. Among them, the T2Candida® magnetic resonance platform (T2 Biosystems®, Lexington, Massachusetts, USA) was notable as the only FDA-approved molecular assay for candidemia, with sensitivity and specificity rates around 90–95%. Unfortunately, this technology has recently been withdrawn from the market.

The main advantage of combining biomarkers and molecular tests lies in their ability to increase the negative predictive value (NPV) for IC. Conversely, their positive predictive value (PPV) improves—either individually or in combination—as the pretest probability of IC increases (Table 2).

Among all biomarkers, BDG is the most widely used in clinical practice. Nevertheless, evidence regarding its utility for guiding treatment decisions or antifungal discontinuation in critically ill patients remains conflicting [9,10]. Further multicentre studies are warranted to clarify its role and inform clinical management in this population.

Invasive Aspergillosis (IA). IA primarily occurs in patients with significant comorbidities, including moderate to severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), decompensated liver cirrhosis, uncontrolled HIV infection, and solid tumors. Additional risk factors include prolonged or high-dose corticosteroid therapy, invasive procedures in the ICU, prolonged antibiotic treatment, and recent or concurrent viral infections [17].

Several diagnostic tools are available for the detection of IA, including biomarkers such as GAL (Aspergillus-specific) and BDG (nonspecific), as well as rapid assays like the GAL or JF5 lateral flow assays, with high accordance with ELISA test, and NAATs [18,19]. A metanalysis showed a serum GAL sensitivity (11% IA prevalence, Platelia Bio-Rad® cut-off: 1) of 72% in neutropenic population [20]. However, this sensitivity decreases to 16.7% in non-neutropenic population [21]. In contrast, its use in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) demonstrates higher sensitivity, around 60–90% [18,21]. Notably, the combined use of GAL in BAL and serum BDG significantly enhances diagnostic performance, with sensitivity and NPV reaching up to 100% for IA in critical ill population [18]. However, the addition of NAAT tests to this combination does not result in further improvement in PPV or specificity [18] (Table 3).

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP). PJP primarily occurs in patients with underlying risk factors such as immunosuppression, prolonged corticosteroid therapy, respiratory comorbidities (i.e COPD), sepsis, use of novel molecular inhibitors, advanced age, and underlying immunodeficiencies such as HIV infection. Although its true incidence remains unknown, PJP is increasingly recognized as an emerging infection, with reported hospital outbreaks and a growing number of cases in non-traditional risk groups [17].

The gold standard for diagnosing PJP remains immunofluorescence microscopy on respiratory tract specimens. However, NAAT (primarily quantitative RT–PCR) is playing an increasingly prominent role in the diagnostic approach. When performed on BAL samples, NAAT offers near 100% sensitivity and a high NPV, making it a valuable tool to rule out PJP infection. The use of quantitative cut-off values to distinguish between colonization and true infection has been proposed. However, no consensus has been reached to date, primarily due to the lack of standardization across diagnostic techniques [23].

Serum BDG levels are typically elevated in PJP, but BDG alone is insufficient to definitively rule out the disease [24]. Non-invasive specimens such as nasopharyngeal aspirates and oral rinses are now commonly used for NAAT, particularly in patients with a high pretest probability. In such cases, the combined use of BDG and NAAT can support the PJP diagnosis. However, a negative result from both tests does not definitively exclude PJP infection [23].

Mucormycosis. The diagnosis of mucormycosis remains a significant challenge due to the absence of specific biomarkers and reliable non-invasive tests [17]. Currently, the detection of mucorales by NAAT in serum for the non-invasive diagnosis of mucormycosis is gaining importance, as reflected in the MODIMUCOR trial [25]. Multicenter reproducibility studies are being conducted in the following areas. Recently, an immunochromatographic assay have been developed using a monoclonal antibody targeting Rhizopus arrizhus (the most common causative species of mucormycosis in Europe) or panmucoral for the detection of a hyphal growth–associated polysaccharide in both BAL and serum samples [26,27].

Conclusion

The diagnosis of invasive fungal infections requires a multifaceted approach that begins with a thorough clinical history and an accurate assessment of pre-test probability. Routine use of direct microscopy and culture remains essential as a first diagnostic step. Understanding local prevalence, epidemiological patterns, and individual risk factors is key to guiding both clinical suspicion and test selection. Given the variability in diagnostic performance across fungal pathogens, it is crucial to choose the most appropriate test based on the suspected organism. In general, available diagnostic tools offer higher NPVs, making them especially valuable for ruling out infection. When possible, combining different diagnostic tests can significantly improve sensitivity and overall diagnostic accuracy, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes.
